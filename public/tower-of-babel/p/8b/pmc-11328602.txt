Pain, function and peritendinous effusion improvement after dry needling in patients with long head of biceps brachii tendinopathy: a single-blind randomized clinical trial
Authors: I-Wei Chen, Ying-Tzu Liao, Hsin Tseng, Hsiao-Che Lin, Li-Wei Chou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLong head of biceps brachii tendinopathy, a frequent source of anterior shoulder pain, may lead to discomfort and diminished function. The objective of this study is to assess the efficacy of dry needling and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation in these patients.Patients and methodsThirty patients were randomized into dry needling and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation groups and assessed before treatment, 8 and 15 days after treatment using a visual analogue scale, shoulder pain and disability index, pressure pain threshold, tissue hardness, and biceps peritendinous effusion.ResultsBoth treatments significantly reduced the visual analogue scale in immediate (p < 0.001), short-term (p < 0.01), and medium-term effects (p < 0.01). Dry needling outperformed transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation for the pain (p < 0.01) and disability (p < 0.03) subscales of the shoulder pain and disability index in the short-term and medium-term effects, respectively. Pressure pain threshold increased after both treatments but didn’t last beyond 8 days. Neither treatment showed any improvements in tissue hardness of the long head of biceps brachii muscle. Notably, only the dry needling group significantly reduced biceps peritendinous effusion in both short-term and medium-term effects (p < 0.01).ConclusionsDry needling showed non-inferior results to transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation in reducing pain and disability and demonstrated even superior results in reducing biceps peritendinous effusion (see Graphical Abstract).Trial registrationThe Institutional Review Board of the China Medical University Hospital (CMUH107-REC2-101) approved this study, and it was registered with Identifier NCT03639454 on ClinicalTrials.gov.Keywords: Shoulder pain, myofascial trigger point, ultrasound, acupuncture, dry needling

Introduction

Patients with shoulder pain have a higher risk of work disability compared to healthy individuals, and the resulting loss of productivity poses a burden to their families and society [1]. The prevalence of shoulder pain increases with age and physically demanding occupations, with a point prevalence of 26% and lifetime estimates of 67% [2]. Common symptoms of biceps brachii tendinopathy are pain in the anterior shoulder and radiating pain in the biceps brachii muscle. This condition often results from secondary causes, such as movements that are not commonly experienced, trauma, underlying inflammatory disease, and tendon instability [3]. Nonsurgical treatment remains the first-line treatment for long head of biceps brachii(LHBB) tendinopathy [4].

A myofascial trigger point (MTrP) is defined as a palpable, hypersensitive spot found in the skeletal muscle taut band and can be categorized into active and latent forms [5]. The development of MTrPs can alter normal motor control patterns and subsequently accelerate muscle fatigue, including local and referred pain. Patients with shoulder pathologies showed many more active and latent MTrPs in the shoulder muscles when compared to healthy individuals [6]. Dry needling (DN) is a technique that uses monofilament needles to penetrate the skin and directly target the MTrP. This results in the mechanical disruption of muscle fibers and nerve endings without delivering any drugs [5]. Based on neurophysiological mechanisms, we hypothesize that DN insertion to the MTrP of the biceps muscle will not only influence the needle insertion site locally but also the proximal LHBB tendon, resulting in improved pain levels, function, and ultrasonographic evaluations of the LHBB.

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) was selected for the comparison group in our study due to its cost-effectiveness and safety with minimal side effects [7]. The analgesic mechanism of TENS is primarily attributed to the gate-control theory of pain, involving the inhibition of pain by stimulating non-nociceptive Aß fibers [8]. Based on a systematic review, there is moderate-certainty evidence indicating that TENS results in lower pain intensity during or immediately after treatment compared to a placebo, with no serious adverse events reported. Therefore, we have selected TENS for comparison with DN [9].

LHBB tendinopathy is frequently identified as a secondary condition that commonly coexists with various shoulder pathologies, such as anterior glenohumeral instability, rotator cuff disease, subscapularis injury, internal impingement, and subacromial impingement [10–13]. According to studies, a significant proportion, ranging from 76% to 85%, of patients diagnosed with rotator cuff tears also exhibit LHBB tendinopathy [14–16]. In contrast, primary bicipital tendinopathy is less prevalent and is typically observed among younger individuals involved in athletic activities, particularly those with a history of engaging in sports with repetitive provocative movements [4]. Moreover, it is noteworthy that the sheath surrounding the LHBB tendinopathy extends from the synovial lining of the glenohumeral joint, indicating a direct potential for rotator cuff pathology to impact the LHBB tendon [4]. Although LHBB tendinopathy is often associated with other shoulder pathologies, such as rotator cuff injury, it should be noted that a high prevalence rate of asymptomatic rotator cuff tendon tears exists (ranging from 8% to 40% for partial-thickness tears and 0% to 46% for full-thickness tears) [17]. Therefore, in symptomatic patients, LHBB tendinopathy should be considered as a potential primary cause of pain [18]. Thus, treating LHBB tendinopathy alone may relieve the symptoms even in the presence of other shoulder pathologies, such as rotator cuff injury.

Well-defined physical therapy treatments for LHBB tendinopathy are currently lacking. Experts have agreed on using a combination of exercises, manual therapy, and patient education to manage this condition [19]. Individuals who undergo biceps tenodesis surgery have lower chances of arm pain and Popeye deformity than those who undergo biceps tenotomy. The impact of routinely removing the LHBB tendon on rotator cuff tear progression and long-term shoulder function remains unclear, necessitating further research [20]. However, very few studies have focused specifically on the MTrPs of LHBB tendon.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of DN as a treatment for LHBB tendinopathy and compare its results with TENS in the immediate, short-term, and medium-term. Previous studies have only observed the effects of DN on the needle insertion site of the LHBB muscle [21]. Shoulder pain has multiple causes, including biceps tendinopathy [22]. We hypothesize that DN insertion into the MTrP of the biceps muscle will not only alleviate pain in the bicipital groove but also enhance function and ultrasound evaluation of the proximal LHBB tendon, thereby reducing shoulder pain and comparing its effect to TENS.

Patients and methods

Study design

The single-blind randomized clinical trial was conducted from September 2018 to April 2019 at the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation and the Department of Acupuncture in the China Medical University Hospital. The Institutional Review Board of the China Medical University Hospital (CMUH107-REC2-101) approved this study, and it was registered with Identifier NCT03639454 on ClinicalTrials.gov. Before participating in the study, all patients provided written informed consent.

Participants

The visual analogue scale (VAS) was chosen as the primary outcome measure. Based on a prior study [23], we employed a significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.95 in a paired samples t-test. A total of 27 patients were estimated to be required for the study. Accounting for a 15% dropout rate, the sample size was calculated using G-power 3.0.18 software, which indicated that 32 patients needed to be recruited. Depending on parametric data distribution, between-group effect sizes were calculated with the difference between the data of ‘post-intervention’ and ‘pre-intervention’. A moderate effect size (Cohen’s dz = 0.61) was observed between groups for the post-intervention outcome [24].

We clinically diagnose LHBB tendinopathy based on a history of anterior shoulder pain and physical examination findings. However, ultrasound is ultimately used to confirm the diagnosis, as it is considered the most reliable method for definitively diagnosing biceps tendinopathy [22]. The patients with a clinical diagnosis of LHBB tendinopathy were screened for eligibility and recruited based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) age over 20 years old; (2) experiencing anterior shoulder pain for more than one month; (3) tenderness over the bicipital groove; (4) VAS score greater than 5; (5) positive Yergason’s test; (6) biceps peritendinous effusion (BPE) of more than 1 mm thickness on ultrasound [24]; (7) Patients had to sign consent forms after understanding the procedures and purpose. We use a VAS score greater than 5 because we aim to treat chronic patients whose shoulder pain can significantly impact their daily activities, as a previous study suggested that a VAS score exceeding 54 mm indicates ‘severe pain’ [25]. As Yergason’s test has a high specificity (79%) but a relatively low sensitivity (43%) in diagnosing biceps tendinopathy, we also incorporated clinical history, tenderness point, and ultrasound in the diagnosis of biceps tendinopathy [26]. High-resolution ultrasound demonstrated high specificity for diagnosing LHBB tendon conditions. The accuracy of orthopedic special tests varied significantly among studies, with Yergason’s maneuver as the sole valuable test for confirming proximal LHBB tendon pathologies [27]. However, Yergason’s test continues to exhibit a wide spectrum of sensitivity and specificity, ranging from 14% to 75% for sensitivity and 78% to 89% for specificity [28].

Exclusion criteria included patients with (1) acute medical diseases [29], sensory deficits, pregnancy, dementia, or organic mental abnormalities; (2) a history of drug abuse that could affect pain measurement; (3) prior neck, upper back, or upper limb surgery; (4) central or peripheral nerve disease; (5) intra-articular injection treatment within the last six months; (6) rheumatoid arthritis; and (7) contraindications for clinical trial interventions. Rheumatoid arthritis can impair shoulder function due to inflammation [30]. In summary, we apply these exclusion criteria to prevent inaccurate patient data, biased study results due to comorbidities, and increased risk of adverse events.

Outcome measurements

VAS was chosen to determine the pain level, which consists of a 10 cm scale with two endpoints. Patients were asked to draw an indicator between the two endpoints, and the distance from point 0 to the indicator drawn by the patient was measured and recorded as the level of pain [31]. The VAS assessments were performed before treatment, immediately after each treatment, on day 8, and on day 15 after treatment. The minimal clinically important difference in patients treated for rotator cuff disease was estimated to be 1.4 cm [32]. Patients were categorized based on their response to a 4-item question about pain improvement after treatment. Those who chose ‘Good – satisfactory effect with occasional pain or stiffness’ were deemed to have experienced a minimally important difference, indicating a small change [32]. VAS reliability is good for chronic musculoskeletal pain patients, but its validity is questionable. Nevertheless, VAS remains the most commonly used tool for assessing musculoskeletal pain [33]. To compare the between group comparison, we used percentage change (%) defined as the post-treatment value minus the baseline value divided by the baseline value and multiplied by 100.

The Numerical Rating Scale version of the Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI) was used to determine the participants’ shoulder pain and disability [34]. The total SPADI includes 13 items subdivided into two categories: pain (5 items) and disability (8 items) subscales. Each item is scored from 0 to 10. The pain scale sums up points from 5 items, which are then divided by 50 and multiplied by 100. The disability scale sums up points from 8 items, which are then divided by 80 and multiplied by 100. The total score sums up points from all 13 items, which are then divided by 130 and multiplied by 100. Higher scores indicate worse pain and disability. SPADI assessments were conducted before the first treatment, on day 8, and on day 15 after treatment. The minimal clinically important difference for the SPADI ranges between 8 and 13 [35].

Pressure pain threshold (PPT) is defined as the minimum pressure that would induce pain, as suggested by Fischer AA [36]. We utilize the OE-220 Tissue Hardness Meter/Algometer Combo by ITO to measure the PPT. It consists of a force gauge attached to a 1 cm2 surface rubber disc. The patients were seated in chairs with backrests, with their arms in a neutral position, elbows flexed at 90 degrees, and forearms supinated. The MTrP in the LHBB muscle belly was marked with a pen, and the measurement was repeated three times at 60-second intervals. The pressure was steadily increased in a perpendicular direction by 1 kg/sec over the marked area until the patient reported a transition from a non-painful to a painful pressure sensation. The same procedure was used to measure the LHBB tendon site, but the measuring point was located in the bicipital groove. Both the DN and TENS groups were measured at the same location. PPT assessments were conducted before treatment, immediately after each treatment, on day 8, and on day 15 after treatment. The hand-held dynamometer proved to be a valid and reliable instrument for assessing the PPT [37].

The estimation of muscle hardness is defined by the relationship between applied pressure and soft tissue indentation, with the latter being recorded by a position sensor within the pressure algometer [38]. We utilize the OE-220 Tissue Hardness Meter/Algometer Combo by ITO to measure the tissue hardness. It quantifies the tissue hardness of MTrPs in the LHBB muscle, which is more objective than relying on palpation by clinicians alone. The OE-220 Tissue Hardness Meter/Algometer Combo by ITO used in this study had two built-in pressure sensors: the outer and the inner circumferential sensor. When the outer circumferential sensor reached a value of 30 N, the value of the inner circumferential sensor was defined as the hardness of the tissue [39]. The tissue hardness assessments were conducted before treatment, immediately after each treatment, on the day 8 and day 15 after treatment. The tissue hardness meter appears valid and reliable for measuring tissue stiffness [40].

Mild BPE showed the strongest association with biceps tendinopathy in sonographic examinations, highlighting the rationale for using ultrasound in its diagnosis [24]. Based on the literature review, the sensitivity of ultrasound in detecting LHBB tendon pathology ranges from 50 to 96%, while its specificity ranges from 98 to 100%, as compared to magnetic resonance arthrography and arthroscopy [22]. The patients were in a sitting position with their forearm resting on their thigh, the arm mildly abducted, elbow flexed at 90 degrees, and the forearm supinated. The thickness of the BPE was measured in the short-axis view, and the most prominent thickness of the effusion was determined visually [24]. The BPE assessments were done before treatment, on day 8, and day 15 after treatment, using a LOGIQ™ P9 ultrasound system from GE Healthcare Inc., USA, with a linear-array probe, all administered by the same skilled doctor. Inter-rater reliability for tendinopathy was high with a standardized procedure [41].

Procedure and intervention

The participants were randomly assigned to either the TENS or DN group using a computerized randomization program, which was conducted by a researcher who was not involved in data collection or patient treatment. The TENS and DN treatments were carried out by different physicians, and the program for each participant lasted 15 days. TENS or DN was applied on days 1, 2, and 4. Outcome measures included VAS, SPADI, PPT, tissue hardness, and ultrasonographic evaluation of the BPE, assessed by the same researcher blinded to the treatment groups. Assessments were conducted before treatment, immediately after each treatment, and on days 8 and 15 (Figure 1). The patient’s baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, pain level, baseline function, and BPE, were recorded (Table 1). The dry needle was inserted into one active MTrP in the LHBB muscle, where the patient felt soreness and pain (Figure 2a). A local twitch response was observed during the procedure. The fast screwed-in and screwed-out technique was performed with a speed of up to 30 back-and-forth movements within 30 s [42]. TENS pads were applied to the active MTrPs in the LHBB muscle of the arm and forearm (Figure 2b). The treatment lasted for 20 min with a constant mode, a frequency set at 200 Hz, and a pulse width set at 200 µs [43]. The intensity of the TENS was set when the patients felt a strong but non-painful electrical paresthesia beneath the electrodes.

Statistical analysis

The researchers used the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS 18.0) software for Windows to analyze the data. The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. The within-group analysis was conducted using the paired sample t-test to compare the measurements taken before and after treatment within each group. The between-group analysis was conducted using the independent two-sample t-test to compare the measurements between the DN and TENS groups. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance for all analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the two groups

Initially, 32 patients were included in the study; however, 2 patients could not follow the schedule and were subsequently excluded. The remaining 30 patients were randomly allocated into two groups, with 15 patients in each group: TENS and DN (Figure 3). There were no significant statistical differences between the two groups before treatment except for the thickness of the BPE, which was worse in the DN group (TENS group: 1.34 ± 0.28; DN group: 1.76 ± 0.50; p < 0.01 between groups), as shown in Table 1.

Visual analogue scales

Both the DN and TENS groups showed significant reductions in VAS scores on Day 1, Day 2, and Day 4 post-treatment compared to pre-treatment (DN: Day 1: 6.27 ± 1.79 Vs 4.27 ± 1.91, p < 0.001; Day 2: 5.13 ± 2.17 Vs 3.60 ± 2.06, p < 0.001; Day 4: 4.80 ± 2.01 Vs 2.80 ± 1.93, p < 0.001; TENS: Day 1: 6.07 ± 1.39 Vs 5.20 ± 1.32, p < 0.001; Day 2: 5.67 ± 1.59 Vs 4.57 ± 1.68, p < 0.001; Day 4: 4.47 ± 1.85 Vs 3.60 ± 2.13, p < 0.001) (Table 2). The DN group demonstrated significant differences in short-term (6.27 ± 1.79 Vs 3.80 ± 2.91, p < 0.01) and medium-term effects (6.27 ± 1.79 Vs 3.03 ± 2.70, p < 0.01). Similarly, the TENS group showed significant differences in short-term (6.07 ± 1.39 Vs 3.53 ± 2.07, p < 0.01) and medium-term effects (6.07 ± 1.39 Vs 3.80 ± 2.31, p < 0. 01). No differences in percentage change (%) were observed in the between groups comparison for short-term and medium-term effects (Table 4, Figure 4).

Shoulder pain and disability index

The DN group showed significant differences in both short-term (47.53 ± 24.15 vs. 32.53 ± 23.58, p < 0.01) and medium-term (47.53 ± 24.15 vs. 34.67 ± 27.48, p < 0.01) effects for the SPADI total score. Similarly, the TENS group showed significant differences in both short-term (40.73 ± 20.87 vs. 32.60 ± 22.70, p < 0.01) and medium-term (40.73 ± 20.87 vs. 29.87 ± 22.11, p = 0.02) effects for the SPADI total score.

When separating the SPADI into two subscales, the pain and the disability scale, the DN group showed significant differences in both short-term (56.13 ± 21.71 vs. 37.73 ± 28.48, p < 0.01) and medium-term (56.13 ± 21.71 vs. 38.93 ± 31.15, p < 0.01) effects for the pain subscale. The TENS group showed significant differences only in the medium-term effect (48.80 ± 20.45 vs. 35.60 ± 24.22, p = 0.02), but not in the short-term effect. For the disability subscale, the DN group showed significant differences in both short-term (38.67 ± 29.08 vs. 27.00 ± 19.64, p < 0.01) and medium-term (38.67 ± 29.08 vs. 29.93 ± 25.23, p = 0.03) effects, while the TENS group showed significant differences only in the short-term effect (32.27 ± 24.70 vs. 25.73 ± 23.88, p < 0.01) but not in the medium-term effect. No significant differences in percentage change (%) were observed in the between-group comparison for either short-term or medium-term effects. In summary, the DN group showed a significantly better outcome in the pain subscale for the short-term effect and in the disability subscale for the medium-term effect compared to the TENS group (Table 3, Figure 4).

Pressure pain thresholds

The PPT outcomes were assessed at two different sites: the MTrP in the LHBB muscle and the LHBB tendon in the bicipital groove. While there was no significant difference after each treatment, there was a consistent trend in the immediate PPT score at the LHBB tendon site following both DN and TENS treatment (Figure 4). No significant difference in the percentage change (%) was noted in the short-term or medium-term effects for each group (Table 4, Figure 4).

Tissue characteristics of MTrP in the LHBB muscle

Before conducting the research, we assumed that the tissue hardness (TH) of the muscle would primarily decrease at the insertion site of the DN due to the potential of alleviating taut bands. However, our results did not show any significant immediate, short-term, or medium-term effects in both groups (Table 2). Regarding the between-group comparison, our results indicated no statistically significant differences, except for the short-term effect, which suggested that the DN technique was not inferior to the TENS group (p = 0.03) No significant difference in the percentage change (%) was noted in the short-term or medium-term effects for each group (Table 4, Figure 4). Although tissue hardness appeared to be lower in the DN group on day 8, the difference did not reach statistical significance. We will further discuss the possible explanations for this observation in the discussion section.

Ultrasonographic evaluation of the bicipital peritendinous effusion

Regarding the thickness of BPE, significant outcomes were observed in the short-term effect (1.76 ± 0.50 vs. 1.33 ± 0.28, p < 0.01) and medium-term effect (1.76 ± 0.50 vs. 1.27 ± 0.25, p < 0.01) in the DN group. However, neither the short-term nor medium-term effect showed any significant findings in the TENS group. Furthermore, we observed significant differences in the percentage change (%) between groups in the short-term effect (p = 0.04) (Table 4, Figure 4).

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that DN targeting the MTrPs in the LHBB muscle could be a useful alternative for relieving chronic pain caused by LHBB tendinopathy before surgery. Our study is consistent with prior research indicating the effectiveness of DN in improving MTrPs across various body regions. As demonstrated in previous studies [21,23,44,45], these regions include the upper trapezius muscle, chronic mechanical shoulder pain of unspecified origin, chronic bicipital tendinopathy, and upper-quarter myofascial pain, etc. Treatment of MTrPs in the biceps brachii muscle has been shown to alleviate the referred pain [6]. A case series conducted by McDevitt AW et al. found that combining DN with eccentric-concentric exercise and stretching led to significant improvements in both mean numeric pain rating scale and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand in 10 patients with chronic biceps brachii tendinopathy. However, since the intervention variables were not analyzed separately, it is unclear whether the improvements were solely due to DN or exercise.[21]

This study is the first to compare the effects of DN and TENS or the combined nature of the two interventions for the treatment of LHBB tendinopathy. Our findings show that DN is superior to TENS in reducing BPE. Both DN and TENS groups demonstrated immediate improvement in VAS scores. While both groups showed improvement in short-term and medium-term effects on the SPADI total score, there were no statistically significant differences in between-group comparisons. Neither group demonstrated statistically significant improvements in tissue hardness of the LHBB muscle.

These findings suggest that both DN and TENS can serve as potential treatment options for providing immediate pain relief and improving overall quality of life in patients with LHBB tendinopathy. However, it’s important to note that a comprehensive treatment approach incorporating other modalities or interventions may be needed to effectively address the broader aspects of managing these patients.

Pain level and functional improved after DN and TENS treatments

Both DN and TENS groups showed a significant decrease in VAS scores at all follow-up measurement points. However, compared to TENS, DN treatment showed greater improvements in pain reduction, function, and range of motion among patients with chronic low back pain from the literature review [46]. In a single-blinded randomized control trial, it was found that neck pain intensity and post-needling soreness improved to a greater extent in patients with chronic myofascial neck pain and active MTrPs in the upper trapezius muscle who received a combination of DN and percutaneous TENS (PENS), compared to those who received DN alone [47]. The VAS is commonly used to assess health states alone or in combination with other methods. Therefore, improvements in VAS scores can be seen as enhancements in patient outcomes and quality of life [48].

Both groups showed significant differences in short-term and medium-term effects in the SPADI. Moreover, the DN group demonstrated significantly better outcomes in the pain subscale of the short-term effect and the disability subscale of the medium-term effect. A systematic review of 19 randomized control trials concluded that inserting DN into MTrPs is effective in reducing pain and tenderness in multiple body parts, including the head, trunk, upper extremities, and lower extremities [49]. Additionally, five studies showed significant changes in multiple disability indexes [49]. A prior review focus on patients experiencing shoulder pain in the glenohumeral joint, the acromioclavicular joint, the sternoclavicular joint, and/or the subacromial and scapulothoracic connections, lasting between six weeks and one year, which found that improved SPADI correlated with enhanced overall quality of life [50].

In our research, no significant findings were observed for PPT at the MTrP in the LHBB muscle at all follow-up measurement points in both groups. While previous studies only observed the effect of DN at the needle insertion site, most studies suggested no significant differences in the immediate effect of DN without combining PENS [47].

Zaeifar et al. found that DN did not show immediate improvement in PPT due to muscle soreness. The pain intensity and PPT improvement due to DN were observed after 2 days [44]. Post-needling soreness has been reported in up to 100% of cases immediately after inserting DN into latent MTrPs in the upper trapezius trigger point, but it typically resolves within 72 h [51]. This may explain the lack of significant improvement in PPT of the MTrP in the LHBB muscle after DN in our study. A significant correlation was observed between pressure hyperalgesia at various body sites (measured at the lowest PPT) and the impairment of SF-36 physical function and mental health parameters. Given the widespread use of SF-36 for assessing Health-Related Quality of Life, this finding suggests a potential link between PPT and Quality of Life [52].

We suggest that the lack of apparent improvement in PPT immediately after DN treatment in our study may be attributed to muscle soreness experienced right after the treatment. Furthermore, the absence of improvement in both short-term and long-term effects could be due to the limited number of DN treatment sessions (only three times) and the small sample size [53,54].

Ultrasonographic evaluation of the bicipital peritendinous effusion

BPE is linked to various shoulder abnormalities, and the strength of these associations intensifies with the severity of BPE. Therefore, a reduction in BPE may suggest an improvement in shoulder pathology [24]. The immediate PPT increment and BPE reduction observed in our study suggested that DN insertion to the MTrP in the biceps muscle can influence the proximal part. Baraja-Vegas L et al. [55] used magnetic resonance imaging and found that intramuscular edema significantly increased just one hour after DN treatment in the direct muscle location receiving DN, indicating a localized effect. No surrounding muscle edema was found.

The physiological mechanism of DN is not yet fully understood but can be categorized into two categories: mechanical and neurophysiological. The mechanical mechanism focuses on the disruption of dysfunctional end plates and the resulting sarcomere lengthening [56]. The neurophysiological mechanism of DN emphasizes the modulation of the biochemical environment of the MTrP in a dose-dependent manner. This includes enhancing β-endorphin levels in the target muscle and serum, reducing substance P in the target muscle and dorsal root ganglion, and activating the inhibitory pathway at the spinal cord level [57,58]. Inserting DN at a distance from the symptomatic MTrPs may activate the descending inhibitory pain pathway, leading to the relief of remote pain [59]. Hsieh et al. demonstrated that inserting DN into distant myofascial trigger points can relieve symptoms by reducing substance P levels in both proximal muscle and spinal cord [60]. An electrophysiological study focusing on the remote effect of DN found that if the afferent pathway is intact, the endplate noise of the proximally affected muscle decreased [57]. We hypothesize that the reduced BPE after DN in our research may be related to the mechanical mechanism majority. Inserting DN directly into the taut band would cause relaxation in the LHBB muscle, resulting in decreased tension and pulling force on the insertion site, leading to a reduction in BPE over the bicipital groove. In contrast, TENS would not have direct access to the taut band, and the hypothesized effect would not be as significant as in the DN group.

Tissue hardness of the LHBB muscle

To target the source of myofascial pain syndrome, we palpated and inserted DN into the taut band to alleviate discomfort. Research by Okada-Ogawa and colleagues revealed that muscles with taut bands exhibit significantly higher tissue hardness compared to healthy muscles [61]. The hand-held pressure algometer employed for tissue hardness measurement offers flexibility as it can assess not only trigger points or muscles but also subcutaneous tissue. This versatility accounts for the potential influence of long-term low-level muscle contraction, muscle and fascia thickening, and subcutaneous tissue edema on the results [61]. Furthermore, opting for a hand-held pressure algometer rather than a fixed hardness meter may introduce some variability in the measuring point but also enhance adaptability. Andersen H et al. also confirmed that the increase in tissue hardness did not correlate with increased pain sensitivity in the upper trapezius muscle [38]. They found that the least hard sites detected by pressure algometry turned out to be the lowest PPT. In summary, we still believe that tissue characteristics may change after DN treatment. However, we did not find an ideal correlation between our hypothesis, study, and the literature review. The disappointing results may be attributed to the absence of a perfect method for detecting tissue hardness at the present time. Therefore, future research is required.

Limitations of our study

First, there were statistical differences in the baseline patient characteristics of the BPE between the two groups, with the DN group having a thicker BPE. Second, our study design did not include a sham control group due to concerns about patients suffering from not receiving active treatment and a potential loss of follow-up rate in the sham control group. Therefore, we could not exclude the possibility of these effects. Third, we only followed patients for 15 days, which may not provide a complete understanding of the long-term outcomes of DN. The long-term effect of DN on trigger points remains unknown based on our literature review [45,62]. However, the improvement in VAS and SAPDI scores is clinically meaningful, as it can increase patient tolerance to other interventions within this 15-day timeframe. Fourthly, a small sample size of only 30 patients was randomized into two groups, with each group comprising only 15 patients. Fifth, we didn’t exclude patients with secondary LHBB tendinopathy, such as those with suspected or known rotator cuff or labral pathology, or other sources of subacromial shoulder pain. These conditions can contribute to secondary LHBB tendinopathy and may influence the results, as pain levels could potentially be driven by mechanisms related to labral or other subacromial shoulder pain pathology, not just LHBB tendon pathology/pain.

Currently, there are no standardized guidelines for the use of DN in specific diseases, and it is often combined with other treatments. Future research is necessary to investigate the long-term outcomes of DN and to explore different frequencies and intensities of treatment.

Conclusions

Both DN and TENS were found to be effective in reducing VAS and SPADI in the treatment of LHBB tendinopathy. Although, it would not be appropriate to conclude based on two non-homogeneous baselines. However, we intended to highlight that despite thicker biceps peritendinous effusion in the DN group, it performed better than the TENS group. In fact, only DN showed a significant reduction in BPE, likely due to the direct decrease in pulling force on the LHBB insertion site. While TENS also improved pain and functional outcomes, no reduction in BPE was observed. No improvement in tissue hardness was found in either group, which may be due to limitations in current measuring tools. DN targeting MTrPs in the LHBB muscle appears to be a promising alternative for relieving chronic pain caused by LHBB tendinopathy. Considering the limited sample size and this study’s limitations, it’s important to interpret these findings cautiously. Further exploration with larger sample sizes is essential. Nevertheless, the results of this trial present a promising area of interest.
