A comparative evaluation of large language models in diagnosis and treatment planning in restorative dentistry
Authors: Ebru İrem Teke, Ayşenur Güngör Borsöken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWith the advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), large language models (LLMs) have become an alternative source of information in dentistry. These LLMs, which can be trained on large data sets, can answer medical questions and provide references, but they can pose problems in terms of ethics and accurate information. This research aims to evaluate the accuracy of five different LLMs in diagnosis and treatment planning in the field of restorative dentistry.MethodologyThe 20 most common cases encountered in a restorative dentistry clinic were formulated into questions. The validity of the questions was assessed using the Lawshe Content Validity Index. The questions were posed to five different LLMs: ChatGPT-5, Deepseek V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini 3 Flash. Each model was asked to create a diagnosis and treatment plan for each case. The responses were evaluated by 42 restorative dentistry specialists using a Likert Scale. Additionally, the accuracy of the references provided in the responses was evaluated by the article authors. The obtained data were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, and the Dunn multiple comparison test was applied in cases where significant differences were detected.ResultsStatistically significant differences were found between the models for 15 out of 20 questions (p < 0.05). A significant difference was also found in terms of total median scores (p < 0.001), with Google Gemini 3 Flash (median:83) and ChatGPT-5 (median:81) achieving the highest scores. Reference quality was evaluated using a four-category framework. Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Google Gemini 3 Flash demonstrated the highest proportions of accurate and relevant references (85.7% and 84.2%, respectively), while DeepSeek V3.2 exhibited the highest fabrication rate (55.6%).ConclusionsBased on specialist-evaluated response quality, no model demonstrated consistent and superior performance across all clinical scenarios. LLMs appear to have potential as supplementary information resources for clinicians in restorative dentistry; however, their clinical integration, impact on patient outcomes, and real-world usability remain to be established in future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09154-0.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Large language model, Restorative dentistry

Introduction

Restorative dentistry is a discipline based on systematic approaches that requires the evaluation of numerous clinical variables in diagnosis and treatment planning. In recent years, with the acceleration of digitalization in health sciences, the use of artificial intelligence (AI)-based technologies in diagnosis and treatment processes has increased. AI is transforming the diagnosis and treatment of dental disorders thanks to its ability to process large data sets and identify complex patterns [1]. With the changing healthcare environment, the integration of AI into restorative dental procedures is no longer just an alternative, but also a necessity for advancing the sector. The growing importance of AI in the healthcare sector is indicative of this transition. Dentistry, which has relied on human knowledge until now, is increasingly using AI to improve patient care [2].

Large language models (LLMs), which can generate human-like text and are trained using large datasets, are AI systems based on multi-layered and recurrent neural network structures. LLMs have had a transformative impact on the field of AI by advancing the understanding and generation of natural language, thereby influencing various disciplines. One of the most advanced examples of LLM, ChatGPT (Chat Generative Pre-training Transformer) developed by OpenAI (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA), can generate text by gathering information from various data sources such as articles, books, journals, and open-access websites, perform cross-language translation, and answer questions using information obtained from these sources [3]. DeepSeek (DeepSeek AI, Hangzhou, China), a China-based LLM that stands out for its principles of transparency and reproducibility, has achieved success worldwide with its chatbot launched in December 2024, boasting low costs and high download rates [4]. Claude (Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA) was developed with an emphasis on safety and offers an extended context window of up to 100,000 tokens, combined with advanced reasoning skills, which makes it well-suited for handling complex applications in fields such as healthcare and education [5]. Microsoft Copilot (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) is a digital assistant that can be used in literature-based information access and clinical decision support processes with its LLM-based infrastructure [6]. Google Gemini (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) is an LLM that enables the interpretation of clinical cases and the generation of responses thanks to its advanced contextual analysis and multimodal data processing capabilities [7]. The integration of AI in dental practice is increasing, from diagnosis and treatment planning to treatment delivery. However, research examining the adequacy of LLM models in dental knowledge remains limited [8].

It has been suggested that LLMs are useful in diagnosing diseases, predicting prognosis, or developing patient-specific treatment strategies [9]. The use of AI in the treatment process is finding an increasingly growing application area in dentistry by improving diagnostic accuracy and optimizing treatment plans [10]. Traditional restorative dentistry can produce inconsistent results due to clinician variability, resource constraints, and subjective diagnosis and treatment planning. AI has the potential to reduce these issues by processing data quickly and accurately, thereby providing more reliable diagnoses and treatment plans [11].

Our aim is to compare the accuracy of the responses of five different LLMs in the diagnosis and treatment of common cases in restorative dentistry by obtaining the opinions of specialist physicians. The hypothesis of this study is that the accuracy of responses to clinical questions related to restorative dentistry shows significant differences among LLMs.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethic approval

This study was conducted to compare the accuracy levels of responses provided by AI-based chatbots (LLMs) to questions regarding the diagnosis and treatment planning of cases in the field of restorative dentistry. The study protocol was approved by the Gaziantep University Clinical Research Ethics Committee on September 24, 2025, under approval number 2025/288. Informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study, and the research process was conducted in accordance with ethical principles.

This study utilized five different LLM models: ChatGPT-5, Deepseek V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini 3 Flash. The specific versions accessed were: ChatGPT-5 (OpenAI, accessed via chat.openai.com), DeepSeek V3.2 (DeepSeek AI, accessed via chat.deepseek.com), Claude Sonnet 4.5 (Anthropic, accessed via claude.ai), Microsoft Copilot (Microsoft Corporation, accessed via copilot.microsoft.com), and Google Gemini 3 Flash (Google LLC, accessed via gemini.google.com). All models were accessed on December 25, 2025, through their free-tier web interfaces using standard user accounts. Web browsing was enabled for all models during data collection. Chat history was disabled before each session to prevent carry-over effects between queries. These LLM models were selected because they are accessible to everyone and free of charge. A total of 20 questions were specifically developed by the authors for this study, based on commonly encountered clinical scenarios in restorative dentistry. The responses were evaluated by 42 restorative dentistry specialists using a Likert scale.

Preparation of questions

The content validity of the clinical questions used in the study was assessed using the Lawshe Content Validity Ratio (CVR) method [12]. For this purpose, 10 experts experienced in the field of restorative dentistry were asked to evaluate each question as ‘essential’, ‘useful but not essential’, or ‘not essential’. According to the Lawshe method, the critical CVR value for 10 experts is accepted as 0.62. Therefore, 20 questions were selected, including questions with a content validity index value ≥ 0.62.

The clinical cases were selected based on the most frequently encountered patient presentations at the Restorative Dentistry Clinic of the Faculty of Dentistry at Gaziantep University. Case selection was further informed by restorative dentistry textbooks, specialty examination question banks, and the clinical experience of the authors. This combined approach ensured that the selected cases reflected both real-world clinical frequency and diagnostic diversity. The cases used in our study are based on actual patient cases selected to include different radiographic and clinical scenarios in order to ensure diversity. The questions were prepared in accordance with the specialty exam. The questions were asked to 5 different LLMs on December 25, 2025, and the answers were recorded. To ensure that the answers are not affected, the search history and browser cookies were cleared before each question. These models were accessed as end-users through publicly available web interfaces, reflecting real-world clinical usage conditions rather than controlled API environments. All models were accessed through their standard web-based interfaces using default settings; therefore, temperature and other generation parameters were not manually adjustable. Each question was entered as a new, independent session to prevent carry-over effects between cases. No prompt engineering techniques were applied; a single standardized prompt structure was used consistently across all models and all cases. Each case was queried only once per model, and no response regeneration was performed. All clinical case descriptions and prompts were prepared and submitted to the LLMs in English. The responses generated by the models were also obtained in English and presented to the specialist evaluators in English.

Each prompt followed a standardized three-step structure. First, the clinical case information was presented, including patient history, symptoms, and where applicable, radiographic findings or intraoral photographs. Second, the following diagnostic and treatment question was posed:

‘Could you please provide 1 diagnosis for the patient and a treatment consisting of 3 sentences for this diagnosis?’

Third, after receiving the diagnostic and treatment response, a follow-up prompt was submitted requesting supporting references from the scientific literature, without specifying a required number of references. The answers have been saved to the Word file (Supplement 1).

Method of evaluating responses

Questions and answers directed to the LLM are provided in Supplement 1. A Google survey form was created with each question and 5 LLM answers. After the relevant question was provided in the created survey form, the responses from five different LLM models (ChatGPT-5, Deepseek V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini 3 Flash) were written as response 1, response 2, response 3, response 4, and response 5. The response provided by each LLM model was evaluated by 42 restorative dentistry specialists using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = somewhat disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = somewhat agree, and 5 = strongly agree) to assess the accuracy of the response. The study was conducted blindly, meaning that the restorative dentistry specialists who participated in the study and evaluated the responses were not told which answer belonged to which LLM. This allowed the survey participants to score without bias. All participants’ responses were recorded digitally. To provide a reference point for diagnostic accuracy, the correct diagnosis for each clinical case was included in the survey form. Specialists evaluated the LLM-generated treatment recommendations against this established diagnosis using the 5-point Likert scale. This approach ensured that diagnostic correctness was anchored to an objective standard, while the appropriateness of treatment planning which may vary among clinicians, was assessed through expert judgment. To assess inter-rater reliability among the 42 specialist evaluators, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement (ICC(A, k)) for each question and for the total score.

Method of evaluating references

The references cited in each LLM’s responses were recorded. All references were searched in the PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. Reference assessments were conducted independently by both study authors, and consensus was reached through joint evaluation in cases of disagreement. References were classified into four categories: “accurate and relevant”, “partially accurate”, “misleading/irrelevant”, and “fabricated”. References classified as “accurate and relevant” were defined as sources retrievable from databases that correctly supported the relevant statement. “Partially accurate” references were defined as sources that, although retrievable, contained incomplete information or demonstrated only partial content alignment. The “misleading/irrelevant” category included references that, although present in databases, did not sufficiently support the relevant statement or were misinterpreted. References that could not be retrieved from any database were classified as “fabricated”. This approach allowed assessment of not only the presence of references, but also their relevance to the clinical context and content appropriateness (Supplement 2).

Statistical analysis

The analysis of the data obtained from the study was performed using the SPSS 22.0 statistical package program (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data that did not show a normal distribution were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR). The nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare the performance scores of five different LLM models. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. When a significant difference was found between groups, Dunn’s multiple comparison test was applied to identify the groups responsible for the difference. Dunn test results are indicated in the tables with superscript letters (a, b, c). Groups sharing the same letter were considered to have no statistically significant difference, while groups with different letters were considered to have a significant difference. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage (%) distributions. To account for the potential inflation of type I error due to multiple comparisons across 20 questions, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the Kruskal–Wallis p values. Additionally, effect sizes were calculated using epsilon-squared (ε²) to quantify the magnitude of differences between LLM groups for each question. Effect size interpretation followed conventional thresholds: ε² < 0.04 was considered small, 0.04–0.16 moderate, and > 0.16 large.

Inter-rater reliability among the 42 specialist evaluators was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(A, k)). The average measures ICC across the 20 questions ranged from 0.602 to 0.991, indicating good to excellent agreement among evaluators. The ICC for the total score was 0.878 (95% CI: 0.680–0.980), confirming strong consistency in specialist assessments. According to the results of the Kruskal-Wallis test conducted to compare the Likert scores of five different LLM models, statistically significant differences were found between the groups in 15 out of 20 questions (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4, Q5, Q6, Q9, Q12, Q13, Q14, Q15, Q16, Q17, Q18, Q20) (p < 0.05). Following Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction, all 15 questions retained statistical significance, confirming the robustness of the findings. In five questions (Q7, Q8, Q10, Q11, Q19), no significant difference was found between the LLM groups (p > 0.05). All models were similar in these questions and generally received high median scores (ranging from 3.95 to 4.50 on average). Effect sizes calculated using epsilon-squared (ε²) ranged from negligible to large across questions, with Q9 (ε²=0.554) and Q20 (ε²=0.601) demonstrating the largest effects, indicating substantial performance differences between models in these clinical scenarios (Table 1).

Evaluation of LLMs

Post-hoc Dunn multiple comparison analyses revealed that the performance of LLMs varied depending on the question.

ChatGPT-5 achieved high median scores particularly on questions Q3, Q4, Q9, Q13, Q14, Q15, Q17, Q18, and Q20. Furthermore, ChatGPT-5 and Google Gemini 3 Flash’s median scores on questions Q9, Q18, and Q20 were found to be statistically significantly higher than the other three models (p < 0.001). In contrast, ChatGPT-5’s scores on questions Q1, Q6, and Q12 were found to be lower than those of the other models (p < 0.05).

Deepseek-V3.2 achieved high median scores on questions Q14, Q16, and Q17 (p < 0.05). In contrast, Deepseek-V3.2’s median scores on questions Q2, Q9, Q15, Q18, and Q20 were found to be significantly lower than those of other models (p < 0.05).

Claude Sonnet 4.5 scored high median scores, particularly on questions Q1, Q2, Q4, and Q14, and this was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). In contrast, the low median scores of Claude Sonnet 4.5 on questions Q9, Q15, Q18, and Q20 were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Microsoft Copilot achieved high median scores on questions Q5 and Q12 (p < 0.001). In contrast, it achieved low median scores on questions Q6, Q14, Q15, Q18, and Q20 (p < 0.05).

Google Gemini 3 Flash has achieved high evaluation scores on many questions. Google Gemini 3 Flash achieved high median scores on questions Q6, Q9, Q12, Q13, Q14, Q17, Q18, and Q20 (p < 0.05). In contrast, Google Gemini 3 Flash achieved low median scores on questions Q2 and Q15 (p < 0.05).

Evaluation of LLM references

Claude Sonnet 4.5 demonstrated the highest proportion of accurate and relevant references (18/21, 85.7%), followed closely by Google Gemini 3 Flash (16/19, 84.2%) and ChatGPT-5 (15/22, 68.2%). Microsoft Copilot provided 6 accurate and relevant references out of 10 (60.0%), while DeepSeek V3.2 showed the lowest proportion (3/9, 33.3%).

Regarding fabricated references, DeepSeek V3.2 exhibited the highest fabrication rate (5/9, 55.6%), whereas Microsoft Copilot and Google Gemini 3 Flash produced no fabricated references. ChatGPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 each produced one fabricated reference (4.5% and 4.8%, respectively).

Partially accurate references were most common in Microsoft Copilot (3/10, 30.0%), followed by ChatGPT-5 (3/22, 13.6%) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (2/21, 9.5%). Misleading or irrelevant references were identified in Google Gemini 3 Flash (2/19, 10.5%), Microsoft Copilot (1/10, 10.0%), and ChatGPT-5 (1/22, 4.5%), while none were found in DeepSeek V3.2 or Claude Sonnet 4.5.

Overall, across all 81 references evaluated, 58 (71.6%) were classified as accurate and relevant, 10 (12.3%) as partially accurate, 4 (4.9%) as misleading or irrelevant, and 7 (8.6%) as fabricated.

Discussion

This study aimed to comparatively evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning performance of five different LLM systems in the field of restorative dentistry, based on Likert scale scores given by specialist dentists. The hypothesis of the study was that the accuracy of responses to clinical questions related to restorative dentistry would show statistically significant differences among LLMs. The findings support this hypothesis; statistically significant differences were detected between models in 15 of the 20 clinical problems. These results reveal that LLM performance in restorative dentistry varies depending on the model and clinical case.

The integration of AI-based tools into dental practice has accelerated in recent years, with LLMs increasingly being explored as potential aids for clinical decision-making [1, 13, 14]. Although dentists rely primarily on their education and clinical experience for diagnosis and treatment planning, LLMs have been proposed as supplementary tools that may reduce uncertainty in complex cases and support evidence-based decision-making [9, 15, 16]. The reason for comparing ChatGPT-5, DeepSeek V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini 3 Flash stems from their widespread use, accessibility, and established performance in various text generation tasks. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to simultaneously evaluate five different recently released LLM models in the specific context of restorative dentistry diagnosis and treatment planning, using a blinded specialist evaluation design and reference accuracy analysis.

Comparative LLM studies across dental specialties consistently report model-dependent variability in clinical performance, a pattern strongly reflected in our findings [17]. In the field of ophthalmology, ChatGPT-3.5 outperformed Google Gemini and Microsoft Copilot in neuro-ophthalmology questions, yet all models showed considerable error rates in case-based reasoning [18]. In periodontology, Chatzopoulos et al. reported that ChatGPT-4 provided more clinically appropriate responses than its competitors, though all models occasionally produced incomplete or misleading information [6]. In prosthodontics and restorative dentistry, Chau et al. demonstrated that GPT-4.0 outperformed Claude-2 and Llama-2 in clinical reasoning tasks [19]. Similarly, Yılmaz et al. evaluated ChatGPT-4o, Claude 4, and Gemini 2.5 in oral and maxillofacial diagnostic reasoning and found that Gemini achieved the highest mean diagnostic score, consistent with our finding that Google Gemini 3 Flash demonstrated strong performance across restorative dentistry scenarios [20]. In our study, statistically significant differences were found between models in 15 out of 20 clinical cases, which is consistent with these findings across specialties. Notably, ChatGPT-5 and Google Gemini 3 Flash received significantly higher Likert scores than other LLMs across restorative dentistry scenarios, demonstrating that specialist-perceived response quality is sensitive to both the model version and the nature of the clinical scenario.

The total median scores further illustrate these performance differences, with Google Gemini 3 Flash (median: 83) and ChatGPT-5 (median: 81) receiving significantly higher specialist ratings than DeepSeek V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and Microsoft Copilot (p < 0.001; ε²=0.211), indicating a large overall effect. These differences are clinically meaningful in the context of restorative dentistry, where treatment decisions such as choosing between direct and indirect restoration, assessing pulp vitality, or managing non-carious cervical lesions require precise and evidence-based reasoning. The fact that DeepSeek V3.2 and Microsoft Copilot received lower specialist ratings particularly in complex restorative scenarios suggests that these models may have limited training exposure to specialty-specific dental content, making them less suitable as supplementary information resources in restorative dental practice at this stage [6, 18, 19, 21, 22].

Notably, no statistically significant differences were found between models in five questions (Q7, Q8, Q10, Q11, Q19), all of which involved relatively straightforward clinical scenarios such as dentin hypersensitivity, molar incisor hypomineralisation, and anterior diastema. In these cases, all five LLMs received similarly high median scores, suggesting that when clinical scenarios have well-established diagnostic criteria and standard treatment algorithms, LLMs converge on a common evidence base and produce consistently appropriate responses. This finding is consistent with Qu et al., who reported that LLMs performed well in structured otorhinolaryngology scenarios with clear diagnostic pathways, but exhibited significant limitations in complex case-based reasoning [17]. In restorative dentistry, this implies that LLMs may be more reliable as supplementary resources in routine clinical scenarios than in complex or atypical presentations. Additionally, their consistent performance in straightforward scenarios suggests potential utility as educational support tools in undergraduate dental training, where they could assist students in case-based reasoning and self-directed learning. This is supported by Wójcik et al., who demonstrated that ChatGPT-4, Gemini, and Claude achieved passing scores on Polish dental licensing examinations, with Claude showing the highest overall accuracy, further suggesting that LLMs may serve as valuable supplementary resources in dental education [23].

The strong performance of ChatGPT-5 across multiple restorative dentistry scenarios is consistent with the broader trend of progressive improvement observed across successive ChatGPT versions. Othman et al. reported that GPT-4 produced more comprehensive dental responses than GPT-3.5, yet cautioned against its use as a standalone clinical decision-making tool [21]. Özbay et al. similarly found that GPT-4 achieved the highest accuracy in endodontics, while all models retained the capacity to generate incorrect or incomplete information [22]. In our study, ChatGPT-5 maintained high and consistent Likert scores across the majority of questions, suggesting that newer model generations demonstrate improved capacity for specialty-specific clinical reasoning. However, the variability observed across questions confirms that even the highest-performing models are not uniformly reliable across all restorative dentistry presentations.

The relatively poor performance of DeepSeek V3.2 in our study warrants specific discussion. While Kaygısız et al. reported that DeepSeek-V3 demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy than ChatGPT-4o in oral pathology cases [24], our findings suggest that this advantage does not extend to restorative dentistry. DeepSeek V3.2 received the lowest overall median scores and exhibited the highest fabricated reference rate (55.6%), raising serious concerns about its reliability for evidence-based clinical use in restorative dental practice. This discrepancy across studies highlights that LLM performance is highly domain-specific and cannot be generalized across dental specialties based on results from a single clinical field.

Kandemir and Sarıbaş reported that LLM performance was lower in clinical application questions compared to theoretical questions in periodontology specialty examinations [25]. This pattern is directly relevant to our findings. Scenarios requiring nuanced clinical judgment such as distinguishing between abrasion and abfraction, selecting appropriate restoration materials for non-carious cervical lesions, or formulating multi-step treatment plans for complex esthetic cases yielded greater variability between models and lower scores for DeepSeek V3.2 and Microsoft Copilot. This suggests that while LLMs may perform adequately on knowledge-based questions with clear correct answers, their capacity for contextual clinical reasoning specific to restorative dentistry remains limited. Collectively, the findings of the present study suggest that specialist-perceived response quality varies substantially across LLM models and clinical scenarios in restorative dentistry. The large effect sizes observed in questions such as Q9 and Q20 indicate that performance differences between models are not merely statistical but potentially clinically meaningful. These results highlight the importance of model selection based on the specific clinical context and support the use of multi-model approaches in future research.

Biases such as race, occupation, and country present in training data may affect the accuracy of ChatGPT’s recommendations for different groups of people [26]. An example of this bias is a study in which ChatGPT recommended a less effective analgesic treatment for chest pain to a Black patient compared to a white patient [27]. LLMs can generate incorrect information based on patterns learned during training based on user input. This type of incorrect information generation is referred to as ‘artificial hallucinations’ [28]. Moreover, the references for the information provided may also be fake [29]. A study examining the accuracy of references provided by ChatGPT-3 revealed that 219 of the 343 references provided by the model were actually generated by the model itself. Despite the references being fake, the authors of the article stated that the vast majority of the responses were correct [30]. Another study found that only 12 of ChatGPT-4o’s 62 references and 40 of DeepSeek-V3’s 48 references were based on actual scientific sources [24]. In the present study, reference quality was evaluated using a four-category framework assessing not only the existence of references in databases but also their clinical relevance and content appropriateness. DeepSeek V3.2 exhibited the highest fabrication rate (55.6%), confirming its limited reliability for evidence-based clinical use. Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Google Gemini 3 Flash demonstrated the highest proportions of accurate and relevant references (85.7% and 84.2%, respectively), suggesting stronger alignment between cited sources and the clinical content of their responses. ChatGPT-5 performed well overall (68.2% accurate and relevant) and achieved high total median scores, indicating consistency between clinical response quality and reference reliability. Microsoft Copilot produced no fabricated references; however, 30% of its references were only partially accurate and one was misleading or irrelevant, which may explain its comparatively lower clinical performance scores despite an apparently adequate reference base. These findings underscore that the mere existence of a reference in a database does not guarantee its clinical relevance or content appropriateness, and that a more nuanced evaluation framework is necessary when assessing LLM reference quality in clinical contexts. Training LLM models using objective and scientific databases is thought to reduce artificial hallucinations and biases, enabling them to generate more reliable responses. The question of whether AI can completely replace doctors’ diagnoses and assessments has yet to find a clear answer. In this context, it can be argued that a hybrid system in which AI and medical experts work together could deliver more effective and reliable results in diagnostic processes. This approach can provide a more comprehensive diagnosis by combining the strengths of both sides [31].

Limitations

LLMs have the potential to generate responses that appear logical but are not actually based on a correct foundation. The information provided by these models may offer answers that align with popular beliefs and emotions rather than evidence-based information. This situation aligns with some models in our study receiving low accuracy scores on certain clinical questions and supports the risk that LLMs can generate responses that appear logical but lack evidence. In our study, questions were asked to LLM models at a single point in time; however, some studies indicate that ChatGPT may generate different responses when the same question is asked at different times [32]. This variability can reduce the reliability and consistency of the information provided and create confusion for users seeking accurate and reliable answers. Although the use of free versions of LLM models is beneficial in terms of accessibility, it constitutes one of the limitations of our study in terms of information presentation. There may be updates to the LLMs we use after our study. Model updates occurring in LLM’s may affect the results of subsequent work. The use of LLMs in clinical dentistry raises important ethical and legal considerations that extend beyond data privacy and copyright concerns. From a medico-legal perspective, the accountability for AI-generated clinical errors remains unclear; when an LLM provides an incorrect diagnosis or inappropriate treatment recommendation, responsibility cannot be attributed to the model itself, placing the burden of oversight entirely on the clinician. This creates a significant patient safety risk, particularly if LLM outputs are accepted uncritically in time-pressured clinical environments. Furthermore, data governance presents a growing concern, as patient information entered into publicly accessible LLM interfaces may be processed by third-party servers without adequate safeguards, potentially violating patient confidentiality. These ethical dimensions underscore the importance of developing clear regulatory frameworks and professional guidelines for the responsible integration of LLMs into dental practice. As Paradowska-Stolarz highlighted, despite the promising potential of AI in supporting dental diagnosis and treatment planning, significant challenges related to clinical reliability, ethical transparency, and data governance must be addressed before these tools can be widely adopted in everyday practice [33].

Conducting the study in a blinded design, deriving questions from diverse clinical cases, simultaneously evaluating five separate LLMs, and involving a high number of expert physicians in the evaluation process enhance the reliability of the findings. These factors constitute the strengths of our study. Additionally, the inclusion of cases from a single institutional setting may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader clinical contexts. Furthermore, although the correct diagnosis was provided as a reference standard in the survey, a structured multi-dimensional scoring rubric was not employed for the evaluation of treatment recommendations. Future studies may benefit from the development and validation of explicit scoring criteria to further reduce evaluator subjectivity.

Conclusion

LLMs show promising potential in generating answers to clinical questions related to diagnosis and treatment planning in restorative dentistry. Although Google Gemini 3 Flash and ChatGPT-5 provide more accurate answers to questions about restorative dental treatment, no model has demonstrated consistent and superior performance in all clinical scenarios. Therefore, based on specialist-evaluated response quality under standardized clinical prompting conditions, LLMs appear to have potential as supplementary information resources for clinicians in restorative dentistry, although their clinical integration, impact on patient outcomes, and real-world usability remain to be established in future research. Future studies should evaluate the clinical applicability of these models more comprehensively through the use of multimodal data and integration into real clinical settings. Additionally, incorporating human clinician control groups in future research would provide a valuable benchmark for contextualizing LLM performance against expert dental practice.
