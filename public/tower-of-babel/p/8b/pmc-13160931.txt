Functional connectome metrics reveal distinct prognostic subtypes in two Phase 3 gantenerumab trials
Authors: Susanna Gobbi, Erica Silvestri, Matteo Tonietto, Gregory Klein, Martijn van den Heuvel, Stefano Magon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease (AD) heterogeneity poses significant challenges for drug development, identification of individuals at risk, and treatment response prediction. Scientists have leveraged graph theory and resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fMRI) to successfully stratify people with AD. Still, the prognostic value of rs‐fMRI graph metrics in AD clinical trials remains unclear.METHODSWe analyzed rs‐fMRI from participants in amyloid‐lowering clinical trials. Four graph metrics—global efficiency, clustering coefficient, modularity, and shortest path length—were computed and baseline clusters defined using unsupervised k‑means. We investigated the baseline connectome of each cluster to assess the level of network dysfunction and impairment (i.e., loss of global integration, resulting in disrupted communication between brain regions and reduced global efficiency). These clusters were related to a 116‐week change in cognition and brain volume using covariate‐adjusted mixed‐effects models.RESULTSThree clusters emerged with distinct functional connectome efficiency, demographic, and AD‐related biomarkers profiles. These baseline differences led to significant variations in disease progression. The most impaired‑connectome cluster declined fastest, whereas the most integrated declined slowest.DISCUSSIONrs‐fMRI graph metrics might effectively stratify participants with AD in clinical trials and serve as potential prognostic biomarkers.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD), one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative diseases, is characterized by extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular tau neurofilaments. 1 , 2 These cause neural/synapse loss, chronic inflammation, and cognitive impairment, disrupting brain function. 3 , 4

Network science and graph theory, applied to neuroimaging, have been used to investigate the complexity of the structural and functional brain architecture (connectome) in both normal and pathological conditions. This graph‐based approach represents the brain as a combination of nodes (brain regions) and edges (structural or functional connectivity between brain regions). This representation can reveal the fundamental features of brain architecture such as modularity, that is, the degree to which the network is organized into distinct communities with dense intra‐group connections and sparse inter‐group connections. 5 Although in a healthy brain those features are optimized for efficient communication and minimal metabolic cost, the AD connectome shows disruptions in functional brain network organization 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 and reduced modularity, which impair information segregation and integration. 6 , 7 These alterations co‐localize with Aβ deposition in the frontoparietal network and tau deposition in the limbic/anterior temporal areas. 4 This supports a bidirectional view: the connectome is a vulnerable scaffold that shapes pathology accumulation and propagation while being progressively impaired by the disease.

Hence, the connectome signatures might be seen as measures of biological vulnerability or functional reserve. High‐reserve phenotypes may maintain network integration despite significant pathology, whereas vulnerable phenotypes exhibit rapid degradation of communication hubs. 8 Identifying these distinct AD phenotypes is a significant challenge for precise diagnosis and targeted clinical interventions, 8 , 9 , 10 as heterogeneity often masks treatment signals and necessitates larger sample sizes to detect efficacy. 11 , 12 To improve clinical trials and facilitate the move toward personalized care, effective stratification methods are essential for tracking disease progression, enabling earlier diagnosis, and accelerating intervention. 13 Recent long‐term extension data of donanemab and lecanemab treatments further underscore this need, showing that clinical benefits might be most sustained in early‐start (treatment) or low‐pathology (tau or amyloid) subgroups. 14 , 15 Although graph‐based metrics have proven effective in identifying distinct pathological subgroups and high‐risk cohorts in AD, 10 , 16 , 17 , 18 offering insights into disease progression and individual stratification in observational studies, 19 their benefits in clinical trials remain underexplored.

Herein, we combined graph theory metrics and functional magnetic resonance imaging(fMRI) to characterize the AD population of a unique dataset obtained from a large cohort of participants recruited in the gantenerumab Phase 3 clinical trials GRADUATE I and GRADUATE II. Although the gantenerumab program was terminated because of negative readout, it still offered the unique opportunity to investigate the role of resting‐state fMRI (rs‐fMRI) in clinical trials. This dataset encompasses over 1000 well‐phenotyped participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD or mild AD dementia, for whom MRI, fMRI, demographic, clinical, and fluid biomarker data were collected. We tested if baseline connectome metrics could be used as prognostic/predictive biomarkers. Specifically, if they can identify clusters of individuals with distinct AD pathology traits that lead to different cognitive decline, brain volume loss, and treatment response.

METHODS

Study design

We analyze rs‐fMRI data from a subgroup of participants of the Phase 3 clinical trials GRADUATE I and II (NCT03444870 and NCT03443973). The study design is explained in detail in Bateman et al. 20 and reported here briefly. GRADUATE I and II are two identical global Phase 3, multicenter, randomized, double‐blind, placebo controlled, parallel‐group trials designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of gantenerumab in participants with early AD. A total of 1965 participants were recruited with age between 50 and 90 years and evidence of MCI or mild dementia due to AD. The study lasted 116 weeks, with MRI acquired at screening, Week 48, Week 104, and 116. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers were acquired at screening and at Week 116, whereas plasma biomarkers and clinical scores assessments were collected across several time‐points during the entire study (screening/baseline and Weeks 24, 52, 104, and 116). The fluid biomarkers were measured using the Elecsys NeuroToolKit 21 (details in Supplementary Methods and Bittner et al. 22 ). As scores, Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB, primary outcome; higher scores = greater cognitive impairment), Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE; lower scores = greater impairment), 13‐item cognitive subscale of the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale (ADAS‐Cog13; higher scores = greater impairment), and Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study–Activities of Daily Living Inventory (ADCS‐ADL; lower scores = impairment) were selected (see Table S1). A total of 1240 participants were enrolled for the functional MRI substudy.

Data acquisition

Resting‐state fMRI (rs‐fMRI) and MRI data collection protocols

The rs‐fMRI and structural MRI (T1‐weighted [T1w]) images were collected across 189 different study sites for a total of 192 scanners. The scanning sequences were harmonized by a centralized imaging vendor. Different scanner manufacturers were used depending on the site (GE, Philips, Siemens), with scanner magnetic field strength of 1.5T or 3T (Table S2). The sequences and their parameters were adjusted and harmonized accordingly based on both the scanner manufacture and the magnetic field (Tables S3,S4) such that the acquisition time was between 5 and 10 min depending on the scanner/acceleration for the T1w image and exact 10 min for the resting state (voxel resolution 1.2×1.2×1.2 mm and 3.5×3.5×3.5 mm, respectively). During the resting state image acquisition, the participants were instructed to keep their eyes open and to try not to concentrate on any one thought.

Comparability across sites and scanner types for rs‐fMRI acquisition was ensured through the implementation of highly standardized, scanner‐specific protocols, including an ADNI‐like T1w sequence. Crucially, each rs‐fMRI scan required mandatory B0‐mapping acquisition to facilitate the correction of geometric distortions. Furthermore, consistency was maintained by requiring each subject to be scanned using the same coil throughout the study, often with mandatory use of scanner‐specific intensity uniformity filters.

MRI and rs‐fMRI preprocessing

Both T1w images and rs‐fMRI data were quality checked using the MRIQC toolbox 23 combined with visual reading. T1w images with acquisition artifacts (e.g., high ghost to signal ratio), high motion and low signal to noise ratio (SNR) were excluded. In addition, visits were removed if a scanner change happened, that is, magnetic field, scanner maker, model, or protocol differ between two consecutive visits. From these curated T1w images brain volume change was computed with the Jacobian integration method, 24 a voxel‐wise measure. Specifically, the Jacobian determinants of deformation fields obtained through nonlinear registration of MRI scans across the different visits were calculated and then integrated over regions of interest (ROIs) to assess volume changes. Among the several regions included two representative ones were selected, the whole cortex and the Mayo signature 25 (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, angular gyrus, and inferior frontal gyrus).

Systematic review: Prior research has shown that network‐based metrics derived from functional MRI (fMRI) can be used to stratify people with Alzheimer's disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in observational cohorts. However, evidence on their benefit and prognostic utility in clinical trials remains limited. Interpretation: In a large amyloid‐targeting clinical trial cohort, we identified three stable baseline clusters using k‐means on four resting‐state fMRI (rs‐fMRI) graph metrics. Clusters differed in brain network efficiency, reporting distinct amyloid/tau/neurodegeneration profiles and leading to different pathology progression. These findings show that functional network‐based metrics can be used as prognostic biomarkers in AD clinical trials. Future directions: Our findings emphasize the potential of network‐based approaches to stratify people with AD in clinical trials, improving the tracking of disease progression and facilitating the implementation of targeted treatments. These results should be validated in independent cohorts, exploring the utility of these stratification methods in predicting treatment response.

After removing dummies (or non‐steady‐state images), the quality control (QC) rs‐fMRI images were preprocessed using the toolbox fMRIPrep 20.2.4, 26 and images exclusion criteria were implemented at different steps to remove poor temporal Signal to Noise Ratio (tSNR) images and spot motion outliers (see Figure 1, details in Supplementary Methods).

Schematic of the resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fMRI) quality control (QC) and preprocess pipeline. In this figure a summary of the QC and the preprocess steps for the rs‐fMRI data is reported.

Connectome formation and graph metrics extraction

Functional connectomes were generated for each participant by post‐processing the fMRIPrep outputs with the eXtensible Connectivity Pipeline‐DCAN (XCP‐D). 27 Pairwise functional connectivity was calculated using Pearson's correlation between all regions defined by the Schaefer Supplemented with Subcortical Structures (4S) atlas (details in Supplementary Methods). The Brain Connectivity Toolbox 28 was then used to extract from these functional connectomes different graph metrics. Specifically, for each participant, a sparse functional connectivity matrix was created using a 20% proportional threshold, that is, the top 20% of the highest connectivity values are included in the sparse matrix. 29 In addition, other thresholds (0.15, 0.25, and 0.3) were tested and the obtained participants' average connectome matrices compared to check for potential bias in the connection selection due to the threshold choice (Figure S1). For each participant, from the sparse weighted connectivity matrix the global efficiency, 28 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 the clustering coefficient, 34 the shortest path length, and the Louvain modularity 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 were calculated (details in Supplementary Methods). These four graph metrics describe both global and small‐world network features useful to investigate whole brain functional behavior and efficiency often impaired in AD, 4 namely they are helpful to detect issues in the whole brain communication flow as well as unbalances in integration and segregation of information between brain regions.

Cross‐sectional analyses

To stratify the population at baseline, a clustering analysis was performed using R version 4.3.2 and Rstudio. 40 , 41 As input variables, the four graph metrics extracted from the entire dataset at baseline were used and no prior information on the participants was added.

The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the majority rule after computing 23 different clustering indices (e.g., silhouette, marriott, duda, cindex) using the package NbClust v3.0.1. 42 Then a k‐means clustering algorithm was applied to partition the data into the clusters. To assess the robustness and stability of the cluster analysis, an internal validation was conducted (details in Supplementary Methods). After k‐means partitioning, we compared demographic, clinical, and fluid biomarkers across individuals to characterize the clusters at the clinical level and check for differences. For overall cluster comparison, the p‐values were computed using Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test, Pearson's chi‐square, test or Fisher's exact test, depending on the variable type (continuous or discrete). For comparing graph metrics and imaging/fluid biomarkers between pairs of clusters, the non‐parametric Wilcoxon test was used. To control for potential biases, the results on the graph metrics (global efficiency, clustering coefficient, shortest path length, and modularity) comparisons were confirmed using the marginal means, that is, means adjusted for age, sex, and magnetic field strength. Pairwise T‐tests were performed between these marginal means and the p‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) method. In addition, Pearson's correlations between fluid biomarkers and graph metrics were calculated. Furthermore, Pearson's correlations between fluid biomarkers and graph metrics were calculated. Due to the exploratory nature of these fluid‐association analyses, both uncorrected and false discovery rate (FDR)–corrected p‐values were reported. The correction for multiple comparisons was performed within the cluster.

Longitudinal analyses

Longitudinally, two analyses were performed to assess differences among clusters in (1) disease progression and (2) treatment response. The first analysis was restricted to the placebo arm and disease progression was defined as worsening cognitive performance and increasing brain volume loss. In the second analysis, both the placebo and gantenerumab arms were included and treatment response was examined looking at differences in CDR‐SB trajectories between arms across the three clusters.

Disease progression

To investigate longitudinal changes, a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) was implemented using the R package mmrm. 43 We included cluster, visit, and their interaction term as fixed effects and sex, age, and base (value of the dependent variable at baseline) as covariates (details in Supplementary Methods). In addition, a type III analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Satterthwaite's approximation was employed to evaluate the overall significance of the interaction term Cluster*Visit. Whenever the interaction effect was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons between clusters at each visit were performed using estimated marginal means (EMMs; R package emmeans 44 ). These comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Tukey HSD method to control the family‐wise error rate (p < 0.05). Models for each of the four cognitive scores available in the study, CDR‐SB, MMSE, ADAS‐Cog13, and ADCS‐ADL, and the brain volume change were created.

Treatment response

For the treatment analysis (including both placebo and gantenerumab arms), a similar MMRM was implemented with CDR‐SB change from baseline as the dependent variable, maintaining the same covariates but including a three‐way interaction term (Cluster*Visit*Arm). Type III ANOVA was used to test the interaction term, and whenever significant, post hoc comparisons between treatment arms at each visit were performed using estimated marginal means. No outcome imputation was performed but all available data under a missing‐at‐random (MAR) assumption were included.

Results

We characterized the AD population of the GRADUATE I and II clinical trials at baseline to explore the possibility of using graph metrics to stratify participants in AD trials. Furthermore, with a longitudinal analysis in the placebo subgroup, we investigated whether the different baseline clusters show differences in disease progression.

Population

Of 1240 participants enrolled at baseline in the functional MRI substudy, 1096 (560 and 536 for GRADUATE I and II, respectively) were included in the analysis. In the QC and preprocessed steps, around 11% of the images were removed because of artifacts, excessive movements, or brain mask segmentation failure. The participants included in the study had a mean age (± SD) of 70.6 ± 7.8 years, 602 were female (55%), and 443 (41%) were affected by mild AD. Main demographics and clinical characteristics are reported in Table S1. For the longitudinal analysis, only the placebo arm of the functional MRI substudy was included with a total of 526 participants.

Cross‐sectional analyses

Cluster identification

At baseline, three different clusters were identified applying the k‐mean partitioning to the four input graph metrics (global efficiency, cluster coefficient, modularity, shortest path length) extracted for each participant from the Schaefer 4S connectome. The clusters included 189, 431, and 476 participants, respectively. Of interest, these clusters differed in terms of overall global brain functional connectivity, with no metrics prevailing in the partitioning (Figure 2B); namely participants in Cluster 1 showed lower cluster coefficient (median: 0.20, interquartile range [IQR]: 0.05) compared to the ones in Clusters 2 (median: 0.33, IQR: 0.05) and 3 (median: 0.27, IQR: 0.05) as well as global efficiency (median: 0.19, IQR: 0.03 vs Cluster 2 median: 0.27, IQR: 0.04 and Cluster 3 median: 0.23, IQR = 0.03) and higher shortest path length (median: 5.36, IQR: 0.50; median: 3.72, IQR: 0.45; median: 4.46, IQR: 0.42, respectively) indicating a global disruption of small‐world features and brain efficiency. Conversely, participants in Cluster 2 presented a more intact connectome functionality with the highest global efficiency value. Compared to these two clusters, participants in Cluster 3 showed in‐between values for cluster coefficient, global efficiency, and shortest path length but overall the highest modularity (i.e., network segregation) (median: 0.34, IQR: 0.08 vs Cluster 1 median: 0.31, IQR: 0.09 and Cluster 2 median: 0.31, IQR: 0.07). On average, the connectome of Cluster 1 showed the most reduced connectivity both within and between networks among the three clusters (Figure 3A, Figure S2). Moreover, the three clusters had significant differences in functional connectivity in the seven main networks of the Schaefer (Visual, Default Mode, Limbic, Salience Ventral Attention, Dorsal Attention, SomatoMotor, and Executive Control) and in the subcortical network (adjusted p < 0.0001, Figure 3B). No differences were found for the cerebellum and the thalamus networks.

The cluster structure demonstrated robustness and reproducibility, as indicated by a qualitative assessment (Figure S3). The identified cluster centroids were highly stable (Table S5), and strong agreement was observed between the cluster assignments in the test sets and the reference assignments derived from the full dataset (see Supplementary Results).

Clusters phenotyping

Participants across the three clusters obtained using only functional connectivity information showed differences at baseline in terms of clinical and demographic characteristics as well as pathology hallmarks. In Cluster 2 we found a significant age difference, with more elderly people compared to both Clusters 1 and 3 (age mean: 71.9 ± 7.5 years; p < 0.001; Table S6, Figure S4A) and individuals with higher body mass index (BMI mean: 25.7 ± 4.2; p = 0.006; Table S6, Figure S4B). Moreover, the percentage representation of North America and East Asia countries in the populations was similar in Clusters 1 and 3, whereas in Cluster 2 there was the highest percentage of North Americans and the lowest of East Asians across the three clusters (29% and 13%, respectively; p < 0.001; Figure S4D). In terms of cognitive and functional performance, individuals in Cluster 2 showed high CDR‐SB scores, highlighting greater impairment compared to those in Cluster 1 (mean CDR‐SB Cluster 2: 3.7 ± 1.6 vs mean CDR‐SB Cluster 1: 3.3 ± 1.5; p = 0.005; Figure S4C). However, no statistically significant differences were found between Clusters 2 and 3 or Clusters 1 and 3. We did not find any difference in sex, education, AD disease stage (MCI due to AD and mild AD dementia), or apolipoprotein E (APOE) e4 allele/carrier status. In addition, the overall Aβ load (quantified as Centiloid [CL] value) was comparable across clusters (Table S6).

Regarding the AD pathology characteristics extracted from the fluid biomarkers, we detected a significant distinction between Cluster 1 and 2, with individuals in the latter reporting higher presence of amyloid pathology but lower tau pathology and fewer signs of neurodegeneration. Indeed, compared to Cluster 1, in Cluster 2, concentrations of Aβ 42 and 40 were higher in plasma (Figure 4B and Figure S5), whereas phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181), total tau, neurofilaments, and neurogranin in CSF were the lowest among the two clusters (Figure 4A,B). Still, the CSF ratios Aβ42/Aβ40 and p‐tau181/Aβ42 were not significantly different between the three clusters (Figure 4A). Furthermore, the relation between the main fluid biomarkers and graph metrics depended on the cluster type (Figure 5A). In Cluster 1, where individuals had higher levels of CSF total tau and CSF neurogranin, a significant positive relation was found between these biomarkers and the network modularity (Pearson's coefficient R CSF Neurogranin: 0.23, p‐uncorrected = 0.0023 and Pearson's coefficient R CSF total tau: 0.33, p‐uncorrected = 0.025, respectively; Figure 5B). Moreover, a positive correlation was reported in Cluster 1 between p‐tau181 in plasma and modularity (Pearson's coefficient R: 0.17, p‐uncorrected = 0.02) and cluster coefficient (Pearson's coefficient R: 0.23, p‐uncorrected = 0.002). Higher values in both of these graph metrics indicate an increase in brain network segregation that could potentially impair global integration, contributing to cognitive decline. This positive correlation with plasma p‐tau181 and CSF total tau was not found in Cluster 2 or 3. Still, a positive correlation was found between CSF neurogranin and modularity in Cluster 3 (Pearson's coefficient R: 0.23, p‐uncorrected = 0.02). Longitudinal analyses

Disease progression

The longitudinal analysis in the placebo group (N cluster 1 = 81, N cluster 2 = 208, N cluster 3 = 237, Table S10) revealed statistically significant differences in disease progression among the three clusters. Specifically, a significant overall interaction effect (cluster type/visit) was found for MMSE (F = 2.934, p = 0.008), ADAS‐Cog13 (F = 3.516, p = 0.002), and brain volume changes in both whole cortex (F = 3.47, p = 0.002) and Mayo regions (F = 4.62, p = 0.0001). These findings suggest different time trajectories in cognitive decline and brain volume loss, worsening depending on the baseline cluster (details on the MMRM results in Tables S11–S13).

The pairwise analysis helped to further highlight these differences between clusters by looking at each visit, namely significantly lower MMSE scores were reported at Week 116 in Cluster 1 with respect to Clusters 2 and 3 (estimate cluster1‐cluster2 at w116: −1.43 ± 0.49, p = 0.01; estimate cluster1‐cluster3 at w116: −1.45 ± 0.48, p = 0.007). In addition, the ADAS‐Cog13 scores were statistically higher in Cluster 1 in comparison to Clusters 2 and 3 (estimate cluster1‐cluster2 at w116: 4.27 ± 0.94, p < 0.0001; estimate cluster1‐cluster3 at w116: 3.51 ± 0.91, p = 0.0003). These findings suggested a worsening in cognitive performance in individuals in Cluster 1 compared to Clusters 2 and 3 (Figure 6B).

Furthermore, at both Week 104 and 116, Cluster 2 had significantly lower brain volume loss in the Mayo regions compared to Cluster 1 (estimate cluster1‐cluster2 at w104: −0.67 ± 0.26, p = 0.03; estimate cluster1‐cluster2 at w116: −0.84 ± 0.26, p = 0.004) and Cluster 3 (estimate cluster2‐cluster3 at w104: 0.52 ± 0.19, p = 0.02; estimate cluster2‐cluster3 at w116: 0.49 ± 0.19, p = 0.03). No statistically significant differences were observed between Clusters 1 and 3 or in any other visit. In the whole cortex, similar differences in brain volume loss were found between Clusters 1 and 2 at both Weeks 104 and 116 (estimate cluster1‐cluster2 at w104: −0.43 ± 0.18, p = 0.04; estimate cluster1‐cluster2 at w116: −0.62 ± 0.18, p = 0.001), whereas other comparisons did not reach statistical significance (Figure 6A).

Treatment response

Regarding the treatment analysis, the MMRM showed a significant three‐way interaction effect between Cluster, Arm, and Visit (F = 2.26, p = 0.036). Cluster 1 reported a significant difference in the trajectory of the CDR‐SB between the placebo and the gantenerumab arm, whereas no difference was found for Clusters 2 and 3. Moreover, in Cluster 1, lower scores (less cognitive impairment) were reported at Week 116 (end of the study) in the gantenerumab group compared to placebo (difference in the adjusted mean CDR‐SB change from baseline between placebo and treated: 0.88; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.19 to 1.56; p = 0.01; Figure S7).

DISCUSSION

This study effectively stratifies people with AD in clinical trials using resting‐state fMRI connectome metrics, identifying three distinct clusters with unique profiles and disease trajectories. Cluster 1 showed the most impaired connectome, leading to the fastest decline (brain volume loss and cognitive). Cluster 2 had the least impaired connectome and slowest progression. Cluster 3 was less impaired than Cluster 1 but faster‐progressing than Cluster 2. Of note, treatment analysis revealed greater separation in CDR‐SB progression for Cluster 1, whereas Clusters 2 and 3 showed minimal or no separation, despite comparable baseline CDR‐SB scores between treatment arms across all clusters.

Cluster 1 exhibited the most disrupted connectome profile (lowest global efficiency, loss of small‐world properties, and reduced connectivity in Salience, Dorsal Attention, and Default Mode networks), suggesting impaired network integration and information processing, 4 , 5 , 7 features often linked to faster AD progression and worse cognitive function. 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 Longitudinal data confirmed this showing faster brain volume loss and worse cognitive scores. This impaired connectome might be partially explained by the pathology profile revealed with the fluid biomarkers. Although amyloid pathology (CSF Aβ42/40 and CSF p‐tau181/Aβ42 ratios) 49 , 50 was similar to other clusters, Cluster 1 showed more pronounced CSF tau hyper‐phosphorylation. CSF p‐tau181 is a biomarker of both amyloid and tau pathology above all at lower levels, but to certain degrees it might also reflect the presence of tau in the brain. 51 , 52 These findings might imply that tauopathy could be a primary driver of network disruption at baseline and faster cognitive decline in this subgroup.

Previous studies have shown that memory and cognitive decline can be accelerated by Aβ interacting with tau, 52 and that tau might be a key downstream effector of Aβ‐mediated neuronal damage. 53 Here, tauopathy is also associated with synaptic dysfunction (indicated by high CSF neurogranin). In addition, Cluster 1 reported high levels of CSF neurofilament and total tau, signs of neural injury or neurodegeneration. 50 This disease trajectory in Cluster 1, within the framework of the amyloid‐tau‐neurodegeneration (ATN 50 , 54 ) model of AD, suggests that the simultaneous presence of tau and amyloid pathologies plays a pivotal role, leading to neurodegeneration. 19 Notably, in Cluster 1, the markers of tauopathy and neurodegeneration were positively associated with increased network segregation. This segregation might reflect either compensatory mechanisms, 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 as network hyperactivation, 59 or non‐linear network responses to pathology 60 (strengthening local connections to compartmentalize damage, potentially maladaptive), or a sign of cognitive decline due to disrupted global information transfer. 4

The combination of connectome features and biomarker profiles might suggest a potentially aggressive, possibly early‐onset, form of AD.

Moreover, Cluster 1 showed a significant treatment effect on CDR‐SB, likely due to the rapid decline in its placebo subgroup. Detecting treatment benefit is harder in slow‐progressing subgroups, but isolating individuals with aggressive disease ensures substantial decline, making effects statistically detectable. 14 , 15 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 Although this does not change the trial's negative primary outcome, it suggests that connectome architecture can identify fast progressors, offering a framework for more efficient trials by selecting cohorts with rapid predicted progression to enhance therapeutic signal detection.

Conversely, from a connectome perspective, Cluster 2 showed robust network integration along with efficient information processing, despite higher baseline cognitive and functional impairment (CDR‐SB) and older age. This group showed stronger functional connectivity in default and attentional networks and lower tau pathology/neurodegeneration than Cluster 1, suggesting a more resilient, less aggressive AD phenotype, confirmed by a slower rate of cognitive and brain volume decline. These individuals might represent a preclinical AD stage, where a separate co‐pathology could be driving the cognitive decline indicated by the CDR‐SB scores. The combination of strong functional connectivity in key networks and a favorable fluid biomarker profile suggests a protective effect against rapid neurodegeneration, potentially through effective compensatory mechanisms that maintain strong connectivity within the default mode network (DMN) and between DMN/Attention/Salient networks, which are critically affected early in the disease. 45

Cluster 3 presents an intermediate connectome between Clusters 1 and 2, with functional connectivity higher than Cluster 1 but lower than Cluster 2. This profile is linked to faster brain volume changes than in Cluster 2, yet similar cognitive decline rates. This suggests an intermediate AD phenotype. The faster progression versus Cluster 2 might relate to slightly more elevated synaptic dysfunction (CSF neurogranin) and plasma p‐tau (181/217) levels. However, Cluster 3's stronger and more efficient connectome compared to Cluster 1 may reflect better compensatory capacity, potentially slowing cognitive decline. Individuals in Cluster 3 are at risk for an aggressive AD form primarily impacting brain volume loss, suggesting the need for rapid intervention to maintain connectome functionality and mitigate long‐term neurodegeneration.

The observed heterogeneity in functional connectivity and disease progression among AD clusters highlights the need for better stratification in clinical trials, moving beyond simple MCI/mild AD dementia classifications. Improved stratification could also identify deviations from the expected AD course due to copathologies or resilience factors. 65 Given that brain network organization has been associated with pathological hallmarks (amyloid, tau, genetic risk factors), 4 we suggest that incorporating network‐based metrics would allow us to view AD as a disorder of network dysconnectivity, not solely of protein misfolding. 66 By tracking functional connectivity changes, these metrics could potentially add a biomarker of function, augmenting the current pathology‐based ATN staging. However, this integration might be challenging due to the complex nature of resting‐state data exhibiting non‐linear trajectories driven by compensatory mechanisms in response to pathological changes. In this scenario, the use of simple threshold‐based approaches is unfeasible. To overcome this, a crucial step would be to define a connectome‐based similarity metric to characterize individual network topology. Given their stability, cluster centroids might be used as representative of the connectomes. For example, patients with AD whose connectome is more similar to Cluster 1's centroid might show faster progression. Although a cluster's centroid could theoretically act as its representative connectome for deriving these scores, further validation with additional clinical datasets, including planned out‐of‐sample testing, is required before clinical application.

Integrating connectivity measures into AD research could lead to improved treatment strategies, fostering a better understanding of the complex interplay between molecular and network‐level pathologies. It could inform new therapeutic targets, improve drug efficacy assessment, and refine study enrollment strategies, particularly by identifying fast progressors to facilitate earlier intervention. Despite this potential, clinical adoption might be limited by the need for specialized technical expertise, the time‐consuming protocol, and the time burden for the patients.

Future studies are needed to confirm the findings reported here and investigate their generalizability in independent cohorts, exploring the utility of these stratification methods in predicting treatment response.

This study demonstrated the feasibility of extracting functional connectivity metrics from clinical trial images. This was achieved despite variability from multi‐site scanners, by implementing stringent quality control measures. 29 Limitations include potential biases from the chosen atlas for parcellation and thresholding methods. 67 The analyses presented here are post hoc, focused on a subgroup and exploratory, conducting without formal control for type I errors. Moreover, CSF and positron emission tomography (PET) data were available for a subset of participants, limiting statistical power and representativeness. Hence, future studies require larger longitudinal PET and CSF datasets to confirm and generalize these findings.

In conclusion, our findings emphasize the potential of network‐based approaches to stratify the population for AD clinical trials, improving the tracking of disease progression and facilitating the implementation of targeted treatments. By recognizing the importance of brain network dysconnectivity in AD and leveraging connectome‐derived models, we can move toward a more nuanced understanding of the disease and develop more effective, personalized treatment strategies.
