Craniospinal irradiation planning and delivery: a comparison between Halcyon Ring Gantry and TrueBeam C Arm LINAC delivery systems
Authors: Venugopal Sundaram, D Khanna, P Mohandass, Rekha Arya, Nilotpal Chakravarty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis research assesses the dosimetric differences in volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) between True- BeamTM C-arm and Halcyon™ ring gantry linacs, specifically for craniospinal irradiation (CSI) treatment.Materials and methodsThe fifteen patients retrospectively chosen for this study were treated with the TrueBeam linear accelerator between 2020 and 2023. Initially, VMAT patient plans were planned and delivered in TrueBeam (TB6X), and using the same objective function, TB6FFF and Halcyon™ (HAL6FFF) plans were generated. Plans were compared dosimetrically using dose volume histogram (DVH) parameters for the cranial, spine targets and Organs At Risk (OARs) in the treatment planning system (TPS). The different dose dosimetric criteria were evaluated such as dose received by D98%, D95%, D50%, D 1cc, volume receiving ≥ 107% dose, max dose (Gy), homogeneity index (HI), conformity index (CI), Monitor Units (MU) and dose spillage [V20Gy, V10Gy, V5Gy, V2Gy (%), DBody — planning target volume (PTV)]. Delivery accuracy was evaluated by performing pre-treatment Portal Dosimetry (PD) measurements applied with 3%/2 mm acceptance criteria and a 95% passing rate.ResultsBrain PTV dose received by 95% of the PTV volume (D95% (Gy) PTV) for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF plans were 35.9 ± 0.3 Gy, 35.7 ± 0.4 Gy, and 35.6 ± 0.4 Gy respectively and the difference was statistically insignificant (paired sample t-test). Spine PTV dose D95% in the same sequence, 35.7 ± 0.3 Gy, 35.5 ± 0.6 Gy, and 35.4 ± 0.3 Gy with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.016) for TB6X vs. HAL6FFF. Brain PTV HI for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF were 0.07 ± 0.03, 0.08 ± 0.03, and 0.09 ± 0.02, respectively, and in the same sequence, Spine PTV 0.07 ± 0.01, 0.08 ± 0.02 and 0.09 ± 0.02. MUs for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF plans were 1004.9 ± 97.7, 1454.3 ± 197.9, and 1195.8 ± 204.7, respectively, with a mean difference of 449.4, 190.9 and 258.5 MUs and found statistically significant (p < 0.005).ConclusionsClinically and dosimetrically acceptable VMAT-based craniospinal irradiation plans can be generated by the newly introduced dual-layer Multi-Leaf Collimator (MLC) model in a Halcyon™ linac.

Introduction

Through multidisciplinary approaches, craniospinal irradiation (CSI) is crucial in the comprehensive care of brain tumors in children and adults [1, 2]. CSI has evolved into a vital treatment approach for primary tumors, with commonly addressed malignancies encompassing medulloblastoma, high-risk germ cell tumors, and certain secondary malignant tumors of the meninges that are responsive to radiotherapy [3, 4].

Patients in the adolescent or childhood age range typically undergo treatment, and children with localized disease exhibit five-year recurrence-free survival rates exceeding 80%. In comparison, those with high-risk features approached 70% rate. Among the notable long-term consequences of radiotherapy in these individuals are neurocognitive dysfunction, growth retardation or anomalies, and impacts on their overall quality of life [5–7]. Performing Craniospinal irradiation poses significant technical challenges due to the extensive planning target volume (PTV) and the heightened radiosensitivity of critical organs such as the spinal cord. The constraints of conventional linear accelerators (LINAC) used for CSI necessitate the alignment of treatment fields with distinct isocenters, leading to dose inhomogeneity and inaccuracies at the junction areas [8].

Traditionally, CSI has been delivered using a conventional LINAC, but several issues have been identified. These include gaps between radiation fields due to the impracticality of treating CSI in a single field, suboptimal dose distribution within the planning target volume, and high doses reaching organs at risk (OARs). The inadequate dose distribution in the PTV can heighten the risk of treatment failure, while higher radiation doses to OARs may result in both acute and delayed toxicities. In the conventional approach, CSI treatments typically involve a 3-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) technique utilizing two opposing whole-brain fields and one or two posterior spinal fields, depending on the length of the spine [9, 10]. 3D-CRT methods require ‘‘feathering’’ of the midline-matched junction between the two lateral brain fields and the direct spine field. Radiotherapy planning of the craniospinal axis remains an intricate task because of the hurdles involved in creating a uniform dose to the brain and the spinal axis and avoiding hot and cold spots at the field junctions [11, 12].

Recently, there has been a notable rise in the adoption of intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), particularly volume-modulated arc therapy (VMAT). Consequently, both methodologies have garnered attention for treating various diseases, including tumors affecting the craniospinal axis. Multiple centers have implemented the VMAT RapidArc capability on their linacs and started treating patients for CSI with this modality [12–17].

VMAT delivers the dose to the PTV in a single source rotation with a volumetric approach. The Varian solution for VMAT, RapidArcTM, which is based on Karl Otto’s original investigations, aims to optimize the instantaneous simultaneously multi-leaf collimator (MLC) positions, dose rate, and gantry rotational speed variations to achieve the desired dose distribution [18].

First described by Fogliata et al. in 2011, several researchers investigated different properties of the VMAT-based CSI technique, which is currently well adapted in clinical practice [12–17]. These investigations were limited to the C-arm LINAC or helical tomotherapy (Accuray Inc., Sunnyvale, CA) treatment delivery systems [15, 16, 19–23].

Recently, Varian Medical Systems (Palo Alto, CA) has introduced a ring-gantry type treatment delivery system called Halcyon™ dual-layer MLC linac (HA SX2). The mean energy and the nominal depth of maximal dose are 1.3 MeV and 1.3 cm, compared to the TrueBeamTM linac (6MV FFF beam) at 1.4 MeV and 1.5 cm, respectively. In contrast to the TrueBeamTM platform, the Halcyon ™ is equipped with newly designed (MLC) SX2 double-stacked and staggered 1 cm width MLC layers [24]. The proximal and distal layers are offset by 5mm, allowing for a projected 5 mm effective MLC width at the isocenter, similar to True- BeamTM M120 (TB M120) Linac (Fig. 1). A double stack configuration that reduces MLC leakage and decreases the transmission dose to less than 0.1%. The maximal field size of this jawless Halcyon ™ Linac is 28 × 28 cm2. Halcyon™ has a maximum treatment length of 36 cm when using a dual isocenter (8 cm extension length of actual field size of 28 cm). This extended treatment field is useful in planning tumors with a length > 28 cm but not more than 36 cm. Due to the field size constraint, planning for CSI and large tumors is challenging. The Halcyon™ linac is additionally equipped with a fast 15-second kilo Voltage Cone Beam CT (kV‐CBCT) imaging system that includes a high‐quality iterative CBCT reconstruction algorithm (iCBCT) [25]. Daily patient setup times are significantly reduced on the Halcyon ™ with a new one‐step setup that automatically applies couch shifts during a patient’s setup, thus eliminating the need to apply isocenter shifts manually.

This study aimed to perform a dosimetric analysis between VMAT CSI plans designed for TrueBeam ® C-arm and Halcyon™ ring gantry linacs.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

Fifteen patients retrospectively chosen for this study were treated between 2020 and 2023 using a TrueBeamTM LINAC. The median PTV length was 72 cm, and the median age was 21 years. A comparative plan was retrospectively created for each patient in the alternate Halcyon™ ring gantry LINAC for dosimetric comparison between the two LINACs.

CT scan datasets and patient immobilization

The patients were immobilized using a three-point head thermoplastic mask for the head, lower, and upper body, using a back with both arms by the side of the body. After the patient was adequately immobilized, a computed tomography (CT) scan was performed from head to mid‑thigh on a CT scanner (Siemens, Somotam definition AS — 78 cm bore size) with a 3 mm slice thickness. Three fiducial markers were placed on the thermoplastic cast. Images obtained were imported to the workstation Eclipse™ Somavision version 15.6 (Varian Medical System Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Target definition

A clinical radiation oncologist contoured, target volumes, and OAR. The cranium and spine PTV is generated by a 5 mm margin from the cranium clinical target volume (CTV) and 10 mm from the spine CTV, respectively. Eyes, lens, optic nerves, cochlea, esophagus, parotids, oral cavity, larynx, thyroid, lungs, heart, liver, bowel, spleen, pancreas, stomach, genitals, scrotum OAR were delineated. Finally, total normal tissue volume was created, and PTV was removed from the body to evaluate the dose of non-target tissue. All patients were prescribed a dose of 36 Gy in 20 fractions, 1.8 Gy per fraction.

TrueBeamTM VMAT planning

Initially, patient clinical plans VMAT Rapidarc were created and delivered using Varian True-BeamTM 6X (TB6X) equipped with a millennium 120 MLC (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) medical LINAC. The plans for the patients were planned in the treatment planning system (TPS) Varian Eclipse™ version 15.6 using Photon Optimizer (PO version 15.6.03) optimization algorithm for inverse planning and Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA version 15.6.03) for the final dose calculations, with a set dose grid size of 2.5 mm.

The PO algorithm generates a sequence of control points that define MLC leaf positions and MU/deg as a function of the gantry angle. This information is transferred to the treatment machine, and the machine control system determines how the dose rate and gantry speed will be modulated to deliver during the plan. PO algorithm working function was discussed elsewhere [26–17].

The total cranio-caudal planning target volume exceeds 60 cm; we chose three isocentric techniques for placing the isocentre position in the target; otherwise, the isocenter is placed in only two. Typically, the first isocenter is placed in the cranium, and two isocenters are placed in the spine target volume. The beam arrangement is presented in Figure. 2. The isocenter of each field was placed so that the isocenters differed only in the longitudinal axis. It is noteworthy that in treatment delivery, the multiple-isocenter plans need to be split, each plan including one of the original plan’s isocenters. Hence, each plan will be delivered individually, and the total will be equal to the initial multiple-isocenter plan. Therefore, the patient is repositioned, and new kV/kV, kV/MV, or CBCT imaging is acquired before each plan.

For cranium planning volume, two full arcs from a gantry range of 179° to 181° [clockwise (CW), counterclockwise (CCW)] with collimator rotation were arranged to reduce interleaf leakage. For spine PTV, two full arcs (CW, CCW) were arranged, but the radiation beam gets switched only on those symmetrically placed 110° left to right side from the 180° gantry range angle. The anterior entrance with an avoidance sector were given (total arc length 140°). Avoidance sectors are ranges of gantry rotation where no dose is delivered. In the avoidance sectors, the beam is forcefully turned off. In this way of arrangement, most of the anterior portion of the patient’s low-dose area is completely reduced.

For controlling dose outside the target, a ring structure of 2.5 cm was made around the target, and this ring helps make more conformal dose distributions in the target and minimize dose to normal tissue irradiation, with a combination of normal tissue objective (NTO) tools to further reduce dose outside the PTV. Optimization for creating fluence distribution inside the cranium and spine PTVs was done simultaneously in the synchronous setting, creating a field-overlapping of around 10 to 15 cm in length. In this method, no field matching is required. All arcs were optimized simultaneously, and the plan was optimized, implementing dose-volume constraints on the PTV, OAR, and control structure. Further, the off-target dose was reduced using the NTO tool in Eclipse™ TPS. Due to many arcs, optimization took a long time. The final plan was optimized using the auto-feathering approach and dose calculation, resulting in a final plan.

Clinical Plans were designed for a Varian TrueBeam ® 6X (TB6X), using the same objective functions: one 6 MVFFF (TB6FFF) and one Halcyon™ 6FFF (HAL6FFF) for each patient plans and the total of 45 plans were created.

HalcyonTM VMAT planning technique

For both Halcyon™ and TrueBeamTM linacs, the same optimization engine (PO version 15.6.03) and the same dose calculation algorithm [analytical anisotropic algorithm (AAA)] were used. For comparison, all clinical plans were reoptimized in Eclipse™ TPS using the same numbers of partial arcs, and identical arc geometry (including TrueBeamTM VMAT arc length). Optimization objectives were identical to TB6X and TB6FFF plans. HAL6FFF VMAT plans were normalized identically to clinical TB6X VMAT plans as described above. However, jaw tracking is not available for the Halcyon™ plans due to its jawless MLC field design. All the Halcyon™ plans were then normalized to achieve identical target coverage to the corresponding TrueBeam® plans.

Dosimetric evaluation

Our institutional criteria were set to target coverage minimum of 95% PTV volume receiving at least 95% of the prescription dose coverage based on the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) 83 guidelines. Furthermore, all plans were compared dosimetrically considering dose-volume parameters for the cranial, spine targets and OARs in the treatment plans were compared and evaluated which includes dose received by D98%, D95%, D50%, D1cc, volume receiving ≥ 107% dose, max dose (Gy), homogeneity index (HI), conformity index (CI), MU and dose spillage (V20Gy, V10Gy, V5Gy, V2Gy (%), Dbody-PTV). Treatment delivery efficiency and accuracy for each plan were assessed by evaluating the total number of MU per fraction, and the beam Modulation Factor (MF) was defined as the ratio of the total number of MU to the fractional prescription dose in Gy.

Patient specific pretreatment verification

For each plan, the dose delivery accuracy was evaluated by performing patient-specific Quality Assurance (QA) measurements using a portal dosimetry VMAT QA procedure that was already established in our clinic on both platforms. The delivered fluence maps were measured on the Electronic Portal Imaging Device (EPID). The EPID on TrueBeamTM Digital Megavoltage Imager (DMI) panel consists of a 43 × 43 cm2 area corresponding to a pixel matrix of 1,280 × 1,280. The aS1200 EPID has a higher pixel resolution (0.336 mm) and increased dosimetric active area (40 × 40 cm2). The EPID on Halcyon™ has a 43 × 43 cm2 area with the same resolution as TrueBeamTM. Delivery accuracy was evaluated by performing pre-treatment Portal Dosimetry (PD) QA measurements on the linac used for each plan with gamma passing rate applied with 3%/2 mm acceptance criteria, 95% passing rate, and a 10% low dose threshold according to the Task Group 218 of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM TG 218) [28].

Data analysis

Comparison of dosimetric parameters and metrics was performed using the paired samples t-test (parametric) and a significance level of p-value of < 0.05 in SPSS 27 data analysis software (IBM, New York, NY USA) and Microsoft Excel 2010.

Results

Target coverage

Plan acceptability criteria were that 95% PTV should receive at least 95% of the prescribed dose and less than 3% volume should receive ≤ 107% dose. All the plans satisfied the requirements generated on TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF beam energies. Table 2 summarizes the evaluation of target coverage for 15 CSI VMAT patients.

All plans were clinically acceptable as per the requirements. Brain PTV dose (mean ± standard deviation), received by 95% of the PTV volume [D95% (Gy) PTV] for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF plans were 35.9 ± 0.3 Gy, 35.7 ± 0.4 Gy, and 35.6 ± 0.4 Gy, respectively, and the difference is not statistically significant (paired sample t-test). Spine PTV dose D95% was in the same sequence, 35.7 ± 0.3 Gy, 35.5 ± 0.6 Gy, and 35.4 ± 0.3 Gy with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.016) for TB6X Vs HAL6FFF.

Brain PTV volume receiving ≥ 107% dose for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HALFFF plans were 0.4 ± 0.5%, 0.4 ± 0.6%, and 0.9 ± 0.9%, respectively. Spine PTV volume was in the same sequence, 0.3 ± 0.5%, 0.2 ± 0.3%, and 0.6 ± 0.7 % with a statistically insignificant difference. Brain PTV HI for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF were 0.07 ± 0.03, 0.08 ± 0.03, and 0.09 ± 0.02, respectively, and in the same sequence, Spine PTV 0.07 ± 0.01, 0.08 ± 0.02 and 0.09 ± 0.02. CI combined PTV for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF was 1.14 ± 0.06, 1.11 ± 0.04, and 1.12 ± 0.04, respectively. The difference between TB6X and HAL6FFF Brain HI (p = 0.046) and Spine HI (p = 0.027) was statistically significant. The brain and spine PTVs with color wash are 95% to 110 %. Overall, the PTV length is around 84.5 cm. Figure 3 shows the dose-volume histogram (DVH) for the example patient. Treatment delivery efficiency and accuracy

MUs for TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF plans (mean ± standard deviation) were 1004.9 ± 97.7, 1454.3 ± 197.9, and 1195.8 ± 204.7, respectively, with a mean difference of 449.4, 190.9 and 258.5 MUs and were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.005). Compared to HAL6FFF VMAT plans, TB6FFF VMAT plans delivered slightly higher total MU, corresponding to the total MU/fraction, MF, and PD. QA pass rates for the clinical True- BeamTM VMAT vs. Halcyon™ VMAT plans are shown in Table 5. Treatment delivery accuracy of the CSI VMAT plans was evaluated by delivering each plan in QA measurement to both linacs via on‐board EPID imager and recording the gamma analysis pass rates via PD. Our clinic’s VMAT QA program complies with the AAPM TG‐218 recommended pretreatment QA tolerance level (overall pass rate ≥ 95% at a γ– criteria of 3%/2 mm with a low threshold of 10%). All 45 plans satisfied this requirement and were similar. The dose delivery accuracy of the TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF VMAT plans were 99.96 ± 0.06, 99.99 ± 0.04, and 99.97 ± 0.09, respectively.

Discussion

This study expands the investigation into the newly introduced Halcyon™ linac. The research compares and evaluates CSI VMAT plans designed for the Halcyon™ and TrueBeam® systems. The assessment primarily examines plan quality and the extent of dose sparing at OARs. First, we compared plans with the default energies used for CSI treatment plans in our institution, which is 6 MV for TrueBeamTM with Halcyon™ 6FFF. Second, we generated a plan with 6FFF in TrueBeamTM to compare it with a 6FFF plan in Halcyon™ regarding the same energy conditions and, additionally, TB6MV and TB6FFF were compared. We aimed at the best achievable dosimetric outcome for each plan between dose constraints at OARs and PTVs coverage.

Our results show that the plan quality, in terms of HI and CI, was similar to the generated plans of the two treatment units for both patients. This may originate from differences in beam profiles or mean energy. As a result, the final point contributes no dosimetric attributes to this study. The dose coverage and hot volume to the craniospinal axis were comparable between the two competing arms with no statistical difference. Plan homogeneity was comparable between the three plans. The only similar characteristics used in this study were the arc length and the same optimization parameters. The difference between the two machines was in terms of field size, beam energy, beam characteristics (flatten and unflatten), dose rate, MLC speed, gantry speed, and optimization algorithm.

Advanced techniques, such as VMAT, are safer and offer an accurate treatment for CSI; the VMAT technique reduces the geometrical uncertainties and improves the dose homogeneity [15, 16, 20, 21, 29].

The significant hardware differences between Halcyon™ and TrueBeamTM LINACs are as follows: (1) Maximum gantry speed (24 deg/sec for HAL and 6.0 deg/sec for TB) (2) MLC speed and configuration (5 cm/s for HAL and 2.5 cm/s for TB) (3) Halcyon™ has less MLC leakage than TrueBeamTM Millennium 120 (HAL: 0.4% and TB: 1.5%:) (4) The mean energy and the nominal depth of maximal dose are 1.3 MeV and 1.3 cm for Halcyon compared to the TrueBeam (6MV FFF beam) at 1.4 MeV and 1.5 cm [25]. The following differences are apparent: not all 6X FFF are equal. This is because of the fact that not all 6X-FFF beams are created in the same way. The head design differs between a Halcyon™ and a TrueBeamTM or C series linac. The Halcyon™ does not use a bending magnet, so the electron energy never gets ‘purified’ by the energy slits in the magnet. Energy slits reject any electrons which have too high or too low energy for bending magnet linacs. So, the beam spectrum produced by a Halcyon™ differs from that made on a TrueBeamTM [24, 25]. Due to field size limitations, Halcyon™ required an additional isocentre, producing slightly higher MUs. The number of isocentres was a function of three factors: (1) cranio-caudal PTV length, (2) field size in the y direction, and (3) adjacent field overlap of 10–15 cm. For patients in the pediatric and adolescent age groups (PTV length ≤ 60 cm), TB required three isocentres, and HAL required four isocentres. With increased PTV length, additional isocenters need to be added.

Various research groups have compared the performance of the Halcyon™ linac to that of the TrueBeamTM and C series linac for multiple sites and techniques such as CSI, head & neck, liver SBRT, spine SBRT, prostate SBRT, and pelvic node SBRT [30–35]. All authors reported acceptable Halcyon™ dosimetric plans regarding target coverage, dose conformity, and OAR dose constraints. Stroubinis et al. observed minimal variations in dosimetric parameters across different linacs (Halcyon, Truebeam 6X, and Truebeam 6FFF) for two patients [36]. Our study, evaluating a larger cohort of 15 patients, also found good agreement between the three dosimetry models. However, we observed statistically significant differences in some dosimetric parameters, such as homogeneity index and OAR doses, suggesting potential variations in plan quality depending on the linac used. The Sarkar et al. study, comparing Halcyon and TrueBeam 6FFF, observed statistically significant differences favoring Halcyon regarding OAR sparing (lower spillage doses) and conformity index [37]. Our study, comparing TB6X, TB6FFF, and HAL6FFF, found minimal clinically significant differences in OAR doses between the linacs, with some exceptions. Our study, however, showed statistically significant differences in monitor units (MUs) between the linacs, with TB6FFF plans requiring the highest number of MUs.

The Barsing et al. study, utilizing Halcyon, reported outstanding PTV coverage (V95% > 98%), excellent homogeneity (HI ≤ 0.1), and acceptable OAR doses [38]. Our study, evaluating a larger cohort, observed some statistically significant differences between linacs regarding homogeneity index and OAR doses. Biswal et al. observed comparable MU deliveries and statistically insignificant differences in most dosimetric parameters, including target coverage and conformity [22]. The Matsumoto et al. study, utilizing a novel O-ring linac, achieved high conformity and homogeneity with acceptable OAR sparing. Matsumoto et al. focused on minimizing off-target doses using advanced optimization techniques, while the present study emphasized inter-linac dosimetric comparisons [39]. Our findings indicate potential disparities in treatment plan quality and delivery accuracy across different linear accelerator platforms. Specifically, we observed statistically significant differences in key target coverage metrics (D95%, HI) and doses received by specific organs at risk.

Petroccia et al. (2019) compared Halcyon™ spine SBRT to TrueBeamTM plans and found that the former has a slower dose falloff and lower target conformity [34]. According to the literature, Halcyon™ provided better overall performance in plan quality and delivery time than comparative C-arm linac, which supports our result of its having a better dosimetric result, with 50% of OARs receiving a statistically significant lower dose than TrueBeamTM linear accelerators. Biswal et al. compared CSI in Halcyon™ and Novalis Tx linacs, which differed significantly in MLC width, beam energy, field size, and gantry speed. They showed results of comparable target coverage and statistically improved low-dose spillage and treatment time, favoring Halcyon ™ but with a higher setup margin [22]. Pokhrel et al. (2021) demonstrate improved OAR doses and plan quality for prostate, abdominal, and pelvic single lymph node irradiation and lung SBRT [31, 32]. Visak et al. found that knowledge-based Halcyon™ planning improved dosimetry over manual Halcyon™ and non-coplanar TrueBeamTM lung SBRT plans [35]. The paired sample t-test revealed no statistically significant difference concerning dose sparing in OARs between Halcyon™ and Edge Plans. Similar studies [32–34] observed consistent findings, where VMAT plans designed for Halcyon ™ and TrueBeam® in various anatomic regions (such as the head and neck, breast, and prostate) were compared, yielding comparable results.

In the case of Halcyon™, reduced MLC leakage is further supported by increased gantry and MLC speeds, enhancing plan quality and minimizing spillage dose. This discovery is noteworthy in our investigation and may hold significant dosimetric implications, particularly in light of the AAPM report TG-158. The report discusses out-of-field doses and their association with increased risks of second malignancy and cardiac toxicity among long-term cancer survivors [43]. Approximately 10% of cancer survivors develop a second cancer, with around 2% attributed to early radiotherapy [43, 44]. The dose-risk relationship for second cancer appears linear within our investigated dose range. The most common treatment toxicities were hematologic and gastrointestinal events. Low-dose spill increases myelosuppression (bone marrow suppression) using complete arcs in the spine. The bone marrow doesn’t make enough blood cells or platelets. Generally, this happens after 7 to 10 fractions due to bone marrow irradiation [45, 46].

As expected from any intensity-modulated techniques (or any technique requiring multiple entry angles per beam), the dose bath, i.e., the low dose delivered to large surrounding volumes and structures, is increased with respect to the conventional techniques. The increase in the low-dose bath may result in an increase in the rate of secondary malignancies. Several authors [47, 48] have conducted risk estimations, and their conclusions generally assert that intensity-modulated techniques heighten the risk associated with secondary cancer induction, primarily due to the substantial number of MUs required for the delivery of IMRT plans.

Halcyon™ met the clinical requirements and guidelines for doses to OARs in treating CSI patients established in our center. Halcyon™ also has a maximum output rate of 800 MU/min as opposed to TrueBeamTM’s linac maximum output rate of 1400 MU/min. Still, due to the gantry rotation speed limitation of TrueBeam® (typically at least 1 RPM), Halcyon’s one-step patient setup and faster gantry rotation speeds (up to 4 RPM) may compensate for this and lead to a reduction in the overall treatment times.

Conclusions

Clinically and dosimetrically acceptable VMAT-based CSI plans can be generated by the newly introduced dual-layer MLC model in a Halcyon™ linac. In terms of OAR and low-dose spillage, these plans are statistically similar to True-BeamTM plans. The slight variations may originate from the differences in beam profile or mean energy, the degree of the modulation for each plan, the architecture, and the characteristics of MLCs for each treatment unit. Halcyon™ gives delivery faster treatment time, which could be crucial for CSI patients receiving treatment under general anesthesia.
