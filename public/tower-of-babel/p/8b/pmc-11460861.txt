Clinical effect analysis of vertebroplasty with high viscosity and standard bone cement for Kummell disease
Authors: Jian Wang, Jianxin Zhang, Jianbiao Huang, Jie Chen, Qunwei Dong, Hui Zhu, Xusheng Huang, Jianxian Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aims to observe and compare the effects of high-viscosity bone cement versus standard bone cement on the postoperative outcomes of percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) in patients with Kummell disease. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 135 patients with Kummell disease who underwent PVP between January 2019 and May 2023. Patients were divided into 2 groups: the high-viscosity group (group H, 63 cases) received high-viscosity bone cement during surgery, while the standard group (group S, 72 cases) received standard bone cement. Surgery duration, bone cement volume, bone cement leakage rate, as well as preoperative and postoperative evaluations using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), anterior vertebral height, and kyphotic Cobb angle were recorded and compared between the 2 groups. In the group H, significant improvements were observed in VAS scores, ODI, anterior vertebral height, and Cobb angle at 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively compared to preoperative values (P < .01). In the group S, VAS scores and ODI showed significant improvement at 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively (P < .01), but there were no significant differences in anterior vertebral height or Cobb angle compared to preoperative values (P > .05). Comparatively, the group H demonstrated superior outcomes in terms of bone cement volume, leakage rate, recovery of anterior vertebral height, and Cobb angle correction at 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively (P < .05). However, there were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of surgery duration, VAS scores, and ODI at the same time points (P > .05). PVP is an effective treatment for Kummell disease, providing significant pain relief and improved spinal function. The use of high-viscosity bone cement allows for greater volume infusion, better restoration of vertebral height, and correction of deformities, while also reducing the risk of cement leakage.

1. Introduction

Patients with Kummell disease usually have back pain resulting from minor trauma or no apparent cause on a background of osteoporosis. After an asymptomatic period lasting several weeks or months, pain recurs at the original site and progressively worsens, with severe cases developing progressive spinal kyphosis. Dr Hermann Kummell first reported the condition in 1895.[1] Kummell disease is more common in women, with an incidence of approximately 3.5% in those aged 50 to 54 years, rising to 16.6% at age 80. Imaging studies reveal collapsed vertebrae with linear, transverse, or crescent-shaped radiolucent shadows, known as the “intravertebral vacuum cleft” (IVC).[2] Advances in imaging technology have made MRI the most effective tool for diagnosing IVC, confirming that Kummell disease is not rare.[3] Studies show that due to varying gas and liquid content within the IVC, affected vertebrae exhibit low signal intensity on T1-weighted MRI and variable signals on T2-weighted MRI.[4] Currently, percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) has been established as an safe and effective surgery for treating Kummell disease, with notable advantages such as minimal invasiveness, rapid recovery, and good therapeutic effects. However, local fissures surrounding the intravertebral clefts in Kummell disease can lead to cement leakage when using standard bone cement, causing incomplete diffusion and suboptimal surgical outcomes. To mitigate these risks, our research team employed high-viscosity bone cement for injection into the affected vertebral clefts, resulting in significant clinical improvements.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. General information

The Department of Orthopedics at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University and Zhangping City Hospital treated and diagnosed 135 patients with Kummell disease who required PVP surgery between January 2019 and May 2023. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University, and all patients signed an informed consent. Surgical indications were determined based on preoperative clinical symptoms, specialist examination, and imaging studies. All cases used a percutaneous puncture system provided by Shanghai Kailitai Medical Technology Co., Ltd., high-viscosity bone cement provided by Heraeus Medical GmbH (model: OSTEOPAL®V), and standard bone cement provided by Tecres S.P.A. (model: MENDEC).

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) history, signs, and X-ray, CT, and MRI results indicating “vacuum cleft sign,” meeting the diagnostic criteria for Kummell disease; (2) age ≥ 60 years; (3) bone density T-score ≤ −2.5; (4) postoperative follow-up time ≥ 6 months; (5) signed informed consent and completed follow-up. Exclusion criteria: (1) presence of severe medical conditions precluding surgery; (2) spinal infection, tumor-induced vertebral necrosis or collapse; (3) isolated osteoporotic thoracolumbar compression fractures; (4) coexisting metabolic bone diseases or malignant tumors; (5) coexisting neurological symptoms requiring decompression; (6) refusal of surgery. One hundred thirty-five patients were included in our study, divided into the standard bone cement group (group S) and the high-viscosity bone cement group (group H). The group H consisted of 12 men (19.05%) and 51 women (80.95%); the average age was 82.7 ± 7.2 years (range 62–91 years). The lesions affected a total of 5 vertebrae, including T11 (6 cases), T12 (27 cases), L1 (12 cases), L2 (6 cases), and L3 (12 cases). The group S consisted of 15 men (20.83%) and 57 women (79.17%); the average age was 81.3 ± 7.2 years (range 68–90 years). The lesions affected 6 vertebrae, including T10 (3 cases), T11 (9 cases), T12 (27 cases), L1 (12 cases), L2 (12 cases), and L3 (12 cases). Between the 2 groups, there were no significant differences in general data and vertebral distribution (P > .05). All patients underwent X-ray, CT, and MRI examinations preoperatively, and X-rays were rechecked at 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively (Table 1).

2.3. Treatment methods

Patients were positioned prone, and the affected vertebrae were located under X-rays, marked on the body surface. Local infiltration anesthesia was administered using 1% lidocaine + 1% ropivacaine. Depending on the width of the inward angle and the pedicle of the T9–L1 vertebrae, puncture was performed either transpedicularly or extrapedicularly. For L2–L5 vertebrae, transpedicular puncture was preferred. A cannula needle was drilled into the vertebral body, stopping when the needle tip reached the middle and second third of the vertebra. The inner core was removed, and a manual drill was used to drill approximately 2 cm deeper, ensuring satisfactory position under fluoroscopy. Bone cement was gradually injected under fluoroscopic monitoring, observing closely with ECG monitoring. The group H received high-viscosity bone cement, while the group S received standard bone cement, both using the same cement filling apparatus. During the low viscosity phase, the pressure range for injecting the cement into the vertebra was 370 to 470 Pa s, while for the high viscosity phase, it was 1500 to 1600 Pa s. The injection continued until the cement fully filled the fissures in the affected vertebra. The puncture needle was removed after the cement solidified.

2.3.1. Postoperative care

Patients rested in bed and were monitored with ECG for 2 hours post-surgery. X-ray examinations were conducted on postoperative day 1. On postoperative day 2, patients started mobilizing and performing rehabilitation exercises with a lumbar support or brace. Patients were followed up at 1 month, 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively. Routine treatment for anti-osteoporosis included active vitamin D and calcium supplements, with additional medications to inhibit bone resorption or promote bone formation based on individual patient conditions (Fig. 1 for a typical case).

2.4. Observation indicators

Analyze the clinical and radiographic data of the 2 groups.

2.4.1. Clinical conditions

Record Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score for back pain and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) at preoperation, 24 hours post-operation, and during follow-ups at 3 months and 1 year. The ODI comprises 10 items covering pain intensity, personal care, walking, sitting, standing, etc, each with a maximum score of 5. Higher scores indicate more severe functional disability.

2.4.2. Radiographic measurements

On lateral X-ray images, measure the anterior height of the affected vertebrae at preoperation and follow-ups of 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year post-operation; also measure the Cobb angle at the same time.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical processing and analysis were performed using SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS Inc., USA). Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD (x̅ ± s). Independent sample t tests were used for comparisons between groups, paired t tests for comparisons within groups at preoperation and post-operation, and chi-square tests for categorical data. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Comparison of VAS scores, ODI, anterior vertebral height, and Cobb angle in different time phases between the 2 groups

In the group H, VAS scores, ODI, anterior vertebral height, and Cobb angle at 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year post-operation were significantly improved compared to preoperation (P < .01). In the group S, VAS scores and ODI were significantly improved at 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year post-operation compared to preoperation (P < .01), while anterior vertebral height and Cobb angle showed no significant difference at 24 hours and 3 months post-operation (P > .05) but were significantly different at 1 year post-operation (P < .05). Between the groups, the group H had a lower cement leakage rate and better recovery of anterior vertebral height and Cobb angle at 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year post-operation (P < .05). VAS scores and ODI showed no significant difference between the groups at preoperation, 24 hours, 3 months, and 1 year post-operation (P > .05) (Table 2).

3.2. Surgical time, cement injection, and leakage

The surgical time between the group H and the group S was almost identical, with no statistically significant difference (P > .05). The average volume of cement injection per vertebra in the group H was 4.80 mL, compared to 4.19 mL in the group S. In the group H, the injection volume was significantly higher than in the group S (P < .05). There were 3 cases of cement leakage in the group H, all of which were intervertebral, with a leakage rate of 4.76%. In the meantime, there were 15 cases of cement leakage in the group S, including 9 intervertebral and 6 paravertebral, with a total leakage rate of 20.83%. None of the leakage patients showed symptoms of back pain or nerve root compression. The leakage rate in the group H was significantly lower than in the group S (P < .05) (Table 3).

4. Discussion

4.1. Clinical status of Kummell disease

The pathophysiological mechanisms of Kummell disease remain unclear. Previous studies suggest that Kummell disease is the result of multiple factors, including biomechanical changes following fractures, osteoporosis, and ischemic necrosis of the vertebrae.[5,6] The most representative is ischemic osteonecrosis after vertebral fracture.[7,8] When the vertebra is injured, the blood vessels supplying the vertebra can be damaged. Fractures of the anterior third of the spinal canal may injure the small intramedullary arteries leading to poor fracture healing because this region is rich in blood vessels.[9,10] Furthermore, vascular pathologies themselves may also cause ischemic osteonecrosis. Risk factors such as prolonged steroid therapy, chronic alcoholism, and hemoglobinopathies can lead to atherosclerosis, reducing the blood supply to the vertebrae,[11] delaying the formation of new blood vessels in the fractured vertebrae, and eventually leading to nonunion or delayed healing of bone necrosis. Clinically, Kummell disease is classified into 3 types. Type I, where vertebral height is reduced by <20% with no adjacent segment disc degeneration. Type II, where vertebral height is reduced by more than 20%, accompanied by adjacent segment disc degeneration or mild instability. And Type III, characterized by cortical rupture of the posterior vertebral body and dural sac compression, sometimes with spinal nerve damage.[12] Typically, Kummell disease does not heal on its own. However, conservative treatments such as bed rest and bracing conventions are usually ineffective, resulting in chronic back pain or disability. Teriparatide, an anabolic drug, is thought to be beneficial for patients receiving conservative treatment, but it may take longer to take effect.[13] Surgical treatment can more effectively and quickly relieve pain, correct kyphotic deformity, and reduce complications from prolonged bed rest, thus being widely adopted. Surgical treatments mainly include PVP, percutaneous kyphoplasty, and open anterior and posterior spinal surgeries.[2,14–19]

4.2. Performance of bone cement with different viscosities

In recent years, bone cement has gradually been used as a routine filling material for the treatment of Kummell disease in stages I–II. It features several advantages including better fatigue resistance, stronger analgesic effects, higher resistance to twisting and compression, and lower porosity. These characteristics allow it to effectively fill the hollow cavities of vertebrae, prevent further loss of vertebral height, and significantly improve spinal function. During PVP, bone cement is injected into the affected vertebra, and the heat generated can damage the nerves within the vertebra, providing analgesia. The filling and expansion of bone cement not only restore vertebral height and increase vertebral strength but can also partially correct kyphotic deformity. However, complications after PVP remain relatively challenging. Previous studies have indicated that 66% of postoperative complications are related to bone cement leakage, which mainly occurs through damage to the vertebral cortical bone, vertebral basilar veins, or intervertebral veins. Research has shown a direct correlation between the occurrence of complications and cement leakage. Mild leakage may not present significant adverse symptoms, but severe leakage can cause spinal cord injury, increase patient suffering, and even result in disability.[20] Bone cement leakage is associated with the timing of injection after mixing and the viscosity of the bone cement. Overly diluted and rapidly dispersing bone cement often leads to leakage complications. Generally, bone cements used for spinal vertebrae filling can be classified into high-viscosity and standard types based on their composition and ratio. Traditional standard bone cement mainly consists of polymethyl methacrylate, composed of polymethyl methacrylate, zirconia, benzoyl peroxide powder, and liquid methyl methacrylate mixed with N,N-dimethyl-p-toluidine. It generates heat during polymerization, and the monomers are toxic. Due to its low viscosity, the liquid phase has strong fluidity, but the dough phase has poor dispersibility, with a short transition time from the dough phase to the curing phase. High-viscosity bone cement, on the other hand, is composed of polymethyl methacrylate, barium sulfate powder, methyl methacrylate, benzoyl peroxide, methyl acrylate butyl ester, hydroquinone liquid, and N,N-dimethyl-p-toluidine. It has no liquid phase after mixing and quickly becomes dough-like, with a prolonged transition time from the dough phase to the curing phase and lower polymerization temperatures. Clinically, choosing the appropriate viscosity of bone cement is crucial for improving the surgical efficacy for Kummell disease, reducing intraoperative risks, and minimizing postoperative complications. However, standard bone cement’s injection timing is difficult to control, with its early flow properties being too strong and a short curing time, combined with high heat during polymerization, increasing the risk of damage to surrounding tissues, intervertebral spaces, and even spinal canal nerves. Inadequate technique can increase the risk of severe complications such as cement leakage and pulmonary embolism. Existing studies have identified low-viscosity bone cement is an independent risk factor for cement leakage after PVP.[21,22] Traditional low-viscosity bone cement, due to its small polymer volume and long liquid state during injection, experiences higher pressure and thus a greater likelihood of leakage, especially in significantly compressed vertebrae.[23] Hiwatashi et al[24] and Koh et al[25] found through pre- and postoperative imaging that incomplete vertebral walls and cracks are associated risk factors for leakage. Segments with such features have a higher risk of leakage, while vertebral height loss does not significantly correlate with leakage. High-viscosity bone cement used in vertebroplasty is an improved product based on standard bone cement composed mainly of polymethyl methacrylate. It has high viscosity immediately, requires lower pressure during injection, and provides better dispersion due to its longer injection time. The polymerization process maintains a lower temperature of around 50 °C to 60 °C, reducing thermal damage. Based on preoperative imaging data, targeted puncture and staged injection during the procedure avoid edge cracks of the vertebra, better fill the internal fissures of the vertebra, and closely connect with the surrounding bone, achieving a more satisfactory dispersion effect of bone cement and reducing the risk of postoperative displacement or extrusion. Given the presence of false joints in Kummell disease vertebrae, fissures within the vertebra may expand during spinal flexion and extension activities.[26] Specifically, during PVP, spinal extension can partially restore the collapsed vertebral height, and the bone cement is confined to the fissures, contributing to the correction of kyphosis. The puncture guide is placed as centrally as possible in the cavity, and the entire process of bone cement injection is monitored under fluoroscopy. Utilizing the characteristics of high-viscosity bone cement, long dough phase time and short curing phase time, staged injections are performed. The curing phase’s rapid solidification can seal perforations at the anterior edge or endplates of the vertebra. Subsequently, the injection rod is gradually retracted or its side hole direction adjusted to fill the cavity. The bone cement in the cavity forms multiple connections with the surrounding bone, and small amounts can also fill the pedicle channels, creating posterior column anchorage to further prevent anterior displacement or vertical displacement of the cement. Additionally, the high-viscosity bone cement eliminates the need to estimate and judge the injection time, reducing the complexity of the procedure. Even less experienced surgeons can effectively lower the rate of bone cement leakage and significantly reduce the risks of pulmonary embolism and nerve injury, thereby ensuring the safety of vertebroplasty.[27,28] In this study, we found that the bone cement leakage rate in the high-viscosity PVP group was 4.76%, lower than the 20.83% leakage rate of standard bone cement PVP, indicating that high-viscosity bone cement significantly reduces the leakage rate compared to standard bone cement PVP. Both high-viscosity and standard bone cement groups demonstrated significant pain relief and improved spinal function. Postoperative VAS scores and ODI in both groups were significantly improved compared to preoperative scores, with no significant difference between the groups, suggesting that both techniques are clinically effective and consistent with previous research findings.[27,29–33]

4.3. Analysis of factors related to the effective treatment of Kummell disease with PVP

Spinal deformity is the main cause of aggravated back pain and functional impairment.[34,35] Multiple or severe vertebral deformities significantly impact the quality of life of patients with Kummell disease.[35,36] Fechtenbaum et al[36] further demonstrated that anterior and middle vertebral deformities negatively affect the quality of life. Additionally, spinal deformity is a major predictor of long-term vertebral fractures.[37] This study indicates that restoring vertebral height and correcting kyphotic deformity are crucial. Feng Xinmin et al[38] showed that the leakage rate in the group H (6 out of 44 cases, 13.6%) was significantly lower than in the low-viscosity bone cement group (19 out of 42 cases, 45.2%). Furthermore, the leakage rate in the group H was lower than reported in other studies (26.3–75.0%).[39,40] Our study also reached the same conclusion: using high-viscosity bone cement in PVP can significantly reduce the leakage rate. Previous studies indicate that vertebral fissures are mainly located in the thoracolumbar region,[41,42] similar to this study. Fissures are often near the upper or lower endplates of the vertebrae, suggesting that the occurrence of Kummell disease may be related to repetitive stress activities and higher spinal mobility in the thoracolumbar region, leading to nonunion and ischemic necrosis of the vertebrae. Both groups of patients observed significant pain relief postoperatively, maintained until the final follow-up, with no significant differences between the groups. Filling the fissures within the vertebrae with bone cement partially restores and corrects vertebral height and kyphotic deformity, eliminating abnormal activity of the injured vertebra, which is a key factor in pain relief.[2] Radiographically, patients in the group H showed significant postoperative improvements in anterior vertebral height and kyphotic Cobb angle compared to the standard bone cement PVP group, with an approximate increase of 4mm in anterior vertebral height and a 4° improvement in the kyphotic Cobb angle. The standard bone cement group showed no significant deformity improvement at 24 hours and 3 months postoperatively, with deformity worsening at 1 year postoperatively, indicating that high-viscosity bone cement PVP provides better vertebral improvement, effectively restores vertebral height, corrects kyphotic deformity, and improves thoracolumbar sagittal balance. Over time, group S did not show significant improvements in vertebral compression and kyphosis, with gradual loss of vertebral height and kyphotic angle over time. This is likely due to lower pressure in the fissure area of the vertebrae, with surrounding sclerotic areas forming perfusion barriers. During surgery, the fissure area acts as a “reservoir” for bone cement, which fills the fissure in a solid state, but the limited amount of bone cement cannot connect with the adjacent endplates or strengthen the vertebral trabecular bone, failing to support normal physiological stress and leading to recurrent collapse.[43]

5. Conclusion

Compared to standard bone cement treatment, high-viscosity bone cement treatment for Kummell disease achieves more significant effects. Specifically, postoperative pain relief, functional improvement, and, importantly, correction of thoracolumbar kyphotic deformity were observed. High-viscosity bone cement allows sufficient mixing time, low-pressure perfusion, longer dispersion after injection, achieving maximum compressive strength, and reducing the risk of bone cement leakage. These advantages are not present in standard bone cement. Utilizing the characteristics of high-viscosity bone cement, targeted puncture, and stepwise injection, the procedure is simple and highly safe. This study is a retrospective analysis, which may involve subjective bias in follow-up, and statistical results may have some errors. Future large-sample prospective studies are needed to further standardize the treatment of Kummell disease, providing more clinical experience and evidence.
