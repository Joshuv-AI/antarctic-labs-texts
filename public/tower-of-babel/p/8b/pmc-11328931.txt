Promotion of maturation of human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes via treatment with the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha agonist Fenofibrate
Authors: Seul-Gi Lee, Jooeon Rhee, Jin Seok, Jin Kim, Min Woo Kim, Gyeong-Eun Song, Shinhye Park, Kyu Sik Jeong, Suemin Lee, Yun Hyeong Lee, Youngin Jeong, C-Yoon Kim, Hyung Min Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs research on in vitro cardiotoxicity assessment and cardiac disease modeling becomes more important, the demand for human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hPSC-CMs) is increasing. However, it has been reported that differentiated hPSC-CMs are in a physiologically immature state compared to in vivo adult CMs. Since immaturity of hPSC-CMs can lead to poor drug response and loss of acquired heart disease modeling, various approaches have been attempted to promote maturation of CMs. Here, we confirm that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), one of the representative mechanisms of CM metabolism and cardioprotective effect also affects maturation of CMs. To upregulate PPARα expression, we treated hPSC-CMs with fenofibrate (Feno), a PPARα agonist used in clinical hyperlipidemia treatment, and demonstrated that the structure, mitochondria-mediated metabolism, and electrophysiology-based functions of hPSC-CMs were all mature. Furthermore, as a result of multi electrode array (MEA)-based cardiotoxicity evaluation between control and Feno groups according to treatment with arrhythmia-inducing drugs, drug response was similar in a dose-dependent manner. However, main parameters such as field potential duration, beat period, and spike amplitude were different between the 2 groups. Overall, these results emphasize that applying matured hPSC-CMs to the field of preclinical cardiotoxicity evaluation, which has become an essential procedure for new drug development, is necessary.

Significance Statement

In this study, we demonstrate the promotion of hPSC-CMs maturation via treatment of PPARα agonist Fenofibrate. Fenofibrate upregulated expression of PPARα in hPSC-CMs and promoted mitochondrial maturation via activation of MFN1 but not MFN2. Consecutively, these effects of Fenofibrate promoted the structural, metabolic, and electrophysiological maturation of hPSC-CMs. Finally, MEA-based cardiotoxicity evaluation was performed to verify the drug reactivity of the matured hPSC-CMs. As a result of treatment with proarrhythmia-induced drugs E-4301 and nifedipine, different values of field potential change between immature and mature hPSC-CMs were confirmed.

Introduction

Research on cardiomyocytes (CMs), which constitute most of the heart, has been conducted for decades. By producing human pluripotent stem cells-derived CMs (hPSC-CMs), it is possible to overcome limitations in the supply of primary CMs and differences due to heterogeneity.1,2 Due to rapid increase in demand for hPSC-CMs, most differentiation protocols have now been established and commercialized. This could improve areas of cardiac research, including heart disease modeling, cell therapy, and cardiotoxic drug screening.1,3 In the field of drug-induced cardiotoxicity, to prevent loss of new drugs that are indiscriminately withdrawn, the FDA has proposed a revised cardiovascular pharmacology test project.4,5 The comprehensive in vitro proarrhythmia assay (CiPA) project recommends the use of hPSC-CMs.5 The use of hPSC-CMs has overcome limitations of drug reactivity to single ion channels based on the hERG assay of the existing ICH S7B/E14.6 However, despite the wide versatility of hPSC-CM, it has a limitation in that in vitro differentiated CM is very immature compared to in vivo cardiac CM.7 Immature hPSC-CMs exhibit low drug reactivity due to immature ion channel development, which causes a very serious problem in that the reliability of Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR), an essential procedure for drug development, is lowered.4,7,8 Therefore, it is necessary to develop a technology that can produce highly mature hPSC-CMs in vitro for the field of improved heart research.

During maturation of CMs in the heart, neonatal CMs undergo extensive morphological, metabolic and functional maturation processes until they reach adulthood.4 There are differences between immature and mature CMs. Morphologically, when immature CM matures, DNA synthesis proceeds once more without cell division, resulting in a 10-20-fold increase in cell volume. The heart also enlarges through the development of a highly ordered sarcomeric structure with an increase in the volume of CMs.7,9,10 In terms of functional changes, electrophysiological signals of CMs generated by potential difference according to the flow of ions also undergo functional changes as the concentration gradient changes through maturation of ion channels (including SCN5A, KCNJ2, CACNA1C, RYR2, and ATP2A2).7,11,12 Maturation of in vivo CMs begins at the developmental stage of the heart. The developing heart relies on glycolysis-based metabolism in utero.13,14 However, after birth, intake of lipid-rich maternal milk accelerates metabolic conversion from lactate oxidation to fatty acid oxidation.14 A metabolic switch with exposure to elevated oxygen levels results in an acceleration of oxidative metabolism requiring higher amounts of ATP, which is met by an increase in the number and activation of mitochondria (accounting for 20%-40% of the volume of CMs) in CMs.7,15 It has been reported that the increase in mitochondrial biogenesis and mitochondrial respiration capacity is closely related to peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling and PPAR-α/PPAR-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α).16,17 Such dynamic regulation of PPARα-PGC-1α complex activity in the heart is known to play an important role in failing, diabetic, and developing hearts.16 Pathological hypertrophy can lead to lipid accumulation due to reduced PPAR-PCG1 axis and reduced dependence on oxidative mitochondrial metabolism.16 Conversely, it has been reported that the expression of the PPAR-PCG-1 axis is increased in a postnatal heart than in a fetal heart.16 Studies identifying mechanisms related to heart development and maturation have been reported steadily from the past to the present, investigating whether signaling such as PPARα regulation, which can lead to mitochondrial activation, can be reflected in hPSC-CMs to help advance cardiac research.

In this study, we investigated changes in the maturation of hPSC-CMs according to regulation of PPARα. We selected fenofibrate (Feno), a representative hyperlipidemia drug and a PPARα agonist, and demonstrated that administration of Feno could promote structural/mitochondrial metabolism/functional maturation of hPSC-CMs. Furthermore, most reports have focused on revealing the maturation mechanism of CM. However, we performed cardiotoxicity evaluation of matured CMs through multi-electrode array (MEA)-based electrophysiological analysis using hPSC-CM presented by CiPA. Interestingly, although the same dose of proarrhythmia drugs was used to treat control (CTL) and Feno-treated groups, different numerical values were presented. This is the first study to evaluate drug reactivity using mature hPSC-CMs. Results of this study have not been seen in previously reported in vitro maturation promotion studies.

Materials and methods

Meta-analysis using mRNA seq data

The GEO dataset was investigated to analyze gene profiling related to cardiac maturation during human fetal to adult development. A reported GEO dataset containing fetal heart (Z6583N) and adult hearts (AM7966 and R1234138) was selected and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. PCA was performed through the R 4.2.1 program and was separated into 2 clusters using the K-Means tool. Next, gene expression patterns of cardiac structure genes, cardiac ion regulatory genes, and metabolism genes between fetal and adult hearts were analyzed through heat map and hierarchical clustering of MeV program.

Cell culture

Human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) (WA09, H9; WiCell) were cultured in StemMACS iPS-Brew XF medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) in 5% CO2 37 °C incubator. Subculture was performed when confluence reached approximately 90% on days 3-4 of culture. During the subculture, hESCs were detached by treatment with 0.5 mM EDTA (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), and the detached cells were seeded in hESC-qualified matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) coated culture dishes (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) using StemMACS iPS-Brew XF medium containing 10 µM Y27632 (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), a ROCK inhibitor. hESCs were performed by procedures approved by Institutional Review Board (IRB) at The Konkuk University Medical Center of Korea (approval no. KUH1280080).

Generation of hPSC-CMs and induction of maturation via Fenofibrate treatment

Differentiation into CMs was induced based on previous reports.18 In detail, when the density of hPSCs reached 90% or more in the culture dish, it was treated with RPMI1640 (Gibco) + B27 minus insulin supplement (B27(−); Gibco) medium containing 6 μM CHIR99021 (R&D systems) for 2 days. After 2 days, cells were replaced with RPMI1640 + B27(−) medium containing 2 μM C-59 (Selleckchem) for 2 days. On day 4, it was replaced with advanced MEM (AD-MEM) containing 1% GlutaMax and 1% anti-anti, and cultured for 6 days, replacing the medium every 2 days. When beating started on day 10, the cells were detached using TrypLE (Gibco) and re-seeded on matrigel-coated dishes to synchronize hPSC-CMs for 2 days. To remove non-CMs, purification was performed for 2 days using glucose-free RPMI1640 (Gibco) + B27 supplement (B27(+); Gibco) medium containing 4 mM sodium lactate. To induce maturation of hPSC-CMs on day 14, fresh AD-MEM medium was replaced every 2 days and cultured up to day 20. During maturation for 6 days, 10 μM fenofibrate (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), a PPARα agonist was added to AD-MEM to further promote the maturation of hPSC-CMs. Furthermore, to clarify whether the maturation mechanism of hPSC-CMs is related to PPARα, 10 μM GW6471 (Sigma-Aldrich), an antagonist of PPARα, was treated with Fenofibrate.

Western blot

hPSC-CMs were harvest and lysed in RIPA buffer. For protein quantification, BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were prepared by boiling the equal amount of protein (25 μg) together with the sample buffer at 98 °C for 10 minutes. Samples were resolved on SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Then, the membranes were blocked by 5% skim milk for 1 hour at RT and then incubated with primary antibodies for O/N at 4 °C. As primary antibodies, 1:1000 anti PPARα (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, INC., Dallas, TX, USA), 1:1000 anti-Mitofusin1 (MFN1; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and 1:1000 anti-Mitofusin2 (MFN2; Cell Signaling Technology) were used. After O/N, washing using Tris-buffered saline including 0.1% Tween 20 (1× TBST) was performed 3 times for 10 minutes each. Then, the membranes were incubated with respective secondary antibodies (Cell signaling Technology) for 2 hours at RT. The protein was detected through ECL (Invitrogen) and visualized through iBright CL1000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

hPSC-CMs were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 minutes at 4 °C. For permeabilization and blocking, PBS containing 0.03% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 3% normal goat serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the fixed hPSC-CMs and incubated at RT for 30 minutes. Next, cells were stained with primary antibodies O/N at 4 °C. As primary antibodies, 1:200 anti-α-actinin (Sigma-Aldrich, A7811), 1:200 anti-cardiac troponin T (cTnT; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA; ab45932), and 1:200 anti-human Mitochondria (hMito; Abcam) were used. After O/N cells were incubated with secondary antibodies on a shaker at RT for 2 hours. As secondary antibodies, 1:1000 Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-Mouse IgG (Invitrogen) and 1:1000 Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-Rabbit IgG (Invitrogen) were used. Nuclei were stained with 1:1000 DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, Nikon TE2000-U (Nikon; Japan), and quantification and analysis were performed using ImageJ. Cell area and multinucleation were analyzed for hPSC-CMs from 3 or more random images in each group. The sarcomere length was measured by measuring the length of 6 z-disks at once through a-actinin staining and dividing the length between each z-disk.

Mitochondrial bioenergetic analysis

After differentiation and maturation, the hPSC-CMs of the CTL and Feno groups were seeded in an XF e96-well plate (Seahorse Biosciences, North Billerica, MA, USA) at 3 × 104 cells/well. The AD-MEM was changed every 2 days and stabilized for 5-7 days until beating in 5% CO2 37 °C incubator. For calibration prior to measurement, XF calibrant medium (Seahorse Biosciences) was injected for cartridge hydrate and incubated O/N in a non-CO2 37 °C incubator. For measurement, the medium in the XF e96-well plate in which the cells were seeded was removed and washed once with PBS. Then, the medium was replaced with XF RPMI medium containing 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 1% glutamax, and incubated in a non-CO2 37 °C incubator for 1 hour. For 1 hour of incubation, 2.5 μM oligomycin was injected into the A port of the cartridge, 2 μM FCCP into the B port, and 0.5 μM Rot/AA into the C port, respectively, and mounted on the XF Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Biosciences). After injecting the XF e96-well plate into the XF analyzer, a program was performed to inject drugs in the order of each port, and the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured. Data were quantified with seahorse software.

Multi-electrode assay

Before seeding hPSC-CMs of CTL and Feno groups, 50 μg/mL fibronectin (Sigma-Aldrich) was coated in the form of a 6-μL drop for 1 hour in the center of an MEA 24-well plate with an electrode. After coating, fibronectin was suctioned and 5 × 104 cells were seeded per well in the form of a 6-μL drop for 1 hour 30 minutes. After confirming that the hPSC-CMs are attached, 500 μL AD-MEM is added, the medium is replaced every 2 days for 7 days, and the culture is performed on 5% CO2 37 °C incubator. After 7 days, the hPSC-CMs seeded MEA plates were directly transferred to the MEA device Maestro Edge version (Axion BioSystems Inc., Atlanta, GA, USA) for measurement. Environmental controls (37 °C and 5% CO2) were used to maintain temperature and pH. After 30 minutes of stabilization in the MEA device, FPs of CTL and Feno were recorded for 5 minutes. In order to ensure the reliability of experimental results by minimizing lot variation, we set standards for functional quality of control (QC) of hPSC-CMs through MEA and performed analysis of groups that met the standards as much as possible. The QC standards established based on hPSC-CMs at day 20 are (1) FPD: 150-300 ms, (2) beat period (BP): 20-60 bpm, (3) spike amplitude (SA): > 150 μV. Furthermore, in order to compare drug reactivity according to maturity between groups, ion channel blockers capable of inducing cardiac arrhythmias were prepared. The drug was prepared at 10 folds the final dose, 10% of the medium volume was removed from each well, and the same volume of drug was added. The final dose of DMSO in the well was <0.1%, and nifedipine and E-4031 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. FP was recorded for 5 minutes after 25 minutes of drug injection, and the high-dose drug prepared in the same way was replaced for dose-dependent evaluation. The raw data of FP recorded in AxIS software were analyzed using Cardiac Analysis Tool and AxIS Metric Plotting Tool (Axion BioSystems Inc.).18

siRNA transfection

Both siRNA against CTL (siCTL; sc-37007) and siRNA targeting MFN1 (siMFN1; sc-43927) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Transfection was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen). After diluting 50 nM siRNA and 25 μL lipofectamine RNAiMAX in AD-MEM, the 2 samples were mixed and incubated for 5 minutes. Then, the mixture for siRNA transfection was added to AD-MEM medium containing 10 μM fenofibrate and incubated for 5 minutes. Finally, the final mixture medium was added to the hPSC-CMs after metabolic selection, and transfection was performed for 6 days with replacement every 2 days. After transfection was completed, sampling and analysis were performed (Supplementary Figure 2A).

Statistical analysis

All the in vitro experiments were performed at least 3 times. Statistical analyses were performed with Student’s t test and ANOVA one-way for comparisons between groups. Quantitative data are presented as the mean values ± SEM. A P-value (*P < .05, **P < .01, and ***P < .001) was considered statistically significant. Data histograms were fit with GraphPad Prism, version 5.

Results

Gene profiling of in vivo human fetal heart and adult heart through meta-analysis

To investigate changes of gene expression patterns during development and maturation from a human fetal heart to an adult heart, we performed a meta-analysis based on the previously reported GEO dataset (Figure 1A). As a result of clustering through principal component analysis (PCA) of one fetal heart sample (Z6583N) and 2 fetal heart samples (AM7966 and R1234138). The 2 adult heart samples were grouped into the same cluster (group B). They showed distinct differences from the fetal heart (group A; Figure 1B). In addition, we compared changes in main indicators related to cardiac maturation, including cardiac structure genes, cardiac ion regulatory genes, and metabolism genes, using heat maps and hierarchical clustering between groups (Figure 1C-E). Similar to previous reports showing that maturation of the heart is accompanied by maturation of cardiac structures and ion channels,7,19 meta-analysis results confirmed that most cardiac structure genes (ACTC1, MYH7, MYL2, TNNI3, TNNT2, etc.) and cardiac ion regulatory genes (KCNH2, KCNJ8, RYR2, TRDN, CASQ2, S100A1, etc.) were expressed higher in the adult heart than in the fetal heart (Figure 1C and D). On the other hand, among cardiac structure genes, TNNI1 is known to be downregulated as CMs mature.20 Its expression was found to be significantly lower in the adult heart than in the fetal heart (Figure 1C). Furthermore, as a result of comparing genes related to the development of metabolism, which is important in cardiac maturation, it was confirmed that most genes related to PPAR signaling (PPARα, PPARδ, PPARγ, PPARGC1α, etc.), mitochondrial (COX41, COX6A2, CKMT2, MFN2, etc), and fatty acid oxidation (CPT1B, FABP3, LPL, etc.) were expressed higher in the adult heart than in the fetal heart (Figure 1E). Among PPAR signaling molecules, PPARα and PPARGC1a (PGC1α), a co-activator of PPARα/γ, were particularly highly expressed in adult hearts (Figure 1E). These results confirm that expression patterns of cardiac structure, ion regulatory, and metabolic genes would change as the heart matures from fetal to adult. These results also show that PPARα signaling is closely related to cardiac maturation.

Treatment with Fenofibrate, a PPARα agonist, promotes maturation of hPSC-CMs

The protocol for promoting maturation of human pluripotent stem cells derived cardiomyocytes (hPSC-CMs) was improved based on a previously established in-house protocol for differentiating hPSCs to CMs.18 hPSCs were induced to mesoderm by treatment with CHIR99021, a GSK3β inhibitor, for 2 days and then to cardiac progenitor cells (CPC) by treatment with C-59, a Wnt inhibitor, for 2 days. Next, differentiated cells were cultured in AD-MEM medium for differentiation into CMs. It was confirmed that beating occurred on day 10. Although beating occurred, since non-CM was still present, differentiated cells were reseeded for purification and metabolic selection was performed for 2 days with a glucose-free RPMI medium containing B27 supplement and sodium lactate. Purified hPSC-CMs were maintained and cultured in AD-MEM to induce maturation. A group treated with only AD-MEM was set as a control (CTL). The AD-MEM medium containing 10 μM Fenofibrate (PPARα agonist) for maturation promotion was replaced every 2 for 6 days. This group was set as the Feno group (Figure 2A). First, to verify the purity of hPSC-CMs, differentiation was performed under 4 conditions [Condition 1: non purification (without sodium lactate) CTL, Condition 2: non purification (without sodium lactate) Feno, Condition 3: Purification (with sodium lactate) CTL, and Condition 4: Purification (with sodium lactate) Feno] and analyzed through FACS. As a result, close to 90% of cTnT positive cells (hPSC-CMs) were distributed in all groups regardless of the presence or absence of sodium lactate (Supplementary Figure S1). According to previous studies, maturation occurs when hPSC-CMs are cultured in vitro for a long period of time. We compared protein expression levels of PPARα between hPSC-CMs cultured for 20 days (D20 group) and 40 days (D40 group) to determine whether PPARα was related to the maturation of hPSC-CMs. As a result, it was confirmed that the D40 group had higher PPARα expression than the D20 group (Figure 2B). Next, WB was performed to determine whether Feno treatment actually enhanced PPARα expression. Results revealed that PPARa protein was expressed more in the Feno group than in the CTL group (Figure 2C and D). Taken together, these results shown in Figures 1 and 2B confirm that PPARα is related to cardiomyocyte maturation and that Feno is effective as a PPARα agonist in hPSC-CMs.

Feno promotes structural maturation of hPSC-CMs

When mouse fetal CMs on postnatal day 6 (P6) and P20 were compared, structurally different features were shown. Mouse fetal CMs on P20 showed increased inter-Z-line distance (often called sarcomere length) with a clear banding sarcomere Z-line.21 In addition, as a result of various studies (mechanical stimulation, fatty acid treatment, chemical treatment, etc.) using hPSC-CMs to induce in vitro maturation, structural maturation indicators of CMs such as cell area, multinucleation, and sarcomere length will increase.7,22-24 Based on these reported indicators, co-staining of cTnT (TRITC) and α-actinin (FITC), which are CMs-specific markers, was performed to confirm whether structural maturation of hPSC-CMs was accelerated by Feno treatment. Results are shown in Figure 3. Cell area was confirmed to be increased more in Feno (2688.04 ± 265.79 μm2; n = 83) than in CTL (1801.39 ± 265.76 μm2; n = 96) (Figure 3A and B). Multinucleation was also more distributed in hPSC-CMs of the Feno group (41.15 ± 4.90%; n = 9) than in CTL (29.02 ± 2.92%; n = 9) (Figure 3A and C). As a result of comparing α-actinin, which represents the Z-disk of CMs, Feno showed clearer Z-disk banding than CTL (Figure 3A). Sarcomere length was also significantly increased in Feno (1.87 ± 0.04 μm; n = 62) than in CTL (1.72 ± 0.04 μm; n = 42) (Figure 3D). These results demonstrate that Feno treatment can promote structural maturation of hPSC-CMs.

Magnified image for observation of sarcomere structure. Scale bar = 50 μm. Comparison of (B) cell area, (C) multinucleation, and (D) sarcomere length between CTL and Feno. Data are presented as mean values ± SEM. *P < .05.

Feno promotes mitochondrial metabolism maturation of hPSC-CMs

It has been reported that mitochondrial maturation in CMs is closely related to cardiac maturation through metabolic changes. Mature mitochondria are enlarged morphologically. They show increases in number and size up to 40% of the volume in CMs.21 Based on this evidence, we stained with hMito (Green) to compare the number and distribution of mitochondria in CTL and Feno groups (Figure 4A). As a result of hMitochondria staining, Feno showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity (more than 4 folds) than CTL (Figure 4B). Based on the increased distribution of mitochondria in CMs, protein levels related to mitochondrial maturation were analyzed. Results are shown in Figure 4C-E. Immature mitochondria undergo mitochondria fusion through activation of MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 with upregulation of AMPK and PGC-1α/PPAR. It has been reported to play an important role in mitochondria-related heart development through functional maturation of mitochondria in stages of development from human embryonic to fetal heart.25,26 Feno treatment significantly upregulated MFN1 (Figure 4C and E), but not MFN2 (Figure 4D and E). Furthermore, oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was evaluated to determine whether Feno treatment could lead to metabolic maturation through mitochondrial maturation in hPSC-CMs (Figure 4F).23 As a result of OCR measurement, basal respiration was approximately 3 folds higher in Feno (34.22 ± 3.21 pmol/minute) than in CTL (11.19 ± 5.95 pmol/minute). In addition, Feno (30.19 ± 2.87 pmol/minute) showed higher ATP production (approximately 3 folds higher) than CTL (10.40 ± 4.83 pmol/minute). Feno (111.93 ± 9.46 pmol/minute) also showed significantly higher (approximately 4 folds higher) maximal respiration than CTL (26.99 ± 20.20 pmol/minute). Taken together, these results demonstrate that Feno not only can lead to morphological development of mitochondria in hPSC-CMs but also can lead to functional mitochondrial metabolic maturation.

Feno promotes electrophysiological signal maturation of hPSC-CMs

Immature hPSC-CMs show different electrophysiological signals compared to adult CMs due to their lower activity levels of ion channels that control the flow of ions such as Na+, K+, and Ca.2+27 To analyze electrophysiological signals, field potential (FP) was measured using an electrocardiogram (ECG) system-based multi-electrode array (MEA). Main parameters of FP reflecting depolarization/repolarization coupling are: (1) field potential duration (FPD), which indicates the QT interval of ECG, (2) BP, the interval at which the next depolarization occurs, and (3) SA, which indicates the potential difference generated by the flow of ions during depolarization (Figure 5A).18 As a result of FP measurement between CTL and Feno groups, both groups showed regular depolarization/repolarization coupling. However, they showed different patterns of raw waveforms (Figure 5B). FPD did not show a difference between CTL (0.20 ± 0.01 s) and Feno (0.19 ± 0.01 s) (Figure 5C). BP did not show a difference between CTL (1.15 ± 0.05 s) and Feno (1.18 ± 0.02 s) either (Figure 5D). However, in the case of SA, as shown in the raw waveform (Figure 5B), a higher SA value was detected in Feno (1.68 ± 0.38 mV) than in the CTL (0.36 ± 0.11 mV) (Figure 5E). SA occurrence pattern was also induced to be high in Feno than in the CTL (Figure 5F). SA from FP is similar to SA from patch-clamp-based action potential. Results of AP measurement also showed a similar tendency to immature hPSC-CMs having lower amplitude than maturation CMs due to insufficient expression of ion channels.7,18,28,29 These results demonstrate that Feno can promote both functional maturation and morphological maturation of hPSC-CMs.

Verification of PPARα correlation in maturation via treatment with both Feno and GW6471, a PPARα antagonist

We demonstrated that Feno, a PPARα agonist, could promote structural, mitochondrial metabolism, and electrophysiological signal maturation of hPSC-CMs (Figures 2-5). Whether upregulation of PPARα by Feno treatment directly promoted maturation of hPSC-CMs was then determined by treating hPSC-CMs with both GW6471, a PPARα antagonist, and Feno (GW + F) (Figure 6).30 To verify drug reactivity, PPARα protein expression was determined. It was found that the increase of PPARα expression caused by Feno compared to CTL was decreased in the GW + F group (Figure 6A). To verify the offsetting effect of Feno according to co-treatment of GW in terms of structural maturation, a-actinin and cTnT were co-stained (Figure 6B). Cell area, multinucleation, and sarcomere length of each group were then quantified (Figure 6C). Staining results showed that cell area (CTL: 3211.89 ± 242.52 μm2; n = 92 vs. Feno: 4789.53 ± 273.13 μm2; n = 101 vs. GW + F: 3475.38 ± 247.83 μm2; n = 89), multinucleation (CTL: 23.33 ± 5.25%; n = 6 vs. Feno: 49.83 ± 3.90%; n = 6 vs. GW + F: 34.57 ± 2.99%; n = 7), and sarcomere length (CTL: 1.54 ± 0.04 μm; n = 27 vs. Feno: 1.79 ± 0.04 μm; n = 27 vs. GW + F: 1.46 ± 0.04 μm; n = 29) significantly increased by Feno treatment compared to CTL were significantly decreased in GW + F, showing levels similar to those of CTL (Figure 6B and C). Next, MFN protein expression and OCR analysis were performed to verify the offset effect between groups in terms of maturation of mitochondrial metabolism (Figure 6D-F). As a result of comparing expression levels of MFN1 and MFN2 (factors related to mitochondrial metabolism maturation), it was revealed that the expression of MFN1, which was increased by Feno treatment compared to CTL, was remarkably decreased in GW + F, whereas MFN2 showed no difference in expression pattern among all groups (Figure 6D). In OCR analysis results, OCR parameters including basal respiration (CTL: 30.98 ± 1.29 pmol/minute vs. Feno: 45.78 ± 2.25 pmol/minute vs. GW + F: 32.26 ± 2.46 pmol/minute), ATP production (CTL: 25.81 ± 1.07 pmol/minute vs. Feno: 37.05 ± 1.84 pmol/minute vs. GW + F: 25.52 ± 2.01 pmol/minute), and maximal respiration (CTL: 47 ± 5.33 pmol/minute vs. Feno: 104.27 ± 8.26 pmol/minute vs. GW + F: 46.37 ± 6.97 pmol/minute) significantly increased by Feno were decreased to levels similar to those of CTL in GW + F (Figure 6E and F). Furthermore, MEA analysis between groups was performed to determine whether by GW had an offset effect on the maturation of electrophysiological signals (Figure 6G and H). As a result, there was still no difference between FPD and BP, although increased SA (CTL: 0.21 ± 0.03 μV vs. Feno: 0.75 ± 0.08 μV vs. GW + F: 0.30 ± 0.04 μV) in Feno decreased to a level similar to that of the CTL in GW + F (Figure 6G and H). Taken together, these results indicate that co-treatment with GW6471 could offset the effect of Feno in promoting maturation of hPSC-CMs, proving that PPARα is a close mediating factor in the maturation of hPSC-CMs.

Furthermore, to confirm whether the activation of MFN1 that occurred along with the upregulation of PPARα by Feno was also related to the maturation of hPSC-CMs, MFN1 knockdown was performed through siRNA transfection (Supplementary Figure S2A). It was confirmed that the expression of MFN1 was reduced in Feno + siMFN1 than in Feno + siCTL (Supplementary Figure S2B), and these results demonstrated that the morphological maturation parameters improved by Feno were significantly reduced by MFN1 knockdown (Supplementary Figure S2C-F). This suggests that not only upregulation of PPARα by Feno but also activation of MFN1 is related to the maturation of hPSC-CMs.

Analysis of drug reactivity between CTL and Feno through MEA

To determine whether there was a difference in drug reactivity between immature and mature hPSC-CMs, we performed MEA-based cardiotoxicity evaluation proposed by CiPA in accordance with the recent trend. Nifedipine known as an L-type Ca2 + channel blocker and E4031 known as a hERG (K+) channel blocker were selected as arrhythmia-inducing drugs for cardiotoxicity evaluation.31 MEA analysis was performed based on the protocol reported previously.18 Main parameters of MEA-based FP analysis are presented in 2 patterns. As a result of nifedipine (L-type Ca2 + channel blocker) analysis between CTL and Feno groups, it was found that FPD and BP levels were decreased by nifedipine in both groups in a dose-dependent manner (0.1 and 0.3 μM) (Figure 7A). Interestingly, FPD and BP, which did not differ at baseline between groups, showed different values after cells were treated with nifedipine (Figure 7B-D). At nifedipine 0.3 μM, the FPD (CTL: 87.91 ± 2.48 mS vs. Feno: 75.79 ± 2.19 mS; CTL: -52.73 ± 1.55% vs. Feno: -59.92 ± 1.16%) was significantly more reduced in Feno than in CTL (Figure 7B). On the contrary, at nifedipine 0.1 μM, the BP (CTL: 567.81 ± 11.21 mS vs. Feno: 597.40 ± 7.24 mS; CTL: -32.48 ± 0.91% vs. Feno: -26.82 ± 0.54%) was significantly more decreased in CTL than in Feno (Figure 7C). SA did not change regardless of nifedipine treatment or dose, showing no significant difference between groups (Figure 7D). As a result of E4031 analysis, FPD and BP increased as the dose of E4031 (0.03, 0.1, and 0.3 μM) increased in both groups. On the contrary, SA decreased in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7E). There was no significant difference in FPD between groups (Figure 7F). However, there were significant differences in BP and SA (Figure 7G and H). At E4031 0.1 μM, BP (CTL: 1227.08 ± 33.80 mS vs. Feno: 1098.75 ± 36.46 mS; CTL: 27.93 ± 7.51% vs. Feno: 16.78 ± 4.21%) of Feno was increased much less than that of CTL. Interestingly, SA (0.03 μM_CTL: -39.50 ± 8.24% vs. Feno: -14.20 ± 4.24%; 0.1 μM_CTL: -54.59 ± 3.11% vs. Feno: -25.85 ± 5.82%; 0.3 μM_CTL: -66.46 ± 2.96% vs. Feno: -54.25 ± 3.77%) decreased rapidly from 0.03 μM in CTL and further decreased in a dose-dependent manner, while in Feno, it was confirmed that it gradually decreased from 0.03 μM to 0.3 μM (Figure 7G). In addition, there were significant differences between groups at all doses of E4031 (Figure 7H). These results revealed that nifedipine and E4031 treatment showed similar drug response trends. However, there were numerical differences in main parameters of FP between groups.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that treatment with Feno, a hyperlipidemia drug, and a PPARα agonist accelerated the maturation of hPSC-CMs and that mature hPSC-CMs showed different drug reactivity than immature hPSC-CMs. We verified that PPARα was closely related to the maturation of CMs through results including (1) structural maturation (such as increases of cell area, multinucleation, and sarcomere length), (2) mitochondrial metabolism maturation (such as increases of MFN1 expression, number of mitochondria, and ATP production), and (3) electrophysiological signals (such as increased SA). In addition, as a result of MEA-based FP analysis after treatment of matured hPSC-CMs with proarrhythmia-induced drugs nifedipine and E-4031, nifedipine resulted in differences in FPD and BP whereas E-4031 resulted in differences in BP and SA.

As the importance of PPAR signaling, one of the signals mediating mitochondrial maturation and function, has been highlighted, in vitro maturation studies through regulation of PPAR expression in hPSC-CMs have been continuously reported. Murphy et al have reported that PGC-1/PPAR signaling through YAP1 and SF3B2 promotes PSC-CMs maturation at the single cell level.32 In the most recent report, Wickramashinghe et al have reported that PPARδ signaling plays a major role among PPARs in inducing the metabolic switch from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation in hPSC-CMs and that PPARδ promotes maturation of hPSC-CMs.33 In addition to direct regulation of PPAR signaling, it has been reported that treatment of hPSC-CMs with AICAR, an adenosine 5‘-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) agonist, can promote maturation.34 In hPSC-CMs matured by AICAR, expression levels of both PPARα and PGC1α are increased while the expression of phosphorylated AMPK (p-AMPK) is increased. This report indicates that AMPK, an enzyme that increases ATP production and decreases ATP consumption, is related to PPAR signaling while improving mitochondrial physiological functions in CMs.34,35 Feno used in our study has been reported to have pharmacological effects through improved metabolic functions by increasing the expression of p-AMPK and upregulating PPARα in CMs and other organ cells.36,37 These results are supported by the evidence that Feno, which is used clinically for treatment,38,39 can also promote maturation of hPSC-CMs. Furthermore, different expression patterns of mitochondrial fusion-related proteins have been observed in PSC-CMs matured with each signaling agonist treatment in previous reports. It has been reported that GW0742 (PPARδ agonist) can induce upregulation of MFN2 and AICAR (AMPK agonist) can induce upregulation of both MFN1 and MFN2.33,34 Interestingly, as shown in Figures 4 and 6, there was no change in MFN2 expression by Feno. However, as a result of inducing upregulation of MFN1, different side effects occurred depending on the drug even with similar signaling in the maturation of hPSC-CMs. Therefore, accumulation of a database through various studies for promoting maturation of CMs will bring us closer to understanding the mechanism of heart development in detail.

Most reports related to maturation of CMs show only functional maturation of CMs by performing patch clamp, calcium flux imaging, and measuring contractile force.7 Functionality such as drug reactivity and disease modeling of matured PSC-CMs has not been applied yet. In this study, functional maturation was evaluated based on MEA with a different approach (Figures 5 and 6). We tried to verify how drug reactivity was different according to maturation (Figure 7). As a result of patch clamp, the action potential amplitude and upstroke velocity of AP increase according to maturation.7 As a result of calcium imaging, increased amplitude of calcium transient has been reported.19 In addition, it has been confirmed that maturation of cardiac spheroid is promoted through a self-developed micropillar electrode array chip and that peak amplitude is increased as a result of measuring the electrophysiological signal.40 When cardiomyocytes (CMs) maturation is promoted, changes in electrophysiology and Ca2+ handling occur. In particular, calcium channels are essential for contractile function, and depolarization of the cardiac membrane induces an influx of Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into CMs, which increases the myoplasmic free Ca2+ concentration.41 Previous reports have shown that as CMs maturation is promoted, ventricular ion channels (eg, KCNJ2) increase, automatic ion channels (ex. HCN4) decrease, and Ca2+ handling channels (Cav1.2, RYR2, and SERCA2) increase.21 It has been reported that this change in gene pattern functionally leads to the enhancement of Ca2+ transients.21 These results can be explained by the phenomenon that the SA, which represents the peak of depolarization at the FP shown in Figure 5, increases in the Feno group due to accelerated maturation of hPSC-CMs. However, changes in BP and FPD were not observed. Next, as a result of treating proarrhythmia-induced drugs to compare drug reactivity, the tendency of drug reactivity between CTL and Feno was similar regardless of the dose. There were significant numerical differences in each parameter, although these differences were not large. When treated with nifedipine, increased BP at 0.1 μM and decreased FDP at 0.3 μM were observed in Feno than in CTL (Figure 7). When treated with E4031, Feno showed decreased SA at all doses and decreased BP at 0.1 μM than CTL (Figure 7). These results are thought to be related to the evolution of the electrophysiological phenotype established by the rebuilding of ion channels due to increase of Kir2.1 encoded by KCNJ2, increased expression levels of Ca2+ handling channels (such as RYR2 and SERCA), and decreases of automaticity ion channels (such as HCN4) in CMs during maturation.7 19 Compensation of ion flow due to the development of these ion channels is known to show different drug responses. Results of different drug reactivity in matured hPSC-CMs are expected to bring a new focus to the field of generalized hPSC-CMs-based cardiovascular pharmacology.

Although hPSC-CMs has not yet reached the maturity of adult CMs, various approaches such as mechanical/electrophysiological/signaling stimulation have been attempted. These active studies will lead to improvements in health and welfare by helping us develop new drugs and improve cardiovascular pharmacology tests.
