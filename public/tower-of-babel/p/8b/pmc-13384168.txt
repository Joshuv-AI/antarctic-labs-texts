Current evidence on radiofrequency denervation for chronic low back pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kamilla My Thanh Lê Truong, Esther Maas, Mikkel Mylius Rasmussen, Raymond Ostelo, Heikki Hurri, Maurits W van Tulder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the effects of radiofrequency (RF) denervation for chronic low back pain (LBP) from facet joint, sacroiliac joint, discogenic and non-specific pain.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesWe searched Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PsycINFO, ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP up to 18 February 2025 for randomised controlled trials (RCTs).Study selection, data extraction and synthesisPairs of reviewers independently selected studies, extracted data and assessed risk of bias (Cochrane RoB 2). Meta-analyses with clinically homogeneous studies were performed and certainty of evidence was assessed using Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation.Results55 RCTs (n=4394) were included; many outcomes were at some risk of bias. Low to moderate certainty evidence suggested small improvements in pain and function with RF denervation versus sham for facet joint pain, sacroiliac, discogenic and non-specific chronic LBP. None exceeded predefined minimally clinically important between-group differences, suggesting limited clinical relevance.ConclusionsRF denervation showed small improvements in pain and function compared with sham, steroid injection or standard care, but the effects did not reach clinically meaningful thresholds and the certainty of evidence was very low to moderate. Additional research is needed to clarify the effects of RF denervation and guide clinical decision-making.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42023460743.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP), defined as pain, muscle tension or stiffness from the costal margin to the inferior gluteal folds with or without leg pain,1 affects approximately 7.5% of the global population2—either in acute or chronic forms.3 LBP is a major public health concern due to its high prevalence, impact on individual well-being and socioeconomic burden.1 2 4 Roughly 90% of LBP cases are ‘non-specific’,5 meaning the cause of pain is unknown,6 and arise from any pain-generating spinal structure, including discs, vertebrae, facet or sacroiliac (SI) joints, muscles and ligaments.7 8 Most people improve with conservative treatment, but 5%–20% develop chronic LBP, defined as pain persisting for more than 3 months.8

The diagnosis of pain sources relies on clinical assessments and/or radiological examinations.6 8 9 Medial branch blocks (MBBs) are commonly used to identify pain generators such as the facet or SI joints10 by administering local anaesthetic around the medial branch nerve (MBN). If substantial pain relief occurs, the source is considered identified.1115 MBBs are recommended by The Spine Intervention Society16 prior to radiofrequency (RF) denervation to select appropriate patients.10 Pain derived from the intervertebral disc (discogenic) is commonly diagnosed using discography, in which contrast with or without local anaesthetics is injected into the nucleus pulposus under X-ray, CT or MRI.17 18

RF denervation has been used and modified for decades as a treatment for chronic LBP from the facet joint, SI joint or disc.8 14 15 19 20 The use of RF denervation in the USA has increased from 49 to 113 per 100 000 Medicare or commercially insured patients between 2007 and 2016.3 RF denervation provides pain relief by applying thermal energy through an electrode near nociceptive pathways, disrupting the transmission or modulation of nociceptive impulses from sensory nerve endings around spinal structures.21 It is used as a minimally invasive and low risk alternative to conservative therapies such as exercise, massage and spinal manipulation.6 15 Techniques have evolved to include variations in temperatures, targets, application time and approaches such as pulsed, cooled and endoscopically guided RF denervation.2224

Research on RF denervation has grown exponentially, with recent studies focusing on various techniques and temperature settings. A 2015 Cochrane review assessed the efficacy of RF denervation in sham-controlled trials.14 Subsequent reviews have investigated single source of chronic LBP and RF denervation compared with sham222531 with the latest 2022 review specifically addressing RF treatments for facet joint-derived chronic LBP.22

The objective of this systematic review is to assess the effects of RF denervation of any kind in patients with chronic LBP, compared with sham or other treatments, on pain intensity, function, health-related quality of life and global perceived effect (GPE).

Methods

We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) updated guideline statement for the reporting of systematic reviews.32 The methods used for this systematic review are based on recommendations of the Cochrane Back and Neck review group33 and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.34 Our protocol was published in the PROSPERO registry (CRD42023460743).

Data sources and searches

An experienced medical information specialist from Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam conducted systematic searches in the bibliographic databases Wiley/Cochrane Library, OVID/Medline, OVID/Embase, Ebsco/CINAHL, Ebsco/APA PsycINFO, ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP from inception to 18 February 2025. The following terms were used (including synonyms and closely related words) as index terms or free-text words: “Back pain”, “Radiofrequency treatment”, “Denervation”, “Clinical trials” (see complete search strings in online supplemental appendix 1). We checked the references of identified relevant articles and reviews. We consulted experts in the field of RF denervation to identify studies that might have been missed.

Data collection and analysis

Study selection

We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing RF denervation with other treatments or sham in patients with chronic LBP and a positive response to a diagnostic block or discography. Patients with acute trauma, fracture, malignancy or inflammatory disease were excluded. Crossover trials were included; however, only the data collected in the interval up to the crossover time was added. No language or date restrictions were used.

Two reviewers (KMTLT and EM) independently screened titles and abstracts and assessed full-text articles for eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when needed.

Data extraction

Pairs of review authors independently extracted the data, using a standardised form. We extracted the following data: characteristics of study design, population, intervention and procedure method, control, duration of follow-up, outcomes and results. When data were missing, we contacted the study authors to obtain the required information; however, no additional data were received. We used a consensus method to resolve disagreements, consulting with a third review author if disagreements persisted.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

We assessed the risk of bias (RoB) using the revised Cochrane RoB tool version 2,3335 which assesses each outcome across structured domains of trial design, conduct, and reporting. Two review authors (KMTLT and EM) independently assessed the RoB, resolving disagreements by consensus or by involving a third review author if disagreements persisted. For studies with potential conflicts of interest or selection bias, a neutral reviewer, either a systematic review specialist (RO)36 or spinal neurosurgical specialist (MMR),37 independently assessed the RoB. We scored the criteria as ‘high risk’, ‘low risk’ or ‘some concerns’. A study was judged to be of low risk if all domains were of low RoB. ‘Some concerns’ was defined as a trial having at least one domain as some concern but no high RoB for any other domain. Lastly, ‘high risk’ was assigned if at least one domain was of high RoB, or there were some concerns for multiple domains that substantially lowered the confidence in the result. Missing outcome data due to loss to follow-up were not imputed but considered in the RoB assessments. As all outcomes were PROMs assessed at all follow-up intervals, RoB was consistent across outcomes and time points.

Data synthesis and analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) V.5.38 Analyses were stratified by suspected pain sources—facet joints, SI joints, discs or non-specific chronic LBP with/without radicular pain (when a specific pain source was not identified)—since RF denervation methods varied by pain source. Furthermore, analyses were based on the types of interventions and comparisons, as well as outcomes and follow-up. Outcomes were classified as short-term (1 month), intermediate (greater than 1–6 months) or long-term (more than 6 months), with follow-up times assigned to the closest interval. Heterogeneity was assessed by χ² test, I2 and visual inspection of forest plots. We considered the data statistically homogeneous if χ² was not statistically significant; I2 was below 50% and CIs overlapping.

Although our study protocol specified standardised mean differences (MDs) for continuous outcomes, we calculated MDs for the primary outcomes pain, function and health status, since all studies measured their outcomes on the same scales, allowing more interpretable results while adhering to the random-effects model. All Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) or Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) scores were converted to scales ranging from 0 to 10, when necessary. All comparisons of function used the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) ranging from 0 to 100, while health status was assessed using EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level Instrument (EQ-5D-5L) ranging from −0.33 to 1 and Short-Form 36 ranging from 0 to 100. We expressed precision with 95% CIs. If SDs were not reported, we calculated these using reported values of the CI. If the CI was not available, we used SDs of baseline scores, or estimations of SDs based on other studies with the same population, treatment and score. The secondary outcome was GPE ranging from 1 to 7. The distinction between primary and secondary outcomes was not prespecified in the protocol.

When studies compared two intervention groups to a single control group, the number of participants in the control group was halved and allocated equally to each intervention group for analysis.

If meta-analysis was not possible, we described results from clinically comparable trials.

Assessment of certainty of evidence and clinical relevance

Overall certainty of evidence was assessed per outcome, comparison and follow-up using the adapted Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, and based on: (1) limitations in design (>25% of participants from high RoB studies), (2) inconsistency of results (severe heterogeneity (I2>50%) or inconsistent findings among studies), (3) indirectness in targeted population, interventions or outcomes differing those of interest, (4) imprecision of results across all studies measuring that specific outcome (total number of participants <400 for each outcome) and (5) publication bias. Starting from ‘high-certainty evidence’, we downgraded one level for each factor encountered.

Assessment of clinical relevance included whether characteristics of participants, interventions and treatment settings were described precisely enough to be comparable with those in practice. Further, we assessed whether clinically relevant outcomes were measured, if their effects were clinically important which was determined by a predefined minimally clinically important difference (MCID) for between-group difference, and if treatment benefits were worth the potential harms. MCID for pain was defined as at least 20 mm on a VAS or 2.5 point difference on an NRS39 and for function at least 10 points on ODI39 while for patient satisfaction with treatment outcome categories 1 (‘completely recovered’) and 2 (‘much improved’).39

Results

We identified 2960 unique records, reviewed 196 in full, and included 55 RCTs with 4394 participants (see figure 1) with chronic LBP from the facet joint (28 studies; n=2258),3637 4053 disc (9 studies; n=688),185461 SI joint (10 studies; n=782),376270 and non-specific (8 studies; n=606)377177 (see online supplemental appendix 2 for all characteristics of included studies). Included studies were from the Netherlands (n=11), the USA (n=12), Turkiye (n=7), South Korea (n=5), and 1 to 3 studies from Egypt, China, India, Germany, Canada, Spain, the UK, Iran, Denmark, Sweden and Norway. Pain was measured by 0–100 VAS in 28 studies or 0–10 NRS in 27 studies with follow-up, ranging from 2 days to 36 months. Function was measured in ODI ranging from 0 to 100 (n=35). The overall RoB assessment yielded 17 studies with low RoB, while 20 studies had some concerns and 18 had high RoB (see figure 2 and figure 3). All reported randomisation, but details on sequence generation and allocation concealment (n=20), blinding (n=14, including 4 unblinded studies), reasons for missing outcome data (n=9) or dropouts (n=30) were often unclear, and most lacked an available protocol or statistical plan. We assessed heterogeneity with several analyses showing moderate to high variability (I²>40%). We therefore applied random-effects models to all meta-analyses, while outcomes from single trials are reported narratively (see online supplemental appendix 4). 62 meta-analyses were conducted, of which five Summary of Findings tables were presented (see tables15).

Facet joint-derived chronic LBP

28 studies assessed RF denervation for facet joint-derived pain: thermal (TRF) (n=22), pulsed (PRF) (n=4)44 45 48 78 and cooled (CRF) (n=2),51 52 RF denervation. One study combined PRF with TRF denervation.40 Comparators included sham (n=7),3644 47 7982 steroid injection (n=6),42 45 48 49 83 standard care (n=2),37 41 other RF techniques (n=10)4043 51 53 78 8488 or variations in application time (n=1)/temperature (n=2).88 89 Six studies had a second comparator, either sham (n=2),78 84 another RF technique (n=2)45 46 or other treatments (n=4) such as cryoneurolysis36 and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation.88 Most studies targeted the MBN (n=25), while others targeted the facet joint capsule (n=2)84 86 or were unspecified (n=2).37 79 Application time and temperature ranged from 50 to 90 s at 70°C to 90°C for TRF, 240 to 360 s at 42°C for PRF and 165 s at 60°C for CRF.

Comparing TRF denervation with sham, low-certainty evidence (5 RCTs; n=300)36 47 50 78 82 suggested that facet joint RF denervation was effective in reducing pain intensity, as measured by the VAS, at 1 month (MD: −0.97; 95% CI −1.75 to −0.19), and at more than 6 months (5 RCTs; n=327; MD: −1.67; 95% CI −2.88 to −0.46).36 47 78 79 84 Low-certainty evidence (5 RCTs; n=378)36 50 80 81 84 showed minimal to no difference at 1 to 6 months follow-up (MD: −0.56; 95% CI −1.11 to −0.01). Regarding function, low-certainty evidence (1 RCT; n=60)78 found that TRF improved function, as assessed by the ODI at 1 month (MD −5.53; 95% CI −8.66 to −2.40) and moderate-certainty evidence (2 RCTs; n=137)36 78 at more than 6 months (MD: −2.62; 95% CI −5.02 to −0.22) compared with sham. Conversely, low-certainty evidence (1 RCT; n=77)36 found no improvement in function at 1 to 6 months (MD: −0.6; 95% CI −4.24 to 3.04) (see table 1).

Comparing TRF with other treatment modalities, very low-certainty evidence (2 RCTs; n=180)42 45 suggested that TRF was more effective on pain intensity than steroid injection at 1 month follow-up (MD: −2.23; 95% CI −2.38 to −2.08), at 1 to 6 months (3 RCTs; n=232; MD: −2.13; 95% CI −3.45 to −0.81),42 45 49 and more than 6 months (2 RCTs; n=180; MD: −2.65; 95% CI −3.43 to −1.88). Low-certainty evidence (1 RCT; n=52)49 showed no effects of TRF on function at 6 months (MD: 5.00; 95% CI −15.19 to 5.19) compared with steroid injection (see table 2).

Comparing TRF denervation with standard care, low-certainty evidence (1 RCT, n=209)37 indicated that RF denervation was more effective in decreasing pain at less than 1 month (MD: −0.75; 95% CI −1.31 to −0.19), and moderate-certainty evidence (2 RCTs; n=479)19 57 at 1 month (MD: −1.09; 95% CI −1.47 to −0.72) and 1 to 6 months (MD: −0.81; 95% CI −1.19 to −0.42). Very low-certainty evidence (1 RCT; n=270)41 also showed that function was improved by RF denervation at 1 month (MD: −3.50; 95% CI −6.59 to −0.41) and low-certainty evidence (2 RCTs; n=479)37 41 at 1 to 6 months (MD: −3.52; 95% CI −6.48 to −0.56).

SI joint-derived chronic LBP

10 studies assessed the effect of RF denervation in chronic LBP derived from the SI joint. Five studies compared TRF to sham,636568 one study to standard care37 and one to steroid block.67 Application time and temperature setting were 90 to 180 s at 60°C to 90°C in TRF denervation, while 360 s at 42°C for PRF and 90 s at 80°C for CRF denervation. Lateral Branch Nerve was the intended target in six of the studies,636568 70 while one study targeted MBN64 and the other three the SI joint without specification.37 62 69 TRF denervation compared with sham showed low-certainty evidence (3 RCTs, n=139)63 66 68 indicating no difference in pain reduction at 1 month (MD: −1.31; 95% CI −3.06 to 0.44), while low-certainty evidence (2 RCTs, n=68)65 66 showed a similar effect size but significant mean difference at 1 to 6 months (MD: −1.53; 95% CI −2.23 to −0.82). Results for function showed low-certainty evidence (2 RCTs, n=75)63 66 at 1 month (MD: −14.06; 95% CI −30.42 to 2.30) and low-certainty evidence (1 RCT, n=49)66 at 1 to 6 months (MD: −11.00; 95% CI −17.91 to −4.09) (see table 3).

Discogenic chronic LBP

Nine studies assessed the effect of RF denervation as a treatment for discogenic chronic LBP. Five studies compared RF denervation to sham,545761 while three studies compared it to standard care.37 55 56 RF denervation techniques varied in temperature (45°C–90°C), application time (60–360 s) and targets, which were the intervertebral disc,37 55 56 58 59 the basivertebral nerve (BVN)55 57 and the ramus communicans nerve (RCN).60 61 Comparing RF denervation with sham, low-certainty evidence (1 month: 2 RCTs, n=11656 58; 1 to 6 months: 3 RCTs, n=3345658) suggested that RF denervation was more effective in reducing VAS (0–10) at 1 and 1 to 6 months (MD: −1.21; 95% CI−2.00 to −0.43, MD: −0.90; 95% CI−1.45 to −0.36, respectively) and moderate-certainty evidence (6 months: 4 RCTs, n=348626466; >6 months: 2 RCTs, n=23957 59) at 6 months (MD: −1.24; 95% CI −1.78 to −0.70). A small insignificant improvement was found at more than 6 months (MD: −0.67; 95% CI −1.39 to 0.05). Similarly, low to moderate-certainty evidence showed no difference in function at 1 month (MD: −0.52; 95% CI −5.34 to 4.29), while a significant improvement in function was shown at 1 to 6 and 6 months (MD: −5.84; 95% CI −9.31 to −2.36, MD: −5.73; 95% CI −8.93 to −2.53, respectively) (see table 4).

Non-specific chronic LBP with or without radicular pain

Eight studies assessed the effect of RF denervation as a treatment for non-specific chronic LBP with or without radicular pain. TRF denervation was compared with sham (n=1),71 standard care (n=1)37 or PRF (n=1),75 while three studies compared PRF with an application time between 120 to 540 s and temperature of 42°C with sham.7375 Three studies compared different techniques of PRF,72 temperature89 and voltage.77 All studies targeted the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) except one study, which did not specify the target.37 TRF denervation compared with sham showed low-certainty evidence (1 RCT, n=50)74 of no difference in reduction of pain intensity at one and more than 6 months (MD: −0.88; 95% CI −3.98 to 2.22, MD: −2.24; 95% CI −6.35 to 1.87, respectively) and moderate-certainty evidence (2 RCTs, n=130)71 74 of no difference at 1 to 6 months (MD: 0.60; 95% CI −0.40 to 1.60). Low-certainty evidence (1 RCT, n=50)74 suggested a greater improvement in function at 1 and 1 to 6 months (MD: −14.45; 95% CI −17.12 to −11.78, MD: −23.45; 95% CI −27.03 to −19.87, respectively) (see table 5).

Secondary outcomes

No important differences in results were found in the secondary outcomes (health status and GPE). Comparisons yielded the same conclusions as in the primary outcomes (see online supplemental appendix 3).

Discussion

Main findings

For facet, SI and discogenic joint pain, low-certainty to moderate-certainty evidence suggested that RF denervation provided short-term and intermediate-term improvements in pain and function compared with sham, with very low-certainty evidence versus steroid for facet joint pain. In non-specific chronic LBP, low-certainty evidence indicated small improvements in pain and function versus standard care, but not versus sham. However, none of these effects reached the predefined MCIDs for pain (at least 20 mm on a VAS or 2.5 point difference on an NRS) or function (at least 10 points on ODI), indicating that, although some results were statistically significant, RF denervation is unlikely to provide clinically meaningful benefit in reducing pain intensity or improving functional outcomes in patients with chronic LBP.

Comparison of the methodologies of the included studies

The studies revealed methodological and clinical heterogeneity (see online supplemental appendix 2), which were taken into account in the RoB and GRADE assessments.

Patient selection criteria

There was considerable variability in diagnosing pain sources. For example, some studies on facet joint-derived chronic LBP applied different criteria to define chronicity (such as a minimum duration of 3, 6 or 12 months), as well as varied in how they defined the condition (medical history alone or with clinical examination findings). Pain intensity thresholds also differed, with only seven studies using a minimum NRS score of 3, 4, 5 or 736 40 43 52 80 84 88 while the other studies did not specify a minimum pain score or had no minimum. Similarly, studies on SI joint-derived chronic LBP differed in eligibility, with some using medical history and specific physical tests,3762 64 6870 while others defined it as axial pain below L566 or tenderness over the SI joint.63 These variations in diagnostic criteria, combined with a lack of a standardised definition for what constitutes chronic LBP, could affect the consistency of the results and the ability to draw generalised conclusions about facet joint-derived and SI joint-derived pain.

Furthermore, although all studies required a positive response to a diagnostic block (or discography), the technique and definition of a positive response varied widely, which decreased the homogeneity of the trials. There were different cut-off thresholds for a positive response: between 50% and 80% pain relief, at least a 2-point improvement on the NRS,82 or a description of a significant improvement as assessed by the participants.50 Additionally, six studies opted to include two diagnostic blocks administered at separate times,40 43 52 84 85 89 using either lidocaine or bupivacaine. In contrast, two studies used intra-articular diagnostic blocks44 86 rather than MBN blocks.

Intervention technique and target

Many studies (up to 84% of all included trials) were not comparable due to significant heterogeneity in intervention modalities, techniques and targets (ranging from RF on DRG to IDET, biacuplasty and pulsed RF), as well as differences in control groups.

Limitation or challenge

The heterogeneity in methodology reflects the wide variation in clinical practice, but also reflects the ongoing development in the field of RF denervation.

New developments in RF denervation research

In the 2015 Cochrane review, 13 studies compared RF denervation with sham, three with steroid injection and only five with alternative RF modalities or approaches. Among the 32 new studies, nine compared RF denervation with sham, five with steroid injection and 18 with other RF modalities and parameters, including variations in time and temperature, reflecting a shift towards refining and differentiating RF techniques for chronic LBP. Notably, 29% of the new studies were conducted in five emerging countries (China, India, Turkiye, Iran and Egypt), as opposed to 19% in the 2015 Cochrane review, highlighting a growing global interest in RF denervation for chronic LBP that is aligned with calls from the Global Spine Care initiative.9094

Comparison to the literature

The 2015 Cochrane review reported conflicting evidence on facet joint RF denervation, though moderate-certainty evidence supported short-term pain relief versus sham, and low-certainty evidence indicated improved function across short-term, intermediate-term and long-term follow-up.14 Since then, 32 new studies on RF denervation for chronic LBP have been published, and our findings align with those earlier conclusions.

Between 2017 and 2022, four systematic reviews were published on the effect of RF denervation in facet joint-derived chronic LBP.22 25 29 30 Al-Najjim et al25 found short-term pain relief and low-certainty functional improvements with tunnel vision (intermediate) and endoscopic guidance (short, intermediate, long), though intermediate-term pain results were conflicting, consistent with our moderate to low certainty findings. Contreras Lopez et al29 concluded that pulsed RF was less effective than TRF for pain and function, aligning with our low-certainty findings. Li et al22 found that TRF, endoscopic RF denervation and pulsed RF of DRG improved short-term pain over sham, while pulsed RF, cooled RF, endoscopic RF, and RF denervation of the facet joint capsule improved long-term outcomes. Janapala et al30 concluded that moderate-certainty evidence could support RF procedures over sham and other treatment modalities for short-term and long-term improvements. This aligns with our results, suggesting that RF denervation was more effective than sham in managing facet joint-derived chronic LBP.

For discogenic chronic LBP, Chappell et al26 found low to very-low certainty evidence that RF denervation had a small short-term positive effect, depending on the precise anatomical target of the procedure. This is consistent with our findings, in which low-certainty evidence showed that intradiscal RF denervation (Intradiscal Biacuplasty (IDB)/Percutaneous Intradiscal Radiofrequency Thermocoagulation (PIRFT)) was effective over short and intermediate term, compared with sham, while RF denervation of RCN showed insignificant short-term effect, but significant intermediate-term benefit.

Conger et al28 reviewed three RCTs and four observational studies on RF denervation of BVN for chronic LBP with Modic changes, finding moderate-certainty evidence for short-term reductions in pain and disability in appropriately selected patients, compared with standard care and sham.

Li et al22 reported that adverse effects following RF denervation were typically rare, mild, and self-limiting, with serious neurological complications being uncommon.23 Of the 55 studies included, only 5 documented neurological complications, 8 provided no data and 47 reported none; however, this contrasted with concerns raised by Chappell et al, Conger et al and the Cochrane review regarding under-reporting of adverse effects.142628

Strengths and limitations

This comprehensive overview of RF denervation included different sources of chronic LBP (facet joint, SI-joint, disc and non-specific chronic LBP). This has resulted in a large number of studies (n=55) and participants (n=4394). However, most of the studies included a small number of participants, and the variety in patient selection, diagnostic block, intervention technique, application time and temperature resulted in statistical and clinical heterogeneity, which was the main limitation. However, this variation reflects daily clinical practice. This review aimed to be inclusive, to better reflect real-world clinical practice and summarise all available studies on RF denervation, rather than being selective in order to give a limited, more specific overview of a specific type of RF denervation for a specific pain generator. Therefore, we included detailed information on the RF techniques used in the included studies and the reported adverse effects in online supplemental appendix 2. And although all participants received diagnostic blocks to determine the source of chronic LBP and to predict good outcome of RF denervation, there might have been a risk of false-positive. However, no reliable data can be found on the diagnostic accuracy or clinical utility of diagnostic facet joint blocks, SI joint blocks or selective nerve root blocks.7 11

Recommendations

We recommend additional RCTs with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up. Furthermore, we recommend a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanism and origin of chronic LBP prior to RF denervation. Chronic LBP is a heterogeneous diagnosis group and therefore we advocate for more studies on the validity/accuracy of diagnosing patients with different sources of chronic LBP before determining the outcome of RF denervation.

We do not know how RF denervation is applied in a multidisciplinary setting, or whether this facilitates physical activity and consequently speeds up recovery. Therefore, we recommend a more comprehensive view of how RF denervation is applied as part of a (multidisciplinary) chronic pain treatment plan.

Conclusions

RF denervation is a widely used intervention for chronic LBP, but the evidence presented in this study does not seem to support a routine use of it. This systematic review of 55 RCTs on the effect of RF denervation in managing specific sources of chronic LBP (facet joint pain, SI joint pain, discogenic pain) and non-specific chronic LBP with or without radicular pain showed very-low to moderate evidence for pain relief and improved function over short-term and intermediate-term compared with sham, steroid injections and standard care. Although some statistically significant improvements were observed, none reached the predefined MCIDs for pain or function, suggesting limited clinical benefit. Overall, the effects were rather small, the certainty of evidence often low and some heterogeneity was present. Larger studies with a low risk of bias are needed.
