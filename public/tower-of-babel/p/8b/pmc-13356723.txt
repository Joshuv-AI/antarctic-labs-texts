Establishment and efficacy of a novel point-of-care ultrasound curriculum for Emergency Medicine residents in an East African program
Authors: Said S Kilindimo, Evelyne G Mapunda, Winnie K Mdundo, Humphrey G Medarakini, Alphonce N Simbila, Robert Lindsay, Timothy Gleeson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs Emergency Medicine (EM) training expands in Africa, integration of comprehensive and sustainable point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) curricula and building expertise are important considerations. The objectives were to describe and assess the efficacy of a novel ultrasound curriculum for EM residents in Tanzania and POCUS quality assurance training for local faculty. Novel aspects included digital didactics and testing, the incorporation of ultrasound image storage and longitudinal image review using a cloud-based platform, and faculty training.MethodsThis was a pre/post intervention study involving first-year EM residents at Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences and local faculty. A new POCUS curriculum was developed and implemented, including digital didactics, testing, hands-on instruction, and independent scanning with longitudinal review of participants’ ultrasound studies. Local faculty experienced in POCUS completed quality assurance training. We analysed participants’ pre- and post-knowledge assessments, observed structured clinical exam performance, and evaluated ultrasound study quality and accuracy. Agreement between local faculty and fellowship-trained external faculty was analysed for ultrasound study quality scores and accuracy.ResultsA total of 17 first-year EM residents participated, along with five local faculty members. Altogether, 4557 ultrasound studies were performed and reviewed. The knowledge assessment scores for the residents increased by 38.9% (95% CI: 32.9–54.0). while OSCE scores increased by 40.7% (95% CI: 35.6–45.8). Using the ACEP 5-point scale, a comparison between the participants’ first 25% of independent ultrasound scans and their final 25% showed an improvement in overall study quality by 0.12 (95% CI: 0.06–0.18), and accuracy increased by 3.3% (95% CI: 1.3–5.3). The agreement between local faculty and fellowship-trained external faculty was excellent for study accuracy (Kappa 0.83, 95% CI: 0.80–0.86) and fair for overall image score (Kappa 0.30, 95% CI: 0.25–0.35).DiscussionEM trainees in Africa should be equipped with POCUS expertise through participation in structured, quality curricula. We implemented an effective curriculum that includes new technologies that facilitate key aspects of training such as independent scanning and longitudinal image review. Development of local faculty quality assurance skills through didactics and joint image review is feasible and important to program sustainability.Keywords: Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), Emergency medicine, Training, Curriculum, Low – and middle–income (LMIC), Tanzania

Introduction

Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) is recognized as an important clinical tool for Emergency Mmedicine (EM) providers and of significant utility in resource-limited settings where other imaging modalities may be less accessible [1]. POCUS use has expanded over the past two decades due to its wide range of applications, relative affordability, and portability [2,3]. There are multiple described applications for POCUS in emergency medicine, several of which are considered first-line studies due to diagnostic accuracy and lack of ionizing radiation [4].

In many countries POCUS programs are well-established in EM residencies and fellowships, across several specialties, and even incorporated into medical school curricula. However, in low-and-middle-income-countries (LMICs) resources such as administrative support, equipment, and local expertise are less commonly available [5,6]. As EM becomes more established in Africa, residents as emergency care providers are an important group to be trained because they are future experts in EM. After completing their training, graduates may be widely spread across their respective regions and countries where they can play key roles in disseminating knowledge and skills. Training programs developed for these settings should address local needs, ensure appropriate equipment is available, provide sufficient assessment of goals, and be culturally appropriate [7].

Ultrasound training programs in LMIC previously described in the literature mostly have relied on brief, intensive workshops and in-person didactics, frequently conducted by visiting content experts. Several studies have shown success, most often demonstrated by pre- and post-testing, and observed structured clinical examinations (OSCE) [[8], [9], [10]]. Two prior studies included faculty review of ultrasound images obtained by trainees [11,12]. To our knowledge, no prior studies have described the design, implementation, and efficacy of a POCUS training curriculum for an EM residency program in Africa or incorporated ultrasound quality assurance training for local faculty.

Advances in technology allow for new approaches to POCUS training. Video conference technology can be utilized for didactics, group discussions, and review of images/cases. Digital workflow platforms allow for independent scanning by training participants, storage of images to secure cloud networks, and timely review by content experts. Online resources provide access to digital recordings of lectures and technique videos, customizable online testing, teaching images/cases, and reading materials. In addition, the technology for tele-ultrasound – live ultrasound with trainees and trainers at different sites – has advanced significantly. Access to this technology and connectivity still presents a challenge in many environments, but barriers are becoming fewer [13,14].

In this study, we designed and implemented an introductory ultrasound training curriculum for novice EM residents in Tanzania while heavily utilizing new technologies. The description, feasibility, and efficacy of the program have the potential to guide future training and need not be limited to emergency medicine. This curriculum and research represent a new step in an evolving approach to ultrasound training in LMIC. While hands-on training is of key importance for ultrasound training and was included, this curriculum relied more heavily on new technology, asynchronous learning, longitudinal image review, and faculty training. The study aims were to describe and assess the efficacy of the developed curriculum. Outcomes included pre/post knowledge assessments, pre/post OSCE performance, ultrasound study quality and accuracy, and agreement of quality assurance between external and local faculty.

Methods

This was a pre/post intervention study assessing the efficacy of a POCUS curriculum for EM trainees through objective testing and longitudinal quality assurance of ultrasound studies. Additionally, image quality assurance agreement between local faculty and external faculty with POCUS expertise was evaluated. Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE 2.0) were followed [15].

Study setting

The ultrasound curriculum was designed at Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences (MUHAS) and implemented at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) – a large, national referral hospital in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. MUHAS hosts numerous medical training programs, including the EM residency, which was established in 2010 and remains the largest training program in the country. The Emergency Department at MNH is the first full-capacity public department in Tanzania and has an average of 45,000 patients annually with an admission rate of 65%. The department is staffed continuously by locally trained emergency physicians who oversee the care of patients and the training of interns, EM residents, and registrars.

Study participants

Participants in the curriculum were 17 MUHAS EM residents, all of whom were in their initial year of EM residency, and none had prior formal POCUS training. Five MUHAS EM faculty participated in POCUS quality assurance and image review training.

Study protocol

This curriculum took place over six months from March to August 2024 (Fig. 1). Faculty included five MUHAS EM specialists within residency leadership with an average of 4.9 years POCUS teaching experience, and two external faculty with ultrasound fellowship training.

Timeline of training elements and assessments.

During the initial curriculum development, a local needs assessment was performed, and several POCUS applications were identified as priority exam types based on common pathologies seen in the MNH Emergency Department. The exam types were Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (FAST), Lung, Cardiac, IVC, Renal, Gallbladder, Aorta, Pelvic, and lower extremity Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT). All resident participants completed an online pre-training knowledge assessment consisting of questions related to general principles of ultrasound and the priority exam types. This assessment was graded on a 0–100 scale.

The first phase of the training consisted of didactic sessions conducted via teleconference and recorded as unlisted videos hosted on a video streaming platform (YouTube, Inc.) to facilitate asynchronous learning. Additionally, videos demonstrating ultrasound exam techniques for all priority exam types and a related online multiple-choice quiz accompanied each didactic session. The quizzes focused on study indications, anatomy, image acquisition, image interpretation, identification of sonographic landmarks, pathology, and artifacts. The quizzes were posted on an online platform (Google Forms) and featured text and de-identified ultrasound images. Responses were scored (0–100-point scale) based on pre-assigned question values and were available for review by course faculty.

Before hands-on training, each participant completed an initial observed structured clinical exam (OSCE) for all priority exam types. Scores were recorded on standardized forms and on a scale of 0–100 with point values assigned for specific components (Appendix A). Subsequently, proctored hands-on training was conducted with each trainee. These sessions had a 3:1 trainee-to-instructor ratio. Hands-on sessions were conducted over five days and covered each of the priority exam types. During these sessions, a combination of standardized patient models and clinical patients in the emergency department were scanned.

Thereafter, participants performed independent scanning. They were required to perform a minimum of 25 proficient studies for each of the priority exam types and thus, a minimum of 225 total studies. Proficiency was defined as a score of ≥ 3 as per the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) ultrasound assessment scale (Appendix B). Furthermore, a pre-determined number of studies with positive findings was required (Appendix C). Handheld ultrasound probes (Butterfly Inc, Burlington, MA, USA) were used and all studies were transmitted to their secure cloud-based platform. This platform allowed the participants to record their studies, document their findings, and for faculty to perform image review/quality assurance. The initial interpretation of the ultrasound for pathological findings was reported by the trainee and the final interpretation and scoring were reported by the course faculty. Once a study was reviewed, feedback was sent to the trainee via automated email. Image quality, accuracy, and technical errors were reported. It was agreed that the review would occur for educational and training purposes only. Clinical and ultrasound instructors were available if a trainee had any immediate clinical questions about their findings as per usual practice at MNH.

Before undertaking a review of the participants’ ultrasound exams, quality assurance and image review training were conducted for local faculty. This training was facilitated by external faculty with emergency ultrasound fellowship training and a focused practice designation (FPD) through the American Board of Emergency Medicine (ABEM). Training consisted of eight hours of didactic sessions on the principles of ultrasound quality assurance and joint image review sessions, after which time the local faculty independently reviewed the participants’ scans.

Upon completion of independent scanning requirements, all participants repeated the online POCUS knowledge assessment and the OSCE for pre/post training comparison. Testing results were recorded in Microsoft Excel and stored on an encrypted laptop. Ultrasound studies and worksheets were stored on the Butterfly Network secure cloud platform for the purpose of faculty review. No patient information was recorded, and data was exported to Excel for analysis after the training. To track participant progress and provide feedback, participants were not anonymized until the completion of the training.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected and visualized using Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365, Version 2511 (Microsoft Corporation, USA). Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism, Version 10.4.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Continuous variables, including pre- and post-intervention OSCE and written examination scores, were analyzed using two-tailed paired t-tests. Diagnostic performance measures (sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy) were compared between the first and last study quarters using 2 × 2 chi-square tests with Yates’ continuity correction. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using the Wilson score method. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa and weighted kappa statistics, calculated with the GraphPad QuickCalcs web application (GraphPad Software, USA).

This study was approved by the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences institutional review board (Ref. No. DA.282/298/01.C/3044). No site-specific regulations affected the monitoring and reporting of this training program.

Results

All 17 first-year EM residents completed the training program. There were 5 women and 12 men, all of whom had limited prior experience with POCUS. None of the trainees had undertaken prior formal POCUS training and 8 of the 17 participants (47.1%) reported no prior POCUS instruction at all. A total of 4557 ultrasound studies were recorded and reviewed. The average number of studies completed by each participant was 268. Proficiency (ACEP score ≥ 3) was achieved for 93.6% of studies (Table 1).

A randomized subset of 1000 ultrasound studies were selected for comparison of image review scoring between local faculty and ultrasound fellowship-trained external faculty. There was excellent agreement between the reviewer groups for study accuracy with a Kappa of 0.83 and agreement in 91.6% of exams. Overall study score agreement was fair with a Kappa of 0.30 and a weighted Kappa of 0.38. There was agreement scoring the study as interpretable (ACEP score ≥ 3) or not in 94.7% of studies, with a Kappa of 0.43 (95% CI: 0.30–0.56).

Discussion

This POCUS curriculum for EM residents in Tanzania was effective in preparing the participants to independently perform and interpret core EM POCUS exams. Following a period of online didactics and brief, intensive hands-on training, participants were equipped to undertake independent scanning with high accuracy and demonstrated improvement over time. There was a significant advancement in knowledge assessments, OSCE performance, and comfort level performing POCUS following completion of required independent scans which aligns with ACEP guidelines for proficiency of 25–50 quality-reviewed studies per exam type and 150–300 total exams [16].

Through the utilization of newer technologies, most of the curriculum could be completed asynchronously without direct faculty oversight, apart from the two weeks of hands-on sessions, which were conducted with a 3:1 trainee-to-faculty ratio. Faculty were available throughout the training to assist with questions and provide individualized guidance and technical support. However, the asynchronous components allowed for greater flexibility for local faculty managing clinical and residency leadership roles, as well as for participation of external faculty by limiting travel time.

The ability to monitor trainee progress and longitudinally review scans is a critical part of the evaluation process in a POCUS program and one that has been rare in LMIC training programs. A recent review of ultrasound training programs in resource-limited settings reported longitudinal training in only 13% of studies, and no >30% exceeded one month’s duration [17]. Two prior studies of ultrasound training in Africa that incorporated longitudinal review and feedback both demonstrated improved trainee performance over time, consistent with the findings in this study [11,12]. In this study, >4500 studies were reviewed, which is the largest in Africa to date.

Few prior studies in this setting have incorporated local faculty training, which is an important contributor to sustainability. In the previously referenced review of ultrasound training programs in resource-limited settings, only 13% reported training of trainers (ToT) [17]. In our study, most instructors were local faculty and members of the residency leadership with extensive clinical POCUS experience but limited image review practice. Advanced training in emergency ultrasound such as in a fellowship, typically incorporates a robust amount of image review practice. Presently, such training is not offered in Tanzania and is rare in most LMIC. While storage of ultrasound studies on secure cloud platforms such as Butterfly, Inc. does allow for remote access, reliance on external faculty for quality assurance limits sustainability and is challenging due to a lack of familiarity with specific clinical cases, the inability to directly follow up with learners for feedback or hands-on guidance, and potential delays due to time zone differences. Our approach produced strong agreement for study accuracy (Kappa 0.83) and interpretability (Kappa 0.43), arguably the most critical elements of quality assurance. However, agreement for overall score was only fair (Kappa 0.30), likely due to the more nuanced scoring of technical factors such as depth, gain, probe orientation, and subjective interpretation of study quality.

This study demonstrates the feasibility of establishing a POCUS curriculum in an EM training program in Africa. However, local context and resources are important to consider when determining replicability. A pre-training needs assessment is uniformly recommended to identify exam types and approaches that are relevant to local settings. Additionally, resource availability must be considered. To accomplish best practices such as a low trainee-to-instructor ratio, adequate hands-on instruction, and longitudinal image review, a significant time commitment is required by faculty. There are also costs associated with ultrasound equipment and data management. See Appendix D for an outline of resources required for this training and considerations for program replicability. Expenses associated with POCUS programs may be offset in systems capable of billing for clinical ultrasound exams. This study did not investigate the impact on subsequent clinical ultrasound volume, though it is plausible that trained providers would utilize POCUS skills in the clinical environment.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. While demonstrating an effective model for POCUS training, the curriculum’s generalizability to other settings is unknown, as it was implemented at a single EM residency program in Africa. Detailed reporting of expenses for equipment and data usage, as well as time required for longitudinal image review, would have better informed generalizability. Additionally, while specific numbers of positive findings were required for exam types, we did not require that all exams be clinically indicated. Some studies were performed on patients without suspected pathology relevant to the ultrasound or on healthy volunteers. Thus, the abnormal findings may not represent the patient population with complete accuracy.

Quality assurance was provided longitudinally only for ultrasound exams performed during the curriculum, but not afterwards for the residents’ clinical scans. This would have enabled assessment of skill retention and post-training clinical scanning volume, the latter of which could be examined to determine if increased volume and billing could offset training expenses. Finally, observational biases may have occurred during image review and OSCE administration. Faculty were not blinded to the curriculum participants because image review and personalized feedback were required. For OSCE administration, faculty examiners used a standardized score sheet to limit subjectivity, were randomly assigned to trainees, and blinded to pre-OSCE scores. However, we were unable to utilize different or multiple examiners or conceal trainee identities.

Conclusion

The specialty of EM in Africa continues to grow and POCUS should be an integral part of training programs. This study demonstrated the effectiveness of a novel POCUS curriculum, leading to significant improvements in trainee proficiency, accuracy, and confidence. The program successfully combined online didactics, focused hands-on training, high-volume independent scanning, and longitudinal image review in an African EM residency program. Incorporation of POCUS quality assurance training for local faculty was efficacious and may enhance sustainability and reduce reliance on external contributors. Future efforts should explore strategies to strengthen local faculty development, assess long-term skill retention, evaluate the impact on patient care, and determine cost-effective approaches for ultrasound equipment and data management in similar settings.
