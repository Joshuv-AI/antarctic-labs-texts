Role of Addiction Medicine Consultation Service in a Community Hospital: Evaluation of the Novel Service⋆
Authors: Srikrishna V Malayala, Wesley C Perez, Rukshar Thapa, Archie Madan, Kushagar Singla, Pragyat Singh, Pelash Kumar, Akshay Thaper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesFewer than 2500 physicians nationwide are certified in addiction medicine. Inpatient addiction hospitalist services have been shown to improve patient outcomes, but their capabilities and impact are underexplored in community hospitals. This study evaluates the role of an addiction medicine hospitalist service in enhancing patient outcomes in two community-based hospitals.MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted on patients who received care from addiction medicine service in two hospitals. The data were collected within the first 120 days of the establishment of service. The reason for consultation, substances used, types of management, impact on transition of care, readmissions and length of stay were evaluated.Results152 patients were seen by the addiction medicine service during the study period. Majority of consultations came from internal medicine followed by general surgery, family medicine, burns and maternity. Ongoing substance use accounted for 47.7 % of consultations, 31.8 % for managing withdrawal, and 21.9 % for managing overdose and toxicity. Medications for alcohol use disorder were started in 87.5 % of cases and opioid use disorder in 96 %. Buprenorphine was the most commonly initiated medication for opioid use disorder and naltrexone for alcohol use disorder. In 14.7 % of cases, the service made a positive impact on length of stay. The readmission rate in these patients was 8.4 % as compared to 13 % for all the patients admitted to the hospital during the study period.ConclusionsAddiction medicine hospitalist service can offer quality care to a wide variety of SUDs in a community hospital setting and supports multiple services.Keywords: Community hospital, Addiction medicine, Consultation

Introduction

Substance use disorders remain a daunting challenge for the public health system in the United States, with the National Institute on Drug Abuse reporting nearly 107,000 drug overdose deaths in 2020, 75 % of which were contributed by opioids alone.1 The escalating crisis has put a strain on the healthcare system, especially in community hospitals, where emergency department (ED) visits related to substance use disorders have increased dramatically. In 2020, approximately 4.7 million ED visits were associated with substance use, with opioids being a leading contributor.2 These trends have also translated into increased hospital admissions which rose by 54 % from 2005 to 2014, with further increases observed in subsequent years.3 There are less than 5000 board-certified addiction medicine physicians in the U.S., showcasing a critical gap in available expertise, and highlighting the need to integrate addiction medicine knowledge into the care continuum to enhance clinical decision-making, offer targeted therapies, and facilitate quicker transitions to outpatient care.4

Though the implementation of addiction medicine services in university hospitals has proved to be efficacious, the concept remains alien in community settings where the surge in substance abuse-related admissions has overwhelmed hospital resources, and the scarcity of addiction medicine specialists has constricted the capacity of healthcare facilities to deliver comprehensive addiction treatment.4 Through our study, we seek to address the role of addiction medicine service in community hospitals and more importantly, evaluate the role of addiction medicine hospitalist consultation services in community settings.

Methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care academic regional level 2 trauma and burn community based hospital, and a nearby smaller community hospital in Southeast Pennsylvania. Both institutions serve diverse populations significantly affected by substance use disorders.5 Addiction medicine hospitalist service was established in April 2024 at both these institutions to provide multidisciplinary care for patients with substance use disorders. The team composed of addiction medicine physician and internal medicine residents on elective rotations was created who collaborated with other departments to conduct comprehensive evaluations, craft detailed treatment plans, and with social workers to coordinate the discharge process to ensure continuity of care. The team was consulted by various departments throughout these two hospitals, including internal medicine, surgery and other subspecialties. The service implemented a systematic rounding pattern for all the consults, conducting daily rounds on patients identified with substance use issues. The same service travelled between both sites for performing the consults.

2.2. Participants

The study included all patients who received care from the addiction medicine consultation service within the first 120 days of its establishment in April 2024 intending to assess the service's early impact on patient outcomes and management processes, following methodologies established in similar studies.6,7 The study was exempt from institutional board review as it was categorized as a performance improvement study by the institution. Patients were identified through hospital records, and inclusion criteria encompassed those who had a documented consultation with the addiction medicine team. Then, we created a structured survey to standardize data collection and systematically capture all pertinent information.8

2.3. Variables, data collection and management

The data was collected retrospectively by internal medicine residents and medical students from patient charts, and entered into a secure database. Charts were analyzed to identify the main reasons for addiction medicine consultation, including specific issues related to overdose and withdrawal management. The primary variables included types of substances used and the substances that tested positive on a urine drug screen, categorizing patients based on the primary substance and their route of misuse (e.g., opioids, stimulants, alcohol). The review also focused on the various interventions provided, such as medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD), medication assisted treatment (MAT), alcohol withdrawal recommendations, pain management recommendations, tobacco use disorder management, education on harm reduction strategies, and behavioral therapy recommendations. Additional variables also included screening and ruling out substance use disorder, prescribing medications to prevent relapse, and evaluating any anticipated side effects from the medication started inpatient by the addiction medicine service. Assessing patients' disposition status, and determining whether they should return home or be referred to a post-acute care or rehabilitation center, was a key factor in evaluating how the service facilitates transitions to outpatient care, including referrals to community resources. Data on readmission rates and duration of hospital stays for patients who received consultations was compiled to evaluate the service's influence.

2.4. Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, reasons for consultation, types of substance use, and interventions provided. Data on readmission rates and duration of hospital stays for patients who received consultations was compiled to evaluate the service's influence.

2.5. Ethical considerations

The study was deemed exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) review at Crozer Chester Medical Centre and Taylor Hospital due to its classification as problem-based learning. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study, and all data were anonymized to ensure patient privacy.

Results

3.1. Participant characteristics

A total of 152 patients were included in the study. The majority of patients (30.3 %) were in the age range of 31–40 years. This was followed by 24.3%(37 patients) in the age range of 51–60 years, and 21.1 % (32 patients) in the age range of 41–50 years. A smaller proportion of patients were less than 20 years old (2.6 %), and 6.6 % were in the range of 21–30 years. Additionally, 15 % (23 patients) were more than 60 years old. The sample comprised 59.9%(91)b male patients and 40.1 % (61) female patients. About 57.9 %(88 patients) identified as White or Caucasian, 38.2%(58 patients) as African-American, and 3.9%(6 patients) as Hispanic or Latino.

Out of 152 encounters, 87.9 % (134) were from a tertiary care teaching hospital, while 12.1 % (18) were from a community hospital. Consultations for addiction medicine were predominantly requested by the hospitalist service at the tertiary care hospital (44.1 %, 67 consultations) and the internal medicine residency services (32.9 %, 50 consultations). Other consultations were from hospitalists at the community hospital (8 %, 12 consultations), general surgery (4.6 %), labor and maternity (2 %), burns unit (3.9 %), family medicine teaching service at the community hospital (2.6 %), and the emergency room at the tertiary hospital (1.3 %) (Table 1).

3.2. Substances used

Half of the patients (50 %, 76 patients) were hospitalized due to substance use disorder-related pathology, while the other half were admitted for non-substance use-related issues. Tobacco was the most commonly used substance (60.3 %), followed by opioids (54.9 %), cocaine (40.4 %), alcohol (34.4 %), cannabis (21.2 %), benzodiazepines (18.5 %), amphetamines (13.2 %), dissociatives like phencyclidine (6.6 %), inhalants (6 %), and xylazine (4 %) (Table 1).

Urine drug screens were positive in 79.5 % of cases, negative in 5.3 %, and not performed in 15.2 %. Intravenous drug use was reported in 19.3 % of patients, intranasal in 35.6 %, and inhalational in 41.8 %. Of the patients, 36 % were enrolled in an outpatient practice for substance use disorders, with 19.5 % reporting compliance and 16.1 % non-compliance (Table 1).

3.3. Consultation reasons and management offered

Consultations were primarily for ongoing substance use disorder without overdose or withdrawal symptoms (47.7 %, 72 consultations), followed by management of withdrawal symptoms (31.8 %, 48 consultations), and overdose or toxicity management (21.9 %, 33 consultations). Pain management in patients with substance use disorder was requested in 11.3 % (17 consultations), optimization of medication regimens in 7.9 % (12 consultations), and screening for substance use disorders in 10.6 % (16 consultations).

Tobacco use disorder was co-managed in 87 % of patients, opioid use disorder in 41.7 %, stimulant use disorder in 39.1 %, alcohol use disorder in 19.2 %, and alcohol withdrawal in 18.5 %. Pain management recommendations were given in 19.9 %, and substance use disorder screening and ruling out occurred in 11.9 % (Table 2).

3.4. Medical management

Medication for alcohol use disorder (MAUD) was offered to 36 patients, with 30.6 % (11 patients) refusing. Naltrexone was the most prescribed (61.1 %), followed by acamprosate and topiramate (8.3 % each). For opioid use disorder, 85 % of the 55 applicable patients were initiated on medication, with 15 % refusing. Buprenorphine was the most common medication (72.3 %), while 27.3 % were referred for methadone outpatient initiation. For stimulant use disorder, medication was started for 50 % of the 76 applicable patients, with 19.7 % refusing. Based on the profile of the patient, medications like topiramate, naltrexone or wellbutrin were prescribed on discharge.9 Nicotine replacement therapy was prescribed in 85.5 % of patients, with 14.5 % refusing.

3.5. Disposition, transitions of care and quality

Of the 152 patients, 78.1 % (119) were discharged as planned, and 21.9 % (33 patients) left against medical advice. Most discharges (62.9 %, 95 patients) were to home with self-care or home health care support, 19.2 % (29 patients) to a rehabilitation center, 4 % (6 patients) to a shelter supporting patients experiencing homelessness, and 13.9 % (21 patients) to a physical rehab or post-acute care facility. The addiction medicine service assisted in finding a disposition for 38 % (57 patients).

Among the 119 anticipated discharges, 86.6 % (103 patients) were not readmitted within 30 days, 8.4 % (10 patients) were readmitted within 30 days, and 5 % (6 patients) within 90 days. Of those who left against medical advice, 90.9 % (30 patients) were not readmitted within 30 days, 6.1 % (2 patients) were readmitted within 30 days, and 3 % (1 patient) within 90 days. The overall readmission rate for the health system during the same time period was 13 % (Appendix 1).

The length of stay (LOS) was less than 3 days for 37.1 % of patients, 3–7 days for 35.8 %, 8–14 days for 14.6 %, 15–30 days for 9.9 %, and 31–60 days for 2.6 %. Recommendations from the addiction consult service reduced LOS for 14.7 % of patients (Table 3).

Discussion

This study confirms that Addiction medicine consultation services in community hospital settings have the potential to improve patient outcomes for individuals with substance use disorders significantly. The study population represents a diverse age range, with the majority between 31 and 60 years old, and included a mix of racial backgrounds, illustrating the wide-reaching impact of SUDs.

4.1. Principal findings

Initial findings reveal variability in service utilization between the two hospital settings, with only 12.1 % of eligible patients from the community hospital receiving addiction medicine consultations. This disparity reflects the systemic challenges often faced by hospitals that offer greater assistance to historically underserved populations, which typically have fewer resources for comprehensive SUD services.9 Nonetheless, patients who received addiction medicine consultations showed promising outcomes, mirroring trends observed in urban hospitals with more established addiction services.10 By examining these early results, our study aims to provide insights into the impact of implementing addiction medicine services and identify potential areas for improvement in care delivery for patients with SUDs.

The implementation of our addiction medicine service revealed a complex landscape of substance use patterns and treatment needs. While tobacco use was most prevalent (60.3 %), the high rates of opioid (54.9 %), cocaine (40.4 %), and alcohol use (34.4 %) illustrate the multifaceted nature of SUDs in our patient population. Notably, only 36 % of patients were enrolled in outpatient SUD management prior to admission, with less than half reporting compliance, indicating substantial gaps in community-based care. Against this backdrop, our addiction medicine service demonstrated promising outcomes in initiating evidence-based treatments. The high rates of pharmacotherapy initiation for opioid use disorder (85 %, predominantly with buprenorphine) and incidentally for tobacco use disorder (87 %) align with best practices and previous studies showing the effectiveness of inpatient addiction consultations.11,12 The success in connecting 42.9 % of opioid use disorder patients to outpatient MOUD clinics upon discharge represents a crucial step in bridging the gap between inpatient and community care, potentially improving long-term outcomes.13 For alcohol use disorder, while medication acceptance rates were lower, the 61.1 % initiation rate of naltrexone among those accepting treatment surpasses figures from previous studies, suggesting the value of a robust addiction consultation service.14 However, the variability in treatment acceptance and initiation across different SUDs points to the need for tailored approaches and further research into barriers to care engagement.

Building upon the success of treatment initiation, our study also examined the impact of addiction medicine consultations on hospital utilization metrics. The data reveal a nuanced picture of length of stay (LOS) and readmission rates, reflecting the complex nature of caring for patients with SUDs. Notably, 86.6 % of patients with planned discharges avoided readmission within 30 days, suggesting effective discharge planning and aligning with recent studies showing reduced readmission rates through targeted interventions like medication-assisted therapy.15,16 Our overall readmission rate of 13 % for medicine and surgical consultations compares favorably to previous studies, hinting at the potential long-term benefits of inpatient addiction services. The variability in LOS, with 37.1 % of patients staying under three days and 2.6 % remaining hospitalized for over 31 days, illuminates the diverse needs of this patient population.17 The individuals who stayed for more than 31 days suggest that more comprehensive care may be required in certain cases, such as those with severe or co-occurring conditions. This aligns with studies showing that patients receiving substance use resources may have longer stays due to the added complexity of care.18

Overall, we were able to observe that 14.7 % of patients experiencing reduced LOS due to addiction consult services point to the potential of these interventions to optimize hospital resource utilization. 19 These results collectively indicate that while addiction medicine consultations can facilitate timely discharges and reduce readmissions for many patients, they also reveal the need for flexible, patient-centered approaches to address the varied and often intricate healthcare needs of these individuals (Fig. 1).

4.2. Interpretation

While our study demonstrated positive outcomes in LOS and readmission rates for many patients, the challenge of retaining patients through complete treatment remains significant. In our cohort, 21.9 % of patients left against medical advice (AMA), highlighting a persistent obstacle in the substance use disorder population. Notably, 90.9 % of these AMA patients avoided readmission within 30 days, suggesting that even brief interventions by addiction consult services may have lasting benefits. This finding contrasts with traditional assumptions about AMA discharges and points to the potential efficacy of early, targeted interventions. Our results diverge from some previous studies, which found no significant change in AMA rates or 30-day readmissions following the implementation of an inpatient buprenorphine protocol.19 However, our higher rate of avoiding readmission among AMA patients aligns with their observation that patients receiving any form of medication assisted treatment (MAT) had substantially lower AMA discharge rates compared to those receiving supportive care only. This signals the importance of developing earlier and more comprehensive treatments, like MOUD or MAT, to better address the needs and concerns of this vulnerable population.

4.3. Limitations

Our study, while informative, has several limitations that warrant consideration. The lack of long-term follow-up restricts our ability to assess crucial outcomes such as re-hospitalization at other facilities, continued substance use, or engagement with outpatient services. This is particularly significant given that only 38 % of patients with planned discharges were successfully connected to follow-up care. Future studies could prioritize post-discharge tracking to evaluate these critical endpoints. The study also doesn't account for potential confounding variables such as psychiatric comorbidities or social factors like housing insecurity, which could significantly impact patient behavior and outcomes. A more comprehensive analysis incorporating these elements would provide deeper insights into the complexities of inpatient addiction care. Future investigations should explore strategies to optimize the length of stay while ensuring thorough care, especially for patients with intricate needs. Despite these constraints, our findings highlight the potential value of integrating addiction medicine services into inpatient care, demonstrating improvements in treatment initiation and readmission rates. These results emphasize the importance of expanding such services, particularly in settings with limited resources.

There is also no control group for us to compare our findings with the utility of such a service in any other community hospital setting.

Conclusion

This study serves as a foundation for future improvements in inpatient addiction medicine services, highlighting both the progress made and the work that lies ahead in optimizing care for this complex patient population at our tertiary care community hospital in Pennsylvania. Our examination of a newly implemented consultation service revealed areas of significant positive impact, particularly in reducing readmission rates and optimizing length of stay. However, it also exposed aspects requiring improvement, such as the rate of patients leaving AMA. These findings suggest that addiction medicine services must be implemented in community hospital settings to meet the needs of this high-risk population. Future research should focus on developing more integrated care strategies and improving post-discharge follow-up to enhance overall treatment outcomes. By addressing these areas, we can strive to improve the continuity of care and, ultimately, the recovery trajectories of patients with SUDs in our rural and suburban communities.
