Impact of non-tumor size related features on staging in non-small cell lung cancer: a population-level analysis of the SEER-medicare database
Authors: Ravi Rajaram, Barbara H. Johnson, Qing Huang, Balaji Laxmanan, Stephen Johnston, Aisha Hasan, James M. Giuffre, Iftekhar Kalsekar, Anil Vachani, Clifford Robinson, Kiran Devisetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to quantify and characterize the presence of non-tumor size features among non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients in a surgical cohort versus those in a non-surgical cohort and assess the potential impact of key features on staging. A total of 22,876 patients aged 66 years and older with newly diagnosed (2010–2015) stage I–III NSCLC (T1–T3, N0/N1, M0) without prior or concurrent primary cancer were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-Medicare Database. Reported T stage data (American Joint Committee on Cancer 7th Edition) were compared with a hypothetical tumor size only T stage (Tsize-Only), which did not account for non-tumor size defining characteristics. Frequencies of key non-tumor size features were analyzed among surgical and non-surgical cohorts. In the surgical cohort, a lower Tsize-Only vs. SEER-reported T stage was observed in 2,531 patients (20.8%). Specifically, T stage would be lower in 1,542 (32.6%) patients with T2 disease and in 989 (66.4%) patients with T3 disease. In the non-surgical cohort, lower Tsize-Only vs. Reported T was observed in 1,446 patients (13.5%) [342 (8.8%) patients with T2 disease and 1,104 (60.1%) patients with T3 disease]. The largest disparity between cohorts was in T2 disease and visceral pleural invasion (VPI): VPI was identified in 87.5% of surgically resected patients with lower Tsize-Only versus only 37.4% in non-surgical patients. Our findings highlight the need for better T stage characterization in non-surgical patients. Development of predictive models or standardized radiographic methods to accurately identify VPI in the non-surgical NSCLC population are recommended in light of the established impact of VPI on clinical outcomes. Trial investigators should consider the potential impact of un-identified VPI on their effect size estimates and sample size calculations. Attention towards non-tumor size related features, particularly VPI, may lead to improved treatment decisions and outcomes

Fundamental to the treatment of lung cancer is accurate staging as defined by the tumor, node, metastasis (TNM) staging system (1), which is a primary determinant of both prognosis and treatment. Inaccurate clinical staging may impact survival for patients with lung cancer who may not be provided optimal treatment recommendations. In a cohort of 698 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients who underwent surgical resection, Navani et al. reported 34% were under-staged on pre-operative clinical staging when compared to post-operative pathological staging (2). Consequently, more accurate tumor (T) and nodal (N) staging would have led to different treatment decisions, as clinical staging failed to detect T4 disease in 10% of cases and mis-classified nodal disease in 38% of cases.

Aside from tumor size, several other criteria impact the T stage such as satellite lesions or mediastinal invasion. Understanding the epidemiology of key features beyond tumor size that can alter T stage is critical to ensure accurate staging and disease management. This is increasingly important given the growth in non-surgical therapies in the treatment of early or locoregionally-advanced NSCLC.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the presence of non-tumor size features among patients with NSCLC who underwent surgical resection compared to those non-surgically treated and assess the potential impact of pathologic features that cannot be reliably detected radiographically. The discordance between clinical and pathologic staging is well established. Our research offers insight into the proportion and prevalence of T stage features driving this discordance.

Data from patients aged 66 years and older with newly diagnosed [2010–2015] stage I–III NSCLC (T1–T3, N0/N1, M0) without prior or concurrent primary cancer from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-Medicare Database were analyzed. The T stage data, obtained from the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 7th Edition as recorded in the database, were compared with a hypothetical tumor size only-T stage (Tsize-Only), derived solely from tumor size thresholds per the AJCC 7th Edition and did not consider any non-size features [e.g., visceral pleural invasion (VPI)]. For example, a patient with a 2 cm tumor and VPI would be hypothetically staged as T1 based on tumor size only (Tsize-Only) as opposed to T2 (due to the presence of VPI). When the hypothetical Tsize-Only was different than the recorded T stage in the database, this represented the possibility of misclassification with clinical staging. The proportion of possible mis-classified patients were calculated and compared between surgical and non-surgical cohorts. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

The AJCC clinical stage is based on the extent of disease present before definitive treatment is instituted. It includes physical examination, radiologic imaging, endoscopy, biopsy, and laboratory findings. The AJCC pathologic stage is based on findings from surgical extirpation and subsequent pathologic examination.

The frequencies of non-tumor size T stage features were analyzed and characterized between surgical and non-surgical cohorts to generate Tsize-Only stage and then compared to the reported T stage to identify potential real-world impact on staging.

A total of 22,876 patients were identified with a mean age of 76.5 years [standard deviation (SD) =6.6 years], including 12,189 females (53.3%). There were 12,159 patients (mean age 74.7 years, SD =5.6 years) in the surgical cohort, with a histology distribution that included 7,509 (61.8%) with adenocarcinoma, 3,709 (30.5%) with squamous cell carcinoma, 205 (1.7%) with neuroendocrine carcinoma, and 736 (6.0%) with other sub-types. The basis for assigned stage in the surgical cohort was 94.9% pathological, 5.0% clinical (due to the receipt of neoadjuvant therapy), and 0.1% unknown. Overall, there were 5,934 (48.8%) patients with T1, 4,735 (39.0%) with T2, and 1,490 (12.2%) with T3 disease.

There were 10,717 patients (mean age 78.6 years, SD =7.1 years) in the non-surgical cohort, with a histology distribution that included 3,770 (35.2%) with adenocarcinoma, 3,796 (35.2%) with squamous cell carcinoma, 117 (1.1%) with neuroendocrine carcinoma, and 3,034 (28.3%) with other subtypes. The basis for assigned stage in the non-surgical cohort was 96.2% clinical and 3.8% unknown or indeterminate. Overall, there were 4,981 (46.5%) with T1, 3,898 (36.4%) with T2, and 1,838 (17.1%) with T3 disease.

In both cohorts, the proportion of Tsize-Only possible mis-classification increased with T stage. In the surgical cohort, a lower Tsize-Only vs. SEER-reported T stage was observed in 2,531 patients (20.8%), occurring in 1,542 (32.6%) patients with T2 disease and 989 (66.4%) patients with T3 disease (Figure 1). In the non-surgical cohort, a lower Tsize-Only vs. SEER-reported T stage was observed in 1,446 patients (13.5%), occurring in 342 (8.8%) patients with T2 disease, and 1,104 (60.1%) patients with T3 disease (Figure 2). Irrespective of T stage, a difference of 7.3% was observed across surgical and non-surgical cohorts.

In the T2 surgical cohort, the major feature leading to higher SEER-reported T stage vs. Tsize-Only was the presence of VPI in 87.5% of patients. In the T2 non-surgical cohort, VPI led to higher SEER-reported T stage vs. Tsize-Only in just 37.4%. The difference between surgical and non-surgical cohorts suggests a gap in VPI identification during clinical staging.

The results of this analysis reinforce the importance of accurate clinical staging beyond tumor size early stage and locoregionally advanced NSCLC. Using a large United Stated (US) cancer registry, we found that 20.8% of surgical and 13.5% of non-surgical patients with stage I-III NSCLC could potentially be mis-classified if non-tumor size T features were neglected. This 7.3% difference between the two cohorts suggests a gap between clinical and pathological staging that has been previously reported (2,3). Failure to detect key non-tumor size T features in non-surgical patients could lead to inaccurate clinical staging and complicate evaluation of treatment outcomes.

VPI has long been known to be a negative prognostic factor in lung cancer. Kang et al. reported VPI as a predictor of poor prognosis independent of both T and N status in 2003 (4). Consequently, VPI was included as a factor upstaging T1 tumors (<3 cm) to T2a in the AJCC 7th Edition TNM staging manual in 2010 (5).

Contemporary studies have confirmed an association between VPI and survival outcomes in NSCLC. Altorki et al. recently reported a post-hoc analysis of Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB) 140503 that compared survival and recurrence in a surgical cohort of patients with pT2 (with VPI) NSCLC with small tumor size (<2 cm) versus pT1 tumors (no VPI) (6). Compared to those without VPI, patients with VPI had worse 5-year disease-free survival (53.3% vs. 65.9%), worse recurrence-free survival (58.2% vs. 73.1%) and a higher proportion of local (15.0% vs. 10.8%) and distant disease (23.9% vs. 14.6%) recurrence.

These results suggest that surgical patients upstaged due to VPI may be at higher risk for micro-metastatic disease. Per National Comprehensive Cancer Network® (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology (NCCN Guidelines®), patients with VPI (and other high-risk factors such as poor tumor differentiation and lymphovascular invasion) should be considered for adjuvant treatment following surgical resection (7).

While identification of VPI is a standard practice during surgical resection, detecting it during clinical staging through radiographic assessment presents significant challenges. Improved identification of radiographic VPI could lead to more accurate staging and enhance treatment decision-making for patients with NSCLC treated non-surgically. Sun et al. reported several radiographic characteristics associated with VPI on computed tomography (CT) imaging including jellyfish sign, pleural thickening, pleural-attached nodules, and pleural-tag nodules, though these criteria have not been validated broadly (8).

Cancer pathology reports are already standardized using College of American Pathologists templates (9). In contrast, radiographic reports are not currently standardized for factors that impact lung cancer staging. Ideally, following a lung cancer diagnosis, the subsequent radiographic report should include all non-size T-feature factors in a checklist format, such as the presence or absence of VPI.

Zuo et al. recently described encouraging results of a pre-operative CT radiomics model for predicting VPI in lung adenocarcinoma patients (10). Presumably, this model or others will improve over time with advancement in machine learning algorithms and provide clinicians with the necessary information to stage non-surgical patients accurately. Until such time that VPI is consistently identified in non-surgical NSCLC patients, investigators should consider the potential impact of un-identified VPI on their effect size estimates and sample size calculations when designing clinical studies.

Our findings quantify the presence of key non-tumor size features that may affect clinical staging and treatment decisions in NSCLC patients. Development and utilization of novel diagnostics tools that can identify/predict the presence of these key pathological parameters will increase the likelihood of patients receiving guideline-concordant care and potentially improve survival outcomes in this population.
