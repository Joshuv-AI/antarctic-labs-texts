Kidney Allograft Outcomes in Borderline and T Cell-Mediated Rejection With Microvascular Inflammation
Authors: Wai H. Lim, Jiayue Wang, Armando Teixeira-Pinto, Julie Ho, Ryan Gately, Dharshana Sabanayagam, Esther Ooi, Farzaneh Boroumand, K Shuvo Bakar, Lin Zhu, Helen Pilmore, William Mulley, Jennifer Li, Brian Nankivell, John Kanellis, Peter Nickerson, Chris Wiebe, Germaine Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMicrovascular inflammation (MVI) is a hallmark feature of antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR), but its prognostic significance when it occurs with T-cell-mediated rejection (TCMR) in the absence of other ABMR features remains unclear.MethodsWe examined the association between pure TCMR phenotypes, defined by the presence or absence of MVI, and graft survival using restricted mean survival time (RMST), and assessed interactions with rejection involving a “v” lesion.ResultsAmong 1614 recipients with first biopsy-proven pure TCMR (2010–2023), 85% had no MVI, 8% subthreshold MVI (Banff glomerulitis [g] + peritubular capillaritis [ptc] = 1), and 7% with MVI (g + ptc ≥ 2). Compared with TCMR without MVI, recipients with TCMR + MVI (hazard ratio [HR]; 95% confidence intervals [CI] 1.57 [1.06–2.34]) had a higher risk of all-cause graft loss, whereas subthreshold MVI was not associated with increased risk. The 5-year RMST was lowest in the MVI group (3.93 [3.53–4.26]), compared with the subthreshold group (4.39 [4.11– 4.60]) and the no MVI group (4.32 [4.24–4.40]). Rejection with a “v” lesion modified this association. Recipients with TCMR + MVI and positive “v” lesion had a 2.77-fold higher risk of graft loss (2.77 [1.64–4.67]), whereas no excess risk was observed in those without vascular lesions.ConclusionPure TCMR with MVI, particularly in the setting of a “v” lesion, represents a high-risk phenotype associated with poorer graft survival, warranting mechanistic investigations and targeted therapeutic strategies.

Acute TCMR is a significant impediment to long-term kidney graft survival.1 Despite advances in modern immunosuppression, acute TCMR occurs in 13% to 20% recipients within the first-year after kidney transplant, with an incidence that varies according to clinically indicated versus protocol-based biopsy detection.2 Previous observational studies have shown that even mild TCMR phenotypes, including borderline and subclinical rejection, are independently associated with an increased risk of early graft loss.3, 4, 5, 6 Moreover, there is a dose-dependent relationship between increasing severity of acute TCMR, such as vascular rejection and adverse longer-term outcomes, including graft loss.3 Finally, acute TCMR is a strong predictor of subsequent de novo donor-specific anti-human leukocyte antigen (HLA) antibody (donor specific antibody [DSA]) development, a key causal factor of acute and chronic ABMR with limited effective therapies.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Collectively, these findings emphasize the clinical relevance and importance of early recognition and optimal management of acute TCMR.

MVI is an important diagnostic feature of ABMR and is pathologically defined by the accumulation of intraluminal g and/or ptc of leukocytes, respectively. The Banff diagnostic schema separately scores “g” and/or “ptc” lesions from 0 to 3, with summed scores representing the MVI score (out of 6).12,13 Moderate MVI score of ≥ 2 was then incorporated into Banff 2013 as a surrogate criterion 2 marker for “C4dptc negative ABMR.”14 Banff 2022 reappraised MVI ≥ 2 in criterion 2 and recommended “DSA-negative, C4d-negative MVI” be reported using a “diagnosis-agnostic” description without inferring antibody causality. MVI ≥ 2 in the presence of C4d positivity, and/or DSA is diagnostic of ABMR, and is associated with inferior graft outcomes compared with patients without ABMR.15 However, the increasing recognition of MVI in the absence of detectable DSA or C4d positivity has questioned the pathophysiology of these cases in the absence of other features of ABMR, and the clinical significance and treatment of MVI below threshold (i.e., g + ptc ≥ 2) are uncertain. The clinical significance of isolated MVI or MVI accompanying acute TCMR is unknown, and postulated biological mechanisms include a distinct immunopathological phenotype of acute TCMR, rejection driven by non-HLA antibodies,16 nonhumoral “missing-self” natural killer-cell-mediated activity, direct monocyte allorecognition, or primary T-cell activation towards mismatched HLA endothelial molecules, or technical limitations in current DSA assays leading to potential misclassification or under- or potentially missed detection of anti-HLA antibodies.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25

Moreover, the prognostic significance of MVI in TCMR without concurrent ABMR remains uncertain, particularly among recipients with a milder form of endothelial injury and inflammation. The Banff TCMR 2024 working group have formally posed the question “What is the clinical course of pure TCMR presenting in conjunction with DSA-negative, C4d-negative MVI?.”26 Addressing this knowledge gap is essential to inform interventional strategies and long-term graft prognosis. This study characterized Banff-defined TCMR phenotypes, with and without MVI, described treatment strategies and examined associations with all-cause and death-censored graft loss, including whether these differed by the presence of rejection with a “v” lesion.

Methods

Study Cohort

Using data from the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant registry (ANZDATA), all patients with kidney failure who received first and subsequent living or deceased donor kidney-only transplants in Australia and New Zealand between 2010 and 2023 were identified. Of those, only kidney transplant recipients who experienced a first biopsy-proven “pure” TCMR episode were included in the study cohort. Recipients who did not experience acute rejection or had experienced acute rejection, but the first episode of rejection was not attributed to TCMR (irrespective of whether they experienced subsequent TCMR episodes) were excluded. We also excluded recipients with features of mixed ABMR and TCMR. Approval to conduct this study with a waiver of consent was granted by the human research ethics committee of the University of Western Australia (Perth, Australia; reference number 2024/ET001189). Reporting of this study was undertaken in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.27

Study Covariates

Data extracted from the ANZDATA included donor characteristics of age, sex, and hypertension status; recipient characteristics of age, sex, ethnicity, dialysis duration, primary cause of kidney failure, body mass index, and comorbid conditions at time of transplantation (coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease and diabetes); and transplant characteristics of total ischemic time, the number of HLA mismatches, peak calculated panel reactive antibody, and the use of induction therapy.

Study Exposure

Acute rejection episodes reported to the ANZDATA were classified according to the 2022 Banff classification. Pure TCMR (as a single group) included the following: “subthreshold TCMR” (Banff i1 + t1–3 or t1 + i2–3, with v = 0); acute TCMR, including Banff 1a/b (Banff i2–3 + t2–3 with v = 0), and Grade II/III (Banff v1–3) TCMR, in the absence of recorded ABMR episodes or DSA detection. Rejection episodes were excluded if ABMR or humoral rejection codes were recorded as ‘Yes,’ or if Banff C4d score or DSA (pretransplant/de novo) was coded as “Yes” at the time of rejection. In cases where ABMR (including C4d) or DSA fields were left blank, these were assumed to indicate no recorded histological evidence of ABMR or absence of DSA, respectively. Accordingly, the TCMR episodes included in this study were those with ABMR and/or humoral code, C4d, and DSA negative and/or not recorded. Details of the class of pretransplant DSA were not available. The ANZDATA Graft Rejection Survey form can be downloaded from the website (https://anzorrg.org.au/data-management/data-forms#anzdata-forms).

DSA and ABMR negative “pure” TCMR was categorized into the following 3 exposure groups according to the absence or presence of MVI as: (i) TCMR without MVI (MVI = 0, i.e., Banff g + ptc scores = 0); (ii) TCMR with subthreshold MVI (MVI = 1, i.e., Banff g + ptc = 1); and (iii) TCMR with clinically significant MVI (MVI ≥ 2, Banff g + ptc ≥ 2).

Study Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were all-cause graft loss, defined as a return to dialysis treatment after graft loss or death with a functioning graft, and death-censored graft loss, defined as graft loss excluding death with a functioning graft. Secondary outcomes included time to the next episode of repeated rejection after the initial TCMR event. The follow-up period continued until graft loss, loss to follow-up, or until the end of the available follow-up period (December 31, 2023) if no event of interest occurred.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort were expressed as numbers (percentages), means (SD), and medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]), with comparisons between the 3 exposure groups using chi-square tests, t tests, or Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum tests, as appropriate. The incidence rates for all-cause and death-censored allograft failure were expressed as events (95% CI) per 1000-person years and compared between the exposure groups using incidence rate differences. TCMR without MVI was considered as the referent group. Descriptions of the treatment types and responses to treatment of each TCMR group are shown.

We applied inverse probability weighting to reduce confounding and balance baseline covariates across the TCMR exposure groups. Propensity scores were estimated from clinically relevant baseline covariates known to be associated with graft loss, including donor age, recipient age, recipient sex, dialysis duration, prevalent comorbid conditions (coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and diabetes), HLA-mismatches, sensitization status (maximum calculated panel reactive antibody of < or ≥ 80%), total ischemic time, induction therapy, and time from transplantation to rejection episode (< or ≥ 1 year). The individual recipient was weighted by the inverse probability of being in their observed exposure groups. To assess for covariate balance, we compared the mean standardized differences between groups before and after weighting, with < 10% considered evidence of adequate balance. A love plot was generated to visually demonstrate standardized differences across covariates, and kernel density plots of the propensity score distributions were examined to evaluate the degree of overlap between the exposure groups. The resulting stabilized weights were included in the weighted Cox proportional hazard models to estimate the effects of the exposure groups on all-cause and death-censored graft loss. Both raw and trimmed weights were also applied to improve the stability of the estimates. Results were reported as HRs with 95% CI, reflecting the average treatment effect in the study population. Inverse probability-weighted Kaplan-Meier curves for all-cause and death-censored graft loss by exposure group were plotted, and a log-rank test was used to compare differences between exposure groups.

We also assessed the association between TCMR groups and graft loss using competing-risks methods, with death with a functioning graft as a competing event. Subdistribution HRs were estimated using Fine-Gray models to compare the risk of graft loss across the 3 TCMR exposure groups—no MVI, subthreshold MVI, and MVI. In addition, RMST analyses were performed to quantify the average event-free survival up to a prespecified time horizon (τ). Bootstrap resampling (B = 1000) with replacement was used to estimate the 95% CI. RMST models were fitted for both all-cause and death-censored graft loss, providing estimates of differences in restricted mean graft survival across 3 TCMR + MVI exposure groups. To evaluate subsequent allograft rejection episodes, a time-to-event model was constructed to estimate the time to the first recurrent rejection after the index TCMR episode, using a Cox proportional hazards model to compare the subthreshold MVI and MVI groups with the no MVI group as the referent.

Sensitivity Analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were undertaken—(i) Delayed graft function was included as a potential covariate in the inverse probability weighting of the main model; (ii) Stratified analyses according to the Banff “v”-lesion (to denote presence or absence of vascular rejection) were conducted, with RMST models and separate weighted Cox proportional hazard models fitted to estimate the association between MVI and all-cause graft loss and death-censored graft loss within each stratum of Banff “v”-lesion positivity; (iii) Subgroup analyses restricted to transplants between 2015 and 2023 (i.e., representing a greater recognition of MVI in ABMR-negative rejections), with weighted Cox proportional-hazard models for all-cause and death-censored graft loss, using the same modelling approach as the primary analysis; (iv) Complete case analysis including only cases where there were no missing data/blanks for the “g” or “ptc” scores); (v) Subgroup analyses restricting to those patients with subthreshold MVI and MVI with either isolated g and without ptc (i.e., “g ”score of 1–3 and “ptc” score of 0) or with isolated ptc and without g (i.e., “ptc” score of 1–3 and “g” score of 0); (vi) A separate analysis was conducted excluding borderline TCMR cases.

Analyses were undertaken using STATA statistical software IC 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) and R statistical software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), with P-values of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Cohort Characteristics

During the study period, 3478 of 14,946 recipients (23.3%) experienced at least 1 episode of acute rejection. Of those who experienced acute rejection, 1864 (53.6%) were excluded (ABMR, mixed rejection or not reported, and data inconsistencies), leaving a study cohort of 1614 recipients (48.2%) with TCMR (Figure 1). Most recipients with TCMR (n = 1370) had no MVI (84.9%), 133 (8.2%) had subthreshold MVI, and 111 (6.9%) had MVI (Figure 1). The proportions of subthreshold and grade I or higher grades TCMR for each exposure group are shown in Supplementary Table S1. RMST Analyses

For all-cause graft loss, the 5-year RMST was similar between recipients in the subthreshold MVI group (4.39 [4.11–4.60]) and those without MVI (4.32 [4.24–4.40]). In contrast, the MVI group had the lowest 5-year RMST (3.93 [3.53–4.26]). A similar pattern was observed for death-censored graft loss. Five-year RMST estimates were comparable between no MVI (4.49 [4.42–4.56]) and subthreshold MVI groups (4.59 [4.34–4.76]) but were lower in recipients with MVI (4.06 [3.68–4.37]). In the study cohort, 394 (25.4%) recipients experienced repeated rejection episodes. Discussion

Our study findings suggest that in kidney transplant recipients with biopsy-proven TCMR and without features of ABMR, the presence of MVI was associated with at least a 1.5-fold increased risk of all-cause and death-censored graft loss compared with that in TCMR without MVI, with an RMST loss at 5 years postrejection of 0.4 years. The association between MVI and graft loss was only observed in those with a “v” lesion, such that the presence of MVI was associated with over a 3-fold greater risk of death-censored graft loss. TCMR episodes with histological features of g or ptc but not meeting the Banff MVI criteria (subthreshold MVI) had graft outcomes comparable to TCMR without MVI. Treatment strategies were highly variable within this national cohort; however, most transplant recipients were treated with higher steroid doses. Although 70% of recipients responded to treatment with graft function returning to prerejection levels, recipients with acute TMCR and MVI were more likely to experience persistent rejection and graft loss within 1 month of diagnosis than recipients with TCMR alone and those with TCMR and subthreshold MVI. These findings indicate that TCMR with Banff-defined clinically significant MVI, especially those with a “v” lesion, represents a distinct high-risk phenotype with poorer prognosis and validate the original threshold of a summed score of 2 or more, particularly in those with more severe TCMR.14

Recognition of MVI as an active inflammatory lesion attributable to ABMR led to its incorporation into the 2019 Banff updated histological criteria for ABMR,28 with a new category of MVI without evidence of DSA and C4d staining introduced in the 2022 Banff update.15 The identification of this distinct MVI phenotype in the absence of antibody-antigen interaction suggests the involvement of other antibody-independent pathways, including TCMR, ischemic reperfusion injury, infections such as cytomegalovirus, and other injurious processes.15,29 Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests a role for missing-self natural killer cell activation-induced association with HLA-DSA-negative MVI.19,20,30, 31, 32 These findings of shared pathogenic mechanisms and natural killer cell transcripts in both ABMR and TCMR suggest that antibody-dependent and independent pathways may coexist despite differences in antigen recognition systems.33 Multiomic profiling of kidney graft biopsies with evidence of MVI and transplant glomerulopathy, but without DSA or C4d staining, showed higher glomerular abundance and activity of CD3pos T cells. These findings suggest MVI exists along a continuum of immune-mediated endothelial injury rather than representing a discrete rejection phenotype.34

The presence of MVI is an important predictor of adverse graft outcomes and is associated with a substantially increased risk of graft loss, particularly in the setting of ABMR. Recent cohort studies have shown that MVI is also frequently observed in TCMR, including cases with MVI scores below the diagnostic threshold for ABMR. Emerging evidence suggests that both TCMR with and without MVI are associated with the worst graft outcomes, with the poorest prognosis observed when MVI occurs with mixed TCMR and ABMR.21,23,35 In contrast to previous studies, which have not specifically examined outcomes among recipients with TCMR and subthreshold MVI, we found that subthreshold MVI was not associated with poorer graft outcomes, with graft survival comparable to recipients with TCMR without MVI. Similarly, the treatment strategies are highly heterogeneous, reflecting the ongoing uncertainty regarding the underlying mechanism of this phenotype. The most common approach was escalation of immunosuppression, with 36% of recipients with TCMR and MVI receiving T cell-depleting therapy. A subset of recipients with TCMR and MVI (up to 15%) received ABMR-directed therapies, including plasmapheresis and/or i.v. Ig, whereas these strategies were used less frequently in recipients with subthreshold or absent MVI. In addition, we have identified that severe TCMR and Banff-V lesions represent a more severe TCMR phenotype with the poorest graft outcome, suggesting that future studies should focus on this phenotype to validate our initial findings and inform potential effective treatment options. The improved understanding of the pathogenesis of distinct MVI phenotypes is essential to guide targeted, mechanism-based treatment strategies and to refine prognostic stratification for adverse graft outcomes. Integration of omics-based diagnostics may allow treatment to be aligned with the dominant effector pathways driving MVI.

Our study has several strengths, including the completeness of the dataset and extended follow-up duration. Nevertheless, these must be balanced against key limitations when interpreting the study findings. Although multiple donors, recipient and transplant-related factors were adjusted in the weighted models, residual and unmeasured confounders likely remain. Potential sources include variation in maintenance immunosuppression (and target therapeutic drug level), antirejection medications and post-TCMR-management, center variation in the accuracy of rejection (and Banff criteria) reporting, donor-specific anti-HLA antibody testing (including frequency and reason [routine vs. indication]), variation in undertaking protocol and follow-up indication biopsies and center-level clinical expertise, which may have influenced graft outcomes across all TCMR phenotypes. In addition, granular details of the cumulative intensity of immunosuppression following TCMR diagnosis were not available in ANZDATA. Misclassification bias and era effects from changes in the Banff definition and reporting are also possible. The ANZDATA registry does not independently verify rejection reporting or require the entry of negative data (e.g., a “g” score of 0 in the absence of glomerulitis), and sites are only required to enter the Banff indices with a > 0 score. Greater detection of MVI in recent eras may reflect evolving Banff definitions, rather than true incidence, although sensitivity analyses from 2015 produced similar findings. Incomplete registry reporting and missing Banff indices (assumed absent) may have led to under-reporting of ABMR features, potentially resulting in some cases classified as pure TCMR being reclassified as mixed rejections. Similarly, inflammation in the area of interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy and other chronic Banff scores were also missing, with these scores introduced for data collection from 2021. Many of these chronic Banff scores may lie between the causal pathway of acute TCMR and graft survival and therefore adjusting for these factors may lead to overadjustment bias and attenuate the true association.36 However, misclassification is unlikely to differ across exposure groups, as these processes are largely random across sites. Therefore, bias would likely attenuate rather than exaggerate the observed associations.

In conclusion, kidney transplant recipients with TCMR and clinically significant MVI phenotype had a higher risk of graft loss than those with TCMR alone, particularly when rejection with a “v” lesion was present. In contrast, subthreshold MVI (below the Banff ABMR threshold) was not associated with an increased risk. These findings support refining the TCMR classification to integrate MVI and emphasize the need for a mechanistic understanding of the immune pathways in TCMR phenotypes to inform future targeted therapies.
