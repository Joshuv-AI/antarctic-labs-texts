Fractal–fractional model for analysis of tuberculosis infection using Ethiopia incidence data
Authors: Kumama Regassa Cheneke, Fatmawati Fatmawati, Mustafa Bayram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, a fractional‐order SEIuITR model was proposed to examine the transmission of tuberculosis (TB) in the community. The proposed model was rigorously examined for well‐posedness. The basic reproduction number was also derived, and the disease‐free equilibrium was obtained. The local and global stabilities of the proposed model were also examined. The proposed model was fitted with the actual TB incidence in Ethiopia, while numerical simulations were carried out in MATLAB to validate the results obtained in this study. Local and global sensitivity analyses were carried out to determine the key parameters that influence the transmission of TB in the community. Phase‐plane portraits and phase‐plane diagrams demonstrate the dynamic process of the state variables. The results indicate that as the fractional‐order increases, the number of infections decreases due to the memory effect of early detection and treatment, while as the order decreases, the number of infections increases. Early detection and treatment have a profound effect on recovery and prevalence.Keywords: convolution theorem, Euler fractional method, fixed point theory, fractional‐order, Laplace transform, numerical simulation, tuberculosis

INTRODUCTION

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the most dangerous infectious diseases caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. One of the most important features of TB is its latent form, where people who have been in contact with the bacteria or are carriers may not even know their status.

The primary infection stage of TB is when the immune cells in the body recognize and fight off the invading bacteria. In some instances, the immune system is able to destroy the bacteria entirely [6, 7]. However, some of the bacteria may survive and live inside the body. One of the biggest problems in the fight against TB is that many people infected with the disease do not show symptoms during the early stages of infection. When symptoms do appear, they are usually mild and may include influenza‐like symptoms such as low‐grade fever, tiredness, and coughing. After the primary infection, a person may develop latent TB infection, where the immune system is able to hold off the bacteria by creating a barrier in the lungs. During this period, the bacteria remain alive but inactive, and no clinical symptoms are observed. Active TB develops when the immune system fails to suppress the infection, allowing the bacteria to proliferate and cause disease in the lungs or other parts of the body.

Active TB may occur shortly after initial infection or emerge months or even years later due to reactivation of latent infection. The symptoms of active TB typically worsen gradually and may include persistent cough, hemoptysis, chest pain, fever, chills, night sweats, loss of weight, loss of appetite, fatigue, and general malaise [8, 9].

The symptoms of this type of TB depend on the organs that are affected and may include the kidneys, liver, central nervous system, heart, lymph nodes, bones and joints, skin, blood vessels, and larynx. The symptoms include fever, night sweats, loss of weight, fatigue, pain, and loss of organ function. In children, especially infants, the symptoms of TB can be very different from those of adults. In adolescents, the symptoms may resemble those of adults, while in younger children, the symptoms may include fever, failure to gain weight, developmental delays, and neurological problems due to TB meningitis [7, 8].

Mathematical modeling is a key component in the study of complex phenomena in the real world by formulating them in a mathematical framework [10]. In the field of epidemiology, mathematical models have been extensively employed to describe the dynamics of disease transmission and assess the efficacy of control measures [11]. Traditional integer‐order dynamical systems have been employed to model TB transmission, including the role of exogenous reinfection and endogenous reactivation of recovered or pseudo‐recovered individuals [3, 12]. Nevertheless, these models have limitations in describing the memory and hereditary properties of TB, which is associated with long latent periods and delayed treatment responses.

Fractional‐order models have been recognized as useful tools for implementing memory effects and have been extensively employed to improve the accuracy and realism of infectious disease models [13, 14, 15]. Specifically, the non‐singular memory kernels of the Caputo–Fabrizio (CF) and Atangana–Baleanu–Caputo (ABC) operators make it possible to implement memory effects without encountering singularities, which is advantageous for modeling long‐term dynamics of infectious diseases.

The fractional‐order α measures the intensity of memory activation by incorporating the entire history of disease evolution into the current state of the system. Lower values of α indicate higher memory effects, which represent important epidemiological processes such as disease latency, waning immunity, and pathogen reactivation [16, 17]. These properties are particularly important in the case of TB, in which treatment compliance and late diagnosis are key factors in the persistence of the disease.

Recent research has emphasized the importance of awareness, reinfection, and effective intervention strategies in controlling TB transmission [9, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. However, TB still constitutes a major health problem worldwide, especially in developing nations. It is in light of these challenges that this paper proposes a CF fractional TB model that includes both informed and uninformed compartments to explore the effect of memory activation on TB transmission based on incidence data from Ethiopia.

DISCUSSION

This section provides an interpretation of the results of the suggested mathematical model for fractional‐order TB transmission. The discussion of results entails the model fitting, sensitivity analysis, reproduction number determination, fractional order dynamics, and phase plane dynamics, where every figure is analyzed in light of the epidemiological model. Figure 1 depicts the data on the TB incidence in relation to the model predictions of incidence from 2000 to 2023. From the results, it can be noted that there is good agreement between the model predictions and the observed data, hence suggesting that the estimated parameters effectively model the transmission dynamics of the disease. The figure below provides an illustration of the local sensitivity indices for the basic reproduction number R0 with respect to the model parameters. As can be seen from the results, β and Λ have the highest positive indices which imply that they contribute significantly in increasing R0 and promoting transmission of the infection.

On the other hand, parameters like μ,δ, and ω have negative sensitivity indices which imply that they contribute to the decrease in transmission and control of the disease. The figure below also represents the PRCC global sensitivity indices analysis for the basic reproduction number R0. As expected from the local analysis, β and Λ have very high positive correlations with R0 while μ,δ, and ω exhibit strong negative correlations.

In Figure 4, we present the behavior of the susceptible individuals with respect to time. It can be seen that the number of susceptible is decreasing in time due to the propagation of disease. Fractional order leads to slower decrease as memory effect reduces the rate of infection. In Figure 5, the behavior of the exposed group is shown. Initially, the exposed group rises with an increase in infection rates. Then it starts declining with an advancement towards either infectious or another compartment. Smaller values for α result in more persistence of the exposed group, showing delayed progression of TB infection. Figure 6 demonstrates the dynamics of the unreported infectious Iu. The obtained findings clearly demonstrate the effect of the fractional order on the magnitude of the unreported infections. It can be stated that a higher level of fractional order significantly decreases their occurrence and makes them appear later. Unreported infection plays a crucial role in TB transmission within the population. In Figure 7 the dynamics of active infection is presented.

The changes in the treatment population can be viewed in Figure 8. In view of the outcomes presented above, the number of treatments grows with increasing diagnosis and inclusion of patients into the process. However, a smaller fraction order leads to lower growth rates due to delays associated with the detection and treatment process. The change in the recovered population is depicted in Figure 9. As is seen from the outcomes provided above, recovery steadily increases because of treatment procedures performed. Recovery is characterized by delayed growth rates due to memory effects impacting disease development and treatment.

Figure 10 depicts the phase plane for susceptible S and infectious I populations. As can be seen from the outcome obtained, an increase in infection rate leads to a decrease in the susceptible population. This clearly indicates that the transmission of TB infection takes place as was expected from the outset. The relationship between the exposed E and infectious I populations is illustrated in Figure 11. As is evident, there is a smooth passage from latent infection to active disease state indicating stable and biological consistent dynamics. Phase plane between I and Iu populations is shown in Figure 12. The simulation shows both continue increasing for a while and both decrease after a long period. Hence, an unaware and aware population should be considered to control the dynamics of TB infection in Ethiopia. Phase plane between T and I populations is shown in Figure 13. It is evident from the outcomes that an increase in T results in a decrease in I, thereby highlighting the role of the effectiveness of treatment intervention in disease control. Phase plane between R and I is provided in Figure 14. It is noted that an increase in recovery occurs when infection is minimized over time. Phase plane between R0 and the parameters β and θ is shown in Figure 15. It is observed that an increase in either of the two parameters causes an increase in R0; hence the significance of these transmission parameters in disease persistence.

Finally, it is evident that the fractional‐order TB model proposed gives an effective way of modeling and studying TB dynamics. In addition, it has been observed that the use of memory effects in the model significantly enhances disease modeling, and sensitivity and phase plane analysis reveal the significant disease control parameters.

CONCLUSION

In this manuscript, a fractional‐order SEIuITR mathematical model was proposed and used to explore the transmission of TB. The basic properties of the model, including the existence, positivity, and boundedness of solutions, were proved to guarantee the feasibility of the model. The basic reproduction number R0 was calculated and used to establish the threshold condition for disease persistence and elimination. The model was successfully used to fit the TB incidence data from Ethiopia, and it was found that there was a good agreement between the model and real data, which confirmed the accuracy and reliability of the model. The sensitivity analysis was carried out and showed that the transmission rate β and the modification parameter θ are directly proportional to R0 and play a significant role in disease transmission, and the parameter ω associated with intervention is the most sensitive parameter in reducing R0 and thus is significant in disease control through strategies such as early diagnosis, treatment, and awareness. Additionally, the fractional‐order analysis showed that increasing the fractional‐order decreases the level of infection and improves stability, as a result of the memory effect included in the fractional derivative. Stability of the DFE was proved for R0<1, which confirms that TB can be controlled if appropriate intervention strategies are put in place. Future work can be carried out by adding other epidemiological parameters, which include vaccination, reinfection, and drug resistance, and developing optimal control strategies to determine how to control TB. Stochastic, spatial, and co‐infection factors, which include TB and lung cancer progression, can be included to make the model more realistic. This can be done in future work to make the model more applicable in supporting public health decisions and improving TB control strategies.
