Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) strains modulate inflammatory genes in RTgutGC cells, and triploid Chinook salmon (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha) challenged with V. anguillarum

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are a group of beneficial microorganisms often selected for probiotic applications [1,2]. These non-motile and non-sporulating Gram-positive bacteria, including the genera Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, are known for their ability to ferment sugars into lactic acid, which helps maintain a healthy balance of gut microbiota by inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria [[2], [3], [4]]. LAB contribute to digestive health by enhancing nutrient absorption, supporting immune function, and potentially alleviating symptoms of gastrointestinal disorders [1,5,6]. Additionally, they can promote overall well-being and contribute to a health-promoting microbiome, making them a valuable component in many functional foods and dietary supplements [[5], [6], [7]].

In aquaculture, LAB are increasingly recognized for their potential as probiotics to enhance the health and growth of various species [[8], [9], [10]], in part through reducing the presence of pathogenic bacteria and stabilizing the microbial balance of freshwater and saltwater systems [[11], [12], [13]]. Studies have shown that probiotic strains can help bolster the immune response of fish and shellfish, promote better digestion and nutrient absorption, and reduce the incidence of common diseases and infections [14,8,15]. Some strains can enhance growth rates and feed conversion efficiency, ultimately supporting sustainable and efficient aquaculture practices [7,16]. Thus, the use of LAB in aquaculture not only benefits the health of aquatic organisms, but also contributes to more environmentally friendly and cost-effective farming practices [8,17].

To date, studies have shown that rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) exhibit enhanced immune responses following supplementation with Lactobacillus spp., including increased superoxide anion production, serum lysozyme levels, non-specific humoral responses, and complement activity [18,19]. Additionally, supplementation with Lactococcus spp. has been reported to boost phagocytic activity in this species [[18], [19], [20]]. Moreover, Carnobacterium and Lactobacillus strains have shown to improve fish growth, lysozyme activity, and innate antiviral response in Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) by upregulating the expression of toll-like receptor 3 and interferon-alpha [21,22,16]. Recently, our research group was able to determine that long-term supplementation with a multi-strain probiotic containing different strains of Lactococcus spp., Lactobacillus spp., and Bifidobacterium spp., can modulate the early inflammatory response and increase survival rates in Vibrio anguillarum-infected Chinook salmon (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha), representing the first approach of a potential commercially available probiotics that can exert a benefit in this species [23]. While the role of LAB within the digestive tract of the two more studied salmonids (Oncorhynchus mykiss and Salmo salar) has been widely studied, it is imperative to better understand the role that single or dual LAB supplementation can have in non-traditional aquaculture species, such as Chinook salmon. In addition, it has been suggested that human-derived probiotics may negatively impact tissue integrity and could even lead to host death. Therefore, it is crucial to test the safety of the strain as a supplement in Chinook salmon, despite its previous success in other salmonid species [24].

Chinook salmon, also known as King salmon, is an anadromous species native to the sub-arctic North Pacific Ocean [25]. Renowned for its size and high-quality flesh, this species is highly valued by Indigenous communities, sport fishers, and commercial fisheries [23,[26], [27], [28]]. However, the popularity of Chinook salmon has led to a decline in wild stocks over the past decade [25,28], making aquaculture an increasingly viable alternative for both commercial and conservation efforts. Since Chinook salmon are semelparous, their flesh quality deteriorates as they approach reproduction. To mitigate this, aquaculture operations often use sterile triploid fish, which help maintain flesh quality and extend harvest periods [29,30]. However, triploid Chinook salmon exhibit 10–30 % higher mortality rates and increased susceptibility to diseases compared to their diploid counterparts [15]. This heightened disease vulnerability underscores the potential benefits of probiotic supplementation for triploid Chinook salmon.

Understanding the mechanism by which lactic acid bacteria (LAB) strains modulate the digestive tract of Chinook salmon is crucial for their effective application in commercial aquaculture and can help to narrow down the strains to be tested in this fish species. This study evaluated three lyophilized LAB products currently available in the market: Limosilactobacillus reuteri LRE2, Limosilactobacillus reuteri 830, and a mixture of Limosilactobacillus reuteri RC-14 and Limosilactobacillus rhamnosus GR-1. As a field trial was planned on Chinook salmon for the first time, an initial in vitro evaluation was performed using the RTgutGC cell line, derived from rainbow trout, to assess the strain effects on cell viability and immune gene expression. This was followed by a study on Chinook salmon's survival, growth, and expression of tight junction and immune molecules in response to bacterial infection following supplementation of the three lyophilized LAB products in feed pellets over four months. V. anguillarum, a common aquatic pathogen that afflicts farmed fish worldwide was utilized as an infection model in this study.

Materials and methods

In vitro evaluation of the inoculation with LAB in RTgutGCTissue culture maintenance

The rainbow trout intestinal epithelial cell line RTgutGC was cultured as described by Kawano et al. [31] with modifications. Briefly, cells were grown in a 75 cm2 culture flask with Leibovitz’s 15 media (HyClone, Cytiva), supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated FBS and incubated in plates or flasks sealed with Parafilm (Bemis) at 22 °C with atmospheric conditions. Cells were sub-cultured every 2–3 weeks.

To prepare plates for the cell viability and gene expression studies, cells were washed with 4 mL sterile PBS at room temperature (RT), and residual buffer was aspirated. Then, 4 mL of trypsin (0.05 % w/v; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added and cells were incubated for 10 min at RT in a flask to facilitate detachment. Upon detachment, 8 mL of culture medium (L-15–10 % FBS) was added to inactivate the trypsin protease activity and the cell suspension was transferred to 15 mL tubes (Fisher Scientific). Cells were then centrifuged at 400 x g, at 4 °C for 10 min. Supernatants were discarded and pellets were resuspended in 10 mL of culture medium. Cells were counted using a hemacytometer (Hausser Scientific) and seeded into 96-well plates (cell viability) or 12-well plates (RNA extraction) for following experiments.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The bacterial strains L. reuteri LRE2 and L. reuteri 830 were generously provided by Seed Health, Inc. The strain mixture consisted in L. reuteri RC-14 and L. rhamnosus GR-1 was commercially available (Pro-B, RepHresh™). The above-mentioned strains were routinely cultured anaerobically at 37 °C in Mann, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) medium (BD Difco).

Cell viability assay in RTgutGC cells exposed to LAB

RTgutGC cell viability was quantified after exposure to L. reuteri LRE2, L. reuteri 830, or L. reuteri RC-14 + L. rhamnosus GR-1 using AlamarBlue™ cell viability reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). AlamarBlue™ was used to monitor the reduction of resazurin to resorufin by metabolically active cells. RTgutGC cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well on a 96-well plate and incubated overnight at 22 °C in l-15 + 10 % FBS. The medium was aspirated, and the cells were treated with three different concentrations of each probiotic treatment (103, 105, and 107). After 24 h of incubation, cells were washed with PBS and the medium was replaced with 100 μL of AlamarBlue™ and incubated 1–4 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quantification of fluorescence was performed using a Take3 plate on a Synergy H1 Hybrid plate Reader (Biotek Instruments, Inc., USA) at excitation wavelength of 560 nm and emission wavelength of 590 nm. RTgutGC viability for each treatment/concentration was calculated using the time 0 cells as a reference for 100 % viability.

RNA extraction in RTgutGC cells exposed to LAB

RNA samples were obtained from RTgutGC exposed to either L. reuteri LRE2, L. reuteri 830, or L. reuteri RC-14 + L. rhamnosus GR-1 at a concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/mL. Cells were treated with the respective concentration for 24 h prior RNA extraction.

To prepare RNA for subsequent gene expression analyses, RTgutGC samples were homogenized using 1 mL of TRIzol following the manufacturers protocol (Invitrogen). Samples were vortexed for 15 s, incubated at RT for 10 min, then centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous layer was collected and transferred to a new 1.7 mL tube. Samples were kept on ice henceforward. One volume of 100 % isopropanol per 1 vol of sample was added to the new aqueous fractions, samples were vortexed briefly, and incubated at RT for 5 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C, and supernatant was discarded. After washing the RNA thrice with 70 % ethanol, the residual ethanol was carefully removed, and pellets were air-dried for 15 min. The RNA was then resuspended in 40 μL of nuclease-free water and allowed to dissolve in a water bath at 55 °C for 15 min.

After extraction, the RNA was treated with Ambion DNase I (RNase free) (Ambion™ DNase I, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions to degrade any residual genomic DNA. RNA samples were quantified and evaluated for purity (A260/280 and A260/230 ratios) using a Take3 plate of a Synergy H1 Hybrid plate Reader (Biotek Instruments, Inc., USA). Column purified RNA samples had A260/280 ratios between 2.0 and 2.1 and A260/230 ratios between 2.0 and 2.2.

First-strand cDNA templates for qPCR were synthesized from 250 ng of DNaseI-treated, column-purified total RNA using qScript cDNA Supermix (Quanta Biosciences) and DEPC-treated water to a maximum volume of 10 μL following the manufacturer's instructions. Each sample was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min, at 42 °C for 30 min, and at 85 °C for 5 min. Samples were stored at −20 °C until utilization.

qPCR in RTgutGC cells exposed to LAB

All qPCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 10 μL, containing 5 µL of 2x WISENT ADVANCED™ qPCR master mix (Wisent, Canada), 2.5 µL of forward and reverse primer mix (Sigma Aldrich, USA) at a final concentration of 0.5 µM each, and 2.5 µL of cDNA (2.5 ng/µL, 6.25 ng per reaction). All samples were amplified and detected using the LightCycler® 480 II (Roche, USA). The reaction mixtures were pre-incubated for 2 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation for 5 s at 95 °C, annealing for 30 s at 60 °C, and finally extension for 8 s at 72 °C. The melt curve to ensure primer specificity was completed for each run every 5 s from 65 °C to 97 °C.

Gene discovery, qPCR primer design, and initial quality testing were performed as described in Soto-Davila et al. [32]. In vivo evaluation of the inoculation with LAB in Chinook salmon

Fish holding

Triploid Chinook salmon, Oncorhynchus tshawytscha (10 ± 2 g) were obtained from Yellow Island Aquaculture Ltd. (www.yellowislandaquaculture.ca, Quadra Island, British Columbia, Canada), and maintained in 12 Powell River troughs (500 L) in freshwater (FW, 14 ± 2 °C) at a density of ∼8 kg/m3 using a flow-through system under natural photoperiod (12:12 h dark:light). Five treatments were conducted to evaluate the effect of LAB probiotics administered to Chinook salmon, each treatment in duplicates, with 150 fish per tank (300 fish per treatment, 1500 fish in total). Until four months before the infection trial, the fish were fed commercial dry pellet (EWOS Harmony 2 mm: 47 % protein, 18 % fat, 0.7 % fiber, 2.9 % calcium, 1.2 % phosphorus, and 0.6 % sodium) twice a day with a ration of 2 % body weight, in addition to their respective dietary treatments.

All fish were kept and handled under a permit (ethical protocol #43,212) from the University of Waterloo Animal Care Committee according to CCAC guidelines.

Feeding and probiotic supplement preparation

Commercial Chinook salmon dry food (EWOS Harmony 2 mm) was used as a control diet for the supplementation of probiotic. Additionally, a sodium alginate control, the component utilized to maintain the probiotic stick to the pellet, was tested. The probiotic treatments used in this experiment were the following: i) L. reuteri LRE2; ii) L. reuteri 830; iii) L. reuteri RC-14/L. rhamnosus GR-1. Fish were fed for four months prior to the infection challenge.

V. anguillarum growth conditions

V. anguillarum serotype O1 (J382) [44] isolated from winter Steelhead trout obtained from Little Campbell River (British Columbia, Canada) was utilized for the infection trial. Briefly, a single colony of V. anguillarum from a TSA 2 % NaCl (Trypticase Soy Agar 2 % Sodium Chloride) stock was grown in 2.5 mL of Trypticase Soy Broth 2 % sodium chloride (TSB 2 % NaCl; Multicell Wisent, Quebec, Canada) at 20 °C in a 16 mm diameter glass tube and placed in a shaker for 24 h at 200 rpm. After growth, 150 μL of the overnight culture were added in 150 mL of TSB 2 % NaCl media using a 250 mL flask and incubated for 24 h at 20 °C with aeration (200 rpm). After overnight culture, the bacterial inoculum was centrifuged at 2500 x g at RT for 10 min. The pellet was washed three times with PBS and centrifuged at 2500 x g at RT for 10 min and finally resuspended in 25 mL of PBS (∼7.8 × 108 CFU mL−1). The concentrated bacterial inoculum was serially diluted and quantified by plating onto TSA 2 % NaCl for 2 days.

LAB supplemented Chinook salmon challenged with V. anguillarum

After four months under control diet, control coating diet, or LAB supplementation (L. reuteri LRE2; L. reuteri 830; L. reuteri RC-14/L. rhamnosus GR-1), Chinook salmon juveniles were intraperitoneally (I.P.) injected with 100 µL of live V. anguillarum (1 × 105 CFU mL−1). Time 0 (n = 5 fish per tank per treatment) corresponds to fish sampled before the injection. Fish survival was recorded for 18 days, and 5 fish were sampled 0, 1-, 3-, 7-, and 14-days post‐inoculation (dpi) (25 fish in total per treatment). For sampling, fish were anesthetized with a non-lethal dose of clove oil, followed by euthanasia by cervical dislocation. Growth data (forklength and weight) was collected, and then spleen and hindgut tissues were isolated and immediately snap‐frozen in liquid N2 prior to being stored at −80 °C until utilization.

RNA extraction

For detailed information on RNA extraction and DNase treatment, please refer to Section 2.1.4. Whole tissue RNA samples were resuspended in 100 μL of nuclease-free water and allowed to dissolve in a water bath at 55 °C for 15 min.

First-strand cDNA templates for qPCR were synthesized from 250 ng of DNaseI-treated, column-purified total RNA using qScript cDNA Supermix (Quanta Biosciences) following the manufacturer's instructions. Each sample was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min, at 42 °C for 30 min, and at 85 °C for 5 min. Samples were stored at −20 °C until utilization.

qPCR analysis

All qPCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 10 μL, containing 5 µL of 2x WISENT ADVANCED™ qPCR master mix (Wisent, Quebec, Canada), 2.5 µL of forward and reverse primer mix (Sigma Aldrich, USA) at a final concentration of 0.25 µM each, and 2.5 µL of cDNA (2.5 ng/µL, 6.25 ng per reaction). All samples were amplified and detected using the LightCycler® 480 II (Roche, USA). The reaction mixtures were pre-incubated for 2 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation for 5 s at 95 °C, annealing for 30 s at 60 °C, and finally extension for 8 s at 72 °C. The melt curve was completed for each run every 5 s from 65 °C to 97 °C.

The primer sequences of interleukin 1 beta (il1b), interleukin 8 (il8), interleukin 10 (il10), interleukin 17a (il17a), transforming growth factor-beta (tgfb), Myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (myd88), zonula occludens-1 (zo-1), junction adhesion molecule 1 alpha (jam1a), occludin (ocln), e-cadherin (cdh1), tricellulin (marveld2), claudin 15 (cldn15), claudin 28b (cldn28b), mucin 2 (muc2), and villin 2 (vil2) used in this study are listed in Table 1. Gene discovery, qPCR primer design and initial quality testing were performed as described in Soto-Davila et al. [45]. Since the reagents, cycling conditions and samples were different in the current study, primer efficiencies were reassessed. Briefly, a 7-point 1:3 dilution series starting with cDNA representing 5 ng of input total RNA was generated, and efficiencies then calculated using the formula E = 10(−1/slope) [33].

Transcript levels of the target genes were normalized to transcript levels of three endogenous control genes. Levels of five candidate normalizers [ef1a, beta actin (actb), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (gapdh), 18S ribosomal RNA (18S), and internal transcribed spacer 2 (its2)] were assessed in 50 % of the samples (i.e., in 3 random samples per treatment) using cDNA representing 2.5 ng (6.25 ng per reaction) of input total RNA. Reference gene stability was assessed using geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, and Delta Ct comparison, through the bioinformatic open-access portal RefFinder [32,46]. Most stable genes assessed were its2, gapdh and actb.

After normalization testing was completed, transcript levels of the genes of interest were analyzed in the individual study samples, with normalization to the average of the three endogenous genes selected as described previously [47]. In all cases, levels were assessed (in technical triplicates) in five individuals per treatment per time-point using cDNA representing 2.5 ng/µL of input total RNA (6.25 ng/reaction). A -RT control was included on each plate. Gene expression was determined using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method [43].

Statistical analyses

Cell viability and RTgutGC gene expression data are shown as the mean ± standard error (SE). Assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were tested for the detected variances. RTgutGC cell viability data was analyzed using a two-way ANOVA test, followed by Sidak multiple comparisons post hoc test to identify significant differences of each treatment in different times or concentrations and between treatments in the same time point. For qPCR data, a Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric test was performed. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Fish survival, growth, and gene expression data are shown as the mean ± standard error (SEM). Assumptions of variance, normality, and homogeneity were tested. A two‐way ANOVA was performed using the different treatments and time-points as factors of variance, followed by a Tukey post-hoc test to identify differences between groups. Differences were considered significant at p < .05 (*), p < .01 (**) and p < .001 (***).

All statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA v7.0 (StatSoft software, Tulsa, USA) and graphs performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla California USA).

Discussion

The mechanisms by which LAB produce local and systemic effects in Chinook salmon are not understood, but it is thought that after entering the gut, these beneficial microbes would supplement commensal bacteria and stimulate the immune cells associated with the mucosa, and these, in turn, would amplify the effect when translocated into immune organs [48,49,3]. The in vitro analysis of the aforementioned parameters is a fundamental step to investigate the mechanism of action and determine the safety of the strains in salmonids, before assessing this effect in live Chinook salmon.

Even at high concentrations (107), the strains selected did not impact RTgutGC viability. This is in agreement with previous studies on large concentrations of other probiotic strains on the viability of fish intestinal mucus and epithelial cells of Oreochromis niloticus and Miichthys miiuy respectively [50,51]. These data suggested that L. reuteri LRE2, L. reuteri 830, and L. reuteri RC-14/ L. rhamnosus GR-1 would be unlikely to have negative effects when administered to Chinook salmon in feed.

In addition to determining the safety in RTgutGC cells, examining the modulation of immune parameters is important to determine if probiotic supplementation could induce a positive impact in farm raised Chinook salmon. A previous study has shown that stimulation with functional feed ingredients such as mannanoligosaccharides and beta-glucan can increase the expression of immune canonical genes such as il1b, il6, and tnfa in RTgutGC [38]. Interestingly, our results show a differential expression of il1b and il6 expression in L. reuteri LRE2 stimulated cells compared to L. reuteri 830 and L. reuteri RC-14/ L. rhamnosus GR-1 treatments. Additionally, RTgutGC cells incubated with L. reuteri 830 were the only group to show a downregulation of tnfa compared to the time 0 control. Although an increase of the immune genes was expected based on previous evidence, the differential response observed among LAB strains made promising candidates for an in vivo trial. Researchers have debated whether a single probiotic strain is sufficient [52], or multiple strains are better by contributing synergetic effects against multi-factorial diseases [53,52]. The in vitro tests showing that the LAB strains selected had no signs of detrimental effects on viability, led to testing in living fish.

The effectiveness of probiotic supplementation as protection against infectious pathogens has been widely studied in salmonids. LAB strains, such as L. sakei, L. lactis, L. mesenteroides, Bacillus sp., and Carnobacterium sp., delivered either in food or bioencapsulation, have helped to increase the survival rate of S. salar and O. mykiss after challenge with the main fish pathogens A. salmonicida, V. ordalii, Yersinia ruckeri, and V. anguillarum [18,54,55]. Additionally, supplementation with P. acidilactici MA18/5 M has been shown to be safe to use in Chinook salmon aquaculture [15]. However, in the present study, fish supplemented with L. reuteri LRE2, L. reuteri 830, and L. reuteri RC-14/ L. rhamnosus GR-1 did not show a differential increase in survival when challenged with V. anguillarum. Although it has been reported that probiotic supplementation may not necessarily lead to significant protection against diseases [56,57], one explanation for failure here could be that the strains did not grow and persist in the Chinook salmon gut, and that the intraperitoneal injection of V. anguillarum had already infected the fish organs.

In triploid Chinook salmon juveniles, LAB supplementation did not alter fish weight after four months of supplementation and during the V. anguillarum challenge. Although previous publications have claimed that supplementation of certain LAB strains can promote growth in salmonid species [58,20], other studies have found no such effect [56,57,59]. Recent studies with either P. acidilactici MA18/5 M or a multi-strain product did not improve growth parameters between 4 months and 14 months in Chinook salmon [15,23]. It has been hypothesized that LAB enhances nutrient absorption [3]. It is not clear if the strains used here lack the metabolic repertoire needed to affect Chinook salmon weight over four months, or they simply did not have sufficient time to grow and persist in the gut. Further studies should focus on increasing LAB supplementation during the whole production cycle and using transcriptomic analysis to determine if the strains are indeed metabolically active in the salmon gut.

The spleen works as a secondary lymphoid organ in fish, playing a key role in haematopoiesis, antigen degradation and antibody production [60]. Over the course of infection, an inflammatory insult will result in a cascade whereby il1b will increase, followed by il8 upregulation [61]. In spleen tissues collected in this study, a sustained inflammatory response was observed after one day of infection with V. anguillarum and lasted up to 7 dpi. This response was not different between L. reuteri LRE2, L. reuteri 830, and L. reuteri RC-14/ L. rhamnosus GR-1 supplemented fish, indicating the host was responding to the infection but there was no interfering probiotic effect. The data suggest that the anti-inflammatory response in Chinook salmon was mediated by il10, a key and fast regulator of the immune system, instead of tgfb, which plays an important role as an immune modulator of T cell activity [62].

In teleosts, the intestine plays a key role in physiological processes such as digestion, absorption of nutrients, osmoregulation, and immune response [38]. Probiotic supplementation has been shown to improve immune and epithelial parameters, especially upregulating cellular and humoral immune responses at the intestinal mucosal interface [63,64]. For instance, Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout supplemented with P. acidilactici 18MA/5 M, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum subsp. plantarum CLFP 3, or L. rhamnosus GG have been shown to significantly upregulate the expression of the proinflammatory markers il1b, il8, and tnfa [21,65,66,58]. Immune gene upregulation, in addition to significantly lower mortality rates, is needed to determine that probiotic-mediated modulation of immune parameters might be a mechanism for improvement in survival during bacterial infections [67]. However, survival and immune response modulation were not improved by the LAB strains tested in this study. Chinook salmon supplemented with L. reuteri 830 were the only group to have a significant increase in the expression of il8 compared to the sodium alginate control treatment, however, this did not lead to differences in survival and growth. Further studies could examine long-term probiotic supplementation as well as multi-strain or multi-species probiotic supplementation.

The complex milieu of the salmonid gut is composed of several inter-dependent factors. These include a rich microbial community, mucosal epithelial cells, secreted antimicrobial peptides & antibodies, mucus, microbial metabolites, and resident host immune cells, which together greatly impact organismal health [68]. Probiotic-mediated improvement of gut barrier function has been positively associated with health benefits in several vertebrate species [69]. Nonetheless, their interaction with tight junction molecules and mechanism of improving gut health in salmonids remains unknown. Tight junctions consist of large, dynamic protein complexes that form the circumferential seal between adjacent epithelial cells. Some of the main protein families found in tight junctions are claudins, occludins, junction-associated membrane proteins (JAM), and zonula occludens (ZO-1) [70]. Due to this, we selected tight junction molecules representative of four main characteristics of the intestinal epithelium. The tight junction molecules, zo-1, jam1a, ocln, cdh1, and marveld2, are implicated in the barrier-forming cell-cell connections [35]. The second group, cldn15 and cldn28b, has pore-forming abilities that enables the selectively permeable transport of compounds across the epithelial barrier [71,72]. The third is mucus secretion, represented by muc2, which is a key structural component of the colonic mucus layer [73]. Finally, vil1, is a Ca2+-dependent actin binding protein involved in the structural remodelling and nucleation of microvilli [74]. After four months of LAB supplementation and 14 days of infection with V. anguillarum, there was no evidence that tight junction molecules evaluated in this study were modulated differently from the regular fish diet.

We did not detect improvements in host gut barrier with L. reuteri LRE2, L. reuteri 830, and L. reuteri RC-14/ L. rhamnosus GR-1. The lack of modulation in presence of V. anguillarum might be associated with the IP injection utilized. The interaction of host and pathogen might have occurred in other organs such as spleen, head kidney, or blood, instead of intestine. It would be interesting to test if oral gavage of the pathogen led to different outcomes.

In terms of limitations, the observed effects were only descriptive of the host and microbe species in which these studies were conducted and cannot be generalized to all probiotics or other fish species. The investigation of the innate and adaptive immune response in other relevant immune organs in teleosts (such as head kidney, gills, and skin) could have provided a clearer picture of the effects of LAB supplementation in Chinook salmon during infection. The transit time for food, and the embedded bacteria, through the intestine of the salmon is approximately 12 h, meaning that the dried organisms have a short period to hydrate, become metabolically active and exit their sodium alginate covering to make an impact via the microbiota and epithelial layer before being excreted [75]. Future studies using a delivery system that retains the probiotic strains for longer would be worthwhile.

Conclusion

The present study provides novel information on how three LAB strains interact with RTgutGC, and triploid Chinook salmon reared under aquaculture conditions. In vitro results indicated that L. reuteri LRE2, L. reuteri 830, and L. reuteri RC-14/L. rhamnosus GR-1 do not have cytotoxic effects at different concentrations but do produce distinct modulation patterns of the inflammatory response. The field trial, however, did not show improvements in survival, growth, immune response, or gut barrier function compared to regular feed. While these effects were small, they indicate that the tested probiotics are not deleterious to Chinook salmon growth and can influence immune gene expression. Together, these findings provide a valuable baseline for understanding host–probiotic–pathogen interactions in this species. Future studies should explore longer supplementation periods, multi-strain or fish-specific formulations, and alternative pathogen delivery methods to better assess their potential to enhance disease resistance and gut health in Chinook salmon aquaculture.
