Acute Coronary Syndrome Linked to Massive Coronary Artery Aneurysms as Late Complications of Kawasaki Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Halime Tanrıverdi, Elif Durak Ediboğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKawasaki disease (KD) is an acute childhood vasculitis that may cause late coronary complications. We report a 25‐year‐old man with childhood KD who presented with acute coronary syndrome without conventional cardiovascular risk factors. High‐sensitivity troponin T was elevated, and transthoracic echocardiography showed regional wall motion abnormalities with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. Coronary angiography revealed subtotal ostial left anterior descending artery stenosis with a giant aneurysm and a giant right coronary artery aneurysm. The heart team decided to proceed with coronary artery bypass grafting. Clinicians should consider late KD‐related sequelae in young adults with acute coronary syndrome.

Introduction

Coronary artery ectasia is defined as dilatation of a coronary artery segment exceeding 1.5 times the diameter of an adjacent normal vessel segment (Xi et al. 2023). Coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) represent a localized form of ectasia and are characterized by focal dilatation of the coronary arterial wall. CAAs may be associated with atherosclerotic disease, familial hyperlipidemia, and inflammatory or connective tissue diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus, scleroderma, and Kawasaki disease (KD) (West et al. 2025; Aboeata et al. 2012).

KD is an acute systemic vasculitis that primarily affects children under 5 years of age. It is characterized by fever, cervical lymphadenopathy, rash, bilateral non‐exudative conjunctivitis, extremity changes, strawberry tongue, and erythematous lips (McCrindle et al. 2017). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is routinely used by pediatric cardiologists to evaluate and monitor coronary artery involvement, which may lead to late complications such as CAAs (de Ferranti et al. 2018).

Although the acute phase of KD typically resolves with treatment, its cardiovascular sequelae may persist into adulthood, including CAAs, coronary stenosis, thrombosis, and myocardial infarction. In this report, we present a young adult patient with acute coronary syndrome associated with giant CAAs as late complications of KD.

Case Report

A 25‐year‐old man presented to the emergency department with resting retrosternal chest pain that had started intermittently the previous day and worsened overnight. He had no known chronic diseases, conventional cardiovascular risk factors, or history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or illicit drug use. He also had no family history of premature cardiovascular disease. According to his family, he had been hospitalized at the age of five with high fever and desquamation of the hands and mouth and was diagnosed with Kawasaki disease; however, pediatric medical records were unavailable, childhood coronary involvement could not be confirmed, and he remained asymptomatic without structured cardiovascular follow‐up until adulthood. On admission, his vital signs were stable, with a blood pressure of 118/70 mmHg, oxygen saturation of 98%, and respiratory rate of 14/min. Cardiac auscultation revealed rhythmic S1 and S2 sounds without additional heart sounds. No pretibial edema was observed.

Twelve‐lead electrocardiography revealed a sinus rhythm at 66 beats per minute without any ischemic changes (Figure 1). Laboratory tests showed an elevated high‐sensitivity troponin T level of 69.46 ng/L (reference range: 0–14 ng/L). Other biochemical parameters, complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and C‐reactive protein were within normal limits. The lipid profile was also normal, with a total cholesterol level of 158 mg/dL and an LDL cholesterol level of 93 mg/dL.

Admission 12‐lead electrocardiogram.

TTE revealed apical and anterolateral wall motion abnormalities, and left ventricular ejection fraction was measured as 45%. The left main coronary artery and circumflex artery were found to be normal during the coronary angiography. A subtotal stenosis and a highly calcified, hazy‐appearing giant aneurysm were detected in the osteal of the left anterior descending artery (LAD). Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) Grade 2 Flow was also observed (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5). Additionally, a massive aneurysm was identified in the proximal right coronary artery (RCA) (Figure 6).

The findings indicated a substantial, approximately 99% occlusion of the osteal LAD. Adequate balloon pre‐dilation of the severe calcified occlusion was impossible and resolution with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was impossible (Figures 4 and 5); therefore, the cardiac team planned a coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) for the patient with extensive anterior ischemia. Due to the patient's elevated high‐sensitivity troponin levels, hypokinesis of the apical and anterolateral walls observed on TTE, and TIMI Grade 2 Flow in the LAD, early surgical intervention was scheduled.

The patient underwent surgery on the same day. A left internal mammary artery graft to the LAD was performed, and after ligation/exclusion of the aneurysmal RCA segment, a saphenous vein graft from the aorta to the posterior descending artery was placed (Figure 7). The operation was completed successfully.

Discussion

This case highlights the need for heightened awareness of late cardiovascular complications of KD and the importance of considering a remote or undocumented history of KD in young adults presenting with acute coronary syndrome and CAAs. Although late coronary sequelae of KD have been previously described, this case is clinically instructive because the patient had no conventional cardiovascular risk factors, had remained asymptomatic without structured cardiovascular follow‐up since childhood, and presented in adulthood with acute coronary syndrome associated with bilateral giant CAAs requiring urgent surgical revascularization.

KD is a medium‐vessel vasculitis characterized by its tendency to affect the coronary arteries.

The extent of CA involvement can vary from transient mild dilatation or ectasia to gigantic coronary artery aneurysms (Pilania et al. 2020). Advanced imaging techniques, including coronary computed tomography angiography and magnetic resonance angiography, are essential for diagnosing and monitoring coronary abnormalities in patients with KD, especially when invasive angiography is impractical or when screening asymptomatic individuals suspected of having childhood KD.

In a recent study, the CAA regression rate was reported as 75% in one cohort, which is higher than the rate previously reported (Friedman et al. 2016). In a significant pre‐intravenous immunoglobulin era, which performed serial coronary angiography in KD patients with CAA, it demonstrated a regression rate of approximately 55%–60%, typically occurring within 1–2 years after disease onset. However, patients with giant aneurysms did not experience regression (Lao et al. 2025).

Giant coronary aneurysms (defined as > 8 mm in diameter) are associated with an increased risk of thrombosis, rupture, and myocardial infarction, even decades after the resolution of the acute illness. These aneurysms may remain clinically silent until they present with life‐threatening complications. Although there is no standardized approach to antithrombotic therapy in adult patients with large CAAs, the risk of thrombosis may be reduced through the use of combination therapy that includes antiplatelet agents and anticoagulants.

Acute coronary syndrome is the primary cause of cardiovascular mortality and is generally associated with middle‐aged or elderly individuals with established risk factors and typical ischemia symptoms. Nevertheless, in young adults presenting with chest pain, KD should be considered as a potential etiology, particularly in the absence of conventional cardiovascular risk factors. On the other hand, in adults, a history of KD is often unclear or undocumented, making the diagnosis of late coronary complications more challenging, especially in young adults.

In conclusion, prolonged monitoring is crucial for patients with a history of KD and coronary involvement, due to the persistent risk of thrombotic events, ischemia, and graft failure, even decades post‐acute disease. Early intervention is essential in acute coronary syndrome despite young age. Multimodal imaging techniques (TTE, angiography) are important in diagnosis and surgical planning.
