Effect of multilayer laser cladding on the microstructure and wear resistance of SiC/Ni60A coatings
Authors: Zhen Wang, Changbao Qi, Kai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A fiber laser was used to perform multiple laser cladding passes on AISI 1045 steel to fabricate SiC particle-reinforced Ni60A alloy coatings. The microstructure and phase composition of the cladding layers were analyzed using optical microscopy, X-ray diffraction scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive spectroscopy. The microhardness and wear performance of coatings with different numbers of cladding layers were tested using a microhardness tester and a wear testing machine. The results show that the SiC/Ni60A composite coating, prepared by synchronously adding 20% SiC to a Ni60A matrix during laser cladding, is primarily composed of phases such as FeN3, Gr0.19Fe0.7Ni0.11, Gr23C6, and Ni31Si12.In the single-layer cladding coating, blocky crystals dominate the microstructure, whereas dendritic structures are predominant in the double- and triple-layer coatings. At the interface between the first and second layers of the four-layer coating, the maximum tensile stress reaches 350 MPa. As the number of coating layers increases, the number of cracks and pores increases, while the average microhardness and wear resistance decrease. For repairing components with deep damage (greater than 0.5 mm), two- or three-layer cladding should be employed, resulting in a coating thickness of approximately 1.5–2.5 mm.For the two-layer SiC/Ni60A composite coating, the average microhardness of the cladding layer is 962 HV, 4.3 times that of the AISI 1045 steel substrate. When using two-layer laser cladding, the relative wear of the cladding layer reaches 0.0018 g, and the wear resistance at this time is 4.7 times that of the substrate. The primary wear mechanism of the multilayer SiC/Ni60A cladding is adhesive wear, accompanied by abrasive wear.

Introduction

Key components of rail vehicles, such as axles, gears, and bearing housings, operate under high loads, strong impacts, and complex corrosive environments, making them highly susceptible to surface wear and fatigue cracking1–3. These failures not only shorten the service life of components but also pose potential threats to operational safety. Direct replacement of new components is costly and time-consuming. Therefore, developing efficient and reliable remanufacturing and repair technologies is of great engineering and economic significance for extending equipment life, reducing maintenance costs, and promoting green manufacturing.

Currently, the commonly used technologies for component repair mainly include traditional arc welding and thermal spraying4–6. However, arc welding involves high heat input, which can easily cause workpiece deformation, microstructural coarsening, and a high dilution rate. Thermal spraying, on the other hand, produces coatings that are mechanically bonded to the substrate, resulting in low bonding strength and insufficient coating density. As an advanced surface modification and additive manufacturing technology, laser cladding stands out for its high energy density, controllable heat input, rapid cooling rate, and low dilution. This technique can produce high-performance coatings that are metallurgically bonded to the substrate, with dense microstructures, fine grains, and controllable chemical compositions7,8. Consequently, it enables surface strengthening and damage repair of base materials and is regarded as one of the most promising technologies in the field of remanufacturing9.

Among the various cladding materials, the nickel-based self-fluxing alloy Ni60A has been widely applied due to its excellent wettability, self-fluxing ability, and overall mechanical properties10. However, its hardness and wear resistance remain insufficient under extreme working conditions. To further enhance its performance, researchers often introduce hard ceramic phases to fabricate composite coatings. Among these, silicon carbide (SiC) particles are considered an ideal reinforcement phase because of their high hardness, excellent wear resistance, good thermal stability, and relatively low cost. Numerous studies have confirmed that during the laser cladding process, SiC particles partially decompose and react in situ with metallic elements such as Ni, Fe, and Cr to form various high-hardness carbides and silicides (e.g. Cr7C3, Ni31Si12)11. These in situ formed strengthening phases exhibit better interfacial bonding with the substrate and significantly improve the microhardness and wear resistance of the cladding layer. For instance, Y. T. Pei et al. reported that the addition of SiC to Ni-based materials markedly enhances the coating’s microhardness and wear resistance12. Similarly, Wenjie Chen et al. fabricated a Ni35 + 20%SiC + 20% Ni/MoS2 self-lubricating coating on AISI 1045 steel via laser cladding, achieving superior overall wear and corrosion resistance13.

Although extensive research has been conducted by scholars at home and abroad on the microstructure and properties of single-layer laser-cladded SiC/Ni-based composite coatings, and considerable progress has been achieved, challenges remain in practical industrial repair applications. For components with deep damage, a single cladding layer often fails to achieve the required repair thickness. When the surface damage depth of the component is greater than 0.5 mm, the thickness of the repair cladding coating needs to be between 1.5–2.5 mm. Therefore, we often need to use multi-layer and multi-channel laser cladding technology14.This process introduces complex thermal cycling effects, as the subsequent layers repeatedly reheat the previously deposited ones, effectively subjecting the coating to a series of non-standard heat treatments. Such repeated thermal cycles significantly influence the microstructure of the cladding layer—including grain morphology, second-phase precipitation, and distribution—as well as the accumulation and distribution of residual stresses15. These effects may further induce the formation and propagation of defects such as cracks and pores, leading to non-uniform hardness distribution, reduced wear resistance, and even deteriorated bonding strength with the substrate15. At present, there is still a lack of in-depth and systematic studies on how the number of cladding layers, as a key process parameter, affects the microstructural evolution of SiC/Ni60A composite coatings and its correlation with macroscopic properties, particularly wear resistance16.

In this context, AISI 1045 steel, a commonly used material for rail vehicle components, was selected as the substrate for systematic laser cladding experiments involving one to four layers of SiC/Ni60A composite coatings. The objective of this study is to investigate in depth the effects of cumulative thermal cycling during multi-layer cladding on the phase composition, microstructure, defect formation, microhardness, and wear properties of the coatings, as well as to elucidate their wear mechanisms. This research aims to reveal the intrinsic relationships among process parameters, microstructure evolution, and performance in multi-layer cladding, thereby providing a solid theoretical foundation and experimental support for the development of high-performance and high-reliability laser remanufacturing processes for AISI 1045 steel and other high-strength steel components.

Materials and methods for the experiment

Experimental materials

To ensure consistency and reproducibility of experimental conditions, multi-layer laser cladding of SiC/Ni60A coating was performed on a standard substrate. The experimental substrate material is AISI 1045 steel, the size is 100*100*10 (mm). Before conducting multi-layer and multi-channel laser cladding tests, the plate is polished to remove the surface oxide film, and surface stains are cleaned with alcohol before being dried and stored for future use.The substrate was not preheated before laser cladding. The mass fraction of the melted powder is 20% SiC, 80% Ni60A, with uniform particle size, suitable for laser melting manufacturing. Microstructure characterization and performance testing

The laser-cladded samples were cut into 7 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm specimens using a metallographic cutting machine (DK733-A). The specimens were ground, polished, and etched. The macroscopic morphology and surface cracks of the cladding layer were examined using an optical microscope. A Zeiss scanning electron microscope (SIGMA300) was used to observe the microstructure of the cladding layer and the worn surface morphology of the coating. The phase composition and residual stress of the cladding layer were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 ADVANCE). The microhardness of the coating was measured using a microhardness tester (HVS1000). Cut the laser cladding sample into 15mm × 10mm × 10mm, and use a fixture to clamp the wear test coating. A ball-on-disk friction and wear tester (MMQ-02) was employed to perform the tribological tests. A Si3N4 ceramic ball with a diameter of 4.75 mm was used as the counterface. The test conditions were set as follows: normal load of 50 N, rotation speed of 200 r/min, and wear duration of 20 min. The wear loss was determined by weighing the specimens before and after the test using an electronic balance (Lichen FA123C).

Microstructure analysis of SiC/Ni60A cladding layer

Figure 6 shows the microstructure of the central region of SiC/Ni60A cladding coatings with one to four layers. The cladding layer consists of a multi-phase eutectic structure formed between γ-Ni equiaxed grains and dendrites, with noticeable segregation of Cr and Fe around the microstructures. In the single-layer cladding, the microstructure is dominated by equiaxed grains with relatively disordered growth. In the double- and triple-layer coatings, dendritic structures become predominant. As shown in Fig. 6D, granular intermetallic compounds are formed on the surface of the cladding layer. Fig. 6E displays a crack in the four-layer cladding coating. The crack exhibits straight edges with no significant branching and propagates along the cladding direction, which aligns with the principal direction of residual stress, indicating a brittle fracture mode20.The EDS analysis of the crack region in the three-layer cladding coating (Fig. 6F) shows elevated contents of Cr, C, and Si, indicating that the blocky particles inside the crack are likely Cr23C6 hard phases. These phases possess much higher hardness than the Ni-based matrix. During the rapid solidification and cooling of the laser cladding process, the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients generates significant residual stress concentrations at the phase interfaces. When these stresses exceed the fracture strength of the matrix, microcracks are initiated around the hard phases.Fig. 6Microstructure of SiC/Ni60A fusion coating. Microhardness changes of SiC/Ni60A cladding layer

Figure 9 shows the schematic of microhardness variation across the three-layer SiC/Ni60A coating. Taking the interface between the first and second layers as the reference line, the microhardness trend toward the first layer shows an initial decrease, followed by an increase, and then a subsequent decrease. As seen in Fig. 3B, during multilayer laser cladding, the high temperature from the upper cladding layers induces a thermal treatment effect on the underlying layers, affecting grain growth and ultimately influencing the microhardness distribution.After the second-layer cladding, the first-layer coating can be divided into three distinct zones: the remelted zone, the secondary quenched zone, and the tempered zone, as shown in Fig. 9A. The remelted zone in the first layer and the second-layer coating together undergo rapid melting and solidification during the second cladding process. Excluding the effect of substrate dilution, the microstructure and hardness in this region are comparable to those of a single-layer coating. Hence, from the bottom of the remelted zone in the first layer toward the second layer, the hardness increases gradually. In the secondary quenched zone, due to prior laser-induced hardening, a second laser quenching occurs during the second-layer cladding. As both treatments result in a quenched microstructure, this region exhibits relatively high hardness. In contrast, the tempered zone does not experience sufficient thermal energy during the second-layer cladding to undergo quenching, resulting only in tempering, which reduces the hardness. Moving from the interface toward the second layer, the microhardness continues to increase to a peak and then decreases again. This trend is further affected by the third-layer cladding, which induces a similar effect on the second layer as the second-layer cladding does on the first. Each heat-affected zone (remelted, secondary quenched, and tempered) in the second layer exhibits increased hardness values compared to those in the first layer. Entering the third layer, the microhardness rises again, and near the interface with the fourth layer, a similar variation pattern is observed23.Fig. 9(A) Microstructure of multi-layer laser cladding layer; (B) Microhardness changes of multi-layer SiC/Ni60A cladding layer.

(A) Microstructure of multi-layer laser cladding layer; (B) Microhardness changes of multi-layer SiC/Ni60A cladding layer.

Figure 10 presents the average microhardness of the SiC/Ni60A coatings. As shown in the figure, the average microhardness of the coating gradually decreases with the increasing number of cladding layers. When the first layer has mostly cooled, the application of the subsequent cladding layer effectively serves as a tempering treatment for the previous one. Therefore, during multilayer laser cladding, the underlying layer adjacent to a new cladding layer is exposed to a higher tempering temperature, leading to a more significant reduction in hardness.The greater the total number of cladding layers, the more tempering cycles the lower layers undergo. Consequently, the bottom-most layers, which are closest to the substrate, experience the highest number of tempering treatments and exhibit the most pronounced reduction in hardness24,25. Although the rate of hardness reduction varies between layers, the overall trend indicates that the average hardness of the entire sample decreases as the number of cladding layers increases. The average microhardness of the double-layer fusion coating is 962HV, which is 4.3 times that of the repaired substrate AISI 1045 steel.Fig. 10Microhardness changes of multi-layer SiC/Ni60A cladding layer.

Wear resistance of SiC/Ni60A cladding layer

Dry sliding wear experiment on the cladding layer. Figure 11 illustrates the wear performance of SiC/Ni60A coatings with different numbers of layers. As shown in the figure, the wear loss of the coatings gradually increases with the number of cladding layers, reaching up to 0.0018 g for the double layer coating, at this point, the wear resistance is 4.7 times that of the substrate. This increase in wear loss is attributed to the progressive reduction in microhardness with more layers, as well as the thermal accumulation effect during multilayer cladding, which leads to the complete decomposition of SiC. The decomposition results in the formation of a larger amount of brittle phases such as Cr23C6 and Gr0.19Fe0.7Ni0.11. These brittle phases are prone to spalling during the wear process, thereby increasing material loss and reducing wear resistance26.During multi-layer cladding, the HAZ is subjected to multiple thermal cycles, causing local residual stress concentration in the coating and inducing interface microcracks, which reduces the wear resistance of the laser cladding coating.Fig. 11Wear resistance of SiC/Ni60A cladding coating.

Conclusions

In this study, multilayer SiC/Ni60A coatings were fabricated on AISI 1045 steel substrates using laser cladding. The effects of the number of cladding layers on the microstructure and properties of the coatings were systematically investigated. The main conclusions are as follows:

For SiC/Ni60A coatings fabricated by laser cladding on AISI 1045 steel, the incremental increase in coating thickness decreases with each additional layer. As the number of layers increases, both the number and width of cracks significantly increase. In the four-layer coating, the maximum crack width exceeds 42 μm, and the maximum tensile residual stress reaches 350 MPa at the interface between the first and second layers. For repairing components with deep damage (greater than 0.5 mm), two- or three-layer cladding should be employed, resulting in a coating thickness of approximately 1.5–2.5 mm. The microstructure of the laser-cladded SiC/Ni60A coating consists of γ-Ni blocky crystals and interdendritic multiphase eutectics. Under laser irradiation, SiC decomposes into Si and C, which react with Fe and Cr in the Ni60A alloy powder to form metal compounds, including FeNi3, Gr0.19Fe0.7Ni0.11, Cr23C6, and Ni31Si12.Blocky Cr23C6 particles are present in the crack regions of the multilayer laser-cladded coatings. With the increase in the number of cladding layers, the average microhardness of the coating gradually decreases, accompanied by a decline in wear resistance. For the double layer cladding, the coating exhibits a maximum average microhardness of 962 HV, which is 4.3 times that of the substrate. At this stage, the relative wear loss of the coating reaches 0.0018g, and the wear resistance is 4.7 times that of the substrate.The wear mechanism is primarily adhesive wear, accompanied by abrasive wear. The worn surfaces of multilayer cladding coatings show abundant furrows and flaky spalling.
