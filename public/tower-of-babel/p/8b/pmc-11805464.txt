A cascade of care for diabetes in people living with HIV in a tertiary care center in Mexico City
Authors: Cristian E Espejo-Ortiz, Nancy Sierra-Barajas, Angelina Silva-Casarrubias, Lorena Guerrero-Torres, Yanink Caro-Vega, Yamile G Serrano-Pinto, Alvaro Lopez-Iñiguez, Juan G Sierra-Madero, Brenda E Crabtree-Ramírez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Diabetes affects 4.5% of people living with HIV in Mexico. This study aims to describe the diabetes cascade of care (DMC) in people with HIV in a tertiary center in Mexico City.Methods:We conducted a single-center review of people with HIV aged over 18, using medical records of active people enrolled at the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (INCMNSZ) HIV Clinic (HIVC). Our analysis focused on their last visit to describe the DMC, aiming to identify gaps in control goals. We included people who had a consultation within the 12 months preceding May 2020.Results:Out of the 2072 active people, medical records were available for 2050 (98.9%). Among these, 326 people (15.9%) had fasting glucose (FG) abnormalities, of which 133 (40.7%) had diabetes. The prevalence of diabetes among people with HIV was of 6.4% (133/2050). Regarding the DMC, the following proportions of people achieved control goals: 133/133 (100%) received medical care in the last 12 months, 123/123 (100%) had blood pressure (BP) <140/90 mmHg, 73/132 (55.3%) had LDL cholesterol (c-LDL) <100 mg/dl, 63/132 (47.7%) had FG <130 mg/dl, 50/116 (43.1%) had glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) <7%. ABC goals (HbA1c <7%, c-LDL <100 mg/dl, BP <140/90 mmHg) were met in 28/109 (25.6%) people. 126/133 (94%) people with HIV achieved HIV-viral load <50 copies/mL.Conclusions:Despite the high rate of viral suppression among people with HIV and diabetes, significant challenges remain in achieving comprehensive diabetes control. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve metabolic outcomes and the overall management of diabetes in people with HIV.Keywords: People with HIV, diabetes mellitus, cascade of care, non-communicable diseases, Mexico, Latin America

Introduction

More than half a billion people had diabetes mellitus worldwide in 2021; diabetes prevalence increased from 3.2% to 6.1% from 1980 to 2021. Projections indicate this number will more than double over the next 30 years [1,2]. In Mexico, diabetes prevalence increased from 13.7% in 2016 to 18.3% in 2022, with 87.8% under medical treatment but nearly 2/3 experiencing poor glycemic control [3,4]. Diabetes is one of the leading causes of heart disease, stroke, blindness, kidney disease, neuropathy, and amputations [5]. In Mexico, it is the second cause of death, representing high costs for our health system [6,7].

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved life expectancy among people living with HIV, leading to a shift in primary causes of death [8]. Non-AIDS-related conditions, especially non-communicable diseases, now dominate morbimortality, especially in high-income countries [9–11]. This trend is increasingly observed in low- and middle-income countries as well [12].

People with HIV face a significantly higher risk of developing diabetes, with complications emerging earlier compared to the general population [13]. Multiple factors contribute to this heightened risk, including persistent inflammation and chronic immune activation from HIV, as well as increased prevalence of diabetes risk factors like insulin resistance and the effects of ART [13].

In order to reduce morbimortality in people with diabetes, guidelines recommendations include fasting glucose (FG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), blood pressure (BP) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (c-LDL) goals that should be met, as well as lifestyle changes such as smoking cessation [14–16].

The description and characterization of the diabetes cascade of care (DMC) may help design interventions to improve diabetes care among people with HIV [17]. To provide a comprehensive overview of the DMC in our population of people with HIV in a context of the high prevalence of diabetes mellitus, we aim to report and to visualize the gaps in the goals such as screening, diagnosis, treatment, and control goals.

Methods

Study design

We collected data of people enrolled in the HIV Clinic (HIVC) of the INCMNSZ (a tertiary hospital in Mexico City), where clinical and demographic information is recorded prospectively in every clinical visit and stored in the HIVC database. We included all active people seen at least once from May 31st, 2019, to May 31st, 2020. Active people were defined as those who had attended the clinic at least once in the last 12 months for a clinical visit, pick-up ART medication, and/or HIV lab monitoring. People without clinical records were excluded.

To develop the DMC in people with HIV, their medical records including demographic information were collected from the updated HIVC database. People with previous diabetes mellitus diagnosis up to May 31st, 2020, were included.

The comorbidities included were those reported by physicians in the medical records. Anthropometric data to elaborate the cascade including BP, FG, HbA1c, and lipid levels were extracted from the medical records, the hospital database of laboratory results or the HIVC database, corresponding to the information available in their most recent visit in the past 12 months until May 31st, 2020. Tobacco consumption status was collected from a questionnaire carried out at each HIVC consultation and from medical records. Viral load was retrieved retrospectively from their last visit to the clinic within the study period.

Definitions

Type 2 diabetes was considered a diagnosis if HbA1c was ≥6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥126mg/dl, diagnosis in clinical records or use of medication (tablets or insulin) for diabetes; Prediabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥5.7 and ≤6.4%, fasting plasma glucose ≥100mg/dl and ≤125mg/dl, or diagnosis documented in the clinical history [18].

Type 2 diabetes was considered a diagnosis if HbA1c was ≥6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥126mg/dl, diagnosis in clinical records or use of medication (tablets or insulin) for diabetes; Prediabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥5.7 and ≤6.4%, fasting plasma glucose ≥100mg/dl and ≤125mg/dl, or diagnosis documented in the clinical history [18].

Systemic arterial hypertension was defined according to international standards for screening as at least two determinations with mean systolic BP ≥140mmHg and/diastolic BP ≥90mmHg and/or diagnosis documented in the medical record, and/or current use of antihypertensive medications [19].

Systemic arterial hypertension was defined according to international standards for screening as at least two determinations with mean systolic BP ≥140mmHg and/diastolic BP ≥90mmHg and/or diagnosis documented in the medical record, and/or current use of antihypertensive medications [19].

Dyslipidemia was defined as at least one blood lipid quantification with one of the following values: c-LDL ≥100mg/dl, HDL cholesterol ≤40mg/dl, and triglycerides ≥150mg/dl [19].

Dyslipidemia was defined as at least one blood lipid quantification with one of the following values: c-LDL ≥100mg/dl, HDL cholesterol ≤40mg/dl, and triglycerides ≥150mg/dl [19].

Underweight, healthy weight, overweight, or obese were based on body max index (BMI) <18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, and >30, respectively. The formula to calculate BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in square meters [20].

Underweight, healthy weight, overweight, or obese were based on body max index (BMI) <18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, and >30, respectively. The formula to calculate BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in square meters [20].

ABC goals: achieving HbA1c <7%, c-LDL < 100 mg/dL, BP <140/90mmHg [21].

ABC goals: achieving HbA1c <7%, c-LDL < 100 mg/dL, BP <140/90mmHg [21].

Non-smoker: a person who has not smoked tobacco or who has no record of tobacco use in the last 12 months.

Non-smoker: a person who has not smoked tobacco or who has no record of tobacco use in the last 12 months.

The targets considered for control in the DMC were BP < 140/90mmHg, c-LDL <100mg/dl, FG <130 mg/dl, and HbA1c <7%. The cascade was constructed in the following order: 1) people enrolled in the HIVC, 2) total of people with diabetes, 3) clinical visit to the HIVC in last 12 months until 31 May, 2020, 4) non-smoking status, and those who were within target ranges of 5) BP, 6) c-LDL, 7) fasting glucose, 8) HbA1c, 8) ABC goal, and ABC goal in non-smokers.

Statistical analyses

Description of the DMC

For each component of the cascade, proportions were calculated based on the total of people with available information and represented in percentage. People without complete data were not included in the determination of the percentage of each component within the DMC.

The CD4 cell count was reported as those with levels less than 200, >200-350, >350-500, or greater than 500 cells per milliliter of blood (cells/ml). Viral load of HIV at the last visit was reported as undetectable viral load, defined as <50 copies/ml; low-level viremia, defined as 50-200 copies/ml; virological failure, defined as >200 copies/ml. Regarding antiretrovirals, they were included by group: HIV integrase strand-transfer inhibitors (INSTI), nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTI), non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTI), and protease inhibitors (PI). They were reported as frequencies and percentages.

We compared sociodemographic variables, HIV diagnosis, HIV care and comorbidities between people with and without diabetes. We summarized data using descriptive statistics, reporting the median and interquartile range of continuous variables. Also, we examined the differences by performing a bivariate regression analysis, categorical variables were compared using a chi-squared test or T-student, and we consider a significant value p < 0.05. The analysis was made using SPSS® v22 (IBM, Ch, IL, USA).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee REF 3525.

Results

Characteristics of people with diabetes vs people without diabetes

Until May 31, 2020, there were 2072 active people enrolled at the HIVC; of those, 2050 had complete medical records from their last visit (Figure 1). In the last clinical visit, a total of 326/2050 (15.9%) had abnormal fasting glucose levels, and 133/2050 (6.4%) had a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. The median age was 54 (IQR: 47-62) in people with diabetes and 44 (IQR: 36-52) in people without diabetes; of people with diabetes 115/133 (86.4%) were men vs 1725/1917 (89.9%) of people without diabetes (p = 0.083); 79/133 (59.3%) of people with diabetes were on INSTI vs 920/1901 (48.3%) of people without diabetes (p = 0.014), bictegravir has been the main INSTI used, a few of the people had dolutegravir or raltegravir on their regimen. A greater proportion of BMI equal to or above 30 was found in people with diabetes compared to people without diabetes (31/128 (24.2%) vs 232/1873 (12.3%), [p < 0.001] respectively).

Flow chart of the selection of people for the DMC.

The most common comorbidity in people with diabetes was dyslipidemia, present in 103/133 (77.4%) vs 573/191 (29.8%) in people without diabetes (p < 0.001). Hypertension was the second most frequent comorbidity in people with diabetes present in 39/123 (31.7%) vs 203/1747 (11.6%) in people without diabetes (p < 0.001). Chronic kidney disease occurred in 14/133 (10.5%) people with diabetes and in 40/1917 (2%) of people without diabetes (p < 0.001). Cardiovascular disease occurred in 7/133 (5.2%) of people with diabetes vs 20/1917 (1%) of people without diabetes (p = 0.002).

Variables related to HIV care

At their last visit, the HIV cohort achieved an undetectable viral load in 94.1% (1930/2050) of the cases. Of people with diabetes, 126/133 (94.7%) had an undetectable HIV viral load, 4/133 (3%) had low-level viremia and 3/133 (2.2%) had virological failure. No significant difference was found when comparing this group to the one of people without diabetes (Table 1).

Diabetes mellitus Cascade care

Diabetes screening, diagnosis and treatment

Of the total cohort, 326/2050 (15.9%) active people had abnormal FG levels or previous diagnosis of diabetes or pre-diabetes. Of these, 193/2050 (9.4%) had pre-diabetes and 133/2050 (6.4%) diabetes mellitus. 73/2050 (3.6%) did not have glucose or HbA1c determination in the last 12 months and only one had history of abnormal FG levels.

Most people with diabetes, 99/133 (74.4%), received medication for glycemic control; of those, 26/99 (26.2%) had HbA1c below 7%. The most frequent medication used was metformin, 78/99 (78.7%), followed by insulin, 20/99 (20.2%), and iSGLT2, 17/99 (17.1%). Of people with diabetes 39/133 had hypertension and all of them 39/39 (100%) received medication for BP and met BP < 140/90mmHg. The most frequent medications were angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors used in 17/39 (43.5%) of the people, calcium antagonists in 13/39 (33.3%), diuretics in 5/39 (12.8%), and beta-blockers 3/39 (7.6%). People with diabetes and dyslipidemia were 103/133 (77.4%); of those with lipid-lowering medications were 58/103 (56.3%); and only 34/58 (58.6%) on medication had the c-LDL less than 100mg/dl. According to medications, statins were used in 47/58 (81%) people and fibrates in 27/58 (46.5%).

Diabetes goals

Regarding the cascade of care, all people with diabetes 133/133 (100%) had received diabetes care at least once during our study period (from May 2019 until May 2020); additionally, 108/133 (81.2%) were non-smokers, as reported in their last visit. BP was evaluated in 123/133 (92.4%) people, and all of them met the goal of <140/90mmHg. c-LDL was determined in 132/133 (99.2%) people, and 73/132 (55.3%) had c-LDL lower than 100mg/dl. Glucose determination was performed in 132/133 (99.2%) people, of these; 63/132 (47.7%) had a FG below 130mg/dl, and HbA1c was determined in 116/133 (87.2%) individuals with 50/116 (43.1%) of them having a value below 7%. Only 109/133 (81.9%) people had complete information to calculate the ABC goals. Of these, 28/109 (25.6%) met the targets. ABC goals were achieved in 21/109 (19.2%) when considering only non-smokers (Figure 2). Three (2.2%) people were diagnosed with diabetes during our study period; two achieved HbA1c targets, and one did not have an HbA1C measurement.

Discussion

This study describes the DMC among people with HIV in a tertiary referral Institution in Mexico City from May 2019 until May 2020. Our clinic’s diabetes prevalence was 6.4%, higher than reported in previous studies from the region, for instance, Cano-Torres et al., had reported a prevalence of diabetes in people with HIV of 4.5% at the same tertiary health center in Mexico in 2017 [1, 2]. People with HIV and diabetes enrolled in our clinic had adequate virologic suppression but suboptimal diabetes control. Although hypertension was controlled within target levels in 100% of the cases, cholesterol targets were met in only half of the people. Among people with diabetes and dyslipidemia, only half of them were on lipid-lowering medication, and only 58% had controlled c-LDL levels; it is necessary to determine whether this was due to insufficient dosage or poor adherence to medication in those who were on treatment but did not meet targets. Adequate fasting glucose and HbA1c were met in a quarter of the people analyzed. These findings were similar to those of the general Mexican population, where only 42% of the people with diabetes achieved HbA1c <7% [4]. An effective approach should include BP, glucose, cholesterol level control, and lifestyle changes such as alcohol and tobacco cessation. This ensures optimal management and reduces the risk of future complications [15, 22]. These results enhance the need of interventions to increase adherence to international recommendations for diabetes control.

Compared to other studies on the DMC, notable differences and similarities have emerged [23–26]. All people with diabetes in our study who had BP measurements meet the control goal, compared to 65% in the study of Ali, Mohammed K. et al. [23] Conversely, regarding Hb1ac, less than half of our population meet the target goals, while 64% of the US cascade population achieve said goal [23]. However, less than a third of the people achieved ABC goals in both studies. This suggests similar underlying factors in diabetes management in both healthcare settings. Additionally, our study is composed of a very small sample when compared to the US one and our population is generally more closely monitored than most people with diabetes in the country, this level of care is not a reality for the general Mexican population either for HIV or for diabetes.

Moreover, a Malaysian study similarly described cascades of care involving people with HIV and encompassed the same ABC variables [24]. However, this study described four cascades separately for each comorbidity without taking into consideration the ABC goals simultaneously. This could contribute to the differences in the results; in our study we evaluated individuals’ BP, c-LDL levels, glycemic control, and viral load in people with diabetes regardless of previous diagnosis of dyslipidemia or hypertension. Therefore, by including normotensive subjects, we could expect a higher proportion of people within BP targets compared to people with diagnosed hypertension. Viral load goals were achieved in 94% of individuals in both studies[24]. Likewise, an African cohort that also described the DMC regardless of HIV status, also studied factors related to people’s awareness of their diabetes diagnosis, they found that living with HIV was a factor associated with the increased likelihood of diabetes diagnosis awareness [25].

According to current recommendations for diabetes care, it is essential to implement strategies to ensure that all people undergo glycemic assessment at least biannually for those meeting treatment goals and at least quarterly for those not meeting targets [18]. In our cohort despite regular clinic visits, diabetes care in people with HIV was suboptimal; less than a quarter of people underwent laboratory tests for diabetes control, yet all of them had laboratory monitoring for HIV. This may be explained due to the fact that these people are mostly seen by infectious diseases specialists who may be more focused on HIV related variables.

Our findings highlight the importance of aligning diabetes control with HIV programs with a multidisciplinary approach. This collaborative effort can improve outcomes and quality of life for people with HIV and diabetes. Furthermore, it is crucial to raise awareness among healthcare professionals, including doctors, nurses and nutritionists, about the importance of screening not only for diabetes, but also for metabolic comorbidities in people with HIV. Informing and educating medical staff will contribute to reducing the risk of complications from metabolic conditions, enhancing the quality of care and ensuring timely interventions for people with HIV.

The fact that 94% of people in our sudy achieved HIV viral load suppression compared to only 25% of them achieving the ABC goal deserves further reflection and analysis. These results may indicate a perception gap on health threats among people with HIV and their health providers. Given the fact that there is a shift in cause of death of people with HIV from AIDS events to non-communicable diseases in low- and middle-income countries, there is an increased need for more education in people with HIV in this regard.

Our study has several limitations, such as its retrospective nature, in which missing information prevented an analysis of diabetes mellitus control in all enrolled person. However, only 3% of the cohort did not have glucose determination, and 18% of people with diabetes did not have all the data to assess control goals. Additionally, this is a single-centered study carried out in a tertiary level institution which may not be generalized to other contexts within our country. Nonetheless, it is a large cohort with a long follow-up in Latin America, where information about non communicable diseases care in people with HIV is scarce.

Conclusions

In this Mexican cohort, 6.4% of people with HIV had diabetes mellitus. Although 94% had an undetectable HIV viral load in their last visit, less than half of them achieved Hb1Ac and fasting glucose targets; only a quarter met ABC targets. Our findings highlight the importance of non-communicable disease care in people with HIV and the need for awareness and education within this population.
