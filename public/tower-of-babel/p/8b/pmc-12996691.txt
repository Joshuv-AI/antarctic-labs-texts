FIB-4 index predicts short-term mortality in Vietnamese acute heart failure patients
Authors: Thien Tan Tri Tai Truyen, Dieu Hien Tran, Chau-Minh Nhut Do, Khanh Ly Le, Phuc Dai Vo, Vu Ngoc Anh Pham, Tri Cuong Phan, Han My Nguyen Le, Hoai Ngoc Thi Tran, Bao Minh Ton Luu, Huong-Dung Thi Nguyen, Thanh Phong Tran, Van Khoa Nguyen, Thi Dieu Truong, Thanh Phong Pham, Minh Vu Nguyen, Minh Nghiem Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute heart failure (AHF) has high early mortality. The FIB-4 index indicates hepatic dysfunction in AHF, but its predictive power for short-term outcomes in developing countries is not well established.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study at Can Tho Central General Hospital, Vietnam, from May to December 2024 including adults aged ≥18, hospitalized with AHF and NT-proBNP levels ≥300 pg/mL. Exclusions were for those who died before blood sample collection, transferred, declined consent, or had chronic liver disease, active cancer, or end-stage renal disease. Survivors were followed up via phone for 30 days post-discharge. The primary endpoint was 30-day all-cause mortality.ResultsThe final analysis included 413 patients (mean age 70, 47.7% male). In-hospital mortality was 5.1% and did not associate to FIB-4. At 30 days post-discharge, 42(17%) of 247 patients with follow-up data died (37.3% loss to follow-up). Non-survivors had higher FIB-4 scores than survivors (3.3 vs. 2.3, p = 0.006). After adjusting for age, sex, comorbidities, LVEF, and NT-proBNP, each one-point increase in FIB-4 raised the 30-day mortality risk by 9% (RR 1.09; 95%CI: 1.01–1.17). A cut-off of 3.1 identified high-risk patients, with a RR of 1.82 (95%CI: 1.03–3.22). FIB-4 demonstrated an AUC of 0.634 (95%CI: 0.535–0.733) which was comparable to NT-proBNP (AUC 0.650; 95%CI: 0.559–0.742).ConclusionIn this exploratory single-center study of patients with AHF, FIB-4 demonstrated an independent association with 30-day mortality, with a threshold of 3.1 identifying high-risk individuals. These findings are hypothesis-generating, and external validation is warranted before FIB-4 can be recommended for risk stratification.

Introduction

Acute heart failure (AHF) is a life-threatening condition with high rates of mortality and readmission, characterized by an abrupt reduction in cardiac output that leads to systemic hypoperfusion and venous congestion in multiple organ systems [1], [2]. Liver dysfunction is commonly observed in this setting, occurring both at admission and during hospitalization [3], [4]. Reduced blood flow and elevated central venous pressure contribute to ischemic hepatitis and hepatic congestion, raising concern for the heart-liver interaction in heart failure [5]. Abnormal liver laboratory indices including AST, ALT, total bilirubin, and albumin are frequently observed in AHF at baseline and during hospitalization, and are associated with worse outcomes [3], [4]. However, single parameters may not provide strong prognostic information, whereas composite hepatic parameter such as MELD-XI or FIB-4 scores show stronger associations with mortality and rehospitalization after adjustment for other risk factors in patients with AHF [6], [7].

Several short-term risk models for AHF have been developed and validated, including MEESSI-AHF, ADHERE, EHMRG, and GWTG-HF, with c-statistics ranging from 0.68 to 0.80 [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. While these models incorporate key clinical and laboratory variables, they generally do not include hepatic indices. Incorporating biomarkers that reflect hepatic congestion could potentially improve short-term risk stratification. In this context, the FIB-4 index may serve as a complementary predictor. The FIB-4 index is an accessible, noninvasive score that incorporates age, AST, ALT, and platelet count. It was initially developed to assess liver fibrosis due to viral infections but has since been adapted as a prognostic tool for predicting outcomes in patients with AHF. A higher FIB-4 score at admission is linked to increased all-cause mortality and rehospitalization rates in AHF patients [6], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. This index likely reflects hepatic congestion and elevated right heart pressures, which are significant factors in the pathophysiology of AHF. Previous studies have shown that the FIB-4 index is an independent risk predictor for all-cause mortality and hospitalization in AHF patients, with hazard ratios (HR) ranging from 1.01 to 1.09 [6], [16], [18]. Various thresholds have been identified to define high-risk patients using FIB-4, typically from 2.7 to 4.2, demonstrating good predictive value for adverse outcomes, with HRs varying from 1.7 to 1.9 [14], [15], [16], [17]. These findings highlight the prognostic value of the FIB-4 index in AHF across different populations, although the specific cut-off values can differ between studies.

Although most prior research has focused on the long-term outcomes of the FIB-4 index, evidence on its short-term prognostic value remains limited. This is particularly important because the early post-discharge phase represents the most vulnerable period for patients with AHF, carrying substantially elevated risks of in-hospital and 30-day mortality [19]. Furthermore, evidence regarding the utility of the FIB-4 index in developing countries is scarce, and appropriate thresholds for risk stratification have yet to be established. To address these gaps, we conducted this study aims to (1) evaluate the predictive value of the FIB-4 index for in-hospital and 30-day mortality and (2) determine the optimal cut-off point for short-term risk prediction in patients hospitalized with AHF in Vietnam.

Methods

Study design and population selection

To address our primary objective, we conducted prospective cohort of patients hospitalized for AHF with 30-day follow-up at Can Tho Central General Hospital—a tertiary hospital in southern Vietnam—from May 1 to December 31, 2024. We enrolled all patients aged ≥18 years admitted to the cardiology ward with AHF as the primary diagnosis. Eligible participants also underwent liver enzyme testing and a complete blood count within 24 h of admission, as well as echocardiographic assessment of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). For those with multiple hospitalizations during the study period, only data from the first admission were included. We excluded any patient who died before blood sample collection, was transferred to another facility, declined informed consent, had chronic liver disease, active cancer, or end-stage renal disease. For our secondary objectives, all patients survived to discharge were followed for 30 days by telephone. When possible, we interviewed the patients directly; if they had died, we obtained information from their family members.

Definition of demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and outcomes

Demographic variables (age and sex) and comorbidities—including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (CKD), coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebrovascular accident (CVA), atrial fibrillation (AF), and a history of congestive heart failure (CHF)—were retrieved from the hospital's electronic health record (EHR) at the time of each admission. AHF was diagnosed according to the Framingham criteria with new or worsening symptoms (dyspnea, orthopnea, or lower-extremity swelling) plus signs (elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary congestion, or peripheral edema) in conjunction with an NT-proBNP concentration ≥ 300 pg/mL [20], [21]. We also extracted laboratory data (complete blood count) and echocardiographic measurements from the EHR.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Distributional normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed continuous data, comparisons between groups were made using independent-samples t-tests (or one-way ANOVA when comparing more than two groups); non-normally distributed variables were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The optimal FIB-4 cut-off for identifying high-risk patients was determined by maximizing the Youden index, which balances sensitivity and specificity [23]. To examine the relationship between FIB-4 and in-hospital mortality among AHF patients, univariable logistic regression was first used to estimate odds ratios (OR). Variables with a significant association in univariable analyses were then included, along with FIB-4, into a multivariable logistic regression model to derive adjusted OR, if needed.

Among those discharged alive, the primary short-term outcome was all-cause mortality within 30 days. Because the event rate exceeded 10%, odds ratios would tend to overstate the relative risk (RR). Therefore, we employed a modified Poisson regression with robust “sandwich” variance estimation, as described by Zou et al., to directly estimate adjusted RR (Exp[B]) with 95% confidence intervals, even when the Poisson variance assumption is violated [24]. RR was also adjusted in multivariable model. To minimize overfitting in the multivariable model for 30-day mortality, covariate selection was guided by statistical significance in univariable analysis. Given that 42 post-discharge deaths were observed, the final adjusted model was restricted to eight covariates (age, sex, CAD, CHF, CKD, CVA, LVEF, and NT-proBNP) plus FIB-4, resulting approximately five events per variable. While this is below the conventionally recommended threshold of ten, it reflects a deliberate and transparent decision to limit model complexity given the available event count. Discriminative performance was demonstrated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and compared using DeLong's test.

To reduce potential selection bias, we evaluated the mechanism of loss to follow-up (LTFU) using several diagnostics. First, we applied Little's test to assess whether the missingness pattern was consistent with Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) [25]. Second, we compared baseline characteristics between patients with complete follow-up and those lost to follow-up using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. We also computed standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all variables, considering SMD > 0.20 as a meaningful imbalance [26]. Third, we fitted a multivariable logistic regression model including all baseline characteristics as candidate predictors to identify factors associated with LTFU and to estimate the probability of being observed. Little's test was non-significant (Chi-square statistic: 77.03, df = 63; p = 0.11). However, LTFU was associated with a history of CVA (OR 4.01; 95% CI 1.32–13.8), and CVA showed a meaningful imbalance (SMD = 0.24) between observed and lost groups (Tables S1–S2). The missingness model demonstrated modest discrimination (AUC 0.63; 95% CI 0.57–0.69) and excellent calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.95). Together, these findings argue against MCAR and support a Missing At Random (MAR) assumption, whereby missingness is explained by observed covariates (notably CVA). However, the MAR assumption is plausible but not formally demonstrable, and unmeasured factors beyond CVA — such as functional status, distance from hospital, or socioeconomic barriers — may have additionally influenced the probability of loss to follow-up. Because MAR is not testable and Missing Not At Random (MNAR) cannot be excluded, we conducted complementary sensitivity analyses using three approaches: (1) complete-case analysis; (2) inverse probability weighting (IPW) under the MAR assumption; (3) and extreme-case scenarios to evaluate potential bias under missing not at random (MNAR) [27], [28].

Specifically, for IPW analysis, we estimated each patient's probability of complete follow-up based on CVA status, which was strongly associated with LTFU (OR 4.01). We calculated stabilized inverse probability weights as the marginal probability of observation divided by the CVA-specific probability of observation. These weights were applied to the modified Poisson regression models to create a pseudo-population in which follow-up was independent of CVA, resulting in valid estimates under MAR. We verified that mean weights approximated 1.0 and no extreme weights (>5) were present. Finally, for MNAR sensitivity analyses, we performed best-case (all LTFU patients assumed alive, mortality = 0) and worst-case (all LTFU patients assumed dead, mortality = 1) imputations and refit the same regression models. The range between these extreme scenarios bounds the maximum potential bias if outcomes differ systematically between observed and unobserved patients beyond what is captured by measured covariates. Statistical significance was set at two-sided p < 0.05, and all analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.

Ethical consideration

All participants agreed and signed the consent form before anonymously enrolling in the study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Can Tho Central Hospital (1871/QD-BVTWCT).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

From May 1 to December 31, 2024, a total of 675 patients presenting with AHF were admitted to Can Tho Central General Hospital. Of these, 413 individuals met our inclusion criteria, provided informed consent, and were enrolled in the study. By the time of hospital discharge, 392 participants (94.9%) remained alive and were followed for a 30-day assessment of all-causes mortality. Follow-up data on the primary endpoint were successfully obtained for 247 patients, while 145 (37.3%) were lost to follow-up due to refusal or inability to contact. Among those with complete data, 205 patients (83.0%) survived to day 30, yielding a 30-day all-causes mortality rate of 17.0% (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of study population selectionLegends. Prognostic value for short-term outcome of FIB-4 in patients with AHF

At 30-day follow-up, our complete-case analysis demonstrated that non-survivors were older, more often male, and had higher rates of CAD, CHF, and CKD, as well as elevated NT-proBNP levels and lower LVEF compared to survivors (Table 2). Non-survivors also had a significantly higher median FIB-4 index (3.3 vs. 2.3; p = 0.006) (Fig. 2). In unadjusted modified Poisson regression with robust variance, each one-unit increase in FIB-4 was associated with a 12% higher risk of 30-day all-cause mortality (RR 1.12; 95% CI: 1.05–1.19). After adjusting for age, sex, CAD, CHF, CKD, CVA, LVEF, and NT-proBNP, FIB-4 remained an independent predictor (adjusted RR per unit increase 1.09; 95% CI, 1.01–1.17), while higher LVEF was protective (adjusted RR 0.05 per percentage-point increase; 95% CI, 0.003–0.816) (Fig. 3). The FIB-4 index demonstrated discrimination for 30-day all-cause mortality comparable to NT-proBNP and LVEF, with AUCs of 0.634 (95% CI: 0.535–0.733), 0.650 (95% CI: 0.559–0.742), and 0.605 (95% CI: 0.516–0.695), respectively. By maximizing the Youden index, we identified a FIB-4 cut-off of 3.1 as the optimal threshold for balancing sensitivity and specificity. In a multivariable Poisson regression using this dichotomized FIB-4, an elevated score (≥3.1) remained independently associated with 30-day mortality (adjusted RR 1.82; 95% CI: 1.03–3.22) (Table S3).

In sensitivity analyses, the significant association between FIB-4 and 30-day mortality observed in the complete-case analysis persisted under IPW based on CVA status and under the best-case MNAR scenario (all LTFU assumed alive) (Table 3). In the worst-case MNAR scenario (all LTFU assumed dead), the association was attenuated and not statistically significant: RR 1.02 (95% CI: 0.98–1.06; p = 0.283). The direction of effect was consistent across all scenarios (RRs > 1.0). The maximum percentage change in the estimated risk ratio across scenarios was 6.1%, indicating moderate sensitivity to extreme assumptions about missing outcomes. Discussion

Our study demonstrated an independent association between the FIB-4 index and 30-day all-cause mortality in a single-center cohort of patients admitted with AHF in Vietnam. This association remained significant in both univariable analyses and after multivariable adjustments for demographic factors and comorbidities, with the risk of mortality increasing by approximately 9% for each one-point rise in the FIB-4 score. Furthermore, we identified a FIB-4 threshold of 3.1 as a potential cut-off to stratify high-risk patients, corresponding to an adjusted RR of 1.82. These findings suggest that FIB-4 may serve as a candidate variable in future predictive models for short-term risk stratification in AHF patients. It is important to note that the FIB-4 index did not show a significant association with in-hospital mortality. However, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, pending external validation in independent Vietnamese cohort and broader Southeast Asian populations.

In our study, AST, ALT, and platelet counts showed no significant differences between survivors and non-survivors at 30 days post-discharge. However, the FIB-4 index was independently associated with all-cause mortality, with each one-point increase linked to a 9% higher risk of death. This emphasizes the prognostic value of FIB-4 as a comprehensive index rather than relying on its individual components, aligning with previous studies [6], [14], [15], [16], [17]. A FIB-4 threshold of ≥3.1 identified a subgroup of patients at higher risk, with an adjusted 30-day mortality approximately 82% greater compared to those below this threshold. Since patients with chronic liver disease were excluded, these findings suggest that FIB-4 reflects secondary liver dysfunction due to congestion and hypoperfusion in AHF rather than primary hepatic pathology. While the prognostic utility of FIB-4 in AHF is well-documented, optimal cut-off points vary across populations. Our threshold of 3.1 aligns with findings from the Philippines (3.3) but differs from those in Japan (2.7, 4.1, and 4.2) [6], [14], [15], [16], [18]. This variability can be attributed to two main factors. First, differences in follow-up durations may influence FIB-4 thresholds: our study and the Philippine study assessed outcomes during hospitalization or within a month, whereas Japanese studies extended follow-up to a year. Second, variations in optimal FIB-4 cut-off values across populations may reflect differences in underlying population characteristics, including genetic backgrounds, lifestyle factors, and comorbidity profiles.

The association between the FIB-4 index and adverse outcomes in heart failure may be attributed to the liver's vulnerability to hemodynamic changes. Reduced cardiac output results in hepatic hypoperfusion, while elevated filling pressures cause passive hepatic congestion [29]. FIB-4 is closely associated with right atrial pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, indicating right heart preload [30]. Increased central venous pressure leads to blood stasis in hepatic sinusoids, triggering several pathogenic mechanisms. Shear stress activates hepatic stellate cells, promoting fibrosis and reducing nitric oxide production. Concurrently, decreased arterial and portal flow worsens hepatic hypoxia, and interstitial fluid accumulation hinders oxygen and nutrient diffusion. Sinusoidal congestion can also promote thrombosis, advancing fibrosis through protease-activated receptors [31]. A sudden drop in cardiac output or perfusion pressure may cause diffuse hepatic injury and predispose patients to ischemic hepatitis, especially in the presence of pre-existing congestion [32]. This condition, known as cardiogenic ischemic hepatitis or “shock liver,” typically occurs following episodes of severe hypotension in acute HF, leading to acute hepatocellular necrosis and significant elevations in aminotransferase levels [29], [32].

Our study revealed that FIB-4 was not significantly associated with in-hospital mortality outcomes, and several factors may explain this finding. Firstly, the relatively small sample size and low in-hospital event rate (5.1%) likely reduced its statistical power. Secondly, intensive management during admission, particularly rapid decongestion and early implementation of guideline-directed therapies, might quickly alleviate liver congestion, as evidenced by trials like DOSE-HF, CARESS-HF, and STRONG-HF [33], [34], [35]. Therefore, FIB-4 may be more suitable for predicting medium- to long-term outcomes rather than very early mortality, as in-hospital deaths in AHF are often caused by acute factors such as advanced age, infections (e.g., pneumonia and sepsis), cardiogenic shock, or malignant arrhythmias, rather than hepatic dysfunction [36].

Limitation and strengths

When interpreting our findings, several limitations must be considered. Firstly, as a single-center prospective cohort study, causal inference remains limited, as we cannot exclude reverse causation between FIB-4 and mortality. Prospective studies with external validation are needed. Secondly, despite efforts to maintain contact, up to one-third of patients were lost to follow-up, which could introduce bias into our short-term outcome analysis. Although sensitivity analyses under the MAR assumption (IPW) and best-case MNAR scenarios support the robustness of the main findings, some degree of effect overestimation cannot be entirely ruled out, particularly if patients lost to follow-up experienced disproportionately higher mortality. Third, the inability to exclude metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) during patient selection likely introduced unmeasured confounding, as MASLD may independently affect both FIB-4 values and cardiovascular outcomes. Future studies with systematic MASLD assessment are needed. Lastly, the lack of specific information on the number of days until death after hospital discharge prevented us from performing a survival analysis, potentially limiting our findings. Additionally, FIB-4 was measured only at admission; the absence of serial measurements and detailed in-hospital treatment data restricts our interpretation to a single baseline assessment and prevents evaluation of dynamic changes in hepatic congestion during hospitalization. Despite these limitations, our study has several notable strengths. It is the first study in Vietnam to evaluate the FIB-4 index and report its predictive value for in-hospital and short-term mortality among patients with AHF. Additionally, our main findings demonstrated stability under the MAR assumption. IPW analysis, which adjusted for CVA-related differential loss to follow-up, produced results consistent with complete-case analysis, supporting the validity of our conclusions. Finally, our prospective recruitment and follow-up approach provide a more reliable estimate of the association between FIB-4 index and patient outcomes compared to retrospective methods.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates an independent association between the FIB-4 index and 30-day all-cause mortality within this local Vietnamese AHF cohort. A FIB-4 cut-off of 3.1 identified patients at elevated short-term risk in this exploratory analysis. These findings are hypothesis-generating, suggesting that FIB-4 might be considered as a component of a multivariable risk prediction model rather than establishing it as a validated clinical tool for immediate routine use. Future studies with external validation in independent cohorts are warranted before FIB-4 can be incorporated into standard clinical risk stratification to improve prognostic accuracy and clinical decision-making in AHF management.
