Analysis of factors affecting smooth extubation in delayed extubation patients following intracranial surgery: an ambispective cohort study
Authors: Rui Wang, Yan Liu, Weina Li, Chao Zhang, Xia Zhang, Feilong Wang, Yuhong Li, Xue Yang, Binbin Tan, Tunan Chen, Jishu Xian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDelayed extubation is common after intracranial surgery, and is associated with adverse reactions that compromise patient safety and comfort. Identifying factors that influence smooth extubation is therefore critical, but available evidence is limited. This study aims to explore risk factors of extubation adverse outcomes in patients undergoing craniocerebral surgery, with the goal of enhancing extubation safety and patient comfort.MethodsAn ambispective cohort study was conducted at a university hospital from May 2022 to September 2023, involving 157 patients with delayed extubation post-intracranial surgery. Clinical data encompassing demographic characteristics, neurological status, respiratory parameters, intraoperative and anesthetic management, and postoperative factors. Smooth extubation was defined as extubation with stable blood pressure, respiratory function, and absence of pain or agitation. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent factors influencing adverse extubation.ResultsOf 157 patients (mean BMI 23.88 ± 3.25 kg/m2, 33.1% male), adverse extubation occurred in 80 (51.0%). Univariate analysis showed several significant variables (P < 0.05): age, FOURE, FOURM, airway patency, Duration from command obedience to extubation, time to extubate, types of surgical procedure, operation duration, intraoperative propofol use, sedation before. Multivariate analysis showed: age [OR = 2.791, 95%CI (1.268, 6.147)], intraoperative propofol use [OR = 0.298, 95%CI (0.012, 0.796)], preoperative airway patency [OR = 2.463, 95%CI (1.159, 5.234)], time to extubate [OR = 7.884, 95%CI (1.337, 46.472)] as independent factors of dverse extubation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03394-3.

Introduction

Delayed extubation, defined as the interval ≥ 15 min between the end of surgery and extubation, occurs in 49.8% to 65% of patients following intracranial surgery [1, 2]. Neurosurgical patients often require delayed extubation due to factors such as lesion location, brainstem involvement during surgery, prolonged operation durations, and the need for postoperative stabilization of intracranial parameters like intracranial pressure (ICP) and cerebral perfusion pressure [3]. These factors necessitate sustained respiratory support, making immediate extubation in the operation room less feasible. The extubation phase following intracranial procedures, especially in patients undergoing delayed extubation, is a critical and vulnerable period. Delayed extubation increases intracranial risks, as the patient’s consciousness, sensory perception, and respiratory function gradually recover. During this phase, patients may experience agitation, pain, respiratory irregularities, and even hemodynamic instability [4]. Critically, the unique anatomy of the cerebral venous system—a valve-less system directly communicating with the right atrium—means that venous return and cardiac function are closely linked to intracranial pressure dynamics [5]. Consequently, even transient physiological disturbances during the vulnerable extubation period, such as hypertension, coughing, Valsalva maneuvers, or hypoxemia-induced sympathetic surges, can be transmitted to the intracranial compartment, potentially causing dangerous fluctuations in ICP, cerebral blood flow, oxygenation, and metabolism [6, 7]. The ideal outcome of extubation should be a calm and straightforward process that minimizes patient distress, maintains stable vital signs, ensures adequate ventilation and oxygenation, and allows for early evaluation of intracranial conditions [8]. However, delayed extubation increases the incidence of adverse reactions, with rates reaching up to 49.8% [9]. Therefore, identifying the risk factors associated with adverse reactions during delayed extubation is crucial to ensure a smooth extubation process. These adverse reactions not only jeopardize patient safety but also compromise comfort, often leading to symptoms such as bucking, pain, and agitation, which are frequently overlooked during the extubation process [10]. Identifying the factors that affecting smooth extubation can reduce adverse reactions, improve the quality of extubation and reduce complications by ensuring safe and comfort.

Despite this, studies investigating the factors affecting smooth extubation in intracranial surgery patients remain limited and often constrained by methodological issues. Most interventional studies have focused on the impact of pharmacological agents and extubation techniques, each with its limitations and potential drawbacks [11–13]. Few retrospective studies have examined the risk factors for adverse effects, but these tend to focus on one or two specific outcomes, often measuring the entire resuscitation period and introducing multiple confounders [9, 14, 15]. Additionally, relevant studies have suggested that smooth extubation may be influenced by factors such as consciousness, lesion location, surgical methods, and other neurological variables; however, the relationship among these factors requires further exploration and verification [16].

This study aims to investigate the incidence of delayed extubation, identify independent predictors of adverse extubation outcomes in patients undergoing delayed extubation following intracranial surgery, and address key gaps in understanding the specific needs and risks associated with extubation management after neurosurgery. To achieve this, we conducted an ambispective cohort study of patients who experienced delayed extubation after intracranial surgery, aiming to identify independent influencing factors by comprehensively analyzing various perioperative factors.

Methods

Study design and oversight

An ambispective cohort study was conducted in the neurosurgical intensive care unit (NICU) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University from May 2022 to July 2023. The study protocol ((A)KY202244, April 10, 2022) was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University, PLA, and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200057400, March 11, 2022). Given the observational nature of the study and anonymized data collection, informed consent was waived.

Patient population

Eligible participants were adult intracranial surgery patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent delayed extubation following tracheal intubation during intracranial procedures under general anesthesia. Delayed extubation was defined as an interval of ≥ 15 min between the end of surgery and extubation. Extubation was delayed until the patient was transferred to the NICU. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, a history of upper airway injury or surgery, significant respiratory comorbidities (e.g., chronic oxygen therapy or COPD classified as Gold stage II or higher), presence of a tracheotomy, anticipation of a second operation prior to extubation, death before extubation, extubation at the end of life, and the absence of an extubation attempt during the NICU stay, including unplanned extubation. All enrolled patients were planned for extubation after their surgery, and those who did not undergo extubation or required reintubation were excluded based on predefined criteria.

Routine practice of extubation protocols

All intracranial surgeries were conducted under general anesthesia, with anesthetic management—including medication, respiratory, and circulatory care—tailored by the attending surgeon and anesthesia team. Due to the need for postoperative stabilization of intracranial and general anesthetic recovery, the attending clinicians and anesthesiologist determined whether delayed extubation was necessary [2]. All enrolled patients were transferred to the NICU, where during the 10-minute transfer, the anesthesiologist manually ventilated patients using a self-inflating bag. Once in the NICU, all patients were planned for elective extubation based on standardized criteria. In the NICU, management of mechanical ventilation, weaning, and extubation was a collaborative effort among clinicians, respiratory therapists, and nurses. A spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) was conducted as part of the extubation protocol. The protocol was as follows:

Step I

Once spontaneous breathing resumed, the following criteria were assessed: (1) Absence of signs indicating intracranial deterioration(e.g., increased intracranial pressure or hemorrhage; (2) hemodynamic stability (mean arterial pressure within 10–20% of baseline without vasopressor); (3) positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) ≤ 5 cmH2O; (4) arterial partial pressure of oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) ratio adequate; (5) no hyperpyrexia (temperature < 38 °C); (6) no scheduled surgeries under general anesthesia within the nest 72 h. Meeting these criteria allowed progression to Step II.

Step II

A spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) was initiated, with respiratory therapists typically selecting synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) or T-piece trials. SBT failure was defined as: (1) respiratory rate (RR) ≥ 35 breaths/min for over 5 min; (2) oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≤ 90%; (3) sustained changes in heart rate (HR) or mean arterial pressure (MAP) changes exceeding 20% of baseline; (4) increased work of breathing, swelling, or agitation lasting over 10 min. Patients who passed the SBT progressed to Step III; those who failed returned to Step I.

Step III

Following a successful SBT, a consensus on extubation was reached based on the patient’s overall condition, including consciousness level and airway protective reflexes. The managing clinician made the final decision. Patients who were able to wean from the ventilator but required continued intubation received oxygen supplementation and were reevaluated the next morning at 8:00 AM.

Step IV

The patient’s head of bed was elevated to 45°, and oxygen flow was increased to asist in clearing secretions from the oropharynx and nasal passages. The endotracheal tube cuff was completely deflated, and the tube was removed at the same time. After extubation, a nasal cannula was used to deliver 4–6 L/min of oxygen to maintain peripheral SpO2 at or above 95%. In case of respiratory distress, non-invasive ventilation or high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy was initiated. Reintubation criteria included: severe respiratory failure, defined as RR > 35 breaths/min for over 5 min, SpO2 < 90%, and evidence of accessory muscle recruitment or thoraco-abdominal paradox despite respiratory support; neurologic deterioration requiring continuous intravenous sedation due to coma or agitation; and emergencies such as respiratory or cardiac arrest.

Postoperative care

patients underwent routine monitoring including electrocardiograph, SPO2, HR, non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), RR. Neurological examinations were performed per hour. Hypertension was managed with a single slow bolus of 25 mg urapidil, with repeated dose administered if initial treatment was ineffective. Dexmedetomidine (0.2-0.2.2.2.2.2.7ug/kg/h) was administered for sedation, and butorphanol tartrate was used for analgesia as needed. All patients underwent a computerized tomography (CT) scan on the day of surgery.

Data collection and outcome measures

Observation data

Data were collected by a trained investigator to ensure consistency. For quality control, a second clinical team member cross-verified a subset of the collected data. Five categories of variables were collected: (1) Patient characteristics (e.g., sex, age, history of diabetes); (2) neurological factors encompassed prior intracranial surgery history, Location of lesion, Preoperative muscle strength, FOUR score which assesses eye response (FOURE), brainstem reflexes (FOURB), motor response (FOURM), and respiratory pattern (FOURR), a higher FOUR score indicates better level of consciousness and neurological recovery [17]; (3) respiratory factors, such as airway patency (e.g., any of these satisfied is defined abnormal: history of snoring, mouth opening < 3 cm, Mallampati class III or IV, limited mandibular advancement, or thyromental distance < 6 cm); (4) intraoperative and anesthetic factors, including American society of Anesthesiologists(ASA)physical status classification, types of surgical procedure, intraoperative position, operation duration, intraoperative medication; (5) other postoperative factors assessed upon NICU return, including lesion location, intubation path, Ventilator off-line times, endotracheal tube intolerance, extubation time (time from end of anesthesia to extubation), hyperpyrexia, sedation, analgesia and pre-extubation hypotensive drug use. Preoperative data, including airway assessments such as mouth opening, were obtained from pre-anesthesia evaluation forms completed during routine anesthesia consultations. Intra-operative and anesthetic information was retrieved retrospectively from electronic medical records (EMRs) and anesthesia charts documented during the surgery. Postoperative extubation outcomes and related variables were prospectively collected in the NICU. A complete list and definition of variables is provided in the appendix 1.

Outcome measures

Following extubation, the investigator conducted a comprehensive assessment to document outcome measures. Adverse outcomes were assessed within 5 min post-extubation, as prior studies indicate that hemodynamic and respiratory instability most commonly occur immediately after tube removal. Smooth extubation was defined as the absence of all these signs [18]. The primary outcome of interest was adverse extubation outcome, defined as the presence of at least one of the following clinical signs within 5 min post-extubation:

Hypertension or hypotension [19, 20]: systolic blood pressure (SBP) deviation of ± 30% from baseline sustained for more than 1 min;Desaturation [21]: SPO2 < 90% maintained for at least 10 s;Tachypnea [22]: RR > 35 breaths/min or < 10 breaths/min for at least 10 s;Poor respiratory mechanics [22]: visible accessory muscle recruitment or thoraco-abdominal paradox;Upper airway obstruction or bronchospasm [23]: presence of laryngeal stridor, audible with or without a stethoscope;Pain [24, 25]: Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS) ≥ 5;Agitation [26]: Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) ≥ 2.

Statistical Analysis: handling, and statistical methods

Given the cohort study design, the formula N = [Zα√ (2P(1-P)) + Zβ√(P₁(1-P₁) + P₀(1-P₀))]²/(P₁ - P₀)², was used to calculate the sample size, We assumed an adverse extubation rate of 55.8% in the reference group (age < 50 years, derived from pilot data) and 73.8% in the exposed group (age ≥ 50 years). With a risk difference of 18%, α = 0.05 (two-sided), and 80% power (β = 0.2), the minimum required sample size was 98 participants. Accounting for a 20% attrition rate, the final target was 118 patients.

Comparisons were made between smooth and adverse extubation outcomes groups to screen for factors associated with adverse extubation. Quantitative data, including age, BMI, and operation duration, underwent normality testing. Normally distribution data were expressed as‾x±s and compared using an independent samples t-test. Skewed quantitative data were presented as M (P25, P75), and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Qualitative data, including sex and smoking history, were expressed as n (%), and compared using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to identify the independent factors associated with adverse extubation, using the outcome groups (smooth vs. adverse extubation) as the dependent variable. Only variables that were significantly associated with the outcome in the univariate analysis were included as independent predictors. Multicollinearity between predictors was assessed with the use of the variance inflation factor (VIF); a threshold of a VIF of less than 5 is based on statistical criteria and is generally considered to indicate the absence of significant multicollinearity. Unplanned subgroup analyses were performed to estimate interactions of clinically meaningful variables in population subgroups. No more than 10% of missing data were imputed with the use of means, medians, or modes. IBM SPSS 26.0 was used for statistical analysis. α = 0.05 was used as the threshold of statistical significance, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient characteristics

The mean BMI of the patients was 23.88 ± 3.25 kg/m2. Among the patients, 52(33.1%) were male, and 55(35.0%) were under 50 years of age. Of the patients, 110(70.1%) were extubated on the day of surgery, 37(23.6%) on the first postoperative day (POD1), and 10(6.4%) after the first postoperative day(> POD1). Univariate analysis of factors related to extubation outcomes

Univariate analysis identified several factors associated with adverse extubation outcomes. Patients aged ≥ 50 years were significantly more likely to experience adverse extubation outcomes compared to those in the smooth extubation group (73.8% vs. 55.8%; P = 0.019). FOURE scores were significantly higher in the adverse extubation group (3.96 ± 0.25 vs. smooth group3.7 ± 0.96; P = 0.023). And FOURM scores were also higher in the adverse group (3.98 ± 0.22vs. smooth group 3.75 ± 0.86; P = 0.031). Abnormal airway patency was significantly more common in the adverse extubation group (71.2% vs. 54.5%; P = 0.030). The duration from command obedience to extubation was longer in the smooth extubation group (adverse group 1029 min vs. median 965 min; P = 0.017). The distribution of time to extubate was significantly between the groups (P = 0.028), with a higher proportion of patients extubated on the surgery day in the adverse group (73.7% vs. 66.2%) and a lower proportion extubated beyond the first postoperative day in the adverse group (1.3% vs. 11.7%). Craniotomy procedures were less common in the adverse extubation group (43.8% vs. adverse group 64.9%; P = 0.008). The adverse extubation group also had a significantly lower proportion of operations lasting ≥ 6 h compared to the smooth extubation group (7.5%vs. 22.1%; P = 0.010). Intraoperative propofol use was lower in the adverse extubation group (42.5% vs. smooth group 71.4%.; P < 0.001). and the use of sedation before extubation was also lower in the adverse group (18.8% vs. smooth group 32.5%; P = 0.049). Discussion

In this prospective study of 157 patients undergoing delayed extubation after intracranial procedures, we assessed factors influencing adverse extubation outcomes, focusing on patient safety and comfort. 51% of delayed extubation patients experienced adverse extubation outcomes, which aligns closely with previous retrospective studies [9]. We identified time to extubation, intraoperative propofol use, age, and airway patency as independent predictors of adverse extubation outcomes. Other factors, including consciousness level before extubation (FOUR-E, FOUR-M), duration from command obedience to extubation, operation duration, and sedation before extubation, were significantly associated with adverse extubation in univariate analysis but did not remain significant in multivariate logistic regression. Lesion location, patient position during extubation, and other factors were not found to be associated with adverse extubation outcomes.

Among the patients who experienced adverse extubation outcomes in this study, the most common issues were pain (33.8%) and systolic blood pressure (SBP) disorder (19.7%), followed by agitation (9.5%) and decreased SpO₂ (8.9%). Although we focused on the extubation period, our rates of adverse outcomes were similar to those reported in studies with longer observation windows [15, 27, 28]. This further validates that delayed extubation increases the risk of adverse effects. Previous studies, which typically had longer observation periods, might have missed signs occurring shortly after extubation, further emphasizing the importance of close monitoring during this critical phase. Moreover, most studies have focused on patients in post-anesthesia care units, which are typically associated with shorter extubation times and less severe illness compared to patients requiring NICU transfer. Successful extubation is particularly critical in patients undergoing intracranial surgery. Our study contributes by highlighting specific perioperative factors that influence the success of delayed extubation in these patients. By identifying key factors such as age and pre-existing airway compromise (e.g., snoring, high Mallampati score), we offer valuable insights for risk stratification in neurosurgical patients undergoing delayed extubation. Additionally, the protective association observed with intraoperative propofol use suggests that this is a modifiable factor clinicians can leverage. Proactively identifying high-risk patients (e.g., older age, compromised airway) combined with the judicious use of intraoperative propofol may enhance both safety and comfort during the extubation process.

While numerous studies have explored pharmacological strategies to reduce adverse extubation events, limited attention has been given to the comparative effects of intraoperative anesthetic agents on extubation quality in neurosurgical patients [29]. Our study demonstrates that propofol-based anesthesia (versus inhaled anesthetics) was independently associated with adverse extubation in delayed extubation neurosurgical cases (OR = 0.298). This aligns with propofol’s known advantages: accelerated respiratory recovery, reduced intracranial pressure, and a lower incidence of postoperative nausea—collectively facilitating timely neurological assessment [30]. However, the observational nature of this study precludes definitive causal inference. Selection bias may exist, as propofol is often preferred for hemodynamically unstable cases. Additionally, pharmacokinetic interactions between propofol and co-administered opioids (e.g., sufentanil) could theoretically influence recovery [31, 32]. This study did not quantify drug concentrations or directly assess such interactions, suggesting the need for future pharmacokinetic modeling and randomized comparisons of anesthetic regimens to elucidate optimal protocols.

This study also found that delayed extubation to the first postoperative day (POD1) was associated with a 7.884-fold higher risk of adverse extubation outcomes compared to extubation on the surgery day. However, it is important to recognize that this association may be influenced by reverse causality. Patients in poorer clinical conditions (e.g., higher intracranial pressure, neurological deterioration) may be more likely to experience delayed extubation, rather than delayed extubation directly causing adverse outcomes. Stampo et al. [33] Suggest that prolonged endotracheal intubation after cranial surgery increases the risk of complications such as aspiration, laryngospasm, and pneumonia, which could contribute to adverse outcomes. Furthermore, pain, agitation, and airway irritation during tube indwelling may lead to fluctuations in hemodynamics, exacerbating cerebral congestion and complicating high intracranial pressure and hemorrhage [6]. It is crucial to assess the readiness of patients for extubation not just based on time but also considering factors like consciousness, airway protective reflexes, and hemodynamic stability. In line with our study’s findings, we used the FOUR score to assess brainstem reflexes and respiratory drive, recommending extubation once brainstem reflexes and respiratory functions are adequately restored (high FOURB and FOURR scores). Our analysis suggests that patients who had higher FOURE (eye response) and FOURM (motor response) scores were more likely to experience difficulty during extubation. While awake extubation may contribute to adverse outcomes, deep extubation under anesthesia also poses respiratory risks [34]. Most patients are treated with other palliative methods, such as medications to inhibit the adverse reactions of extubation [35, 36]. This study emphasizes that while consciousness scores (such as the FOUR score) are important in assessing readiness for extubation, a relatively lower consciousness score does not necessarily preclude successful extubation as long as brainstem reflexes and respiratory functions are restored. Based on our findings, we recommend prioritizing extubation on the surgery day once these parameters are stable. Delaying extubation until the first postoperative day may expose patients to higher risks of complications.

Age ≥ 50 years independently predicted adverse extubation outcomes (OR = 2.791). Although age itself is non-modifiable, its associated risks can be mitigated through targeted interventions. Age-related declines in physiological reserve increase vulnerability to hemodynamic fluctuations and airway compromise during extubation [37, 38]. Management should focus on mitigating these consequences, with enhanced monitoring of hemodynamics and respiratory effort. Incorporating geriatric assessment tools (e.g., Fried Frailty Criteria) during preoperative evaluation can help identify at-risk patients [39]. Additionally, the use of dexmedetomidine or remifentanil TCI to attenuate catecholamine surges has been shown to reduce adverse effects [40]. Transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS) has also proven effective in reducing extubation reactions in craniotomy patients [41]. These findings emphasize the need for tailored extubation strategies in elderly patients, considering adjunctive techniques like TEAS to improve extubation quality and promote smoother emergence.

Preoperative airway patency assessment, particularly identifying habitual snoring or a short neck, was also an independent predictor of adverse extubation outcomes. Anatomical factors significantly increase risk. (1) Snoring: Often indicative of underlying obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), relaxation and edema of upper airway tissues post-extubation can dramatically increase the risk of obstruction and hypoxia. Snoring patients are also more prone to postoperative sore throat [42]. (2) Short Neck: This anatomical feature, often associated with limited neck extension, reduced thyromental distance, and increased soft tissue in the neck, can impede optimal positioning for effective mask ventilation and airway manipulation during extubation. It also correlates with a higher risk of pharyngeal collapse and obstruction after tracheal tube removal, complicating airway management and increasing the likelihood of re-intubation [43]. In this study, 20 patients with abnormal airway assessments (primarily snoring or short neck) experienced adverse respiratory outcomes like hypoxia or obstruction. Management should focus on anticipation and preparation: a thorough pre-extubation airway evaluation is crucial, incorporating assessments like the Mallampati classification and thyromental distance measurement [44]. For patients with known or suspected OSA or a short neck, strategies such as elevating the head of the bed, ensuring optimal positioning (e.g., “sniffing position”), having advanced airway equipment immediately available, and planning for post-extubation non-invasive ventilation (NIV) support should be strongly considered to mitigate the risk of airway obstruction.

In this observational cohort study, three factors demonstrated significant associations with adverse extubation outcomes in neurosurgical patients: advanced age (≥ 50 years), abnormal airway anatomy, and delayed extubation timing (POD1 vs. same-day). Intraoperative propofol use correlated with reduced risk. These findings suggest that for high-risk populations, clinicians should consider same-day extubation when feasible and implement enhanced airway evaluation protocols. However, causal inference is limited by the observational design, potential residual confounding, and the wide confidence interval for extubation timing, indicating substantial statistical uncertainty. Future randomized trials are needed to verify these protocol recommendations.

Limitations: Although our study achieved the calculated sample size, the relatively modest number of patients (n = 157) may limit the statistical power for subgroup analyses (e.g., extubation timing > POD1). Larger, multicenter cohorts are needed to validate our findings and enhance generalizability across diverse neurosurgical populations. While aligned with acute instability patterns, longer monitoring may capture delayed complications such as reintubation. Although anesthesia and sedation were protocolized, drug exposure gradients (e.g., propofol, dexmedetomidine duration) were not quantified. Future pharmacodynamic studies should model the effects of sedation titration. As there are currently no standardized extubation guidelines for postoperative neurosurgical patients, extubation decisions were made by a multidisciplinary team in the NICU based on extubation protocols, considering factors such as intracranial pressure, neurological recovery, and overall clinical status [45]. While these decisions may introduce potential confounders, we aimed to control for them by including a broad range of clinical variables in our analysis (Fig. 2).
