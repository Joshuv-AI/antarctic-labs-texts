The association between Healthy Eating Index‐2020 and epilepsy: Insights based on NHANES from 2013 to 2018
Authors: Kun Yu, Yingxin Wang, Huaiqing Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpilepsy is a serious chronic brain disease. However, limited study is focused on the association between dietary pattern and epilepsy management. Hence, we aim to investigate the association between the Healthy Eating Index (HEI‐2020) and epilepsy odds.MethodsTotally 5311 participants from 2013 to 2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were included. The HEI‐2020 scores were calculated based on the 24‐h dietary recall data. A multifactor logistic regression model was applied to analyze the potential association between the HEI‐2020 and epilepsy odds. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used to evaluate the nonlinear correlation.ResultsAfter adjusting for all confounding variables, there was a notable negative correlation between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds (OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–1.00, p = 0.046). Compared to the lowest HEI‐2020 quartile (Q1), a marked reduction in epilepsy odds was found in the highest HEI‐2020 quartile (Q4) (OR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.16–0.75, p = 0.007). RCS analysis showed a negative dose–response relationship between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds. In addition, the scores of fatty acids, whole fruits, greens, and beans were negatively correlated with the epilepsy odds, while the refined grain score was positively correlated with the odds of epilepsy.ConclusionIn conclusion, HEI‐2020 is negatively correlated with the odds of epilepsy, indirectly reflecting the association between dietary pattern and epilepsy odds. Scientific dietary structure reflected by the HEI‐2020 indicates a potential role for population‐level dietary guidelines in epilepsy prevention.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a common, serious neurological disorder that affects individuals across all ages. It is estimated that over 50 million people worldwide suffer from this condition. 1 Beyond the seizures themselves, epilepsy patients often experience a higher incidence of physical injuries, such as fractures and concussions resulting from seizure‐related accidents, as well as a range of psychological disorders, notably depression and anxiety. 2 , 3 These comorbidities can substantially impair patients' quality of life. Moreover, compared with the general population, individuals with epilepsy have an approximately threefold increased risk of premature death. 4 While antiepileptic drug therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, about 30% of patients continue to experience poor seizure control despite medication. 5 Therefore, it is urgent to develop more effective methods for the prevention and management of epilepsy.

Diet is an essential component of human life, directly influencing survival and health while also playing a pivotal role in disease management. 6 Recent research has increasingly focused on the potential of dietary interventions in the prevention and treatment of epilepsy. For instance, Vitamin D deficiency has been indicated as a critical risk factor for epilepsy based on a meta‐analysis. 7 A cross‐sectional study of dietary intake among epilepsy patients indicates that individuals who consume excessive calories and insufficient vegetables are more likely to experience uncontrolled seizures. 8 Additionally, Yary et al. have demonstrated a correlation between higher dietary magnesium intake and a reduced risk of epilepsy in middle‐aged and elderly Finnish men. 9 More recently, accumulating studies have shifted the research focus from the single nutrients to overall dietary patterns. It is noteworthy that the ketogenic diet (KD) has been validated as an effective nonpharmacological therapy for refractory epilepsy. This diet can significantly reduce seizure frequency and offers sustained antiepileptic benefits, thereby improving long‐term prognosis. 10 , 11 Moreover, variations of the KD, such as the modified Atkins diet (MAD) and low‐glycemic index treatment (LGIT), have also proven effective in managing refractory epilepsy. 12 , 13 , 14 These above findings underscore the critical role of diet in the control and management of epilepsy. However, rare studies have examined the association between overall dietary quality measured by the Healthy Eating Index (HEI), a validated index reflecting Dietary Guidelines for America (DGA) adherence, and epilepsy risk in a representative US population. This gap is notable because population‐level preventive strategies require practical, composite dietary metrics rather than single nutrients or highly restrictive therapeutic diets. The HEI serves as a sophisticated metric for evaluating dietary quality, particularly in assessing the compliance of the American population's diet with the dietary pattern recommended by the DGA. 15 , 16 , 17 The original HEI was introduced in 1995 by the US Department of Agriculture's (USDA) Center for Nutrition Policy and Promotion. Since 2005, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the United States Department of Health and Human Services, in collaboration with the USDA Center for Nutrition Policy and Promotion, has undertaken comprehensive revisions of the HEI to align it with updates to the DGA. The latest iteration of the HEI comprises two distinct components, including the HEI‐2020 (applicable to individuals aged 2 years and older) and the HEI‐Toddlers‐2020 (designed for children aged 12–23 months). 18 Despite the growing body of research highlighting the influence of diet on epilepsy, there remains a notable gap in the relationship between the HEI‐2020 scores and the odds of developing epilepsy.

Therefore, we herein employed the data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to investigate the association between HEI‐2020 as well as its components and the odds of epilepsy. Given the comparatively high prevalence and mortality rates of epilepsy in the United States, 1 along with the critical role of diet in health promotion, the findings from this research will provide a robust evidence base for optimizing public health strategies for epilepsy. Unlike therapeutic diets for refractory epilepsy, HEI‐2020 is expected to assess habitual dietary adherence to DGA guidelines and may inform primary prevention strategies.

METHODS

Study population

All data were accessed from the NHANES database (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx). We analyzed NHANES data from three cycles (2013–2014, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018). NHANES is a cross‐sectional survey based on the American population conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’ s National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). It is designed to assess the health and nutritional status of American adults and children. 19 This database collected a comprehensive range of health and nutrition data, covering demographics, dietary, examination, laboratory, and questionnaire data. The study protocol was approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board. All participants signed written informed consent before participating in this study. Thus, our present study was exempted from the ethical review.

Definition of HEI‐2020

HEI‐2020 is a useful tool to evaluate dietary quality. HEI‐2020 consisted of adequacy components, including total fruits, whole fruits, total vegetables, greens and beans, whole grains, dairy, total protein foods, seafood, and plant proteins, and fatty acids, as well as moderation components, including refined grains, sodium, added sugars, and saturated fats (detailed information was listed in Table S1). 18 HEI‐2020 was calculated based on the dietary intake data obtained from two separate 24‐h dietary recalls in the NHANES database, combined with the USDA FoodData Central database. HEI‐2020 scores span from 0 to 100, where higher scores reflect better dietary quality. Participants were stratified into quartiles depending on their HEI‐2020 scores, with the group having the lowest scores serving as the reference group (Q1).

Epilepsy assessment

The epilepsy related data was sourced from the “Prescription Drugs” section of the questionnaire. The participants listed any prescription drugs they had taken in the past 30 days and the reasons for their use. The participants who do not know, have not answered, or refuse to reply to this question were excluded during the data filtration. In this study, participants who had used drugs to treat “epilepsy and recurrent seizures” (the International Classification of Diseases G40) were defined as epilepsy participants (EPI group). 20 The list of drugs to treat “epilepsy and recurrent seizures” was summarized in Table S2.

Covariates

To minimize the influence of confounding factors on the research conclusions, we chose demographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and chronic diseases were selected as covariates. The covariate selection was based on previously published studies using NHANES data that identified these factors as potential confounders in the association between dietary quality and neurological outcomes. 21 , 22 The demographic covariates included age (20–60 and ≥ 60 years old), gender (male and female), race (Mexican American, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, other Hispanic, and other race), education level (less than high school, high school or equivalent, and college or above), marital status (never married, married/living with a partner, and separated/divorced/widowed), and poverty‐to‐income ratio (PIR) (low income: <1.5, medium: 1.5–3.5, and high: >3.5).

The behavioral covariates included body mass index (BMI) (low: <25, medium: 25–30, and high: ≥30), alcohol consumption (mild: never drinking, or females: <2 drinks/day, and males: <3 drinks/day, moderate: females: 2 drinks/day, and males: 3 drinks/day, and heavy: females: ≥3 drinks/day, and males: ≥4 drinks/day), 23 and smoking status (never: ≤100 cigarettes in a lifetime, former: >100 cigarettes but no longer smoking, and now: >100 cigarettes but occasionally or continuously smoking).

The chronic disease covariates included hypertension and hyperlipidemia, which were identified as per participants' self‐reports in the questionnaire. Hypertension was identified by taking antihypertensive medications or elevated average blood pressure (systolic pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure ≥90 mmHg). Hyperlipidemia was identified by total cholesterol level >200 mg/dL, triglyceride level >150 mg/dL, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) level <40 mg/dL for male or <50 mg/dL for female, or low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) level >130 mg/dL.

For all covariates, the variance inflation factor (VIF) in car package (version 3.1–3) was used to determine the multicollinearity among them. Then the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) and GVIF^(1/(2*Df)) were calculated. When GVIF^(1/(2*Df)) >5, there was significant multicollinearity among covariates. Our data indicated that all covariates exhibited GVIF^(1/(2*Df)) values <5 (ranged from 1.02 to 1.15) (Figure S1), thus here was no multicollinearity among the covariates included in the logistic regression model.

Statistical analysis

Weighted t‐test was performed for continuous variables, and chi‐squared test was performed for categorical variables to compare differences between groups. Continuous variables were represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were represented as quantity (proportion). Four multivariate models were developed using multifactor logistic regression analysis: No adjustments were made to Model 1; Model 2 was adjusted for age and gender; Model 3 was adjusted for age, gender, race, marital status, education level, and PIR; Model 4 also incorporated BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking status, hyperlipidemia status, and hypertension status in addition to the variables in Model 3. The above multivariate logistic regression model was employed for calculating odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to simulate the dose–response relationship between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds. Statistical significance of the difference was indicated by p < 0.05. The aforementioned statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.2).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study population

After deriving all participants ranging from 2013 to 2018 in NHANES, we have filtered the data. First, 13 906 participants younger than 20 years were excluded from the 59 484 participants. 20 Among the remaining 45 578 participants, 38 171 participants lacking epilepsy‐related information and 2096 participants lacking HEI‐2020‐related data were excluded. Finally, we included 5311 participants, including 153 epilepsy patients (EPI group) and 5158 individuals without epilepsy (non‐EPI group) (Figure 1).

Flowchart of study inclusion: Flowchart of the participants' selection from NHANES (2013–2018). HEI, Healthy Eating Index.

The average age of the participants was 39.59 ± 14.10 years old, with 54.2% being male and 45.8% being female. The average HEI‐2020 score was 50.48 ± 12.32 (Tables 1 and 2). Participants in the EPI group were older than those in the non‐EPI group (45.31 ± 17.03 years vs. 39.44 ± 13.98 years), and the proportion of individuals over 60 years old was significantly higher (22.7% vs. 10.1%, p = 0.003). Significantly more non‐Hispanic Whites were present in the EPI group than in the non‐EPI group (71.9% vs. 57%, p = 0.044). Compared to non‐EPI individuals, the HEI‐2020 score of EPI patients was significantly reduced (46.10 ± 10.58 vs. 50.60 ± 12.34, p < 0.001), and the proportion of participants with the lowest HEI‐2020 scores (Q1) was higher (36.3% vs. 25.8%, p = 0.046). In contrast, EPI patients tended to be never married (47.7% vs. 24.6%, p < 0.001), and separated, divorced, or widowed (18.1% vs. 11.7%, p < 0.001), and have lower education levels (Proportion of individuals below high school: 11.5% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.023), and a higher hypertension prevalence (44.9% vs. 17.9%, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in gender, PIR, BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and hyperlipidemia prevalence between the two groups.

According to their HEI‐2020 scores, participants were divided into quartiles (Q1: <41.74; Q2: 41.74 ≤ HEI‐2020 < 49.82; Q3: 49.82 ≤ HEI‐2020 < 58.79; Q4: ≥58.79), as shown in Table 2. Participants in the highest HEI‐2020 quartile (Q4) had a higher likelihood of being female, older, other races, married, or living with a partner, mild drinkers, never smokers, and having higher education levels, higher family income, lower BMI, as well as lower hyperlipidemia prevalence. Notably, compared to the Q1 and Q2 groups, the prevalence of epilepsy in the Q3 and Q4 groups gradually decreased significantly (p < 0.001).

Association between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy

Multivariate logistic regression models were constructed to investigate the association between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy, as presented in Table 3. In all four models, HEI‐2020 score, when treated as a continuous variable, exhibited a significant negative correlation with epilepsy odds (Model 1: OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–1.00, p < 0.001; Model 2: OR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.96–0.98, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–1.00, p = 0.015; Model 4: OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–1.00, p = 0.046).

Subsequently, all epilepsy patients were stratified into quartiles basing on the HEI‐2020 scores. The results showed that in Model 1, compared with the lowest HEI‐2020 quartile (Q1), participants in the highest HEI‐2020 quartile (Q4) had a significantly lower odds of epilepsy (OR = 0.33, 95% CI 0.19–0.56, p < 0.001). After adjusting for covariates, the similar association was observed (Model 2: OR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.16–0.45, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.21–0.69, p = 0.002; Model 4: OR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.16–0.75, p = 0.007). To further validate the robustness of the association between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy, a sensitivity analysis was then conducted. We regrouped all participants according to the HEI‐2020 score tertiles (T1: <44.53; T2: 44.53 ≤ HEI‐2020 < 55.55; T3: ≥55.55). For all four models, participants with T3 HEI‐2020 exhibited a significantly lower odds of epilepsy comparing to individuals with T1 HEI‐2020 (Model 1: OR = 0.43, 95% CI 0.28–0.66, p < 0.001; Model 2: OR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.24–0.56, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.28–0.76, p = 0.002; Model 4: OR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.25–0.85, p = 0.014) (Table S3). The above results indicated that participants with higher HEI‐2020 scores were associated with lower epilepsy odds.

Dose–response relationship between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds

RCS analysis was performed on Model 4 (adjusted with all covariates) to investigate the dose–response relationship between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds. The findings revealed a negative dose–response relationship between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds, but the nonlinearity of this association was not typical (p for nonlinear = 0.109) (Figure 2). Meanwhile, a two‐piecewise linear regression model was constructed for Model 4, and the breakpoint was 35.44. Our data showed that for participants with HEI‐2020 scores greater than 35.44, there was a strong negative correlation between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds (OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.94–0.99, p = 0.002). When the HEI‐2020 scores did not exceed 35.44, increasing the HEI‐2020 score was no longer associated with reduced odds of epilepsy (Table 4).

Stratified analysis was conducted in different subgroups, including age, gender, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and hypertension status, to explore potential changes in the association between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds. All detailed results are shown in Table S4. A notable negative correlation was observed between HEI‐2020 scores and epilepsy odds in specific subgroups, including males (Q4 vs. Q1: Model 1: OR = 0.25, p < 0.001; Model 2: OR = 0.19, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.23, p = 0.004; Model 4: OR = 0.22, p = 0.004) (Figure 3), individuals aged 20–60 years old (Q4 vs. Q1: Model 1: OR = 0.18, p < 0.001; Model 2: OR = 0.18, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.31, p = 0.006; Model 4: OR = 0.31, p = 0.013) (Figure 4), mild drinkers (Q4 vs. Q1: Model 1: OR = 0.35, p = 0.019; Model 2: OR = 0.22, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.28, p = 0.002; Model 4: OR = 0.34, p = 0.019) (Figure 5), and never smokers (Q4 vs. Q1: Model 1: OR = 0.31, p = 0.001; Model 2: OR = 0.26, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.37, p = 0.026; Model 4: OR = 0.26, p = 0.027) (Figure 6), as well as individuals without hypertension (Q4 vs. Q1: Model 1: OR = 0.25, p = 0.002; Model 2: OR = 0.22, p < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 0.24, p = 0.012; Model 4: OR = 0.28, p = 0.033) (Figure 7). DISCUSSION

Epilepsy is a serious neurological disorder that affects over 50 million people worldwide. 1 Frequent epileptic seizures can cause physical injuries and psychological problems, and in severe cases may lead to disabilities or even threaten patients' lives. 2 , 3 , 24 Although drug therapy remains the preferred clinical option, about 30% of patients still experience poor outcomes, 5 highlighting the urgent need for more effective prevention and treatment strategies. Therefore, we herein have evaluated the association between HEI‐2020 and epilepsy, and a crucial negative association between them was found.

The focus of dietary research has transitioned from isolated foods or nutrients to studying overall dietary patterns. For instance, it has been previously indicated that higher dietary magnesium intake is associated with significantly reduced epilepsy prevalence in US adults. 22 More recently, the analysis of dietary patterns comprehensively considers the combined effects of diverse foods and nutrients, demonstrating higher accuracy and reliability in disease odds prediction. 25 The research on dietary patterns in the prevention and management of epilepsy is rapidly developing. For example, many studies have shown that KD is effective in reducing seizure frequency in epilepsy, especially in drug‐resistant cases. 26 The mechanism by which KD reduces epileptic seizures is not fully understood. Several hypotheses have been proposed, such as an increase in ketone bodies and polyunsaturated fatty acids, inhibition of cell apoptosis and death, changes in the intestinal microbiota, and alterations in proinflammatory and anti‐inflammatory cytokines. 10 , 11 KD derivatives have been developed to improve the clinical tolerability and compliance of KD, such as the medium‐chain triglyceride diet (MCTD), 27 MAD, 28 and LGIT, 26 which have been proven to be equally effective in controlling epileptic seizures as KD. Nevertheless, unlike the above dietary patterns, HEI was a comprehensive assessment tool for dietary quality constructed based on DGA, 18 which was more suitable for the American population. Accordingly, we herein evaluated the association between the comprehensive index HEI‐2020 with epilepsy odds. We noticed that better dietary quality reflected by higher HEI‐2020 scores was associated with reduced risk of epilepsy. The recent cross‐sectional studies from NHANES showed that antioxidant‐rich diet 29 and low carbohydrate intake 30 could exert positive effects on the treatment of epilepsy, which indirectly supported our data. Baldeon et al. have demonstrated that HEI‐2020 was positively associated with microbiota richness, 31 thus intestinal microbiota might also be an important part of impacts of higher HEI‐2020 in epilepsy. Moreover, Wang et al. have also revealed a nonsignificant, nonlinearity between the HEI‐2020 and epilepsy odds, 21 in line with our data. Our present discovery opened up new research directions for the intersection of a high‐quality diet and epilepsy management. Furthermore, it is important to distinguish HEI‐2020 from intensive therapeutic diets such as KD and LGIT. While KD is used for seizure control in drug‐resistant epilepsy, HEI‐2020 reflects overall dietary quality in the general population. Thus, our findings primarily suggest that promoting higher dietary quality could contribute to the primary prevention of epilepsy, rather than acute seizure control.

Subgroup analysis data showed that the protective role of the HEI‐2020 dietary pattern in epilepsy was more significant in the male population but not statistically significant in the female population. This tendency was probably owing to a series of complex multifactorial differences between men and women, such as physiological functions, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and population heterogeneity. 32 , 33 On the one hand, estrogen in women has antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties, which can effectively alleviate oxidative stress and modulate the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines. 34 , 35 This protective mechanism to some extent, weakened the additional benefits of a high‐quality diet. Moreover, some evidence suggested that compared to men, women typically exhibit higher activity levels of antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), forming a stronger oxidative stress defense system. 36 Thus, we have the reason to speculate that this system in female weakened the protective benefit of the HEI‐2020 dietary pattern on epilepsy. More recently, Aycan et al. have demonstrated that gender‐related differences in eating behavior were observed in the epilepsy. 37 Meanwhile, the observed sex‐specific differences in our study may be influenced by unmeasured confounders such as physical activity, medication adherence, or dietary supplement use, which were not fully adjusted due to the limited data of NHANES. In short, future prospective studies are needed to confirm these subgroup findings.

Furthermore, after evaluating the association between dietary constituents included in the HEI‐2020 and epilepsy odds, our data indicated that higher scores of fatty acids, whole fruits, as well as greens and beans were correlated with reduced epilepsy odds, while higher refined grains were associated with increased epilepsy odds. Fruits, vegetables, and nuts are rich in anti‐inflammatory constituents and antioxidants, such as vitamin C, vitamin E, and polyphenols. These components help reduce the formation of free radicals and DNA oxidative damage, lower oxidative stress, inhibit inflammatory responses, thus reducing the odds of epilepsy. 38 , 39 Meanwhile, there were rich dietary fiber in fruits and vegetables. A recent study has documented that dietary fiber could drive substantial metagenomic shifts of microbial community, and dietary fiber content was related to the epilepsy outcome via changing the gut microbiome. 40 On the contrary, refined grains belong to proinflammatory foods, 41 and therefore may be associated with a higher odds of epilepsy. Collectively, to improve the epilepsy prevention and management, it is a promising dietary pattern to increase the intake of fruits, vegetables, and nuts, while reducing the consumption of refined grains.

Although our study suggested a potential association between higher HEI‐2020 scores and lower epilepsy odds, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precluded causal inference in this work. Second, due to the data limitation of the NHANES database, the lack of detailed clinical information (e.g., seizure type, severity) prevented adjustment for potential confounders. Therefore, our findings warranted further investigation and validation in real‐world prospective cohorts with detailed clinical information of epilepsy.

CONCLUSIONS

Taken together, our study has revealed a negative correlation between dietary quality as measured by the HEI‐2020 and epilepsy odds for the first time. The higher HEI‐2020 scores were associated with lower odds of epilepsy. From the dual perspectives of clinical medicine and nutriology, a scientific, and reasonable dietary structure not only provides effective support for the health management of epilepsy patients but also has the potential to open a new path for the prevention and treatment of epilepsy. Collectively, our findings indicate the vital value of HEI‐2020 score in population‐level prevention rather than therapeutic diets, highlighting its role in the early management of epilepsy.
