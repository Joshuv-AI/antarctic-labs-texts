Sex differences in MASLD-related atherosclerosis: plaque phenotype, vascular dysfunction, and cardiovascular outcomes
Authors: Mohamad Jamalinia, Ralf Weiskirchen, Amedeo Lonardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a major risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Emerging evidence suggests that the vascular manifestations of MASLD differ substantially between men and women. We therefore hypothesized that the mechanisms, plaque phenotypes, and clinical implications of MASLD-related ASCVD are sex-specific.Methods and resultsIn this narrative review, we searched PubMed/MEDLINE for studies published up to April 2026 and synthesized current evidence regarding sex differences in MASLD-related atherosclerosis. Available data support a sex-specific vascular paradigm in MASLD. In men, MASLD is more strongly associated with calcified and obstructive coronary atherosclerosis. In contrast, women with MASLD more frequently exhibit non-calcified atherosclerotic phenotypes and functional vascular abnormalities (e.g., endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and coronary microvascular dysfunction). Importantly, despite lower coronary artery calcium (CAC) burden, women with MASLD experience a disproportionately greater risk of adverse cardiovascular events. Therefore, calcification-centered assessment may inadequately capture ASCVD risk in women with MASLD by failing to identify non-calcified plaque and functional vascular abnormalities. These sex differences are likely mediated by interacting pathobiological and socio-cultural factors, including sex hormones and menopausal transition, adipose tissue distribution, immune-metabolic and inflammatory profiles, genetic susceptibility, and gender influences.ConclusionMASLD is associated with distinct sex-specific cardiovascular phenotypes that have important clinical implications for ASCVD risk assessment and prevention. Women with MASLD may develop clinically significant ASCVD despite lower CAC burden, supporting a sex- and menopause-aware approach to cardiovascular evaluation. In selected high-risk women, complementary use of multimodality imaging beyond CAC may improve risk stratification and facilitate earlier detection of clinically relevant cardiovascular disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13293-026-00952-3.Keywords: Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Coronary artery calcium, Cardiovascular imaging, Coronary artery disease, Endothelial dysfunction, Gender differences, MASLD, Myocardial infarction, Plaque phenotype, Sex differences

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has become one of the most prevalent chronic liver diseases worldwide and represents a growing global health challenge [1, 2]. Beyond its hepatic consequences, MASLD is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disorder with substantial extrahepatic complications [3]. Among these, cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in individuals with MASLD [4]. Strong evidence indicates close associations between MASLD and subclinical atherosclerosis, coronary artery calcification (CAC), adverse coronary plaque characteristics, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality [5–7]. Importantly, these associations often persist independently of traditional CVD risk factors, suggesting that MASLD itself contributes to the development and progression of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [5–8].

Accumulating data indicate that the relationship between MASLD and ASCVD differs substantially by sex [9, 10]. For example, men generally develop MASLD and coronary artery disease (CAD) earlier in life and more commonly exhibit calcified and obstructive atherosclerotic plaque [10–12]. In contrast, women tend to develop MASLD later in life and more frequently demonstrate non-calcified or mixed plaque phenotypes and non-obstructive CAD [10–12]. However, female-specific life stages, including puberty, pregnancy, premenopausal, and menopausal transitions, are each associated with distinct risks for the development of ASCVD [13]. Furthermore, the relative CVD risk associated with MASLD may be greater in women than in men [6, 14]. Socio-cultural differences between men and women (i.e., gender differences) may also influence MASLD-related ASCVD and contribute to differences in CAD risk between the sexes [11, 15].

Previous reviews have largely addressed sex differences in either atherosclerosis or MASLD, but not their intersection. Atherosclerosis-focused literature describes sex-specific differences in plaque burden, composition, calcification, and vulnerability, whereas MASLD-focused reviews primarily emphasize hepatic pathogenesis, hormonal regulation, and cardiometabolic risk [13, 16, 17]. As a result, how biological sex modifies MASLD-related atherosclerotic remodeling remains insufficiently defined. Given that plaque phenotyping is central to ASCVD risk stratification and prevention by linking upstream metabolic dysfunction to downstream cardiovascular events, this knowledge gap is clinically relevant [18]. We assumed the biologically plausible hypothesis that the mechanisms, plaque phenotypes, and clinical implications of MASLD-related ASCVD are sex-specific. Therefore, in this narrative review, we synthesize current evidence on sex differences in MASLD-related atherosclerosis, with a focus on plaque phenotype, vascular imaging, and subsequent cardiovascular outcomes. We also provide a critical appraisal of the principal limitations of the existing literature and delineate future research priorities to address these knowledge gaps.

Method

A literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE for studies published up to April 2026 (Supplementary Table 1) to identify original studies that evaluated the sex-specific association between MASLD and ASCVD, coronary plaque burden or phenotype, vascular calcification, endothelial dysfunction, or cardiovascular outcomes. Due to the narrative nature of this review, formal quantitative meta-analysis or structured risk-of-bias assessment was not performed. Instead, the extracted evidence was qualitatively synthesized, with a focus on sex-specific differences in plaque characteristics and cardiovascular outcomes in individuals with MASLD.

Pathophysiology of MASLD-related atherosclerosis with sex-specific modulation

MASLD promotes a multisystem proatherogenic cardiometabolic milieu through highly interconnected metabolic, lipotoxic, inflammatory, thrombotic, and gut-derived pathways. Biological sex modulates each pathway, leading to distinct patterns of disease expression in men and women (Fig. 1). Insulin resistance and atherogenic lipid remodeling

MASLD-associated insulin resistance promotes hepatic de novo lipogenesis via SREBP-1c activation, leading to overproduction of triglyceride-rich VLDL particles and accumulation of atherogenic remnant lipoproteins [8, 19–22]. Cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP)-mediated lipid exchange facilitates the formation of small dense LDL, while concurrent reductions in HDL impair cholesterol efflux and foam-cell clearance [8, 23]. Together, these abnormalities accelerate lipid deposition within the arterial wall, thereby promoting the expansion of the necrotic core and thinning of the fibrous cap.

In premenopausal women, estrogen attenuates hepatic lipogenesis, enhances LDL receptor activity, and maintains a less atherogenic lipoprotein profile [24]. After menopause, loss of estrogen removes this metabolic restraint, leading to a shift toward atherogenic dyslipidemia [25]. In men, physiological testosterone levels contribute to preserving insulin sensitivity and lipid partitioning, whereas testosterone deficiency promotes visceral adiposity and metabolic dysfunction [13]. In women with androgen excess (e.g., polyendocrine metabolic ovary syndrome, PMOS, previously known as polycystic ovary syndrome, PCOS), steatotic liver disease (SLD) and insulin resistance are further amplified [13]. These divergent trajectories likely contribute to earlier plaque formation in men, whereas women exhibit a sharper postmenopausal acceleration in lipid-driven atherogenesis.

Hepatic inflammation, immune activation, and plaque instability

MASLD fosters systemic inflammation through the hepatic release of cytokines, chemokines, acute-phase reactants, hepatokines, extracellular vesicles, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby promoting monocyte recruitment and macrophage activation within the vascular wall [26–28]. These inflammatory signaling pathways promote matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activation and extracellular matrix degradation, weakening fibrous caps and increasing plaque vulnerability [27, 28]. Progression from uncomplicated SLD to advanced liver fibrosis (F3–F4) amplifies systemic inflammation and atherogenic risk [5, 7, 29, 30].

Estrogen suppresses inflammatory signaling, macrophage activation, and macrophage MMP expression in premenopausal women, thereby supporting plaque stability [31, 32]. After menopause, loss of estrogen shifts the balance toward sustained inflammatory activity and impaired inflammatory resolution [33]. Women generally exhibit stronger innate and adaptive immune responses, including enhanced toll-like receptor (TLR) and interferon signaling [34], whereas men show earlier monocytosis and foam-cell formation [35]. These pathobiological differences likely influence both the temporal development and the magnitude of plaque inflammatory burden across sexes, although MASLD-specific evidence remains limited.

Lipotoxicity, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular remodeling

Lipotoxic mediators such as ceramides, diacylglycerols, and oxidized lipids, among others, induce oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, endothelial injury, and phenotypic switching of vascular smooth muscle cells [36]. Collectively, these processes reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, impair efferocytosis, and promote microcalcification and necrotic core expansion. In premenopausal women, estrogen preserves endothelial homeostasis by sustaining nitric oxide synthase activity and constraining oxidative stress [31, 32]. With menopause, this vasculoprotective effect is lost, thereby heightening susceptibility to lipotoxicity-induced vascular injury [37]. In men, earlier exposure to visceral adiposity–driven lipotoxicity may accelerate endothelial dysfunction and plaque progression, whereas in women these changes emerge later but intensify after menopause [38, 39].

Prothrombotic state and plaque destabilization

MASLD is characterized by a prothrombotic milieu with elevated PAI-1, fibrinogen, factor VIII, and tissue factor, leading to increased thrombin generation and platelet activation [3, 40]. These changes favor intraplaque hemorrhage, neovascularization, and recurrent micro thrombosis, all of which accelerate necrotic core expansion and fibrous cap thinning.

Estrogen exerts vasculoprotective and antithrombotic effects through endothelial and platelet-mediated mechanisms [19], whereas hypoestrogenic states favor a shift toward a procoagulant milieu [41]. Men generally exhibit greater basal thrombin generation and platelet reactivity [42, 43], which may act in concert with MASLD-associated coagulation abnormalities to potentiate thrombotic propensity. Collectively, these sex-related differences may modulate susceptibility to intraplaque hemorrhage and accelerate plaque destabilization.

Adipose tissue-vascular crosstalk and coronary microvascular dysfunction

Epicardial and visceral adipose tissue act as active endocrine organs, releasing cytokines, adipokines, and proteases that promote endothelial activation, monocyte recruitment, and extracellular matrix degradation [44, 45]. Perivascular adipose dysfunction further impairs vasomotor regulation, thereby amplifying local vascular injury [46]. MASLD-associated metabolic and inflammatory abnormalities may also be associated with coronary microvascular disease (CMD), characterized by impaired coronary flow reserve, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular remodeling [47, 48]. CMD is a key contributor to ischemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA), which is more frequently observed in women [49].

Sex-related differences in adipose tissue distribution may further modulate these associations. Men typically accumulate visceral and epicardial adiposity earlier in life, whereas women experience a shift toward central and epicardial fat accumulation postmenopausally [46]. This transition may contribute to both plaque progression and CMD, potentially explaining persistent ischemic symptoms despite low or modest CAC burden in some women [33, 46, 50–52].

Gut-liver axis dysfunction and systemic vascular inflammation

Gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability in MASLD promote endotoxemia and TLR4 activation, while microbial metabolites such as Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) enhance foam-cell formation, platelet reactivity, and endothelial dysfunction [8, 53, 54]. Disruption of bile acid signaling (FXR/TGR5) further contributes to vascular inflammation and calcification [3].

Estrogen may preserve gut barrier integrity and restrain inflammatory signaling, whereas post-menopasaul hypoestrogenemia could amplify endotoxin-driven vascular inflammation [55]. Although direct evidence in MASLD-related atherosclerosis remains limited, this axis may contribute to sex-specific differences in systemic vascular inflammatory burden.

Genetic, epigenetic, and X-chromosome–linked regulation

Established genetic variants such as Patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 3 (PNPLA3), Transmembrane 6 Superfamily Member 2 (TM6SF2), and Membrane-bound O-acyltransferase domain-containing protein 7 (MBOAT7) influence hepatic lipid metabolism and fibrosis risk [11, 56], while epigenetic regulators and microRNAs (e.g., miR-122) modulate macrophage and vascular smooth muscle cell behavior [57, 58]. These pathways may interact with hormonal status to produce sex-dependent vascular effects.

Beyond autosomal loci, emerging evidence suggests that X-chromosome gene dosage, through escape from X-inactivation, may contribute to sex differences in immune and metabolic pathways [59, 60] and may influence susceptibility to atherosclerosis independently of circulating sex hormones [61–63]. Proposed mechanisms include altered regulation of metabolic, epigenetic, and innate immune pathways [59, 64]. However, the contribution of X-chromosome dosage to MASLD-related atherosclerosis remains largely speculative, and direct evidence linking these mechanisms to coronary plaque phenotype or cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD is scarce, underscoring an important priority for future research.

Gender-related determinants of MASLD-related atherosclerosis

Gender-related factors may also shape the development and clinical expression of MASLD-related ASCVD through their influence on lifestyle, environmental exposures, and healthcare utilization [65]. Differences in diet, physical activity, smoking, alcohol use, sleep patterns, occupational exposures, and psychosocial stress may collectively modulate cardiometabolic risk, hepatic disease severity, and downstream vascular injury [11, 15]. In many settings, men are more frequently exposed to smoking- and alcohol-related risk behaviors, whereas women may face barriers to sustained physical activity and greater caregiving responsibilities, both of which may adversely affect metabolic health [66, 67].

Additionally, gender may shape healthcare-seeking behavior, disease recognition, and treatment patterns. Although women are generally more engaged in preventive healthcare [68], they may nonetheless experience under-recognition of cardiovascular symptoms and lower use of evidence-based therapies, including statins [15]. In parallel, the growing population of transgender individuals and the use of gender-affirming hormone therapy may introduce additional metabolic and cardiovascular determinants that could influence MASLD-related ASCVD [11, 69, 70]. Overall, however, evidence regarding the contribution of gender-related determinants to MASLD-related atherosclerosis remains limited, suggesting the necessity of additional and more focused research.

Sex-specific plaque phenotype and cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD

The relationship between MASLD and ASCVD differs significantly between men and women in terms of plaque burden, composition, and clinical outcomes (Table 1). Several imaging studies support the idea that MASLD in men is more likely to be linked with calcified and obstructive coronary atherosclerosis. In a cohort of 8,705 individuals, MASLD was associated with CAC only in non-obese men, with no significant association observed in women [71]. Similarly, among individuals undergoing coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), moderate-to-severe MASLD was strongly linked to severe CAC (CAC ≥ 300), and both single-vessel and multivessel coronary calcification in men, but not in women [9]. Sex-stratified machine learning models have also suggested that MASLD contributes more strongly to CAC prediction in men than in women [72]. Furthermore, in postmenopausal women, MASLD was not an independent predictor of CAC [73]. This apparent sex-specific calcification pattern may extend beyond the coronary circulation and has also been observed for abdominal aortic calcification (AAC); however, the available evidence remains inconsistent, with some studies reporting stronger associations in women and others demonstrating a higher risk in men [74, 75].

In contrast, MASLD in women appears to be associated with non-calcified atherosclerotic phenotypes and functional markers of vascular dysfunction. Independent associations between MASLD and carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) have been reported predominantly in women, even after adjustment for traditional cardiometabolic risk factors [76–78]. Beyond structural vascular abnormalities, MASLD in postmenopausal women has been associated with increased arterial stiffness, higher carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity, and impaired flow-mediated dilatation independent of metabolic syndrome [79]. Longitudinal data further demonstrate that the relationship between MASLD and progression of arterial stiffness was stronger in women than in men [80].

Further support for this sex-specific paradigm comes from a large real-world cohort study, which found that MASLD was associated with a higher risk of microvascular events in women than in men (adjusted Hazard Ratio [HR] 7.01 vs. 4.26) [14]. This finding extends MASLD-associated vascular injury beyond the macrovascular domain and demonstrates that women may exhibit a disproportionate susceptibility to microvascular dysfunction. Additionally, a large meta-analysis including approximately 18.5 million individuals, showed that MASLD was linked to a significantly higher risk of fatal and/or non-fatal cardiovascular events in women than in men (pooled HR 1.59 vs. 1.37, respectively) [6]. The impact of MASLD severity (variably assessed) on cardiovascular events also appeared to be greater in women than in men (pooled HR 2.40 vs. 1.69, respectively) [6].

These observations support the idea that MASLD-related vascular disease in women is not simply characterized by “less calcification,” but rather by a shift toward functional and microvascular pathology. Evidence from the CAC Consortium also shows higher relative cardiovascular mortality among women than men at comparable CAC levels [81]. Moreover, CAC density appears less predictive of ASCVD outcomes in women [81]. Collectively, data support the notion that calcium-centric assessment may systematically underestimate ASCVD risk in women with MASLD by failing to capture the combined high-risk phenotype of non-calcified plaque, endothelial dysfunction, and CMD (Fig. 2). Consideration of sex discrepancies further underscores the importance of a multimodality vascular assessment framework encompassing both macrovascular and microvascular domains.

Knowledge gaps and future directions

Despite growing evidence that men and women exhibit distinct patterns of MASLD-related atherosclerosis, several important knowledge gaps limit current understanding. First, much of the available evidence is derived from observational and predominantly cross-sectional studies, restricting causal inference regarding the temporal relationship between MASLD progression, plaque evolution, and cardiovascular outcomes.

In addition, substantial heterogeneity exists in the definition and staging of MASLD, with studies relying on varying combinations of ultrasonography, computed tomography, non-invasive fibrosis scores, administrative coding, or histology. Cardiovascular phenotypes have likewise been assessed inconsistently across studies using CAC, CIMT, arterial stiffness, endothelial function, CCTA, or clinical outcomes, which limit direct comparability.

Another major limitation of the current literature is the reliance on calcification-centered assessments. Although CAC is a validated marker of atherosclerotic burden, it does not adequately capture non-calcified plaque, vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, or CMD [82]. This limitation may be particularly relevant in women, who frequently demonstrate lower calcific burden but a higher risk of MASLD-related ASCVD events. Whether these observations reflect fundamentally different plaque phenotypes, differences in disease progression, or limitations of current imaging approaches remains uncertain.

Several biological mechanisms proposed to explain sex differences in MASLD-related atherosclerosis also require further validation. While the roles of sex hormones, adipose distribution, immune dimorphism, and metabolic dysfunction are supported by substantial evidence, the contribution of genetic, epigenetic, and X-chromosome-related pathways remains largely hypothetical.

The generalizability of current findings also remains uncertain. Many of the available cohorts originate from the Middle East or East Asia, and sex-specific associations may differ across ethnic, genetic, environmental, and sociocultural contexts. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the vascular phenotypes described in current studies are broadly applicable across diverse populations.

Another important but underappreciated area is the contribution of gender-related determinants. Lifestyle behaviors, healthcare utilization, socioeconomic factors, health literacy, preventive treatment patterns, and gender-related differences in symptom recognition may substantially influence ASCVD risk and outcomes in MASLD. However, these variables are rarely incorporated into existing studies, making it challenging to distinguish between biological sex effects and sociocultural influences.

Addressing these limitations will require multinational studies that adopt prespecified sex-stratified designs and systematically capture menopausal status, reproductive history, hormone therapy, androgen status, and relevant gender-related variables. In parallel, standardized characterization of MASLD severity and incorporation of mechanistic biomarkers may clarify the biological pathways linking MASLD to sex-specific ASCVD risk. Finally, multimodality cardiovascular phenotyping (Fig. 3) should move beyond calcification alone [83–87]. Future investigations should evaluate how plaque composition, vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and CMD contribute to cardiovascular outcomes in men and women with MASLD. Linking these imaging-derived phenotypes to longitudinal cardiovascular events will be essential for determining whether distinct vascular phenotypes translate into different clinical trajectories and for developing sex-informed approaches to ASCVD risk stratification and prevention in MASLD (Fig. 4).

Conclusion

MASLD is increasingly recognized as a major contributor to ASCVD, yet its vascular manifestations differ substantially between men and women. Current data support a sex-specific paradigm in which men with MASLD more commonly develop calcified and obstructive coronary atherosclerosis, whereas women with MASLD more often exhibit non-calcified plaque and functional vascular abnormalities (e.g., endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and CMD). Importantly, this dissociation between relatively lower CAC burden and greater functional and microvascular vulnerability in women suggests that calcification alone is an incomplete marker of ASCVD severity in this population.

These differences have important clinical implications. CAC scoring should be interpreted cautiously in women with MASLD, particularly in those with persistent metabolic or inflammatory risk, as low or absent CAC does not exclude clinically relevant atherosclerotic or CMD. In selected high-risk women (e.g., those with symptoms, high cardiometabolic risk, or discordant clinical profiles), complementary evaluation with CCTA and functional or microvascular testing may improve risk stratification and guide earlier preventive intervention, including more proactive management of cardiometabolic risk factors and evidence-based therapies such as statins.

Future studies should also adopt sex- and menopause-stratified designs with standardized MASLD phenotyping and longitudinal multimodality imaging linked to cardiovascular outcomes to enable more precise, sex-informed ASCVD prevention strategies.
