Internet use and COVID-19 vaccination attitudes among older adults in Switzerland during the pandemic
Authors: Arnaud Mabillard, Clément Meier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the critical importance of COVID-19 vaccination for older adults, vaccine hesitancy has been observed within this population. At the same time, internet use among older adults has increased substantially, making it important to understand how digital engagement shapes access to vaccination-related information. This study examines the associations between internet usage, self-estimated level of internet knowledge, and mistrust in online information with COVID-19 vaccination uptake and hesitancy among adults aged 60 + in Switzerland during the pandemic. The analytical sample includes 986 Swiss respondents from the Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) Corona Questionnaire 2 (2021). The associations in this article were estimated using probit regressions, controlling for socio-demographic, health, and regional characteristics. The findings reveal a higher likelihood of vaccination among older adults who use the internet as a source of information on COVID-19 and related regulations. Additionally, respondents with greater confidence in their internet knowledge are more inclined to get vaccinated. Conversely, those who find it challenging to differentiate between truthful and misleading information online are less likely to choose vaccination. Our findings highlight the potentially crucial role of internet use and digital literacy in shaping COVID-19 vaccination behavior among older adults in Switzerland. Promoting digital literacy and enhancing trust in reliable online health information could be key strategies to reduce vaccination hesitancy in this population. Addressing concerns about misinformation is also essential for improving vaccination uptake.

Introduction

Older adults are particularly at risk when it comes to COVID-19 infection, like many other pulmonary infections, hospitalization rates rises exponentially with age (Palmer et al. 2021) as age itself, along with other factors, is associated with increased mortality risk (Conde-Sala et al. 2020). Despite being more vulnerable to the virus, a portion of the older adults’ group still show indecisiveness towards getting vaccinated. For instance, in Switzerland, in the 50–64 age group, a proportion of 15.5% remained undecided about vaccination in 2021. Among younger retirees (ages 65–79), 11.1% expressed reluctance, while 10.9% of the oldest group (80 and older) were hesitant toward getting vaccinated (Bergmann et al. 2022). Across comparable populations, several factors have been cited to explain reluctance toward vaccination. These include concerns related to vaccine safety and effectiveness, such as fear of side effects and limited confidence in vaccine efficacy (Bhanu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2023), trust-related factors, including lower trust in government (Bhagianadh and Arora 2022), and information-related issues, such as insufficient or misleading information about the vaccine (Bhanu et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2023). In addition, a perception of good health has also been associated with lower vaccination uptake (Bhanu et al. 2021).

Alongside this trend of vaccine hesitancy, the proportion of internet users among older adults has increased substantially in recent years (König and Seifert 2023). Evidence from Switzerland shows that between 2009 and 2019, internet use among people aged 65 years and older rose markedly, from 37.8% to 74.2% (Seifert et al. 2020). Given these growing online habits and the vast amount of health-related content now available, the internet has become an increasingly important source of health information, complementing more traditional channels such as healthcare professionals and mass media. While turning to the internet for health-related research is a positive first step, it is equally important to understand how older adults navigate and engage with online resources. Their browsing habits and ability to discern reliable information are crucial factors that influence the quality of their knowledge and decisions. In this context, turning to online information should not be assumed to automatically lead to behavioral change. Rather, communication research suggests that the effects of internet-based information depend on how content is processed, interpreted, and trusted (Valkenburg and Peter 2013; Metzger and Flanagin 2013). Vaccination attitudes are shaped through intermediate mechanisms such as comprehension, trust in information sources, and perceived risk, processes that have been shown to be particularly influential in the context of online COVID-19 vaccine information (Loomba et al. 2021).

Beyond general internet use, the extent to which individuals rely on the internet as a source of health information may play a distinct role in shaping vaccination attitudes. Reliance on online health information can affect decision-making through several channels, including differential exposure to information of varying quality and engagement with misleading or confirmatory content in online environments (van der Linden et al. 2020). In this context, reliance on digital sources may influence how health information is interpreted and weighted, particularly when it complements or substitutes guidance from healthcare professionals (Diviani et al. 2015; Tan and Goonawardene 2017).

To measure a person's proficiency in using the internet, the concept of digital literacy can be used, which evaluates how effectively an individual searches for, processes, and understands the information available online (European Commission. Joint Research Centre. 2017; Van Laar et al. 2017; Tinmaz et al. 2022). While digital literacy is often conceptualized in the literature through such specific competencies, our study relies on a broader, self-reported measure of individuals’ internet knowledge available in the SHARE survey, which we interpret as capturing overall perceived digital competence rather than these skills directly. Interestingly, a higher level of digital literacy is linked to improved self-rated health (Gracia and Herrero 2009), highlighting the positive impact that greater digital competences can have on health outcomes. This suggests that individuals who are more adept at navigating and understanding online health information may be better equipped to make informed decisions about their well-being.

A related concern in the context of online health information is mistrust in the credibility of digital sources, which has been identified as an important factor shaping individuals’ responses to health-related information (Metzger and Flanagin 2013; Diviani et al. 2015). The fact that nearly anything can be found online means that information of varying quality coexists, and difficulties in assessing credibility may expose individuals to misleading or inaccurate health information, posing challenges for informed decision-making and public health more broadly (Lucassen and Schraagen 2011). Different studies have highlighted the contrast between individuals who obtain their information from traditional media, and those who rely on social platforms or other informal sources. All of these studies consistently found a negative association between using social media as an information source and vaccination acceptance or uptake (Allington et al. 2021b; Park et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2022). In some cases, relying on social media is also negatively associated with desired behavior such as social distancing (Fridman et al. 2020). Moreover, social media usage is linked to the spread of conspiracy beliefs, which in turn are associated with lower vaccination rates (Allington et al. 2021a).

To our knowledge there are no studies in the literature that inspects how internet use, self-estimated level of internet skills, and mistrust in online information affect COVID-19 vaccination attitudes in older Swiss adults who report using the internet. The objective of this study is thus to investigate these associations in the context of the pandemic.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data used in this study is sourced from the Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), an extensive longitudinal survey since 2004. SHARE targets individuals aged 50 and above across 28 European countries, including Israel (Börsch-Supan et al. 2013; SHARE-ERIC 2024) and collects detailed information on participants' health, socioeconomic conditions, and social networks through Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI). Fieldwork was carried out between June 10 and August, 2021, at a time when the COVID-19 pandemic had led to a widespread shift toward online activities. Computer-Assisted Telephone interviewing (CATI) was chosen as the preferred alternative to face-to-face interviews due to both methodological considerations and the need to protect the health of respondents and interviewers (Scherpenzeel et al. 2020). The study sample was drawn from the 1,748 Swiss participants of the COVID-19 CATI2 survey. From this group, only the participants who completed the Swiss paper-and-pencil questionnaire, specifically designed to assess technology use among older adults in Switzerland, were included, resulting in a sample of 1,566 participants. In this questionnaire, respondents were asked: “Since the start of the COVID-19 epidemic, have you used the internet at least once to send emails, search for information, make purchases, or for any other reason?” (Yes/No). This question assessed whether participants used digital technologies in their daily activities. Based on their responses to this question, participants were classified into two groups: those who reported using the internet (“onliners”) and those who did not (“offliners”). As the aim of this study is to examine the association between internet use and COVID-19 vaccination outcomes, only respondents who reported using the internet were retained for the analyses, resulting in the exclusion of 341 offline participants. In addition, although SHARE is designed to be nationally representative of its target population through regular sample refreshment, the Swiss sample has not been refreshed since 2011. For this reason, the present analysis was restricted to respondents aged 60 years and older. As a final step, respondents with missing values on key variables of interest, including sociodemographic characteristics and main explanatory variables, were excluded. After applying these criteria, the final analytical sample consisted of 986 internet-using respondents aged 60 years and older with complete data on all relevant variables (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Sample selection flowchart, SHARE Switzerland, 2021

Outcome variables

Vaccinated against COVID-19. This variable was constructed based on the question: “Have you been vaccinated against Covid-19?” (Yes/No). Respondents who answered “yes” were coded as 1, and all others as 0. In this study, being vaccinated refers to having received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine.

Hesitant about COVID-19 vaccination. This variable was constructed from the question: “Do you want to get vaccinated against COVID-19?” (Yes, I already have a vaccination scheduled/Yes, I want to get vaccinated/No, I don’t want to get vaccinated/I’m still undecided). Respondents who answered the last two options were coded as 1 and classified as “vaccine hesitant”, while all other responses were coded as 0.

Independent variables

Internet use for COVID-19 news. This variable captures whether respondents used the internet during the pandemic to stay informed about COVID-19 and related regulations. It is based on the following statement: “Since the start of the Covid-19 pandemic, our daily lives have been turned upside down. The Internet has been an important factor in keeping us informed, connected and entertained during this period. Please indicate to what extent you agree with the following statements: The internet allows me to stay more easily informed about the evolution of the COVID-19 pandemic and the current regulations.” Response options ranged from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Respondents who answered “agree” or “strongly agree” were coded as 1, while all other responses were coded as 0.

Self-estimated levels of internet knowledge. Participants were asked: “On a scale from 1 to 10, where 1 means 'no knowledge' and 10 means 'very good knowledge,' how would you rate your knowledge of using the Internet.

Based on this scale, three categories were created: low (1–4), intermediate (5–7), and high (8–10). This categorization allows for a more fine-grained analysis of the relationship between self-estimated digital skills and vaccination decisions. This categorization allows for a more fine-grained analysis of the relationship between self-estimated digital skills and vaccination decisions. It also enables the use of the lowest skill level as a reference group, representing approximately 17% of the study population, thereby ensuring robust comparisons.

Mistrust in online information. This variable was constructed to assess respondents perceived difficulty in evaluating the credibility of online information. Participants were asked whether they agreed with the following statement: “On the internet, it is difficult to assess the quality of information, to discern truths from the untruths.” Participants who answered “agree” or “strongly agree” were coded as 1 and classified as expressing mistrust in online information, while all other responses were coded as 0.

Sociodemographic variables. These include sex (0 = male, 1 = female), age group (60–69 years, 70–79 years, 80 + years), and education level, classified according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) (OECD et al. 2015) into three categories: low (levels 0–2), secondary (levels 3–4), and tertiary (levels 5–6). We also accounted for partnership status (0 = has a partner, 1 = has no partner), as well as respondents perceived financial situation, based on the question "Is your household able to make ends meet?" with responses categorized as 1 = easily, 2 = fairly easily, and 3 = with difficulty. Additionally, regional characteristics included linguistic regions: Swiss-German, French, or Swiss-Italian and living environment (0 = urban, 1 = rural). Health status was measured by a general self-rated health question, categorized into 1 = poor/fair, 2 = good, and 3 = very good/excellent. Finally, the frequency of internet use was assessed with the question: “On average, how frequently have you used the Internet during the past six months?” (1 = Daily, 2 = Several times a week, 3 = several times a month and 4 = more rarely).

Statistical analysis

The study population's characteristics were summarized using counts and proportions (Table 1), and bivariate analysis (Figs. 2 and 3). The associations between COVID-19 vaccination status, vaccination hesitancy, and the variables of internet usage, self-estimated level of internet skills, and mistrust in online information were analyzed through probit regressions. Regression estimates are presented as average marginal effects (AMEs), which indicate the change in the average conditional probability P(Y = 1 | X) in percentage points when a given explanatory variable shifts from 0 to 1, while keeping all other independent variables at their observed values. Separate probit models were used to examine each of these independent variables' associations with vaccination status and hesitancy. All models were adjusted for sex, age, education level, partnership status, linguistic region, subjective financial difficulties, living environment, self-rated health and frequency of internet use. To account for potential unobserved dependencies between respondents and their partners, error terms were clustered at the household level. All analyses were performed using the statistical software Stata version 18. Results

Table 1 summarizes the main characteristics of our sample. The proportion of women was 50.4%, with a median age of 69 years. Among the respondents, 41.3% were aged between 70 and 79 years, while 12.5% were aged 80 years or older. The majority had attained a secondary level of education, and 10.6% had a lower educational level. Most respondents reported that they could make ends meet easily (60.3%) or fairly easily (29.8%). A small majority (54.8%) lived in rural areas. Regarding self-rated health, only 12.8% reported having poor or fair health. In terms of linguistic region, 73.6% were from the German-speaking part of the country, 24.1% from the French-speaking part, and 2.3% from the Italian-speaking part. Most of the respondents use the internet daily (68.5%) to several times a week (23%). Examining the independent variables, 70% of respondents used the internet to stay informed about COVID-19 and related regulations. Concerning self-estimated level of internet skills, the majority reported having an intermediate (48.6%) to high (34.8%) level, while only 16.6% indicated a low level. Furthermore, 73.2% did express mistrust in the information found on the internet. Finally, 84% of the respondents were vaccinated against COVID-19, while 13.3% were hesitant about vaccination.

Figure 2 (panels a, b and c) presents the proportion of participants who were vaccinated against COVID-19 (“vaccinated”) and those who were not (“not vaccinated”), according to internet usage, self-estimated internet skills and mistrust in online information. Panel 2a shows vaccination rates among individuals who reported using the internet to stay informed about the pandemic. Overall, the majority of respondents were vaccinated, with a slightly higher uptake among those who used the internet for COVID-19-related information. Panel 2b illustrates vaccination rates by level of self-estimated digital skills. A clear gradient can be observed, with vaccination uptake increasing from low to high levels of internet skills. Finally, panel 2c shows vaccination status by level of mistrust in online information. While most respondents were vaccinated regardless of their level of trust, vaccination rates were slightly higher among individuals who reported mistrust in online information.

Similarly, Fig. 3 (panels a, b, and c) illustrates the proportion of participants who were hesitant about COVID-19 vaccination (“hesitant”) and those who were not (“not hesitant”), according to internet use, self-estimated internet skills, and mistrust in online information. Panel 3a shows that low levels of vaccine hesitancy are observed in both groups, those who reported using the internet to stay informed about the pandemic and those who did not. Panel 3b indicates that low levels of hesitancy are consistently observed across all levels of self-estimated internet skills, with a slight decrease in hesitancy as skill level increase. Finally, panel 3c shows that while most respondents reported not being hesitant, vaccine hesitancy was slightly more frequent among individuals who expressed mistrust in online information.

Table 2 presents the results from adjusted probit regression models examining the associations between internet-related factors, sociodemographic characteristics, and COVID-19 vaccination outcomes among internet users. Regarding the main explanatory variables, using the internet to stay informed about the pandemic and related regulations is positively associated with being vaccinated against COVID-19 (AME = 0.13, p = 0.03) and with a lower likelihood of vaccine hesitancy (AME = − 0.13, p = 0.03). In addition, higher self-estimated levels of internet skills are associated with increased vaccination uptake, with positive associations observed for both intermediate (AME = 0.09, p = 0.04) and high skill levels (AME = 0.12, p = 0.04). With respect to hesitancy, only the highest category of self-estimated internet skills shows a significant negative association (AME = − 0.10, p = 0.04). Finally, mistrust in online information is weakly but positively associated with vaccine hesitancy (AME = 0.04, p = 0.02), while no significant association is observed with vaccination uptake. Turning to sociodemographic and health characteristics, older age is positively associated with vaccination uptake (70–79 years: AME = 0.09, p = 0.03; 80–99 years: AME = 0.15, p = 0.04) and negatively associated with vaccine hesitancy (70–79 years: AME = − 0.07, p = 0.03; 80–99 years: AME = − 0.14, p = 0.03). Similarly, higher educational attainment contributes positively to vaccination (tertiary education: AME = 0.09, p = 0.05) and is associated with lower levels of hesitancy (AME = − 0.09, p = 0.05). In contrast, having difficulty making ends meet (AME = 0.11, p = 0.05), living in a rural area (AME = 0.05, p = 0.03), and reporting very good self-rated health (AME = 0.06, p = 0.03) are positively associated with vaccine hesitancy, while these same characteristics are linked to a lower likelihood of being vaccinated (difficulty making ends meet: AME = − 0.12, p = 0.05; rural residence: AME = − 0.05, p = 0.03; very good self-rated health: AME = − 0.06, p = 0.03). As a robustness check, additional logistic regression and linear probability models were estimated, yielding results closely aligned with those obtained from the probit models and thereby confirming the robustness of the main findings.

Discussion

Overall, our analysis shows that vaccination decision and attitudes among older adults in Switzerland are associated in different ways with internet use.

Using the internet to stay informed about the pandemic

The use of the internet to stay informed about the pandemic seems to play an important role in increasing the vaccination uptake and decreasing vaccination hesitancy for older adults, by allowing an access to reliable and accurate sources of information. Our study reveals that the likelihood of COVID-19 vaccination is higher among individuals who report more frequent internet use. In parallel, our study showed that a higher usage of internet is associated with lower vaccination hesitancy, this result is related to the fact that using the internet more often is associated with better qualities in searching this information and getting better informed avoid being polluted by misinformation, often present online (Pakalniškienė et al. 2022).

Self-estimated levels of internet skills

Self-estimated levels of digital skills increase internet use (Seifert and Charness 2022), as individuals become more proficient in using online tools, they naturally increase their use. In addition, self-estimated internet knowledge is positively associated with vaccination attitudes: the more confident individuals are in using online tools, the higher their likelihood of being vaccinated and the lower their likelihood of vaccine hesitancy. These findings highlight the importance of digital skills, suggesting that higher levels of digital competence are needed to positively influence vaccination-related attitudes.

Mistrust in online information

Mistrust in online information appears to play a nuanced but limited role in shaping vaccine-related behaviors among older adults in Switzerland. Although it does not exhibit strong explanatory power in the models, its conceptual relevance should not be dismissed. Mistrust is modestly associated with vaccine hesitancy, pointing to the potential influence of alternative or competing sources of information. However, the relatively weak statistical association suggests that other factors, particularly self-estimated digital skills and regular internet use, play a more substantial role in shaping vaccination decisions and attitudes. Taken together, these findings indicate that fostering trust in online information may serve as a complementary strategy rather than a primary intervention, reinforcing the importance of broader efforts to enhance digital literacy and informed internet use.

Existing literature

Although no prior studies have specifically examined the association between internet use, self-estimated internet skills, and vaccination decisions among older adults in Switzerland, a substantial body of related literature has investigated similar relationships in other contexts and reports largely convergent findings.

The association between higher levels of self-estimated digital skills and a greater willingness to vaccinate has been documented in several studies conducted among adult populations across different countries (Paige et al. 2017; Pakalniškienė et al. 2022). Although these studies do not focus specifically on the population examined in the present article, their findings remain highly relevant for older adults who use the internet. Despite generally lower levels of digital proficiency, older individuals can benefit from access to reliable online information and from the social connections facilitated by internet use, which is even more important during pandemics, when social isolation is stronger and poses a real danger for their mental well-being (Song et al. 2021; Kassam and McMillan 2023; Li et al. 2024). These factors may, in turn, increase their willingness be vaccinated, highlighting the important role of digital skills in promoting positive health behaviors among older adults. In addition, evidence from a study conducted among older adults living in urban Japan shows that higher self-estimated internet skills are associated with greater likelihood of COVID-19 vaccination (Takayama et al. 2023). These findings also point to disparities between urban and rural populations, which is consistent with the patterns observed in the present study, and help identify specific groups that may benefit most from targeted public health interventions. Trust in online information further plays a central role in research on digital technologies and vaccination behavior. Higher levels of internet proficiency are often associated with greater trust in online information, particularly when individuals rely on credible sources (Paige et al. 2017). More broadly, trust constitutes a key factor in the dissemination and uptake of health information, especially among individuals with limited digital experiences and lower digital skills. Although trust was not the primary focus of this study, its role should not be overlooked when designing effective health communication strategies.

Policy recommendations

To improve vaccination rates and reduce hesitancy among older adults in Switzerland, a multi-faceted policy approach is needed that focuses on strengthening digital skills, fostering trust in online health information, and promoting internet use. Community-based digital skills programs can play an important role in equipping older adults with the competencies needed to navigate online health resources effectively, particularly when combined with improvements in digital infrastructure and accessibility (Scherpenzeel et al. 2020; Seifert et al. 2020). In addition, user-friendly health platforms designed with older users in mind can further facilitate digital engagement and access to reliable information. Building trust in online health information is equally essential. Transparent, secure, and clearly communicated health messages, along with efforts to counter misinformation, are key components of effective public health strategies. Encouraging results from similar interventions have already been documented in comparable populations, demonstrating improvements in both digital engagement and health-related outcomes (Seifert and Charness 2022; Song et al. 2021). At the same time, caution is warranted when implementing such initiatives, as older populations are highly heterogeneous int term of socio-economic background, education, and digital experience (Van Deursen and Helsper 2015). Policies should therefore be carefully targeted to those most likely to benefit. In this regard, direct interventions, such as personalized reminders via phone or electronic health records, have also shown promise in increasing vaccine uptake (Tinmaz et al. 2022; Van Deursen and Helsper 2015). Overall, tailored, inclusive, and evidence-based strategies are essential to ensure that digital health interventions effectively reach and support older adults.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this analysis lies in the use of high-quality, standardized data from the SHARE survey, whose extensive documentation and strong policy relevance enhance the robustness and interpretability of the findings. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of self-reported measure, particularly for variables such as internet skills, may introduce recall or social desirability bias. In addition, panel attrition resulting from mortality or health decline may lead to selective dropout overtime (Brilleman et al. 2010; Okpara et al. 2023). To mitigate this issue, SHARE aim to follow respondents for as long as possible, including those residing in nursing home, thereby reducing but not fully eliminating potential attrition bias. Furthermore, the shift to telephone interviews during the COVID-19 pandemic may have affected data quality by limiting interviewer-respondent rapport and potentially influencing response behavior, legitimacy perceptions, and measurement precisions (Bowling 2005). However, there is no indication that data quality was substantially compromised for the variables used in the present analysis. A key strength of this study is its focus on a relatively underexplored topic: the relationship between digital skills, internet use, and vaccination behavior among older adults in Switzerland who use the internet. By addressing this gap, the study provides novel insights that can inform the design of targeted public health and digital inclusion policies.

Future research

Future research could explore the relationship between internet use and vaccination decisions and behaviors across European countries, comparing results with Switzerland to assess the influence of digital infrastructure, cultural attitudes, and health policies. This cross-country analysis would help determine whether the observed patterns in Switzerland are generalizable or context specific. Longitudinal studies could examine how changes in digital literacy and internet use over time impact vaccination decisions and hesitancy, while qualitative research could explore psychological and socio-cultural drivers of mistrust. Experimental designs could test targeted interventions across different subpopulations, comparing digital and non-digital approaches (e.g., community programs, digital literacy workshops, or personalized health messaging). Expanding the focus to other public health issues (e.g., flu vaccination, preventive screenings) would offer broader insights into digital abilities and health engagement.

Conclusion

This article examined the associations between internet use, self-estimated levels of internet knowledge and mistrust in online information in relation to COVID-19 vaccination uptake and vaccine hesitancy. The findings indicate that individuals who use the internet to seek pandemic-related information and who report higher levels of internet skills are more likely to be vaccinated and less likely to express vaccine hesitancy. In contrasts, mistrust in online information appears to play a more limited role, showing only a modest association with increased hesitancy. Taken together, these results highlight the importance of promoting informed and effective internet use among older adults as a mean of supporting vaccination uptake and reducing hesitancy. Policy efforts should therefore prioritize strengthening digital skills among older internet users and promoting access to reliable online health information. At the same time, encouraging the use of trusted sources for health-related content may further help reduce vaccine hesitancy and support higher vaccination uptake.
