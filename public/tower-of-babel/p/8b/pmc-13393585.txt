Wilkie syndrome combined with nutcracker syndrome presented as syncopal episodes: A case report
Authors: Aikaterini Marini, Anastasios Pagonas, Panagiotis Kitsoulis, Kalliopi Iliou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Combined aortomesenteric compression is an extremely rare vascular condition where both the duodenum (Wilkie) and the left renal vein (nutcracker) are compressed between the aorta and the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). The causal factor is the abnormal course of the SMA originating from the abdominal aorta with reduced angle. Here we describe the case of a young male patient presenting with repetitive episodes of loss of consciousness, arrhythmias and hyperamylasemia, not associated with other symptoms. Color Doppler revealed an aortomesenteric angle of 15°, compression of the left renal vein and the third part of the duodenum. An abdominal Computed Tomography (CT) scan confirmed the ultrasound findings. Cardiological examination with 24-hour Holter monitoring reported frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVC). In lean patients with syncopal episodes and abnormal amylase levels, combined aortomesenteric compression syndrome should be considered. Patients with nutcracker syndrome have to be evaluated for autonomic nervous system dysfunction.

Introduction

The Wilkie and nutcracker syndrome are rare vascular pathologies that are caused by narrowing of the angle between the aorta and the superior mesenteric artery, which compresses the duodenum or the left renal vein respectively [1,2]. Imaging criteria for the diagnosis of nutcracker syndrome include an aortomesenteric angle of < 35° and an aortomesenteric distance < 8 mm, with left renal vein (LRV) compression [3]. Wilkie syndrome is characterized by aortomesenteric angle of < 22–25° and aortomesenteric distance < 8 mm with compression of the duodenum [4]. Rapid weight loss, low body fat, pancreatic neoplasm, aortic aneurysm, overarching testicular artery, strangulating fibrolymphatic tissue and spinal surgery can be causative factors, but both syndromes may be congenital as well [5,6]. Demographics are unknown but prevalence is higher in women. Most symptoms appear in the second and third decade of life [7]. The combination of the two syndromes is extremely rare [8].

The nutcracker syndrome is characterized by impeded outflow of the left renal vein (LRV) into the inferior vena cava (IVC), dilatation of the LRV proximal to the kidney and subsequent varicocele [9]. The symptoms include hematuria, orthostatic proteinuria, flank and abdominal pain, varicocele, dyspareunia and dysmenorrhea. Symptoms are aggravating during exercise [10]. Its rarest manifestations are hypotension, tachycardia and syncope due to autonomic dysfunction [11]. To date, there is lack of knowledge on the exact incidence and mechanisms behind nutcracker syndrome’s impact on autonomic nervous system dysregulation. On the other hand, Wilkie’s syndrome is characterized by duodenal compression resulting in vomiting, post-prandial pain and weight loss. The reduction of the fat surrounding the abdominal aorta and the SMA is the main pathogenetic factor for the acquired form of the syndrome [12]. Typical symptomatology includes abdominal fullness, post-prandial pain, nausea, biliary reflux and vomiting. But occasionally manifestation may be only weight loss or even atypical [13].

Here we describe the first reported case of concomitant Wilkie’s and nutcracker’s syndrome, with the unusual manifestation of arrhythmias, syncopal episodes and hyperamylasemia.

Case report

Our patient was an 18-year-old male admitted to the hospital for the third episode of loss of consciousness within 6 months. His height and weight were 1.75 m and 63 kg respectively, thus he was characterized as underweight (body mass index [BMI] of 17.37). His medical history was unremarkable, though he was a smoker (10 cigarettes daily). His arterial pressure was 134/71 mmHg, his heart rate 71 bpm and his oxygen saturation 98%. His laboratory data are presented in Table 1. The patient was examined by a neurologist and a cardiologist without pathological findings. He then underwent brain CT, carotid duplex ultrasound and electroencephalogram (EEG), with normal findings. The 24-hour Holter monitor revealed more than 200 PVCs, without episodes of atrial fibrillation or other conduction abnormalities. On the second day of his hospitalization, extensive blood and urine tests revealed significant hyperamylasemia (237 U/L) and hyperamylasuria (1911 U/L). An abdominal ultrasound was conducted in order to assess pancreas. No pathological findings were recorded. The patient was kept nil per os for two days and serum amylase levels returned to normal. At the one-week outpatient follow-up, further examination was conducted. A Color Doppler scan of the abdominal aorta showed a reduction of the aortomesenteric distance (3 mm) (Fig. 1A), the aortomesenteric angle (15°) (Fig. 2) and of compression of the left renal vein and of the duodenum between the aorta and the SMA. Ultrasound demonstrated a dilatation of the left renal vein proximal to the renal hilum, which was approximately fivefold greater than its diameter at the aortomesenteric interval (ratio 5:1) (Fig. 3). The examination was conducted using an ALPINION E-CUBE 8 machine and a probe of 1-6 MHz. The patient presented no changes in laboratory analysis and urine did not present protein or blood. He remained asymptomatic and thus he was subsequently subjected to an abdominal CT examination. CT verified the diagnosis of combined aortomesenteric compression, Wilkie and nutcracker syndrome (Fig. 1, Fig. 4,B). Moreover, as part of the investigation of syncopal episodes, a cardiac MRI was performed, with normal findings. Discussion

Aortomesenteric compression syndromes are caused by the decreased aortomesenteric angle and distance, which are normally > 25°and > 10 mm respectively [14]. In the present case the angle was 15°and the distance 3 mm. The one side of the coin is Wilkie syndrome which may lead to obstruction of the duodenum. Main predisposing factors include malignancy, trauma and weight loss. Our patient was stably underweight (BMI: 17.37), without reporting any loss of weight, anorexia, abdominal distension or vomiting in the past. Consequently, neither vitamins nor electrolyte imbalance was observed, which are usually present in Wilkie syndrome. The only abnormal laboratory test was amylase. Hyperamylasemia warranted the ultrasound and CT of the pancreas, where the compression of the third part of the duodenum was noted. Duodenal compression can cause elevated amylase through the increase of the intraluminal pressure and the obstruction outflow at the ampulla, impairing pancreatic duct drainage. The resulting back-pressure and pancreatic irritation may cause leakage of amylase into the bloodstream, leading to hyperamylasemia [15]. nutcracker syndrome has the same anatomical etiology as Wilkie syndrome, but the compression refers to the LRV. Classical signs include hematuria, proteinuria, flank and abdominal pain and sometimes varicoceles in males [16,17]. Our young patient did not present any of the above even during vigorous exercise. No proteinuria or microscopic hematuria were observed. Fainting and tachycardia was described as a symptom of nutcracker syndrome by Takemura et al., in a 14 year-old female patient [18]. Daily et al. presented a very rare case of a young woman with multiple syncopal episodes and unilateral hematuria [11]. In accordance with the above, in a sample of pediatric population, with diverse symptoms, 55.5% had orthostatic symptoms, 13% palpitations and only 6% presented syncopal episodes [17]. It has been also shown that children with nutcracker syndrome had higher heart rate compared to control group [19]. In line with our case, no other cause for orthostatic disturbance was found. Tachycardia has been rarely described as a persistent symptom, but always with co-existent hematuria or proteinuria [17,20]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of combined aortomesenteric syndrome, where the only manifestation was numerous PVCs and syncopal episodes, regarding the autonomic nervous system.

Data regarding the impact of nutcracker syndrome on the autonomic nervous system is scarce. Nevertheless, it seems that the syndrome is often accompanied by an autonomic dysfunction of varying severity. Renal vein compression and decreased blood flow back to the heart, may cause tachycardia and palpitations [11,18]. Other mechanisms may include disruptions in renin-angiotensin and noradrenaline system, elevated resistance in arterioles and immunological cascade leading to inflammation and increased cytokines [21,22].

In the few reported cases of concomitant Wilkie and nutcracker syndrome postprandial abdominal pain and vomiting were the only or the prominent symptoms [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. The fact that in our case the manifestation was solely syncopal episodes renders our case even more distinctive. Additionally, our case highlights an area for future research, as it remains unknown whether autonomic dysfunction improves with somatic growth and weight gain in parallel with the typical symptomatology [28].

Conclusion

The importance of this unique case lies not only in the extremely rare combination of Wilkie and nutcracker syndromes, but also in the unusual presentation. Ultrasound has been proven as the most valuable diagnostic tool for the compression of the renal vein and the duodenum. Physicians should bear in mind that in lean patients with syncopal episodes and elevated amylase levels, a 24-hour Holter and at least an abdominal ultrasound should be conducted, even in the absence of typical symptomatology, as autonomic dysfunction is a rare but presumed manifestation of compression syndromes. Furthermore, in cases with nutcracker phenomenon, it is important to consider the probable concurrent occurrence of Wilkie syndrome as well.
