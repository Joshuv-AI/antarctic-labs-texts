Pneumococcal surface protein A (PspA) prevents killing of Streptococcus pneumoniae by indolicidin
Authors: Natalha T Waz, Barbara Milani, Lucas Assoni, Guilherme Rabelo Coelho, Juliana M Sciani, Thaís Parisotto, Lucio F C Ferraz, Anders P Hakansson, Thiago R Converso, Michelle Darrieux
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPneumococcal surface protein A (PspA) is an important virulence factor in Streptococcus pneumoniae that binds to lactoferrin and protects the bacterium from the bactericidal action of lactoferricins—cationic peptides released upon lactoferrin proteolysis. The present study investigated if PspA can prevent killing by another cationic peptide, indolicidin. PspA-negative pneumococci were more sensitive to indolicidin-induced killing than bacteria expressing PspA, suggesting that PspA prevents the bactericidal action of indolicidin. Similarly, chemical removal of choline-binding proteins increased sensitivity to indolicidin. The absence of capsule and PspA had an additive effect on pneumococcal killing by the AMP. Furthermore, anti-PspA antibodies enhanced the bactericidal effect of indolicidin on pneumococci, while addition of soluble PspA fragments competitively inhibited indolicidin action. Previous in silico analysis suggests a possible interaction between PspA and indolicidin. Thus, we hypothesize that PspA acts by sequestering indolicidin and preventing it from reaching the bacterial membrane. A specific interaction between PspA and indolicidin was demonstrated by mass spectrometry, confirming that PspA can actively bind to the AMP. These results reinforce the vaccine potential of PspA and suggest a possible mechanism of innate immune evasion employed by pneumococci, which involves binding to cationic peptides and hindering their ability to damage the bacterial membranes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73564-9.Keywords: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pneumococcal surface protein A, Indolicidin, Cationic peptide, Immune evasionSubject terms: Microbiology, Bacteria, Bacterial immune evasion, Bacterial pathogenesis

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a pathogen that colonizes the human respiratory tract asymptomatically; it can persist for months in the nasopharynx until clearance or, under situations such as influenza viral coinfections, disseminate to other niches and cause otitis media, conjunctivitis, pneumonia, meningitis and septicemia1. Pneumococcal diseases cause great morbidity and mortality worldwide, affecting primarily children and the elderly, and accounting for over 1 million annual deaths2,3.

New protein-based vaccine formulations, able to confer serotype-independent protection, have been under investigation to overcome the limitations of the current polysaccharide conjugate vaccines. Among the candidates is pneumococcal surface protein A (PspA), a choline-binding, surface-exposed molecule expressed by most clinically relevant pneumococcal strains. Immunization with PspA fragments including the protein’s N-terminal region protects against experimental colonization, pneumonia and sepsis3,4.

PspA contributes to pneumococcal virulence through different mechanisms. It prevents CRP binding to the bacterial cell wall, thus inhibiting the classical complement pathway activation5. It can also inhibit the formation of the alternative pathway C3 convertase and accelerate C3b decay, resulting in reduced clearance by phagocytosis5–8.

PspA has also been shown to bind to human lactoferrin (hLf)9,10, an innate defense protein produced by epithelial cells, phagocytes and found in several fluids including milk, saliva and tears, that contributes to protection against mucosal infections11. The presence of PspA reduces pneumococcal killing by apo-lactoferrin (Apo hLf), the iron-free form of the molecule, which exerts broad bactericidal effects12. Adding free PspA fragments further limits pneumococcal killing by Apo hLf, while anti-PspA antibodies enhance the bactericidal action of Apo hLf12,13.

The negatively charged and exposed surface of PspA is responsible for binding to the cationic moieties of hLf, thus preventing the protein from reaching the bacterial membrane. Another cationic peptide with bactericidal action is indolicidin, a molecule belonging to the family of cathelicidins, first isolated from bovine neutrophils. It comprises 13 amino acids (ILPWKWPWWPWRR), with a high proportion of tryptophan residues, contributing to its insertion into microbial membranes14. Indolicidin and indolicidin-derived molecules display wide microbicidal effects against bacteria, fungi and viruses, as well as antibiofilm, anticarcinogenic and immunomodulatory roles14–18.

The antimicrobial mechanism of action of indolicidin has not been completely elucidated, although it is known to involve interactions with multiple targets. Due to its amphipathic nature and positive charge, indolicidin can bind negatively charged membranes and cause a temporary disturbance, allowing it to penetrate into the cytoplasm. Once inside the microbial cell, it can interact with proteins and DNA, blocking replication and transcription and leading to cell death19–21. Indolicidin can also bind to LPS on Gram-negative bacteria and act as an anion carrier16. Other antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) of the cathelicidin family have been shown to promote formation of reactive oxygen species within the microbe cells, which impair their metabolic functions and contribute to membrane destabilization leading to cell death22. Due to its potent antimicrobial action and simple structure, indolicidin has been studied as a new class of antimicrobial agent against various microbes16,17,19.

Since indolicidin and lactoferricins share the same cationic nature, we sought to investigate if PspA can also interfere with indolicidin action on pneumococci, as shown for lactoferrin.

Results

Mutant pspA- S. pneumoniae strains display reduced resistance to indolicidin

To evaluate the effect of PspA expression on pneumococcal resistance to indolicidin, wild-type or pspA-negative mutant pneumococci were treated with different concentrations of indolicidin for one hour and plated for viable plate counts. Figure 1 shows the percent survival comparison between wild type and PspA− bacteria. For the serotype 2 bacterium D39 (carrying Family 1 PspA) and its isogenic mutant JY53, while both strains were susceptible to indolicidin, wild-type D39 pneumococci showed an increased survival at all concentrations of the AMP (Fig. 1a). The mutant strain, on the other hand, was highly sensitive to indolicidin, showing a marked reduction in viability at concentrations of 15 µg/ml and higher. The percent survival of the wild-type strain was at least 3 times higher than that of the mutant strain at all concentrations of indolicidin. The type 4 strain, TIGR4 (carrying Family 2 PspA) had a more similar susceptibility pattern when compared with its PspA− counterpart, BR61.1 (Fig. 1b); both strains displayed equivalent resistance to the lower doses of the AMP, but the mutant lacking PspA was significantly more susceptible when a higher concentration of indolicidin, (30 µg/ml) was used. These results suggest that capsule variations influence the impact of PspA on the pneumococcal ability to resist indolicidin.

Effect of CBP removal on pneumococcal resistance to indolicidin

PspA, as well as other choline binding proteins (CBPs), is attached to the bacterium through non-covalent interactions with choline residues present in the cell wall; therefore, it can be removed by washing the bacterium with choline chloride. To determine how CBP removal affects susceptibility to indolicidin, a bactericidal assay was performed in pneumococci after incubation with choline chloride. CBP removal was confirmed as a reduction in anti-PspA binding to the surface of choline washed bacteria (Supplementary Fig. 1). The choline treatment of strain D39 had a less marked, but still significant effect on susceptibility to indolicidin when compared with PspA genetic depletion. The washed bacteria were more sensitive to the AMP at the concentration of 7.5 and 60 µg/ml (Fig. 1c). This indicates that chemical removal of CBPs (including PspA) affects the ability to resist indolicidin in pneumococci, although to a lesser extent when compared to the mutant that does not express PspA. Because pneumococci express other CBPs such as PspC, which could also play a role in the AMP effect, we tested the susceptibility of a PspC mutant strain to indolicidin (Supplementary Fig. 2). The loss of PspC did not affect the bacterium’s resistance to the AMP.

Combined effects of capsule and PspA on indocilicidin action

A pneumococcal strain lacking both capsule and PspA (Rx1 PspA-negative) was used to investigate the combined effects of capsule and PspA on the activity of indolicidin. Rx1 is a natural rough mutant of the D39 strain that does not produce capsule. The double-negative strain was more sensitive to the AMP when compared with Rx1 expressing PspA (Fig. 2a). Similarly, removal of PspA by choline chloride wash rendered Rx1 more sensitive to the AMP (Fig. 2b) than what was observed with D39.

To compare the combined effects of PspA and capsule on bacteria of the same genetic background, the capsule negative derivative of D39 (AM1000) was washed with choline chloride and subjected to treatment with indolicidin (Fig. 2c). Confirming the data from Rx1, the loss of PspA led to a reduced resistance to the AMP. Furthermore, the choline washed mutant strain was significantly more sensitive to indolicidin than each individual mutant (PspA- or capsule -), and the parental strain. Taken together, these results demonstrate that PspA and capsule have an additive effect in protecting the bacterium from AMP attack.

Free recombinant PspA prevents pneumococcal killing by indolicidin

Since mutant strains lacking PspA were more susceptible to killing than the wild-type strains, we sought to investigate if free PspA could affect indolicidin action. The addition of rPspA1 and rPspA4 fragments to the indolicidin solution prior to incubation with the bacteria completely abrogated the effect of the AMP on D39 (Fig. 3a); the rPspA-treated groups showed 100% survival in comparison to 20% in the control group. Interestingly, PspA fragments of both major families (1 and 2, clades 1 and 4, respectively) had a similar protective effect, preventing indolicidin-mediated killing. A similar effect was observed with the PspA-negative strain, JY53; incubation with rPspAs led to a significantly higher survival to killing when compared with the control protein, BSA (Fig. 3b). It is important to notice that, to reach similar levels of killing in the control groups, D39 was treated with 30 µg/ml of indolicidin, while JY53 was treated with 15 µg/ml of the AMP.

Anti-PspA antibodies enhance the bactericidal effect of indolicidin over pneumococci

The effect of anti-PspA antibodies on the action of indolicidin was determined by comparing pneumococcal killing in the presence of specific antibodies or serum from sham-immunized mice. Previous data from our group and others has shown that anti-PspA antibodies can efficiently bind to both strains of pneumococci4,23,24. The bacteria opsonized with anti-PspA were killed more efficiently by indolicidin, an effect that was observed in both serotype 2 and 4 strains (Fig. 4). A similar response was observed with 5 and 10% of serum (not shown).

The control group was incubated with serum from sham-immunized mice. Each column represents the percent survival in comparison to untreated control. PspA binds to indolicidin

In MALDI-MS analysis using sinapinic acid as matrix, the recombinant PspA1 fragment was measured as 50,610 m/z, in agreement with the expected molecular mass. The peptide was measured as 2256.9857 m/z. After the incubation of indolicidin and rPspA1, we could observe two peaks, being one of unbound rPspA1 and a second peak with a mass shift of the protein to 52,674 m/z, corresponding to the mass of the protein bound to the peptide, indicating a chemical interaction between them (Fig. 5).

Discussion

PspA is a crucial component in pneumococcal virulence, contributing to immune evasion during different disease stages25. Its surface exposure allows it to interact with different host components, protecting the bacterium. PspA can bind to CRP and complement proteins to prevent C3b deposition on the pneumococcal surface, limiting complement-mediated opsonization26,27. It can also function as an adhesin, binding to GAPDH on dying lung cells during infection, which contribute to bacterial dissemination in the lungs28.

PspA exhibits some structural and serological variability, being classified in two major families and four more prevalent clades29. Since the N-terminal region includes the domains responsible for PspA activities—which correspond to the most variable regions of the protein—it is important to compare the effect of such sequence variations when investigating PspA’s activities.

One described virulence mechanism for PspA is the ability to prevent the bactericidal activity of apolactoferrin (the iron-free form of human lactoferrin) against pneumococci12. The present study expands the previous data, by demonstrating that PspA can also interfere with indolicidin, another important cationic peptide with broad antimicrobial activity.

Previous studies have shown that indolicidin and synthetic peptides derived from it can reduce pneumococcal viability in vitro30, while pneumococcal capsular polysaccharides may interfere with indolicidin activity31,32. However, the role of PspA on pneumococcal interactions with indolicidin has not been explored.

To determine PspA’s role in the indolicidin action, we compared in vitro killing of wild-type and pspA-negative pneumococcal strains by indolicidin. The protective effect of PspA against indolicidin killing was partially dependent on the pneumococcal strain; the loss of PspA on D39, a serotype 2 strain, had a great impact on the bacterium resistance to the AMP, with the mutant displaying a marked reduction in viability at all concentrations of indolicidin tested. For the type 4 strain, TIGR4, on the other hand, the loss of PspA led to a significant decrease in viability only at a high concentration of indolicidin.

Since the polysaccharide capsules are known to interfere with the bactericidal activity of indolicidin over pneumococci, we hypothesized that the capsule differences are the main factor responsible for the observed variations between the mutant strains derived from D39 and TIGR4. However, other factors including surface proteins and variations in the bacterial envelope may also play a role in this mechanism.

PspA is the major component in a family of proteins anchored to the choline residues on the bacterial cell wall through non-covalent bounds. Choline-chloride wash can remove choline binding proteins. Therefore, we tested if PspA removal by choline-chloride (CC) wash would have a similar impact on bacterial resistance to the AMP as the genetic mutation. For the D39 strain, CC-wash rendered the bacterium more susceptible to indolicidin at lower concentrations, although the effect was much less pronounced when compared with the genetic mutants. Pneumococci also express other CBPs such as PspC, which could affect killing by the AMP. Therefore, we tested if this mutant would have lower resistance to indolicidin; no differences were observed between the mutant and the parental strain, indicating that PspC does not play a role in pneumococcal resistance to indolicidin. A similar result was obtained using lactoferrin12, indicating that the main CBP involved in resistance to AMPs is PspA.

The combined effects of capsule and PspA were tested using Rx1, which does not express capsule, and its isogenic PspA-negative mutant. The double PspA and capsule mutant was more susceptible to indolicidin killing than Rx1, with a similar effect obtained through CBPs removal by CC-wash. Furthermore, CBPs removal of the D39 derived capsule negative strain, AM1000, rendered the bacteria much more susceptible to the AMP. The data suggests that capsule and PspA have an additive contribution to pneumococcal resistance to the AMP.

Next, we investigated if soluble rPspA fragments would interfere with indolicidin’s bactericidal efficacy. Pneumococcal killing by indolicidin was strongly reduced in the presence of free PspA fragments. The demonstration that PspA fragments including the exposed region of the protein, can rescue the bacteria from killing by indolicidin indicates that the N-terminal part of the molecule is responsible for interacting with the AMP. It also demonstrates that PspA does not need to be attached to the bacterial surface to exert its protective effect against indolicidin. Similar results have been obtained when our group and others analyzed the impact of PspA on lactoferrin12,13. Moreover, free rPspA was equally effective at reducing killing of the PspA-negative mutant strain, further suggesting the ability of free PspA to prevent bacterial death by indolicidin.

PspA fragments of families 1 and 2 prevented killing by indolicidin, suggesting that the regions responsible for interacting with the AMP are conserved within PspAs of different families. A previous study has identified PspA fragments that could bind to lactoferricins and prevent their lytic action on pneumococci. One fragment within the clade-defining region of PspA, SM-1 (AAKKAELEKT), demonstrated high efficacy in protecting the bacteria from being killed by lactoferricins12. This fragment is present in both rPspAs used in the current work, with small variations. By contrast, one study found that variations in pneumococcal susceptibility to apolactoferrin were strongly associated with the PspA type expressed by each strain, with little influence of the capsule type33. Interestingly, the ability of a particular PspA to bind to lactoferrin positively correlated with resistance to killing by apolactoferrin.

The ability of rPspAs to block indolicidin also suggests that the two proteins may interact directly. Molecular docking studies have identified PspA as a potential target for indolicidin binding30.

Such binding has also been shown to occur between PspA and lactoferrin. Binding experiments using different recombinant fragments of PspA determined a region in the C-terminal half of the α-helical domain of the protein as the primary binding site for lactoferrin9. In a later study, the N-terminal domain of PspA has been co-crystalized with the N-lobe of lactoferrin, and the specific interaction moieties have been mapped in both molecules34. Both studies demonstrated that the negatively charged surface-exposed helices in PspA are the main responsible for lactoferricin binding.

Pre-opsonization of pneumococci with anti-PspA antibodies enhanced the bactericidal action of indolicidin on pneumococci, further reinforcing PspA’s ability to interfere with indolicidin action. The effect was observed with bacteria of different serotypes, suggesting that antibodies against PspA can limit the protein’s ability to interfere with the bactericidal action of indolicidin regardless of the capsule type. Antibodies against PspAs of two different families exhibited a similar effect at promoting bacterial killing by indolicidin, suggesting that polyclonal mouse antisera against PspA can conceal the protein’s domains that protect the bacterium from indolicidin. This result follows previous studies demonstrating that serum from mice immunized with PspAs promotes killing of pneumococci by lactoferrin12,13.

Based on the increased sensitivity of the pspA-negative strain to indolicidin and the protective role of rPspAs in this mechanism, we hypothesized that the ability of anti-PspA antibodies to enhance the bactericidal activity of indolicidin would involve blocking PspA domains responsible for interacting with the AMP; this would make indolicidin available to penetrate the bacterial membrane, leading to pneumococcal death. A direct interaction between PspA and indolicidin was observed by mass spectrometry, confirming that PspA binds chemically to indolicidin. Although the nature of the PspA-indolicin interaction could not be determined, in silico analysis suggests the presence of hydrogen bonding interaction. This result corroborates our hypothesis and in silico analysis described in the literature, while provides a possible mechanism of PspA mediated inhibition of killing by indolicidin.

One limitation of this study is the absence of complementation mutants for PspA. However, the mutant strains used in this work have been extensively characterized in previous publications, with no evidence of polar effects9,12,27,35. Furthermore, we have confirmed that addition of rPspA increases resistance of the mutant strain to indolicidin and augments resistance in the wild-type bacteria, supporting its role in preventing AMP-mediated death.

Due to its high prevalence and contribution to bacterial virulence, PspA has been evaluated as a vaccine candidate in different infection models, with promising results36. The present data further support the inclusion of PspA in future pneumococcal vaccines, with the induction of antibodies that can enhance pneumococcal clearance by AMPs.

PspA’s ability to bind to different cationic antimicrobial peptides suggests that the protein can prevent the bactericidal effect of different molecules. When considering non-human peptides that may be used as alternative antimicrobial agents, such as indolicidin, PspA’s protective effects may limit the potential therapeutic efficacy of the AMP. Furthermore, indolicidin belongs to the cathelicidin family of antimicrobial peptides, which in humans is represented by LL-37. Thus, although the two molecules share important differences in structure and action, it is possible that PspA could also interact with the human cathelicidin. Thus, the current study provides evidence of an important evasion mechanism employed by pneumococci to prevent killing by cationic antimicrobial peptides.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study demonstrate that PspA protects the bacterium against the antimicrobial effects of indolicidin through a direct interaction with the AMP, which prevents it from reaching the pneumococcal membrane and cause damage. These results provide important insights into the mechanisms responsible for PspA’s role in bacterial virulence and highlight the contribution of innate defenses to pneumococcal clearance. Due to the increasing antibiotic resistance in bacteria worldwide, antimicrobial peptides such as indolicidin have been investigated as alternatives to current therapies with promising results. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms responsible for pneumococcal resistance to AMPs can help develop more effective molecules.

Bacteria and growth conditions

The S. pneumoniae strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. The bacteria were kept as frozen stocks (-80ºC) and, when needed, thawed on ice, plated on blood agar (BA) and cultured in microaerophilic conditions for 18 h. Bacterial colonies were suspended in 5 ml of Todd-Hewitt broth supplemented with 0.5% yeast extract (THY) and cultured until an optical density at 600 nm of 0.3–0.4 (corresponding to around 107 CFU/ml).

Analysis of the bactericidal action of indolicidin on pneumococci

Pneumococcal susceptibility to indocilidin was determined using an in vitro bactericidal assay adapted by our group32. The wild-type and mutant bacteria were grown as described, pelleted and washed twice in sterile PBS by centrifugation at 8,600 x g for 5 min, resuspended in the initial volume of sterile PBS, and incubated with increasing concentrations of indolicidin (ANASPEC), ranging from 7.5 to 60 µg/ml, for one hour at 37 ºC. The control group was incubated with PBS alone. After the incubation, the bacteria were serially diluted, plated on BA and counted after 18 h incubation at 37 ºC. The number of bacteria surviving treatment was compared between wild-type and mutant pneumococci using two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. The difference was considered significant when p ≤ 0.05. Each experiment was conducted in triplicates and repeated to confirm the results.

Recombinant PspAs

The PspA fragments used in this study were cloned from strains St 278—family 1, clade 124 and P490—family 2, clade 44. The amplified gene fragments (including the sequences encoding the N-terminal, alfa-helical domain plus the proline-rich domain of the proteins, supplementary Fig. 1) were cloned into pAE vector; the proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21DE3 and purified through affinity chromatography as described by Converso et al., 2017 4. After purification, the excess LPS was removed from the proteins using the protocol described by Liu et al.44.

Effect of rPspA and anti-PspA antibodies on indolicidin action

To determine if soluble PspA would interfere with indolicidin action on pneumococci, recombinant PspA fragments (rPspA) or BSA (control group) were added to the indolicidin solution 15 min prior to addition of the bacteria, and the bactericidal assay was performed as described. The effect of antibodies on pneumococcal killing was analyzed by pre-opsonizing the bacteria with anti-PspA antibodies for 15 min before adding indolicidin. The serum was obtained from BALB/c mice injected subcutaneously with three doses of 10 µg of rPspA clade 1 or clade 4 using Al(OH)3 as adjuvant and normalized with control sera to achieve similar antibody concentrations in all groups. The control group was treated with serum from mice injected with adjuvant only. The bacterial percent survival was compared among groups using Student’s t-test or ANOVA with Dunnett’s posttest when indicated. Each experiment was conducted in triplicates and repeated once to confirm the results. The animal experiments were approved by the São Francisco University Animal Ethics Committee (protocol number 001.06.2019) and conducted in accordance with the current regulations and ARRIVE guidelines. The animals were kept in appropriate cages with food and water ad libitum, placed in ventilated racks using filtrated air. At the end of the experiments, all animals were euthanized through parenteral injection using a mixture ketamine (300 mg/kg) and xylazine (30 mg/kg).

Effect of choline wash on the bactericidal activity of indolicidin

To test if the removal of choline-binding proteins (including PspA) would affect bacterial sensitivity to indolicidin, wild-type and capsule-negative pneumococci were grown in THY, pelleted by centrifugation and incubated with 2% choline chloride in PBS for 20 min at room temperature12. The control was incubated with PBS only. Next, the samples were washed twice to remove the excess of choline chloride, and the bactericidal assay was performed as previously described. Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test.

Analysis of PspA interaction with indolicidin

The peptide (0.2 uL, 1 mg/mL) was added to rPspA1 (5.0 µL,1 mg/mL) and 5.0 µL of 25 mM ammonium acetate pH 5. The mixture was maintained at 25 ºC for 30 min. After that, 1 µL of peptide-protein solution was mixed with 1 µL of a saturated solution of sinapinic acid diluted in 50% acetonitrile in ultrapure water, containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, and inserted in a plate. After drying at room temperature, the plate was placed in a MALDI mass spectrometer (Axima Performance, Shimadzu, Japan), operating at positive ionization, with a 70 V laser and pulsed extraction of 50 kDa. The spectra were obtained in a range of 30,000 to 60,000 m/z.
