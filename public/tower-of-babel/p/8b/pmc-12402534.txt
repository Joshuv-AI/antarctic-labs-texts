Assessing and predicting crash dynamics with and without road safety measures on the Dejen to Bahir Dar highway in Ethiopia
Authors: Gedefaye Geremew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReducing road traffic accidents and enhancing road safety remain pressing concerns for effective transportation systems worldwide. However, in developing countries like Ethiopia, addressing these issues faces significant challenges. Despite the success of cost-effective safety measures in developed countries, similar strategies are often lacking in Ethiopia. This study aims to assess traffic crash patterns, contributing factors, and the effectiveness of safety measures along the Dejen to Bahir Dar highway in Ethiopia. The primary objective is to identify the key determinants of accident frequency and severity and evaluate the impact of safety interventions using advanced statistical models, including Empirical Bayes. The study finds that several factors significantly influence crash occurrence, including vehicle type, crash type, weather conditions, operational factors, road geometry, and driver demographics such as age, sex, and experience. By analyzing these factors, the paper proposes a set of practical engineering solutions, prioritizing high-risk groups like young and inexperienced drivers and advocating for stricter regulations on high-risk vehicles. Further recommendations focus on improving road safety during adverse weather conditions, enhancing road maintenance on straight sections, and implementing better enforcement of speed limits and driver fatigue regulations. Other proposed interventions include the installation of roadside barriers, new traffic signage, and improved pedestrian facilities. This study contributes valuable insights into the determinants of traffic crash in Ethiopia and offers data-driven recommendations for improving road safety. It also outlines future research avenues, such as improving data quality, conducting spatial analyses of crash hotspots, and exploring the influence of environmental and road conditions through advanced statistical, and Empirical Bayes methods.Keywords: Safety measures, Crash, Crash patterns, Empirical Bayes, Crash severities

Introduction

Road safety is a global concern that demands serious attention. Despite efforts to reduce traffic crash worldwide, statistics from the World Health Organization (WHO) in 20151 reveal a concerning trend: the number of traffic crash has not decreased but has actually increased from 2001 to 2013.

The consequences of road accidents are profound, impacting individuals, property, and society as a whole. According to CARE2, in the European Union alone, more than 40,000 people lose their lives, and over a million others are injured each year due to road accidents. The UK Department for Transport3 highlights the significant economic burden associated with these accidents, which includes direct economic losses, medical costs, vehicle and property damage, and administrative expenses. For example, in Great Britain, the total cost of reported road accidents was estimated to be around £15 billion in 20103. In high-income countries, the economic impact of road accidents can be as high as 2% of their Gross National Product4. As a result, enhancing road safety has become a key priority in transport policy across the globe.

Over the past decade, there has been significant focus on improving safety conditions on two-lane highways. A variety of cost-effective solutions and proactive measures, such as low-cost treatments and roadway safety monitoring programs, have been implemented to enhance traffic safety performance under current conditions. Numerous studies have explored engineering-based countermeasures designed to improve safety outcomes. For instance, Bagdade et al.5 introduced traffic engineering enhancements to improve safety for seniors in Michigan. However, many of these studies have centered on individual improvements, such as widening shoulders or adding passing lanes. Schneider et al.6 and Bauer and Harwood7 developed safety prediction models and assessed safety impacts on rural two-lane highways. Their research highlighted the significant impact of factors like horizontal curves on crash frequency.

Although there is extensive research in this field, most studies have been conducted in developed countries. It’s important to recognize that the outcomes of these studies may not fully apply to developing countries due to differences in traffic management and road configurations. We acknowledge that these differences exist between developed and developing nations. Altwaijri et al.8 suggest that certain factors influencing road safety may have different effects in developing countries. With low- and middle-income countries experiencing a disproportionate number of global road fatalities9, there is an urgent need for research in these regions. However, challenges such as the absence of comprehensive road accident databases present significant barriers to conducting research in developing countries.

Given the differences in factors and implications between developed and developing countries, as well as urban and rural areas, it is crucial to investigate the safety effects of various factors in different contexts. As a result Ethiopia is among developing countries, and its road safety is a pressing issue, particularly on vital transportation corridors like the Dejen to Bahirdar highway. Despite facilitating the movement of goods and people between major cities, this highway has been identified as a hotspot for traffic crash.

This study aims to evaluate road safety and traffic crash patterns on the Dejen to Bahir Dar highway, while predicting future trends with and without safety measures. Specifically, it addresses the following objectives: assessing crash patterns, identifying key contributing factors, and predicting future trends with safety interventions using the Empirical Bayes (EB) method and advanced statistical approaches. By employing these robust methods, this research seeks to contribute to the development of effective strategies for reducing traffic crash and enhancing road safety in Ethiopia.

Traffic flow characteristics in relation to road safety

Numerous foundational studies have examined how traffic volume, flow rates, and congestion levels affect crash rates and severity. Belmont and Forbes10 identified a linear relationship between hourly traffic flow and accident frequency on two-lane roads, showing that accident rates rise proportionally with traffic volume under daylight conditions. Building on this, Gwynn11 observed a U-shaped relationship on four-lane segments, indicating elevated crash risks during both low and high traffic volumes.

Ceder and Livneh12 modeled accident rates using power functions, showing variations by crash type. Later, Ceder12 confirmed a U-shaped relationship between total accident rates and hourly traffic flow under free-flow conditions, suggesting that both light and heavy congestion increase accident risk. However, their use of multi-vehicle crash percentages to define congestion may not capture its multidimensional nature (e.g., travel time reliability or delay duration).

Turner and Thomas13 further found that fatal and severe injuries often occurred in low-traffic early morning conditions, possibly due to higher travel speeds when roads are uncongested.

Peirson et al.14 and Dickerson et al.15 addressed the external costs of accidents, highlighting how increased road usage amplifies exposure and risk-particularly in dense urban areas like London.

Lord et al.16 developed predictive models for rural and urban freeway segments, finding a positive but decelerating relationship between traffic flow and crash frequency. This aligns with Martin17, who stressed that low-volume traffic, especially at night, can lead to high crash severity.

Real-time traffic data has further enhanced predictive modeling. Golob and Recker18 found that crash severity often inversely correlates with traffic volume-more severe crashes tend to occur in lighter traffic. This underscores the value of integrating real-time data in safety modeling.

Wang19, using econometric and GIS tools, revealed that congestion might reduce crash severity but increase the frequency of serious and fatal crashes-highlighting the complex and sometimes contradictory effects of congestion on safety.

Speed is another critical factor influencing crash outcomes. Studies by Aarts and van Schagen20, Elvik et al.21, Nilsson22, and Taylor et al.23 consistently show that higher speeds increase both crash likelihood and severity. Elvik et al.21 validated this through the Power Model, where even minor speed changes significantly alter crash outcomes.

However, the relationship between speed and safety is not without complexity. While Taylor et al.23 supported a positive correlation using rural England data, their methodology-double-counting AADT and potentially misapplying Poisson models over more suitable negative binomial models-raises some limitations.

In contrast, Baruya24 reported a negative relationship between average speed and accident frequency, suggesting geometric design rather than speed may better explain crash patterns-emphasizing the importance of context.

Recent studies by Subasish Das et al.25 and Subasish Das et al.26 employed Bayesian network models to predict crash severity, highlighting speeding, alcohol use, and driver error as dominant risk factors. These findings further validate the importance of targeted interventions.

The work of Nilsson22 and Elvik et al.21 underscores that reduced speeds consistently lead to fewer and less severe crashes, strongly supporting speed management policies-especially in regions with poor enforcement or limited road user discipline.

In summary, the literature largely supports a U-shaped relationship between traffic flow and crash rates. Speed, congestion, and driver behavior are intricately linked to crash dynamics, though their precise effects vary depending on vehicle composition, road geometry, and enforcement-factors especially relevant to the Ethiopian highway context.

Road infrastructure in relation to safety

The physical and geometric characteristics of roads also play a pivotal role in crash dynamics. Improvements in roadway design are widely recognized as effective in reducing both accident frequency and severity.

Lamm et al.27 stressed the importance of aligning design speed with actual operating speed, noting that discrepancies often lead to driver misjudgment and crashes. Shankar et al.28 found that a higher frequency of horizontal curves per kilometer on rural roads correlates with an increased risk of injury crashes. Similarly, Milton and Mannering29 linked higher crash rates to narrow lanes, longer tangent sections, and sharper curves.

However, many of these studies overlook spatial correlation-where crash occurrences on adjacent road segments are not independent. This is a critical methodological issue for corridor-level analysis, such as the Dejen to Bahir Dar highway.

Area-level analyses by Noland and Oh30, using Illinois county data, revealed that wider shoulders tend to reduce crashes, while adding lanes may increase them-likely due to increased lane-change conflict. Kononov et al.31 echoed this, cautioning that multi-lane expansions can introduce additional conflict points if not properly managed.

Interestingly, road curvature does not always increase crash risk. Haynes et al.32, using data from the UK and New Zealand, and Wang et al.33 found that increased curvature in urban settings can lower crash rates by promoting slower, more attentive driving. This challenges the widespread belief that straighter roads are inherently safer-particularly relevant in rural Ethiopia, where long, straight segments may contribute to driver fatigue and speeding.

Despite these insights, the effects of curvature are inconsistent across contexts, influenced by local driving behavior, land use, and data resolution. Thus, applying these findings to Ethiopian highways requires careful calibration and interpretation.

Additionally, infrastructure upgrades-such as shoulder widening, roundabout installation, and pavement resurfacing-have demonstrated effectiveness in enhancing road safety. Navin et al.34 identified these interventions as cost-effective crash mitigation measures. These findings underscore the importance of conducting before-and-after safety evaluations on highways such as the Dejen to Bahir Dar corridor.

Summary of the literature

The reviewed literature offers a comprehensive basis for evaluating and predicting crash dynamics on Ethiopian highways. The evidence highlights the importance of integrated models that combine traffic flow patterns, speed behavior, and geometric design to assess crash likelihood and severity. Crucially, the mixed findings-particularly on speed and curvature-underscore the need for localized studies. This research seeks to address that gap through an in-depth investigation of crash dynamics with and without safety interventions on the Dejen to Bahir Dar highway.

Research methods

This study aims to systematically assess road safety, crash patterns, and crash trends along the Dejen to Bahirdar Highway, with a focus on addressing potential confounding variables, including the relationship between driver demographics (e.g., young, male, and inexperienced drivers) and crash rates. The methodology combines data collection, crash severity analysis, statistical techniques, and safety measure evaluation, with careful attention to confounding factors to ensure accurate conclusions.

Regression analysis

To analyze the relationships between these variables and crash occurrence/severity, a negative binomial regression model was applied. This model is suitable for count data and accounts for overdispersion (where the variance exceeds the mean). The model was designed to isolate the independent effects of each variable, allowing for a clearer understanding of how each factor contributes to crash risk.

Analysis and discussion

The analysis and discussion section of this paper separately evaluates the impact of road infrastructure and traffic conditions on road safety. Rather than addressing “road infrastructure” as a broad category, this study focuses on eight specific aspects of geometric features and roadway characteristics, such as horizontal curve radius and longitudinal gradient. These elements were selected based on their critical role in influencing road traffic crashes. The analysis of road infrastructure is informed by relevant literature, which underscores the significant relationship between geometric features and crash occurrence.

On the other hand, traffic conditions are assessed through traffic volume and crash data, instead of merely considering "road traffic condition." This shift allows for a more precise evaluation of how traffic flow and crash rates contribute to safety outcomes. By examining these factors independently, the study provides a clearer understanding of how each element-roadway geometry and traffic conditions-affects overall safety, leading to more targeted recommendations for infrastructure improvement and traffic management strategies.

Drainage optimization

The road features side ditches with wide rectangular cross-sections and depths ranging from 45 to 60 cm. While these ditches serve to collect water, they become problematic when poorly maintained. During the rainy season, silt and debris accumulate in the ditches, obstructing water flow. This blockage causes water to spill over onto the paved road, creating hazardous flooding conditions that contribute to traffic crashes and accelerate road deterioration. The design and maintenance of these drainage systems are critical to preventing such issues and enhancing road safety during wet conditions.

Sight distance

Sight distance along the road is inadequate in several critical locations, including intersections, mountainous terrain, switchbacks, and sharp curves. In these areas, poor visibility severely impairs a driver’s ability to anticipate potential hazards, leading to increased risks of collisions. Sight distance limitations are particularly problematic in the mountainous sections of the road, where abrupt changes in terrain and curvature restrict visibility for oncoming vehicles, thereby elevating the likelihood of crashes.

Road surface or pavement distress condition

The road surface exhibits extensive distress, including cracking, patching, potholes, surface deformation, and deflection. These surface conditions are particularly hazardous on high-speed sections of the road, where they compromise traction and vehicle control. Poor road conditions are associated with a higher frequency of both single-vehicle and multi-vehicle crashes, often resulting in severe injuries or fatalities.

Roadside hazards

The study area includes various roadside hazards such as trees, utility poles, and other fixed objects. These hazards pose significant threats to driver safety, especially in situations where vehicles run off the road (ROR crashes). Empirical Bayes analysis

Few resources have been devoted to crash evaluation in the literature. In this study, the safety effects of treatments were evaluated using the Empirical Bayes (EB) safety analysis method, based on the severity of traffic crashes. The EB estimation procedure can be either abridged or full. The abridged version relies on crash counts from the most recent 2–3 years and the corresponding average traffic flow during that period. This reflects the widely accepted notion that crash data older than 2–3 years may not accurately represent current conditions.

However, the full EB procedure addresses most of the concerns associated with using older data. It incorporates a longer history of crash counts and traffic flow, which results in a more precise estimate due to the larger dataset. Accordingly, the full EB procedure was employed in this study.

The task of the EB method is to make joint use of two clues to the safety performance of a roadway segment: the observed crash record of that entity and the expected crash frequency at similar roadway entities. This expected frequency is calculated using the Safety Performance Function (SPF). Conclusion

The analysis of road traffic crash data from 2019 to 2023 reveals a concerning upward trend in crashes along the highway segment between Dejen and Bahir Dar. In particular, the number of crashes peaked in 2022, reaching 411 incidents. Although traffic volume slightly decreased in 2023, crash rates remained high, indicating persistent safety concerns along this stretch.

Crash severity analysis shows that fatal crashes represent 44.28% of all recorded incidents, while serious injuries account for 21.14%. Although less frequent, property damage and minor injuries also contribute to the overall crash statistics, underlining the need for comprehensive safety measures.

The Empirical Bayes (EB) analysis, which incorporates both historical crash data and expected crash frequencies from similar segments, provides a more accurate prediction of future incidents. The analysis indicates that, without safety interventions, the projected number of crashes over the next 5 years for the 320 km segment would be approximately 1606, with a mean crash rate of 1.21 ± 0.23 crashes per kilometer per year. However, the implementation of effective safety measures could reduce this to around 816 crashes over the same period, resulting in a mean crash rate of 0.51 crashes per kilometer per year. This highlights a potential reduction of 53.13% in crashes with the adoption of appropriate safety measures.

Regression analysis reveals several critical factors influencing both the frequency and severity of crashes. Male drivers are more frequently involved in crashes compared to female drivers, with the highest crash rates found in the 18–30 and 31–50 age groups. Drivers under 18 and over 51 years do not show significant differences in crash rates, while drivers with unknown ages seem to be involved in fewer crashes, possibly due to data inaccuracies regarding age.

Driver experience also plays a role, with individuals having 1–2, 2–3, and 5–10 years of experience more likely to be involved in crashes. Conversely, drivers with no license or those with more than 10 years of experience show no significant effect on crash rates.

In terms of vehicle types, passenger cars, buses, and truck trailers are more frequently involved in crashes, while lighter vehicles such as light trucks, dump trucks, and bajajs are less implicated. Weather conditions also contribute to crash frequency, with cloudy weather being associated with an increased likelihood of crashes. In contrast, clear, rainy, and snowy conditions do not show similar effects.

Crash type analysis reveals that side and rear-end collisions, along with run-off-road crashes, significantly increase the severity of crashes. Head-on collisions and skidding tend to have less severe outcomes. Additionally, the time of day influences crash severity, with higher severity rates occurring during the early morning (0–4 h), morning to early afternoon (5–8 h), and between 13:00 and 18:00 h.

Crashes on straight roads tend to be more severe than those occurring at intersections, curves, or grades. Road failures and speeding are identified as major contributors to severe crashes, with pedestrian factors and driver fatigue also playing significant roles.

In conclusion, the findings underscore the critical impact of environmental factors (such as cloudy weather), crash types (side and rear-end collisions, and run-off-road crashes), driver demographics (gender, age, and experience), road conditions, and operational factors like speeding on the occurrence and severity of traffic crashes. Implementing safety measures tailored to address these factors along the Dejen to Bahir Dar highway could substantially reduce the number of traffic crashes, ultimately improving road safety in the region.
