Highly efficient CRISPR/Cas9-RNP mediated CaPAD1 editing in protoplasts of three pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) cultivars
Authors: Hanyi Choi, Hyunjae Shin, Chan Yong Kim, Jeongbin Park, Hyeran Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParthenocarpy, characterized by seedless fruit development without pollination or fertilization, offers the advantage of consistent fruit formation, even under challenging conditions such as high temperatures. It can be induced by regulating auxin homeostasis; PAD1 (PARENTAL ADVICE-1) is an inducer of parthenocarpy in Solanaceae plants. However, precise editing of PAD1 is not well studied in peppers. Here, we report a highly efficient clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) ribonucleoprotein (RNP) for CaPAD1 editing in three valuable cultivars of pepper (Capsicum annuum L.): Dempsey, a gene-editable bell pepper; C15, a transformable commercial inbred line; and Younggo 4, a Korean landrace. To achieve the seedless pepper trait under high temperatures caused by unstable climate change, we designed five single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the CaPAD1 gene. We evaluated the in vitro on-target activity of the RNP complexes in three cultivars. Subsequently, we introduced five CRISPR/Cas9-RNP complexes into protoplasts isolated from three pepper leaves and compared indel frequencies and patterns through targeted deep sequencing analyses. We selected two sgRNAs, sgRNA2 and sgRNA5, which had high in vivo target efficiencies for the CaPAD1 gene across the three cultivars and were validated as potential off-targets in their genomes. These findings are expected to be valuable tools for developing new seedless pepper cultivars through precise molecular breeding of recalcitrant crops in response to climate change.

Text

Pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) is a species of the Solanaceae family that is consumed worldwide and contains abundant amounts of vitamins A and C, and carotenoids.1 In addition to being used as food products, peppers are bred for industrial use, particularly as colorants in cosmetics.2,3 Peppers are classified into two categories based on their pungency and fruit shape: sweet and hot. Similar to other plants, peppers are sensitive to temperature,4 experiencing high-temperature stress when the temperature exceeds 34°C5,6 which restricts plant growth and development, leading to decreased fruit production. Parthenocarpy has been proposed as a solution to these limitations because it allows the development of seedless fruits without pollination or fertilization,7 making it a valuable agricultural trait for fruits grown under harsh environmental conditions.8 Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) is a critical phytohormone in parthenocarpy, and its levels in ovaries are regulated by the PAD1 gene, which converts indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPyA) into tryptophan.9 Mutations in PAD1 lead to increased IAA levels and parthenocarpy in the Solanaceae family ovaries, including those of eggplant, tomato, and pepper.8 The Pad-1 mutants were isolated from either spontaneous mutation, RNAi-mediated, or EMS mutation.8 However, precise editing of PAD1 is not well studied in peppers. This study aimed to produce seedless peppers by generating edited mutants of the CaPAD1 gene in three pepper cultivars using the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) system under high temperatures caused by climate change. Therefore, to successfully generate the seedless peppers, we devised and evaluated CRISPR/Cas9 tool for editing the CaPAD1 in peppers.

Three cultivars of peppers were used in the experiments: the sweet pepper ‘Dempsey’ and hot peppers ‘C15’ and ‘Younggo 4.’ As shown in Figure S1, Dempsey is a bell pepper that can be gene-edited via CRISPR/Cas9 and Cpf1 tools10 and its whole-genome sequence is available11; C15 is a transformable commercial inbred line in Korean seed company3; and Younggo 4 is a popular Korean landrace studied for comparative analyses of plastomes.12 The genomic regions of the CaPAD1 gene in the three cultivars were first analyzed via Sanger sequencing to confirm the conserved sequences. The sequencing results revealed conserved sequences of the CaPAD1 gene in the sweet and hot pepper cultivars, as illustrated in Figure 1a. To construct the CRISPR/Cas9 system, we designed five single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the CaPAD1 gene (sgRNA1, 5′-CTTACAGAAACACCTGTTA-3′; sgRNA2, 5′-AGATTCAAGAATTGGTACG–3′; sgRNA3, 5′–AACCTTCAGTTAGTCGCTA–3′; sgRNA4, 5′–CACCATAGCGACTAACTGA–3′; sgRNA5, 5′–AGCTCAGGAAGGCCCTCAT–3′) (Figure 1b) and synthesized them via in vitro transcription. The on-target activity of the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes, each of which consists of one of the five CaPAD1 sgRNAs and a Cas9 was evaluated in vitro in the three cultivars (Figure 1c–e) via restriction fragment length polymorphism. All five sgRNAs cleaved the target CaPAD1 gene into long amplicons (1.3–1.5 kb) and short amplicons (200–400 bp) in the three cultivars, in contrast to the control samples which had only the original template (1.7 kb). Therefore, we confirmed that the five designed sgRNAs efficiently cleaved the target site of CaPAD1 in the three cultivars, Dempsey, C15, and Younggo 4. To test the in vivo on-target activity of the sgRNAs, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated CRISPR-RNP delivery was performed on the three pepper cultivars. The sterilized seeds of each cultivar were grown for 5–6 weeks in a medium consisting of 2.4 g/L Murashige and Skoog medium with MES (Duchefa Co., The Netherlands) and 3% sucrose (Figure 2). Pepper leaves were sliced and agitated in an enzyme solution of Viscozyme, Celluclast, and PectinEX (VCP),10 and 2.0 × 105 protoplasts from each cultivar were transfected with the five CRISPR-Cas9 RNP complexes, followed by incubation for 48 h in the dark at 25°C (Figure 2). The CRISPR/Cas9 RNP delivered protoplasts across three cultivars were all viable and without cellular damages, directly after 0 h and 48 h incubation (Figure 2). Genomic DNAs were extracted from the harvested protoplast samples, and insertion/deletion (indel) frequencies and patterns at the target site were analyzed using targeted deep sequencing. The CRISPR/Cas9 RNP delivery was replicated at least five times for each cultivar, and the average indel frequencies are shown as graphs (Figure 3). The most efficient sgRNAs were sgRNA2, with an average indel frequency of 7.7% in ‘Dempsey’ (Figure 3a), and sgRNA5, with frequencies of 7.2% in ‘C15’ (Figure 3c) and 11.3% in ‘Younggo 4’ (Figure 3e). Conversely, sgRNA3 exhibited the lowest indel frequencies across all cultivars. The indel patterns of targeted Cas9-sgRNA2 complexes varied at the CaPAD1 sgRNA2 locus in ‘Dempsey’ protoplasts, with one nucleotide insertion (T or A) and several nucleotide deletions (−4, − 5, − 3, and − 6) within the first seven top-ranked reads (Figure 3b). The other targeted Cas9-sgRNA5 complexes also showed varied indel patterns, with distinct nucleotide deletions for the CaPAD1 sgRNA5 locus in both ‘C15’ and ‘Younggo 4’ within the first seven top-ranked reads (Figure 3d,f). To validate the specificity of CRISPR/Cas9 RNP-mediated CaPAD1 editing, we surveyed the pepper genomes in silico using the Cas-OFFinder program (http://rgenome.net).13 Initially, to avoid off-target effects, we specifically designed five sgRNAs that did not have two nucleotide mismatches based on the entire homology of the pepper reference genome, with the exception of the target sites, using Cas-Designer (http://rgenome.net).14 In this manner, we identified potential off-target (OT) sites for each cultivars (Figure 4); Dempsey was analyzed with 11 potential OTs for CaPAD1sgRNA2 (Figure 4a), C15 and Younggo 4 were analyzed with 5 potential OTs for CaPAD1sgRNA5 (Figure 4b,c). No Indel mutations were detected at the examined loci compared with the Cas9-only protoplasts from the three cultivars in graphs presented at Log10 scale (Figure 4). Consequently, we identified sgRNA2 for ‘Dempsey’ and sgRNA5 for ‘Younggo 4’ and ‘C15’ as highly efficient sgRNAs for in vivo CRISPR/Cas9 RNP-mediated CaPAD1 gene targeting. Thus, despite the conserved CaPAD1 sequences among the three cultivars, the highly efficient sgRNAs differed between the sweet and hot pepper cultivars. In conclusion, the selection of highly efficient sgRNAs for Cas9 targeting CaPDA1 lays the foundation for producing parthenocarpic peppers through precise molecular breeding of a recalcitrant crop in response to climate change.
