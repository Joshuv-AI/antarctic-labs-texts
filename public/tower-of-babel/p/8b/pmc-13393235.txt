Foliar application of gamma aminobutyric acid regulates sulfur metabolism and restores redox balance to improve aluminum tolerance in Camelina sativa
Authors: Ghulam Murtaza, Zeeshan Ahmed, Najmaldin Ezaldin Hassan, Sajid Ullah, Nazih Y. Rebouh, Mohammed S. Alotaibi, Saqer S. Alotaibi, Sajad Ali, Sabina Jafarzadeh, Rashid Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAluminum toxicity severely limits crop productivity by disrupting nutrient balance, photosynthesis, and cellular redox homeostasis. Gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) is an important signaling molecule involved in plant stress adaptation, but its role in regulating sulfur metabolism and antioxidant defense under aluminum stress remains poorly understood. This study investigated the impact of foliar application of GABA (100 μmol L−1) on aluminum tolerance in Camelina sativa exposed to Al stress (50 mg kg−1 soil Al) using controlled experiments and naturally aluminum-contaminated mining soil. GABA application significantly improved plant performance by increasing plant height, biomass, root growth, photosynthetic pigments, and gas exchange parameters compared with Al-stressed plants. In addition, GABA enhanced antioxidant protection by increasing the activities of ascorbate peroxidase, catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase, while reducing reactive oxygen species accumulation and the malondialdehyde content by improving the redox balance. GABA treatment increased glutathione levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio, whereas the accumulation of oxidized glutathione and dehydroascorbic acid induced by aluminum stress decreased, indicating improved antioxidant recycling capacity. Furthermore, GABA restored sulfur metabolism by regulating cysteine, glucosinolate, glutathione S-transferase activity, and the expression of sulfur-related genes, including ST5a b c, CYP83A1, and BCAT4. Functional inhibitor experiments demonstrated that the protective role of GABA depends on calcium signaling and reactive oxygen species signaling pathways. Transcriptome analysis further revealed extensive regulation of genes involved in antioxidant defense and sulfur metabolism. These findings demonstrate that GABA enhances aluminum tolerance through coordinated regulation of sulfur metabolism, redox homeostasis, and stress signaling, providing new insights into sustainable strategies for improving crop resilience in aluminum-affected soils.

Introduction

Aluminum toxicity represents one of the major constraints limiting crop productivity, particularly in acidic soils where aluminum becomes soluble and enters plant tissues in toxic forms. Excessive aluminum accumulation primarily affects root growth by disrupting cell division, elongation, nutrient acquisition, and root system architecture, thereby restricting water and mineral uptake. 1 In addition, aluminum toxicity disturbs cellular metabolism by impairing photosynthesis, altering membrane stability, and inducing excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, which results in oxidative damage and reduced plant performance. 2 Plants have evolved complex adaptive mechanisms to counter aluminum toxicity, including organic acid secretion, aluminum chelation, vacuolar sequestration, and activation of antioxidant defense systems. Among these responses, maintaining redox homeostasis through the coordinated regulation of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants is essential for protecting cellular components from aluminum-induced oxidative damage. 3

Reactive oxygen species production is a central component of aluminum toxicity because aluminum disrupts electron transport processes, damages cellular membranes, and alters antioxidant capacity. 4 Plants activate antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, peroxidase, and ascorbate peroxidase, to detoxify ROS and maintain the cellular redox balance. In parallel, non-enzymatic antioxidants such as ascorbate and glutathione provide essential buffering capacity through the ascorbate‒glutathione cycle. 5 , 6 However, the efficiency of antioxidant defense depends strongly on nutrient availability and metabolic regulation, particularly sulfur metabolism, because sulfur provides the building blocks for cysteine, glutathione, glucosinolates, and other sulfur-containing compounds involved in detoxification and stress signaling. 7 Therefore, understanding how sulfur metabolism is coordinated with antioxidant defense under aluminum stress remains an important research question.

Camelina sativa is an emerging oilseed crop with potential applications in sustainable agriculture and biofuel production. Compared with many other crop species, Camelina exhibits strong environmental adaptability and can accumulate aluminum, making it a suitable model for investigating aluminum tolerance mechanisms. 8 , 9 The species also contains an active sulfur metabolic network responsible for producing sulfur-rich metabolites, including glucosinolates and glutathione, which contribute to stress adaptation. However, the regulatory mechanisms connecting sulfur allocation, antioxidant defense, and aluminum tolerance in Camelina remain poorly understood.

Gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) is a naturally occurring non-protein amino acid that functions as both a metabolic intermediate and a signaling molecule in plants. Previous studies have demonstrated that GABA accumulation contributes to tolerance against drought, salinity, temperature stress, and heavy metal toxicity by regulating carbon metabolism, ROS homeostasis, and stress-responsive pathways. 10 , 11 Several interconnected mechanisms support the theoretical basis for GABA-mediated regulation of sulfur metabolism under aluminum stress. 12 First, GABA metabolism through the GABA shunt links nitrogen and carbon metabolism by providing succinate to the tricarboxylic acid cycle, thereby supporting the cellular energy supply under stress conditions. 13 Second, GABA signaling can regulate calcium-dependent pathways and ROS signaling, which are important upstream regulators of antioxidant defense and stress adaptation. 14 Third, improved redox conditions may enhance sulfur assimilation by maintaining glutathione recycling and promoting the synthesis of sulfur-containing metabolites. 15 Despite these potential connections, whether GABA reorganizes sulfur metabolism to improve aluminum tolerance remains largely unknown.

Although GABA has shown protective effects when applied externally, the rationale for foliar application under aluminum stress requires consideration. Aluminum toxicity primarily occurs in acidic soils where root growth and nutrient uptake are severely restricted. Foliar application provides a direct route for GABA uptake into leaf tissues, allowing rapid activation of systemic signaling responses without depending on aluminum-damaged root systems. This approach may therefore help overcome limitations associated with impaired root absorption under aluminum stress and enable the investigation of GABA-mediated physiological and molecular responses.

In this study, we investigated the role of foliar-applied GABA in improving aluminum tolerance in C. sativa under controlled aluminum stress and naturally aluminum-contaminated mining soil conditions. We hypothesized that GABA enhances aluminum tolerance by coordinating calcium and ROS signaling pathways with sulfur metabolism and antioxidant defense systems. Specifically, we examined the effects of GABA on plant growth, photosynthetic performance, aluminum accumulation, ROS homeostasis, antioxidant capacity, sulfur-containing metabolites, sulfur-related gene expression, and transcriptomic responses. Furthermore, inhibitor-based experiments were performed to determine the involvement of ROS and calcium signaling pathways in GABA-mediated aluminum tolerance.

Materials and methods

Materials selection and experimental framework

C. sativa seedlings were grown in plastic pots containing an acid-washed sand substrate to minimize aluminum adsorption and provide a chemically defined environment for Al stress evaluation. Sand culture was selected because it allows the controlled availability of Al ions and avoids the strong metal-binding effects associated with organic substrates. The seedlings were maintained under controlled greenhouse conditions with 65% relative humidity, a temperature of 25 °C, a light intensity of 5000 lux, and a 14 h light and 10 h dark photoperiod.

GABA (GABA; Sigma Aldrich, catalog number A2129, USA) stock solution was prepared by dissolving the powder in absolute ethanol and storing it at −20 °C. The working solution was then made by diluting the stock with double-DI water, while keeping the final ethanol concentration below 0.1% v/v. At the three-leaf stage, the seedlings received a uniform foliar spray of 100 μmol L−1 GABA containing 0.05% v/v Tween 20 on both leaf surfaces. The spray was applied at the start of the dark period until the leaves were fully wetted without runoff, using about 3.5 mL of plant−1. Twenty-four hours after the first GABA application, AlCl₃ working solution was applied once to the growth substrate. Each pot contained 100 g of dry substrate. To reach a final Al concentration of 50 mg kg−1 substrate, 5 mL of 1000 mg L−1 Al solution was added uniformly to each pot. The AlCl₃ solution was prepared in ultrapure water and adjusted to pH 4.0 with dilute HCl to maintain Al3+ availability. After application, the substrate was mixed gently to ensure a uniform Al distribution. The controlled substrate was maintained under acidic conditions, and the naturally Al-contaminated soil used for validation had a measured pH of 5.41. The experiment then continued for twenty more days. During this period, the seedlings received foliar GABA treatment every 7 d for a total of three applications. The selected treatment concentrations were based on the preliminary experimental results presented in Tables S1 and S2. An aluminum level of 50 mg kg−1 was selected because it caused clear growth inhibition in C. sativa, while 100 µmol L−1 GABA was identified as the most effective concentration for reducing aluminum toxicity. Based on these results, the experiment included four treatments: (a) control group (0 mg kg−1 Al + 0 µmol L−1 GABA); (b) Al treatment (50 mg kg−1 Al + 0 µmol L−1 GABA); (c) GABA treatment (0 mg kg−1 Al + 100 µmol L−1 GABA; and (d) GABA + Al treatment (50 mg kg−1 Al + 100 µmol L−1 GABA). The 3rd and 4th leaves from the top were collected, frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Each treatment included three biological replicates.

A separate pot trial was carried out with naturally Al-polluted soil taken from the Khushab mining region in Punjab, Pakistan. The soil exhibited pH (5.41), SOM (19.33 g kg−1), CEC (14.11 cmol kg−1), as well as Al levels (16.71 mg kg−1). Two independent experimental systems were used. The controlled experiment was designed to investigate the molecular and physiological mechanisms of GABA-mediated aluminum tolerance under defined aluminum exposure. The natural soil experiment was performed separately to validate the protective effects of GABA under field-relevant aluminum-contaminated conditions. Therefore, the two experiments were analyzed independently and were not directly compared for biomass response. The experiment included 2 groups: an untreated control group named NS-CL and a GABA-treated group named NS-GABA, with forty seedlings in each group.

Integrated analysis of biomass, photosynthetic characteristics, and pigment content in C. sativa

Following Wuyts et al. 16 , harvested crops were separated into shoots, leaves, stems, and roots at the base of the stem. The plant height and primary root length were then measured with a ruler according to the procedure described by Montazeaud et al. 17 The roots and shoots were desiccated at 60 °C until a consistent weight was attained prior to measuring their dry weights. The concentrations of total chlorophyll, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids were evaluated via the methodology of Longjam et al. 18 The net photosynthesis rate (Pn), stomatal conductivity (Gs), intercellular CO2 levels (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr) were assessed between 10:30 and 12:30 utilizing a portable photosynthetic system (LI-COR LI-6400XT).

Analysis of GSH and GSSG contents

GSSG and GSH concentrations were quantified according to the procedures established by Liedschulte et al. 19 A specimen (0.1 g) was extracted with 5% 5-sulfosalicylic acid (1.5 mL) and spun at 11,500 × g at 4 °C for 15 minutes. The supernatant (50 μL) was combined with 5-sulfosalicylic acid, vinyl pyridine, and triethanolamine, followed by incubation for 1 hour at 25 °C. Next, phosphate buffer at pH 7.5, NADPH, 5,5-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid, and glutathione reductase were added to the reaction mixture. After complete mixing, the enzyme reaction was started and allowed to proceed at 25 °C. The absorbance was recorded at 412 nm, and the total glutathione content was calculated from a seven-point standard curve prepared with GSH standards. To measure the GSSG content, 0.4 mL of the collected supernatant, was reacted with 2-vinylpyridine for 1 hour at 25 °C. GSH levels were then obtained by subtracting GSSG from total glutathione.

Analysis of AsA and DHA contents

AsA content was determined according to the method described by Chao et al. 20 A 0.3 g sample was homogenized at low temperature in metaphosphoric acid containing ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid and then centrifuged at 20,500 × g at 4 °C for 5 minutes. The supernatant was utilized for the analysis. P-buffered saline, water, TCA, ferric chloride, 1,2-monodipyridine, and orthophosphoric acid were added to the supernatant (200 µL) and incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C. Subsequently, the specimen was analyzed at 525 nm using a spectrophotometer for the reductive form of AsA. Total AsA was synthesized by using dithiothreitol to decrease DHA. The DHA quantity is equivalent to total ASA minus the quantity of decreased AsA.

Assessment of hydrogen peroxide, superoxide, proline, and malondialdehyde concentrations

The accumulation patterns of O2 − and hydrogen peroxide in leaves were evaluated via nitro blue tetrazolium staining and 3,3′diaminobenzidine, respectively, using the procedure described by Zhang et al. 21 Following the methodology outlined in the prior publication by Junglee et al. 22 , the hydrogen peroxide concentration will be assessed using a spectrophotometric technique after its reaction with KI, with resultant concentration determined via a standard curve. The O2 − content was assessed via the methodology described by Grellet Bournonville and Díaz-Ricci 23 . The sample (0.1 g) was subjected to centrifugation two times in KPi buffer solution at pH 7.7. A total of 0.5 mL of the supernatant was mixed with hydroxylamine hydrochloride and P-buffer solution for 25 minutes. Subsequently, α-naphthylamine and aminobenzenesulfonic acid solutions were added to measure at 530 nm after 20 minutes. Lipid oxidative damage is assessed using TBA reaction, with the MDA content evaluated according to the approach of Du and Bramlage. 24 The proline concentration was assessed using the ninhydrin colorimetric technique. The proline concentration was assessed with a spectrometer at 520 nm, as outlined by Carillo and Gibon. 25

Quantification of aluminum content and its subcellular partitioning

Following Zarcinas et al. 26 , dried plant samples were digested in a nitric acid and perchloric acid mixture at a 3:1 v/v ratio. The Al concentration was then measured with an atomic absorption spectrometer (Thermo Scientific iCE 3500). The translocation factor was calculated as: TF=Alinleaves/Alinroots .

The subcellular localization of Al was analyzed according to the method of Gao et al. 27 In brief, leaf tissue (0.3 g) was homogenized in an extraction mixture containing sucrose (250 mM), dithiothreitol (1.0 mM), and Tris-HCl (50 mM) at pH 7.5. The homogenate was first passed through a 100 µm cell sieve, and the retained material was collected as the cell wall fraction (FI). The filtrate was then centrifuged at 11,500 × g for 25 minutes. After centrifugation, the supernatant, which mainly represented the vacuolar and cytoplasmic fractions, was labeled FIII, while the pellet was considered the organelle fraction, FII. The Al concentration was measured separately in each fraction.

Profiling of antioxidant enzyme activities

0.1 g of frozen leaf tissue was homogenized in phosphate buffer (1 mL) containing polyvinylpyrrolidone and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. The mixture was then spun at 14,500 × g at 4 °C for 15 minutes, and the supernatant was removed for the enzymatic activity assay. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD) activities were evaluated according to the methodology described by Zhang and Kirkham. 28 The glutathione reductase (GR) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activities were evaluated following the methodology outlined by Tsai et al. 29 The protein level was assessed using the methodology outlined by Jones et al. 30 Enzyme activity was normalized based on protein levels, represented as units per milligram of protein.

Quantification of GSL, Cys contents, and glutathione-S-transferase (GST) levels

The activity of GST was assessed via a GST activity test kit (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.; BC0350; China). The leaf specimens were mashed in an ice bath at a tissue mass (g) to extract a volume (mL) ratio of 1:10. One GST activity unit (1 U) is defined as the enzymatic activity that catalyzes the binding of 1 micromole of 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene to GSH min−1 g−1 of tissue at 37 °C. The total GSL was quantified using the palladium chloride technique. 31 Cys content was measured following the technique of Rauser et al. 32 A sample (0.1 g) was mashed in cold perchloric acid (1 mL; 5%) and then spun at 11,500 × g for 8 minutes. After centrifugation, the extract (120 µL) was mixed with acetic acid (120 µL) and ninhydrin reagent (120 µL), which were prepared from ninhydrin (250 mg), acetic acid (6 mL), and hydrochloric acid (4 mL). The mixture was heated for 10 minutes at 95 °C, after which cold ethanol (840 µL; 95%) was added. After cooling, the absorbance was recorded at 560 nm to determine the Cys content.

Functional analysis of exogenous GABA in response to differential inhibitor treatments

Exogenous NADPH oxidase impacts (dibenziodolium chloride; DPI), 33 calcium ion signaling inhibitor N-(6-aminohexyl)-5-chloro-1-naphthalenesulfonamide (W-7), and GABA-responsive aluminum-activated malate transporter (ALMT) on the antagonistic impacts of GABA were examined. Six treatments were established in the order of: (1) INH-CL: control treatment; (2) INH-Al: Al treatment; (3) INH-GABA: administered 50 mg kg−1 Al and spraying with 100 µmol L−1 GABA; (4) INH- GABA-DPI: administered 50 mg kg−1 Al and spraying with 100 µmol L−1 GABA + 1 µmol L−1 diphenyleneiodonium; (5) INH- GABA-W7: administered 50 mg kg−1 Al and spraying with 100 µmol L−1 GABA + 100 µmol L−1 W-7; (6) INH- GABA-ALMT: administered 50 mg kg−1 Al and spraying with 100 µmol L−1 GABA + 10 µmol L−1 ALMT.

Transcriptome sample processing, library preparation, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using a TRIzol kit (catalog number 15596026, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). RNA quality was evaluated with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer System. Samples with an RNA integrity number of at least 6 and an A260 to A280 ratio ranging from 1.8 to 2.2 were selected for library preparation using TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit, following the manufacturer's protocol, and for transcriptome sequencing and data analysis. The proportion of effective bases ranged from 94.73% to 96.37%, while Q30 values across all the samples ranged from 93.59% to 94.73%.

RT qPCR validation of gene expression profiles

To validate the transcriptome sequencing results, sixteen differentially expressed genes were selected for RT qPCR analysis. RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and PCR amplification followed the procedure reported by Singh et al. 34 Gene expression was quantified using an ABI Step One Plus real-time fluorescence PCR system from the USA. Relative gene expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt technique.

Statistical analyses

Experimental data were processed using SPSS Statistics and Microsoft Excel, and figures were prepared with GraphPad Prism. The data were first examined for a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. Differences among treatments were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the least significant difference (LSD) test at p < 0.05 for mean separation. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. Read counts for protein-coding transcripts in each sample were generated using HT-Seq count, following Vaattovaara et al. 35 Differential expression analysis was then carried out with DESeq2 as described by Cheng et al. 36 The nbinom test function was applied to estimate P values and fold changes for group comparisons. Protein-coding genes with a p value less than 0.05 and a fold change greater than two were considered differentially expressed and subsequently used for Gene Ontology and KEGG enrichment analyses, according to Li. 37

Results

Foliar GABA modulates growth performance in C. sativa exposed to aluminum stress

Figure 1a–d shows that GABA alone did not significantly affect plant height, root dry weight, or root length compared with the control, indicating that 100 μmol L−1 GABA had no adverse effect under non-stress conditions. Aluminum stress markedly inhibited seedling growth. Compared with the control, the Al treatment reduced plant height, root dry weight, and root length by 19.28%, 39.81%, and 35.38%, respectively. However, foliar GABA application under Al stress improved plant height, dry weight, and root length by 17.31%, 18.80%, and 29.33%, respectively, compared with Al treatment alone. These results indicate that foliar GABA partially alleviated Al-induced growth inhibition in C. sativa.

Preliminary dose screening showed a concentration-dependent response to GABA under Al stress. The 100 μmol L−1 GABA treatment produced the strongest protective effect, whereas higher concentrations, particularly 200 and 400 μmol L−1, reduced plant height, root length, and biomass. These findings indicate a biphasic response in which moderate GABA application improves Al tolerance, while excessive GABA may impose physiological stress or disturb metabolic balance. Therefore, 100 μmol L−1 GABA was selected for subsequent physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic analyses.

In a separate pot trial using naturally Al-contaminated mining soil, GABA-treated plants showed significantly higher dry weight than that of the untreated control. This confirmed that foliar GABA also improved plant performance under naturally contaminated soil conditions. However, this validation experiment was analyzed independently from the controlled Al treatment experiment because the two systems differed in soil mass, soil chemistry, and experimental purpose.

Modulation of chlorophyll status and photosynthetic responses by foliar GABA in C. sativa under Al stress

In comparison to the control group, the total chlorophyll level exhibited a slight increase after GABA treatment alone; however, the disparity was not statistically significant (Figure 2d). In the Al-stressed C. sativa, the total chlorophyll, carotenoids, chlorophyll a, and b were reduced by 16.29%, 10.11%, 9.03%, and 29.81%, respectively, relative to the control group (Figure 2a–d). In comparison to Al intervention alone, the levels of total chlorophyll, carotenoids, chlorophyll a, and b in the Al and GABA treatment improved by 15.03%, 23.21%, 5.11%, and 43.10%, respectively. The findings combined demonstrated that foliar GABA effectively mitigated the suppression of chlorophyll production induced by Al stress.

The Gs and Pn levels for GABA administration alone were inferior to control, although the difference lacked significance, indicating that GABA exerted minimal influence on plant photosynthesis performance (Figure 2e, f). The readings of Gs and Pn in the Al treatment alone exhibited a significant reduction, decreasing by 60.12% and 45.29%, respectively, in comparison to the control, but they surged by 39.79% and 38.28% following GABA administration, respectively. In comparison to control treatment, the Ci level in the Al intervention group decreased by 39.18% individually and by 12.93% in the combined Al and GABA regimens, respectively (Figure 2g). In Al-stressed plants, Tr significantly increased compared to control, rising by 68.13% in Al treatment individually and by 20.12% in the combined Al and GABA treatment, demonstrating that GABA efficiently mitigated the elevation of Tr (Figure 2h).

Modulation of ROS homeostasis in C. sativa by foliar GABA under aluminum stress

In Al-treated plant leaves, significant dark blue and brown precipitate coloration were seen in Figure S5(a, b), respectively; however, following the GABA treatment, dark blue and brown spots diminished in intensity and size, indicating that GABA may mitigate hydrogen peroxide and O2 − production under Al stress. The concentrations of O2 − and hydrogen peroxide in plant leaves under various treatments (Figure 3a and b) aligned with the findings from tissue localization staining (Figure S5(a, b). In comparison to the control, the levels of hydrogen peroxide, MDA, and O2 − in the roots and leaves exhibited a significant increase after Al treatment, but a marked decrease was observed following GABA administration (except for hydrogen peroxide levels in the leaves). In comparison to Al treatment individually, the combination of Al and GABA treatment markedly reduced the levels of hydrogen peroxide, MDA, and O2 −. Al-treated leaves showed stronger DAB and NBT staining than control leaves, indicating higher accumulation of hydrogen peroxide and superoxide. In contrast, leaves from the GABA plus Al treatment showed weaker staining intensity, suggesting that GABA reduced Al-induced ROS accumulation. Quantitative measurements supported the staining results. Compared with the control, Al stress significantly increased hydrogen peroxide, superoxide, and malondialdehyde levels in roots and leaves. Compared with the Al treatment alone, the GABA plus Al treatment significantly decreased the hydrogen peroxide, superoxide, and malondialdehyde in both the roots and leaves. These findings confirmed that foliar GABA reduced oxidative stress and membrane lipid peroxidation under Al toxicity.

Foliar GABA modulates proline and Al contents in C. sativa exposed to Al stress

Figure 4a, b illustrates that the proline concentrations in the roots and leaves of the control group were inferior to those in the other regimens. Following Al administration, proline levels exhibited a significant increase; relative to control, Al treatment individually and combined with GABA resulted in increases of 58.31% and 69.47% in roots and 50.23% and 64.10% in leaves, respectively. In Al-stressed C. sativa, foliar GABA may increase proline levels by potentially increasing C metabolism and facilitating the synthesis of ornithine or glutamate as precursors for proline biosynthesis. Proline accumulation can significantly diminish membrane lipid peroxidation, hence improving resilience to Al.

Under identical treatment conditions, the Al level in roots exceeded that in C. sativa leaves, with a maximum concentration of 471.31 mg kg−1 observed in Al-treated roots. Furthermore, foliar GABA significantly decreased Al levels in the leaves and roots of Al-stressed C. sativa (Figure 5a). Moreover, in comparison to Al treatment, the TF (the ratio of the component between the aerial and subterranean portions of crops) diminished by 10.67% in the GABA + Al treatment (Figure 4b). Figure 5c illustrates that the administration of GABA under Al treatment modified the subcellular distribution of Al in leaves. In the absence of any treatment other than Al, the distribution trend was FI (55.2%) > FIII (24.1%) > FII (20.9%). In GABA and Al treatments, the distribution trend was FI (47.9%) > FIII (34.9%) > FII (16.9%). The findings demonstrated that foliar GABA facilitated the Al3+ sequestration into vacuoles via compartmentalization, thus mitigating the toxic impact of Al on cells.

Foliar GABA regulates antioxidant enzyme activities in C. sativa exposed to Al stress

Antioxidant enzymatic activities (APX, CAT, POD, and SOD) in the roots and leaves were greatly elevated compared to the control following Al stress (Figure 6). Furthermore, GABA markedly increased these enzymatic activities in comparison to Al treatment individually, hence augmenting ROS scavenging capacity. In comparison to the control, the activities of APX, CAT, POD, and SOD in the leaves increased by 24.97%, 49.29%, 38.11%, and 25.19% under Al treatment individually, and by 39.87%, 99.10%, 83.96%, 60.09% under the Al and GABA treatment. In the roots, these activities surged by 39.98%, 80.25%, 26.93%, and 43.97% under Al treatment individually and by 34.23%, 130.18%, 54.17% and 70.20% under the Al and GABA regimens, respectively.

Foliar GABA modulates the AsA–GSH cycle in C. sativa under aluminum stress

In comparison to the control group, GR, GSSG, GSH, DHA, and AsA levels in C. sativa roots and leaves exhibited no significant alterations following GABA treatment; however, they were greatly influenced after administering GABA to Al-stressed plants (Figure 7). In Al treatment only, AsA levels in C. sativa roots and leaves decreased by 78.13% and 69.21%, respectively, relative to control, whereas they increased by 129.37% and 95.23% in Al and GABA regimens, respectively (Figure 7a, b). GSSG levels in roots and leaves subjected to Al treatment were elevated by 93.19% and 48.28%, respectively, compared to the control group. Following the administration of GABA to Al-stressed C. sativa, reductions of 15.02% and 48.18% were observed compared to Al treatment only (Figure 7c, d). In comparison to the control group, the GSH levels in the roots and leaves subjected to Al treatment were elevated by only 48.13% and 26.08%, respectively. A reduced GSSG level combined with an increased GSH content improved the GSH/GSSG ratio, consequently enhancing the redox buffering ability and elucidating GABA's effectiveness in alleviating oxidative harm. In comparison to Al treatment only, the GSH levels in the roots and leaves were elevated by 56.02% and 42.33% in the GABA and Al treatment (Figure 7e, f). In comparison to the control group, the DHA levels in C. sativa roots and leaves subjected to Al treatment were elevated by only 64.70% and 251.31%, respectively; Compared with Al treatment alone, adding GABA lowered these levels by 20.31% and 47.30% (Figure 7g, h). Figure 7I and j illustrate the varying alterations in GR activity observed in C. sativa roots and leaves under distinct treatments: GABA + Al > Al > GABA > CL. Nevertheless, no significant differences were detected between GABA regimen and CL group. In comparison to the control group, roots and leaves GR activities under Al treatment group increased by only 19.23% and 29.81%, respectively, and climbed by 64.04% and 84.13% in the combined GABA and Al application.

Evaluation of glutathione S-transferase activity and sulfur metabolite assessment

GABA alone did not significantly alter glutathione S-transferase activity, glucosinolate content, or cysteine content compared with those in the control. Aluminum stress significantly reduced the glucosinolate content and glutathione S transferase activity, while cysteine content increased markedly. Under combined GABA and Al treatment, glucosinolate content and glutathione S transferase activity increased compared with Al treatment alone, whereas cysteine accumulation decreased. These results suggest that GABA improved sulfur metabolic utilization under Al stress by promoting the conversion of cysteine toward downstream sulfur-containing defense metabolites and detoxification-related pathways.

Functional evaluation of foliar GABA under distinct inhibitor treatments

The mitigating impact of GABA on Al toxicity in C. sativa, regarding crop dry weight, is affected by several inhibitors (Figure S1). No significant change in biomass traits was observed between INH-GABA-ALMT group and INH-Al group, suggesting that aluminum-activated malate transporters can negate the effects of GABA, suggesting that GABA likely operates through GABA-mediated signaling channels. INH-GABA-DPI group biomass and INH-GABA-W7 group were significantly lower compared to INH-GABA-group, and also lower than those of INH-Al group. These findings suggest that GABA function is contingent upon ROS signaling channel and calcium signaling mechanism.

Transcriptome profiling of C. sativa leaves under aluminum stress

Identification and characterization of differentially expressed transcripts

RNA-Seq analysis identified 2893 differentially expressed genes among the four treatments, including 1951 upregulated and 953 downregulated genes. The highest number of differentially expressed genes was detected in the GABA plus Al versus GABA comparison, with 869 genes, including 620 upregulated and 260 downregulated genes. The lowest number was observed in the GABA versus Al comparison, with 97 differentially expressed genes, including 54 upregulated and 39 downregulated genes. RT-qPCR validation showed expression trends consistent with RNA-Seq results, with an R 2 value of 0.7951, confirming the reliability of the transcriptomic dataset (Figure S2).

Impact of GABA treatment on ROS elimination and sulfur metabolism

KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes were associated with cysteine and glutathione metabolism, methionine metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and glutathione transferase activity (Figures S3 and 9). The reported gene ratio represents the number of differentially expressed genes assigned to a given KEGG pathway divided by the total number of annotated genes in that pathway. The gene ratios for cysteine and glutathione metabolism and methionine metabolism were 0.12 and 0.18, respectively, while the ratios for glutathione metabolism and glutathione transferase activity were 0.07 and 0.08, respectively. These results indicate that sulfur-related metabolic pathways were transcriptionally responsive to Al stress and GABA treatment (Figure S6a).

Genes involved in glucosinolate biosynthesis, including ST5a, ST5b, ST5c, CYP83A1, and BCAT4, were downregulated under Al stress but were upregulated in the GABA plus Al treatment compared with Al treatment alone. Other sulfur metabolism-related enzymes and transporters, including AGXT2, BHMT2, and AK, also showed Al-induced suppression followed by partial recovery after GABA treatment. In contrast, several GST-encoding genes showed lower transcript abundance in the GABA plus Al treatment than in the Al treatment alone, despite the increase in measured GST enzyme activity. Therefore, the GST response should be interpreted cautiously. This discrepancy may reflect differences between transcript abundance and enzyme activity, including the possible effects of isoform specificity, protein stability, substrate availability, or enzyme activation status. However, because protein abundance and post-translational regulation were not directly measured in this study, no firm conclusion can be made about post-transcriptional control. Further proteomic or immunoblot-based validation will be needed to resolve this regulatory layer.

Genes encoding antioxidant enzymes were strongly induced by Al stress and showed further activation after GABA treatment. This transcriptomic response was consistent with the higher activities of antioxidant enzymes measured under GABA plus Al treatment, supporting the role of GABA in strengthening ROS scavenging capacity and redox homeostasis under Al stress (Figure S6b).

Discussion

Al toxicity significantly impaired C. sativa growth, photosynthetic performance, and redox stability. Foliar GABA application alleviated these adverse effects by improving plant height, root growth, biomass accumulation, pigment stability, antioxidant defense, and sulfur-related metabolism. These responses indicate that GABA protects Al-stressed plants through both physiological regulation and metabolic reprogramming. The improvement observed in naturally Al-contaminated soil further supports the practical relevance of foliar GABA, although controlled and natural soil experiments should be interpreted as independent systems because they differed in soil volume, Al chemistry, and experimental purpose. Al toxicity significantly impairs plants' natural growth and productivity and alters the concentrations of carbohydrates, flavonoids, amino acids, and several secondary metabolites linked to resilience against abiotic stresses. 38 , 39 GABA facilitates the maintenance of redox equilibrium in plants under abiotic stress by regulating their antioxidant capacity and photosynthetic rate. 40 Moreover, foliar GABA enhanced C. sativa development and growth under Al stress, and it mitigated toxic metal stress via the modulation of gene expression associated with nitrogen and carbon metabolism, as well as starch‒sucrose metabolic processes in crops. 41 , 42 The growth of plants is intricately linked to numerous metabolic processes. Consequently, we focused on GABA, Al, and S metabolism, oxidative stress, and growth inhibition. Transcriptome analysis provides insight into the function of GABA in modulating plant development and metabolic processes under Al stress.

Foliar GABA alleviates aluminum-induced growth inhibition in C. sativa

Excessive Al in crops might impede growth and hence diminish yields. 43 , 44 This work demonstrated that GABA significantly mitigated the decrease in height, dry weight, and root length of Al-stressed plants (Figures 1 and S4). This aligns with the impact of GABA on soybean seedlings under saline conditions 41 , 45 and on olive growth under salt stress. 46 , 47 Photosynthesis, which is primarily reliant on chlorophyll levels, is the fundamental physiological function that maintains plant-related activities. 48 In our research, the levels of photosynthetic pigments diminished with Al treatment only; however, GABA significantly mitigated the reduction in photosynthesis-related pigment content (Figure 2). Aluminum toxicity impairs photosynthetic pigments primarily via oxidative stress and disruption of nutritional equilibrium. Exposure to aluminum elevates the generation of reactive oxygen species, which compromise chloroplast membranes, destroy chlorophyll molecules, and obstruct enzymes essential for chlorophyll manufacture. 49 , 50 The use of gamma aminobutyric acid mitigates these effects via multiple protective mechanisms. GABA functions as a metabolic regulator and signaling chemical that amplifies the antioxidant defense mechanism of plants. It enhances the activity of enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase, which eliminate reactive oxygen species and safeguard chloroplast structures from oxidative damage. 51 , 52 GABA aids in osmotic regulation and stabilizes cellular membranes, thereby preserving the integrity of chloroplasts and pigment‒protein complexes. Furthermore, GABA is involved in carbon and nitrogen metabolism via the GABA shunt pathway, which provides intermediates to the tricarboxylic acid cycle and facilitates energy production during stressful circumstances. 53 , 54 This metabolic support enhances cellular homeostasis and enables the plant to sustain chlorophyll production and pigment stability despite aluminum stress. A further significant element pertains to the control of stress-responsive gene expression. GABA signaling can initiate pathways linked to stress resilience and photosynthetic safeguarding, resulting in enhanced chlorophyll production and reduced pigment degradation. GABA significantly alleviates the aluminum-induced reduction in photosynthetic pigments by preserving redox equilibrium, safeguarding chloroplast membranes, and sustaining metabolic activity. The synergistic protective effect elucidates why GABA-treated plants exhibited elevated pigment levels in contrast to those subjected only to aluminum stress.

The present research reveals a significant context-dependent impact of GABA. Although GABA individually did not strongly affect biomass relative to the control, it alleviated Al-induced growth suppression (Figures 1 and S4). This phenomenon can be elucidated via the concept of “stress-priming,” which is a maintained adaptive mechanism whereby external stimuli activate a dormant state of preparedness in plants, facilitating swifter and more efficient reactions to subsequent stressors without triggering metabolic expenses under ideal conditions. This effect aligns with “hormesis,” when small doses of chemicals provoke adaptive reactions that improve resilience to recurrent stress, although not producing overt phenotypic alterations.

GABA-mediated modulation of sulfur metabolism in C. sativa exposed to aluminum stress

GABA is believed to mitigate metal toxicity by improving sulfur assimilation and uptake, as well as elevating the concentrations of thiols and Cys. 55 , 56 Cys acts as a pivotal element in S metabolism, facilitating conversion into significant sulfur-rich secondary metabolites, including GSH and GSL. 57 Furthermore, GSL is a significant step in S metabolism and is posited to be crucial for metal resistance in crops. Metal transport and uptake are intricately linked to the total S content in plant tissues, and glucosinolates may mitigate their toxic effects by modulating S metabolism and secondary metabolic pathways. 58 , 59

Under Al stress, foliar GABA increased GST activity in C. sativa, whereas several GST-encoding transcripts showed lower expression under the combined GABA plus Al treatment than under Al treatment alone. This difference indicates that GST activity and GST transcript abundance were not fully synchronized in the present dataset. Therefore, the increase in GST activity should not be interpreted as direct evidence of post-transcriptional regulation. A more cautious explanation is that total GST activity may reflect the combined contribution of specific GST isoforms, enzyme activation status, substrate availability, protein stability, and changes in the intracellular redox environment. Because protein abundance, isoform-specific GST activity, and post-translational modifications were not measured, future proteomic, immunoblot, and enzyme isoform studies are needed to clarify the regulatory basis of this response. Even with this limitation, the simultaneous increase in GSH level and GST activity suggested that GABA improved the detoxification capacity of Al-stressed plants by strengthening glutathione-dependent antioxidant and conjugation processes. The recovery of sulfur metabolism under GABA treatment also requires consideration of the bioenergetic cost. Reductive sulfur assimilation is energy-demanding because sulfate activation, reduction, cysteine synthesis, glutathione biosynthesis, and glucosinolate production require ATP, reducing power, carbon skeletons, and nitrogen-containing precursors. Therefore, the GABA response cannot be explained only by the transcriptional activation of sulfur-related genes. GABA metabolism through the GABA shunt may provide a metabolic link by converting GABA into succinate, which enters the tricarboxylic acid cycle and supports mitochondrial energy production. This route may help sustain ATP and reduce the equivalents needed for antioxidant regeneration, glutathione turnover, and sulfur-containing metabolite synthesis during Al stress. Thus, GABA may support Al tolerance through a dual function, acting as a signaling molecule that activates stress responses and as a metabolic input that helps maintain the energy supply required for sulfur-based detoxification. Prior research has established that GSL could serve as a sulfur storage system during both sulfur dearth and sufficient supply; additionally, plants meticulously govern thiol compound and GSL metabolism to ensure adequate GSH for antioxidant needs, PCs, and metallothioneins to sustain metal homeostasis. 60 GABA facilitates sulfur absorption through SUT1/2, whereas C. sativa induces GSL production genes to modulate sulfur distribution. GSH is generated utilizing GSL as the S donor, and GSH, crop-chelating peptides, and GSL are intricately connected regarding metallic ion equilibrium in crops. 61 Under Al stress, GABA enhanced GST levels in C. sativa (Figure 8c), but GST-encoding genes expression exhibited a general decline in the presence of both GABA and Al relative to Al treatment individually (Figure 9b). This suggested that the increase in GST function prompted by GABA intervention was facilitated by alternative post-transcriptional regulatory processes.

In brief, GABA treatment is correlated with enhanced gene expression linked with GSH, resulting in enhanced plant GSH levels and GST activity; these alterations may mitigate Al toxicity. Pandey and Gautam 62 discovered that plants initiated the sulfur absorption pathway via elevating the transcript concentrations of pertinent genes, thus improving GSH production in reaction to Al stress. All these studies indicate that Al stress disrupts sulfur metabolism. In contrast, GABA treatment enhanced the sulfur level in C. sativa, potentially functioning as a modulator by increasing the amount of sulfur-rich metabolites produced under Al stress, thereby preserving the equilibrium between S metabolism and metallic ion equilibrium in crops.

Our data indicate that GABA upregulates GSL synthesis genes (ST5a-b-c, CYP83A1, and BCAT4) and increases GSH concentrations (Figures 7e, f); nevertheless, the exact distribution of S flux across these competing processes necessitates additional measurement. Future research should utilize 34S-isotope mapping in conjunction with quantitative flux modeling to measure S reallocation dynamics, which was recently suggested for crop sulfur pathways. 63 This method could clarify how GABA optimizes sulfur resource allocation under Al stress. In this study, MYB28 was not differentially expressed, although several of its downstream glucosinolate pathway targets responded strongly to GABA under Al stress. This result indicates that GABA may influence glucosinolate metabolism through MYB28-independent regulation, through other transcriptional regulators, or through transient MYB28 activity that was not captured at the selected sampling time. Since this study used one transcriptome sampling stage, the absence of differential MYB28 expression should be interpreted cautiously. Time-course transcriptome analysis will be needed to determine whether MYB28 participates in the early regulation of GABA-responsive sulfur metabolism under Al stress. In our investigation, Ca2+ signaling suppression by W-7 negated the protective impact of GABA (Figure S1), thus corroborating the involvement of Ca2+ in GABA-induced Al resistance. Although GABA restored the expression of several glucosinolate biosynthesis-related genes, including ST5a, ST5b, ST5c, CYP83A1, and BCAT4, the present study did not examine epigenetic regulation. Therefore, the observed transcriptional recovery is more appropriately interpreted as a GABA-associated adjustment of sulfur metabolism and redox signaling rather than evidence for methylation-dependent control. Future studies using DNA methylation profiling, chromatin accessibility assays, and promoter activity analysis will be needed to test whether epigenetic regulation contributes to this response.

Oxidative stress responses of C. sativa to exogenous GABA under aluminum stress

Excess reactive oxygen species induce peroxidation of cellular membrane lipids, disrupting redox equilibrium 64 , 65 ; nonetheless, it is crucial to recognize that reactive oxygen species also serve as essential signaling molecules. 66 GABA efficacy was contingent upon reactive oxygen species signaling, as evidenced by the suppression of GABA effectiveness through DPI (Figure S1). Shoaib et al. 67 indicated that GABA significantly mitigated Al-caused oxidative stress, as evidenced by a decrease in the accumulation of ROS and MDA levels. This analysis demonstrated that O2 − and hydrogen peroxide levels remain at minimal concentrations in the control, whereas Al treatment led to a marked rise in reactive oxygen species buildup (Figure 3c–f). A prior investigation demonstrated that green leaves from Al-stressed crops, when stained with NBT and DAB, exhibited pronounced spots, and Al resulted in a significant elevation in O2 and hydrogen peroxide levels. 68 Significantly, GABA treatment diminished the buildup of excessive O2 − and hydrogen peroxide induced by Al stress (Figures 3c–f). Comparable results were noted in Pb-stressed sunflower. 69 The MDA level, a particular result of membrane peroxidation, is a valid indication of oxidative harm severity in crops. 70 , 71 The MDA levels in the roots and leaves of Al-treated C. sativa seedlings greatly increased (Figure 3g–h). This finding aligns with the results of lead-treated soybean. 72 Consequently, we posited that Al stress impairs the biofilm system, whereas foliar GABA diminishes MDA levels under Al stress, thus mitigating cellular harm and augmenting plant resilience to abiotic stressors. The current research indicates that the MDA levels in C. sativa roots and leaves, following GABA application, were reduced compared to those subjected only to Al treatment.

Antioxidant enzymatic activities are generally heightened in plants under Al stress, therefore safeguarding them from damage. 73 Our findings corroborated a prior investigation indicating that Al stress enhanced APX, GR, CAT, POD, and SOD activities in roots and leaves, implying that Al stress induces metabolic disturbances and alters the equilibrium of antioxidant enzymes. 74 At the level of transcription, GABA orchestrates both non-enzymatic and enzymatic antioxidant mechanisms. Transcriptome results validate overexpression of APX, CAT, POD, and SOD genes (Figure 9b), which correlates with increased enzyme activity (Figure 6). Post-GABA treatment, antioxidant enzymatic activities and the ROS-scavenging capacity in Al-stressed C. sativa were markedly elevated, likely because GABA functions as a messenger molecule that activates defense mechanisms, including antioxidant systems, thereby mitigating oxidative stress.

The AsA–GSH cycle, a crucial antioxidant framework in crops, efficiently scavenges reactive oxygen species. In AsA–GSH cycle, AsA and GSH serve as reactive oxygen species scavengers and may chelate metal within cells. 75 , 76 Our data indicate that foliar GABA enhances resilience to Al stress by elevating the levels of GSH and AsA in C. sativa roots and leaves (Figure 6a, b, e, and f). What caused the elevation of AsA levels in Al-stressed C. sativa seedlings? The likely reason is that Al stress induces elevated APX activity, facilitating AsA regeneration; alternatively, Al stress generates substantial DHA, which is transformed to AsA, with GABA increasing both processes and thus augmenting the antioxidant properties. In this study, the GSH levels were heightened under Al stress, and GABA treatment greatly augmented the GSH concentration (Figure 6). This was ascribed to increased GR activity (Figure 7), which converts GSSG to GSH, maintains the intracellular redox equilibrium, and mitigates ROS-induced damage. In summary, GABA alleviated oxidative damage in Al-stressed plants by modulating antioxidant enzyme activity and the non-enzymatic protective system.

Limited studies have demonstrated that GABA engages with phytohormones to orchestrate plant defense mechanisms against abiotic stressors. 77 , 78 The present research lacks obvious proof for GABA-hormone interaction; however, the observed correlations suggest that the GABA-induced modulation of redox balance and sulfur metabolism in C. sativa under Al stress likely folds into a more extensive hormonal system. Further assessment of foliar hormones and the use of hormone-dependent inhibitors will elucidate their role in GABA-induced Al resistance.

Potential of GABA-assisted phytoremediation in aluminum-contaminated soils

The present study shows that GABA can improve C. sativa performance under controlled Al stress and naturally Al-contaminated soil. These findings suggest the potential value for using GABA as a supportive treatment in Al-affected soils. However, the study was conducted under moderate Al exposure, and its application to severely contaminated sites require further validation. High Al concentrations may exceed plant detoxification capacity by saturating chelation systems, damaging root function, and causing irreversible oxidative injury. The extensive implementation of GABA offers potential economic advantages in agriculture by increasing crop productivity, enhancing quality, and decreasing the reliance on chemical fertilizers. 79 , 80 Nonetheless, its economic feasibility presently lacks clear quantitative cost-effectiveness assessments for extensive manufacturing operations. Environmentally, GABA is widely regarded as an ecologically safe chemical with no harmful effects. Consequently, despite the extensive potential applications of GABA, additional research on its ongoing ecological impacts and the durability of its field applicability is essential. This work revealed the effectiveness of GABA in mitigating Al toxicity in C. sativa under mild Al stress, although its relevance to severely polluted soils requires cautious evaluation. Elevated Al concentrations can exceed the detoxifying capabilities of plants via saturating metal-binding ligands and causing irreversible oxidative harm. 81 , 82 Nonetheless, GABA involvement in S metabolism indicates possible solutions for high-Al conditions. Although the combined application of GABA with sulfur nutrition may represent a promising strategy for strengthening sulfur-dependent detoxification, this approach has not yet been fully validated in C. sativa under Al-contaminated soil conditions. Therefore, future studies should test GABA together with sulfate or other sulfur sources under different Al levels to determine whether this combined strategy can enhance sulfur assimilation, glutathione production, metal detoxification, and plant establishment in contaminated soils.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that foliar application of GABA improves aluminum tolerance in C. sativa by coordinating sulfur metabolism, antioxidant defense, and redox homeostasis. Under moderate Al stress, GABA enhanced plant growth, restored photosynthetic performance, reduced Al accumulation and translocation, and promoted Al compartmentalization. GABA also reduced ROS accumulation and membrane lipid peroxidation by increasing antioxidant enzyme activities and strengthening the AsA–GSH cycle. In addition, GABA regulated sulfur-related metabolites and restored the expression of key glucosinolate biosynthesis-associated genes, including ST5a, ST5b, ST5c, CYP83A1, and BCAT4, suggesting that sulfur metabolism contributes to GABA-mediated Al tolerance in C. sativa. However, this response should be interpreted within the limits of the present species-specific study, and no conclusion can be drawn about whether this mechanism is absent or conserved in non-cruciferous species. Overall, GABA appears to support Al tolerance through a combined mechanism involving sulfur-dependent detoxification, glutathione-based redox buffering, antioxidant activation, and improved cellular protection. Further validation under different Al levels, soil conditions, and plant species will help determine the broader relevance of this mechanism for phytoremediation and crop stress management.
