Associations between housing tenure and unscheduled hospital use in people of working age with multiple physical and mental long-term conditions: a retrospective cohort study using linked data from London
Authors: Preveina Mahadevan, Helen A. Blake, Jenny Shand, Manuel Gomes, Jessica Sheringham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with physical and mental long-term conditions typically experience high levels of hospital use. Housing tenure – the arrangements under which individuals occupy their home – has been shown to be associated with the co-occurrence of physical and mental long-term conditions. However, the association between housing tenure and unplanned hospital use is not established. This study investigates the longitudinal relationship between housing tenure and unplanned hospital admissions among individuals of working age with co-occurring physical and mental long-term conditions.MethodsA longitudinal retrospective cohort study was undertaken, utilising the Care City Cohort, a dataset comprising healthcare, social care and wider determinants variables for residents in North East London. Two-part regression models with multiple imputation were used to estimate the odds of experiencing an unscheduled hospital admission and the associated length of stay among individuals with co-occurring physical and mental long-term conditions, by housing tenure. The models were adjusted for socio-demographic characteristics, health-related behaviours, and the number of physical and mental long-term conditions.ResultsAnalysis was conducted on the records of 45,399 individuals diagnosed with physical and mental long-term conditions before April 2016. The most common tenure was owner-occupied was 48%, followed by social renting (27.1%) and privately rented housing (24.9%). After adjusting for sociodemographic variables and health vulnerabilities, the odds of a non-elective admission were higher for social housing residents (OR 1.40; 95%CI: 1.21–1.61) than owner-occupied. There was no evidence of different lengths of stay between tenures.ConclusionsThis study highlights housing tenure as an important determinant of unscheduled hospital admissions amongst working age adults with a combination of physical and mental long-term conditions. Housing tenure status may be a useful indicator for local services to target secondary prevention strategies to reduce unscheduled hospital use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15071-3.

Background

The public health burden of multiple long-term conditions is well recognised and growing. Individuals with multiple long-term conditions experience unplanned hospitalisations nearly five times more frequently and have longer lengths of hospital stays than people with one long-term condition [1–4]. The co-occurrence of a physical and a mental long-term condition (PMLTCs) has increased globally [5–8]. People with this combination of conditions have particularly high use of unscheduled hospital care. In addition to the burden on patients, these outcomes add considerable costs to the health and care system [2, 9].

Household-level markers of socioeconomic circumstances, often overlooked in multiple long-term condition studies, are associated with prevalence [10]. In particular, housing tenure – a household’s relationship with their home – can be highly correlated with prevalence [10]. In England, owner-occupation is the most common tenure (65% of all households), followed by private rented (19%) and socially rented (16%). London has a distinct tenure profile with almost double the proportion of private renters to the rest of the country (32% vs. 19%) [11].

Housing tenure is influenced by sociodemographic and economic circumstances. It is also highly correlated with housing quality [12], which can affect health [10, 13–18]. Social housing renters have lower well-being scores than other tenure types [19]. However, private renters may have the least agency to affect their living conditions. For example, compared with owner-occupiers or social renters, private renters have the highest proportion of non-decent dwellings, dampness and lower average energy efficiency rating [11].

Few studies, however, have examined associations between housing tenure and unscheduled hospital use amongst individuals with PMLTCs [3, 20, 21]. Although some research has focused on PMLTCs in older populations, there are increasing calls to focus on younger populations in PMLTCs research, where distinct issues arise for working-age populations in accessing healthcare compared with adults above the statutory retirement age [16].

The objective of this paper is to examine the association between household tenure and unplanned hospital use amongst people of working age with PMLTCs. Our first hypothesis was that individuals with PMLTCs living in social housing are more likely to be admitted to hospital as unscheduled admissions than individuals who own their home. We hypothesised also that length of stay would be longer for private renters (who would be less able to alter their home environment), than owner occupiers.

Methods

Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using a linked dataset of healthcare and wider determinants records from residents living in the borough of Barking & Dagenham in North East London.

Data

The Care City Cohort dataset comprises linked administrative healthcare and wider determinants data collected by London Borough of Barking and Dagenham (LBBD), health providers, and commissioners [22]. Details on how dataset was created and its composition can be found in Shand et al. 2020 [22]. The dataset comprised patients living in the borough of Barking and Dagenham, a socioeconomically disadvantaged borough of North East London, with approximately 210,000 residents and a younger and more ethnically diverse population than England [23]. The dataset was ordered chronologically by patient identifier and individuals with duplicate records were removed by taking the first record of each duplicate.

Exposure, covariates, and outcomes data were collected from 2017 to 2019, as these years were the latest available data from the Care City Cohort database and better reflective of the cohort in real time.

Cohort

The study cohort was individuals aged between 15 and 64 years old [19], diagnosed with PMLTCs before 1st April 2016. A flowchart for how the cohort was constructed via data linkages, is given in Supplementary Fig. 1. The definition of PMLTCs was adapted from the Academy of Medical Sciences definition [24]. Individuals were included in the cohort if they had a diagnosis recorded in primary care data for both a physical conditions and common mental health condition (for a full list of eligible conditions, please see Supplementary Table 1, file 2). Physical long-term conditions were quantified using an unweighted count of physical health conditions (0, 1, 2, 3, or ≥ 4). Mental health conditions consisted of common mental illnesses defined by National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidance [25]. The presence of any common mental health condition was included as a separate binary variable (≥ 1 or 0) because some individuals were diagnosed with more than one condition. We opted to group depression and anxiety diagnoses as one binary variable in part for simplicity and in part because of evidence that patients can experience significant flux between depression and anxiety diagnoses [26].

Exposure

The exposure was household tenure, grouped into three categories: owner-occupied, privately rented, and social housing. Data on tenure was extracted from LBBD property records from 2017. The reference group was owner-occupied housing tenure. Individuals were classified as owner-occupied if living in a house that was owned outright or with a mortgage. Individuals in social housing properties were identified from the council’s social housing data system. Individuals in privately-rented properties were identified from the council’s private rental licensing system. Residents renting privately but in affordable, subsidised rental properties were grouped with social renters. Ten individuals classified as having unknown tenure were excluded from the cohort.

Outcomes

Unscheduled hospital use was defined as: the number of non-elective admissions between 2017 and 2019 and the length of stay of non-elective admissions. In England’s National Health Service (NHS), a non-elective admission is defined as a hospital admission that is not planned in advance and requires an overnight stay of at least one day [27]. Patients were admitted through emergency departments or via direct urgent referrals from healthcare professionals. Data on non-elective admissions originated from the NHS secondary uses database and were linked to the study cohort using NHS number. The length of stay was measured by the difference in days between the patient’s hospital admission and discharge.

Covariates

Based on the previously published related literature [12, 18, 31–33] and data availability, variables that may affect tenure decisions and hospitalisation were included as covariates adjusted models:

Analysis

All analysis was conducted using R [28]. A descriptive analysis was conducted to summarise the characteristics of the cohort and on the number of individuals who had at least one non-elective admission in a particular year, as a percentage of the total number of individuals with PMLTCs for each covariate. The analysis was conducted on individuals with complete data on physical and mental health conditions.

A two-part model [29] using the twopartm package in R software was used to investigate the association between housing tenure and non-elective admissions and length of stay (LoS). This approach is recommended for the analysis of healthcare utilisation because a high proportion of individuals who do not experience an admission (i.e. have zero utilisation) [29]. Two-part models recognise that individuals with zero healthcare utilisation (non-users) are systematically different from health service users. The approach involves first estimating the probability of having a non-zero outcome (in this case a non-elective hospital admission) via probit or logit and subsequently modelling the outcome of interest (hospital length-of-stay in this study), conditional on having a non-zero outcome via a generalised linear model. A substantial proportion of people had zero hospital length of stay and, by definition, zero hospital admissions.

To assess the association between housing tenure and unscheduled hospital stay, two models were fitted. For transparency, an unadjusted model with no covariates was first fitted. Second, a model was fitted adjusted for sociodemographic variables associated with both household tenure, PMLTCs and healthcare utilisation that were available in the dataset. These covariates were age, gender, ethnicity, IMD 2019, calendar year, BMI, smoking status, recorded alcohol intake, the number of diagnosed physical and mental long-term conditions and household occupancy. Multiple imputation was conducted on variables with missing data, including BMI, ethnicity, and smoking status.

The number of imputations was based on the percentage of incomplete records [30]. In this dataset, around 30% of the records had at least one missing value hence 30 imputations were conducted. Other variables that may affect the relationship between housing tenure and hospital visits were used as auxiliary variables to estimate imputations. Rubin’s rules were used to pool results across imputed datasets. Monte Carlo errors for all pooled coefficients were less than 10% of their corresponding standard errors (Supplementary Table 4, file 2) as recommended by White et al. 2011 [30]. This provides corroborative evidence that our findings would remain even if the MI process was repeated.

Sensitivity Analysis

A complete case analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the findings derived from the main analysis. The underlying assumption for this analysis was that any differences between individuals with missing and observed data were explained by those covariates included in the model.

Involvement of lived experience perspectives

Early study plans were shared Care City Community Board, comprising Barking & Dagenham residents, patients, carers and staff from the voluntary sector, care and health agencies, for review and to obtain approval to access the data. Interim findings were shared with the Community Board to inform the interpretation of results.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Analysis was conducted on the records of 45,399 distinct individuals and 29,767 households with diagnosed PMLTCs before April 2016. The most common tenure in Care City was owner-occupied (48.5%), followed by social housing (28.7%) and private rental (22.9%). The demographic and health characteristics of individuals varied by tenure. There was highest prevalence of three or over long-term conditions in social housing (19.1%) and lowest in private rentals (11.3%). The highest proportion of residents living on their own was found in social housing (12.3%) compared with 7.5% in owner occupied housing.

Out of the total cohort, 2263 (5.0%) individuals had at least one non-elective admission by 2019. (Table 1), with social renters having the highest proportion of patients that had non-elective admissions (6.5%) and the lowest proportion in owner-occupied households (4.2%). Average lengths of stay were 4 days overall with longer durations for privately-renting residents (4.3 days) and social housing residents (4.1 days), and shorter lengths of stay for owner-occupying residents (3.7 days).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

In this retrospective cohort study of working age adults with physical and mental health conditions in East London, residents of social rental sectors had higher odds of unscheduled hospital admissions than individuals who owned their homes. Associations remained after adjusting for health vulnerabilities and socio-demographic characteristics. There was no evidence for longer lengths of stay amongst social housing residents than other tenures after adjusting for other variables.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study lies in the comprehensiveness of the Care City Cohort, which enables data linkage to the local authority dataset and allows for the exploration of tenure data at the household level. The database was granular enough to allow us to examine deprivation in urban settings using social housing data and investigate its impact on healthcare utilisation and the associations between social determinants with healthcare use and outcomes. Additionally, the study incorporates insights from lived experiences, enhancing its applicability to practical, day-to-day settings, which helped to contextualise experiences of living with multiple conditions and possible influences of household tenure on healthcare use.

The study was conducted in one borough of East London, with a distinct ethnicity and socioeconomic profile. Therefore, these findings may not be generalisable to other areas of the UK or other countries, e.g. where there are different drivers of hospital use or where eligibility for social housing is different.

Other limitations include the potential for residual confounding because the dataset did not include potentially relevant information such as resident occupation and housing conditions. We acknowledge the potential for clustering within households, and taking this into account, would increase the precision of the results [31]. It was outside the scope of this study to examine this, but it is a potentially relevant consideration for future work. It was also out of scope to examine cause of hospitalisation, which might have indicated if exacerbations of current physical or mental health conditions were the cause of admissions.

Multiple imputation was conducted to account for missing data. Ethnicity had the highest level of missing data (28%). In the UK, housing tenure is strongly associated with ethnicity, with higher proportions of minority ethnic groups living in social housing than white English groups, so more complete ethnicity data would be important to examine potential effect modification of the relationship between tenure and healthcare use [11].

Possible explanations for our findings

Many studies aim to explain higher unscheduled hospital admissions by examining associations with socioeconomic disadvantage and deprivation [32–34]. Housing tenure may be acting as a proxy for socioeconomic disadvantage, which is consistently associated with higher unscheduled healthcare use [35–37]. While it is not straightforward to compare findings from different studies, the strength of association between unscheduled admissions and housing tenure in this study was larger than area-level deprivation indices (e.g. Bankart et al. reported an increased rate of admissions of 1.02; 95% CI 1.01–1.02) by Index of Multiple Deprivation) [38]. The strength of association found in our study (AOR 1.40; 95% CI 1.21–1.61) was also similar to the risk of frequent hospital admissions reported by Low et al. for individuals living in public rental housing versus other tenures (AOR 1.30; 95% CI 1.13–1.50) [36].

Murphy and Grundy (2024) conceptualise housing tenure as a proxy for socioeconomic position, and – in common with our analysis – find it is more strongly associated with poor health than other socioeconomic markers, particularly in the UK context [39]. We explore some of the potential explanations proposed by Murphy and Grundy to explore why tenure was associated with emergency hospital admissions.

Firstly, there is the possibility of a selection effect. Poor health is one reason for eligibility for social housing, so people in this tenure are already more likely to be in poor health. Whilst we included variables for additional comorbidities and health behaviours, we did not have data on severity of conditions. Relatedly, they propose that health behaviours by tenure may be relevant. Other studies have reported stark inequalities in smoking and quitting rates by tenure, with residents in social housing having over two times the odds of smoking as owner occupiers [40]. However, in common with the analysis of multiple long term condition prevalence [10], we found associations remained after controlling for health behaviours.

Secondly, it is possible that poorer housing conditions, more often found in privately and socially rented than owner-occupied tenures, increase the risk of ill health, necessitating an emergency admission. Although social housing is more likely to meet decent home standards than privately rented homes [41], individuals living in social housing are still more likely to face adverse housing conditions than owner-occupiers [42]. There is evidence that poor living conditions such as overcrowding increase the risk of dampness and mould and can cause respiratory problems [43–45]. Previous research indicates that poor housing conditions like overcrowding can exacerbate pre-existing conditions [46].

A further explanation proposed by lived experience resident contributors was that working individuals with multiple conditions could be motivated to seek unscheduled healthcare differently than those not in employment to maintain their working lives. Working-age adults renting privately are more likely to be in employment than social housing tenants [42]. They also face higher rents and higher risk of eviction than social housing tenants. They may, therefore, avoid using emergency care whenever possible and when in hospital, feel an urgency to leave as soon as possible to return to work to prevent loss of income. This may explain why we did not find longer lengths of stay in private renters after adjusting for other factors, even though private renters have the least agency to adjust their home environment to accommodate changing needs following severe illness after a hospital stay [47, 48]. We did not have the data to explore these potential explanations but we recommend they are considered in future studies.

Implications for research and practice

This study underlines the importance of household tenure in the UK as a determinant of healthcare use, amongst working age adults with long term conditions. Public services (including English integrated care systems) seeking to alleviate strain on healthcare services could target secondary prevention activities using household tenure information. Housing tenure information is widely available at a household level in many settings including the UK. Record linkage can be used to access this information reducing the needs for bespoke data collection and reducing the burden on health care workers. To inform such prevention activities, further studies are needed to estimate the cost implications of increased unscheduled healthcare utilisation and advance our understanding of the pathways by which housing tenure may influence healthcare utilisation amongst people with multiple long-term conditions.
