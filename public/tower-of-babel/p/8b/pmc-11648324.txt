A Comparison of In-Person and Telemedicine Triage in Otolaryngology
Authors: Jacob E Hoerter, Peter M Debbaneh, Kalena Liu, Swapnil Shah, Miranda Weintraub, Nancy Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe integration of virtual visits has been met with skepticism by many surgical specialties, including otolaryngology, due to the lack of a complete physical exam. Analysis of differences in the workup between patients triaged virtually or in-person is warranted.MethodsA chart review was performed for a cohort of adults undergoing septoplasty (January 2021–May 2022). Groups (telemedicine, in-person) were compared by 2-sample t-test and chi-square test to determine the difference in the number of preoperative visits and to assess the variation in patients with preoperative laboratory testing, imaging, or referrals.ResultsOf 338 patients, initial evaluation was in-person for 225 (66.5%) and via telemedicine for 113 (33.5%). The groups were similar in demographics (mean age 39.1 years for telemedicine vs 38.8 years for in-person, female 28.9% vs male 37.7%, P = 0.088). The telemedicine group had a significantly higher number of preoperative visits (3.03) compared to the in-person group (2.38, P = 0.001). There was no significant difference in patients who underwent preoperative laboratory testing, imaging, or referrals. Patients triaged via telemedicine experienced a shorter time to surgery compared to those triaged in person (434 vs 208, P = 0.003).DiscussionIn this cohort, triage by telemedicine allowed otolaryngology patients to have an expedited path to surgery despite having more visits. There is no evidence to suggest that otolaryngologists had an overreliance on diagnostic modalities when triaging by telemedicine.ConclusionAmong patients undergoing septoplasty, those initially evaluated by telemedicine were more likely to have more preoperative visits and shorter time to surgery than those evaluated in person. Telemedicine can serve as an effective method for triaging surgical patients without excess diagnostics.

Introduction

The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in an overhaul of many health care practices as efforts were made to minimize the use of personal protective equipment and maximize social distancing of patients and clinicians alike. As such, the medical community was thrust into practicing telehealth and utilizing multimedia to maintain health education and clinical practice.1 While telemedicine, the practice of medical care through multimedia or digital conduits, has been long practiced in a moderate capacity among primary care and medical specialties, many surgical specialties have deferred its use due to the presumed reliance on physical examination and procedures.2 Beginning in March of 2020, otolaryngology surgeons and many others began a temporary transition to performing the majority of outpatient visits on a virtual platform.3

There has been resistance to adopting telemedicine in otolaryngology largely due to practitioner preferences. Many studies have surveyed practitioner attitudes toward virtual visits in the discipline, revealing reservations toward their implementation, chiefly citing the ability to perform a complete examination and connect with patients.4–7 While concerns over the lack of a physical examination remain, many practitioners surveyed after participating in telemedicine endorse its validity in triaging new patients and performing routine follow-up visits that do not necessitate a physical examination, such as discussing laboratory results and medications.4,6,8 Concerns over billing and reimbursement have provided another barrier to implementing telemedicine. Historically, virtual visits were not reimbursed by Medicaid and Medicare; however, in response to the complex nature of the public health emergency initiated for the COVID-19 pandemic, telemedicine was reimbursable to the same degree as an in-person outpatient visit, allowing further widespread application of telemedicine across multiple specialties.2

In the post-pandemic world, the role of telemedicine in many specialties is in question—particularly otolaryngology. Many surveys have demonstrated that patients and clinicians have found utility for virtual visits in their practices and health care.4–9 Within otolaryngology, many subspecialties, such as facial plastics, have found that a large portion of patients prefer virtual visits going forward, while otology patients were least likely to elect for telemedicine.3 However, the absence of a complete physical examination introduces the possibility of overreliance on other diagnostic tests, which could lead to overutilization of other aspects of the health care system and excess cost or even diagnostic delay. The authors sought to identify if such disparities exist in otolaryngology by utilizing a cohort of patients undergoing nasal septoplasty, a common procedure in otolaryngology, comparing those who had their initial evaluation via telemedicine to those who were first evaluated in person.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective review of all otolaryngology surgical procedures from January 2021 to May 2022 was performed. Nasal septoplasty was among the leading adult surgical procedures performed and was selected for analysis. All patients undergoing nasal septoplasty at 6 surgical centers (Oakland, Richmond, Fremont, San Leandro, Walnut Creek, and Vallejo) were included. Demographic data were collected and analyzed including patient sex, self-reported race, primary residence, age at initial consultation, and surgical center. Patients were placed into either telemedicine (telephone or video) or in-person groups based on their initial visit with the Department of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery. The type of appointment was selected by the patient based on their preference of the three available options. Interpreters were made available to any patient who required them irrespective of visit type and utilized accordingly. The primary outcomes assessed were the total number of visits required to recommend septoplasty and the time interval from first visit to surgery. Secondary outcomes investigated included the laboratory examination orders, preoperative imaging requests, medication prescriptions, and referrals to other departments.

Demographic variables for telemedicine and in-person groups were compared using t-tests and chi-square tests. Two-sample t-tests were performed to determine the difference in the number of preoperative visits and the time from first visit to initial examination. Chi-square analyses were performed to determine differences in the proportion of patients who were ordered labs, imaging, medications, and ancillary referrals. Values were considered significant at P < 0.05. Patients were further subdivided into the 6 main surgical centers for subgroup analysis. The above methods were reviewed and deemed exempt by the institutional review board.

Results

A total of 338 participants (Table 1) from 6 surgical centers were reviewed. The mean number of preoperative visits was 2.6 for the entire cohort, and mean time to surgery was 365.4 days (Table 2). Almost all (99.7%) of patients were trialed on medications prior to surgery, and 82% had laboratory studies ordered. Only 29.6% of patients had preoperative imaging, and 16.9% were referred to one of the listed subspecialties (Table 3). There were 225 patients (65%) triaged by in-person visit, whereas 113 patients (33.5%) underwent telemedicine (82 by video and 31 by telephone).

The 2 groups were similar in demographics (age 39.1 ± 15.1 years for in-person visit vs 38.8 ± 15.2 years for telehealth, female 28.9% vs male 37.7%). The telemedicine group had a statistically significant higher number of preoperative visits (3.03; 95% CI = 2.73–3.33) compared to in-person (2.38; 95% CI = 2.15–2.62, P = 0.0014; Table 4). However, the telemedicine group also showed a significantly shorter interval time between the first visit and surgery: 208.05 days (95% CI = 189.69–267.26) compared to the in-person group at 434.12 days (95% CI = 342.08–526.15, P = 0.0025). There was no statistically significant difference in patients who underwent laboratory testing (77.9% vs 84%, P = 0.167), preoperative imaging (31.9% in the telemedicine group compared to 28.4% in the in-person group, P = 0.518), medication prescriptions (100.0% vs 99.1%, P = 0.158), or referrals (21.2% vs 14.7%, P = 0.128; Table 3).

Discussion

This study identifies a large cohort of patients undergoing a common otolaryngologic procedure. Pertinently, patients undergoing septoplasty initially evaluated by telemedicine or in person were remarkably similar in demographics and experienced comparable workup in laboratory tests, imaging studies, and medications. Despite this, the telemedicine group had a mean increase of 0.65 preoperative visits and a mean decrease of 226 days to surgery when compared to the in-person group, which was statistically significant. These data suggest the potential in improving preoperative care, access, and time to surgery.

Preoperative visits

This study identified that those patients undergoing triage by telemedicine were more likely to have more preoperative visits. Given the convenience telemedicine appointments are to patients with busy schedules or transportation challenges, it is possible that telemedicine expedites access to care and yields shorter and more frequent appointments. Implementation of a standardized telehealth scheduling process in other surgical settings has shown similar success with patient access to health care. General surgery patients undergoing telemedicine had improved access to postoperative follow-up care in 95% of eligible patients by reducing travel needs and increasing appointment availability, enabling consistent follow-up.10 Furthermore, no-show and cancellation rates for telemedicine have been found to be lower than traditional in-person visits, highlighting improvements in both accessibility and clinical efficiency in the postoperative period.11,12

In contrast, patients evaluated by telehealth may lack the reassurance provided by a physical exam. In response, they may seek further evaluation more aggressively in search of validating their symptoms and disease. This need for reassurance could account for the increase in visits from this study's virtual cohort as does the clinician’s utilization of the physical examination to diagnose nasal septal deviation. Concerns over telemedicine leading to an increase in overall clinical visit volume are not unfounded as other studies have shown that, while virtual visits more than doubled from 2008 to 2013, in-person visits remained constant.13

Time to surgery

Despite the increase in number of visits performed before surgical intervention, patients initially evaluated by telemedicine were far more likely to have shorter time to surgery. The time interval between workup and elective surgery is often dependent on surgical backlog and patient urgency. This suggests that, for the eager patient, telemedicine provides an avenue by which access to surgery can be expedited.

Diagnostic workup

While the authors hypothesized that laboratory tests, imaging studies, medication prescriptions, and ancillary referrals would be increased in the telemedicine group, this study identified no significant difference. Nasal septal deviation was readily diagnosed in both the in-person and telemedicine groups with minor differences in laboratory tests, imaging studies, and referrals, none of which were statistically significant. The comparability in workup between the groups suggests no increased reliance on these adjuncts in the absence of a physical examination at triage.

The future of telemedicine in otolaryngology

Telemedicine innovation in otolaryngology predates the COVID-19 pandemic in cases such as telemedicine-assisted neurotology service in post-Katrina Louisiana and many others.14 However, these were widely regarded as conduits of care during crises rather than the standard of care. Instruments of the virtual examination will continue to improve in the coming years as they have already in the immediate pandemic period.15 Early attitudes during the COVID-19 pandemic suggested that telemedicine would be transient in otolaryngology, but both clinician and patient attitudes have suggested permanent integration into the field. In the short term, attitudes to telemedicine in otolaryngology are shifting. Both patients and clinicians alike have attested that telemedicine is adequate if not superior when compared to their attitudes pre-pandemic,16,17 with satisfaction scores ranking comparable to and exceeding those of in-person visits.18

Despite the excitement and expanding utilization of telemedicine, apprehension to the ubiquitous utilization of telemedicine remains. Surveyed otolaryngologists have shared concerns about the limitations of technology such as connectivity and security with patient information,19 as well as trepidation about reimbursement,18 although additional assessments have demonstrated potential increases in revenue for billable visits.10 Clinical outcomes of telemedicine have been promising but not without issue, as some analyses have demonstrated a higher rate of unnecessary tympanostomy tube placement in children.20 While concerns about marginalizing elderly patients11 and those with poor technological access are prudent, evidence exists that the elderly and those lacking technological prowess are not deterred from utilizing telemedicine.21,22 These findings are supported by the present study, which identified no significant differences in age, residence, or Area Deprivation Index between groups.

Strengths

The study population was large and part of an integrated health care system. As such, patient charts were complete, with all preoperative orders, visits, and time to surgery across the entire California East Bay region. To the knowledge of the authors, to date there have been no other published analyses of differences in clinician decision-making and access to surgery for otolaryngology patients in telemedicine.

Limitations

This study was subject to a few limitations as it was a retrospective analysis of only those patients who underwent surgical correction of their septal deviation. Patient hesitancy and scheduling conflicts were not assessed in the time intervals, which could have contributed to delay. Furthermore, there are likely many factors contributing to the timing and decision to pursue surgery that cannot be evaluated on retrospective chart analysis, such as possible decreased interest in elective surgery during the early COVID pandemic.

The modality of initial triage was at the discretion of the patient, which does introduce the possibility of selection bias. However, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were used to minimize the possibility of comorbid medical conditions confounding the analysis. Furthermore, demographic characteristics including age, sex, and residence were not found to differ significantly.

Conclusion

Telehealth is valuable in modern medicine and otolaryngology and a clinical inevitability. In this study, there were no observed differences in unnecessary diagnostic testing, treatment, or surgical delay in the telemedicine group. Providing patients with telemedicine can improve access to care and timely surgical intervention without exposing them to unnecessary laboratory tests, imaging studies, or ancillary referrals. Future studies are warranted to assess the impact of telemedicine on patient satisfaction, outcomes, and other procedures.
