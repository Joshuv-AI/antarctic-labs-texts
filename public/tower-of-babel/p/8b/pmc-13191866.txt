The impact of robot-assisted training on gait, balance, and endurance in patients with partial spinal cord injuries: insights from rehabilitation
Authors: Magdalena Prończuk, Dariusz Skalski, Kinga Łosinska, Jarosław Markowski, Jan Pilch, Michał Toborek, Adam Maszczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study evaluated the effectiveness of robotic-assisted gait training in improving gait function, balance, and endurance in individuals with chronic spinal cord injury (SCI). It compared robotic-assisted therapy with conventional rehabilitation and examined the impact of training frequency.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted at specialized SCI rehabilitation centers. Seventy-five participants aged 19–40 with chronic partial SCI American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grades B, C, or D were enrolled in this randomized controlled trial. A pre-registered athletic subgroup consisting of 45 individuals with intensive competitive sports backgrounds (≥ 4 sessions/week pre-injury) was designated for subgroup analysis. The remaining 30 participants were physically active non-athletes (2–3 sessions/week pre-injury). Participants were divided into three groups: L1 (robotic-assisted gait training once weekly), L2 (robotic-assisted gait training twice weekly), and C (conventional rehabilitation). Functional outcomes were assessed using the 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT), 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury II (WISCI II), Lower Extremity Motor Score (LEMS), Timed Up and Go Test (TUG), and stabilometry before and after the intervention.ResultsThe L2 group demonstrated the greatest improvements: TUG time decreased by 7.7 ± 3.0 s (17.2 ± 2.5 s PRE vs. 9.5 ± 1.8 s POST; −44.8%, η² = 0.18), and 6MWT distance increased by 43.5 ± 16.2 m (35.5 ± 4.8 m PRE vs. 79 ± 14 m POST; +122.5%, η² = 0.25). These absolute improvements substantially exceed established MCID thresholds and were accompanied by clinically meaningful gains in quality of life. Robotic-assisted therapy, particularly at higher training frequencies, significantly outperformed conventional rehabilitation, especially in AIS D patients.ConclusionsRobotic-assisted gait training effectively improves gait function, endurance, and balance, with enhanced benefits observed at higher training frequencies. Higher training frequency demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements across all participants. While improvements were observed across the cohort, future adequately powered trials are needed to systematically investigate whether athlete-specific training histories influence responsiveness to different Lokomat frequencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01977-w.

Introduction

Effectiveness of robotics in rehabilitation

Spinal cord injuries (SCI) are among the most complex and demanding clinical conditions, resulting in both physical and psychological consequences as well as severe functional limitations. Traditional rehabilitation methods include manual therapy, physical exercises, kinesiotherapy, and electrostimulation. While widely used, these approaches often yield limited efficacy, particularly in patients with severe neurological deficits.

Conventional approaches may be constrained by limited volumes of high-quality, task-specific stepping, therapist fatigue and variability, and challenges in maintaining progressive speed, body-weight support, and symmetrical kinematics; in contrast, robotic systems deliver consistent, high-repetition, task-specific practice with tunable assistance that can better target locomotor mechanisms [1–4].

Robotic-assisted gait training (RAGT), such as the Lokomat system, has emerged as a promising intervention for enhancing locomotor recovery in individuals with incomplete SCI. These systems facilitate repetitive, task-specific movements and may promote use-dependent neuroplasticity (experience-dependent neural reorganization) and functional improvements [5–6]. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses (2020–2024) generally support benefits of RAGT for walking ability and activities of daily living, with mixed findings for speed/distance that appear device- and protocol-dependent [1–2]. A network meta-analysis further indicates that wearable overground exoskeletons can outperform treadmill-based systems such as Lokomat on certain speed outcomes, underscoring the importance of device selection and dosing [7]. Wearable overground exoskeletons (e.g., EksoNR/EksoGT, ReWalk, Indego, HAL) provide powered hip and knee actuation and require patient-initiated steps with crutch or walker support over ground; assistance can be adjusted in real time to promote active participation. Compared with treadmill-based systems, overground devices typically permit multidirectional practice and may induce higher cognitive and cardiorespiratory loads, potentially enhancing transfer to unconstrained walking [2, 7–8]. Moreover, emerging assist-as-needed/adaptive controllers in Lokomat-type devices seek to optimize active participation and motor output during training [9–10]. Collectively, these updates align the present work with the evolving evidence base.

Frequency of robotic rehabilitation: past research and limitations

The frequency of RAGT sessions is a critical factor influencing rehabilitation outcomes. While some studies suggest that higher-frequency training may yield better functional improvements, there is a lack of consensus on the optimal dosing [3, 6]. For instance, Nam et al. [6] reported improvements in walking function with RAGT but did not specifically compare different training frequencies. Similarly, Field-Fote and Roach [3] observed gains in walking speed and distance but did not focus on frequency variations. More recent syntheses acknowledge dose as a putative moderator yet conclude that the optimal frequency remains uncertain and likely context-dependent [1–2]. This gap in the literature underscores the need for studies directly comparing different RAGT frequencies to establish evidence-based guidelines.

Gaps in robotic rehabilitation and training frequency research

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting RAGT, several gaps remain. Notably, while most prior studies such as those by Nam et al. and Field-Fote & Roach have focused on general or subacute SCI populations, the question of whether patient heterogeneity, such as differences in pre-injury physical conditioning, neurological severity, or training background, influences responsiveness to RAGT frequency remains underexplored. Athletes or physically active individuals may respond differently to rehabilitation interventions due to their pre-injury neuromuscular conditioning and higher baseline physical performance [11–12]; however, systematic investigation of such individual differences requires adequately powered comparative designs. Understanding how these patient characteristics interact with training frequency could inform personalized rehabilitation approaches, yet current evidence remains insufficient to draw definitive conclusions about athlete-specific responsiveness to RAGT. This gap underscores the need for future, specifically designed trials that compare trained versus untrained populations under controlled frequency protocols.

From a neurophysiological perspective, such individuals could possess a relative “plasticity reserve” (neural efficiency, motor learning history) that might modify responsiveness to task-specific gait practice, especially under higher training frequencies; however, this has not been systematically tested.

This study investigates robotic-assisted gait training frequency effects in a well-characterized cohort of individuals with chronic, incomplete SCI. We stratified analyses primarily by neurological severity (AIS classification: B, C, D) to align with established prognostic indicators of rehabilitation responsiveness. While the cohort includes individuals with varied pre-injury physical activity levels, including some with intensive sports backgrounds (n = 45), the primary analyses examine training-frequency effects across the entire sample. Pre-injury activity level and athlete-versus-non-athlete comparisons were not pre-specified as primary analytical comparisons; exploratory subgroup analyses focused on AIS classification, not athletic status. Any discussion of potential athlete-specific responses remains speculative and hypothesis-generating, reserved for future adequately powered work.

Aim and hypotheses

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of robotic-assisted gait training frequency (once versus twice weekly) on gait function, balance, endurance, and quality of life in individuals with chronic incomplete SCI. Specifically, it examined whether higher-frequency RAGT yields superior functional outcomes compared to lower-frequency RAGT and conventional rehabilitation, with stratification by neurological severity (AIS classification). We hypothesized that participants undergoing twice-weekly Lokomat sessions would exhibit significantly greater improvements in gait speed (10MWT), walking endurance (6MWT), balance (TUG, stabilometry), and lower-limb motor strength (LEMS) compared to those receiving once-weekly training or conventional therapy. Pre-specified outcomes were selected to index locomotor capacity, dynamic balance, and functional mobility, domains critical for safe ambulation and daily living independence. Exploratory subgroup analyses examined whether neurological classification (AIS B, C, D) influenced treatment response patterns.

Methods

Trial design and CONSORT compliance

The study was prospectively registered in the OSF Registries platform using the OSF Preregistration format (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/4BX6W). The preregistration includes clearly stated hypotheses, justification for sample size, data collection procedures, and a comprehensive statistical analysis plan.

The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the of the Jerzy Kukuczka Academy of Physical Education in Katowice, Poland (Resolution No. 9/2012, including annex and decision KB/47/2022), in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

All study procedures adhered to the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for transparent reporting (http://www.consort-statement.org/). A schematic flowchart of participant enrollment, randomization, intervention allocation, follow-up, and data analysis is presented in Fig. 1 (25/25/25 participants per group completed the 12-week follow-up; overall attrition = 0%).

This randomized controlled trial represents the primary analysis of the full enrolled cohort (n = 75). A parallel, pre-registered subgroup analysis focusing specifically on athletes with intensive pre-injury training histories (n = 45) has been published separately [13]. That secondary analysis concentrated on spasticity, pain, and muscle strength outcomes in the athletic subpopulation. The present work includes the entire randomized sample, encompassing both athletes (n = 45) and physically active non-athletes (n = 30), and extends the outcome domain to comprehensive gait, balance, endurance, and quality-of-life measures. No participants were excluded between the two analyses; the difference in sample sizes reflects the analytical focus and pre-registered stratification, rather than participant attrition or post-hoc exclusion criteria. All participants underwent identical screening, randomization, and intervention protocols.

Participants

This study enrolled 75 individuals aged between 19 and 40 years, all diagnosed with chronic partial spinal cord injury (SCI). Neurological impairment was classified according to the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS), with inclusion limited to participants falling within the following categories: AIS B, in which sensory function is preserved below the neurological level but no motor function is present; AIS C, characterized by the preservation of some motor function below the neurological level, though more than half of key muscles exhibit a muscle grade less than 3; and AIS D, where motor function is preserved below the neurological level and at least half of the key muscles score a grade of 3 or higher. Here, grade 3 denotes active movement against gravity on the MRC scale; “grade ≥ 3 in at least half of key muscles” defines AIS D. AIS distribution in the enrolled cohort was: B = 30, C = 36, D = 9.

Of the recruited cohort, 60% were male and 40% were female, with a mean duration since injury of 18 months. All 75 participants met a minimum pre-injury physical activity threshold of at least two sessions per week to ensure safe tolerance of harnessed stepping and reduce floor effects during gait practice. Within this cohort, a substantial proportion (45 individuals; 60%) had a history of intensive pre-injury sports involvement, defined as regular, structured athletic training (football, gymnastics) occurring four to six times per week for a minimum of one year. This athletic subgroup was pre-specified during trial registration for a dedicated subgroup analysis examining neuroplasticity reserve and sport-specific recovery trajectories, which has been published separately [13]. The remaining 30 participants (40%) were physically active non-athletes, maintaining pre-injury physical activity levels of 2–3 sessions per week. No participants were excluded from either the primary or subgroup analyses; stratification by activity level reflects the analytical design and pre-registered hypotheses, not post-hoc exclusion criteria. Participant recruitment was conducted at the MODERN-REH Neurorehabilitation and Dysfunction of Movement Center in Mysłowice, Poland.

We applied a minimum pre-injury physical activity threshold of ≥ 2 sessions per week based on both safety considerations and scientific rationale. First, from a safety perspective, harnessed robotic gait training on the Lokomat requires participants to tolerate sustained body-weight support, progressive increases in treadmill speed, and active lower-limb movement for 30 min per session. Individuals with minimal pre-injury physical conditioning or very limited functional baseline may experience excessive physiological demand, fall risk, or discomfort during early sessions, potentially compromising safety or trial retention. The ≥ 2 sessions/week threshold represents a conservative safety criterion, akin to a minimal baseline fitness requirement for participation in intensive rehabilitation. Second, from a scientific homogeneity perspective, this threshold reduced confounding related to baseline deconditioning and heterogeneity in neuromuscular reserve. By enrolling participants with documented, consistent pre-injury physical activity, we created a more homogeneous cohort regarding baseline motor function, proprioceptive integrity, and responsiveness to task-specific training, factors known to influence rehabilitation trajectories. Proprioceptive feedback circuits are critical for initiating and maintaining locomotor recovery after SCI, and pre-injury motor learning history shapes an individual’s capacity for adaptive plasticity in response to intensive task-specific practice. This design choice strengthened internal validity by reducing noise from extreme baseline variability, thereby enabling clearer detection of training-frequency effects as the primary analytical question.

Limitations and Generalizability: This eligibility criterion does limit the generalizability of findings to more sedentary SCI populations, who likely comprise 30–40% of the chronic SCI population. Sedentary individuals with SCI, who may actually benefit most from intensive rehabilitation, were excluded from this trial. Future research should examine whether training frequency effects replicate in less active or more severely impaired cohorts, and whether modified protocols (extended acclimation phases, reduced initial intensity) might safely extend Lokomat training to populations with minimal baseline activity. Despite this limitation, the present findings remain clinically relevant for the substantial SCI population maintaining regular physical activity pre-injury (estimated at 40–50% of the community-dwelling SCI population), a group commonly encountered at specialized SCI rehabilitation centers.

Screening applied two concurrent inclusion criteria, confirmed AIS B–D classification and pre-injury habitual physical activity of at least two sessions per week; both criteria were required. This activity threshold supported safe tolerance of harnessed stepping and reduced floor effects during gait practice.

Eligibility criteria for inclusion in the study were as follows: a confirmed diagnosis of SCI consistent with AIS grades B or C or D; medical clearance affirming the individual’s ability to participate in physical rehabilitation; and the absence of contraindications to Lokomat training. Specific contraindications included a lower limb length discrepancy exceeding 2 centimeters, body weight above 98 kg, severe osteoporosis or unstable fractures, the presence of open wounds, cognitive impairments, or significant cardiovascular risks.

Pre-registered stratification and subgroup designation

During preregistration (OSF Registries, DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/4BX6W), we a priori specified two strata: (1) Athletic subgroup (n = 45): individuals with ≥ 4 structured training sessions/week for ≥ 1 year pre-injury; (2) Active non-athlete subgroup (n = 30): individuals with 2–3 physical activity sessions/week pre-injury. This stratification was designated for exploratory hypothesis-generating subgroup analyses to investigate potential neuroplasticity reserve effects in trained versus untrained individuals. The athletic subgroup data subsequently formed the basis for a parallel publication investigating spasticity and pain modulation [13]. The present manuscript reports the primary outcomes and pre-specified analyses for the entire randomized cohort (n = 75), with AIS-based subgroup analyses as additional exploratory comparisons.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was carried out using a computer-generated random sequence (block randomization with variable block sizes) performed by an independent biostatistician (J.P.) not involved in participant recruitment, allocation, or intervention delivery. The randomization sequence was generated prior to enrollment using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0) and stratified by baseline AIS classification to ensure balanced distribution across neurological severity groups. Allocation concealment was maintained throughout the trial: (1) participants were informed of their group assignment only after completion of baseline assessments (PRE), preventing selection bias, and (2) outcome assessors remained blinded to group assignment during all measurements (PRE, MID, POST), ensuring that no assessor knowledge of intervention type could bias assessment administration or data recording. Investigators involved in recruitment and intervention delivery were not blinded to group assignment, as this was not feasible given the nature of the physical interventions (Lokomat training is visually distinct from conventional therapy); however, the separation of roles (independent biostatistician for randomization, blinded assessors for outcomes) minimized bias.

All statistical analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat (ITT) basis. Participants were analyzed according to their assigned group, regardless of adherence or intervention completion. As no participants withdrew or dropped out during the 12-week intervention (overall attrition = 0%, see Fig. 1), the ITT and per-protocol populations were identical. Analyses on complete cases without imputation reflected this zero-attrition design (see Section Statistical Analysis).

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three study arms: Group L1 (Lokomat training once per week, n = 25), Group L2 (Lokomat training twice per week, n = 25), or Group C (conventional physiotherapy, n = 25). All participants provided written informed consent prior to study initiation.

Study schedule

The intervention period spanned 12 weeks. Group L1 received a total of 12 Lokomat training sessions (accumulating to 9 h), while Group L2 underwent 24 Lokomat sessions (totaling 18 h). Group C received 24 sessions of conventional rehabilitation, also totaling 18 h. Functional assessments were conducted at three time points: baseline (PRE), midpoint at 6 weeks (MID), and post-intervention at 12 weeks (POST).

Intervention protocols

Lokomat-based rehabilitation was delivered using the Lokomat Pro system (Hocoma AG, Switzerland). Each session was standardized and included individual fitting of the harness and orthotic components based on anthropometric measurements, gradual and progressive increases in treadmill speed and lower limb loading, and visual feedback to enhance motivation and engagement. The total session duration was 45 min, with approximately 15 min allocated to setup and 30 min to active gait training.

Participants in Group C followed a structured yet individualized conventional rehabilitation protocol. The program consisted of joint mobilization, progressive resistance training, flexibility exercises, trunk stabilization routines, and overground gait training with the use of assistive devices where necessary. A detailed description of the conventional rehabilitation protocol is available in Supplementary Material 1.

Equipment and outcome measures

All assessments were conducted in a gait analysis laboratory under controlled experimental conditions. The following standardized outcome measures were employed: the 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT) for gait speed, the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) for walking endurance, the Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury II (WISCI II) for level of assistance required for ambulation, the Lower Extremity Motor Score (LEMS) for assessing muscle strength, the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test for dynamic balance, stabilometry for postural balance, and the EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) questionnaire for quality of life assessment. All measurements were administered by licensed physiotherapists with a minimum of five years of experience in spinal cord injury rehabilitation. Assessment protocols adhered to published standard operating procedures and demonstrated high inter-rater reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] > 0.88).

Testing procedure

Tests were conducted at PRE, MID, and POST time points. Before each session, participants completed a warm-up routine consisting of 5 min of light cycling and dynamic stretching targeting lower limbs and trunk. Tests were performed at a self-selected comfortable pace with 15-minute rest intervals between trials. Measurements were supervised by certified physiotherapists to ensure safety and consistency.

Sample size and power calculation

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. Based on a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and three groups with repeated measures across three time points, the minimum required sample size was 66. To account for a 10% dropout rate, 75 participants were enrolled.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) in a Windows-based analytical environment. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation) were calculated for all outcome variables.

The assumption of normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test for each dependent variable at three time points (PRE, MID, POST). No significant deviations from normal distribution were observed (p > 0.05 for all), which was confirmed by histogram analysis and Q–Q plots. For example, 10MWT values ranged from p = 0.65 to p = 0.70 across assessments; comparable ranges were observed for 6MWT and LEMS.

Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test at each time point. All outcome measures met this assumption (p > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric methods. Representative Levene’s values for 10MWT were p = 0.78 to p = 0.84, and for TUG p = 0.67 to p = 0.76.

Sphericity was examined using Mauchly’s test. The assumption was confirmed for 10MWT (χ²(2) = 3.24, p = 0.19) and 6MWT (χ²(2) = 4.12, p = 0.15). However, a violation was detected for WISCI II (χ²(2) = 7.56, p = 0.03) and a borderline violation for LEMS (χ²(2) = 6.89, p = 0.04). Accordingly, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied for these variables. Corrected results did not alter the interpretation of main outcomes, confirming the robustness of findings.

To assess intervention effects, a 3 (group: L1, L2, C) × 3 (time: PRE, MID, POST) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted for each outcome measure. When significant effects were observed, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were used. Effect sizes were interpreted using eta squared (η²), with η² < 0.01 considered small, η² = 0.01–0.06 moderate, and η² > 0.06 large.

To control for familywise error rate across multiple outcome measures (10MWT, 6MWT, WISCI II, LEMS, TUG, stabilometry), all primary between-group comparisons used Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests with α = 0.05 for the family of tests. For exploratory subgroup analyses by AIS classification, no multiplicity adjustment was applied due to small strata sizes and hypothesis-generating intent; therefore, these results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. All primary outcomes are presented with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) calculated from the repeated-measures ANOVA (see Supplementary Table S1 and Results section). Confidence intervals provide an additional layer of clinical transparency by conveying the precision and range of estimated treatment effects.

Missing data. Analyses were conducted on complete cases; no imputation was performed. To enhance transparency, we report attrition by group and provide all available denominators for each endpoint and time point. Sensitivity/robustness checks will be planned in future pre-registered trials.

Subgroup analyses by AIS (B, C, D) were a priori labeled as exploratory given small strata sizes and absence of multiplicity adjustment; therefore, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Additionally, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted based on AIS classification (B, C, D) to investigate whether neurological status influenced treatment response. Stratified repeated-measures ANOVAs were performed separately within each AIS group to compare the PRE–MID–POST trajectories across intervention types. These analyses indicated that participants with AIS D classification achieved the greatest functional gains, particularly in gait speed (10MWT), endurance (6MWT), and balance (TUG), most notably within the high-frequency Lokomat group (L2). Due to limited sample sizes, these comparisons are considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. A three-way interaction model (group × time × AIS) was not conducted due to insufficient statistical power.

Results

Main effects and interaction effects

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed statistically significant main effects of time for all outcome measures, including gait speed (10MWT: F(2, 144) = 18.21, p = 0.001, η² = 0.22), walking endurance (6MWT: F(2, 144) = 21.55, p < 0.001, η² = 0.28), dynamic balance (TUG: F(2, 144) = 16.79, p = 0.002, η² = 0.25), postural stability (Stabilometry: F(2, 144) = 14.43, p = 0.003, η² = 0.21), WISCI II (F(2, 144) = 12.74, p = 0.005, η² = 0.19), and LEMS (F(2, 144) = 17.31, p = 0.002, η² = 0.22).

A significant group × time interaction was also observed for each of these outcomes (all p < 0.01), indicating differential progression patterns across intervention groups over the 12-week period.

The largest improvements were consistently observed in the high-frequency Lokomat group (L2), followed by the low-frequency group (L1), with the control group (C) showing the least progress. Within-group improvements over time

Group L2 demonstrated the most substantial within-group improvements across the 12-week intervention (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). Timed Up and Go (TUG): Time decreased by 7.7 ± 3.0 s (PRE: 17.2 ± 2.5 s; POST: 9.5 ± 1.8 s; − 44.8%, p = 0.022, η² = 0.18, 95% CI [− 11.1 to − 4.3 s]). 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT): Distance increased by 43.5 ± 16.2 m (PRE: 35.5 ± 4.8 m; POST: 79 ± 14 m; +122.5%, p = 0.022, η² = 0.25, 95% CI [23.4–63.6 m]). 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT): Gait speed improved by 0.22 ± 0.06 m/s (PRE: 0.09 ± 0.01 m/s; POST: 0.31 ± 0.05 m/s; +244.4%, p = 0.011, η² = 0.22, 95% CI [0.12–0.32 m/s]). All improvements substantially exceeded MCID thresholds (10MWT MCID: 0.13 m/s; 6MWT MCID: 45.8 m; TUG MCID: 14.5 s).

For gait speed (10MWT), statistically significant improvements in Group L2 were observed between baseline and week 6 (p = 0.021), week 6 and week 12 (p = 0.012), and overall from baseline to week 12 (p = 0.011).

Group L1 exhibited moderate gains in multiple domains: gait speed (10MWT: p = 0.011 from 0 to 12 weeks), dynamic balance (TUG: p = 0.023), and postural stability (Stabilometry: p = 0.032), with corresponding moderate effect sizes (η² ≈ 0.15).

In contrast, Group C displayed only marginal within-group changes. Most outcomes failed to reach statistical significance, for example, gait speed (10MWT: p = 0.072), endurance (6MWT: p = 0.081), dynamic balance (TUG: p = 0.081), lower limb motor strength (LEMS: p = 0.091), and functional mobility (WISCI II: p = 0.082), with small effect sizes (η² ≈ 0.07), as detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Correlation between functional improvements and quality-of-life outcomes

To contextualize the clinical relevance of functional gains, we computed Pearson correlations between changes in primary functional outcomes (6MWT, TUG, 10MWT) and changes in EQ-5D domains from PRE to POST within Group L2 (Supplementary Table S3). Significant positive correlations were observed between improved 6MWT distance and enhanced mobility (r = 0.58, 95% CI [0.28–0.79], p = 0.003) and daily activities (r = 0.52, 95% CI [0.18–0.76], p = 0.007). Improved TUG performance (decreased time) significantly correlated with reduced anxiety/depression (r = − 0.49, 95% CI [− 0.75 to − 0.16], p = 0.012) and enhanced daily activities (r = 0.54, 95% CI [0.21–0.77], p = 0.005). Faster 10MWT gait speed correlated with improved mobility (r = 0.61, 95% CI [0.33–0.80], p = 0.001) and reduced pain/discomfort (r = − 0.47, 95% CI [− 0.73 to − 0.14], p = 0.019). These moderate-to-strong correlations (r = 0.45–0.61) demonstrate that functional improvements in gait, balance, and endurance are accompanied by meaningful improvements in subjective quality of life and psychological well-being, indicating that benefits extend beyond objective motor metrics to encompass patient-centered psychosocial outcomes critical for return to community participation and daily living independence.

Discussion

Neuroplasticity and functional recovery: implications for diverse patient populations

Robotic-assisted gait training, particularly at higher frequencies, appears to induce task-specific neuromuscular adaptations that may be underpinned by central neuroplasticity.

This interpretation aligns with prior evidence that robotic gait interventions stimulate corticospinal tracts, central pattern generators, and proprioceptive feedback loops through structured, high-repetition movement [3, 6, 14]. Repetitive and symmetric lower-limb movement, as provided by the Lokomat, has been shown to reorganize motor output and improve sensorimotor integration in both acute and chronic SCI populations [14–19]. These interpretations are consistent with recent meta-analyses of RAGT in SCI and parameter-tuning studies showing that device settings and assist-as-needed control influence muscle activation and kinematics during training [1–2, 9, 10].

Our stratified analyses by AIS classification revealed divergent recovery patterns, with AIS D participants demonstrating the most substantial functional gains in gait speed, balance, and postural control. This finding aligns with the therapeutic window hypothesis and existing evidence suggesting that individuals with greater residual motor function possess enhanced capacity for task-specific neuroplastic adaptation. The neurophysiological mechanisms underlying these improvements likely involve enhanced sensorimotor integration, corticospinal pathway recruitment, and motor learning processes, phenomena documented in prior robotic gait training studies [20–21]. While neuroplastic reserve has been theoretically discussed [20–22], our study was not designed to directly test whether pre-injury athletic experience modulates treatment responsiveness to different training frequencies. Future adequately powered trials with explicit athlete-versus-non-athlete stratification would be necessary to examine this hypothesis and clarify the relationship between pre-injury conditioning, neuroplasticity, and RAGT frequency effects.

From an applied standpoint, restoring agility, gait precision, and postural responsiveness is essential for safe return to sport [22]. Robotic training may support this transition by enhancing lower-limb stability and movement coordination, crucial elements in preventing reinjury in high-risk sports [24–26]. Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated that balance-focused and proprioceptive interventions reduce reinjury rates and support neuromuscular recovery [26–28].

The observed improvements in Group L2 reinforce these findings and suggest that even moderate Lokomat frequency (2 sessions/week) can yield functionally meaningful changes. While the role of robotic therapy in elite athletic rehabilitation remains underexplored, the present data encourage its integration into return-to-play protocols, particularly when combined with sport-specific motor retraining [29–31].

In sum, although neuroplasticity remains an interpretative mechanism rather than a measured outcome in this study, the convergence of functional results, previous training status, and existing literature strongly supports its role in mediating the observed gains.

Therapeutic advancements in gait and balance

The current findings reaffirm the clinical value of Lokomat-assisted gait training in enhancing lower-limb function among individuals with incomplete spinal cord injury. Group L2, which underwent twice-weekly sessions, demonstrated the most robust improvements across gait speed (10MWT), walking endurance (6MWT), and balance parameters (TUG, stabilometry). These outcomes are relevant for all individuals with incomplete SCI, including those with prior athletic backgrounds [25, 32]. For athletes engaged in multidirectional or high-speed disciplines such as basketball and soccer, enhanced neuromuscular precision and dynamic equilibrium, as observed in this study, may support return to sport [25, 32], though athlete-specific rehabilitation outcomes require dedicated, adequately powered trials.

The pattern of recovery was stratified by neurological severity. Athletes classified as AIS D exhibited the most pronounced functional gains, a finding that supports the “therapeutic window” hypothesis namely, that individuals with higher residual function are more responsive to intensive rehabilitation [24]. The larger gains in AIS D, are biologically plausible: retained descending pathways and spared white-matter tracts provide substrate for task-specific plasticity; contemporary neuroimaging indicates that corticospinal integrity predicts motor recovery trajectories after SCI [33]. In contrast, AIS B participants exhibited marginal improvements, suggesting the need for individualized protocols with extended duration or adjunctive interventions targeting severe impairments in neuro-muscular connectivity [4, 15].

The observed improvements in balance and gait align with prior mechanistic studies showing that robotic gait training enhances the excitability of motor pathways and facilitates sensorimotor reintegration [6, 14]. Enhanced proprioceptive feedback and motor patterning likely contributed to more stable and symmetric gait, which is critical in reducing lower-extremity asymmetry and fall risk [12]. In parallel, improvements in TUG are clinically meaningful because TUG performance is linked to fall risk and functional mobility in neurological populations, including SCI [1–2].

Sports-specific rehabilitation recommendations

While these benefits are clinically meaningful, future studies should specifically examine whether pre-injury athletic history modulates responsiveness to different Lokomat frequencies and whether such athlete-specific effects are generalizable across diverse SCI populations [6, 11]. The standardized gait cycle provided by Lokomat also ensures safe and consistent stimulation of key musculature involved in running, cutting, and decelerating skills essential for high-performance athletes [29].

Notably, treadmill-based systems such as Lokomat can constrain multidirectional and agility-focused tasks. Hybrid approaches that combine Lokomat with overground/harnessed exoskeleton sessions may better address cutting, acceleration–deceleration, and cognitive–motor demands; recent syntheses report device-specific differences in walking speed and indicate that overground exoskeletons can entail higher cognitive and cardiorespiratory demands, which may enhance transfer to unconstrained walking [4, 7–8]. We therefore recommend integrating psychological readiness tools (e.g., return-to-sport readiness and fear-of-falling scales) alongside biomechanical progressions [23].

To optimize rehabilitation outcomes and facilitate return to sport, Lokomat therapy should be integrated with sport-specific neuromuscular retraining. Sport-specific neuromuscular retraining should be integrated with Lokomat therapy, including perturbation-based exercises, proprioceptive training, and psychological readiness assessment. However, optimal integration strategies remain to be determined through future athlete-specific trials.

Long-term outcomes and practical applications

Although the present study focused on a 12-week intervention, prior literature suggests that extended RAGT programs can support sustained gains in locomotor capacity and psychosocial outcomes; however, future follow-ups are needed to verify durability in our cohort [1, 6]. Emerging tele-rehabilitation models may help maintain benefits and reduce barriers to ongoing training. In elite sports contexts such as rugby, track and field, or gymnastics, these long-term gains translate into regained competition-readiness, enhanced dynamic performance, and reduced injury recurrence [30–31].

The quality-of-life outcomes measured via EQ-5D further emphasize the psychosocial relevance of robotic gait training. Improvements in mobility, independence, pain reduction, and emotional well-being were especially pronounced in the L2 group, with a high proportion of participants surpassing the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). These outcomes are critical for athletes navigating not only physical recovery but also psychological readiness to re-engage with high-performance demands [22, 23].

Taken together, these findings support the integration of Lokomat therapy into both subacute and long-term rehabilitation pathways for individuals with SCI. The data advocate for tailored training intensities based on neurological classification (AIS) and baseline functional capacity.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial provides robust evidence supporting the efficacy of robotic-assisted gait training using the Lokomat system in improving functional and quality-of-life outcomes in individuals with incomplete spinal cord injury. Participants in the high-frequency intervention group (L2) exhibited the most pronounced gains in gait speed, walking endurance, dynamic balance, postural control, and lower-limb motor function. These improvements were not only statistically significant but also met thresholds for clinical relevance, particularly in EQ-5D domains such as mobility and independence.

Importantly, stratified analyses by AIS classification revealed that individuals with greater residual function (AIS D) experienced more substantial functional recovery, consistent with the therapeutic window hypothesis. These findings emphasize the necessity of tailoring rehabilitation intensity to patients’ neurological status and baseline functional potential.

The integration of robotic rehabilitation with complementary neuromuscular training may support safe functional recovery in individuals with SCI, including those with prior athletic backgrounds. However, athlete-specific return-to-sport protocols require future adequately powered trials explicitly comparing athletes and non-athletes. Although neurophysiological biomarkers were not assessed, the functional gains observed suggest engagement of sensorimotor integration and motor pathway recruitment consistent with mechanisms of motor learning and neural adaptation.

Overall, this study contributes to a growing body of literature supporting Lokomat-based protocols as both effective and scalable tools in neurorehabilitation, with direct implications for elite sport populations, physically active patients, and clinical decision-making in spinal injury management.

Future research directions

This study was not powered to compare rehabilitation responsiveness between athletes and non-athletes. Future trials should: (1) pre-register explicit athlete-versus-non-athlete stratification with adequate sample sizes (n ≥ 30 per group), (2) employ interaction testing (group × athlete status × time) to examine whether pre-injury athletic training modulates frequency-dependent responses, (3) apply rigorous multiplicity correction for such analyses, and (4) extend findings to diverse SCI populations and sports disciplines. These comparisons are essential to determine whether athlete-specific rehabilitation protocols are warranted or whether the current frequency-based protocol generalizes across all patient subgroups.

Study limitations

This study’s relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of its findings. Additionally, the 12-week program duration may not fully capture the long-term benefits of Lokomat therapy, particularly for AIS B participants. The absence of a comparison with higher-intensity protocols (3–5 sessions per week) may underestimate the therapy’s potential impact for athletes and severely impaired patients.

Our inclusion criterion of pre-injury physical activity ≥ 2 sessions per week may limit generalizability to more sedentary SCI populations or those with severe pre-injury deconditioning. While this threshold was applied to optimize safety and reduce confounding from baseline heterogeneity, it necessarily restricted the study sample to a population with documented habitual activity. Sedentary individuals with chronic SCI, who may actually benefit most from intensive rehabilitation, were excluded from this trial. Future research should systematically evaluate whether the training-frequency effects observed here extend to less active populations through (1) modified Lokomat protocols with extended acclimation phases and reduced initial intensity, (2) participant-centered safety monitoring, and (3) adequately powered trials enrolling sedentary SCI cohorts. Additionally, comparative effectiveness research examining whether baseline activity level modulates responsiveness to training frequency could further inform personalized rehabilitation approaches. Despite this limitation, findings remain directly applicable to the subset of SCI individuals maintaining regular pre-injury physical activity, a clinically and epidemiologically relevant population treated at specialized rehabilitation centers.

Future research should address these limitations by including larger cohorts, longer study durations, and a broader range of training intensities.

Finally, our protocol did not include perturbation-based balance or multidirectional agility drills, which are core components of effective fall-prevention exercise; future work should test combined Lokomat-plus-balance/agility progressions and compare training intensities and doses explicitly.
