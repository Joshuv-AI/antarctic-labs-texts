Bioequivalence and Safety Assessment of Brivaracetam Dry Syrup Compared With Tablets in Healthy Japanese Participants: Results From Two Randomized Single‐ and Multiple‐Dose Trials
Authors: Yoshinobu Hayakawa, Ryoji Yoshinaka, Tomonobu Sano, Walter Krauwinkel, Tadaharu Soma, Hiroshi Sasamoto, Jan‐Peer Elshoff, Almasa Bass, Ying Chen, Nozomi Kurihara, Harumi Murakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe antiseizure medication brivaracetam is available as tablets, oral solution, and solution for intravenous injection in many countries. A dry syrup formulation is being developed for use in Japan. We report two phase 1, single‐center, open‐label, randomized, two‐way crossover trials that assessed bioequivalence, safety, and tolerability of brivaracetam dry syrup and tablet formulations in healthy adult male Japanese participants. EP0110 (NCT05315947) assessed brivaracetam 50 mg dry syrup versus tablet after a single administration (N = 24). Although pharmacokinetic profiles were similar and bioequivalence criteria for AUC were met, bioequivalence criteria for the observed Cmax were not met in this trial. A subsequent simulation of parameters at steady state, based on EP0110 data, indicated that bioequivalence could be established in a multiple‐dose setting. EP0231 (NCT06312566) was designed employing a group sequential design (N = 96) and assessed brivaracetam 50 mg dry syrup versus tablet after multiple oral doses (N = 64). The point estimates (92.016% CIs) for Cmax and AUC at steady state fell within the acceptable range for bioequivalence (0.80‐1.25). Bioequivalence of dry syrup and tablet formulations was demonstrated after multiple doses. Single and multiple doses of brivaracetam 50 mg administered as dry syrup or tablet formulation were generally safe and well tolerated in healthy Japanese participants.Keywords: adverse events, anticonvulsants, clinical trials, pharmacokinetics, phase I

Brivaracetam (BRV) is an antiseizure medication that selectively binds to the synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A) receptor site with high affinity. 1 , 2 , 3 BRV is currently approved worldwide in >50 countries. 4 BRV is indicated for mono‐ and adjunctive therapy for focal (partial‐onset) seizures in patients ≥1 month of age in the United States, and for adjunctive therapy in patients ≥2 years of age in the European Union. 5 , 6 In Japan, BRV was recently approved for mono‐ and adjunctive therapy for focal seizures in adult patients. 7 , 8 The approved therapeutic dose range for pediatric patients weighing ≥50 kg and for adults is 50‐200 mg/day (25‐100 mg twice daily). Dosing for pediatric patients weighing <50 kg is weight‐based, with exact dose ranges varying slightly between regions.

BRV is available as tablets, oral solution, and solution for intravenous injection in many countries. The pharmacokinetics (PK) of oral solid formulations of BRV have been studied extensively in healthy participants 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 and bioequivalence of BRV oral solution and tablets has been demonstrated. 13 Liquid oral administration is favorable in younger pediatric patients, 14 and may also be required by older patients with swallowing difficulties. 15 Dry syrup, a reconstitutable oral powder formulation, is the preferred pediatric formulation in Japan, and a dry syrup formulation of BRV is being developed to allow dose adjustment by body weight in pediatric patients. However, the PK of the dry syrup formulation has not yet been evaluated in clinical trials. As dry syrup represents a formulation change, a bioequivalence trial between dry syrup and tablet formulations was planned based on Japanese bioequivalence guidelines. 16 , 17

Trial EP0110 (Figure S1, Supporting Information) assessed bioequivalence, safety, and tolerability of BRV 50 mg dry syrup and tablet in healthy participants in accordance with Japanese bioequivalence guidelines, 16 , 17 which recommend a single‐dose trial with a usual clinical dose. Bioequivalence criteria for the observed maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) were not met in this trial. For further investigation of the possibility of bioequivalence, a multiple‐dose PK simulation was conducted using the data collected in EP0110. This indicated that bioequivalence could be established in a multiple‐dose setting with a lower number of participants than would be required for a successful single‐dose trial. Trial EP0231 (Figure S2, Supporting Information) was designed based on this simulation and in alignment with the Japanese bioequivalence guidelines, 16 , 17 which state that multiple‐dose trials may be conducted for drugs that are repeatedly administered to patients. Trial EP0231 assessed the bioequivalence, safety, and tolerability of BRV 50 mg dry syrup and tablet after multiple oral doses were administered to healthy participants.

Materials and Methods

Trial Design

EP0110 (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05315947) was a phase 1, single‐center, single‐dose, open‐label, randomized, two‐way crossover trial evaluating the bioequivalence between the BRV 50 mg dry syrup and tablet formulations in healthy male Japanese participants. EP0231 (NCT06312566) was a phase 1, single‐center, multiple‐dose, open‐label, randomized, group sequential design, two‐way crossover trial evaluating the bioequivalence between the BRV 50 mg dry syrup and tablet formulations in healthy male Japanese participants. Both trials were performed at the same site (Souseikai Sumida Hospital, Sumida‐City, Tokyo, Japan). For both trials, the trial protocol, amendments, and participant informed consent were reviewed by the Hakata Clinic Institutional Review Board (Fukuoka‐City, Japan). Both trials were conducted in accordance with the current version of the applicable regulatory and International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice requirements, the ethical principles that have their origin in the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the local laws of the country involved.

Participants

Both trials were conducted in healthy male Japanese volunteers. Participants had to be 20‐50 years of age, with a body weight of ≥50 kg and a body mass index of 18‐30 kg/m2. Participants had to be overtly healthy as determined by medical evaluation (involving medical history, physical examination, laboratory tests, and cardiac monitoring), of Japanese descent (evidenced by appearance and verbal confirmation of familial heritage [i.e., all four Japanese grandparents born in Japan]), and capable of giving informed consent. All participants provided written informed consent. Certain exclusion criteria and lifestyle restrictions applied (Supplementary Methods: Exclusion Criteria; Lifestyle Restrictions).

A total of 24 participants were planned to be randomized and to complete trial EP0110 (Supplementary Methods: Determination of Sample Size). Although both PK profiles were similar and the area under the concentration‐time curve (AUC) from time 0 to the time of the last quantifiable concentration (AUC0‐t) met the criteria for bioequivalence, the bioequivalence criteria for Cmax were not met in this trial. A subsequent PK simulation based on EP0110 data indicated that bioequivalence could be established in a multiple‐dose setting with a lower number of participants than would be required for a successful single‐dose trial (Supplementary Methods: Determination of Sample Size). The sample size of the multiple‐dose trial EP0231 was calculated based on the results of trial EP0110 and the PK simulation. Up to ≈90 participants were planned to be evaluable for the primary endpoint (Supplementary Methods: Determination of Sample Size). Trial EP0231 was planned to consist of up to two cohorts, with a formal interim analysis once Cohort 1 (≈60 evaluable participants) had completed the trial. If bioequivalence or futility was established in the interim analysis, the trial was to be stopped, and the interim analysis was to become the final analysis. If bioequivalence or futility was not established in the interim analysis, the final analysis was planned at the end of Cohort 2, once ≈90 participants had completed the trial.

Treatment Schedule

Trial EP0110 consisted of a screening period (2‐28 days before BRV administration), two 4‐day dosing periods in which BRV tablet or dry syrup was administered as a single dose on Day 1 (Supplementary Methods: BRV Administration), and a safety follow‐up period 7‐9 days after the last BRV administration. The two dosing periods were separated by a washout period of 7‐10 days between each BRV administration (Figure S1, Supporting Information). On Day −1 or Day 1 of the first dosing period, participants were randomized 1:1 to treatment sequence A–B or B–A (Treatment A: single dose of BRV 50 mg tablet; Treatment B: single dose of BRV 50 mg as dry syrup [1.25 g of granules for oral suspension 4% weight/weight (w/w)]; Supplementary Methods: Randomization Details). During each dosing period, participants were admitted to the trial site on Day −1 and fasted for ≥10 h overnight and continued to fast for 4 h post dose. Coadministration of BRV with a high‐fat meal slows down the absorption rate and decreases Cmax, while the extent of absorption remains unchanged. 5 , 6 , 7 , 9 Blood samples were taken on the morning of Day 1 before BRV administration and at 15, 30, and 45 min, and 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 36, and 48 h after BRV administration for determination of BRV plasma concentrations (Supplementary Methods: Bioanalytical Methods). Participants were discharged in the morning of the final day (Day 3) of each dosing period, ≈48 h after BRV administration, provided the investigator had no medical objections.

Trial EP0231 comprised a screening period (2‐28 days before BRV administration), two 5‐day dosing periods in which BRV tablet or dry syrup was administered five times (Day 1 and Day 2 [morning and evening doses, 12 h apart] and Day 3 [morning dose only]; Supplementary Methods: BRV Administration), and a safety follow‐up period 7‐9 days after the last BRV administration. The two dosing periods were separated by a washout period of 6‐10 days between the final dose in Dosing Period 1 and the first dose in Dosing Period 2 (Figure S2, Supporting Information). On Day −1 or Day 1 of the first dosing period, participants were randomized 1:1 to treatment sequence A–B or B–A (Treatment A: multiple doses of BRV 50 mg tablets; Treatment B: multiple doses of BRV 50 mg as dry syrup [1.25 g of granules for oral suspension 4% w/w]; Supplementary Methods: Randomization Details). BRV was administered twice daily for a total of five doses to allow steady state to be reached. During each dosing period, participants were admitted to the trial site on Day −1. Participants fasted for ≥2 h before and after BRV administration on Days 1 and 2. On Day 3, participants fasted for 10 h before and 4 h after the final BRV administration. For Days 1 and 2, blood samples were taken pre‐dose before each BRV administration. On Day 3, blood samples were taken pre‐dose, and at 10, 15, 20, 30, and 45 min, and 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 6, 9, and 12 h post‐dose for determination of BRV plasma concentrations (Supplementary Methods: Bioanalytical Methods). Participants were discharged in the morning of Day 4 of each dosing period, ≈24 h after the final BRV administration, provided the investigator had no medical objections.

Outcomes

The primary PK endpoints in trial EP0110 were Cmax and AUC0‐t. Other PK endpoints were AUC from time 0 to infinity, extrapolated AUC, time of Cmax (tmax), terminal elimination half‐life, apparent volume of distribution, and apparent total clearance after oral administration.

The primary PK endpoints in trial EP0231 were Cmax at steady state (Cmax,ss) and AUC during a dosing interval at steady state (AUCtau). Other PK endpoints included measured concentration at the end of a dosing interval (Ctrough), apparent total body clearance at steady state (CLss/F), and time of Cmax,ss (tmax,ss).

The secondary safety and tolerability endpoints for both trials were the incidences of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious TEAEs, and TEAEs leading to discontinuation (EP0231 only). Other safety and tolerability endpoints included change from baseline in clinical laboratory assessments (hematology, clinical chemistry, and urinalysis), vital signs (pulse rate, blood pressure, body temperature, and respiratory rate), 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) assessments, and physical examination findings.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. PK noncompartmental analysis was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.3 or 8.3.4 for PK parameter estimation. Adverse events were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 18.1.

PK parameters were calculated using noncompartmental analysis methods. Bioavailability of BRV 50 mg dry syrup (test) was compared with BRV 50 mg tablet (reference) (Supplementary Methods: Statistical Analysis Details). In EP0110, adjusted geometric mean (GeoMean) ratio (GMR) (test/reference) and corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. In EP0231, adjusted GMR (test/reference) and corresponding CIs were calculated corresponding to the Lan‐DeMets alpha spending function approximating a Pocock boundary; 18 92.016% CIs were used at the interim analysis for maintaining the overall two‐sided significance level of 0.1 (two‐sided significance level of 0.03992•2). Bioequivalence was concluded if the CI limits for the adjusted GMRs of Cmax and AUC0‐t (EP0110) or Cmax,ss and AUCtau (EP0231) were fully contained within the 0.80‐1.25 range. In the case that the CIs were not contained in this range, bioequivalence in trial EP0231 could still be concluded if the point estimates of the adjusted GMR (test/reference) for Cmax,ss and AUCtau were contained in the range of 0.90‐1.11 and in vitro dissolution rates of test and reference formulations were available and deemed similar according to the Japanese bioequivalence guideline. 17 At the interim analysis of EP0231, the one‐sided (upper) 95% CI of the adjusted GMR (test/reference) for Cmax,ss was to be calculated in parallel to test if it was <0.8700 (this would have corresponded to the GMR being markedly lower than that of EP0110 and would have led to the trial being stopped for futility) if bioequivalence was not established. Interparticipant and intraparticipant variabilities for each parameter were derived from these analyses as the geometric coefficient of variation (GeoCV).

For the multiple‐dose PK simulation based on EP0110 data, steady state was predicted using nonparametric superposition in Phoenix WinNonlin.

The enrolled set included all participants who signed the informed consent form. The randomized set included all enrolled set participants who were randomized. The safety set included all randomized set participants who received at least one dose of the study drug. The Pharmacokinetic Per Protocol Set in trial EP0110 included participants in the safety set who had no important protocol deviations that were considered to impact the data validity for analysis of the primary objective and had a sufficient number of bioanalytical assessments to calculate reliable estimates for the primary PK parameters for both treatments. The Pharmacokinetic Set in trial EP0231 included participants in the safety set who had at least one quantifiable postbaseline PK measurement; all participants who had a sufficient number of bioanalytical assessments to calculate reliable estimates for the primary PK parameters for both treatments were included in the analysis.

Results

Participants

Trial EP0110 was conducted between April and May 2022 at a single center in Japan. Overall, 63 participants were screened. Thirty‐nine (61.9%) participants were screen failures, most of which (22 [34.9%]) were screened and subsequently not required due to sufficient enrollment. Of the remaining 17 screen failures, 8 (12.7%) were backup participants who were not required; 7 (11.1%) were ineligible and 2 (3.2%) withdrew consent. Twenty‐four participants started the trial and completed both treatments. No participants discontinued after dosing.

Trial EP0231 was conducted between March and June 2024 at the same center in Japan. Bioequivalence between BRV 50 mg dry syrup and tablet was demonstrated at the interim analysis based on Cohort 1. As prespecified, the trial was stopped after Cohort 1, and the interim analysis became the final analysis. Overall, 130 participants were screened. Sixty‐six (50.8%) participants were screen failures, most of which were ineligible (24 [18.5%]) or were screened and subsequently not required due to sufficient enrollment (23 [17.7%]). Of the remaining 19 screen failures, 14 (10.8%) participants withdrew consent and 5 (3.8%) were backup participants who were not required. Sixty‐four participants started the trial and 63 (98.4%) completed both dosing periods and the safety follow‐up visit. One participant (1.6%) randomized to the dry syrup–tablet sequence discontinued the trial due to a protocol violation after completing Dosing Period 1 and before administration of the tablet in Dosing Period 2. The protocol deviation was categorized as procedural noncompliance (positive urine cotinine test).

All participants in trial EP0110 (N = 24) and trial EP0231 (N = 64) were male and of Japanese descent (Table 1). The mean age was 34.5 years in EP0110 and 33.6 years in EP0231. All participants in EP0110 were included in the Safety Set and Pharmacokinetic Per Protocol Set, and all participants in EP0231 were included in the safety set and pharmacokinetic set. All participants in EP0110 received both treatments. One participant in EP0231 did not receive the tablet formulation.

Statistical analyses of primary PK outcomes were undertaken to assess bioequivalence of BRV 50 mg dry syrup versus tablet (Table 2). For AUC0‐t, the adjusted GMR (geometric least squares mean [Geo LSM] ratio) (dry syrup relative to tablet) was 0.9890 (90% CI: 0.9756‐1.003). The 90% CI bounds fell well within the acceptable range for bioequivalence (0.80‐1.25). Furthermore, the intraparticipant variability was smaller than the interparticipant variability (2.7% vs 16.6%, respectively; Table 2; Figure S3B, Supporting Information). The Cmax of dry syrup was 13% lower relative to tablet (Geo LSM ratio: 0.8700; 90% CI: 0.7814‐0.9686). Because the lower bound of the 90% CI was <0.80, bioequivalence criteria for Cmax were not met. Furthermore, the intraparticipant variability was higher than the interparticipant variability (21.9% vs 9.4%, respectively; Table 2; Figure S3A, Supporting Information). For tmax, the location shift (dry syrup − tablet) was 0.000 h (90% CI: 0.000‐0.1250).

After completion of EP0110, we performed a PK simulation based on EP0110 data. This simulation indicated that bioequivalence could be established in a multiple‐dose setting with a lower number of participants than would be required for a successful single‐dose trial (Table S1, Supporting Information).

In trial EP0231, the GeoMean BRV plasma concentration over time profiles were similar between both formulations (Figure 2). After 1 day of twice‐daily administrations, plasma concentrations of BRV reached steady state at 24 h post first dose. The mean plasma concentration of BRV peaked at 30 min after administration on Day 3 and then gradually decreased. After study drug administration on Day 3, the GeoMean Cmax,ss was 2.878 µg/mL for dry syrup and 2.963 µg/mL for tablet (Table 3); interparticipant variability levels were similar (GeoCV: dry syrup 19.5%, tablet 24.1%). The GeoMean AUCtau was 18.53 h µg/mL for dry syrup and 18.55 h µg/mL for tablet; interparticipant variability levels were similar between formulations (GeoCV: 16.8% and 17.4%, respectively; Table 3), but lower than those for Cmax,ss. Median tmax,ss was earlier for the dry syrup relative to the tablet (20 vs 30 min); however, the ranges were similar between both treatments (dry syrup 10‐120 min, tablet 15‐120 min). GeoMean CLss/F were similar between the dry syrup and tablet (2.699 and 2.696 L/h, respectively). GeoMean Ctrough were generally similar for both formulations (Table 3; Figure 3). For the dry syrup, GeoMean Rtrough (ratio between the predose concentration before each dose with the exclusion of morning dose on Day 1 and the Ctrough after the last dose on Day 3) was 0.9047 at 24 h post‐first dose and remained close to 1 thereafter. For the tablet, GeoMean Rtrough was 0.9009 at 24 h post‐first dose and remained close to 1 thereafter. Rtrough data indicate that steady state was reached at 24 h post‐first dose for both formulations.

Statistical analyses of primary PK outcomes were undertaken to assess bioequivalence of BRV 50 mg dry syrup versus tablet (Table 3). For AUCtau, the Geo LSM ratio (dry syrup relative to tablet) was 0.9999 (92.016% CI: 0.9881‐1.012). For Cmax,ss, the Geo LSM ratio (dry syrup relative to tablet) was 0.9726 (92.016% CI: 0.9257‐1.022). Safety and Tolerability

In EP0110, 11 (45.8%) participants reported at least one TEAE (Table 4). Overall incidences of TEAEs were the same between formulations, and overall incidences of drug‐related TEAEs were similar between formulations. No serious TEAEs, discontinuations due to TEAEs, severe TEAEs, or deaths were reported. The most common TEAEs with both formulations were somnolence and dizziness (Table 4). All reported TEAEs were mild in intensity.

In EP0231, 9 (14.1%) participants reported a total of 14 TEAEs (Table 4). Overall incidences of TEAEs and drug‐related TEAEs were numerically higher with the dry syrup than with the tablet. No serious TEAEs, discontinuations due to TEAEs, severe TEAEs, or deaths were reported. The most common TEAEs in all participants were dizziness and somnolence (Table 4). All reported TEAEs were mild in intensity.

There were no clinically relevant findings in either trial for clinical laboratory assessments, vital signs, 12‐lead ECG assessments, and physical examination findings.

Discussion

A single‐dose trial (EP0110) was performed to assess the bioequivalence between the BRV 50 mg dry syrup and tablet formulations in healthy adult male Japanese participants. Although PK parameters were similar between both formulations, the Geo LSM ratio (90% CI) for Cmax for the BRV 50 mg dry syrup relative to the BRV 50 mg tablet was 0.8700 (0.7814‐0.9686), and the 90% CI did not fall within bioequivalence criteria. Therefore, bioequivalence of BRV between the dry syrup and tablet formulations at a dose of 50 mg was not demonstrated in this trial. For the sample size calculation, a mean ratio of 0.95‐1.05 and an intraparticipant variability of 20% were assumed; however, the observed ratio for Cmax was much lower than expected and the observed intraparticipant variability was 21.9%. The lower Cmax ratio in combination with the higher variability resulted in a lower bound of the 90% CI that was outside the acceptable range for bioequivalence (0.80‐1.25). Further investigation of individual Cmax data was therefore conducted to try to identify a potential cause for the high degree of intraparticipant variability. The distribution of individual Cmax values was similar for the dry syrup and tablet formulations; however, on the individual participant level, the Cmax ratio (dry syrup/tablet) was >1.25 in three participants and was <0.80 in eight participants (Figure S3A, Supporting Information). As more participants had a ratio <0.80, the Cmax ratio point estimate was <1 and the lower boundary of the 90% CI was <0.80. Among the eight participants who had a Cmax ratio <0.80, five also had tmax values for the dry syrup that were later relative to the tablet. The lower Cmax and delayed tmax with dry syrup in some of the participants may have influenced the bioequivalence result of Cmax. Of note, bioequivalence of BRV oral solution and tablets was demonstrated in a phase 1, randomized, open‐label, two‐way crossover trial after single administration. 13 Although trial EP0110 had a similar design to this previous trial, bioequivalence between dry syrup and the tablet was not demonstrated.

After the single‐dose trial EP0110 failed to demonstrate bioequivalence, a PK simulation was performed to determine what the outcome after multiple dosing would be. Extrapolation of the data from EP0110 to steady state indicated that bioequivalence could be established in a multiple‐dose setting (Table S1, Supporting Information). Bioequivalence is easier to demonstrate after multiple dosing, as an increase in exposure level leads to a smaller difference in the Cmax values of the tablet and dry syrup and to less intraparticipant variability in Cmax. Further, a multiple‐dose trial is more reflective of BRV treatment in clinical practice than a single‐dose trial. Based on this simulation and in alignment with the Japanese bioequivalence guidelines, which state that multiple‐dose trials may be conducted for drugs that are repeatedly administered to patients, 16 , 17 trial EP0231 was designed to assess the bioequivalence of BRV 50 mg dry syrup and tablet after multiple oral doses in healthy adult male Japanese participants. This trial included a larger sample size of participants than trial EP0110 and had a group sequential design to maximize the chances of success while enabling an early stop to the trial and reducing the number of participants required. In trial EP0231, GeoMean BRV plasma concentration versus time profiles were similar after administration of dry syrup and tablet formulations. All PK endpoints assessed were similar for both formulations, indicating that the extent of exposure of BRV was comparable. As expected, the 92.016% CIs for both primary PK endpoints (Cmax,ss and AUCtau) were within the acceptable range for bioequivalence. Bioequivalence of BRV between the dry syrup and tablet formulations at a dose of 50 mg was demonstrated in this trial. Of note, the results of this trial were close to what was predicted during the multiple‐dose simulation; for Cmax,ss, the GMR (dry syrup/tablet) was 0.9726 (prediction 0.9054) and intraparticipant CV% was 15.7% (prediction 15.3%); for AUCtau, GMR (dry syrup/tablet) was 0.9999 (prediction 0.9893) and intraparticipant CV% was 3.7% (prediction 2.7%); both GMRs were within the 90% CIs of the simulation. The 50 mg dose assessed in these trials was chosen because it is a commonly used therapeutic single dose. Because the PK of BRV is linear over the therapeutic dose range, the bioequivalence demonstrated in EP0231 should be applicable to other doses as well.

Previously, the extent of absorption for the BRV 50 mg oral solid formulation was demonstrated to be ≈100%. 10 The rate of absorption observed in this trial appeared to be slightly faster for the dry syrup than the tablet formulation. This difference was expected, as the dry syrup is dissolved in water prior to administration, while the tablet needs to dissolve in the body after administration.

In trial EP0231, bioequivalence between formulations was assessed at steady state, which was reached at 24 h post‐first dose (before the third dose) for both formulations, as demonstrated by Ctrough data. The dense sampling during the first 1.5 h post dose on Day 3 contributed to accurate estimates of Cmax,ss and tmax,ss. The median tmax,ss for the BRV 50 mg dry syrup and BRV 50 mg tablet formulations were 20 and 30 min, respectively, showing rapid rates of absorption for both formulations.

Single and multiple doses of BRV 50 mg administered as dry syrup or tablet formulation were generally safe and well‐tolerated in healthy Japanese male participants. Safety and tolerability assessments in both trials demonstrated no notable difference between the two formulations in the TEAEs reported. TEAEs were consistent with the known safety profile of BRV, and no new or unexpected safety concerns were observed. The incidences of TEAEs and drug‐related TEAEs were numerically higher for dry syrup compared with tablet formulation in EP0231, but no conclusions could be drawn regarding these differences due to the small number of participants with TEAEs. The open‐label design of the trials meant that the investigator, site staff, and participants knew the formulation received in each treatment period, which may have influenced TEAE reporting.
