A hybrid AI vision model for single-camera tracking of rehabilitation exercises: a proof of concept in clinical settings
Authors: Utpal Chandra Das, Ngoc Thien Le, Timporn Vitoonpong, Chatkaew Pongmala, Chalermdej Prapinpairoj, Kawee Anannub, Pasu Kaewplung, Surachai Chaitusaney, Watit Benjapolakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate measurement of range of motion (ROM) during physical rehabilitation is traditionally achieved using goniometers or multi-camera, marker-based motion capture systems. The latter, while highly accurate, require specialised laboratory infrastructure, are costly, and are unsuitable for home-based use. There is growing interest in markerless, camera-based alternatives that leverage artificial intelligence (AI) for joint angle estimation. This study presents a preliminary proof-of-concept evaluation of a hybrid AI vision system, combining the MediaPipe Pose framework (version 0.8.9.1, Full model variant) with a Mamdani-type fuzzy inference system, for joint angle estimation and exercise repetition counting using a single consumer-grade camera. Fourteen healthy adult rehabilitation staff members performed three exercises (elbow flexion, knee extension, and hip external rotation) simultaneously recorded by the proposed AI system and by a reference optical motion capture system (C-Motion Visual3D™, 14 calibrated infrared cameras, 53 retro-reflective markers). Reliability was assessed using two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2,1}$$\end{document}) with absolute agreement. Individual-measure ICC values ranged from poor to good across joints and conditions (range: 0.005–0.68). The highest reliability was observed for the right hip external rotation ROM (individual ICC =~0.68; average ICC =~0.81, 95% CI: 0.63–0.89). Several measures showed poor reliability, including the right elbow flexion ROM (individual ICC =~0.21) and the minimum angle of the left hip external rotation (individual ICC =~0.005). Confidence intervals were wide throughout. This proof of concept demonstrates that a single-camera AI system can capture general trends in joint angles during simple rehabilitation exercises in healthy adults under controlled conditions. However, reliability is inconsistent and frequently poor, and the system has not been evaluated in patient populations with pathological movement patterns. These findings do not support clinical deployment at this stage. Substantial further development and validation in patient cohorts are required before clinical or home-based use can be considered.

Introduction

Physical rehabilitation exercises are important for helping patients improve their abilities, relieve pain, address physical disabilities and impairments, and regain their full range of physical activity1–3. Research has consistently demonstrated the importance of physical rehabilitation in both home and clinical settings4–7. Following a wide range of medical conditions, patients often experience physical deconditioning, the loss of strength, stamina, and mobility, which substantially impedes recovery8,9. Rehabilitation programs face several well-documented challenges, including lack of patient motivation, inadequate exercise instruction, insufficient time management, limited biofeedback, and absence of real-time supervision10–12. Consequently, there is a growing demand for innovative tools that can enhance and improve both home-based and clinical rehabilitation programs.

Wearable sensors are widely used in clinical examinations to track and monitor patients’ performance during rehabilitation. However, these sensors can be uncomfortable and costly, and their use in the home is often impractical. There is, therefore, growing interest in AI-based computer vision techniques for tracking rehabilitation exercises without wearable sensors. Such systems rely on image-based video data and algorithmic decision-making to estimate joint angles. AI models can be trained on data from patients or physiotherapists, enabling them to predict joint angles during exercise13–15. AI technology has advanced considerably and is increasingly utilised in healthcare to support clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes16,17. However, the accuracy of machine-learning models depends on the quantity and quality of available data, which is particularly challenging for diverse rehabilitation exercise scenarios. The accuracy and effectiveness of AI-based models in real clinical environments remain limited, partly due to the complexity of human movement and the challenges of working with heterogeneous, limited data sources.

This study addresses these challenges by proposing a hybrid AI vision model that integrates computer vision and deep learning techniques to track and evaluate rehabilitation exercises using a single camera. The objective is to eliminate the need for wearable sensors while delivering reliable, real-time feedback to both patients and physiotherapists. The proposed solution serves as a proof of concept, tested on 14 healthy adult participants using both the C-Motion Visual3D™ optical motion capture system and the AI platform simultaneously. A two-way intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) study was conducted to assess the reliability of the model relative to the reference standard.

The present study builds on our previously published model18, but introduces three distinct contributions that are entirely absent from that earlier work. In the prior study, we tracked lower limb only exercises (hip flexion, hip external rotation, and knee extension), validated the angle-estimation accuracy using The Fugl-Meyer Assessment and a K-Nearest Neighbour (KNN) classifier trained on data from 30 healthy volunteer, and did not employ any external motion capture reference system. The current study is the first to: (1) extend the exercise set to include an upper limb exercise (elbow flexion), covering both upper and lower extremities; (2) run the single-camera AI platform simultaneously alongside the C-Motion Visual3D™ optical motion capture system 14 calibrated infrared cameras and 53 retro-reflective markers so that both platforms record the same movement at the same time, enabling a direct platform-to-platform comparison that was never performed in the prior work; and (3) apply formal Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) methodology to quantify the agreement between the two platforms, providing the first objective reliability benchmark for this approach against a recognised gold-standard system in a clinical setting.

It is important to note that this study has two key limitations that must be considered when interpreting all findings. First, all 14 participants were healthy adults with no motor impairments; the performance of the system in clinical patient populations who may exhibit abnormal movement patterns, restricted ROM, compensatory postures, and involuntary movements is entirely unknown and cannot be extrapolated from these results. Second, this is a small-sample proof-of-concept study (n=14); the wide confidence intervals reported throughout reflect this limited statistical precision, and all findings should be considered exploratory rather than definitive.

Related work

Motion tracking and joint angle estimation are central to effective rehabilitation, enabling clinicians to quantitatively assess a patient’s progress and adjust the treatment plan accordingly. Traditional motion tracking systems, such as those using inertial measurement units (IMUs) or multi-camera optical capture setups, can be cumbersome, require complex calibration, and are often cost-prohibitive for widespread use19–21.

An alternative to three-dimensional, marker-based, multi-camera systems is markerless video-based tracking. Ong et al.21 compared a markerless system to a marker-based optical system for gait analysis and found root-mean-square differences of less than 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} for most joint angles, suggesting that markerless systems can capture general movement trends, albeit with reduced precision relative to the gold standard. To address the challenge of joint occlusion, researchers have also designed hybrid systems that combine RGB cameras with self-designed IMUs. On the deep learning side, Zeng et al.22 proposed a sequence-to-sequence recurrent neural network for classifying normal and abnormal gait from multimodal smartphone data. Google’s MediaPipe Pose framework23 provides a lightweight, real-time solution for detecting 33 anatomical landmarks and inferring relative 3D joint coordinates from monocular video. However, its accuracy varies with software version, model variant, lighting conditions, and movement complexity. Known limitations include depth (Z-axis) estimation errors from monocular inference and sensitivity to occlusion and body proportions; prior studies have reported variable results depending on exercise type and camera placement23. It is therefore important to empirically validate MediaPipe-based systems for each specific clinical application.

Rule-based and fuzzy logic approaches have been combined with pose estimation for exercise assessment. Zhao et al.4 proposed a rule-based motion tracking system for rehabilitation that provides real-time assessment and feedback, demonstrating that structured decision rules can complement pose estimation. Systems such as Fitness Done Right24 and Muscle Vision25 further demonstrate the feasibility of real-time exercise recognition from monocular video, though clinical validation in patient populations remains limited for both.

Depth-sensing approaches using the Microsoft Kinect sensor have also been explored26–28. Kinect-based systems directly capture depth information, avoiding the monocular depth estimation problem, and have been applied successfully to physical exercise monitoring. However, Kinect hardware has been discontinued, limiting its future applicability in new deployment contexts.

The current method considered the gold standard for ROM measurement in research is multi-camera marker-based optical capture using established marker models29,30, which provides the accuracy required for clinical biomechanics research but requires specialised equipment and dedicated facilities. To date, few studies have formally benchmarked single-camera markerless AI systems against multi-camera optical capture in a clinical setting using ICC methodology. The present study addresses this gap, while acknowledging the preliminary and exploratory nature of the findings.

Experimental protocol

Both systems captured data simultaneously during each session. Each participant performed 10 consecutive repetitions of each of the three exercises, in the following order: elbow flexion, hip external rotation, and knee extension. A rest period of 60 seconds was provided between exercises. Standardised verbal instruction and a single demonstration by an experienced physiotherapist were provided before each exercise. No individual calibration was required beyond the standard camera setup described in the Methodology.

The AI model visualised the human body skeleton on the participant in real time, predicted joint movement, and displayed the computed angle on screen, allowing therapists to observe performance directly (Fig. 1). Methodology

Hybrid AI vision model overview

Figure 2 shows the overall workflow of the proposed hybrid AI vision model. The system utilises a single external QUAD HD camera (2560 × 1440 pixels, 4 MP, 106\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} field of view, up to 25 fps, approximate retail cost: USD 80–150), mounted on a tripod at a fixed distance of approximately 2.5 metres from the subject, at a height of approximately 1.0 metre, oriented perpendicular to the primary plane of movement for each exercise. All recordings were conducted under standardised artificial lighting in the gait laboratory with no direct sunlight exposure. The model ran on a laptop equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H CPU, 16 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 Ti GPU, implemented in Python 3.9. No dedicated GPU is required for deployment; the GPU was used here for reproducibility.

The model is designed to automatically extract key joint angles during rehabilitation exercises performed in a clinical environment, and to count correctly completed repetitions using a fuzzy inference system. The basic framework, including the CNN-MediaPipe landmark detection pipeline, has been described in detail in our prior work18. The present paper extends that framework to three exercises including an upper-limb and a frontal-plane movement, and provides the first formal ICC benchmark against a multi-camera optical capture reference system.

Pose estimation

Pose estimation was implemented using MediaPipe Pose version 0.8.9.1 (Full model variant, selected for its improved 3D landmark accuracy over the Lite variant). MediaPipe Pose employs a two-stage deep learning pipeline: a detector network localises the subject, and a landmark network, based on a lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN), estimates the 3D positions of 33 anatomical landmarks as normalised image-plane coordinates (x, y) and a relative depth estimate (z). The CNN generates joint-location heatmaps, from which landmark coordinates are extracted. The skeleton is visualised by connecting detected landmarks according to a predefined anatomical graph.

Raw landmark coordinates were smoothed using a one-dimensional Savitzky–Golay filter (window size = 5 frames, polynomial order = 2) to reduce high-frequency noise from frame-to-frame landmark jitter. Frames in which the MediaPipe visibility score for any relevant landmark fell below 0.70 were excluded from angle computation and flagged as tracking failures.

Known limitations of MediaPipe Pose: the Z-axis (depth) estimate is a learned prior from monocular video, not true stereoscopic depth, and is therefore susceptible to systematic errors for out-of-plane movements. The framework also shows sensitivity to lighting conditions, occlusions, and body proportions underrepresented in its training data. These limitations directly affect the reliability of joint angle estimates, particularly for movements with significant out-of-plane components such as hip external rotation (see “Discussion”).

The specific landmark triples used were: (i) elbow flexion: wrist–elbow–shoulder; (ii) knee extension: hip–knee–ankle; (iii) hip external rotation: hip–knee–ankle. These are standard clinical reference points for the respective exercises.

Important limitation: because angles are computed in the image plane from 2D (x, y) coordinates, they represent projections of the true 3D joint angles. For exercises with significant out-of-plane components, particularly hip external rotation, this introduces systematic underestimation of the true ROM that cannot be corrected without depth sensing. This is a fundamental constraint of single-camera 2D pose estimation and is a primary contributor to the lower reliability observed for hip rotation measures.

Fuzzy inference system for stage classification and repetition counting

After extracting joint angles \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _1(t)$$\end{document} (left limb) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _2(t)$$\end{document} (right limb), a Mamdani-type fuzzy inference system was applied to classify the exercise stage and detect repetitions. Membership functions. Each input angle is described by trapezoidal membership functions with 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} overlap between adjacent regions, defined on empirical thresholds established by piloting on three members of the research team prior to data collection:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Starting Position:}&\quad \theta > 89^\circ ,\\ \text {Low:}&\quad 61^\circ \le \theta< 89^\circ ,\\ \text {Okay:}&\quad 31^\circ \le \theta< 60^\circ ,\\ \text {Good:}&\quad 15^\circ \le \theta< 31^\circ ,\\ \text {Perfect:}&\quad \theta < 14^\circ . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}These thresholds were derived empirically for elbow flexion in healthy adults and adapted with minor adjustments for the other exercises. Fuzzy rule base. The rules mirror the expected progression of a curl-type movement:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf {R1:}}\ &\text {IF } \theta _1, \theta _2 \text { are Starting Position THEN Stage = ST,}\\ {\textbf {R2:}}\ &\text {IF } \theta _1, \theta _2 \text { are Low THEN Stage = Low,}\\ {\textbf {R3:}}\ &\text {IF } \theta _1, \theta _2 \text { are Okay AND previous Stage = Low}\\&\quad \text {THEN Stage = Okay,}\\ {\textbf {R4:}}\ &\text {IF } \theta _1, \theta _2 \text { are Good THEN Stage = Good,}\\ {\textbf {R5:}}\ &\text {IF } \theta _1, \theta _2 \text { are Perfect AND previous Stage = Good}\\&\quad \text {THEN Stage = Perfect.} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The same rule structure is applied symmetrically to both limbs. The logical structure was retained across exercises; only the membership thresholds were adjusted to reflect the expected angle ranges for knee extension and hip external rotation.

Inference and defuzzification. Mamdani inference was applied with min–max composition and centroid defuzzification. A repetition was counted when the system transitioned from Good to Perfect, ensuring completion of the full ROM before incrementing the counter.

Elbow flexion

Elbow flexion is a commonly used exercise that requires the subject to actively flex the elbow, aiming to bring the wrist as close to the shoulder as possible. The subject grasps their forearm or wrist with the opposite hand and gently applies overpressure. The flexed position is held for five to ten seconds. Figure 3 illustrates the exercise sequence. The measured angle is computed at the elbow vertex using the wrist–elbow–shoulder landmark triple.Fig. 3Elbow flexion exercise (sequence shown right to left: starting position, mid-range, end position). The overlaid skeleton and angle readout are produced by the hybrid AI model in real time.

Elbow flexion exercise (sequence shown right to left: starting position, mid-range, end position). The overlaid skeleton and angle readout are produced by the hybrid AI model in real time.

Hip external rotation

Hip external rotation (Fig. 4) is performed while seated. The subject rotates the thigh and knee outward while elevating the ankle as high as possible, keeping the knee stationary on the chair surface. The leg is then slowly lowered to the starting position. This exercise targets the muscles responsible for hip external rotation, improving joint mobility and stability. The angle is computed at the knee vertex using the hip–knee–ankle landmark triple, which serves as a proxy for the degree of tibial rotation relative to the hip.Fig. 4Hip external rotation exercise (sequence shown right to left: starting position, mid-range, end position). The knee must remain stationary on the chair throughout.

Hip external rotation exercise (sequence shown right to left: starting position, mid-range, end position). The knee must remain stationary on the chair throughout.

Knee extension

Knee extension (Fig. 5) begins in the seated position. The subject straightens the knee and extends the leg until it is parallel to the ground, without fully locking the knee. The foot is then slowly lowered, and the contralateral leg is used for the next repetition, alternating throughout. The measured angle is computed at the knee vertex using the hip–knee–ankle landmark triple.Fig. 5Knee extension exercise (sequence shown right to left: starting position, mid-range, end position).

Knee extension exercise (sequence shown right to left: starting position, mid-range, end position).

Evaluation metric: intraclass correlation coefficient

Reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). We used a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement, ICC\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2,1}$$\end{document} (single rater/measure) and ICC\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2,k}$$\end{document} (average of k measures), following Koo and Mae31. Under this model, subjects are treated as random effects and measurement methods (AI system vs. reference system) are treated as fixed effects. This model was chosen because the two measurement instruments are specific and Fixed, the AI system is a defined instrument being evaluated against a specific reference standard, rather than random samples from a population of possible instruments.

ICC values are interpreted according to Koo and Mae31: <0.50 = poor; 0.50–0.75 = moderate; 0.75–0.90 = good; >0.90 = excellent (Table 3). The target sample for ICC analysis was 140 measurements (14 subjects × 10 repetitions). Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported for all ICC estimates.

Agreement analysis: Bland–Altman method

To complement the ICC reliability analysis, agreement between the AI system and the C-Motion Visual3D™ reference was assessed using the Bland–Altman method32. Whereas ICC quantifies the consistency of ranking across subjects, the Bland–Altman method quantifies the magnitude and direction of disagreement between the two platforms on an absolute scale. For each ROM measure, we computed: (i) the mean difference (bias), defined as AI minus C-Motion, where a positive value indicates the AI system overestimates relative to the reference; (ii) the standard deviation of the differences (SD\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_d$$\end{document}); and (iii) the 95% limits of agreement (LoA), calculated as bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm \,1.96\times \textrm{SD}_d$$\end{document}32. The 95% confidence interval for the bias was computed using a t-distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom. Analyses were performed on all 140 paired observations (14 subjects × 10 repetitions) for elbow flexion and knee extension, and on 131 and 130 paired observations, respectively, for the left and right hip external rotation (owing to missing data flagged during tracking). Bland–Altman plots were produced for all six ROM measures across the three exercises.

Summary of reliability findings

Across all exercises and limbs, three consistent patterns are observed: (1) left-sided measurements consistently yielded higher ICC values than right-sided measurements, likely reflecting camera placement effects on depth estimation (discussed further below); (2) confidence intervals were wide throughout, reflecting the small sample size; and (3) no exercise or limb achieved the threshold for good reliability (ICC \ge 0.75) on the individual-measure ICC.

Discussion

This study is the first to simultaneously record the same rehabilitation exercise trials with both a single-camera AI platform and a C-Motion Visual3D™ optical motion capture system, enabling a direct, temporally aligned comparison between the two. It is also the first to formally quantify the agreement between these platforms using ICC methodology, and the first to extend the AI framework to elbow flexion, providing combined upper and lower limb coverage. Across the four exercises and bilateral limbs evaluated, individual-measure ICC values ranged from near-zero to moderate–good (0.005–0.68). The overall picture is one of feasibility in principle, but inconsistent and often poor reliability in practice, a result that is meaningful precisely because it comes from a controlled, simultaneous dual-platform study rather than an informal comparison.

Systematic bias and clinical interchangeability

The Bland–Altman analysis provides essential complementary information to the ICC results. Whereas the ICC values describe the consistency of subject ranking between the two platforms, the Bland–Altman method reveals the actual magnitude and direction of disagreement on a clinically interpretable scale.

The biases are systematic and exercise-specific. For elbow flexion, the AI system overestimated ROM by approximately 12–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$14^\circ$$\end{document} on average. In a healthy adult whose typical elbow flexion ROM is around 120–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$140^\circ$$\end{document}, this represents a systematic error of approximately 10%. The overestimation is consistent with the 2D projection effect: the projected image-plane angle is larger than the true 3D elbow angle because the forearm is rarely perfectly parallel to the camera plane throughout the movement arc. For knee extension, the bias reversed direction, with the AI system underestimating ROM by 7–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$11^\circ$$\end{document}. This is consistent with the seated geometry, in which the thigh deviates laterally from the sagittal plane during full extension, compressing the projected angle below its true 3D value. The 95% limits of agreement were wide across all measures, with a total range of approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$37^\circ$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$65^\circ$$\end{document}. This means that for any individual measurement, the AI system could plausibly differ from the C-Motion reference by up to ± 20–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$33^\circ$$\end{document}. A discrepancy of this magnitude would frequently influence a clinical rehabilitation decision, for example, whether a patient has achieved a target ROM milestone or whether exercise progression is warranted. The systematic bias component is, in principle, correctable via a platform-specific calibration offset for each exercise. However, the wide random component (reflected in the large SD of differences: 10–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$19^\circ$$\end{document} depending on the exercise) cannot be corrected by any fixed offset and represents irreducible measurement variability at the single-repetition level under the current system configuration. Taken together, the ICC and Bland–Altman findings consistently indicate that the AI system captures the direction and approximate magnitude of joint ROM, but does not yet achieve the measurement precision required for individual-level clinical decision-making.

Exercise-specific interpretation

The best performance was observed for right hip external rotation ROM, with an individual ICC of 0.68 and an average ICC of 0.81. This result is encouraging for a biomechanically complex movement that involves a sitting posture, suggesting that the AI system can track the gross arc of motion reasonably well when the movement occurs primarily in the frontal plane and is captured from a suitable camera angle. In contrast, the left hip external rotation produced poor individual reliability (ICC = 0.31), with the minimum-angle sub-measure showing essentially no agreement with the reference (ICC = 0.005). This discrepancy between left and right for the same exercise points strongly to a camera-placement effect rather than an intrinsic problem with the AI algorithm: the camera was positioned to optimise the right-sided capture angle, and the left-sided depth estimation error was correspondingly greater.

Knee extension produced poor individual reliability for both limbs (left: ICC = 0.43; right: ICC = 0.27). Knee extension is a sagittal-plane movement, which should theoretically be well suited to a single sagittal-view camera. The relatively poor performance here may reflect the seated starting geometry, in which the thigh is horizontal, and the ankle displacement from the starting position is small, making the projected angle sensitive to small errors in hip and ankle landmark placement. It may also reflect variability in trunk stabilisation that is visible to the optical capture system but not cleanly separable from the knee angle by the AI system.

Elbow flexion, the upper limb exercise introduced for the first time in this study, produced fair individual reliability for the left hand (ICC = 0.41; average ICC = 0.58) and poor reliability for the right hand (ICC = 0.21; average ICC = 0.35). The left–right asymmetry mirrors that seen for hip external rotation and is consistent with the camera-placement hypothesis. Notably, elbow flexion involves forearm movement across a broad angular range in healthy adults, which should produce a strong signal for the AI system. The moderate left-hand average ICC (= 0.58) is therefore a reasonable starting point, and suggests that with optimised bilateral camera placement, the upper limb component of the system may be improvable without algorithmic changes.

The left–right asymmetry: camera placement as the primary explanatory factor

A consistent and striking finding is that left-sided measurements outperform right-sided measurements across all three exercises. This is unlikely to be explained by handedness or biological asymmetry in healthy adults performing standardised exercises. The most parsimonious explanation is geometric: the single camera was positioned to capture the primary movement plane optimally for left-sided movements, whereas for right-sided movements, the angle of incidence between the camera axis and the limb trajectory was less favourable. For MediaPipe Pose, which infers the Z-axis (depth) coordinate from learned monocular priors rather than true stereoscopic depth, any deviation from the optimal viewing angle amplifies depth estimation error and propagates directly into the computed joint angle. This hypothesis is testable and should be the subject of a dedicated camera-placement optimisation study before any further validation work is undertaken.

The 2D projection problem for out-of-plane movements

A second systematic source of error affects all exercises to varying degrees: joint angles are computed from the 2D image-plane projection of the 3D landmarks. For purely sagittal movements captured from a perpendicular sagittal camera (e.g., elbow flexion and knee extension in profile), the projection error is minimal. For out-of-plane movements such as hip external rotation, where the thigh rotates away from the camera plane, the projected 2D angle systematically underestimates the true 3D ROM. This is not a correctable software error but a physical consequence of single-camera geometry, and it explains in part why hip external rotation showed the greatest variability across limbs. Resolving this limitation requires either a second camera capturing the complementary plane, or integration of a depth sensor.

Comparison of the two platforms

It is worth reflecting on what the dual-platform design reveals beyond the ICC values themselves. The C-Motion Visual3D™ system, with its 14 calibrated cameras and 53 retro-reflective markers, captures full 3D kinematics from multiple angles simultaneously, with sub-millimetre marker tracking accuracy. The single-camera AI system captures a 2D projection of one view, infers approximate depth from a neural network prior, and computes angles from three landmarks per joint. The ICC results quantify the gap between these two approaches under ideal conditions in healthy adults, and that gap is already substantial for several measures. This does not invalidate the single-camera approach, but it does establish a clear performance floor below which the system currently operates, and provides a quantitative target for future improvement.

Practical advantages and their current conditionality

Despite the reliability limitations, the single-camera platform offers genuine practical advantages: the camera hardware costs approximately USD 80–150, requires no sensor attachment, no laboratory infrastructure, and no specialist calibration. Real-time biofeedback and automatic repetition counting, features absent from the C-Motion system, are available immediately. These features are potentially valuable for tele-rehabilitation, home-based monitoring, and resource-limited clinical settings. However, these advantages are clinically meaningful only if the system achieves acceptable reliability in target patient populations under real-world conditions. The current evidence, from 14 healthy adults in a controlled laboratory, does not yet establish this. The practical advantages represent a motivating rationale for further development, not a justification for premature deployment.

The critical gap: patient population validation

The most important limitation of this study is that all participants were healthy adults performing exercises in a standardised, controlled environment. The target clinical population of patients with post-stroke, neurodegenerative, or post-illness deconditioning conditions presents fundamentally different movement characteristics: reduced and asymmetric ROM, compensatory postures, involuntary movements, tremor, spasticity, and variable levels of self-occlusion. Each of these factors is likely to degrade AI landmark detection accuracy and, by propagation, joint angle reliability. It is not possible to predict the direction or magnitude of this degradation from the current data. Validation in patient cohorts is therefore not merely a desirable next step; it is a prerequisite for any claim about clinical utility. The variability in ICC scores indicates that further optimisation is required to ensure consistent validity even among healthy participants. We contend that establishing a higher standard of reliability in non-clinical cohorts is a mandatory gateway that must be passed before the system can be applied to patient care.

Limitations and future work

This study has five limitations, each paired with a concrete engineering direction for future work.

Sample and scope. With n = 14 healthy adults, all ICC estimates must be treated as exploratory. Future work should recruit a well-powered healthy cohort (n \ge 80) with deliberate variation in age, BMI, skin tone, and clothing type to stress-test landmark detection robustness. This should be followed by prospective validation in post-stroke, neurodegenerative, and post-illness deconditioning patient populations, where asymmetric ROM, tremor, and compensatory postures are present.

Inadequate healthy-population baseline. The individual-measure ICC values obtained here (0.005–0.68) are insufficient even as a healthy-population baseline. The system must achieve ICC \ge 0.75 across all joints and bilateral limbs, with Bland–Altman limits of agreement narrower than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 10^{\circ }$$\end{document}, before any patient study begins. We propose this as a mandatory gateway criterion. Future modelling work should treat this threshold as a formal pass/fail benchmark during iterative model development.

Camera geometry and depth sensing. The left–right ICC asymmetry reflects single-camera placement geometry. A dual-camera configuration capturing simultaneous sagittal and frontal planes should be evaluated. More substantially, integration of a low-cost RGB-D sensor (e.g., Intel RealSense D435, approximately USD 150–200) would replace monocular depth inference with true per-pixel depth, enabling genuine 3D joint angle computation and eliminating the systematic out-of-plane underestimation observed for hip external rotation.

Pose estimation model improvements. MediaPipe Pose (version 0.8.9.1) was selected for its real-time capability, but it represents only one point in a rapidly advancing model landscape. Future work should systematically benchmark alternative architectures against the same C-Motion Visual3D™ reference: (i) transformer-based pose estimators such as ViTPose ? and TokenPose, which have demonstrated superior landmark localisation accuracy over CNN-based approaches, particularly under occlusion and non-standard viewpoints; (ii) temporal models including video-based pose estimators such as MixSTE and MotionBERT, which exploit inter-frame consistency to suppress the landmark jitter that the Savitzky–Golay filter currently addresses post-hoc; and (iii) multi-person and body-part-aware models such as MMPose with HRNet backbone, which maintain landmark precision under partial occlusion caused by clothing or chair contact during seated exercises. A unified ICC and Bland–Altman evaluation protocol, identical to that used here, should be applied to each candidate model to enable direct comparison.

Data diversity and model robustness. Conclusion

This study provides the first simultaneous dual-platform comparison between a single-camera AI-fuzzy logic system and a reference optical motion capture system (C-Motion Visual3D™, 14 cameras, 53 retro-reflective markers) for joint angle estimation in rehabilitation exercises, and the first application of this AI framework to upper limb assessment (elbow flexion), and lower limb assessment(hip external rotation, Knee extension). Individual-measure ICC values across all exercises and limbs ranged from near-zero to moderately good (0.005–0.68), with the best performance observed for the right hip external rotation (individual ICC = 0.68; average ICC = 0.81) and the weakest for the minimum-angle measure of left hip external rotation (individual ICC = 0.005) and right elbow flexion (individual ICC = 0.21).

Three technical factors account for the majority of the observed reliability variation: (1) the left–right camera-placement asymmetry, which amplified monocular depth estimation error for right-sided measurements; (2) the 2D projection error inherent to single-camera geometry, which systematically underestimates out-of-plane ROM; and (3) the small sample size (n = 14), which produced wide confidence intervals throughout. All three are addressable: bilateral camera optimisation, depth sensing or multi-view integration, and larger future studies can each directly improve on the current results.

The most important outstanding question is not technical but clinical: all participants in this study were healthy adults, and the system has not been tested in any patient population. Reliability in patients with post-stroke, neurodegenerative, or deconditioning presentations. The cause of who exhibits restricted ROM, compensatory movement, and involuntary motion is unknown and cannot be inferred from the current data. This must be established before any clinical application is claimed.

The single-camera platform offers real advantages in cost (USD 80–150 for the camera), portability, ease of setup, and built-in real-time biofeedback and repetition counting, capabilities entirely absent from the C-Motion reference system. These features make the approach genuinely promising for tele-rehabilitation and home-based monitoring, provided that future validation in patient cohorts supports adequate reliability. The present study establishes the quantitative gap that must be closed and the technical and clinical conditions under which this approach could responsibly progress to patient use. We view these findings not as a limitation of the concept, but as a precisely characterised starting point for the next stage of development.
