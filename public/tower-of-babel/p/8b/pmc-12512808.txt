Vaccine hesitancy among legal guardians of children with chronic neurological disorders: a cross-sectional study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Ahmed M Almutairi, Ahmad A Alanezi, Khalid A Almahmoud, Abdulaziz M Aljuhni, Waleed A Alluhidan, Tamim A Abahussain, Khalid Alzaraa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaccine hesitancy poses a significant public health challenge, particularly among vulnerable populations such as children with chronic neurological disorders (NDDs). Despite the proven efficacy of vaccines, limited data exist on parental attitudes toward immunization in this high-risk group in Saudi Arabia. ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the prevalence of vaccine hesitancy and its associated factors among legal guardians of children with NDDs in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2021 to September 2022 at Sultan Bin Abdulaziz Humanitarian City (SBAHC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. A total of 401 legal guardians of children aged 2–18 years with NDDs completed a validated Arabic version of the Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) questionnaire. Statistical analyses included Chi-square tests and multivariate logistic regression to identify predictors of vaccine hesitancy. ResultsThe level of vaccine hesitancy among respondents was 10.2%. Vaccine hesitancy was higher statistically among caregivers of male children (p = 0.031), young mothers (p = 0.016), educated mothers (p = 0.017), less educated fathers (p = 0.046), and low-income families (p = 0.014). Analysis showed that older maternal age (AOR = 0.446, p = 0.028), higher paternal education (AOR = 0.408, p = 0.023), and greater household income (AOR = 0.412, p = 0.035) as protective factors for hesitancy, while younger educated mothers increased the likelihood of greater hesitancy (AOR = 3.400, p = 0.001).ConclusionVaccine hesitancy among caregivers of children with NDDs in Saudi Arabia appears to be comparable to rates reported in prior regional studies. The association between higher maternal education and increased hesitancy warrants further investigation. These findings support the need for nuanced educational and communication strategies to improve vaccine acceptance among caregivers of neurologically vulnerable children.Keywords: Vaccine hesitancy, Neurological disorders, Neurodevelopmental disorders, Parental attitudes, Immunization, Saudi arabia, Pediatric vaccination

Introduction

Vaccination is undoubtedly one of the most significant accomplishments in global healthcare. Vaccination programs have been instrumental in reducing global mortality and morbidity from infectious diseases [1]. However, despite the high acceptance rates of vaccines, particularly in developed countries, vaccine hesitancy is a significant issue threatening the effectiveness of vaccination programs [2]. Although vaccination programs have received widespread acceptance, there are still concerns about potentially under-vaccinated individuals worldwide [3].

Several countries have faced the challenges of vaccine hesitancy, particularly among the vulnerable population. Reports have shown that the vaccination rate is lower among children with neurological diseases than the general population [4, 5]. Unfortunately, no studies on routine immunization among neurologically diseased children were found in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Therefore, it is crucial to study and assess vaccine hesitancy among caregivers of at-risk children in our country.

This study, tailored explicitly for Saudi Arabia’s healthcare providers and policymakers, evaluates vaccine hesitancy among legal guardians of children with chronic neurological disorders regarding the Saudi national essential vaccination schedule. The findings will help identify the factors contributing to vaccine hesitancy and provide insights into how to address them. This will help healthcare providers and policymakers develop effective strategies to improve vaccination rates among the vulnerable population and ensure that all children are protected against infectious diseases.

Methodology

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2021 and September 2022 in Sultan Bin Abdulaziz Humanitarian City (SBAHC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Data was collected by using a self-administered questionnaire. One of the legal guardians of children older than two years and under 18 who was diagnosed with a neurologic or neurodevelopment disorder (NDD) and visited outpatient clinics or were admitted to inpatient facilities at Sultan Bin Abdulaziz Humanitarian City (SBAHC) Riyadh, Saudi Arabia were approached for participation. Neurologic conditions include any disease affecting the human brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerve, or muscle and causing global developmental delay or neurodevelopmental disability (including cerebral palsy, seizure disorders, stroke, cognitive impairment, developmental delay, neuromuscular disorders, spinal cord injury, and other neurodevelopmental disorders recognized by clinical diagnosis). We excluded any child with a relative or absolute contraindication to any vaccine. The research team contacted the eligible candidates and asked about their willingness to take the survey. A trained research assistant explained the study aims and procedures and assured the anonymity and confidentiality of the provided data. After agreeing to participate in the study, the eligible participant signed the consent form and filled out the questionnaire using the Google survey platform. All the data were transferred to a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.

The estimated prevalence of pediatric neurological disorders in the Saudi population is approximately 68.5 per 10,000 children [6]. According to The Saudi General Authority for Statistics, the estimated number of children in Riyadh is around two million [6] Considering that with a margin of error and confidence level of 5% and 95%, respectively, the total number of participants needed was 377. sample size was calculated based on an estimated prevalence of 10% vaccine hesitancy among children with NDDs, with a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level and we managed to reach a total of 401 participants. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at SBAHC (approval No.51-2021-IRB).

Questionnaire

We utilized a questionnaire called The Parents Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) to measure parental vaccine hesitancy [7–9]. This validated tool consists of 15 items measuring three different aspects, including parental behavior, beliefs, and attitudes toward the vaccines. The final score is calculated based on the method described by Opel et al., which converts the scores to a scale ranging from zero to 100, in which zero indicates the lowest score of vaccine hesitancy. We used the validated Arabic version of the PACV tool, which was translated and validated by Alsuwaidi et al. in the United Arab Emirates, after obtaining written permission from the authors [10] We also included a section on sociodemographic characteristics of the child’s legal guardians.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were shown as numbers and percentages. The relationship between vaccine hesitancy and the socio-demographic characteristics of participants was conducted using the Chi-square test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software for Windows (version 26; IBM, Armonk, New York).

We conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors of vaccine hesitancy. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into the model. The regression included child’s gender, maternal and paternal education, maternal age, and household income. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Results

The PACV questionnaire results indicated that 10.2% of participants were classified as vaccine hesitant, while 89.8% were non-hesitant.

This study enrolled 401 participants. As described in Table 1, most participants who filled out the questionnaire were mothers of children (61.8%). 52.4% of the children were males. Regarding mothers’ age, 54.4% were more than 35 years old, while 47.1% of the children were less than eight years old. Most respondents were Saudis (98%) living in the city (63.8%). The mothers’ educational level was mainly bachelor’s degrees (43.9%), and a similar proportion (44.4%) of fathers’ education holders had bachelor’s degrees. In addition, 57.6% had an average monthly household income of less than 10,000 SAR.

A multivariate regression model (Table 3) found that older mothers, educated fathers, and increasing monthly household income were the independent significant predictors of vaccine non-hesitancy. In contrast, among the included variables, higher maternal education was significantly associated with increased odds of vaccine hesitancy (AOR = 3.4; 95% CI: 1.63–7.08; p = 0.001), while higher paternal education, older maternal age, and higher income were protective. This further indicates that compared to younger mothers, the odds of vaccine hesitancy among older mothers will likely decrease by at least 55% (AOR = 0.446; 95% CI = 0.217–0.916; p = 0.028). Compared to less educated fathers, the odds of vaccine hesitancy among educated fathers were predicted to decrease by at least 60% (AOR = 0.408; 95% CI = 0.188–0.886; p = 0.023). A better family’s monthly household income was significantly less likely to be associated with vaccine hesitancy (AOR = 0.412; 95% CI = 0.181–0.935; p = 0.035). In contrast, compared to less educated mothers, the odds of vaccine hesitancy among educated mothers were predicted to increase by at least 3.4 times higher (AOR = 3.400; 95% CI = 1.632–7.080; p = 0.001).

Discussion

The current study addresses a critical gap by evaluating vaccine hesitancy among legal guardians of children with chronic neurological disorders (NDDs) in Saudi Arabia, a population previously underrepresented in vaccine hesitancy literature within the Middle Eastern context. Our findings revealed a vaccine hesitancy rate of approximately 10%, consistent with previous regional studies among Arab communities that reported hesitancy rates ranging from 6.7 to 23% [10–13]. Notably, however, these earlier studies predominantly involved caregivers of healthy children, thereby underscoring the importance of exploring hesitancy specifically within vulnerable pediatric populations.

Limited data exist concerning vaccine hesitancy and coverage among children with NDDs. A study by Tillmann et al. in Switzerland noted significantly delayed vaccination schedules and lower vaccine uptake among children with chronic neurological disorders compared to healthy peers [14]. Conversely, more recent investigations, such as those by Dinleyici et al. and Yang et al., demonstrated relatively high vaccination rates among children with neurological impairments like epilepsy or cerebral palsy, comparable to general pediatric populations [4, 5]. In the United States, Smith et al. similarly reported low influenza vaccination uptake among children with neurological or neurodevelopmental disorders, underscoring that this issue is not limited to low- and middle-income countries [15]. Our findings align more closely with recent studies, suggesting vaccination hesitancy among caregivers of children with NDDs may not markedly differ from general populations. However, adherence to recommended vaccination schedules remains suboptimal across various groups, highlighting an ongoing need for targeted interventions. Further evidence comes from studies among children with rare genetic diseases [16] and children with Down syndrome [17], both of which reported lower vaccination coverage and increased parental concerns. Together, these findings emphasize that vaccine hesitancy spans multiple vulnerable pediatric populations beyond NDDs.

An intriguing and concerning finding of our study was the increased vaccine hesitancy among educated mothers. While higher education has often been associated with better vaccination adherence due to increased health literacy and awareness, our findings reflect a paradox also observed in recent studies within Middle Eastern contexts. For instance, Alsubaie et al. found that highly educated Saudi mothers exhibited greater vaccine hesitancy due to increased exposure to misinformation through various media channels, influencing their perceptions of vaccine safety [11] Similar trends were observed in studies from the UAE and Saudi Arabia regarding influenza vaccinations, where increased educational levels correlated with heightened skepticism toward vaccination safety and effectiveness [10, 13]. This suggests that educational interventions should be meticulously designed to counteract misinformation and address specific concerns raised by educated parents.

In contrast, we observed that older maternal age, higher paternal education, and greater household income were protective factors against vaccine hesitancy. Okoro et al. reported similar protective socioeconomic factors in Nigeria, where parents with higher socioeconomic status demonstrated better vaccine compliance for children with chronic neurological disorders [18] These parallels reinforce the idea that socioeconomic determinants play a significant role in vaccine attitudes and uptake across different cultural settings.

Our study’s limitations has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences from the observed associations. Second, data were collected from a single tertiary care center, which may not be fully representative of all regions or healthcare settings in Saudi Arabia. Third, although the PACV tool is validated, it relies on self-reported data, which may be influenced by social desirability bias or recall limitations. Additionally, the study assessed attitudes toward vaccines rather than actual vaccination status, which may differ in practice. Future studies should consider multi-center designs and longitudinal tracking to better understand how attitudes translate into behaviors.

In conclusion, vaccine hesitancy among guardians of children with NDDs in Saudi Arabia mirrors trends seen in the general pediatric population but highlights unique sociodemographic predictors, particularly concerning maternal education. Targeted educational interventions, strategic public health messaging, and further research are essential to addressing vaccine hesitancy effectively in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

The prevalence of vaccine hesitancy among caregivers of children with neurological disorders in Saudi Arabia was approximately 10%, which aligns with rates observed in previous regional studies. A notable finding was the higher hesitancy reported among more educated mothers, which contrasts with commonly held assumptions about the protective role of education in vaccine uptake. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring public health communication to address vaccine concerns within all socioeconomic groups, including those with higher education. Emphasizing vaccine safety during pediatric and neurodevelopmental visits may serve as an important opportunity for intervention.
