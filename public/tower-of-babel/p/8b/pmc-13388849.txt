Association Between Anthropometric Indices and Presence of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus in Obesity
Authors: Shehla Shaikh, Sambit Das, Sunil Kota, Nitin Kapoor, Sanjay Kalra, Saptarshi Bhattacharya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are global challenges, with obesity increasing risk of T2DM. Body mass index (BMI), the conventional measure of obesity, may not accurately predict metabolic risk, especially in Asian individuals. Evaluation of alternative anthropometric indices may offer additional approaches for risk assessment.MethodsThe multicenter, cross-sectional study examined whether anthropometric indices—such as waist, hip, neck, calf, and wrist circumferences, as well as waist–hip, neck–height, and waist–calf ratios—are associated with T2DM in adults with obesity. The study included 750 adults (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) recruited from four endocrinology centers across India. Anthropometric and clinical data were recorded using a standardized electronic form.ResultsT2DM was present in 73% of the cohort (81.6% of men, 67% of women). Obesity classes did not reliably predict the diabetes risk. In men, univariate analysis showed that T2DM increased with age [odds ratio (OR) 1.09, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.06–1.12, p < 0.001], higher pulse rate (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.00–1.06, p = 0.04), lower BMI (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.88–1.00, p = 0.048), higher waist–hip ratio (WHR) (OR 2.63, 9% CI 1.45–4.76, p = 0.001), and neck–height ratio (NHR) (OR 3.35, 95% CI 1.05–10.65, p = 0.041). On logistic regression analysis, age, pulse rate, WHR, and NHR independently predicted diabetes in men. In women, T2DM was more prevalent with increasing age (OR 1.09, 95% CI 10.7–1.11, p < 0.001), higher systolic blood pressure (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.06, p < 0.001), lower weight (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–1.00, p = 0.032), higher neck circumference (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.05–1.16, p < 0.001), and NHR (OR 7.83, 95% CI 3.52–17.42, p < 0.001), on univariate analysis. On logistic regression analysis, age, systolic blood pressure, and NHR retained statistical significance. Receiver operating characteristic analysis generated an optimal NHR cutoff of 0.24 (AUC 0.63) for predicting T2DM with moderate discriminatory ability.ConclusionNHR showed the strongest association with T2DM risk in both men and women, although with moderate discriminatory ability. Additionally, WHR may be associated with risk of T2DM only in men. Anthropometric measures, such as NHR and WHR, may complement BMI, but require validation in larger multiethnic cohorts.Keywords: Obesity, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Body mass index, Neck–height ratio, Neck circumference, Anthropometry

Key Summary Points

Why carry out the study? Body mass index (BMI) is widely used to classify obesity, but might not always accurately reflect body composition or regional fat distribution.South Asian individuals develop metabolic complications at lower BMI values as a result of greater visceral adiposity and reduced muscle mass.Central adiposity, assessed by waist circumference, waist–hip ratio, and neck circumference, and newer indices such as calf and wrist circumferences may provide additional information related to metabolic risk. What was learned from the study? The study reconfirms that obesity class does not reliably predict diabetes risk, underscoring limitations of BMI-based stratification in South Asian individuals.The study demonstrates that neck–height ratio is independently associated with type 2 diabetes in both men and women, but only with moderate discriminatory ability.Waist–hip ratio predicts diabetes in men but not in women, indicating there may be sex-specific differences in fat distribution and metabolic vulnerability.Anthropometric measures, such as neck–height ratio and waist–hip ratio, may complement BMI in predicting metabolic risk but require validation in larger multiethnic cohorts.

Introduction

The estimated global prevalence of diabetes in the 20–79-year age group is 588.7 million and is projected to increase to 852.5 million by 2050 [1]. The rise is closely linked to increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity, which affects nearly 37.0% of the global population [2]. The twin epidemics of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) constitute a major public health challenge [3]. Excess body weight is a key determinant of diabetes risk and is strongly linked with hypertension, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and several other non-communicable disorders [4]. Body mass index (BMI) is the standard measure for diagnosing and classifying obesity and is widely applied in clinical practice, epidemiological surveys, and public health programs [5].

However, BMI has limitations in identifying metabolic risk in individuals with obesity. It does not address variations in body composition or regional fat distribution, both of which determine metabolic health [6]. The limitations are particularly relevant in the Asian individuals, who develop T2DM and related metabolic complications at lower BMI levels compared with Western counterparts. The thin-fat Asian phenotype is characterized by increased visceral adiposity, higher insulin resistance, and lower muscle mass for a given BMI [7]. Considering these differences, lower BMI cutoffs have been recommended for overweight and obesity in Asian populations [8]. BMI therefore provides only a limited representation of adiposity and may underestimate metabolic risk in the South Asian population [9].

To surmount these shortcomings, several anthropometric measures have been explored to quantify the amount and distribution of body fat. Conventional indices such as waist circumference (WC) and waist–hip ratio (WHR) measure central adiposity and are commonly used in metabolic risk assessment [10]. Additional anthropometric parameters such as neck, wrist, and calf circumferences (CC) are markers of regional fat distribution [11]. Neck circumference (NC) reflects upper body subcutaneous adiposity and has been associated with insulin resistance [12, 13], while wrist circumference (WrC) has been linked to skeletal frame size and metabolic risk [14, 15]. CC may reflect peripheral muscle and fat pattern [16, 17].

Despite increasing interest in these indices, important gaps remain in the literature. Most studies have evaluated individual anthropometric measures rather than examining multiple indices simultaneously [12–17]. Moreover, data from the South Asian population is limited despite their characteristic body composition and high susceptibility to metabolic disease [18]. Comparative evaluation of conventional and less commonly studied parameters may provide additional understanding of predisposition to metabolic disorders.

Risk stratification is a key component of strategies to prevent and detect T2DM at an early stage. Several risk prediction tools have been developed that incorporate clinical and anthropometric variables such as age, BMI, WC, and family history of diabetes [19, 20]. Anthropometric data, particularly measures of central adiposity, are key components of these models, because of their close link to insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction [21, 22]. However, conventional measures may not be able to detect differences in regional fat distribution, especially in the South Asian population, given their higher risk profile at lower levels of adiposity. Identifying additional anthropometric parameters may improve diabetes risk stratification and assist in refining screening and preventive strategies.

The Barophenotype Assessment and Regional Variations Among People Living with Obesity in India (BHARAT) study aimed to assess the anthropometric and metabolic profiles of people with obesity from three distinct geographic regions within the country. The present sub-analysis examines various anthropometric indices in Indian adults with obesity and determines their associations with T2DM. Both central and peripheral anthropometric markers are assessed in the same population. These measurements may be particularly relevant in the South Asian population, where metabolic risk is characterized by greater visceral adiposity along with lower muscle mass.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

BHARAT was a multicenter, observational, cross-sectional study conducted between June 2022 and December 2022. A total of 750 adults with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 were recruited from four centers across India, including two from the east, one from the west, and one from the north of the country. The participating sites are tertiary care endocrinology centers serving outpatient populations. Ethical clearance was obtained from a central ethics committee (ECR/1629/Inst/MH/2021) that covered all four centers. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Informed consent was obtained from individuals fulfilling eligibility criteria and presenting to endocrinology clinics at the four centers. Data were collected using a central electronic case report form (eCRF). Trained nurses or diabetes educators completed the form after attending three online training sessions, which were conducted to ensure uniformity.

Study Participants

Consecutive adults aged 18 years and above with obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 according to World Health Organization criteria for Asian populations) [8, 23–25], with or without T2DM, were considered for inclusion. Pregnant women and individuals with forms of diabetes other than T2DM were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria were severe comorbid conditions (organ failure and advanced cancer), medical conditions such as untreated thyrotoxicosis or active tuberculosis known to alter body weight, inability to obtain anthropometric measurements because of physical constraints, and incomplete clinical or demographic data.

Sample Size Calculation

This was an observational study conducted among adults with obesity attending the clinic during the study period. As the study was exploratory in nature, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. All eligible individuals who attended the centers during the recruitment period and consented to participate were included in the analysis.

Data Collection and Measurement

After obtaining consent, demographic information and anthropometric parameters, including BMI, waist, hip, neck, calf, and wrist circumferences, were recorded, along with vital signs such as pulse rate and blood pressure. Data on dietary patterns, alcohol intake, smoking, tobacco-chewing, and comorbidities were collected. Dietary pattern was recorded as vegetarian or nonvegetarian based on self-report. Alcohol consumption, smoking, and tobacco chewing were documented as binary variables (yes or no). Information on comorbidities was obtained from self-reports and available medical records. Obesity was stratified according to the WHO Classification for the Asian population [8, 23]. Medical, mental, and functional aspects of obesity were assessed using the Edmonton Obesity Staging System (EOSS) [26].

Diabetes status was based on fulfilment of the diagnostic criteria of the American Diabetes Association (ADA) or prior physician diagnosis, or current use of glucose-lowering medication [27]. The medications included metformin, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1)-based therapy (for diabetes), thiazolidinediones, insulin, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, and meglitinides. Of note, GLP-1-based therapies, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, were not approved for obesity management in India during the study period and would not have influenced the inclusion criteria.

All anthropometric measurements were obtained using a non-elastic measuring tape, with participants standing erect, barefoot, and in light clothing. WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest at the end of normal expiration [28]. Hip circumference (HC) was measured at the level of the greatest posterior protuberance of the buttocks, ensuring the tape was horizontal and snug without compressing the skin [29]. NC was measured at a point just below the laryngeal prominence (Adam’s apple) and perpendicular to the long axis of the neck [13]. CC was measured at the widest part of each calf in the standing position, with weight evenly distributed, and the tape was moved up and down to identify the maximum girth. Both calves were measured separately, and the larger value was recorded for assessment [30]. WrC was measured around the narrowest point at the level of the Lister’s tubercle, the bony prominence on the dorsal distal radius palpable on the back of the wrist, with the participant seated or standing, forearm relaxed and palm facing upward [31].

Outcomes

The primary objective of this BHARAT sub-study was to assess the association between various anthropometric indices and T2DM in individuals with obesity. Measurements assessed were BMI, WC, HC, waist–hip ratio (WHR), NC, neck–height ratio (NHR), CC, waist–calf ratio (WCR), and WrC. Secondary objectives were to assess the association between T2DM and demographic parameters, smoking, alcohol intake, chewing tobacco use, dietary pattern (vegetarian vs. nonvegetarian), pulse rate, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Analyses were done separately for men and women, given the sex-related variability in anthropometric indices.

Statistical Analyses

The data were analyzed using R software, version 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2021; https://www.R-project.org/). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation after assessing normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons among the three obesity categories were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, depending on the expected frequencies, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted separately for men and women to identify factors associated with diabetes. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was calculated for each anthropometric measure to evaluate their discriminatory ability for identifying T2DM. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

A total of 750 adults attending the endocrinology outpatient departments at four tertiary centers across India were included. There were 352 participants (47%) from eastern India, 208 (27.7%) from western India, and 190 (25.3%) from northern India. Age Distribution and Sex Differences

The mean age of the participants was 48.2 ± 13.25 years. The mean age declined with increasing obesity severity (p < 0.001), with mean ages of 50.02 ± 12.95 years in obesity category class I and 45.25 ± 13.93 years in class III.

Women constituted 63.6% (n = 477) of the cohort, and their proportion increased significantly with obesity class (class I, 55.2%; class II, 68.1%; class III, 73.3%; p < 0.001).

Women with diabetes were older than those without diabetes (51.85 ± 11.14 vs. 39.39 ± 12.78 years). Compared with women aged ≤ 40 years, the odds of diabetes were significantly higher in those aged 41–50 years ([odds ratio (OR) 4.87, 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.86–8.30), 51–60 years (OR 11.20, 95% CI 6.18–20.30), and > 60 years (OR 12.95, 95% CI 6.31–26.6).

Men with diabetes were also older than those without diabetes (51.34 ± 12.44 vs. 37.76 ± 2.70 years, p < 0.001). Increasing age in men was associated with a higher likelihood of diabetes, with the highest odds observed in the 51–60-year age group compared with those aged ≤ 40 years (OR 11.59, 95% CI 3.85–34.87, p < 0.001).

Anthropometric Parameters Across Obesity Classes

The mean BMI of the study participants was 31.61 ± 4.6 kg/m2, higher in women than men (32.1 ± 4.64 vs. 30.7 ± 4.4 kg/m2). The mean WC measured 110 ± 14.2 cm in women and 106 ± 11.2 cm in men. WC showed a progressive increase across obesity classes in both genders (p < 0.001).

The HC showed a similar pattern across obesity classes, with women recording a mean of 114 ± 15.9 cm and men 105 ± 12.9 cm. The average WHR was 0.965 ± 0.06 in women and 1.00 ± 0.06 in men and did not differ across classes.

Mean NC was higher in men than in women across all groups. The NC was significantly higher in women (p = 0.003) across obesity classes but not in men (p = 0.469). The NHR also increased only in women (p = 0.001), but not in men (p = 0.316). CC and WCR differed significantly across groups in both sexes. In women, mean CC values ranged from 37.1 ± 3.4 cm to 41.3 ± 3.5 cm, and in men, from 37.9 ± 3.4 cm to 41.1 ± 3.9 cm, in classes I and III obesity, respectively. WrC was larger in the higher categories of obesity in men (p = 0.014) and women (p < 0.001). Anthropometric parameters across obesity classes are presented in Table 1.

Prevalence of Diabetes Across Obesity Classes

In the study cohort, diabetes was observed in 67% of women and 81.6% of men. The relative risk of diabetes in men was 1.29 [95% CI 1.16–1.43]. Among men, the highest prevalence was reported in obesity class 1 (86.1%), but did not vary significantly across classes (p = 0.093). The highest prevalence was reported in women with class 2 obesity (70.2%). Of note, the lowest prevalence of diabetes was noted in obesity class 3 in both sexes.

Factors Associated with Diabetes in Men

Higher age was associated with diabetes in men (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.12), and the odds increased across older age categories. Married men had greater risk of diabetes compared with unmarried men (OR 4.89, 95% CI 2.03–11.76). Among the anthropometric measures, WHR was significantly higher in men with diabetes (1.01 ± 0.05 vs. 0.98 ± 0.06 cm; OR 2.63, 95% CI 1.45–4.76), as was NHR (0.24 ± 0.026 vs. 0.231 ± 0.027; OR 3.35, 95% CI 1.05–10.65). Pulse rate was modestly higher in men with diabetes (88.40 ± 11.62 vs. 84.50 ± 13.49 beats per minute; OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.00–1.06). WC, HC, CC, and WrC were not associated with diabetes (Table 2). On multiple logistic regression analysis, age categories 51–60 years and > 60 years, pulse rate, NHR (×10 for analysis), and WHR remained significantly associated with diabetes (Table 3).

Factors Associated with Diabetes in Women

Table 4 depicts the clinical characteristics of women with and without diabetes. Age was strongly associated with diabetes, with increasing odds across older age groups. Married women had higher likelihood of diabetes compared with unmarried women (OR 7.87, 95% CI 3.30–18.80). Women with diabetes had higher systolic blood pressure (132.60 ± 16.22 vs. 123.46 ± 12.52 mmHg; OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.06). Among anthropometric measures, NC (37.25 ± 3.64 vs. 35.86 ± 3.96 cm; OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.05–1.16) and NHR (0.241 ± 0.025 vs. 0.228 ± 0.027; OR 7.83, 95% CI 3.52–17.42) were associated with diabetes. In contrast, BMI, WC, WHR, and peripheral measures including CC and Wrc were not. On multiple logistic regression analysis, higher age, married status, higher systolic blood pressure, and NHR (×10 for analysis) remained independent predictors of diabetes (Table 5).

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Analysis of Anthropometric Parameters

ROC analysis was conducted to assess the discriminatory ability of anthropometric parameters for identifying T2DM. The findings are shown in Fig. 1 and Table 6. Using the Youden index, the optimal cutoff point for NHR was 0.24, corresponding to an optimal criterion value of 0.2339. The model demonstrated an AUC of 0.63, indicating modest discriminatory power. At this threshold, the sensitivity was 0.65 (95% CI 0.61–0.69) and the specificity was 0.59 (95% CI 0.51–0.65). The positive predictive value was 0.80 (95% CI 0.75–0.83), while the negative predictive value was 0.39 (95% CI 0.35–0.46). The favorable likelihood ratio was 1.56 (95% CI 1.32–1.86) and the negative likelihood ratio was 0.60 (95% CI 0.51–0.71). The ROC analysis showed that NC (AUC ≈ 0.62) also demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability among the anthropometric measures evaluated. Other indices showed AUC values close to 0.5, indicating limited discriminatory performance in this population.

Discussion

This sub-analysis of the BHARAT study evaluated the association between anthropometric indices and T2DM in adults with obesity. Higher age increased the likelihood of diabetes, while measures of central and regional adiposity showed variable associations. NHR emerged as a consistent marker associated with T2DM in both sexes, whereas WHR showed an association only in men. ROC analysis demonstrated modest discriminatory ability of NHR for identifying T2DM.

The mean age of the study population was 48.16 years. Women constituted 63.6% of the participants, which may reflect differences in health-seeking behavior. The distribution across obesity classes was 43.2% in class I, 36.8% in class II, and 20.0% in class III obesity.

In this cohort, most anthropometric measures increased with higher obesity classes in both sexes, although some differences were noted. NC and NHR showed a progressive rise across obesity classes in women, whereas this pattern was not evident in men. Prior studies have reported that NC tends to show a stronger relationship with metabolic risk in women, while the association may plateau in men at higher BMI levels [32, 33]. Men generally have larger baseline neck measurements because of greater skeletal and muscular frame size, which may reduce the sensitivity of NC to incremental fat accumulation [13, 34]. Another possible explanation could be the greater accumulation of visceral and truncal fat rather than cervical fat in men [35]. Consistent with this, earlier studies have shown that obesity in men is largely characterized by visceral fat deposition, whereas women tend to demonstrate greater expansion of subcutaneous adipose tissue [36, 37].

The proportion of participants with T2DM in this cohort was high (73%). This is likely related to the recruitment setting, as participants were enrolled from endocrinology outpatient clinics and therefore may not reflect the general population. Diabetes was more frequent in men (81.6%) than in women (67%). This pattern may reflect greater metabolic susceptibility among men, while women may seek medical attention earlier during the course of obesity. The prevalence of diabetes did not increase with higher obesity classes, and the lowest proportion of T2DM was observed among individuals with class III obesity. This pattern may also reflect the clinic-based nature of the cohort, where individuals with severe obesity may present earlier for weight management.

Among men, multivariate analysis revealed that age above 50 years, higher pulse rate, and increased WHR and NHR were independent predictors of T2DM. In women, age above 40 years, married status, higher systolic blood pressure, and elevated NHR remained independently associated with T2DM. ROC analysis showed that the NHR had modest ability to discriminate T2DM, with an AUC of 0.63 and an optimal cutoff of 0.24. WHR was a determinant of T2DM in men but not in women. The difference may be attributed to distinct fat distribution patterns in men, who are more predisposed to visceral fat accumulation [37].

Our findings align with global and regional evidence supporting NC and NHR as clinically relevant markers. A 2023 meta-analysis comprising over 30,000 participants reported that each 1-cm increase in NC increased the odds of T2DM by 16%, independent of BMI and WC [38]. In an Indian cohort, both NC and NHR showed strong associations with metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular risk factors, with NHR demonstrating slightly better predictive accuracy than NC [39].

An Indian community-based study, the Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS), demonstrated a strong positive correlation between NC and WC (r = 0.837, p < 0.0001) and proposed sex-specific cutoff values for identifying abnormal NC (≥ 37 cm in men and ≥ 34 cm in women). Although NC correlated well with central adiposity, it could not fully replace WC for predicting diabetes risk, and substituting WC with NC in the IDRS yielded comparable risk stratification [40]. The proposed cutoff values for NC in IDRS were close to thresholds observed in our cohort.

CC and WrC, though were not independently associated with T2DM, are emerging metrics that require further validation. In a large analysis, inclusion of CC in defining metabolic syndrome improved the prediction of adverse outcomes [16]. A longitudinal study in Iran demonstrated that higher WrC was associated with incident diabetes in women but not in men, a trend that may indicate sex differences in skeletal frame size and insulin-related bone mass accrual [41].

WCR increased significantly across obesity classes in both sexes in our study; however, it did not independently predict T2DM. As an index combining central adiposity and peripheral muscle mass, WCR has shown predictive value for T2DM in other studies [42]. In a population-based Indian cohort, WHR and WC were stronger predictors of T2DM than BMI or overall body fat measures [43]. These inter-study variations likely reflect differences in population structure, ethnicity, sex distribution, and sample size.

Our study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions about causality between anthropometric indices and T2DM. Participants were recruited from endocrinology outpatient clinics, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Although anthropometric measurements were obtained using standardized procedures, some degree of inter-observer variation cannot be excluded. Lifestyle factors such as alcohol intake and smoking were recorded only as binary variables, and the absence of quantitative information limits evaluation of potential dose–response relationships.

Direct assessment of body composition, for example using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, was not performed and therefore anthropometric measures could not be validated against more precise measures of fat distribution. Diabetes status was based on ADA criteria or current use of glucose-lowering medication or prior physician diagnosis, which introduces the possibility of misclassification. However, GLP-1-based therapies used for weight management, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, were approved in India only in 2025, after completion of the study period, and are therefore unlikely to have influenced inclusion.

Information on glucose-lowering medications was not included in the analysis. Different classes of glucose-lowering drugs may influence body weight and fat distribution. GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2i are associated with weight reduction, whereas insulin and sulfonylureas may promote weight gain, thereby influencing anthropometric measurements. In addition, the study population likely included heterogeneous forms of type 2 diabetes, and factors such as disease duration and the presence of complications were not evaluated. Reliance on physician diagnosis may also have influenced the ascertainment and classification of diabetes type.

Despite these limitations, simple anthropometric measures such as NC and NHR have practical value in routine clinical settings. They are easy to obtain, inexpensive, noninvasive, and are less influenced by factors such as posture or recent food intake. These features make them suitable for use in primary care and community-based screening programs, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. Incorporating such indices into existing risk assessment approaches may improve identification of individuals at increased metabolic risk. Future studies with longitudinal follow-up will be important to determine whether these measures predict incident T2DM and related metabolic outcomes. Establishing sex-specific and ethnicity-appropriate cutoffs will also be necessary before wider clinical implementation.

Conclusions

In this multicenter cohort of Indian adults with obesity, anthropometric measures reflecting regional and central adiposity demonstrated variable associations with diabetes. Neck–height ratio emerged as a consistent marker associated with diabetes in both sexes, while waist–hip ratio demonstrated a link with diabetes only in men. Conventional measures such as BMI and waist circumference failed to show a relationship with diabetes. Although the discriminatory ability of neck–height ratio was modest, its simplicity and ease of measurement make it a potential adjunct for metabolic risk assessment. Further longitudinal studies are required to establish its predictive value and to determine population specific cutoff for clinical application.
