Mechanistic Insights Into the Photosensitizing Properties of the Topical Retinoid Tazarotene
Authors: Juan A Soler‐Orenes, Macarena Dillon, Gemma M Rodríguez‐Muñiz, Matilde Ansino‐Ortiz, Inmaculada Andreu, Virginie Lhiaubet‐Vallet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince the FDA approval of tretinoin in 1971, retinoids have been key in treating acne and different skin pathologies. Tazarotene (TZE), a third‐generation retinoid and prodrug, is converted in the skin to its active form, tazarotenic acid (TZA). Its structure allows absorption of UVA/UVB light, prompting concerns about potential phototoxicity, but data on the excited state properties of TZE and TZA are still lacking. This work investigates these retinoids’ behavior under UVA light exposure through a combined spectroscopic, analytical, and biological approximation. The results show that both compounds form long‐lived triplet excited states, which interact with oxygen to produce reactive singlet oxygen. Additionally, TZA interacts with tryptophan (Trp), leading to the formation of a photoproduct with a mass corresponding to a Trp/TZA cyclobutene adduct. These results correlate with reduced cell viability in fibroblasts exposed to light after treatment with TZE or TZA, indicating potential phototoxic effects. Such light‐induced reactivity may also be related to the enhanced therapeutic results observed when TZE is used alongside narrow‐band UVB phototherapy.Keywords: photoadduct, phototoxicity, singlet oxygen, topical drug, triplet excited state

Introduction

Since the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved tretinoin in 1971, retinoids alone or combined with other agents have become essential components in treating both inflammatory and noninflammatory acne [1, 2, 3]. During these last 50 years, these drugs have undergone significant development and are now prescribed for a variety of other dermatological conditions such as psoriasis, photoaging and wrinkles, and Kaposi's sarcoma [2]. Indeed, tretinoin, in its original formulation, exhibited potential for the enhancement of its pharmaceutical properties, and special attention has been paid to its long polyene sidechain that can adopt multiple configurations. This flexibility might lead to a loss of selectivity with some isomers binding to receptors other than the intended target [2]. Therefore, better stability, selectivity, and tolerability were obtained for the third generation of retinoids by replacing this flexible polyene side chain with more rigid structures (see Scheme 1), such as aromatic moieties [2]. In 1996, topical adapalene (0.1% solution, Differin) became the first of these conformationally restrained compounds to be approved for the treatment of acne [4]; and, shortly after, tazarotene gel (0.1%, Tazorac) was authorized in the market [5]. Tazarotene (TZE, Scheme 1), with the chemical name of ethyl 6‐[2‐(4,4‐dimethylthiochroman‐6‐yl)ethynyl] nicotinate, is a prodrug that is rapidly metabolized (half‐life of less than 20 min) to its free‐acid form by esterases located in the skin [6]. Then, rapid systemic elimination is ensured as tazarotenic acid (TZA, Scheme 1), which undergoes phase II drug metabolism by being readily converted into the sulfoxide and sulfone derivatives together with more polar conjugated metabolites, with a total half‐life in the organism of ca. 17–18 h [7, 8].

Chemical structures of retinoic acid (first retinoid generation), tazarotene (TZE, third retinoid generation) and its pharmacologically active metabolite, tazarotenic acid (TZA).

Its pharmacologically active metabolite, TZA, selectively binds to the retinoid acid receptors RAR β and RAR γ and exhibits only little affinity for retinoid X receptors [7, 9]. The most common adverse effects are application site irritation, erythema, and dryness [1]. However, although no photosensitivity disorders have been reported, the FDA recommends avoiding direct sun exposure and use of sunbeds during treatment [5].

In addition to its prescription for mild to moderate acne, TZE is the first receptor‐selective retinoid for the topical treatment of plaque psoriasis [7]. It can be prescribed as a monotherapy or in combination with other drugs or active compounds, such as corticosteroids [9]. Interestingly, some studies report an increased efficacy on psoriasis plaque by combining topical application of TZE with broadband‐UVB (BB‐UVB, 280–320 nm) or narrowband‐UVB (NB‐UVB, 311–313 nm) light [10, 11, 12]. The exact mechanism is still unknown, but various factors have been identified that may contribute to such improvement. In vitro studies on cultured normal human keratinocytes have suggested a synergistic effect in inhibiting cell proliferation and elevating TIG3 expression [13]. More recently, inhibition of matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13) expression in HaCaT keratinocytes and a mouse model of psoriasis was also reported [14].

Photochemical processes might also be considered as the optimization of the tretinoin structure to design the 3rd generation of retinoids has resulted in the incorporation of new UVA‐absorbing chromophores. This is an important feature as these topical drugs are exposed to sunlight due to their direct application to areas of the skin such as the forehead or the cheeks. In this context, we reported in a recent study that adapalene, another third generation retinoid, does exhibit the characteristics of an efficient photosensitizer, and thus might be considered a potentially phototoxic agent [15, 16].

The structure of TZE includes a diaryl acetylene chromophore, a conjugated system which might be promoted to its excited states after absorption of UVB/UVA light. Therefore, according to the OECD In vitro 3T3 NRU Phototoxicity Test Guidelines (Test Guideline no 432, 2019) [17, 18] for chemical compounds with a molar absorption coefficient higher than 1000 cm−1 M−1, TZE and its active metabolite TZA should also be tested to assess their phototoxic potential.

Mechanistically, photosensitization is classified into three main processes [19]. The two oxygen‐dependent mechanisms are known as Type I and Type II. The former corresponds to an electron transfer or hydrogen abstraction between the excited compound and the substrate in its ground state, and leads to the formation of radicals that further react with molecular oxygen giving rise to photoproducts. On the other side, a Type II process involves an energy transfer from the excited photosensitizer to molecular oxygen in its ground state to generate singlet oxygen (1O2) that further attack the biological substrates. In addition, an oxygen‐independent mechanism, classified as Type III, corresponds to the formation of photoproducts including the well‐known cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers, or photoadducts between the photosensitizer and the biological target. This is for example the process taking place in PUVA therapy for psoriasis treatment, during which the excited psoralen reacts through a [2 + 2] photocycloaddition with thymine to yield DNA‐photoadducts [20, 21]. Taking into account previous reports of diaryl acetylene derivative photoreactivity with alkenes [22, 23], it is interesting to investigate the potential formation of a cyclobutene adduct between TZE acetylenic bond and the thymine C5–C6 double bond, or the indole moiety of tryptophan.

Based on these observations, it is surprising that there is no information available on the excited states properties of TZE and TZA as these data are key to understand their potential action towards biological components. Here, we run a series of spectroscopic, photochemical and biological studies to evaluate the ability of the excited states of these retinoids to act as photosensitizers. We especially established the formation of long‐lived triplet excited states and their deactivation by oxygen to yield the reactive 1O2. In addition, quenching by tryptophan was observed and related to the formation of a cyclobutene adduct between the drug and the amino acid. These results are in line with the reduced cell viability when fibroblasts were irradiated after incubation with TZE or TZA. Therefore, on the one hand, our study reveals that, from a toxicological point of view, these topical retinoids might be considered potential phototoxic agents. But, on the other hand, Type II reactivity and/or formation of photoadducts can also provide some explanation on the improved efficacy of combined phototherapy using TZE with narrow‐band UVB irradiation.

Results and Discussion

Singlet Excited State Properties

The photophysical studies were run in EtOH, a solvent where both TZE and TZA are soluble. Singlet excited state characterization was performed by UV‐Vis spectrophotometry, steady‐state and time‐resolved fluorescence. As previously mentioned, a key feature of a photosensitizing drug is its ability to absorb within the UVA/UVB spectrum and/or the visible range of sunlight. In this context, measurements of the UV‐Vis absorption of TZE and TZA ethanolic solutions revealed a band with a high molar absorption coefficient (ε > 3 × 104 M−1 cm−1; see Table 1) in the biologically relevant region, with a maximum at λ A of ca. 350 and 335 nm, respectively (Table 1 and Figure 1).

Phosphorescence emission studied in EtOH glass at 77 K showed similar spectra for both compounds. The two main peaks point at ca. 505 and 543 nm for TZE, whereas the first maximum is blue‐shifted by 4 nm in the case of TZA (Figure 2). The first band was used to determine a triplet excited state energy, E T, close to 240 kJ mol−1 (see Material and Methods section and Table 2).

Further characterization of the triplet excited state properties was performed at room temperature by means of laser flash photolysis (LFP) experiments using the third harmonic of a YAG laser (λ exc = 355 nm). Transient absorption spectra revealed the presence of two bands at 420 and 640 nm for TZE in deaerated ethanol (Figure 3A). Both bands shared the same lifetime, τ T, of ca. 96 µs and were efficiently quenched by oxygen (Figure 3A, inset). Similar results were found for TZA, with two bands peaking at 420 and 580 nm and a lifetime τ T of 124 μs (Figure 3B).

Transient absorption spectrum obtained after the 355 nm laser pulse for ethanol solutions under N2 atmosphere of (A) TZA (A 355 = 0.25) and (B) TZE (A 355 = 0.35) at different time after the laser pulse. Insets: Decay at (A) 580 and (B) 640 nm.

In addition, negative transient absorption changes that were generated within the laser pulse duration were observed. This signal peaking at the respective absorption maximum of TZA and TZE was assigned to ground state depletion and an exponential recovery was observed with a growth lifetime similar to τ T (Figure S3).

Further characterization was performed to assign the triplet excited state nature of this long‐lived transient. For this purpose, triplet‐triplet energy transfer experiments were envisaged using concentrations of 13 and 16 μM for TZA and TZE, respectively. The low triplet excited state energy of these retinoids together with their extended absorption in the UVA‐UVB region, which is hardly compatible with selective excitation of the photosensitizer, does not afford the use of typical triplet energy acceptors (biphenyl, naphthalene) or donors (acetophenone, xanthone). Therefore, two highly conjugated compounds, β‐carotene (βC) and rubrene (Rb), were selected for their low triplet energy and inefficient intersystem crossing quantum yields (Φ ISC < 0.03) [24]. This last feature is key because a selective irradiation of the retinoid cannot be reached without exciting the quencher; but, the low ΦISC avoids direct population of the quencher triplet excited state and ensures that the detected triplet signal is the outcome of the triplet‐triplet energy transfer process. Addition of these quenchers results in an efficient deactivation of the retinoid transient absorption with bimolecular rate constants of ca. 8–12 and 6–7 × 108 M−1 s−1 determined from Stern‐Volmer representation of the data (see Figures S4 and S5 and Table 2). In the case of TZA/Rb solution, the disappearance of the drug signal clearly leads to the appearance of a new band peaking between 450 and 550 nm (Figure 4) and similar to that reported for the triplet‐triplet transient absorption of rubrene [25]. Based on this result and on the efficient quenching by oxygen, the transients detected for TZA and TZE were assigned to triplet‐triplet transitions. For further characterization, an approximation of the molar absorption coefficient (εT–T) was made using the singlet depletion approach, where the observed loss of ground state absorption is compared with the gain in triplet absorption (see Equation (5)) in Material and Methods for more details) [26]. The obtained εT–T values range are of ca. 31 800 and 34 100 M−1 cm−1 for TZA and TZE, respectively (Table 2). On the basis of these values, Φ ISC were estimated by means of the comparative method (see Equation (6) of the Material and Methods section) using benzophenone triplet excited state signal as standard. Low Φ ISC of 0.10 and 0.05 were found for the metabolite and its precursor (Table 2). In this context, it is important to underline that the error associated to εT–T values determination by the singlet depletion method (and consequently Φ ISC) is generally high [26].

Interaction with Biological Components

Then, photosensitizing properties of the active metabolite TZA were investigated, using thymidine (Thd) and tryptophan (Trp) as biological targets. For the former, no clear quenching of TZA triplet excited state was detected by transient absorption. By contrast, the addition of Trp produced a shortening of TZA triplet lifetime (Figure 5); a bimolecular quenching rate constant of approximately 107 M−1 s−1 was determined. In addition, the absence of Trp radical cation detection suggests that an electron transfer process between the amino acid and the excited drug is not occurring, indicating that a different deactivation pathway is taking place, such as a Type III process.

TZA triplet excited state decay at 580 nm in the presence of increasing amounts of Trp (from 0 to 0.71 mM) after laser excitation at 355 nm. Inset: corresponding Stern‐Volmer plot.

To get more insights into the photosensitization process, steady‐state experiments were performed and followed by UV‐Vis spectrophotometry and HPLC. Ethanolic solutions of TZA (40 μM) alone or in the presence of Trp (40 μM) were irradiated with UVA lamps. TZA was not photostable under both conditions (Figure S6), but the presence of Trp results in a higher consumption of the drug (Figure 6A).

These samples were then analyzed by HPLC coupled to UV‐Vis and MS detectors. As shown in Figures 6B and S7, UVA irradiation of TZA in the presence of Trp gave rise to the formation of a photoproduct with m/z of 528.1 (ESI+) or m/z 526.1(ESI−) at 13.7 min. Although the low yield of the reaction did not allow isolation of this photoproduct, the mass is consistent with the formation of a cyclobutene photoadduct resulting from a [2 + 2] photocycloaddition between the acetylenic bond of TZA and the imidazole ring of Trp. Indeed, formation of similar adducts has previously been reported in the literature for diarylacetylenes and indoles [22, 23].

Singlet Oxygen Formation

For many biological and biomedical studies, it is essential to detect and quantify the generation of singlet oxygen. This reactive oxygen species is also produced through an energy transfer process from the excited triplet of a photosensitizer, promoting molecular oxygen from its triplet ground state to its singlet excited state, generally abbreviated as 1O2. The efficient quenching of the drug triplet excited state by oxygen pointed toward the feasibility of such a process. Therefore, the quantum yield of 1O2 formation (Φ Δ) was determined by measuring its steady‐state near‐IR emission at ca. 1270 nm and using perinaphthenone as standard (Φ Δ = 0.97) [27]. The emission spectra area was registered upon excitation at 355 nm for isoabsorptive ethanolic solutions of the two compounds of interest and of perinaphthenone (Figures 7 and S8). A defined band was obtained for TZA. A quantum yield value of Φ Δ = 0.18, unexpectedly higher than Φ ISC was obtained from Equation (7) (see Material and Methods). In the case of TZE, a low intensity 1O2 emission band is overlaid by another emission, preventing an accurate determination of Φ Δ, a value close to Φ ISC was obtained. However, as aforementioned, the lack of accuracy in the determination of εT–T and consequently of Φ ISC might explain these peculiar outcomes.

Cellular Phototoxicity Assessment

In this context, the 3T3 Neutral Red Uptake (NRU) phototoxicity assay is a well‐established and validated in vitro method to assess basal phototoxicity by measuring the ability of viable cells to incorporate and retain the neutral red (NR) dye, which serves as a reliable indicator of cell viability and membrane integrity. Accordingly, BALB/c 3T3 mouse fibroblasts were used to quantify cell viability based on lysosomal NR accumulation. Importantly, this assay allows the evaluation not only of drug‐induced phototoxicity but also of the phototoxic potential of major metabolites generated following biodistribution, providing a more comprehensive assessment of photo‐related safety. The 3T3 NRU assay is endorsed by the OECD (Test Guideline 129) [17, 18] and is widely applied in chemical safety evaluation as a non‐animal alternative in medicinal chemistry research. Thus, the phototoxic potential of a compound is estimated from the photoirritation factor (PIF), defined as the ratio of the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) obtained under dark conditions to that obtained following irradiation (see Equation (8) in Materials and Methods). According to OECD guideline 432, a compound is classified as phototoxic when the PIF is higher than 5, non‐phototoxic when the PIF is lower than 2, and probably phototoxic when the PIF falls between 2 and 5. Accordingly, BALB/c 3T3 fibroblasts were treated with increasing concentrations of TZE (0.03–700 μM) or TZA (0.03–900 μM) and either exposed to UVA irradiation (5 J cm−2) or maintained in the dark. Cell viability was then assessed by measuring the absorbance at 540 nm associated with NR uptake by viable cells. Control experiments were conducted using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and chlorpromazine (CPZ) as established non‐phototoxic and phototoxic reference compounds, respectively (Figure S9).

The IC50 values for TZA (IC50 (dark) = 171 ± 1.42 μM); IC50 (UVA) = 7.50 ± 1.50 μM) were determined from the dose‐response curve (Figure 8), yielding a calculated PIF value of ca. 23; therefore, TZA can be classified as potentially phototoxic. In contrast, TZE exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity upon UVA irradiation as evidenced by (IC50 (UVA) = 0.242 ± 1.3 μM; Figure S10). However, the IC50 value in the absence of UVA light could not be reliably determined due to solubility limitations that prevented the achievement of near‐complete cell death within the tested concentration range (Figure S10). As a consequence, calculation of a robust PIF value for TZE is not feasible. Therefore, although UVA irradiation clearly increases the cytotoxic potency of TZE, its phototoxic classification should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study provides a comprehensive photophysical and photobiological characterization of TZE and its active metabolite, TZA. Both compounds exhibit absorption in the UVA/UVB range and display the typical behavior of excited state photosensitizers, including the population of very long‐lived triplet states and the subsequent generation of singlet oxygen, which can further lead to Type II oxidation processes. Moreover, the ability of the excited state to undergo photoreaction with tryptophan, leading to the formation of covalent adducts, suggests a contribution from oxygen‐independent Type III pathways. These findings provide insights into the molecular basis of the previously unexplained phototherapeutic synergy reported in clinical protocols combining topical tazarotene with NB‐UVB irradiation, supporting the involvement of its active metabolite, TZA. Importantly, the same photochemical properties that may contribute to enhanced therapeutic efficacy also raise concerns regarding potential phototoxic effects, particularly under uncontrolled sunlight exposure. Overall, this dual behavior underscores the relevance of a careful evaluation of the excited state reactivity of topically applied drugs and their active metabolites, not only for assessing their safety but also for harnessing their potential in light‐assisted dermatological therapies.

Material and Methods

Chemicals

Tazarotene (TZE) and tazarotenic acid (TZA) were purchased from BLD Pharmatech GmbH and were used without further purification after checking their purity by 1H NMR. Ethanol (spectroscopic grade UVASOL) was obtained from Merck. Neutral red‐based in vitro phototoxicology assay kit, chlorpromazine hydrochloride (CPZ), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), perinaphthenone (PN), neutral red solution, L‐tryptophan and thymidine were supplied by Merck. For cell culture experiments, Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) were supplied by Invitrogen. Phosphate buffer saline (PBS; 0.01 M, pH 7.5) was prepared by dissolving Sigma tablets in an appropriate volume of distilled and sterilized water. CPZ and SDS stock solutions were dissolved in ultrapure water (Milli‐Q).

Irradiation Set‐up for 3T3 NRU Phototoxicity Assay

All UVA irradiations were carried out with an LCZ‐4 photoreactor fitted with six top and eight sides Hitachi lamps (λ max = 350 nm, Gaussian distribution), which emit 94% UVA radiation and 2% UVB radiation. To reduce the direct effect of UVB radiation over the cell cultures, samples were irradiated through the lid of the 96‐well transparent plates, which does not absorb beyond 310 nm.

Cell Culture Conditions

BALB/c 3T3 mouse fibroblasts (RRID CVCL_0184 were purchased from Merck) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 4 mM L‐glutamine and penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL, and 100 µg/mL) in a CO2 incubator at 37ºC (100% relative humidity, 5% CO2). Cell lines were grown using 75 cm2 plastic flasks and periodically split at 90% of confluence. Cell viability was routinely checked by trypan blue exclusion assay.

Data Analysis and Statistics

Results are presented unless indicated otherwise as mean ± standard deviation (SD) obtained from at least three independent experiments. Regression methods were developed using the GraphPad 5.0 software.

Absorbances of the samples were measured with a single beam Cary 60 Scan (Varian) UV‐vis model spectrophotometer, using 1 cm pathway quartz cuvettes.

Phosphorescence Emission

Phosphorescence spectra were recorded at 77 K using a FLS1000 system equipped with a 60 W flash Xenon lamp as excitation source and, for detection a Czerny‐Turner monochromator with 2 × 325 mm focal length and a PMT 980 in a cooled housing. Solutions of TZE and TZA were prepared in EtOH, adjusting their absorbance at ca. 0.5 at the excitation wavelength λ exc = 350 nm for TZA and 365 for TZE in EtOH with a cuvette of 1 cm optical pathway. Then, experiments were performed in a quartz tube (0.5 mm of diameter) using a quartz Dewar cooled at 77 K with liquid nitrogen. The signals were acquired with a delay time of 0.2 ms and a gate time of 5 ms. Triplet excited state energies (E T) were determined using Equation (1); in this case, the λ 0–0 wavelength corresponds to the maximum of the first vibrational band of the phosphorescence spectrum (λ P, Table 2).

Laser Flash Photolysis

Laser flash photolysis experiments were performed by exciting at 355 nm, using the third harmonic of a pulsed Nd:YAG laser (L52137V LOTIS TII) with a pulse duration of 6–8 ns. The full system comprises a pulsed laser, a xenon lamp (Lo 255 Oriel), a monochromator (Oriel 77200), a photomultiplier (Oriel 70705), and an oscilloscope (TDS‐640A Tektronic). The output signal from the oscilloscope was transferred to a personal computer. All experiments were performed in a quartz cell of optical path length 1 cm. Absorbance of ca. 0.25 for TZA and 0.35 for TZE at the excitation wavelength was used, and measurements were performed using 1 cm pathway cuvettes under air, N2, or O2 atmosphere. Laser intensity of ca. 10 mJ per pulse was applied.

Steady State Photolysis

Stock solutions of tazarotenic acid (TZA, 0.618 mM) and tryptophan (Trp, 1.57 mM) were prepared in ethanol. Two working solutions (TZA alone and TZA with Trp) were prepared by mixing 190 μL of the TZA stock solution with or without 75 μL of the Trp stock solution and adjusting the final volume to 3.00 mL with ethanol. Prior to irradiation, the solutions were purged with nitrogen for 10 min to remove dissolved oxygen. Samples irradiations were carried out with an LCZ‐4 photoreactor fitted with eight sides Hitachi lamps (λ max = 350 nm, Gaussian distribution), which emit 94% UVA radiation and 2% UVB radiation.

HPLC‐MS

Irradiation mixtures were analyzed using an HPLC system composed of Shimadzu modules: a DGU‐405 degassing unit, a LC‐40D xs solvent delivery module, a SCL‐40 system controller, SIL‐40C xs autosampler, a CTO‐40C column oven, an SPD‐M40 Photodiode array detector, and a Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer LC 840 model. A Mediterranean C18 (25 × 0.46 cm, 5 μm) column was used for all the analyses. The analysis was run under gradient conditions for 20 min from a mobile phase of 80% acetonitrile and 20% H2O, both acidified with 0.1% HCOOH to reach 95% of acetonitrile, these conditions were maintained for 20 min; the flow rate was set at 0.8 mL min−1. Analyses were performed using electrospray in positive mode with a capillary voltage of 4.5 kV. The following MS parameters were used: nebulizing gas flow, 3 L min−1; drying gas flow, 15 L min−1; interface temperature, 350°C; desolvation line temperature, 250°C; and heating block, 400°C. The data were processed using Lab Solutions software.

In Vitro 3T3 Neutral Red Uptake (NRU) Phototoxicity Assay

The in vitro 3T3 NRU phototoxicity test was carried out following the OECD Guideline 432 [17, 18] including minor modifications described previously [29]. The positive phototoxic and negative non‐phototoxic controls used in this study were chlorpromazine (CPZ) and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), respectively. For each compound (controls or retinoids), two 96‐well plates were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells/well. On the following day, 3T3 cells were incubated for an hour in dark conditions with retinoid at eight concentrations. Due to solubility limitations, different concentration ranges were used: 0.03–700 μM for TZE and 0.03–900 μM for TZA. The assays were performed in sextuplets. Then, one plate was kept in the dark whereas the other was irradiated (see irradiation setup description above) on ice using a non‐cytotoxic dose of UVA equivalent to 5 J/cm2. Afterward, solutions containing the retinoids or the control compounds were replaced with freshly DMEM medium and plates were further incubated overnight. The third day, neutral red solution (50 μg/mL) was added into the wells and incubated for 2 h at 37ºC. Cells were then washed once with PBS, and neutral red was extracted from lysosomes in 100 μL of the extraction buffer composed of distilled water 50% (v/v), ethanol 49.5% (v/v) and acetic acid 0.5% (v/v). Finally, the absorbance was read at 540 nm on a Synergy H1 microplate reader, and dose‐response curves were established for TZA and TZE and the control compounds to determine the concentration reducing a 50% the neutral red uptake (IC50) under dark and UVA light conditions.
