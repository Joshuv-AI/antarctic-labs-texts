Clinical and molecular dissection of CAR T cell resistance in pancreatic cancer
Authors: M Angela Aznar, Charly R Good, Julie S Barber-Rotenberg, Sangya Agarwal, Wesley Wilson, Alex Watts, Zhen Zhang, Donna Gonzales, Greg Donahue, Wei-Ting Hwang, Austin K Rennels, Andrew J Rech, Shunichiro Kuramitsu, Hua Huang, Karl M Glastad, Katherine A Alexander, Gabriela Plesa, Emily Dowd, Andrea Brennan, Donald L Siegel, Janos Tanyi, Andrew Haas, Drew A Torigian, Gregory Nadolski, Vanessa E Gonzalez, Elizabeth O Hexner, Joseph A Fraietta, Julie K Jadlowsky, Regina M Young, Shelley L Berger, Carl H June, Mark H O’Hara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPatients with advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) have a median survival of less than a year, highlighting the urgent need for treatment advancements. We report on a phase 1 clinical trial assessing the safety and feasibility of intravenous and local administration of anti-mesothelin CAR T cells in patients with advanced PDAC. While therapy is well tolerated, it demonstrates limited clinical efficacy. Analyses of patient samples provide insights into mechanisms of treatment resistance. Single-cell genomic approaches reveal that post-infusion CAR T cells express exhaustion signatures, including previously identified transcription factors ID3 and SOX4, and display enrichment for a GZMK+ phenotype. Single knockout of ID3 or SOX4 enhances efficacy in xenograft models, though with donor-dependent variability. However, single-knockout cells eventually fail. Conversely, ID3 and SOX4 double-knockout CAR T cells exhibit prolonged relapse-free survival, demonstrating a sustained therapeutic effect and a potential avenue for engineering more potent CAR T cells in PDAC. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03323944).Keywords: CAR T cells, clinical trial, pancreatic cancer, T cell dysfunction, immunotherapy, T cell exhaustion, single-cell RNA-seq, cancer, ID3, SOX4

Introduction

Combination chemotherapy is the standard of care for metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), but the overall benefit is marginal, and PDAC remains one of the most lethal malignancies.1,2,3 Furthermore, despite the success of immune checkpoint blockade4,5,6 and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy7,8 in various other cancers, their efficacy in PDAC remains limited. Mesothelin (MSLN) is an attractive target for CAR T cell therapies for solid tumors given its overexpression in numerous cancers, including PDAC.9,10,11,12 Moreover, elevated MSLN levels correlate with a worse prognosis in patients with PDAC10,13 and are implicated in tumor progression.14,15

Our group has performed several phase 1 clinical trials evaluating MSLN-directed CAR T cells. First, we treated patients with chemotherapy-refractory metastatic PDAC with T lymphocytes electroporated with the murine anti-human MSLN ScFv SS-1 mRNA. This yielded a transient expression of the CAR and was associated with stable disease as the best response in two of five patients treated.16 A follow-up study evaluated the stably transduced SS-1-based CAR in patients with malignant pleural mesothelioma, ovarian carcinoma, and PDAC (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02159716). While this treatment was tolerated, there was limited efficacy as the best response was stable disease in 11 out of 15 patients, including two out of five patients with PDAC.17 In the phase 1 clinical trial described here, we evaluated a fully human anti-MSLN CAR construct (huCART-meso) in patients with histologically confirmed metastatic PDAC.

Current challenges limiting the efficacy of CAR T cell therapy for solid tumors include a hostile tumor microenvironment (TME), tumor heterogeneity, poor CAR T cell trafficking, T cell exhaustion and persistence, and the need for robust tumor-specific targets.18 Chronic antigen exposure or a suppressive TME leads to exhaustion characterized by dysfunction of effector responses, sustained co-inhibitory receptor expression, and reprogrammed transcriptional and epigenetic states.19,20,21 This dysfunction contributes to negative feedback mechanisms of T cell activation,22 ultimately leading to diminished function.23 Likewise, CAR T cells are susceptible to exhaustion following prolonged stimulation within the TME, thereby limiting their function.24,25,26 In this regard, CD19 CAR T cells have demonstrated exhaustion phenotypes in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and B cell lymphomas that do not respond to therapy.24,27

Developing synthetically enhanced CAR T cell therapies for solid tumors offers a promising strategy to overcome CAR T cell dysfunction.28 Targeting transcription factors that regulate exhaustion has shown efficacy in reducing T cell receptor and CAR T cell exhaustion in vitro and in preclinical models.26,29,30,31 In our previous work, we developed an in vitro model to generate exhausted MSLN-directed CAR T cells and showed that knocking out the transcriptional regulators inhibitor of DNA binding 3 (ID3) or SRY-box transcription factor 4 (SOX4) in CAR T cells increased their effector function upon chronic stimulation in vitro.26

Here, we report the clinical results of a phase 1 study of huCART-meso cells in patients with metastatic PDAC. To better understand responses, we report correlative analyses of blood, ascites fluid, and paired pre- and post-treatment biopsies of assessable metastases. We coupled these results with in vivo mouse models using ID3 and SOX4 knockout (KO) CAR T cells and high-throughput genomics experiments to provide insight into mechanisms of resistance to CAR T cell therapy for the treatment of patients with PDAC. We found that double KO of ID3 and SOX4 in CAR T cells improves long-term relapse-free survival in vivo, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy.

Results

Efficacy of huCART-meso cell therapy is limited

This was a phase 1, single-institution trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03323944) to evaluate the safety and feasibility of both intravenous (i.v.) administration and local delivery of lentiviral-transduced huCART-meso cells in patients with histologically confirmed unresectable or metastatic PDAC who had received at least 1 prior line of systemic chemotherapy. Patients received 1–3 × 107/m2 huCART-meso cells via i.v. infusion (cohort 1), and the protocol was subsequently amended to include intraperitoneal (i.p.) (cohort 2) or intrahepatic (i.h.) delivery (via hepatic arterial infusion) (cohort 3). Patients infused on cohorts 2 and 3 were also eligible for up to two additional infusions of huCART-meso cells via i.v. administration at the same dose level, given between 21 and 42 days apart (Figure 1A). Three patients were infused in cohort 1, two patients were infused in cohort 2, and four patients were infused in cohort 3. Clinical and disease characteristics for the 9 patients infused with huCART-meso cells are listed in Table S1. The median age at study entry was 61 years (minimum 45 years to maximum 74 years) with three male (33.3%) and six female (66.7%) patients. All infused subjects were Caucasian. All patients (100%) previously received chemotherapy, while 2 (22.2%) were treated with surgery, 2 (22.2%) received targeted therapy, 2 (22.2%) received radiation therapy, and 1 (11.1%) received immunotherapy.

All patients received the protocol-specified dose of huCART-meso cells. All adverse events are summarized in Table 1. In cohort 1, we observed pulmonary toxicity in patients with moderate pulmonary metastatic burden, thought to be in part related to a first pass effect as we have published previously.32 To mitigate this first pass effect and potentially enhance trafficking, we shifted to regional delivery for cohorts 2 and 3. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) was observed in one patient in cohort 1 and one patient in cohort 2; the highest CRS grade was 2. Neurotoxicity was not observed. The infused huCART-meso cells were tolerated and generally safe with no related adverse events above grade 3. All subjects died before transitioning to long-term follow-up. For cohort 1, the three infused subjects were withdrawn from the study prior to transitioning to hospice. Clinical activity of huCART-meso in metastatic PDAC was limited. All nine subjects received the target dose of huCART-meso cells and were thus considered evaluable for response. In cohort 1 (i.v.), the best response was stable disease by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST1.1)33 in 1 of 3 patients at the day 28 scans, but progressive disease was noted on scans at month 3 (Table 2). In cohort 2 (i.p.) and cohort 3 (i.h.), all patients had progressive disease by the day 28 scans. Median overall survival from infusion to date of death or date of last contact was 6.7 weeks (Figure 1B), and median progression-free survival was 3.9 weeks (Figure 1C).

Response data of patients

The post-infusion kinetics of peripheral huCART-meso cell expansion and persistence are displayed in Figure 1D (separated patient data in Figure S1A). huCART-meso cells were detected in all patients by quantitative PCR (qPCR) amplification of CAR-specific sequences in the genomic DNA. While peak levels of huCART-meso cells in circulation were similar, the persistence of huCART-meso cells in the blood was greater when CAR T cells were administered i.v. compared to local delivery. huCART-meso cells were also detected in biopsy and peritoneal fluid cells between days 13 and 26 in patients from all three cohorts (maximum expansion in Figure 1E and expansion per day in Figure S1B). Three patients (05, 06, and 09) on the local delivery cohorts received a subsequent i.v. infusion(s) of huCART-meso cells, but no additional expansion of CAR+ cells occurred in circulation after this second infusion, and persistence dropped below the level of detection within 5 days in two of the three patients.

The kinetics of serum cytokines for 31 different analytes were assessed (Figure S1C) with further evaluation of six cytokines (IFNγ, interleukin [IL]-6, IL-2, IL-8, IL-10, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor [GM-CSF]) and the soluble IL-2 receptor, comparing the fold changes in cytokine production from baseline (pre-CAR T cell infusion) to the peak cytokine expansion (Figure 1F). Two patients in cohort 1 experienced a greater than 500-fold change in levels of IL-6 between pre- and post infusion; of these, one experienced grade 2 clinical CRS. In cohorts 2 and 3, minimal changes in the levels of various analytes were observed between pre- and post-infusion time points, consistent with the low peak expansion of cells.

Cellular analyses reveal a cold-tumor phenotype and limited T cell infiltration in the metastases of locally infused patients

Tumor tissue from metastatic lesions and peritoneal fluid were collected in patients for further analysis (Table 3). Fine needle aspirates of metastatic lesions were collected pre- and post therapy to interrogate the underlying mechanisms of resistance to CAR T cell therapy in PDAC. Flow cytometry was performed on cells isolated from treatment biopsies of patient 05 (cohort 2) and liver metastasis from patients in cohort 3, except for patient 08, from whom day 14 post-infusion sample was not collected for clinical reasons. We monitored changes in T cells (CD3+/CD4+ and CD8+), natural killer (NK) (CD56+CD3−), NK T (CD56+CD3+), B cells (CD19+CD3−CD45+), monocytes (CD14+CD3−CD45+), and erythroid progenitor (CD45+CD235a+) subsets and surface CAR expression on T cells and MSLN expression on EpCAM+ cells (Figure S2A). We observed low CD3+ T cell infiltration at baseline and at day 14 post infusion indicating a persistent cold-tumor phenotype (Figures S2B and S2C). However, despite low infiltration, in patients from whom day 14 post-infusion samples could be collected, there was an increase in the CD3+ population compared to baseline (Figure S2C) (p = 0.0124, p = 0.0392, and p = 0.0159 for patients 05, 06, and 09, respectively). Despite this increase in T cells, flow cytometry analysis using a CAR-specific anti-idiotypic antibody to detect surface CAR expression revealed no CAR staining in the metastatic tissue (Figure S2D and data not shown for patient 07 due to complete absence of immune cells in the biopsy sample analyzed). This observation correlated with the low CAR vector copy number detected in the blood and liver tissue from cohort 3 patients at the same time points (Figure S2E). Interestingly, in contrast to flow analyses in patient 06 (Figure S2D), qPCR detection of CAR revealed high levels of CAR T cells in the peripheral blood and in the day 14 liver biopsy (Figure S2E), potentially indicating CAR downregulation in the tissue and blood.26,34

MSLN protein levels were assessed in patients 05, 07, and 09 by flow cytometry (Figures S2F and S2G). We detected decreased MSLN+EpCAM+ viable tumor cells in post-infusion samples in two out of three patients (Figures S2F and S2G). huCART-meso cells acquire an exhausted T cell phenotype after infusion into the ascites fluid

To better characterize the intratumoral huCART-meso cells, we collected ascites fluid from patients 04 and 05 (cohort 2) following i.p. infusion (Figure 2A). Notably, despite the local delivery of huCART-meso cells into the peritoneal fluid, we observed either low levels of T cells expressing the CAR on the surface at day 7 (Figure 2B) or no surface CAR expression on T cells (Figure S2H) although the transduction efficiency of the corresponding products was 39.5% and 22.0%, respectively. This observation aligned with low levels of CAR copies detected in the blood and peritoneal fluid of both patients (Figures 2C and 2D), indicating low persistence of the infused CAR T cells in the peritoneal fluid of these patients. Next, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on CD3+ T cells sorted from the peritoneal biopsies on days 7 and 26 post CAR T infusion and integrated the samples for analysis (Figures S2I and S2J). Canonical marker genes were used to identify T cell subsets (Figure 2E). CD4 T cell subsets clustered on the left, and CD8 T cell subsets clustered on the right (Figure 2E). In the CD4 population, we detected regulatory T cells (Tregs; FOXP3, IL2RA, and IKZF2),35 naive T cells (SELL, CCR7, and LEF1),35,36 and effector memory T cells (Tem; IL7R and KLRB1)37 (Figure 2E). In the CD8 population, we detected memory T cells (CCR7, LEF1, SELL, IL7R, and TCF7),38 Tem (IL7R, KLRG1, and NKG7),39,40 GZMK+ effector memory T cells (GZMK+ Tem; GZMK, NKG7, PRF1, GZMH, and CCL5),41 terminally differentiated Tem (NKG7, PRF1, GZMH, and CCL5),42 and NK-like T cells (NK-like; NCAM1, FCGR3A, GNLY, GZMB, KLRC1, and KLRC2)41 (Figure 2E). We additionally detected a CD3+ cluster (defined as unknown) that uniquely expresses several calcium-binding proteins (S100A8 and S100A9) and complement proteins (C1QB and C1QA). While the peritoneal fluid consists of mostly endogenous T cells, we detected T cells expressing huCART-meso RNA in the TME via scRNA-seq using ALRA imputation (Figure 2F). We detected 380 huCART-meso-positive cells (CARpos) in patient 04 and 157 cells in patient 05. Next, we downsampled the endogenous CAR-negative (CARneg) dataset to have an equal number of CARpos and CARneg T cells. We examined the differences in T cell subset distribution between CARpos and CARneg T cells and found that CARpos cells were enriched in the CD8 GZMK+ Tem cluster and depleted in the CD4 naive cluster (Figure 2G). Together, our data characterizing T cells in malignant ascites suggest that CAR T cells post infusion display a unique T cell distribution profile that differs from endogenous CARneg T cells; however, increased replicates would strengthen this dataset.

In our previous study,26 we identified a 30-gene dysfunction signature in huCART-meso cells chronically exposed to tumor antigen in vitro. Strikingly, 19/30 of the dysfunction signature genes were highly expressed in CARpos cells compared to CARneg cells in the patient samples, including the transcription factors SOX4 and ID3 (Figure 2H). Of note, NK receptor genes (KLRB1, KLRC1, KLRC2, and KLRD1) were not upregulated in the patient CAR T cells (Figure 2H), agreeing with earlier data (see Figure 2G). Other T cell exhaustion genes not described in our 30-gene signature were increased in the patient CARpos T cells (CTLA4, TIGIT, and PDCD1) (Figure 2I, select dysfunction signature genes also shown for comparison). Taken together, huCART-meso cells obtained from patient biopsies post CAR T cell infusion express common T cell exhaustion markers and the huCART-meso dysfunction signature as previously described,26 including the transcription factors ID3 and SOX4. This finding indicates that the in vitro model replicates many but not all features of CAR T cell dysfunction in vivo.26

Donor characteristics influence the in vivo efficacy of single ID3 or SOX4KO huCART-meso cells

Our previous findings systematically investigated the drivers of CAR T cell exhaustion; we found that the transcriptional regulators ID3 and SOX4 were upregulated following chronic antigen exposure and that genetic deletion of each transcription factor demonstrated improved cytotoxicity compared to wild-type (WT) CAR T cells in chronic activation settings in vitro.26 Since huCART-meso cells isolated from the peritoneal fluid of locally infused patients upregulated ID3 and SOX4, and due to their significant upregulation in exhausted CAR T cells and prior validation in vitro,26 we hypothesized that the acquisition of a dysfunctional phenotype in CAR T cells occupying the TME might contribute to the observed lack of efficacy in the clinic.

To evaluate whether ID3KO and SOX4KO huCART-meso cells have improved in vivo killing, we manufactured huCART-meso ID3 or SOX4KO cells from healthy donor T cells with a high editing efficiency (Figures S3A and S3B). KO CAR T cells showed no differences in viability, population doublings, or cell volume compared to WT huCART-meso cells (Figure S3C). To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of these KO CAR T cells in a xenograft mouse model, suboptimal doses of WT or KO huCART-meso cells were i.v. injected once tumors reached ∼250 mm3 (Figure 3A), allowing a stringent challenge of the CAR T cells in vivo. Although improved responses were observed in KO CAR T cell groups in some donors (Figure 3B, suboptimal doses for ND561 were 1 × 106 CAR T/mouse), without significant differences in body weights (Figure 3C), the observed therapeutic benefit was highly variable among CAR T cell products. Intriguingly, SOX4KO CAR T cells consistently demonstrated better antitumor efficacy compared to ID3KO CAR T cells (Figure 3B). Thus, we further explored this variability in antitumor efficacy in SOX4KO huCART-meso cells compared to WT (Figure 3D). CAR T cell products were classified into good, intermediate, and non-responders based on the improvement observed with SOX4KO CAR T cells (Figure 3E). Experiments with donors of different categories were performed together to rule out cofounding effects in the mouse colony. Curative doses were repeated in independent experiments to rule out batch effects on CAR T cells. Various factors were evaluated to identify associations with in vivo primary response across donors, including cell source (frozen vs. fresh), total cells injected, population doublings at manufacturing, surface CAR expression, age, and reported gender, among others. However, none reached statistical significance (Table S2). Additionally, to investigate if the memory phenotype of the manufactured product was associated with in vivo primary responses, we measured CCR7/CD45RO phenotypes using flow cytometry (Figure 3F) and found no association. Similar distributions were observed across the different subpopulations between the WT and KO groups; however, there was variability across donors within the effector memory (EM) and central memory (CM) subpopulations denoted by the wide sample distribution (Figure 3F).

Taken together, single SOX4 and, to a lesser extent, single ID3KO CAR T cells improve efficacy in vivo. However, the observed variability underscores the complex interplay of CAR T cell product characteristics, including donor variability influencing therapeutic outcomes upon ID3 or SOX4KO.

Mice injected with single KO of SOX4 or ID3 eventually experience tumor recurrence

Some WT and KO huCART-meso-treated mice that initially cleared tumors relapsed at the primary tumor site, beginning approximately 2 months after CAR T infusion (Figures S3D and S3E). To dissect factors contributing to the therapeutic failure of CAR T cells, we worked by exclusion to analyze relapsing tumors from mice treated with WT or SOX4KO huCART-meso cells (Figure 4A). T cells infiltrating the recurrent tumors displayed ∼98% editing efficiency at the SOX4 locus, excluding the possibility of low persistence of the KO cells (Figure 4B). Next, we analyzed recurrent tumor-infiltrating human T cells (TILs) in recurrent tumors using flow cytometry, from donor ND561, which was a good responder (see Figure 3E, left). Notably, abundant TILs were detected within the tumors, ruling out issues related to non-persistence (Figure 4C). Recurrent tumors from mice initially injected with SOX4KO huCART-meso cells may show a slightly higher level of T cell infiltration compared to WT controls (Figure 4C, left; Figure S4A). Interestingly, both WT and SOX4KO CAR T cells demonstrated reduced levels of surface CAR expression compared to the infused product (Figure 4C, right), suggesting surface CAR downregulation; however, SOX4KO CAR T cells from recurrent tumors had a higher number of surface CAR+ T cells than WT (Figure 4C, right). Furthermore, surface expression of MSLN on recurrent tumor cells was confirmed, ruling out the possibility of escape due to antigen loss, although more MSLN−EPCAM+ cells were found in the SOX4KO CAR T-treated recurrent tumors (Figure 4D).

To investigate mechanisms underlying tumor recurrence, we sorted the CD8+ T cells infiltrating the relapsing tumors and performed scRNA-seq. Samples were plotted onto uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) space, and integrated samples did not show major differences in cluster distribution (Figures 4E and S4B). Next, we identified T cell subsets using the expression of canonical T cell marker genes (Figure 4F). Seurat identified 11 cell clusters that broadly fit into two main T cell subsets: an NK-like T cell exhaustion subset dominated by cluster 2 (Figure 4F, NK-like Tex; on left) and a GZMK+ T cell exhaustion subset that consists of all the remaining clusters (Figure 4F, GZMK+ Tex; on right). All clusters expressed high levels of canonical T cell exhaustion markers (LAG3, HAVCR2, TOX, and TIGIT) (Figure S4C) and lacked expression of memory markers (IL7R and CCR7) (Figure S4D). The GZMK+ Tex subset expressed high levels of GZMK and EOMES, a T-box transcription factor that has been linked to T cell exhaustion43 (Figure S4E). The NK-like Tex subset expressed high levels of NK receptor genes (KLRC1, KLRC2, KLRB1, and KLRD1) (Figure S4F) and, of interest, uniquely expressed high levels of genes found in our 30-gene CAR T dysfunction signature identified from in vitro-stimulated CAR T cells, including TNFRSF18, RGS16, CD9, and SRGAP3 (Figure S4G). Of note, 56% of TILs extracted from the recurrent tumors expressed the CAR (Figure 4G), and thus low persistence did not explain the delayed failure of tumor control. Taken together, two distinct CAR T cell exhaustion clusters arise in mice that experience tumor recurrence: one characterized by the expression of dysfunction-associated NK receptors, and one characterized by GZMK and EOMES.

In the recurrent tumor model, SOX4KO CAR T cells did not protect against tumor recurrence compared to WT CAR T cells (Figure S3D). Nonetheless, we were interested if genomic differences between the KO and WT groups could be identified at the molecular level. We found that WT and SOX4KO recurrent tumor TILs did not differ in their cluster distribution (Figure 4H), suggesting that SOX4KO did not impact T cell trajectories in the setting of tumor recurrence. However, additional replicates would strengthen this dataset. Further, common exhaustion markers (TNFRSF9, TIGIT, LAG3, and HAVCR2) were highly expressed in WT and SOX4KO T cells harvested from recurrent tumors (Figure 4I). Our data suggest that SOX4KO CAR T cells can delay but not prevent CAR T cell exhaustion in vivo.

Double KO of SOX4 and ID3 in huCART-meso cells provides sustained protection against tumor recurrence

Considering that both ID3 and SOX4 were upregulated in exhausted cells and the single-KO CAR T cells experienced tumor relapse, we sought to investigate whether simultaneous KO of SOX4 and ID3 genes would improve antitumor responses and long-term protection from relapse. We generated WT, single-KO, and double-KO (DKO) CAR T cells from seven healthy donors and achieved high levels of editing efficiency (Figure S5A). DKO CAR T cells exhibited no difference in proliferation as assessed by population doublings, cell volume, or viability during expansion compared to WT and single KOs (Figure 5A), and no differences were observed in the T cell subset distribution between the groups following expansion (Figure S5B). Further analysis of the DKO revealed that while the majority of the cells were central memory and effector memory phenotypes, donor-dependent differences were observed in the distribution of T cell phenotypes (Figure 5B).

To assess the antitumor efficacy of DKO CAR T cells, suboptimal doses of WT, single-KO, or DKO CAR T cells were injected into AsPC-1 tumor-bearing mice. DKO CAR T cells demonstrated comparable antitumor efficacy to single KOs at suboptimal doses (ND584, 5 × 105 CAR T cells per mouse, Figure 5C), again with no impact on weight (Figure 5D), indicating that the double KO of SOX4 and ID3 did not improve the antitumor efficacy of CAR T cells. Next, we evaluated the efficacy of DKO CAR T cells in preventing relapse after an initial response. AsPC-1 tumor-bearing xenografts were injected with optimal doses of WT, single-KO, and DKO CAR T cells, and mice were monitored for relapse (Figure 5E). We observed that several mice in the WT and single-KO arms relapsed at early time points, starting around 2 months after tumor inoculation. In contrast, DKO-treated mice experienced fewer and delayed relapses or remained relapse-free by the end of the study (1 × 106 CAR T cells per mouse; ND528, Figure 5F; ND584, Figure S5C), and for those that did relapse, tumor volumes in DKO CAR T-treated mice were significantly reduced at the end of the study (Figure 5G). Discussion

This phase 1 study investigated the safety and feasibility of huCART-meso therapy in patients with advanced PDAC and found limited clinical response. Given this, we conducted an in-depth analysis of tumor-infiltrating CAR T cells from patients with PDAC to understand resistance mechanisms in the pancreatic cancer microenvironment, providing valuable insights into their behavior in solid tumors.

Following our prior work with murine-derived anti-MSLN SS1 CAR T cells,16,17 we developed a fully human anti-MSLN CAR construct with a CD3ζ/4-1BB costimulatory domain targeting a membrane-proximal epitope, which has been reported to enhance activity.44 Autologous huCART-meso cells were administered via three different delivery strategies: systemic i.v. infusion (cohort 1), or regionally either into the peritoneal fluid (cohort 2) or via i.h. artery infusion (cohort 3) for patients with peritoneal and liver metastases, respectively. Our findings demonstrated that i.v. and regional delivery strategies were feasible, with no related adverse events above grade 3. The overall safety of i.p. and i.h. administration aligns with previously published reports on regional CAR T cell delivery.45,46 Of note, i.v. delivery was complicated by pneumonitis in patients with moderate pulmonary metastatic burden, consistent with prior observations in mesothelioma and ovarian cancer trials using the same CAR T construct at higher doses.32 Pneumonitis was not observed when huCART-meso cells were delivered regionally or i.v. in patients with low or no pulmonary metastases.

Despite encouraging safety results, the best response observed was transient stable disease in a single patient of cohort 1. This limited clinical efficacy could be explained by a number of factors. First, huCART-meso cells are present at low to moderate levels in blood and tumor metastases, with limited expansion with subsequent infusions. Second, although we confirmed CAR T cell trafficking into metastases following hepatic artery infusion (consistent with prior reports using i.h. delivery47), the levels of CAR T cells detected in the metastases were lower than those reported in other solid tumor trials, such as with CAR T-PSMA-TGFβRDN cells in metastatic prostate cancer or IL15-GPC3-CAR T cells in patients with GPC3-positive cancers.48,49 Our findings align with other clinical trials targeting MSLN16,17 and CD133,50 where safety was achieved, but efficacy was modest. Together, this underscores the need to enhance T cell persistence and expansion within the TME.

We observed a decrease in MSLN+Epcam+ tumor cells in two out of three patients whose metastatic lesions were analyzed for MSLN expression, suggesting some degree of on-target CAR T cell activity. However, baseline MSLN expression was heterogeneous among patients, and its decrease post infusion may have been influenced by MSLN protein shedding51 or sampling variability in different metastatic sites.

Consistent with previous reports, we observed low infiltration of endogenous T cells at baseline in the metastases of all patients analyzed, indicative of a cold-tumor phenotype.52 Notably, all patients showed increased endogenous CD3+ T cell populations post infusion, suggesting that huCART-meso therapy may have stimulated endogenous T cell expansion, a phenomenon previously observed.53 Additionally, flow cytometry revealed no surface expression of the chimeric receptor in the metastatic biopsies from patients in cohorts 2 and 3, possibly due to limited sample material, heterogeneous MSLN expression,54 low infiltration, or downregulation of surface CAR expression due to trogocytosis or chronic antigen exposure.55,56,57,58,59

To further investigate the molecular basis of this limited efficacy, we performed scRNA-seq on post-infusion CAR T cells obtained from the peritoneal fluid. This analysis revealed a transcriptional phenotype consistent with chronic activation-induced dysfunction, including the upregulation of transcriptional regulators such as SOX4 and ID3,26 which have been implicated in T cell exhaustion. Compared to endogenous T cells, huCART-meso cells displayed enrichment for a CD8 GZMK+ phenotype following local delivery, which has been associated with limited cytotoxic potential60 and immune aging61,62 and was found to be an intermediate state between effector and exhausted T cells.41 Furthermore, while not significant, we observed a higher frequency of the CD4+ Treg phenotype, suggesting an immunosuppressive effect that may have further hindered CAR T cell function.63 CAR T cells with a Treg phenotype have been reported in non-responders to CAR19 therapy for large B cell lymphomas,63,64 where increased CAR Treg populations were linked to disease relapse.64 It remains unclear if the observed T cell subsets are present in the infusion product or if they differentiated due to exposure in the TME. In either case, this shift in T cell phenotype may impact and limit the efficacy of huCART-meso cell activity, activation, and expansion in patients. While these samples provide valuable information, it is important to note that the number of patients analyzed was limited and that prior treatments varied in type and number across individuals. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

To enhance huCART-meso efficacy, we targeted transcription factors linked to T cell exhaustion. Our previous work showed that knocking out SOX4 or ID3 improves effector function following chronic antigen stimulation in vitro,26 consistent with other cancer models linking SOX4 and ID3 to T cell exhaustion.41,65,66,67 In the present study, anti-MSLN CAR T cells with single KOs showed strong but donor-dependent benefits in vivo, suggesting that larger sample sizes are needed to clarify phenotype-response correlations. Variable responses may result from functional redundancy among SOX and ID family members,68,69 genetic compensation,70 or donor-specific non-heritable characteristics.71,72 This variability emphasizes the need to account for patient-specific traits when optimizing CAR T therapies. Recent studies in CD19 CAR T cells have suggested that a Th2-skewed phenotype in the pre-infusion products correlates with improved long-term responses,73 potentially adding another layer of complexity to donor-specific therapeutic outcomes. The intricate interplay between genetic modifications and baseline donor characteristics underscores the importance of understanding donor-specific factors for improved outcomes. Testing across multiple donors is essential for robust conclusions.

Given heterogeneity in the single-KO phenotypes, we tested whether dual KO of ID3 and SOX4 improves CAR T effector function using xenograft mouse models. Mice treated with dual-KO CAR T cells exhibited delayed tumor relapse compared to single-KO and WT CAR T cells, suggesting a cooperative role for SOX4 and ID3 in regulating long-term responses. This supports findings that targeting multiple transcription factors simultaneously, such as NR4A family members,30 improves CAR T durability.74,75 Although NSG mice lack proper host immunity, WT and single-KO CAR T cells were dysfunctional in relapsed tumors, probably due to persistent antigen exposure from the MSLN-expressing tumor cells. Continuous CAR engagement with tumor cells likely leads to early cytotoxic activity, followed by gradual loss of function due to sustained stimulation, ultimately allowing a small reservoir of tumor cells to persist and drive relapse in WT and single-KO-treated animals. In contrast, DKO CAR T cells demonstrated improved control of residual tumors, suggesting enhanced resistance to antigen-driven exhaustion and superior long-term functional persistence. Dissecting the transcriptional and signaling programs of DKO CAR T cells, along with in vivo tumor re-challenge studies, will be important next steps to better understand how these factors regulate CAR T cell exhaustion and contribute to long-term antitumor protection.

Lastly, the challenging TME of PDAC likely played a significant role in the limited efficacy of huCART-meso as a monotherapy. Strategies to activate immunity in PDAC are under investigation and hold promise to boost immunotherapy results.76 Given the ability of huCART-meso cells to infiltrate metastases and persist in peritoneal fluid with an acceptable safety profile, combination strategies could enhance therapeutic outcomes. Potential approaches include co-administration with checkpoint inhibitors targeting the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis or blocking immunosuppressive tumor-derived factors such as transforming growth factor β.25,77,78 Preclinical and early-phase clinical studies have shown improved CAR T cell efficacy with such combination therapies.79 Additionally, targeting tumor stroma to enhance CAR T cell extravasation and modulating myeloid compartments via CD40/CD40L targeting could improve antitumor activity.80,81,82,83

In conclusion, this study provides important insights into the challenges of CAR T cell therapy for PDAC. While huCART-meso therapy demonstrated a favorable safety profile, its efficacy was limited by poor persistence and low huCART-meso tumor infiltration. Future strategies, including combination therapies and transcription factor modulation, hold promise for enhancing CAR T cell function in solid tumors. Continued research in these areas will be essential to unlocking the full therapeutic potential of cell-based immunotherapies for PDAC.

Limitations of the study

Limited sample availability, small patient cohorts, and variability in prior treatments challenge definitive conclusions for certain aspects of our investigation.

We focused on uncovering the intrinsic mechanisms driving CAR T cell exhaustion using subcutaneous xenograft models, which provide a controlled platform for assessing CAR T cell dynamics but do not fully mimic the pancreatic TME. The use of immunocompromised mice also limits the evaluation of interactions with endogenous immune cells. Future studies using orthotopic syngeneic models and tumor re-challenge experiments will be important to further validate the long-term efficacy and durability of DKO CAR T cells in more physiological settings. Despite these constraints, our in vivo assays provide valuable insights into the efficacy and persistence of gene-edited CAR T cells.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study participants

This trial was conducted in accordance with guidelines from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practice, the Declaration of Helsinki, and applicable institutional review board (IRB) requirements. The study protocol was approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (IRB protocol number 827644). All patients provided written informed consent in accordance with local regulatory review. Patient samples were collected with written informed consent and ethics approval under the same IRB protocol. The Data Safety Monitoring Board provided oversight and monitored the safety of the study.

The median age at study entry was 61 years (range: 45–74 years). Among the patients infused with huCART-meso cells, three (33.3%) were reported as male and six (66.7%) as female. All participants were Caucasian. Gender information was based on patient self-report. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table S1.

Cell lines

AsPC-1 (human cell line, female) and HEK293T (human cell line, female) cells were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), which reports cell line sex information. AsPC-1 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (1:1) (Gibco, Life Technologies), 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Life Technologies). HEK293T cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% FBS, 2% HEPES (Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Authentication of all cell lines was regularly performed by the University of Arizona Genetics Core, and mycoplasma contamination was routinely assessed using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza).

Mice

Animal experiments were conducted following protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pennsylvania. Six-to eight-week-old NOD/scid/IL2rγ−/− (NSG) female mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratories and bred in the vivarium at the University of Pennsylvania under pathogen-free conditions. The mice were housed and maintained in a pathogen-free environment throughout the study. Sex was assigned based on provider information and visual inspection.

Method details

Lentiviral vector production

The production of lentiviral vectors was conducted following previously established protocols.88 In brief, HEK293T cells were transfected with lentiviral CAR and packaging plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 or Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lentiviral supernatants were harvested at 24- and 48-h time points post-transfection and concentrated using high-speed ultracentrifugation. The resulting concentrated lentivirus batches were then resuspended in cold media (RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, 1% penicillin-streptomycin) to generate lentiviral stocks, which were subsequently stored at −80°C.

Transduction of CAR-redirected human T cells

The huCART-meso is a second-generation CAR comprising a human MSLN-binding single-chain variable fragment (scFv), and CD8α hinge and transmembrane domains fused to 4-1BB and CD3-ζ cytoplasmic signaling domains. CAR T cells were manufactured as previously described.89 Briefly, primary human CD8+ and CD4+ T cells from healthy donors were mixed at 1:1 ratio and activated at a concentration of 1 x 106 cells/ml using Dynabeads CD3/CD28 CTS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 3:1 bead-to-cell ratio. Approximately 24 h later, the T cells were transduced at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3–5. Beads were magnetically removed from the cultures on day 5. T cell cultures were maintained at a concentration of 8 x 105 cells/ml. Cell number and volume were monitored daily using a Multisizer 4 Coulter Counter (Beckman Coulter). Transduced T cells were cryopreserved upon reaching the resting state, as determined by cell size.

Flow cytometry and sorting

Fresh metastatic tissue collected by fine-needle aspiration and recurrent tumors from in vivo experiments were collected, processed and stained in fresh. Samples were digested in RPMI containing collagenase D (400 Mandl units/mL, Sigma) and DNase I (50 mg/mL, Sigma) for 15 min at 37°C to generate single cell suspensions. 12 μL/mL EDTA 0.5 M, pH 8 was added to stop the enzymatic digestion. Samples were then mechanically disrupted and filtered through a 0.7 mm cell strainer (Corning). Ammonium-Chloride-Potassium (ACK, GIBCO) Lysing Buffer was used for the lysis of red blood cells. Human infiltrating T cells from recurrent mice were enriched by magnetic sorting prior to FACS sort by using the EasySepTM Release Human CD45 Positive Selection Kit for Humanized Mice (STEMCELL Technologies) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Single-cell suspensions from processed tumors and in vitro cultures were washed in PBS and stained with Fixable Dead Cell Dyes followed by FcR-Block treatment (Fc Receptor Blocking Solution, Biolegend) as per manufacturer’s recommendations.

Patient ascites were collected and centrifuged at 500g, at 4C for 20min. Patient’s 04 sample was enriched by Ficoll Paque Plus density gradient following manufacturer’s protocol.

Viable T cell counts were determined using a trypan blue stain (Invitrogen) and a Countess automated cell counter (Invitrogen). Samples were then stained in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer, which consisted of PBS (Gibco), 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (GEMINI), 2 mM EDTA (Invitrogen), and 100 μg/mL DNase (Roche). To calculate absolute cell counts in cell suspensions, CountBrightTM Absolute Counting Beads (ThermoFisher) were used as an internal standard following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Antibodies for surface staining are detailed in the key resources table. Surface expression of huCART-meso was assessed using either biotinylated goat anti-human IgG F(ab’)2 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 109-066-006) followed by fluorochrome-conjugated streptavidin (see the key resources table), or an anti-idiotype antibody (provided by Novartis Pharmaceuticals). The anti-idiotype antibody was specifically used for detecting huCART-meso in patient-derived samples.

Live/dead staining was performed using Live/Dead Aqua (Life Technologies) or Zombie NIR (Biolegend) Fixable Viability Kits according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by cell surface staining for 15 min at 4°C in the dark. Samples were analyzed on an LSRII Fortessa and BD Symphony A5 Flow Cytometers (BD Biosciences) and processed using FlowJo v10 software (FlowJo, LLC). Sorting assays were performed using a FACS Aria Fusion Cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Clinical trial design

This was a single-center, single-arm phase 1 study designed to evaluate the safety and feasibility of both intravenous and local administration of lentivirally transduced huCART-meso cells in patients with histologically confirmed unresectable or metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma. The rationale for the phase 1 study was informed by previous CAR T cell trials conducted by our group. Based on prior experience with MSLN-directed CAR T cell therapies, we hypothesized that local delivery of the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of huCART-meso cells (established by i.v. infusion under NCT03054298, 1-3x107 huCART-meso cells/m2) may reduce the risk to patients by mitigating the potential for off-tumor, on-target toxicities. To further balance toxicity and efficacy, the study permitted repeat i.v. infusions of huCART-meso cells every 21–42 days at the clinical discretion of the physician-investigator after the initial local dose. We aimed to test if the repeat i.v. infusions would bolster the anti-tumor effect of persistent huCART-meso cells, which could lead to improved clinical outcomes while mitigating risk of toxicities observed at higher i.v. doses.

Eligible patients for the study have histologically confirmed unresectable or metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Additional enrollment criteria included: (1) failure of at least one prior standard of care chemotherapy for advanced stage disease; (2) measurable disease as defined by RECIST 1.1 criteria; (3) patients ≥18 years of age; (4) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1; (5) satisfactory organ and bone marrow function; (6) blood coagulation parameters: PT such that internal normalized ration is ≤1.5 and a PTT ≤1.2 time the upper limit of normal unless the patient is therapeutically anti-coagulated for history of cancer-related thrombosis and has stable coagulation parameters; (7) provides informed written consent; and (8) patients of reproductive potential must agree to use acceptable birth control methods.

All subjects who received the target dose of huCART-meso cells were considered evaluable for response, including progression-free survival and overall survival. All subjects with radiologically confirmed measurable disease (per RECIST 1.1) at baseline were considered evaluable for overall radiologic response.

Clinical Cell manufacture

Enrolled patients underwent apheresis at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania Apheresis Center. The manufacture and release testing of huCART-meso cells were performed by the Clinical Cell and Vaccine Production Facility at the University of Pennsylvania, according to Phase 1 IND current Good Clinical Manufacturing Practice guidelines and FACT Common Standards for Cellular Therapies. huCART-meso cells are autologous T cells that have been engineered to express an extracellular single chain antibody with mesothelin specificity and 4-1BB/TCRζ intracellular signaling domains. T cells were enriched from autologous leukapheresis products and activated with anti-CD3/CD28 antibody-coated magnetic beads, followed by transduction with the lentiviral vector containing the huCART-meso transgene. T cells were expanded in culture and washed, de-beaded, and cryopreserved in infusible grade cryomedia prior to product release following confirmation of FDA-specified release criteria for sterility, purity, and identity.

Quantitative PCR analysis of clinical samples

Each amplification reaction contained 200 ng of genomic DNA per time point for peripheral blood and tumor samples. To determine copy number per unit DNA, an eight-point standard curve was generated consisting of between 5 and 106 copies of huCART-meso lentivirus plasmid spiked into 200 ng of nontransduced control genomic DNA. The number of copies of plasmid present in the standard curve was verified using digital qPCR with the same primer/probe set and performed on a QuantStudio 3D digital PCR instrument (Life Technologies). Each data point (sample and standard curve) was evaluated in triplicate with a positive cycle threshold value in three out of three replicates with a percentage coefficient of variation <0.95% for all quantifiable values. To control for the quantity of interrogated DNA, a human genomic DNA reference assay was performed using 10 ng of genomic DNA and a primer-probe combination specific for a nontranscribed genomic sequence upstream of the CDKN1A (p21) gene, as previously described.90 These amplification reactions generated a correction factor to adjust for calculated versus actual DNA input. Copies of transgene per microgram DNA were calculated according to the formula: copies/microgram genomic DNA = copies calculated from huCART-meso standard curve x correction factor/amount of DNA evaluated (ng) x 1000 ng.

Serum cytokine profiling

Serum samples obtained before and after huCART-meso infusion and cryopreserved at −80°C were thawed and analyzed for cytokine measurements using a 31-plex panel (Millipore Sigma) as previously described.91

Production of human CRISPR-Engineered CAR-T cells

The design and synthesis of single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences targeting ID3 and SOX4 were performed according to previously established methods.26 Gene disruption, T cell activation, transduction, expansion, and knockout validation followed an optimized protocol as described.92 Briefly, primary T cells were cultured in OPT 7/15 media, which consist of OpTmizer T cell expansion media (Gibco) supplemented with 5 ng/mL of huIL-7 and huIL-15 each (Preprotech). After 24 h, cells were electroporated using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector Core/X Unit and the P3 Primary Cell 4-D Kit. For Cas9 and sgRNA delivery, RNP complexes were formed by separately incubating 10μg of each sgRNA with the Cas9 nuclease (Aldevron, 10 μg) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Cells were spun down at 300xg for 5 min and resuspended at a concentration of 5-10x106 cells/100μL in the specified buffer (P3 Solution with Supplement). The RNP complex, 100μL of resuspended cells, and 4μL of 100μM IDT Electroporation Enhancer (a non-homologous ssDNA oligonucleotide) were combined and electroporated in a cuvette. Following electroporation, the cells were cultured in OPT 7/15 media at a concentration of 5 x 106cells/mL at 37°C and activated 4 to 6h later using anti-CD3/anti-CD28 monoclonal antibody-coated magnetic beads. After 24 h, T cell were lentivirally transduced and expanded as described above.

For each target locus defined by two sgRNA cut sites (spanning 100 and 130 bp for SOX4 and ID3, respectively), PCR and sequencing primers were designed to detect the target loci. LongAmp Taq 2X Master Mix (NEB) was utilized for target sequence amplification following the manufacturer’s protocol, and DNA purification was performed using NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up (Macherey-Nagel). Gene editing efficiency was evaluated by Sanger sequencing as previously described.26 Synthego’s Performance Analysis ICE tool was employed to calculate the editing efficiency. Primer sequences used for KO generation and editing efficiency validation are provided in Table S3.

Mouse experiments

NOD/scid/IL2rγ−/− (NSG) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and bred and housed in the vivarium at the University of Pennsylvania in pathogen-free conditions. Animal studies were approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 106 AsPC-1 cells suspended in 200 μL Matrigel:PBS (1:1) into the right flank. When the mean of tumor volumes reached 250–300 mm3, mice were treated with 0.5 or 1 x 106 huCART-meso cells generated from different healthy donors. Tumor volumes were calculated as length×with2 ÷2. Tumor growth was weekly assessed by caliper measurement. After primary antitumor response mice were monitored for recurrence. Mice bearing recurrent tumors were sacrificed when reached the maximum size or showed evident signs of disease, and tumors were collected.

Standard immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses

Recurrent tumors were collected and processed for standard IHC analysis by the Comparative Pathology Core at the School of Veterinary Medicine of the University of Pennsylvania. Formalin-fixed tissues were routinely processed for paraffin embedding, sectioning, and staining. 5-μm-thick paraffin sections were mounted on ProbeOn slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The immunostaining procedure was performed using a Leica BOND RXm automated platform in combination with the Bond Polymer Refine Detection Kit (Leica). Rabbit monoclonal primary antibody against human CD45 (CD45-LCA; Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 13917) was used at concentrations of 1:300 and incubated on the sections for 45 min at room temperature. A biotin-free polymeric IHC detection system, consisting of horseradish peroxidase–conjugated anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG), was applied for 25 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using the diaminobenzidine chromogen. CD45 infiltration was quantified using Aperio ImageScope v12.4 (Aperio Technologies, Vista, CA, USA).

Single-cell RNA-seq library preparation

scRNA-seq libraries were generated using a Chromium Single-Cell 3′ v3.1 Library and Gel Bead Kit (10x Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, for recurrent mouse tumor and chronic antigen stimulation assays, CD8+ T cells were sorted by FACS and washed with ice-cold PBS +0.04% BSA. After washing, 15,000 cells were loaded onto the chromium controller to generate single-cell gel beads in emulsion. For patient samples, approximately 35,000–100,000 CD3+ T cells were sorted by FACS and cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS +0.04% BSA and spun down at 600g for 5 min. After washing, cells were resuspended in up to 45 μL of ice-cold PBS +0.04% BSA. Five microliters of cell suspension were mixed with 5 μL of trypan blue, and cell numbers were counted via hemacytometer under the microscope. Approximately 10,000–16,000 cells were loaded to create the gel bead in emulsion. Following reverse transcription, gel beads in emulsion were disrupted and barcoded complementary DNA was isolated and amplified. After fragmentation, end repair, and poly A tailing, sample indexes were added and amplified following the manufacturer’s protocol. The final libraries were quality control checked and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500. Patient 04 was sequenced with a 75-cycle kit with parameters Read 1: 28, Read 2: 56, Index 1: 8, Index 2: 0. Recurrent mouse tumors and patient 05 were sequenced with a 150-cycle kit with parameters Read 1: 28, Read 2: 91, Index 1: 8, Index 2: 0.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

To assess the expression of huCART-meso CAR in the scRNA-seq data, the cellranger reference was reindexed (mkref) by adding a single contig for the 818 bp WPRE sequence (a unique sequence in the CAR plasmid) to the GRCh38 assembly of the human genome (the gene annotation GTF file was appended with CDS and exon entries spanning the entire sequence and gene_id ‘‘CAR-Ligand’’). Sequencing data were aligned to the GRCh38 genome with the WPRE sequence and then barcodes and unique molecular identifiers were counted using the Cell Ranger v3.1.0 command cellranger count. See below detailed analyses for each experiment.

Patient and recurrent tumor TILS single cell (Figures 2 and 4)

Data were further analyzed in R (version 4.2.0) using Seurat version 4.3.0.1.85,86 Briefly, genes that were not detected in at least 3 cells and cells with >10% mitochondrial reads were excluded, as well as cells that express <200 genes or >5000 genes. Data were normalized using NormalizeData. CellCycleScoring was performed using s_genes and g2m_genes. Data were normalized using sctransform V 0.3.5 87 with assay = RNA, new.assay.name = ‘SCT’, vst.flavor = ”v2″, vars.to.regress = S.Score, G2M.Score, percent.mt. RunPCA was performed with npcs = 30. Samples were integrated using Seurat’s integration method86 with the following parameters. SelectIntegrationFeatures (nfeatures = 3000) and FindIntegrationAnchors using “SCT” was run. Next, we used RunUMAP on integrated datasets with “pca” reduction and dims = 1:20. FindClusters was performed with a resolution of 0.4 and UMAPs were generated.

Patient single cell (Figure 2)

Marker genes were determined using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat using SCTransform normalized expression values where at least 25% of the cells must be expressing the gene with a logfc.threshold = 0.25. Next, ALRA imputation method93 was performed to reduce the number of technical zeros. ALRA imputed data was used to generate the UMAP feature plot of CAR expression. To isolate CARpos cells, subset function was used with parameter CAR-Ligand>0 using ALRA imputed values. 537 CARpos cells were identified. Next, CARneg cells were randomly downsampled to 537 cells using set.seed equals 12. CARpos and downsampled CARneg Seurat objects were merged using the merge function. After merging, RunALRA was performed on the merged object. Genes differentially expressed between CARpos and CARneg cells were identified using FindMarkers. Imputed values were plotted via violin plot to show expression changes between CARpos and CARneg cells. Z scores were calculated from imputed values for the 30 dysfunction signature genes and pheatmap was used to generate heatmaps with ward.D clustering method using downsampled data.

T cell subpopulations were defined using canonical markers. In the CD4 population, we detected regulatory T cells (Tregs; FOXP3, IL2RA, IKZF2), naive (SELL, CCR7, LEF1), and effector memory T cells (Tem; IL7R, KLRB1). In the CD8 population, we detected memory T cells (Tmem; CCR7, LEF1, SELL, IL7R, TCF7), effector memory (Tem; IL7R, KLRG1, NKG7), GZMK+ effector memory (GZMK+ Tem; GZMK, NKG7, PRF1, GZMH, CCL5), terminally differentiated Tem (Temra; NKG7, PRF1, GZMH, CCL5), and an NK-like T cell population (NK-like; NCAM1, FCGR3A, GNLY, GZMB, KLRC1, KLRC2). To display changes in T cell subpopulations, the number of cells found within each cluster for each condition was normalized by the total number of cells per condition and expressed as percentages. Cell type composition analysis in single-cell data was implemented using propeller from speckle R package.84 Briefly, moderated t-tests method was performed on cell type proportion between huCAR-meso+ (CARpos) and endogenous CAR negative T cells (CARneg) replicates for all cell types, excluding the unknown annotated cluster. P-values were reported as statistical significance.

Recurrent tumor TILs single cell (Figure 4)

To isolate CARpos cells, the subset function was used with parameter CAR-Ligand>0. SCTransform normalized expression values were used to make violin plots and UMAP feature plots. Genes differentially expressed between WT and SOX4KO recurrent tumor CARpos TILs were identified using FindMarkers. Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test was used to measure significance and adjusted p-values were calculated based on Bonferroni correction to determine the level of significance comparing WT to SOX4KO recurrent tumor TILs and data was plotted via violin plot.

T cell subpopulations were defined using canonical markers. All cell clusters expressed exhaustion markers LAG3, HAVCR2, TOX, TIGIT. Clusters were further classified by NK-like Tex which expressed high levels of NK receptors and CAR T cell exhaustion genes identified in 26 (KLRC1, KLRC2, KLRB1, KLRD1, CD9, TNFRSF18, RGS16, and SRGAP3.) The GZMK+ Tex population was identified as having high expression of GZMK and EOMES and low to no expression of NK receptors and CAR T dysfunction signature genes. To display changes in T cell subpopulations, the number of cells found within each cluster for each condition was normalized by the total number of cells per condition and expressed as percentages. Cell type composition analysis in single-cell data was implemented using propeller from speckle R package.84 Briefly, moderated t-tests method was performed on cell type proportion between WT and SOX4KO replicates for all cell types. P-values were reported as statistical significance.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.1.2 GraphPad (GraphPad software) are represented by the following ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05.

Statistical details for all experiments, including the statistical tests used, exact values of n, definitions of n, measures of central tendency, and measures of variability are provided in the figure legends.

For CAR T cell expansion in biopsy samples, statistical analysis was performed using a Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test. For in vitro and in vivo assays, Normality tests (D’Agostino & Pearson test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Shapiro-Wilk test) were used to assess normality of distribution of the variables. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using two-tailed unpaired Student’s test or the equivalent non-parametric Mann-Whitney. For tumor growth data analyses, Mixed effects two-Way ANOVA was used to compare the average tumor growth rates among groups by assessing the significance of the treatment by time interaction term within the timepoints showing linear growths. Statistical significance between multiple groups of two variables was assessed by two-Way ANOVA with post hoc tests.

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for relapse-free survival (RFS) analyses, defined as the number of days between the date of infusion and the first of the following dates: recurrence, death, end of the experiment (censored). A log rank test for RFS, restricting to WT or DKO subjects was performed.

To identify changes in cell populations from single-cell RNA-seq datasets, significance was evaluated using the moderated t-test using the propeller method, speckle package in R. Data were visualized using the following R packages and functions ggplot2, pheatmap, RColorBrewer, gplots, dplyr, plotly. RFS was analyzed using the survival, SurvMisc, survminer packages in R.
