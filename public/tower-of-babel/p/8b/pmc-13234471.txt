Live-cell Pick-Seq (LiP-Seq): Interrogating ultra-rare mantle cell lymphoma persistent cells after CART19 therapy
Authors: Ran Xu, Gongwei Wu, Siobhan Rice, Matthew Booker, Jenalyn Weekes, Hayden Bell, Yifei Gao, Ajit J Nirmal, Huiyun Liu, Zoe Ciantra, Qingyu Luo, Amanda Fortune, Karley Whalen, Timothy Guanci, Yifei Wan, Abner Louissaint, Joshua J Nordberg, Jia-Ren Lin, Michael Hemann, Scott R Manalis, Philippe Armand, Eric Smith, Caron A Jacobson, Michael Tolstorukov, Mark A Murakami, Peter K Sorger, David M Weinstock, Li Song, Andrew A Lane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•LiP-Seq is a novel platform to capture single, viable, and ultrarare persistent lymphoma cells for single-cell sequencing.•LiP-Seq analysis of persistent MCL cells revealed upregulated IFITM2, which confers relative protection from CAR-T killing in vitro.

Introduction

Targeting persistent cancer cells is theoretically advantageous compared with treatment of subsequent overt relapse, as it involves a lower tumor burden and potentially fewer protumorigenic microenvironmental alterations.1 Patients may also be in better condition when tumor burden is limited, allowing for the use of more intensive therapies. However, our understanding of these cells remains inadequate because of the limitations of current techniques. Although circulating tumor DNA detection methods (eg, cancer personalized profiling by deep sequencing [CAPP-Seq], phased variant enrichment and detection sequencing [PhasED-Seq]) enable the quantification of rare persistent populations,2,3 capturing these cells to characterize their phenotypes remains challenging. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting and sequencing (Sort-Seq) can reliably isolate persistent cells only when present at frequencies above 1 in 104 cells.4 Consequently, novel techniques to retrieve less frequent persistent cells are critically needed, as the risk of relapse remains significant even among patients who achieve minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity using standard assays.5,6

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma with a high relapse rate and poor long-term survival. Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy targeting CD19 is effective in patients with relapsed/refractory B-cell lymphomas.7,8 In patients with relapsed/refractory MCL, 3-year follow-up of brexucabtagene autoleucel (KTE-X19) CAR-T therapy reported 68% initial complete remissions (CRs), but only 37% long-term disease-free survival. Furthermore, only 60% of patients who were initially MRD-negative, assessed by next-generation sequencing (reported sensitivity, 1 in 105 cells), remained in long-term remission.9 For patients who experience relapse after CAR-T therapy, therapeutic options are extremely limited. Therefore, our goal was to elucidate the molecular mechanisms associated with cellular persistence to identify next-generation treatments capable of preventing relapse.

Methods

Cell lines

Mycoplasma testing and authentication by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling were routinely performed. The JeKo-1 cell line was transduced with luciferase-mCherry and sorted using a BD LSR Fortessa to obtain 100% transduced populations. Interferon-induced transmembrane protein 2 (IFITM2) was cloned into a Plvx-IRES-neo lentiviral expression vector (Takara). Lentivirus were produced by cotransfecting 293T cells with target plasmids, psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260), and pMD2.G (Addgene, 12259) plasmids. Virus-containing supernatants were collected 72 hours after transfection. Cells were infected with produced lentivirus in the presence of polybrene.

Instrumentation

The RareCyte’s CyteFinder II is a fluorescence microscope with integrated software for semi-automated scanning and rare-cell analysis. The instrument has up to 7 fluorescent channels for rapid imaging of multiple biomarkers. For this study, it was equipped with a CytePicker Retrieval Module, which was initially developed for recovery of circulating tumor cells from blood. The rare-cell analysis software (CyteMapper) detects and scores candidate cells based on criteria set in the scan program and presents images for final classification, thereby enabling the characterization of various rare-cell populations.

Live-cell pick sequencing (LiP-Seq)

All patients were treated with a commercial CD19-targeted CAR-T product (brexucabtagene autoleucel), and clinical data and blood samples were obtained and approved by the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI)/Harvard Cancer Center institutional review board. Cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells from the DFCI lymphoma biobank were thawed and resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] with 2% fetal bovine serum, 1mM EDTA); CD3 and CD33 negative selection was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol (Stemcell Technologies, catalog nos. 17876 and 17851). Next, cells were incubated with 5 μL of Fc block (BioLegend, catalog no. 422302) in 100 μL fluorescence-activated cell sorter (FACS) buffer on ice for 10 minutes followed by addition of fluorescent-conjugated antibodies. Cells were stained for 30 minutes on ice using the following antibodies: CD45-AF750 (Thermo Fisher, catalog no. NP179127A70), CD5-AF647 (BioLegend, catalog no. 300616), CD79b-BB515 (BD Biosciences, catalog no. 565225), CD19-PE (BioLegend, catalog no. 302208), and CD3-CF405S (Biotium, catalog no. BNC041050). Cells were then washed twice and resuspended in PBS. The chamber slides were treated with 0.01% bovine serum albumin for 3 minutes. Then, 0.1 × 106 to 0.5 × 106 stained cells were added into 1 primary well. The slides were covered with coverslips and centrifuged at 500g for 5 minutes at room temperature. Live-cell imaging was conducted using the CyteFinder II instrument (RareCyte). The whole slide was scanned and then analyzed using CyteMapper software. Rare cells with desired cell surface markers were identified. Following scanning, SYTOX orange dead cell stain (Invitrogen, catalog no. S34861; 1:10 000 dilution), was added to the buffer to assess cell viability. Rare-cell locations identified by CyteMapper software were recovered using CytePicker, an integrated cell-retrieval module in the CyteFinder II system. Using software-guided controls, the CytePicker needle (40-μm bore) aspirated 200 nL of retrieval buffer (PBS with 1000× ribonuclease inhibitor) containing the selected cell and deposited it into a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tube, with visual confirmation of successful transfer. The PCR tube contains 10 μL lysis buffer (1× total cell lysate [TCL] lysis buffer with 1% 2-mercaptoethanol and 1% ribonuclease inhibitor) and was snap frozen on dry ice immediately after picking.

SMART-Seq2 library preparation and sequencing

Lysed cells were thawed on ice and transferred into a 96-well plate for processing using the SMART-Seq2 platform.10 Cellular nucleic acids were extracted using 2.2× (volume-to-volume ratio) RNA solid-phase reversible immobilization (SPRI) beads (RNA-clean AMPure beads, Beckman-Coulter). Reverse transcription was performed with the Maxima enzyme (Thermo Scientific), followed by complementary DNA (cDNA) amplification using the KAPA HotStart Readymix 2X kit (Kappa Biosystems). Amplified cDNA libraries were quantified using the Qubit double-stranded DNA high sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) and assessed for fragment size using the BioAnalyzer 2100 high sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent). The cDNAs from each single cell were prepared into sequencing libraries using the Nextera XT kit (Illumina) with unique 8 bp DNA barcodes. The resulting libraries were quantitated with the Qubit double-stranded DNA HS Assay Kit and their quality assessed on the BioAnalyzer. Libraries were pooled at equimolar ratios and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq500 (paired-end 35 bp) to an average depth of 8 × 105 reads per cell at the molecular biology core facility at DFCI.

scRNA-Seq analysis

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) data were analyzed using the package Scanpy v1.9.311 in Python 3.11. The count matrix was based on the transcripts per million reads calculated by Salmon v1.10.1.12 After loading the data into the anndata structure, we first conducted quality control of the input 1809 cells. We filtered cells with <2000 genes or >25 000 genes detected, or >30% reads from mitochondrial genome. In our analysis, B-cell receptor (BCR) and T-cell receptor genes, such as V, J, and C genes, hemoglobin genes, and genes that were detected in <30 cells were excluded from the data analysis. We then performed principal component analysis based on the top 2000 highly variable genes (flavor = seurat_v3 in Scanpy) on the 1683 cells passed quality control. The top 50 principal components (PC) were used for cell clustering using the method Leiden13 with the default resolution 1.0. The marker genes for each cluster were then inferred by the function rankgenegroups in Scanpy.

BCR analysis

In addition to conventional single-cell data analysis, we applied the method TRUST4 v1.0.1414 to assemble the BCR and T-cell receptor for each cell. We regarded the cells bearing the same immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) amino acid sequence as having the same clonotype to calculate the diversity for each donor and each cluster. We examined 2 types of diversities, Shannon entropy and clonality. Shannon entropy was defined as e=∑1N−pilog(pi), where N was the number of distinct clonotypes and pi represented the fraction of cells bearing the i-th clonotype. Clonality was defined as 1 – e/log(N), where a larger value indicates stronger clonal expansion.

No-BCR cancer cell prediction

Many cells from the 1-month time point (M1) to 3-month time point (M3) did not contain any reads from the BCR region, preventing us from identifying posttreatment cancer cells. To address this, we developed a method that searched for single nucleotide variants (SNVs) in the scRNA-Seq data that were detected in cancer cells or normal cells, where the cancer cells were the clonal expanded cells with the same IGH CDR3 amino acid. We first defined a set of informative SNVs. For each position in a gene’s exon, we only considered the cells with at least 3 reads covering this position. When all the normal cells had the same nucleotide consensus (ie, nucleotide supported by the most reads) and at least 60% of the cancer cells had another nucleotide consensus, this position was recorded as informative SNV. For a cell without IGH reads, the method predicted whether it was a cancer or normal cell by examining its reads covering informative SNVs. We only considered the informative positions with at least 3 read supports, and a cell with <4 usable informative positions was reported as unknown. When the usable informative positions nucleotide consensus agrees with the cancer cells, the cell was predicted as a cancer cell. Otherwise, it was predicted as a normal cell. In the case of a tie, the prediction result was reported as unknown. Given that these were RNA-based libraries, observed SNVs could be from somatic mutations in cancer cells or allele-specific expression.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analysis

Sequenced reads were mapped to hg38 version of the human genome with Burrows-Wheeler Aligner - Maximal Exact Matches (BWA-MEM) aligner v.0.7.1515 and preprocessed following Genome analysis toolkit (GATK) v.4.1.8.0 best practices. The quality of alignment was evaluated with the FastQC v.0.11.9 (Babraham Institute), Picard v.2.23.8 (Broad Institute) software tools. Short nucleotide variants (point mutations and indels) were called using GATK Mutect2 in tumor-normal mode. Identified variants were annotated with Ensembl Variant Effector Predictor v. 11016 and transformed into mutation annotation format (MAF) files with the vcf2maf v.1.6.21 script. Structural variant calls were made with Manta17 and SvABA18 software packages.

Flow cytometry

Routine flow cytometry measurements of tumor involvement and CAR-T expansion were performed. Cells were washed with FACS buffer, incubated with Human TruStain Fc receptor blocking solution (BioLegend, catalog no. 422302) for 10 minutes on ice, and then incubated with antibodies for 30 minutes on ice in the dark. Cells were then washed twice with FACS buffer and acquired on a 4-laser, 17-parameter LSR Fortessa instrument (BD). All flow cytometry antibodies used in this study can be found in supplemental Table 3. Data were analyzed using FlowJo V10 software.

CAR-T production for in vitro experiments

A lentiviral vector encoding a CD19 (FMC63)-specific CAR with CD28/CD3ζ domains was ordered from Addgene (catalog no. 135991). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from peripheral blood samples from healthy donors by Ficoll, and primary T cells were enriched by EasySep human T-cell enrichment kit (Stemcell Technologies, catalog no. 19051). To generate CAR T cells, bulk human T cells were activated using CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Life Technologies, catalog no. 11132D) at a 1:2 T-cell to bead ratio cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with GlutaMAX and supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum , penicillin, streptomycin, and 20 IU/mL recombinant human interleukin-2. Cells were then transduced with the abovementioned CAR lentivirus and expanded with media doubling and interleukin-2 replacement every 2 to 3 days. CAR-T expansion was carried out for 10 to 14 days. The Dynabeads were removed using magnetic separation. CAR T cells were assessed for transduction efficiency by green fluorescent protein detection by flow cytometry. Untransduced T cells from the same donor were grown simultaneously as a control.

In vitro CAR-T killing assay

In vitro killing assays were performed using a luciferase-based killing assay with CAR T cells incubated with luciferase-expressing JeKo-1 cells at indicated effector to target ratios. After 22 hours of coincubation, the cell lysate was prepared using the ONE-Glo luciferase assay system (Promega, catalog no. E6120). The luciferase activity was measured with a Synergy Neo2 luminescence microplate reader (Biotek).

All work involving human subjects were approved by the institutional review board of the DFCI and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

High-quality scRNA-Seq profiling of live, individual primary MCL cells using LiP-Seq

To interrogate ultrarare persistent cells, we developed LiP-Seq. The original pick-Seq method involved precise mechanical isolation of small regions of interest in tissue sections, typically containing 5 to 20 cells, for subsequent microregional sequencing using a CyteFinder instrument.19 We built upon this technology to capture individual live cells from peripheral blood, bone marrow, and dissociated fresh tissues. In this workflow, cell suspensions undergo column-based enrichment and immunofluorescence staining before being deposited onto chamber slides by centrifugation. Critically, we used multiplexed live-cell imaging to visualize and identify target cells based on specific immunophenotypes. After identification using CyteMapper software, individual live cells were retrieved using semi-automated needle-based retrieval (CytePicker) and deposited into PCR tubes for SMART-Seq2 full-length transcript scRNA-Seq (Figure 1A).

(C) Venn diagram showing the number of total genes detected in LiP-Seq, Sort-Seq, or both.

To validate the sensitivity of the platform, we first analyzed a bone marrow sample from a patient with MCL in clinical CR after induction chemotherapy. Although standard clinical flow cytometry detected no CD19+ cells (0 of 5 × 105 analyzed events), LiP-Seq successfully identified the ultrarare targets. Following magnetic bead-based depletion of dead cells, T cells (CD3), and myeloid cells (CD33), the sample was stained with an MCL-specific panel (CD45, CD19, CD5). We successfully retrieved 3 CD45+CD19+CD5+ cells from 7.5 × 105 enriched cells, yielding a postenrichment frequency of 4 × 10–6 and an overall frequency of 3 × 10–7 in the total sample (∼3 cells per 10 million) (supplemental Figure 1A-B).

Next, we benchmarked LiP-Seq against standard flow cytometry-based sorting (Sort-Seq) using 3 primary MCL samples with high tumor burden. Transcriptional profiles showed strong correlation between picked and sorted cells (supplemental Figure 1C). However, LiP-Seq demonstrated superior data quality, with 189 of 192 picked cells (98.4%) detecting >5000 genes, compared with 253 of 288 cells (87.8%) for Sort-Seq. Furthermore, LiP-Seq captured a significantly higher median number of genes per cell (11 636 vs 8726), despite comparable total sequencing depth and mitochondrial content (Figure 1B). Notably, the 5262 genes detected exclusively in LiP-Seq exhibited lower expression levels than the shared genes detected by both platforms (Figure 1C; supplemental Figure 1D), indicating an enhanced capacity to capture low-abundance transcripts. We attribute this performance advantage to 2 factors, the direct visual confirmation of cell integrity before retrieval and the preservation of RNA quality by avoiding hydrodynamic shear stress associated with high-pressure sorting.

LiP-Seq in CAR-T treated patients with MCL

Next, we applied LiP-Seq to a longitudinal clinical cohort comprising 44 paired peripheral blood samples from 11 patients with MCL, collected before and after CD19-directed CAR-T therapy (brexucabtagene autoleucel) (Figure 2A-B). All patients initially achieved CR by positron emission tomography-computed tomography. With a median follow-up of 42 months, 3 patients experienced documented disease relapse (MCL01, MCL07, MCL09), 2 of whom subsequently died from the disease. One additional patient died without obvious relapse (MCL02) (Figure 2B; supplemental Tables 1 and 2).

Following negative enrichment, samples were stained with an MCL-specific panel (CD45+CD79b+CD5+CD3−). Critically, although we monitored CD19 expression, we did not mandate CD19 positivity for cell retrieval, ensuring the capture of potential antigen-negative variants frequently observed after CAR-T therapy.6 Parallel flow cytometry quantified tumor burden and CAR-T persistence (Figure 2C-D). As expected, circulating MCL cells were profoundly depleted after treatment, often falling below the limit of detection by standard flow (Figure 2C). CAR-T expansion peaked at M1 and was minimally detectable beyond M3. Total T-cell kinetics mirrored this trend, peaking at M1 and declining at M2, followed by a gradual recovery starting at M3 (Figure 2D; supplemental Figure 2A).

We performed LiP-Seq on longitudinal patient samples (before and after therapy), alongside healthy control B cells (n = 3), capturing a total of 1683 single cells. Unsupervised transcriptional clustering revealed that cells primarily clustered by treatment phase rather than by patient identity (Figure 2E-F; supplemental Figure 2B), suggesting that persistent cells enter a distinct, shared transcriptional state under the selective pressure of CAR-T therapy. Notably, ∼82% of persistent cells retained surface CD19 expression as confirmed by live-cell imaging (supplemental Figure 2C).

Consistently, the pathognomonic MCL driver cyclin D1 (CCND1) was highly expressed in pretreatment samples, but was downregulated in the persistent fraction (Figure 2G; supplemental Figure 2D). This finding aligns with emerging evidence, which links low CCND1 levels to therapeutic resistance in MCL.20 For example, Restelli et al demonstrated that drug-resistant JeKo-1 cells exhibit marked CCND1 downregulation and that re-expression restores its sensitivity, suggesting a mechanism where cells bypass standard checkpoints to survive. To validate this phenotype specifically in the setting of CAR-T therapy, we performed an in vitro coculture assay using JeKo-1 cells. In the assay, we observed a significant 53.5% reduction in CCND1 protein levels in the rare surviving MCL population compared with controls (supplemental Figure 3A), suggesting that persistent cells may undergo a profound shift toward a treatment-tolerant, CCND1-low state to evade immune-mediated clearance.

Next, we reconstructed BCR repertoires from SMART-Seq2 data using TCR-receptor utilities for solid tissue v4 (TRUST4).14 In most pretreatment MCL cells, we detected a dominant monoclonal BCR clonotype (supplemental Figure 3B-C), contrasting with the polyclonal repertoire observed in healthy donor B cells (supplemental Figure 3D). Interestingly, cells recovered from late time points (M4 and beyond) also exhibited polyclonal BCRs, which most likely represent hematogones (early regenerating B cells) that are known to emerge when CAR T cells are no longer present.21 Notably, persistent cells from the early posttreatment phase (M1-M3) showed limited detection of BCR transcripts (supplemental Figure 3C). To rule out technical dropout, we assessed single-cell quality metrics and confirmed that these BCR-undetectable cells maintained a high library quality, with a median of >8400 detected genes per cell (supplemental Figure 3E-G). This suggests that the absence of BCR may represent a bona fide downregulation rather than a technical artifact. Biologically, this likely reflects a global suppression of the immunoglobulin H regulatory axis, which drives both BCR expression and the t(11;14)-translocated CCND1, explaining the simultaneous decreased level of both markers.

Given this profound phenotypic plasticity and the loss of lineage-defining markers, we sought more definitive evidence to confirm the malignant origin of these cells. To resolve this ambiguity, we developed a pipeline to identify tumor-specific SNVs from LiP-Seq data, allowing us to distinguish tumor cells from normal cells based on genotype rather than unstable transcriptional phenotypes.

IFITM2 is upregulated in persistent cells after CAR-T therapy

To assign lineage to cells lacking BCR information, we used cells with verified clonal BCRs as tumor reference and those with polyclonal BCRs as normal reference to identify patient-specific informative expressed variants. We applied this SNV-based classification to 2 patients with sufficient detection of distinguishing variants, including 1 patient who experienced overt relapse at 14 months (MCL07) and 1 who was in sustained remission (MCL11) (supplemental Figure 4A-B). We validated the presence of these informative variants using PCR amplification from scRNA-seq cDNA libraries (supplemental Figure 4C). Then, we applied this method to cells with BCR information and achieved 99% accuracy for normal cells (n = 106) and 97.3% for tumor cells (n = 75) (supplemental Figure 4D).

Next, we classified cells without BCR detection as tumor or normal based on these informative variants. Of these, ∼25% of cells in M1 to M3 were predicted as SNV-defined tumor cells, whereas ∼10% of cells in M4+ were predicted as tumor cells, all of which were from the relapsed patient MCL07 (Figure 3A-B). As additional validation of this classification, visual inspection of the KMT2D locus in patient MCL07 confirmed that predicted tumor cells harbored the exact same somatic mutations as the bona fide BCR+ tumor reference, whereas predicted normal cells retained the wild-type sequence (supplemental Figure 4E).

To provide genetic support for the malignant identity of persistent populations, we performed WGS on a second batch of single cells retrieved using LiP-Seq from patients MCL04 and MCL06. This approach provided a high-resolution, DNA-based lineage tracing that was independent of transcriptional variation or phenotypic plasticity. By comparing pooled pretreatment tumor cells, normal B-cell controls, and posttreatment persistent cells, we identified patient-specific somatic mutations using the GATK pipeline. This analysis revealed a suite of somatic variants that were highly consistent and strongly correlated between the diagnostic tumor and the ultrarare persistent cells retrieved after therapy but not detected in the matched normal cells from the same patient.

Correlation heat maps and scatterplots demonstrated that the somatic mutation profiles of the persistent fraction were representative of those found in the pretreatment tumor samples for each patient (supplemental Figure 5A-C). Furthermore, visualization of reads at specific loci confirmed the presence of identical tumor-specific mutations at both time point (supplemental Figure 5D-E). These shared genomic markers provide additional evidence that the persistent cells captured by LiP-Seq are directly related to the original malignant clone. This genetic validation supports their identity even in instances where hallmark phenotypic marker expression is significantly downregulated, thereby addressing the limitations of transcript-based identification.

By restricting our differential expression analysis exclusively to the rigorously validated, SNV-defined tumor fraction, we identified IFITM2 that was upregulated in the persistent cells of both MCL07 and MCL11 compared with their respective pretreatment specimens (Figure 3C-D). Expanding this analysis to the broader cohort, we found that IFITM2 was recurrently overexpressed in posttreatment cells (M1-M3) across all patients, independent of SNV classification or eventual relapse within the follow-up period (Figure 3E-F). This convergence suggests that IFITM2 upregulation is not merely a patient-specific event but may be more generally associated with persistence in ultrarare residual cells. We reasoned that persistent MCL cells present particularly at M1 to M3, when there were abundant CAR-T in the same sample, may have important adaptations that allowed them to survive in the presence of CAR-T independent of whether the patient experienced later overt relapse.

To functionally validate this finding under therapeutic pressure, we assessed IFITM2 expression using an in vitro coculture assay of MCL cells with CD19-targeted CAR T cells. Remarkably, the rare population of JeKo-1 cells that survived 4 days of exposure to CAR-T cytotoxicity expressed IFITM2 at a level threefold higher than in cells cocultured with untransduced T cells (Figure 3G). This in vitro recapitulation confirms that IFITM2 upregulation is associated with persistence in the setting of CAR-T–mediated selection.

IFITM2 overexpression induces therapy resistance and is associated with poor clinical outcomes

To functionally validate the role of IFITM2 in mediating therapeutic persistence, we engineered JeKo-1 cells to exogenously overexpress IFITM2 before CD19-directed CAR-T exposure (Figure 4A). We observed that IFITM2 overexpression was sufficient to confer significant protection against CAR-T–mediated cytotoxicity compared with empty vector controls (Figure 4B). Collectively, these functional data, integrated with our primary LiP-seq transcriptomic profiling, nominate the cell surface protein IFITM2 as a potential mediator of survival and immune evasion in MCL.

The IFITM family is known to mediate cellular responses to interferons,22 and we previously found that IFITM3 acts as a scaffold to amplify Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) kinase signaling in malignant and nonmalignant B cells.23 Elevated expression of IFITM1, IFITM2, or IFITM3 are predictors of poor prognosis in colorectal,24 prostate,25 ovarian,26 lung,27 liver,28 breast,29 and B-cell cancers.23 To further evaluate the clinical significance of IFITM2 in B-cell malignancies, we queried the National Cancer Institute Cancer Immunology Data Engine (NCI CIDE) database to analyze patient outcomes.30 Our analysis revealed that high IFITM2 expression was significantly associated with inferior overall survival across multiple B-cell malignancies, including MCL, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Figure 4C-E). Notably, in the MCL cohort, high IFITM2 expression correlated with a rapid decline in survival probability within the first 2 years (P = .0019), with similar trends observed in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (P = .0133) and B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (P < .0001) cohorts. These data suggest that IFITM2 is linked with prognosis and may be a driver of therapeutic persistence across B-cell malignancies and types of treatment.

Discussion

Understanding the biology of ultrarare cells that survive CAR-T therapy is critical for preventing relapse in patients with MCL. To address this challenge, we developed LiP-Seq, a novel strategy that couples live-cell retrieval with single-cell transcriptomics to characterize viable, ultrarare persistent cells. A central challenge in this field is to accurately define the malignant state when tumor burden is low and phenotypic markers are unstable. Our study highlights the profound phenotypic plasticity of these persistent cells, which may downregulate hallmark markers such as CCND1 in the setting of therapy evasion. This lineage infidelity creates a significant risk of misclassification in standard clinical assays. To address appropriate skepticism surrounding the identity of these rare cells, we performed expressed SNV-based tumor classification and WGS. The genomic validation confirmed that the persistent cells share a somatic mutation profile with the diagnostic tumor, suggesting that the observed phenotypic shift represents a bona fide biological remodeling of a drug-tolerant persister state rather than a technical artifact.

By focusing on these rigorously validated tumor cells, we identified the IFITM2 axis as a common driver of their survival. IFITM2 overexpression confers relative protection against CAR-T cytotoxicity in vitro. High IFITM2 expression also correlates with inferior clinical outcomes in external B-cell malignancy cohorts. Although it remains unclear whether IFITM2 expression is independently prognostic when combined with known lymphoma risk features, these data suggest that future work on this pathway is warranted. In addition, it is critical to distinguish between the detection of persistent cells and the inevitability of treatment failure. As observed in the pivotal ZUMA-2 trial, patients can remain MRD-positive for extended periods without experiencing overt relapse, suggesting a state of immune equilibrium.9 Our findings provide a molecular basis for this phenomenon: the upregulation of IFITM2, coupled with the downregulation of proliferative markers such as CCND1, may enable these cells to survive treatment pressure while remaining clinically indolent. Thus, rather than representing a rapidly expanding relapse clone, these cells likely constitute a latent reservoir of therapeutic persistence.

Our findings raise several important questions that require further investigations. The limitations of our study include the relatively small cohort size and the low throughput of LiP-Seq compared with standard flow cytometry. Consequently, although IFITM2 upregulation emerged as a consistent feature of the persistent state, larger longitudinal cohorts are necessary to determine if specific expression thresholds can definitively predict the transition from dormancy to overt relapse. In addition, absolute quantification of tumor burden in samples with extremely low tumor content remains technically challenging because of the potential coisolation of nonmalignant bystander cells. Future studies incorporating paired DNA-based profiling, such as simultaneous single-cell genotyping and transcriptomics, will be essential to fully dissect the heterogeneity of these reservoirs.

In conclusion, LiP-Seq complements standard clinical assays by enabling the live retrieval of ultrarare cells for downstream biological characterization and functional validation. Although the method requires rigorous genomic controls to distinguish malignant cells, it uniquely uncovers vulnerabilities like the IFITM2 axis that may be masked in clinically overt relapse populations. Ultimately, technologies that unveil the biology of these viable, drug-tolerant persistent cells will be indispensable for closing the gap between remission and cure in patients with MCL.
