Unmasking the “Mixed-Dysregulated” phenotype: latent profiles of affective temperaments and non-suicidal self-injury in adolescents with depression
Authors: Shaowei Liu, Bin Ma, Yanju Liu, Xiaofang Liu, Xiuli Li, Xingxing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is highly prevalent among adolescents with depression, yet the heterogeneity of underlying temperamental risk factors remains poorly understood. Traditional variable-centered approaches fail to capture how distinct affective temperaments co-occur within individuals. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of affective temperaments and examine their association with NSSI, exploring the statistical mediating role of cognitive emotion regulation (CER).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from February 2025 to September 2025 at the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University. A total of 290 adolescents (aged 10–19) diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder were recruited, with 282 valid responses included in the final analysis. Participants completed the TEMPS-A, CERQ, and ASHS. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) was utilized to identify temperament subgroups. Mediation analysis with bootstrapping was performed to test the indirect effects of CER strategies.ResultsLPA identified three distinct profiles: Resilient/Low-risk (Class 1, 32.6%), Anxious-Depressive (Class 2, 46.1%), and Mixed-Dysregulated (Class 3, 21.3%). The Mixed-Dysregulated group, characterized by simultaneous elevations in depressive, anxious, irritable, and cyclothymic temperaments, exhibited the highest frequency (45.2 ± 21.3 times/year) and prevalence (98.8%) of NSSI compared to other groups (p <.001). Mediation analysis revealed that the impact of the high-risk profile on NSSI was significantly mediated by maladaptive CER strategies, specifically catastrophizing and rumination.ConclusionThe findings delineate a specific “Mixed-Dysregulated” risk phenotype within adolescent depression that is associated with severe NSSI. Interventions should move beyond standard depression care to target cognitive flexibility and emotional regulation skills. Statistical mediation analysis suggests that this risk is mediated by maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation strategies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Adolescent depression represents a critical public health crisis, with its prevalence escalating globally in the post-pandemic era [1]. A particularly concerning manifestation of this pathology is Non-Suicidal Self-Injury (NSSI), defined as the deliberate, self-inflicted destruction of body tissue without suicidal intent [2]. Recent epidemiological data indicate that a substantial proportion of adolescents with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) engage in NSSI, with reporting rates as high as 48.2% [3] and other studies highlighting its high prevalence in this clinical population [4]. Beyond its immediate physical harm, NSSI serves as a robust predictor for future suicide attempts, marking a trajectory of escalating psychopathology [5]. Despite the availability of pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions, recurrence or persistence of NSSI remains a significant clinical challenge [6], and treatment responses are often heterogeneous [5]. This clinical recalcitrance suggests that current diagnostic frameworks, which often treat “depression” as a homogeneous entity, may fail to capture the heterogeneous biological and psychological underpinnings of self-injurious behaviors, as evidenced by complex network interactions between depressive symptoms and NSSI [7], and the varied roles of emotional regulation and other comorbid factors [8]. The evolving trends and persistent high burden of NSSI underscore the need for a more nuanced understanding of its mechanisms within adolescent depression [9].

The construct of affective temperaments, rooted in the biopsychological theory proposed by Akiskal, offers a promising lens for dissecting this heterogeneity. Temperaments are stable, biologically determined personality traits that predispose individuals to specific mood disorders [10]. The five fundamental temperaments—depressive, anxious, hyperthymic, cyclothymic, and irritable—have been extensively linked to psychopathology. Traditional variable-centered studies have consistently shown that depressive and anxious temperaments are positively correlated with NSSI severity [11]. However, these variable-centered linear models possess a significant limitation: they isolate specific traits (e.g., “high anxiety”) while ignoring the clinical reality that patients often present with complex constellations of traits. For instance, an adolescent may exhibit co-occurring high depressive and irritable traits simultaneously, a configuration that may confer a distinct risk for persistent self-injury and suicidality compared to depression alone [12]. To date, few studies have employed a person-centered approach, such as Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), to identify high-risk subgroups based on temperament configurations in clinical adolescent populations [13].

Furthermore, while temperament provides the “biological hardware,” the translation of these traits into behavioral outcomes (like NSSI) is often modulated by “software”—specifically, Cognitive Emotion Regulation (CER) strategies. The diathesis-stress model posits that individuals with vulnerable temperaments are more prone to utilizing maladaptive coping mechanisms [14]. Recent literature suggests that strategies such as rumination (repetitive focus on distress) and catastrophizing (overestimating the magnitude of negative events) are potent transdiagnostic factors for NSSI [15, 16]. Conversely, adaptive strategies like cognitive reappraisal may buffer against risk [17]. It is hypothesized that specific high-risk temperament profiles may be associated with NSSI precisely because they predispose adolescents to cognitive inflexibility and maladaptive regulation [18–20].

Addressing these gaps, the present study aims to: (1) identify latent profiles of affective temperaments in a clinical sample of adolescents with depression using LPA; and (2) examine whether the association between these profiles and NSSI is statistically mediated by specific CER strategies. We hypothesized that a “mixed” or “dysregulated” profile would emerge as the highest risk group and that its association with NSSI would be mediated by maladaptive regulation strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between February 2025 and September 2025 at the Department of Psychiatry, the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University. The reporting of this study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [21]. Participants were adolescents aged 10 to 19 years (consistent with the World Health Organization definition) diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) according to DSM-5 criteria [22].

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age 10–19 years; (2) Primary diagnosis of MDD confirmed by two senior psychiatrists; (3) Ability to comprehend and complete the questionnaires; (4) Voluntary participation with informed consent from both the adolescent and their guardians.

Exclusion criteria:

(1) Comorbidity with schizophrenia, intellectual disability, or organic brain disorders; (2) Active psychotic symptoms preventing assessment; (3) Current participation in other interventional trials.

As illustrated in Fig. 1 (Flowchart), a total of 290 patients were initially screened. Eight participants were excluded due to incomplete questionnaires (> 10% missing data) or withdrawal of consent. The final analytical sample consisted of 282 adolescents.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. 2024199). All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians.

Measures

Demographic and clinical information

A self-designed questionnaire collected data on age, gender, only-child status, parental relationship quality, and duration of illness.

Affective temperaments

The Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris and San Diego-Auto questionnaire (TEMPS-A) was used to assess five temperament dimensions: depressive, cyclothymic, hyperthymic, irritable, and anxious [23]. The Chinese version has demonstrated good psychometric properties. Items are rated on a True/False scale (1/0). Higher scores indicate greater prominence of the respective temperament. In the current sample, the Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.859.

Cognitive emotion regulation

The Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ) assesses nine cognitive coping strategies after experiencing negative events [24]. It comprises two broad dimensions: Adaptive Regulation (e.g., acceptance, positive reappraisal) and Maladaptive/Non-adaptive Regulation (e.g., self-blame, rumination, catastrophizing, blaming others). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.830.

Non-suicidal self-injury

The Adolescent Self-Harm Scale (ASHS) was employed to assess the frequency and methods of NSSI over the past 12 months [25]. The questionnaire includes functional and frequency dimensions. For this study, we utilized the total frequency of NSSI behaviors and a binary variable (Yes/No) for prevalence estimation. Cronbach’s α was 0.963.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and Mplus 8.3 (Muthén & Muthén).

Preliminary analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) characterized the sample. Pearson’s correlation analysis examined the bivariate relationships among study variables. We also assessed multicollinearity for regression models; Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were all < 3.0, indicating no severe multicollinearity issues despite theoretical overlaps between constructs.

Latent profile analysis (LPA)

LPA was conducted in Mplus to identify subgroups based on the five TEMPS-A subscale scores. Models with 1 to 5 distinct classes were estimated. To ensure model stability and avoid local maxima, we used multiple random starting values (STARTS = 500 100). Model fit was evaluated using: (1) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), where lower values indicate better fit; (2) Entropy (values approaching 1 indicate distinct classification); (3) Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR-LRT) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), where significant p-values (< 0.05) indicate the k-class model is superior to the k-1 class model [13]. The final model selection balanced statistical indices with clinical interpretability.

Comparison and mediation

After assigning individuals to their most likely latent class, Chi-square tests and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post-hoc tests compared demographic and clinical variables across classes. Finally, a mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 4) to test the indirect effects of maladaptive emotion regulation between latent profiles (independent variable) and NSSI (outcome). Significance of indirect effects was assessed using 5,000 bootstrap resamples (95% Confidence Intervals excluding zero). It is important to note that due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, this mediation analysis establishes statistical associations rather than causal pathways.

Identification of latent profiles

LPA models with 1 to 5 classes were fitted to the data (see Table 3). The fit indices (AIC, BIC, aBIC) decreased as the number of classes increased. However, the LMR-LRT for the 4-class model was non-significant (p =.182), suggesting no substantial improvement over the 3-class model. The 3-class solution demonstrated high classification accuracy (Entropy = 0.84) and theoretically meaningful subgroups. Average posterior probabilities for the three classes were all > 0.85, indicating reliable classification. Thus, the 3-class model was retained.

NSSI

The Mixed-Dysregulated Group (Class 3) reported the highest frequency of NSSI (45.2 ± 21.3), nearly double that of Class 2 (18.6 ± 12.4), while Class 1 had the lowest (2.15 ± 3.5). The prevalence of NSSI in Class 3 reached 98.8%.

Emotion regulation: Class 3 scored significantly higher on maladaptive strategies (Total score: 61.8 ± 7.5), particularly in subdomains of Catastrophizing and Rumination, compared to Class 2 and Class 1. Conversely, Class 1 demonstrated superior adaptive regulation capacities.

Mediation analysis

Based on the regression results and class differences, we constructed a mediation model with Latent Class membership (dummy coded: Class 1 as reference) as the predictor, Maladaptive Regulation as the mediator, and NSSI frequency as the outcome.

The results (illustrated in Fig. 3) indicated that: (1) Being in the Mixed-Dysregulated Group was associated with higher Maladaptive Regulation (β = 0.65, p <.001). (2) Maladaptive Regulation was associated with NSSI frequency (β = 0.58, p <.001). (3) The indirect effect of Class 3 on NSSI via Maladaptive Regulation was significant (Effect = 12.45, 95% CI [8.32, 17.56]). This confirms that the heightened risk of NSSI in the mixed-temperament group is partially explained by their tendency to engage in maladaptive cognitive coping.

Discussion

This study represents one of the first attempts to employ Latent Profile Analysis to dissect the heterogeneity of affective temperaments in adolescents with depression and NSSI. By moving beyond variable-centered approaches, we identified three distinct phenotypes. Our central finding is the delineation of a “Mixed-Dysregulated” subgroup (Class 3), which, despite constituting only ~ 21% of the sample, accounted for the most severe NSSI behaviors. Furthermore, we elucidated the mechanism behind this risk: a profound reliance on maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation.

The identification of the Mixed-Dysregulated profile conceptually resonates with the concept of “Soft Bipolarity” in the pediatric population, as reflected in contemporary spectrum models [26]. Unlike Class 2 (Anxious-Depressive), which captures a “classic” internalizing depression presentation often characterized by comorbid anxiety [27], Class 3 is distinguished by high irritability and cyclothymic instability superimposed on depressive mood [28]. This clinical constellation mirrors the “dysphoric mania” or mixed states described in the adult bipolar literature [26]. Within this profile, severe irritability may act as a behavioral energizer, transforming static depressive cognition into kinetic self-destructive action, a mechanism underscored in recent translational models of youth irritability [29]. The finding that this group exhibits near-universal engagement in NSSI (98.8%) aligns with recent research by Baldini et al. [30], which highlights how complex social-personal factors and comorbidities amplify suicidal risk in adolescent inpatients. The finding that this group exhibits near-universal engagement in nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) with extreme frequency is consistent with meta-analytic evidence highlighting NSSI as a prevalent correlate in adolescents with significant psychopathology [31]. This supports the notion that NSSI in these patients is not merely a cry for help but may represent a desperate attempt to regulate intense, labile affective storms driven by underlying cyclothymic instability, a function commonly reported in studies of adolescent self-injury [32]. However, it is crucial to interpret this “Mixed-Dysregulated” profile as a high-risk phenotype rather than definitive evidence of bipolar pathology per se, given the cross-sectional nature of our assessment.

Crucially, our mediation analysis suggests a pathway through which biology (temperament) may relate to pathology (NSSI). The Mixed-Dysregulated adolescents scored highest on Catastrophizing and Rumination, a pattern indicative of cognitive inflexibility [20]. When an adolescent with a cyclothymic/irritable temperament encounters a stressor, their “biological hardware” may trigger an intense, rapid emotional surge. Lacking adaptive regulation skills, they resort to rumination (getting stuck in the negative thought) and catastrophizing (amplifying the threat) [33]. This cognitive amplification creates an unbearable state of psychic tension, for which NSSI becomes the most immediate, albeit maladaptive, release mechanism [34]. This finding is consistent with research emphasizing the transdiagnostic role of emotion regulation in self-injury [35].

Clinical implications

The results have profound implications for precision psychiatry. (1) Assessment: Clinicians should routinely screen for temperament profiles using the short version of the TEMPS-A, as latent profile analysis of this instrument can effectively distinguish between major depressive and bipolar disorders [36]. Identifying a patient as “Class 3” alerts the clinician to a high risk of persistent non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and potential bipolar diathesis, which might be missed by standard depression inventories, as cyclothymic temperament is strongly linked to enduring self-harm and suicidality [12]. (2) Treatment: Our findings suggest tailored interventions may be beneficial. For the Anxious-Depressive group (Class 2), SSRIs (as a first-line treatment) and standard CBT might be sufficient, as this patient profile often aligns with less severe clinical presentations responsive to these interventions [37]. However, clinical caution is advised for the Mixed-Dysregulated group (Class 3), where SSRIs monotherapy could theoretically induce agitation or rapid cycling [38]. While our data do not directly test treatment efficacy, mood stabilizers or low-dose atypical antipsychotics—often in combination as recommended for mixed features—might be considered as part of a cautious management strategy [39]. Moreover, psychological interventions must focus specifically on Emotion Regulation Therapy (ERT) or Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) to break the cycle of catastrophizing and rumination, rather than solely focusing on depressive content, given the central role of maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation strategies in NSSI [40].

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference; we can only report associations, not developmental trajectories. Longitudinal tracking is needed to see if Class 3 predicts transition to Bipolar Disorder. Second, all variables were assessed via self-report, which increases the risk of shared method variance and social desirability bias, although we used validated tools to mitigate this. Third, the sample was drawn from a single clinical center in Hebei. The extremely high prevalence of NSSI (86.2%) indicates a referral bias toward severe cases typical of tertiary psychiatric care, limiting generalizability to community populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, adolescent depression is not a monolithic entity. We identified a distinct “Mixed-Dysregulated” temperament profile characterized by high irritability and cyclothymia, which is associated with a disproportionate risk for severe NSSI. This risk is mediated by maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation strategies. These findings advocate for a paradigm shift towards personalized interventions that target specific temperament-regulation interactions to mitigate self-injury risks in youth.
