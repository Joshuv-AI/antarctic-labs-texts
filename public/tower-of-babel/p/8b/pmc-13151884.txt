Gender inequalities and the burden of anorexia and bulimia in Europe, 1990–2023: findings from the global burden of disease study 2023
Authors: Giulia Zamagni, Elisa Fabbro, Maria Paola Rapagnani, Massimiliano Beghi, Jose Luis Ayuso-Mateos, Benedetta Armocida, Paola Bertuccio, Giovanni de Girolamo, Alessandro Gialluisi, Josep Maria Haro, Katarzyna Kissimova-Skarbek, Matilde Leonardi, Eleonora Maurel, Alberto Raggi, Lorenzo Monasta, Giulio Castelpietra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEating disorders (EDs), including anorexia nervosa (AN) and bulimia nervosa (BN), are severe mental health conditions affecting physical and psychosocial health. Structural gender inequality may shape the burden of EDs across sexes and regions. We analyzed Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 estimates to assess the relationship between gender inequality and ED burden in Europe. Years Lived with Disability (YLD) for AN and BN were extracted by sex and five-year age groups (10–39 years) from 1990 to 2023 across 35 countries. Gender inequality was measured using the Gender Inequality Index (GII). Mixed linear regression was applied with year, age group, sex, GII, and their interactions as fixed effects, and country and year as random intercept and slope, respectively. YLDs increased over time across most countries and age groups, with females consistently experiencing the highest burden. For AN, higher levels of GII were significantly associated with greater YLD rates in both males (β = 29.28; 95% CI: 22.2 to 36.4) and females (β = 85.3; 95% CI: 42.1 to 128.6), with a stronger effect for females. For BN, GII was inversely associated with YLDs among males (β = –645.6; 95% CI: –732.8 to –558.3), whereas a positive association was observed among females (β = 794.7; 95% CI: 659.1 to 930.2). Higher burden of AD and BN among young Europeans in more gender-equal settings coexisted with sex-specific patterns associated with increasing gender inequality, underscoring the complexity of gender-related factors and the need for gender-sensitive mental health strategies.

Introduction

Eating disorders (ED) are characterized by either extreme reduction of food or excessive nutrition and high stress and concern for weight or shape, compromising both physical health and social functioning. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, the Fifth Edition (DSM-5), EDs are classified under “Feeding and Eating Disorders” and include six diagnostic categories, with Anorexia nervosa (AN) and Bulimia nervosa (BN) being the most prevalent [1]. In Western countries, EDs are a common, especially among young people (10–40 age range). The lifetime prevalence of EDs is heterogeneous, with AN ranging between 0.8–6.3% in women and 0.1–0.3% in men, while BN ranges between 0.8–2.6% in women and 0.1–0.2% in men [2]. These numbers are more than doubled when considering only young people. Moreover, previous findings from the Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) estimates indicated a rise in the burden of EDs in European young people, with the highest increase of Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) among mental conditions from 1990 to 2019 [3]. Recently, the COVID-19 pandemic has further increased EDs incidence, especially among young people and women [2, 4, 5]. Nonetheless, the prevalence, incidence, and burden of EDs among males are likely much higher than reported, owing to underdiagnosis, misdiagnosis, and stigma [6]. The gender-specific literature on EDs is also limited, with males often underrepresented or excluded. This underrepresentation may reflect systemic and cultural barriers to diagnosis and care for males, highlighting a broader health equity issue [7, 8]. On the other hand, it appears crucial to consider gender-related factors to design effective interventions [2].

There are different metrics measuring gender differences. The Gender Inequality Index (GII) is among the most widely used indices, combining indicators of reproductive health, empowerment, and labor-market participation [9]. Recent studies using the GII show that in societies with higher gender inequalities, females experience higher rates of depression than males, and adolescents in these contexts are more likely to feel fat [10, 11]. No studies, however, have analysed the association between the burden of EDs and GII. Moreover, GBD burden estimates do not capture gendered structural contexts.

Thus, we aimed to quantify the burden of EDs among young people in Europe using GBD 2023 estimates and explore its association with the GII, which was used to assess whether sex-specific burden varies across countries with different levels of gender inequality. Additionally, we evaluated the association between the number of country-level data sources used to estimate the burden of EDs and the GII. Quantifying the burden of EDs and examining implications for health systems can generate important evidence to foster more equitable and effective public health policies.

Methods

GBD 2023 provides estimates of prevalence, incidence, and mortality for 375 causes, following the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) standards. Each new round of estimates replaces those from previous rounds, as the entire time series is updated annually to reflect new data and methodological improvements. Specifically, estimates are produced using a standardized Bayesian meta-regression framework that integrates heterogeneous data sources, borrows strength across locations and over time, and propagates uncertainty from the underlying data to the final estimates, ensuring internal consistency and cross-country comparability. A detailed description of the methods used in GBD 2023 is available in the capstone paper [12]. Data on the GII were retrieved from the Human Development Reports [9].

Data sources

Estimates were derived from data on incidence and prevalence identified through systematic searches of published and unpublished documents, survey microdata, administrative records, registries, and disease surveillance systems stored in the GBD Sources tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org). The number of data sources by GBD region, location, cause, and sex is summarized in Table S2.

GBD measures

The non-fatal burden of EDs was measured using YLDs, calculated as the product of prevalence and the corresponding disability weight [12]. Disability weights were derived from international surveys using a scale ranging from 0 (perfect health) to 1 (death). Additional information on disability weights is provided in the Supplementary Files. Estimates were reported as the mean of 250 posterior draws, with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) derived as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the same distribution.

Gender Inequality Index (GII)

GII captures gender-based disadvantage across three domains: reproductive health, empowerment, and labor-market participation, reflecting the loss in human development potential due to gender inequality. GII values range from 0 (full equality) to 1 (maximum inequality) [9].

Data presentation

Estimates of YLDs of AN and BN were provided from 1990 to 2023 and extracted by sex and 5-year-age groups (i.e. 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39 years) for 45 countries within Western, Central, and Eastern Europe. However, countries with fewer than 1 000 000 inhabitants were excluded from the analysis to ensure robustness and reduce potential volatility in estimates due to small populations. The final analysis covered only 35 countries with available GII. The age range from 10 to 39 years was selected based on previous GBD evidence showing that the burden of EDs peaks in adolescence and early adulthood, with a marked decline after age 40 [13]. Table S1 reports the selected countries by region and GII value in 2023, along with the percentage change from the baseline year. All estimates were reported as rates per 100 000 population and 95% UIs. Although the focus of this paper is on YLDs, estimates of ED prevalence by year, sex, and age group are provided in Tables S7–S10 to support interpretation and comparability with previous studies. These estimates are presented for reference only and not further analysed.

Statistical analysis

To assess the association between GII influence and the burden of Eds, two multivariable mixed-effects linear regression models were fitted on the point estimate of YLDs for AN and BN. Fixed effects included age group, year, sex, GII, and their interactions, to capture joint effects. A random intercept was specified for each location to account for unobserved heterogeneity between countries. A random slope for year was included to allow for location-specific time trends in ED burden.

Model assumptions were evaluated using residual diagnostics. Results were presented as regression coefficients for fixed effects and standard deviation (SD) for random effects, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Residual variance was allowed to vary by sex and GBD regions to account for within-group variability.

The correlation between the number of available data sources for EDs and GII was assessed using the Spearman correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at 0.05. All the analyses were conducted using R [14].

Results

Gender Inequality Index (GII)

GII showed considerable variation across regions in both 1990 and 2023, with attenuating differences over. Throughout the period, Western Europe consistently reported the lowest GII values compared to Central and Eastern Europe, although all regions demonstrated a downward trend, particularly after 2000 (Fig. S1). The geographic distribution of GII across locations is illustrated in Fig. S1a.

Years lived with disability due to anorexia nervosa

Figure S2b shows the geographic distribution of YLDs due to AN in 2023, for both sexes. YLDs of AN increased over time across all regions, with Western Europe persistently exhibiting the highest burden in both sexes (Fig. 1). Among males, YLD rates rose with age, peaking at 20–24 years. Females showed a similar age-related pattern but experienced a higher burden across all age groups, with peaks at 20–24 and 25–29 years.

In general, the non-fatal burden of AN was substantially higher in Western Europe throughout the period compared to both Central and Eastern Europe, highlighting a persistent geographical gradient. Overall, in 1990 the highest YLD rates for AN among males aged 10–39 years were found in Norway (20.9 per 100 000; 95% UI: 11.0–32.4), with a peak in the 15–19 age group (29.4 per 100 000; 95% UI: 14.9–47.3) (Table S3). Among females, Finland showed the highest rate in the 10–39 age range (94.9 per 100 000; 95% UI: 49.4–159.2), again peaking at ages 15–19 (148.0 per 100 000; 95% UI: 70.0–250.7) (Table S4). In 2023, Switzerland ranked highest among males aged 10–39 years (25.7 per 100 000; 95% UI: 12.0–42.8), with the greatest burden in the 20–24 age group (37.7 per 100 000; 95% UI: 17.2–64.5), whereas Finland remained the most affected among females (102.3 per 100 000; 95% UI: 54.6–162.5), still peaking at ages 15–19 (154.5 per 100 000; 95% UI: 75.6–266.4).

The model revealed a complex interplay between age, sex, GII, time, and YLD rates of AN (Table 1). Compared to males aged 10–14 years, no significant differences in YLDs were observed for other male age groups. In contrast, females showed significantly lower YLDs than males in the same age group (β = –230.9; 95% CI: –335.4 to –126.5; P < 0.001). However, the age × sex interaction indicated that, among females, YLDs were significantly higher at 35–39 years than at 10–14 years (β = 176.3; 95% CI: 28.6–324.0; P = 0.019).

GII was positively associated with AN-related YLDs in males aged 10–14 years, with a 0.1-point increase linked to an average rise of 29.3 YLD rate (95% CI: 22.2–36.4; P < 0.001). This effect was significantly stronger in females, as shown by the positive GII × sex interaction (β = 85.3; 95% CI: 42.1–128.6; P < 0.001). The relationship between GII and YLDs was largely consistent across age groups, except for males aged 35–39, for whom a negative GII × age interaction (β = –15.3; 95% CI: –24.2 to –6.3; P = 0.001) indicated a comparatively weaker association.

Over time, the effect of GII showed a modest decline, as reflected in the GII × year interaction (β = –0.015; 95% CI: –0.020 to –0.011; P < 0.001). While in females the attenuation was consistent across age groups, among males the time-dependent effect of GII varied by age: a stronger decline was observed in the 15–19 age group (β = –0.005; 95% CI: –0.010 to –0.0001; P = 0.045), whereas a slight increase emerged in the 35–39 age group (β = 0.008; 95% CI: 0.003–0.012; P = 0.001), both relative to the 10–14 reference group. Random effects indicated substantial between-location heterogeneity in baseline YLDs and modest variation in temporal trends. Residual variance was markedly higher in females and greatest in Western Europe, indicating substantial within-region variability in the female burden of AN.

Years lived with disability due to bulimia nervosa

A geographic distribution similar to that of AN was also found for BN throughout the study period, although YLD rates for BN were generally higher (Fig. S1c and Fig. 2). Western Europe consistently reported the highest burden, with increasing rates from 1990 to 2023. In 1990, Austria had the highest YLD rates for BN among males aged 10–39 years (106.1 per 100 000, 95% UI: 51.1–188.2), with a peak at ages 30–34 (118.3 per 100 000, 95% UI: 59.4–212.2) (Table S3). Among females, the highest rate in the 10–39 age range was observed in Norway (219.4 per 100 000, 95% UI: 118.0–390.2), peaking at ages 20–24 (311.3 per 100 000, 95% UI: 140.0–547.7) (Table S4). In 2023, Austria remained the country with the highest rates among males (114.7 per 100 000, 95% UI: 54.3–203.7), again with the greatest burden at ages 30–34 (142.2 per 100 000, 95% UI: 72.0–242.2), whereas Ireland ranked highest among females (344.3 per 100 000, 95% UI: 183.3–543.2), still peaking at ages 20–24 (351.6 per 100 000, 95% UI: 160.0–661.3).

Moreover, higher GII values were significantly associated with lower BN-related YLDs in the male reference group (β = –645.6; 95% CI: –732.8 to –558.3; P < 0.001). However, this inverse association varied by age, as indicated by positive GII × age interactions, suggesting that the association became less pronounced at older ages. Among females, the association was reversed: the GII × sex interaction was positive (β = 794.7; 95% CI: 659.1–930.2; P < 0.001). This association also varied by age, as indicated by the negative GII × sex × age interaction, suggesting that the inequality-related increase in BN burden was smaller among older females than among the youngest group.

In the reference group (i.e. males aged 10–14), BN-related YLDs increased significantly over time (β = 0.79 per year; 95% CI: 0.68–0.89; P < 0.001). However, time trends differed by age and sex. Among males, only the 20–24 age group showed a modest but significant decline over time relative to the 10–14 age group (β = –0.10; 95% CI: –0.20 to –0.002; P = 0.045). In contrast, females experienced significant increases in YLDs over time across all age groups older than 10–14 years.

The interaction between GII and year was positive (β = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.29–0.37; P < 0.001), suggesting that the inverse association between gender inequality and YLDs among younger males became weaker over time. However, this time-related weakening of the GII effect was itself age-dependent: compared to the 10–14-year group, significant negative GII × year × age interactions indicated smaller year-on-year increases in YLDs at older ages. In females, this interaction was negative (β = –0.40; 95% CI: –0.47 to –0.34; P < 0.001), indicating a gradual reduction in the inequality-associated rise in BN burden over time. Finally, all interactions between GII, sex, year, and age were positive and significant, implying that this attenuation of GII’s effect among females was especially marked at older ages compared to the 10–14-year reference group.

Random effects revealed between-location heterogeneity in both baseline YLD levels (SD = 385.5; 95% CI: 301.6–492.8) and time slopes (SD = 0.20; 95% CI: 0.14–0.24). Residual variance was higher among females than among males and highest in Western Europe (SD = 66.5 for females), indicating greater within-region variability in the female burden of BN.

Number of data sources and Gender Inequality Index

The number of data sources for EDs and the GII in 2023 was significantly correlated (Fig. S3). Specifically, the availability of data sources was substantially lower in locations with higher levels of gender inequality. Among females, the Spearman correlation coefficients were –0.62 (P < 0.001) for both AN and BN. Among males, the correlations were weaker but remained significant, with Spearman coefficients of –0.55 for AN and –0.45 for BN (P < 0.001 and P = 0.006, respectively).

Discussion

The burden of AN and BN increased from 1990 to 2023 in both sexes and regions, with BN carrying more than twice the burden as AN, particularly among males. In general, the burden of EDs was higher in females, and the 20–29 age group had the highest YLD rates in both sexes. At the geographical level, Western Europe had the highest YLD rates, although heterogeneity was observed both between and within GBD regions for AN and BN. In contrast, GII values were substantially lower in Western Europe compared to Central and Eastern Europe, despite all regions showing a declining trend after 2000. Notably, countries with higher gender inequalities had fewer available data sources, which may reflect not only broader limitations in health surveillance systems but also lower institutional or societal awareness of the issue. The model showed an association between GII and YLD rates in both sexes for AN, despite a stronger effect in females. For BN, higher GII was associated with lower YLD rates in males, while the opposite was observed in females. We, thus, found a general higher burden in countries with lower GII, such as Western and Nordic Europe, whereas the regression models show that GII is associated with the sex-specific distribution of the burden, rather than with its absolute geographical level.

Our findings of a higher burden of EDs in females aged 20–29 years in a Western setting, with BN contributing the greatest share, are consistent with the literature [2]. The increasing trend of YLD rates over time is also in line with previously reported increases in ED burden across Europe up to 2019 [3]. Although uncertainty remains as to whether the observed increase reflects a true rise in prevalence or results from changes in diagnostic practices and improved detection, recent European evidence appears to support our estimates [15–17]. Interestingly, this trend continued to rise during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, supporting findings from other studies suggesting that factors such as financial strain, limited food access, reduced availability of care and treatment, disruptions to daily routines, negative media influences, and social isolation during lockdowns may have exacerbated disordered eating behaviors [4, 5]. Overall, both general and specific ED psychopathology were found to worsen during and after the pandemic, with individuals continuing to report a perceived burden even one year later [18, 19]. Moreover, exposure to social media and body stereotypes has been suggested to trigger or exacerbate ED symptoms [20].

Sex differences in the burden of EDs reflect a complex interplay of several factors, including potential differences in true occurrence, help-seeking behavior, clinical recognition, and sociocultural norms, and the relative contribution of these components remains debated [21].

The positive association between the GII and the burden of EDs among females aligns with existing literature highlighting the broader health consequences of structural gender disparities. High levels of gender discrimination have been associated with higher rates of chronic diseases, poorer overall health, and greater exposure to violence and victimization among women [22]. Moreover, gender inequality has been shown to disproportionately affect women across various psychosocial and health dimensions, including body dissatisfaction, somatic complaints, and lower life satisfaction [9, 10]. A positive, though weaker, association between GII and YLDs for AN was also found among males. This may suggest that structural gender inequality shapes the broader social environment in ways that also compromise men’s mental health [23]. In more gender-unequal contexts, rigid masculinity norms that emphasize control, discipline, and self-restraint may foster restrictive attitudes and behaviors that can culminate in AN. Notably, regions with similar sociocultural characteristics often exhibit similar health outcomes, consistent with our observation of geographically clustered patterns in ED-related YLDs.

On the other hand, the negative association between GII and the burden of BN among males may also reflect traditional gender norms that discourage the recognition, diagnosis or even experience of symptoms culturally perceived as inconsistent with masculinity2 .In societies with higher gender inequality, hegemonic male norms and stigma surrounding emotional vulnerability may reduce help-seeking and diagnostic detection of BN, attenuating its apparent burden. Conversely, in more gender-equal settings, greater openness toward mental health and less rigid gender expectations may facilitate increased recognition and reporting of BN among males. In addition, Heinz et al. observed that gender equality is associated with greater convergence in traditionally gendered behaviors among adolescents [11]. Similarly, our findings suggest that the protective association of gender inequality in males has diminished over time, particularly for BN, mirroring the decline in GII values observed across Europe in recent decades.

Lastly, the lower availability of data sources in countries with higher GII, especially in Central and Eastern Europe, raises concerns about potential underestimation of the true ED burden in these regions [24]. In more gender-unequal settings, mental health conditions that predominantly affect women may receive less institutional attention, lower funding, and reduced visibility in national surveillance systems. Moreover, stigma and gender bias may further hinder help-seeking and diagnosis in males, especially where masculinity norms discourage acknowledgment of psychological vulnerability. Although this figure may contribute to the lower burden observed among males, underreporting can affect both sexes, to a degree that cannot be quantified.

To our knowledge, this is the first study examining the burden of EDs in relation to gender inequality among young people in Europe, covering 33 years marked by major social, demographic, and health changes. Although many previous studies at the global level did analyse the epidemiology of these disorders, many of them were based mainly on females, with gender-specific measures seldom considered [2, 25]. However, limitations should be considered. First, results related to males should be taken with caution, as men often experience delays in identification, diagnosis, and referral to specialized services for EDs, resulting in underreporting [2, 6, 25]. Second, due to the structure of the GBD database, we could only account for biological sex, whereas gender could not be assessed [2]. Although studies analyzing gender using a broader definition are few, it has been highlighted that the lifetime prevalence of AN and BN in sexual minority groups is higher compared with cisgender heterosexual groups [26]. Third, as in other GBD research, the study suffers from uncertainty in some estimates, reflected in the width of the 95% UIs, especially in males and certain age groups. Fourth, GII was chosen as a summary measure of gender inequality because it is the most widely used at the global level [9]. However, it has been argued that gender gaps in the European context reached a normatively desirable value years ago, leaving no room for further improvement, while this does not seem likely, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe [27]. Finally, the absence of data from Eastern Europe and the limited availability of sources for Central Europe represent an important limitation: these gaps may have affected the precision of estimates, with significant implications for the interpretation of regional trends.

These findings underscore the need for comprehensive interventions addressing the systemic and social determinants that influence the onset, course, and access to care for EDs. Policy implications include promoting measures to reduce gender inequality, not only for broader social progress but also as a public health strategy to lessen ED risk and burden. For males, the results highlight the importance of improving detection, reducing stigma, and developing gender-sensitive services that recognize how societal gender norms may uniquely shape their experience and reporting of EDs [2].
