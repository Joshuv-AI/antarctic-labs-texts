The unique CARWL score stratifies locally advanced nasopharyngeal cancer patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy into risk groups for radiation-induced trismus
Authors: Sukran Senyurek, Efsun Somay, Nilufer Kilic Durankus, Sibel Bascil, Duriye Ozturk, Ugur Selek, Erkan Topkan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo determine the utility of the novel CARWL score, which integrates C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) and significant weight loss (SWL), in stratifying the locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (LA-NPC) patients into significantly different radiation-induced trismus (RIT) risk groups following definitive C-CRT.Patients and methodsThis retrospective study analyzed the medical records of 286 LA-NPC patients who received C-CRT between January 2010 and December 2022. The maximum mouth opening (MMO) was measured before the C-CRT, at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, and every 6 months after that during the follow-up. Additionally, the CAR value just before the commencement of C-CRT and SWL defined as a weight loss > 5% in the preceding six months were documented for each patient. RIT was defined as a MMO ≤ 35 mm.ResultsThe optimal CAR cut-off was 3.03 (area under the curve: 87.3%; sensitivity: 82.6%; specificity: 80.9%, J-index: 0.635), using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with RIT incidence being the event. We stratified the patients into three CARWL score groups. CARWL-0: CAR < 3.0 and WL ≤ 5.0% (N = 92), CARWL-1: CAR < 3.0 and WL > 5.0% or CAR ≥ 3.0 (N = 99), and WL ≤ 5.0% and CARWL-2: CAR > 3.0 and WL > 5.0% (N = 95). The incidence of RIT increased significantly across CARWL score groups (8.7% for CARWL-0, 23.2% for CARWL-1, and 44.2% for CARWL-2; P < 0.001).ConclusionThe current study indicated that the novel CARWL scoring system is efficient in precisely stratifying LA-NPC patients into distinct RIT risk groups after C-CRT.Keywords: Radiation-induced trismus, Chemoradiotherapy, C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio, Significant weight loss, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma

Introduction

Radiation-induced trismus (RIT) is a severe complication of radiotherapy (RT) or concurrent chemo-RT (C-CRT), which can cause detrimental health problems such as decreased ability to speak, swallow, and eat, poor oral hygiene, and nutritional issues, leading to reduced quality of life (QoL) [1]. The development of radiation-induced trismus (RIT) in locally advanced nasopharyngeal cancer (LA-NPC) patients can be attributed to several primary factors. These factors include the extension of the tumor behind the pterygomandibular raphe into the pterygoid and masseter muscles, as well as hypoxia, persistent inflammation, vascular endothelial damage, and fibrosis resulting from RT [2].While RIT seldom presents as an initial symptom of tumor extension in LA-NPCs, it is nevertheless observed in 7–15% of patients following intensity-modulated RT (IMRT)-based C-CRT, the established treatment modality for LA-NPCs [3]. In a systematic review comprising 22 studies on patients afflicted with head and neck cancer (HNC), the observed prevalence of trismus stands at 25.4% for conventional RT, 5% for IMRT, and 30.7% for combined RT and chemotherapy approaches [4]. Furthermore, the onset of xerostomia and oral mucositis following RT has been causally associated with dysphagia, taste loss, and compromised oral hygiene, thereby escalating the risk of trismus within the initial nine months post-treatment [5].

The RIT occurs after exposure to radiation in the muscles involved in chewing, the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), associated raphes, and synovial fluid [6–8].The radiation dose to which the masticatory apparatus is exposed, RT technique, and concurrent chemoradiotherapy are the main factors contributing to the development of RIT [9, 10]. In a study conducted by Tehug and his colleagues, it was found that a mean TMJ (temporomandibular joint) dose of 51.0 Gy or higher was significantly linked to higher rates of trismus (36.8% compared to 3.2% for doses below 51.0 Gy; P < 0.001) [10]. The study also revealed that the rate of trismus increased by 24% with every additional 10 Gy to the pterygoid muscle [10]. Apart from the exposed RT dose, the recently published systematic meta-analysis has indicated that concurrent chemotherapy increases the incidence of trismus in patients treated with RT [11]. Besides these traditional factors, recent research has indicated immune, inflammatory, and nutritional markers can also effectively predict rates of RIT after RT or CCRT [13–18], as they are instrumental in the development of vascular injury, tissue hypoxia, cellular senescence, and radiation-induced fibrosis (RIF) [12]. For instance, pre-treatment neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), hemoglobin-to-platelet ratio (HPR), and pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) have demonstrated predictive capabilities for the incidence of RIT following C-CRT [13–16]. Furthermore, pre-CCRT Valero's host index and global immune-nutrition-inflammation index (GINI) have emerged as discerning prognostic markers for RIT within the same context [17, 18].

More recently, Topkan et al. created a novel composite immune-inflammatory-nutritional scoring system called CARWL score, which integrates CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR), and significant weight loss (SWL: Weight loss > 5% in the past 6 months) [25]. In the first-of-its-kind study, the authors explored the prognostic value of this scoring system in stage IIIC non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients undergoing definitive CCRT. The study revealed that the innovative CARWL score effectively stratified these patients into three distinct groups with markedly different progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) outcomes (P < 0.001 for each) subsequent to definitive CCRT [12]. However, despite the robust prognostic efficacy of CARWL in NSCLC patients and the comparable efficacy of its components (CAR and SWL) in NPC patients, the potential of the novel CARWL score in predicting radiation- or chemoradiotherapy-induced toxicities has not been previously investigated in head and neck cancers patients, including those with LA-NPC treated with definitive CCRT.

Given that CRP, albumin, and SWL serve as significant indicators of a patient’s immune and inflammatory status, all of which play crucial roles in the development and advancement of radiation-induced toxicities [19–24], our objective was to conduct a retrospective analysis to evaluate the potential utility of the novel CARWL score in stratifying LA-NPC patients into distinct risk groups for RIT after definitive C-CRT.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

The data was collected by conducting a retrospective review of the medical records of LA-NPC patients who received treatment at the Department of Radiation Oncology, Baskent University Medical Faculty. These patients underwent comprehensive oral and dental examinations at our Dentistry Clinics both prior to and following treatment between the period of January 2010 and December 2022. We exclusively included patients with histopathologic evidence of squamous cell NPCs. They had to have locally advanced disease according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging (AJCC) 8th edition (T1-2N1-3M0 or T3–4aN0-3M0) proven through clinical, radiological, nuclear medicine examinations, or suspected neck nodes and/or distant metastases in suspected cases. Patients included were between the ages of 18 and 80 years, had an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1, had no prior history of cancer, had not previously received systemic chemotherapy or radiation therapy to the head and neck, and had undergone definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (C-CRT) with at least one course of chemotherapy. Additionally, patients had no trismus before the initiation of C-CRT. They had available complete blood counts obtained before C-CRT, electronic records of RT dosimetry, chemotherapy details, and oral exams conducted before and after C-CRT.

Patients were excluded from the final analyses due to the following criteria: edentulous individuals, patients with missing maxillary and/or mandibular central incisors, prior TMJ surgery, TMDs (Temporomandibular Disorders), TMJ ankylosis, TMJ dislocation, head and neck trauma, muscle-related pain, myofascial pain syndrome, primary tumor, or nodal invasion in the masticatory muscles, and trismus at the time of diagnosis Patients who had collagen vascular diseases, fibrotic disorders or those who were on immunosuppressive medications were also deemed 'ineligible to minimize their biasing effects on the outcomes.

Ethics and informed consent

Written informed consent was provided to utilize their medical records in academic research and for anonymous publication within the medical domain before the initiation of treatment. This study obtained ethical approval (No: DKA:19/39b) from the institutional review board at Baskent University Medical Faculty and adhered to the ethical standards delineated in the original Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Treatment protocol

Our institution has used the simultaneous integrated boost IMRT technique as the standard treatment for all LA-NPC and other HNC patients since January 2010. We perform target delineations using pretreatment co-registered computed tomography (CT), 18-fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission CT (PET-CT), and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images to identify high-risk, intermediate-risk, and low-risk PTVs. The dose prescription is 70.0, 59.4, and 54.0 Gy in 33 days (5 days/week) for high-risk, intermediate-risk, and low-risk PTVs, respectively. Concurrent chemotherapy is administered at a dose of 80 mg/m2 every 21 days, so patients receive 3 cycles of concurrent chemotherapy during the radiation therapy period. We recommend two cycles of adjuvant cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil combination (administered every 21 days) for patients who can tolerate it.

Determination of radiation-induced trismus

RIT was diagnosed as maximum mouth openings (MMO) ≤ 35 mm anywhere during the follow-up period according to criteria established by Dijkstra et al. [13] for HNC patients, measured by an experienced oral and maxillofacial surgeon (E.S.) The examinations for RIT were performed at baseline and 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months following CCRT, and biannually after that point, or more frequently if suspected. The mean MMO was calculated as the mean of three consecutive measurements in each examination to minimize measurement errors. The disposable Therabite® (Atos Medical AB, Hörby, Sweden) range of motion scale [14] was used for measuring the MMO due to being a user-friendly tool.

Assessment of C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio and weight loss

The pretreatment CAR was calculated for each patient using CRP and albumin measures obtained on the first day of CCRT. Additionally, the percentage difference between the weight measures acquired on the first day of CCRT and those from 6 months before the start of CCRT was calculated. According to the Delphi criteria described by Fearon and colleagues, SWL was defined as weight loss of > 5% in the previous 6 months [15]. Patients were stratified according to CAR cut-off value and weight loss status. CARWL score groups were established following the original methodology outlined by Topkan and colleagues, as described in the Statistical Analysis and Results sections here [12].

Statistical analysis

The main goal of this retrospective research was to reveal the value of the CARWL scores in categorizing LA-NPC patients who received C-CRT into distinct RIT risk categories. We used medians and ranges to represent continuous data, whereas categorical variables were defined using percentage frequency distributions. We used the Chi-square test, Student's t-test, or Spearman correlation to examine intergroup comparisons. The pre-C-CRT biochemistry test results were used to conduct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to determine the optimal cut-off value for CAR. The ideal cut-off was identified as the point on the graph where the J-index achieved its highest value, enabling us to categorize the patient population into two distinct categories with markedly disparate RIT results. The time from C-CRT to RIT development was estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves and calculated as the interval between dates of C-CRT completion and an established RIT diagnosis. Using a multivariate Cox proportional hazard model, the likely relationships between patient, disease, and treatment variables and CARWL score were investigated, with a diagnosis of RIT being the event. All statistical analyses were performed using two-sided tests, with a significance level of < 0.05. We used Bonferroni corrections to correct treatment variables for multiplicity, thereby reducing the likelihood of random false-positive results when comparing three or more subgroups, such as the CARWL score groups. This correction was implemented to enhance the reliability of P-values.

Results

A retrospective evaluation was conducted on 286 patients with LA-NPC. The median age of the patients was 55, and two-thirds were male. The median pre-CCRT MMO, post-CCRT MMO, and average MAD were 41.3 mm (range: 36.8–46.0 mm), 38.4 mm (range: 24.9–44.0 mm), and 34.3 Gy (range: 13.2–66.4 Gy), respectively. The prevalence of smoking and alcohol intake among all patients was 66.8% and 42.7%, respectively. Most patients exhibited the T3-4 stage (74.4%) and the N2-3 stage (79.7%), as shown in Table 1.

At a median follow-up of 68.9 months (range: 10.9–114.7 months), RIT was diagnosed in 73 (25.5% patients using widely recognized Dijkstra and colleagues’ widely recognized RIT definition (MMO ≤ 35 mm) for HNC patients. In the RIT group, the median time to RIT development from the completion of RT was 12.2 (6–61.7 months). Comparisons across the four CARWL score groups showed that the incidence rates of RIT were 8.7%, 21.3%, 25.0%, and 44.2% for Groups 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Since there was no significant difference in the RIT rates between Groups 1 and 2 (P = 0.51), we combined these groups and formed three tiered CARWL score groups: CARWL-0: CAR < 3.0 and WL ≤ 5.0% (N = 92), CARWL-1: CAR < 3.0 and WL > 5.0% or CAR ≥ 3.0 (N = 99), and WL ≤ 5.0% and CARWL-2: CAR > 3.0 and WL > 5.0% (N = 95). Therefore, we repeated the intergroup analysis with the final CARWL score groups, results of which demonstrated that the RIT incidence was gradually and significantly increasing across CARWL score groups: 8.7% for CARWL-0, 23.2% for CARWL-1, and 44.2% for CARWL-2; P < 0.001). This significance level was reached despite the almost indistinguishable baseline and treatment features across the three CARWL score groups.

As shown in Table 2, the univariate analysis indicated that pre-C-CRT MMO group, T stage, N stage, concurrent chemotherapy cycles, mean MAD group, MAD 55.5 Gy group, and CARWL score groups were all significant predictors of RIT incidence (P < 0.05). A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted using all of these factors. It was shown that all of these indicators were independent and significant predictors of RIT in patients with LA-NPC who had definitive C-CRT (P < 0.05 for each) (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Discussion

The present retrospective investigation demonstrated that the recently constructed CARWL score, which combines CAR and SWL, is a reliable biological indicator that categorizes LA-NPC patients into discrete groups with considerably distinct rates of RIT occurrence after C-CRT. Specifically, those with a CARWL-2 score (CAR > 3.0 and WL > 5.0%) had a significantly increased risk (44.2%) of developing RIT compared to those with CARWL-1 (23.2%) and CARWL-0 (8.7%) scores (P < 0.001).

In addition to the novel parameter CARWL score, our multivariate analysis results, in line with the available literature, indicated that pre-C-CRT MMO < 41.3, T3-4 stage, N2-3 stage, concurrent chemotherapy cycles 2–3, mean MAD ≥ 34.3 Gy, and MAD 55.5 Gy > 40.0% were also significant independent predictive parameters for RIT. The pretreatment MMO, dose of MAD, T-stage, and N-stage are well-known risk factors and have been used for creating predictive nomograms for RIT [16, 17]. Variable dose constraints such as V42 Gy and mean 58 Gy for masseter muscle were defined as clinically relevant dosimetric cut-offs for RIT prediction as our result [6, 18]. Considering the influence of chemotherapy on RIT rates, a recently published meta-analysis indicated that chemoradiotherapy significantly (P = 0.0003) increased the prevalence of trismus (OR = 2.55, 95% CI = 1.53–4.23) compared to RT [11]. Our findings in this situation revealed that undergoing 2–3 cycles of concurrent chemotherapy, as opposed to just 1 cycle, was associated with a 2.06-fold higher likelihood of developing RIT (P = 0.011), which supports the meta-analysis results.

The most striking discovery of our study was the presentation of the recently 'introduced CARWL score's unique capacity to stratify LA-NPC patients into three groups with significantly distinct RIT risks (P < 0.001). Specifically, the 44.2% RIT rate experienced by the CARWL-2 patients was nearly two- and fivefold higher than the 23.2% of CARWL-1 and 8.7% of CARWL-0 patients. Motivated by the fact that hyperinflammation, as evidenced by a high CAR value, and the presence of SWL are frequently associated with higher rates of treatment failures and treatment-related toxicities [19, 20], we hypothesized that CARWL scores, unique combinations of these two parameters, could also effectively predict and categorize RIT rates in LA-NPC patients undergoing C-CRT. This hypothesis served as the foundation for our current study. The CARWL score was first presented as a prognostic indicator in stage IIIC NSCLC patients who underwent definitive C-CRT, as reported by Topkan et al. This research found that patients with stage IIIC NSCLC were stratified into three groups based on their CARWL score, which was used to assess OS and PFS. The OS and PFS were considerably shorter in patients with a CRAWL-2 score than those with CARWL-0 and CARWL-1 scores. The OS was 37.3 months for CARWL-0, 23.6 months for CARWL-1, and 12.8 months for CARWL-2 (P < 0.001). Similarly, the PFS was 14.2 months for CARWL-0, 11.4 months for CARWL-1, and 7.5 months for CARWL-2 (P < 0.001). The CARWL-2 score groups' respective 7.5 months PFS, and 12.8 months OS rates were remarkably close to those reported for metastatic patients in the NSCLC literature, demonstrating the robust prognostic potency of the CARWL scores. However, our research is the first to assess and show the usefulness of CARWL scores in determining the distinctive RIT risk levels of LA-NPC patients who undergo exclusive C-CRT treatment.

Explaining the correlation between a higher CARWL score and an increased risk of RIT is challenging due to the absence of similar study results. However, it may be logical to analyze the two elements of CARWL, namely the CAR and SWL, in the context of radiation-induced toxicities in HNC patients to offer sensible reasons for this link. CAR, the first component of CARWL, consists of CRP and albumin. CRP is a resilient biomarker of acute-phase response that signifies varying degrees of systemic inflammation, and its concentrations increase promptly after any inflammatory stimulation. Hypoalbuminemia is a constant companion of elevated CRP levels [21]. The primary reason for the inverse association between CRP and albumin levels is the inhibition of albumin production in hepatocytes brought on by CRP and its byproducts. Due to this robust inverse relationship, CAR levels are significantly elevated in response to even a single inflammatory stimulus. Hyperinflammation is a well-established state associated with decreased treatment tolerance, survival rates, and higher incidences of treatment-related toxicities [22]. While no research has specifically examined the effects of elevated CAR levels on the incidence rates of RIT, a recent study by Li and colleagues found that higher levels of CRP and lower levels of albumin were both independently linked to higher rates of nasopharyngeal necrosis after radical RT or C-CRT [23]. Given the similarities between the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of nasopharyngeal necrosis and RIT, together with the evidence that heightened levels of CRP and decreased levels of albumin imply higher CAR levels, it is reasonable to hypothesize that a higher CAR level is linked to increased RIT rates.

Irrespective of the exact causal association between a higher CARWL score and increased RIT rates, our results led to categorizing LA-NPC patients into three discrete RIT risk groups: CARWL-0, CARWL-1, and CARWL-2. We contend that this risk stratification may be crucial to ensure the timely implementation of preventive measures in the CARWL-1 and CARWL-2 cohorts, including minimizing doses to the masticatory apparatus to the lowest feasible levels without compromising tumor control rates, as well as incorporating early onset exercise programs and instrumentation during and after CCRT. Additionally, we recommend stricter follow-up protocols for these patient groups, including shorter follow-up intervals and phone-call follow-ups. These contentions are in line with the findings of the meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials reported by Wang and colleagues, which indicates that this approach stands out as the most effective method for preventing trismus [24].

The second component of the CARWL score is SWL, which signifies a weight loss of > 5% and serves as a marker for cancer cachexia.30 SWL occurring before or during RT or C-CRT implies malnutrition, which may compromise the capacity of damaged tissues to heal radiation-induced injuries, including the masticatory apparatus [25]. Moreover, acute radiation toxicities and SWL may aggravate each other, creating a detrimental vicious circle [20]. Patients with pre-cachectic or cachectic states also present with elevated CRP and decreased albumin levels [26].The SWL, reduced albumin, and elevated CRP are closely interconnected, primarily due to CRP's proinflammatory byproducts, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-1, IL-6, and interferon-γ (IFNγ) [22]. For example, any increase in CRP levels abruptly leads to stimulation of TNF and IL-6 levels which inhibits albumin synthesis. Therefore, it seems that the novel CARWL score integrates many proinflammatory, inflammatory, immune, and nutritional factors either directly (SWL, CRP, and albumin) or indirectly (TNF-α IL-1). Considering the role of these factors and the CRP byproducts in RIT pathogenesis, it is rational to anticipate that patients presenting with higher CARWL scores would experience higher RIT rates. Nevertheless, this comment should be acknowledged as a plausible scientific inference and supported by future research.

The current study has several limitations. First, this study is a retrospective and single-center cohort analysis, making it susceptible to selection biases. Second, these findings are specific to patients with LA-NPC who underwent definitive C-CRT and may not be generalizable to patients who initially received induction chemotherapy, those treated with RT alone for earlier-stage NPCs, or LA-NPC patients who cannot tolerate it. Third, the absence of crucial immune-inflammation and cachexia biomarkers, such as platelets, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 in our study, limits our ability to establish a direct cause-and-effect relationship between a higher CARWL score and increased RIT incidence rates. Fourth, we only used single-point or interval measurements to determine the CAR and SWL cut-off values. However, CRP, albumin, and SWL levels may change markedly during and after C-CRT because of other radiation-related side effects, changes in the tumor burden, and the host's inflammatory status. Therefore, further studies may determine better CAR and SWL cut-offs at either of these time points, which better discriminate RIT rates across the three CARWL score groups. Thus, unless comprehensive and well-planned research investigating these matters supports the results reported here, it is advisable to regard them as hypothetical rather than definitive suggestions for therapeutic use.

Conclusion

Our retrospective research findings indicated that the CARWL score accurately stratifies patients with LA-NPC who received C-CRT into three risk categories for RIT. While this scoring system is expected to be efficiently used in daily oncology clinics due to its inclusion of readily available metrics and information without additional cost, it must undergo validation via further research before it is implemented in routine practice.
