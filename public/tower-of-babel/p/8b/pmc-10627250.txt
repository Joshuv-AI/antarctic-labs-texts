Carer involvement in medication adherence: carer views and experiences of facilitating medication adherence using pharmacy-filled multi-compartment medication compliance aids and other methods of adherence support—a questionnaire survey
Authors: Lieze Thielemans, Katherine Chin, Alice Hegarty, Rebekah Schiff
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnpaid carers are an increasing proportion of the UK population. One of the many ways in which they help those they care for is assisting with medication adherence. Many older adults have medicines dispensed in pharmacy-filled multi-compartment medication compliance aids (pMCAs). However, evidence suggests that pMCAs may increase medication-related harm, and little is known about the interaction between the user, medication adherence systems and the carer.AimTo explore the views of carers supporting older adults to manage their medications with and without a pMCA.MethodA researcher-administered questionnaire survey of carers supporting older adults to manage their medicines with or without a pMCA. Participants were recruited from inpatient wards, outpatient clinics and community services in central London. Responses were analysed by two independent researchers to identify overarching themes.ResultsEighty-eight unpaid carers were interviewed and responses were categorised according to the medication adherence method used; 47 supported a user with a pMCA and 41 supported without a pMCA. The main themes were: Time, Responsibility and Mistakes, Waste and Sustainability, and Polypharmacy, with sub-themes of design of multi-compartment medication compliance aids, organisation and reassurance.ConclusionSupporting medication adherence puts considerable burden on carers regardless of whether a self- or pharmacy-filled medication compliance aid is used or not. Prescribers could alleviate this burden through regular medication review by considering the prescribing frequency and duration and medication rationalisation. Redesign of both pMCAs and systems surrounding their use could also help reduce carer’s burden and environmental burden.Keywords: carer, frailty, medication adherence, older people, medication compliance aid

Introduction

As the population of older adults increases, so does the number of unpaid carers supporting their family or friends [1]. A growing proportion of carers are older adults themselves, with one-third of over 65-year-olds in the UK providing unpaid care [2]. With the increasing reliance on this cohort, it is important to understand the strains of providing care and how we as health professionals can mitigate this. One well-recognised burden is ensuring the person they support has the correct medical care [3, 4].

The issue of medication management for carers has been well explored. Particular themes described include the responsibility of adjusting medications, especially following acute admissions; difficulty in encouraging adherence to medication regimens; maintaining medication supplies and access to health care professionals for medication reviews [3–6].

Medicines adherence and wastage are major issues within the NHS, with roughly £250 million NHS-prescribed medicines being discarded each year [4]. NICE guidelines recommend multi-compartment medication compliance aids (MCAs) as a method of improving adherence, managing polypharmacy and supporting medication management for those with cognitive impairment [7]. Purported benefits of MCAs include supporting the user in maintaining their autonomy [8] and reducing anxiety by acting as a visual guide to whether the medication was taken [9]. Many of the MCAs in use are pharmacy-filled MCAs (pMCAs). However, a growing body of evidence suggests that pMCAs actually increase the risk of medication-related harm [10]. This is especially evident at transition points in care, such as discharge from hospital [9, 11].

There is a paucity of work looking at the perceptions and experiences of carers of pMCA users. Yet, in designing strategies to support carers, their role in medication adherence is an important consideration. This study, therefore, aimed to:

Explore carer experiences of aiding medication adherence either with or without the use of a pMCA

We defined MCAs as a purpose-built, compartmentalised device that organises tablets per day of the week and time of day [11, 12]. These include pMCAs, where the pharmacist fills the MCA with the prescribed medications, and self-filled MCAs (sMCAs), where a reusable aid is filled by someone other than a trained professional, for example, the carer or the user themselves. sMCAs range from systems involving alarms and automation to simple segmented containers. A third category ‘alternative methods’ was used for those choosing non-MCA methods to help adherence. In this paper, we refer to the person using a medication management system for their own medications as the ‘user’.

Method

A questionnaire survey was designed to determine the views of carers of people over 65 years old who used a pMCA or used other methods to aid medication adherence. The questionnaire was designed by a consultant geriatrician, pharmacist and junior doctors. The consultant frequently sees patients with pMCAs in the hospital and community and has publications related to medication complexity in older adults. Junior doctors were in their third year after qualification and were employed as research fellows. The pharmacist clinically practised in the community and had a research interest in medication adherence. Pilot versions of the questionnaire were modified in response to the user and carer feedback prior to the start of study recruitment. The questionnaire length varied from 22 to 35 items depending on participant group (Appendix S1). The questionnaire was transcribed in real time on OnlineSurveys.com, which was provided by King’s College London.

Between August 2021 and April 2022, carers of people using services at a large central London Integrated Care Trust were recruited from outpatient clinics, inpatient wards and community services. Carers were identified through a mixture of methods depending on health care setting. In inpatient and community settings, treating clinicians identified eligible carers and informed the research team. Outpatient recruitment was done by the research team directly approaching those who accompanied patients attending speciality clinics for older adults. Only carers who spoke adequate English to provide informed consent and complete a verbal interview were included. The questionnaire was researcher-administered either face-to-face or over the phone according to the carer preference. Researchers administering the questionnaires had no direct involvement in the medical care of the participants and had no prior relationship. Recruitment was stopped at thematic saturation.

Analysis

After initial independent data familiarisation by two researchers, the responses were analysed deductively through thematic analysis, in accordance with Braun and Clarke [13], using NVivo (Daresbury, UK).

The study was registered as IRAS ID 289976 with ethical approval granted by the Health Research Authority. The study was supported by a grant from the Sir Halley Stewart Trust.

Results

Eighty-eight unpaid carers were interviewed: 47 cared for someone using a pMCA, 21 cared for someone using an sMCA, 3 ordered a pMCA but decanted into an sMCA and 17 used an alternative method. Age of carers ranged from 29 to 90 years old, and the mean was 61 years (Table 1). Mean age of leaving education was 18 years (range = 14–41).

Themes

Carer responses highlighted four main overlapping themes pertaining to the use of medication adherence systems whether using a pMCA, sMCA or alternative method. These main themes were: Time, Responsibility and Mistakes, Waste and Sustainability, and Polypharmacy. Sub-themes included the design of MCAs, organisation and reassurance (Figure 2).

Themes and subthemes of carer perceptions of medication management.

Time

Carers commonly described all forms of MCAs as more time-efficient to manage the medication administration compared with original packaging, freeing up time to spend with loved ones.

‘I now get to spend more time with my mum instead of doing her medications’. (Carer C031)

However, time consumption was still a prominent theme, with effect depending on the adherence method used.

pMCA carers struggled with the process of ordering medications and ensuring that medication changes had been actioned, citing difficulty in contacting their pharmacy or GP.

‘[There is] often a lag between [medication] changes being made and it’s difficult to get an update because community services are so stretched [...] Only way I can get things done is if I scream and shout at pharmacy as soon as my father is discharged [from hospital]’. (Carer C034)

The physical presence of the carer was often still required to assist in removing medication from the compartments, especially for pMCAs. Design issues led to three carers ordering pMCAs but then taking the time to decant the medications from the pMCA into their own sMCA, as users found these easier to manipulate.

‘I’ll pop the medications out of the blister pack into an eggcup with a big piece of A4 paper next to it saying ‘good morning, tablets’. (Carer C032)

Some preparing sMCAs cited that the time required to fill them was a barrier to use and swapped to a pMCA to save time.

‘Just easier for us [than preparing sMCAs], less time consuming than making it up yourself’. (Carer C069)

Conversely, some carers felt that filling sMCAs saved time relative to using the original packaging. This was particularly the case when the sMCAs only needed to be filled once a week compared to taking the medication out of the packaging for every dose throughout the day.

Responsibility and mistakes

Carers felt a great responsibility to ensure that medications were taken correctly by using the MCA to directly visualise self-administration. Though MCAs offered a way of organising tablets, they did not include a reminder to take them. Hence, many carers acted as reminders, setting alarms or calling the user to ensure that medications were taken.

‘If I couldn’t get there in the morning I used to phone her to tell her to take her medications, and she’d say yeah yeah yeah but then I’d hear her close the door and go straight back to bed’. (Carer C061)

Some felt the responsibility to avoid medication errors. For those using pMCAs, there was significant concern regarding pharmacy errors, with many having experienced these errors.

‘Meds have sometimes been wrong on the pharmacy-filled medication aid—either wrong tablet or wrong dose. It’s lucky I’m savvy with this, it’s quite scary’. (Carer C008)

This caused an emotional burden with reported feelings of worry, stress and guilt, which were exacerbated by perceived lack of support from health care professionals, especially at transitions of care. Some carers reported being asked to amend already supplied pMCAs, while others had to remind pharmacies to enact a medication change and make a new blister pack.

‘Mum had to stop her dementia medication because they were worried about a side effect, but the medication was already in the blister pack, but the pharmacist left it up to me to remove this. I looked it up on Google and tried to remove it but accidentally removed a different pill that looked similar, so she went without that medication for several weeks. Thankfully it didn’t do her any harm, but I felt so guilty’. (Carer C032)

Those who used alternative methods or sMCAs felt more control over the medication taken as they were directly responsible for the tablets given to the user. The concern about making an error themselves was outweighed by the knowledge that they knew exactly what tablet was being taken and when. One carer who had ceased using a pMCA stated:

‘We felt we had no control […] We tried it for a few weeks and just didn’t work out so pivoted to [our own] system’. (Carer C083)

Waste and sustainability

A common theme among carers was the waste of both medication and plastic and the consequent environmental impact.

For pMCAs, any medication changes often led to a new pMCA being issued and the old one being discarded. There was an evident moral burden of the use of pMCAs and a desire to move towards more sustainable design:

‘From a sustainability point of view, I know the ones the pharmacy send are more cardboard, and the blister is paper, so we can pop it straight into recycling. But the one he came home with from the hospital was more plastic and I’m not sure that was recyclable’. (Carer C053)

‘It felt wasteful. We’re quite an environmentally friendly family and we were worried about the packaging and being wasteful’. (Carer C039)

The theme of waste was less remarked on for sMCAs and alternative methods; however, carers commented that each different tablet was supplied in its own cardboard box.

Polypharmacy

The increasing number of prescribed medications was the most common reason for initiating the use of all types of MCA to manage multi-tablet regimens.

‘Her receiving initial pill boxes was a nightmare, I requested [the pMCA] myself. She has 20 tablets a day, how was she meant to do that?’ (Carer C008)

However, an issue with pMCAs was that not all medications prescribed could be supplied within the pMCA, making the system seem redundant.

‘There were so many other tablets involved, there was only 3 in the dosette box and another 13 tablets outside’. (Carer C025)

The objective of using MCAs to solve issues surrounding polypharmacy was complicated by issues of formulation, e.g. liquid form due to an impaired swallow, variable dosages and medication changes.

For carers of alternative methods’ users, polypharmacy meant that it was difficult to ensure a continuous supply of all medications. The large number of different medications often needed ordering at different time points depending on frequency of use and length of repeat prescription.

‘There are so many different medications, so it was getting very complicated, especially as certain medications were running out at different times’. (Carer C075)

There was a clear call for more frequent medication reviews and better access to support from the GP or pharmacy. Some of those with pMCAs felt that their use meant that medications were less likely to be reviewed and possibly rationalised, while others felt that polypharmacy was a barrier to them moving away from pMCAs.

‘If there was a medicines rationalisation so only 1 or 2 tablets then I would maybe consider [discontinuing a pMCA]’. (Carer C075)

Discussion

Despite it being well documented that carer involvement is a crucial aspect of care, it has been highlighted that there is a lack of information regarding carer views on different forms of medication adherence aids, and it is frequently cited as being a topic for future exploration [14–16]. To our knowledge, this is the first large survey of carer views on their role in supporting medication adherence and the different methods used. The wealth of information volunteered by those interviewed highlighted the willingness of carers to raise concerns, with a clear drive to want to improve and change practices to benefit the user.

Our study adds weight to the need for regular medication reviews [17]. Health care professionals can reduce the burden described by carers by rationalising medications in line with national and international campaigns and guidelines [18–20]. Accompanying this is temporal prescription alignment so that all medications can be re-ordered at the same time for the same number of months, which would be a simple but significant measure to reduce polypharmacy-related carer burden.

Users at risk of medication-related harm post-discharge could be identified using tools, such as PRIME and the NHS Discharge Medicines Service, to better support the transition from acute to community care [21, 22]. Medication reconciliation at transitions of care [23] should be routine to reduce errors, which are a significant source of anxiety for respondents no matter the form of MCA used.

Though MCAs have been cited as promoting organisation and empowering both users and carers [9], we have found that the use of pMCAs was associated with a general sense of lack of control among carers, especially in identifying tablets and actioning medication changes. The National Service Act 2006 specifies a duty to promote autonomy, and both patient and carer involvement in care has been repeatedly shown to improve health outcomes and quality of care [24, 25]. Our carer cohort cited user preference, independence and control as reasons not to use a pMCA. However, most still used a form of sMCA to help adherence, suggesting the issues lay specifically with pMCAs rather than MCAs in general.

Despite the intended use of MCAs to promote independence, carers often still needed to be present to facilitate medication adherence by removing the medications from the MCA. This ties in with both the theme of time and MCA design and waste and presents a challenging opportunity to redesign MCAs to improve the usability and reduce the environmental impact of the material waste from MCAs. This builds on work by The Royal Pharmaceutical Society who have reported on flaws in MCA design, including the effect of medication interaction in a single container and the effect on medication of prolonged exposure to the environment within an MCA [26–28].

Interestingly, most MCAs were implemented at the request of the carer or the user’s General Practitioner, often citing carer burden from polypharmacy. This suggests a skew towards carer, rather than user, autonomy, supporting previous literature stating that MCAs are often implemented according to the need of carers and health care professionals [29]. It is clear, however, that no matter what method of adherence is used, it must be individualised to be effective.

We collected data on those using a range of adherence methods. This reflects the growing practice of supporting user and carer choice in the best individualised approach to medication management and adherence. Given the similarity in opinions across pMCA, sMCA and alternative methods groups, their responses have been combined in the formation of our themes. This suggests that the method of medication adherence is not the root cause of the problem, but rather the health care systems that necessitate their use due to issues such as polypharmacy are the root cause of the problem.

A strength of this study is that the views collected are those of a large and ethnically diverse carer population that had experience of users accessing health care across the entire health system. This enabled them to comment on the experiences of a variety of services and transitions between health care settings. The use of a questionnaire survey ensured this wide variation in respondents, avoiding the selection bias that can be introduced with smaller focus groups. However, due to the difficulty in translating the questionnaire into multiple languages, only those with adequate English were included, likely reducing the accessibility to an underrepresented participant cohort. Recall bias may have affected participants’ answers as the questionnaire covered events that occurred in the preceding year. Ensuring neutrality of questions addressed the possibility that researcher positionality may have affected the answers. The potential that responses were analysed through the researchers’ viewpoint was mitigated by the responses being coded, defined and themed independently by two researchers.

Conclusion

Whether an MCA is used or not, carers face considerable challenges in assisting users’ medication adherence. Improved prescriber awareness with regular medication review, medication reconciliation at transitions of care and temporal prescription alignment would make a major difference to carers. MCA product redesign is also required, considering the user needs and the environment. Health care professionals need to recognise that there are many forms of medication adherence support and that providing a pMCA is not the first or only solution to consider.
