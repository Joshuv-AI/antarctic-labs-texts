Hierarchical graph attention knowledge tracing and personalized recommendation for computer courses
Authors: Jian Chen, Dajie Fu, Ang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the development of intelligent education, knowledge tracing and personalized recommendation have become essential techniques to improve learning outcomes. However, existing approaches mainly rely on flat knowledge graphs, which fail to represent the hierarchical relations among chapters, concepts, and sub-concepts. Recommendation strategies often focus only on knowledge coverage, while neglecting learner interests and difficulty progression. This leads to limited prediction and recommendation performance. To address these issues, this study proposed a hierarchical graph attention–based knowledge tracing and personalized recommendation framework (HGAKT + PR). The method constructed a three-level knowledge graph to model hierarchical relationships among chapters, knowledge points, and sub-knowledge points. Multi-head graph attention and a dynamic gating mechanism were applied to learn learners’ knowledge states. Shared representation learning was then used to jointly optimize answer performance prediction and personalized recommendation. The recommendation strategy integrated learners’ weak knowledge areas, difficulty progression, and interest preferences to improve recommendation relevance and diversity. Experimental results showed that the proposed method achieved stable performance and outperformed representative baseline methods across multiple real-world educational datasets. The model effectively characterized learning states under different learning scenarios and supported personalized learning resource delivery. Furthermore, experiments on multiple datasets and parameter sensitivity analyses consistently demonstrated stable performance under different settings. These findings indicated that hierarchical knowledge modeling combined with prediction–recommendation collaborative optimization enabled more accurate learning state estimation and improved personalized learning resource delivery.

Introduction

With the popularization of intelligent education and online learning, how to accurately model learner knowledge states and provide effective personalized recommendations has become a core topic in educational data mining1. Early methods were mainly based on statistical modeling, such as Bayesian Knowledge Tracing2 and Factorization Machines3. These methods utilized response records for initial inference but depended on strong prior assumptions, making them unsuitable for large-scale heterogeneous data. Advances in deep learning have driven sequence-based knowledge tracing models that apply recurrent neural networks or attention mechanisms to capture temporal dependencies in learning behaviors, thereby improving prediction accuracy4,5. Nevertheless, such models often treat concepts as independent units, overlooking the complex hierarchical structures in course content. Knowledge graph approaches provide support for structural modeling, but most remain limited to shallow dependency learning without organic integration with dynamic sequences. More importantly, existing studies often separated response prediction from personalized recommendation. Few works quantified learners’ interests and preferences from behavioral features. The impact of these features on personalization and model interpretability still lacks empirical evidence. This gap limited the coordination between knowledge diagnosis and learning resource delivery.

To address these limitations, the proposed HGAKT + PR framework follows the logic of “structural modeling–state representation–joint optimization–personalized recommendation.” First, during the modeling stage, a hierarchical knowledge graph was used to decompose course content into chapters, knowledge points, and sub-knowledge points. This structure captured multi-level dependency relationships and addressed the limitation of existing sequential methods in modeling structural information. Next, during the representation learning stage, multi-head attention and gating mechanisms were combined to extract informative features from complex graph structures and temporal interaction data. This process generated more stable knowledge state representations. Based on these representations, shared representation learning mapped the knowledge tracing task and the recommendation task into a unified latent space. Prediction and recommendation were therefore optimized jointly at the representation level. This design avoided the serial stacking of prediction and recommendation commonly used in previous approaches. Finally, the recommendation strategy integrated learners’ knowledge weaknesses and learning preferences to generate more targeted learning resource recommendations.

Related work

In recent years, knowledge tracing and personalized recommendation have become important directions in intelligent education research. Existing studies can be broadly divided into four categories: sequence modeling methods, the application of knowledge graphs in education, improvements in graph neural networks and attention mechanisms, and personalized recommendation with joint optimization.

Sequence modeling methods for knowledge tracing

Knowledge tracing was initially dependent on sequence modeling. The Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) model proposed by Piech et al. was the first to apply Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to capture features from learners’ response sequences. It significantly improved prediction accuracy but could not explain structural relations between concepts6. Later, Pandey introduced the Self-Attentive Knowledge Tracing (SAKT) model, which applied self-attention to weight key interactions and addressed the challenge of long-range dependency modeling7. On this basis, Ghosh et al. developed the Attentive Knowledge Tracing (AKT) model that used multi-head attention to enhance relational modeling among concepts, further improving prediction accuracy. Other studies attempted to incorporate Transformer structures to capture global dependencies, but these methods faced efficiency issues with large-scale educational data8. In general, sequence models showed advantages in temporal dependency modeling, yet lacked the ability to represent hierarchical structures of concepts.

Applications of knowledge graphs in education

To overcome the limitations of sequence modeling in capturing structural relations, researchers introduced knowledge graphs. Nakagawa et al. proposed the Graph-based Knowledge Tracing (GKT) model, which used graph convolution to capture prerequisite relationships between concepts, highlighting the potential of graph structures in educational modeling9. Meanwhile, Wang et al. introduced the Knowledge Graph Convolutional Network (KGCN) and Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) in recommender systems, where graph convolution and attention mechanisms were used to model higher-order semantic relations, validating the effectiveness of knowledge graphs for complex dependencies10. In educational settings, Liu et al. designed a three-layer graph consisting of “course–concept–exercise” to strengthen cross-level representation11. However, these approaches mainly focused on flat or shallow structures and lacked full hierarchical modeling. The adoption of knowledge graphs confirmed the importance of structural modeling but did not adequately capture multi-level dependencies in curricula.

Improvements in graph neural networks and attention mechanisms

In graph-based learning, identifying the importance of neighboring nodes has been a critical issue. Velicković et al. proposed the Graph Attention Network (GAT), which employed multi-head attention to assign differentiated weights to neighbors in graph space, significantly improving feature learning12. Zhou et al. introduced gating mechanisms into dynamic graph learning, effectively reducing interference from redundant features13. Sun et al. combined multi-head attention with temporal gating for social network prediction, improving temporal representation14. Meanwhile, recent advances in graph representation learning have focused on modeling complex relationships. Studies in biomedical systems, human–machine analysis, and health data modeling have demonstrated that graph neural networks are effective in representing high-order dependencies and dynamic structures15–17. In the educational domain, Yang et al. applied GAT with residual structures to model concept relations, but they did not account for learners’ dynamic interests18. Existing methods have shown that attention and gating mechanisms are advantageous for feature extraction, but they lack deep integration with hierarchical knowledge structures and temporal dynamics.

Personalized recommendation and joint optimization

Personalized recommendation is a key component for enhancing learning experiences in intelligent education systems. Zhou et al. proposed the Deep Interest Network (DIN), which employed attention to capture users’ recent interests and improved recommendation effectiveness19. He et al. introduced the Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) model, which moved beyond traditional matrix factorization by modeling interactions as nonlinear functions, thus increasing flexibility20. Meanwhile, models such as KGAT integrated knowledge graphs with attention mechanisms to capture higher-order semantic relations and improved explainability in recommendations. In educational contexts, Zhang et al. proposed a joint optimization framework for prediction and recommendation, which effectively enhanced learning outcomes. However, it paid insufficient attention to learner interests and difficulty progression21. Although some studies introduced learners’ interest features into recommendation tasks, most remained at the explicit label level. They lacked quantitative validation and ablation analysis of behavioral signals such as interaction frequency and dwell time. Overall, personalized recommendation research is moving toward the integration of graph structures and attention, but the separation between prediction and recommendation remains a major bottleneck.

Comparison and positioning of deep learning methods

In recent years, with the rapid progress of deep learning, researchers have explored intelligent educational modeling frameworks based on graph neural networks and reinforcement learning. For example, the dual graph convolutional knowledge tracing model enhances complex relationship modeling by constructing a student–skill dual graph22. The Psy-KT approach integrates learning behaviors and psychological factors through a heterogeneous graph, which further improves the accuracy of knowledge state representation23. In addition, deep reinforcement learning–based path recommendation models have achieved end-to-end generation of personalized learning resources24. These artificial intelligence methods generally show strong feature representation ability and high predictive accuracy. However, their architectures are usually complex. They often rely on large-scale labeled data and substantial computational resources. As a result, the deployment cost and operational stability in real-time online education environments may still face practical constraints. In contrast, the proposed HGAKT + PR framework maintains effective modeling capability while keeping the computational overhead controllable and the structure relatively lightweight. Therefore, it is more suitable for real-time online education scenarios.

Methods

This study proposed a hierarchical graph attention knowledge tracing and personalized recommendation method for computer courses. The objective was to accurately capture learners’ knowledge states and generate highly targeted recommendations. The overall framework is shown in Fig. 1 and includes six components: (1) Input Layer: It received students’ interaction records and multi-level labels (chapter–concept–sub-concept), providing raw data for knowledge graph construction. (2) Hierarchical Knowledge Graph: The course structure was modeled hierarchically. An adjacency matrix was used to encode prerequisite relations and semantic dependencies, forming a multi-level knowledge network. (3) Multi-Head Graph Attention Aggregation: Query, key, and value vectors were computed in parallel within the graph space. This step captured high-order associations among nodes and enhanced neighborhood feature representation. (4) Graph Attention Network with Dynamic Gating: A lightweight recurrent neural network (RNN) with gating units was applied to adaptively regulate feature channels. It strengthened key dependencies, suppressed noise, and output temporally consistent knowledge states. (5) Representation Learning: Learners’ knowledge states, together with task features such as item difficulty, labels, and historical statistics, were mapped into a shared space. This unified representation supported both prediction and recommendation. (6) Dual-Task Prediction and Recommendation Optimization: On one hand, LSTM or Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) networks were used to predict learners’ performance on new tasks. On the other hand, the model estimated the alignment between tasks and learners’ needs. The ranking of candidate tasks considered weak concepts, difficulty progression, interests, and diversity, and produced a Top-K recommendation list.

Input and construction of the hierarchical knowledge graph

In the knowledge system of computer courses, there exist multi-level and complex dependencies among chapters, concepts, and sub-concepts25. Traditional flat knowledge graphs can only represent relations between single-level concepts, which fails to reflect hierarchical prerequisite structures and semantic dependencies. As a result, models often lack a global perspective when describing learners’ knowledge states, leading to limited prediction accuracy. To overcome the insufficiency of flat knowledge graph representation, this study constructed a hierarchical knowledge graph with a three-layer structure consisting of chapters, concepts, and sub-concepts.

In Fig. 2, the left side shows student interaction records and multi-level tag inputs, while the middle part demonstrates node division and dependency extraction. The right side presents the final three-layer hierarchical knowledge graph. The three-layer graph was constructed from chapters to concepts and then to sub-concepts, establishing multi-level dependency structures. The adjacency matrix was used to describe prerequisite relations and semantic dependencies. The chapter code C determined the depth of the layer. The propagation depth in the graph convolution or attention layers l represented the semantic hierarchy, enabling the model to capture fine-grained multi-level dependencies. This ensured consistency between hierarchical structures and semantic dependencies, while preserving temporal coherence.

Shared representation learning

In Sect. 3.2, the hierarchical knowledge graph was modeled using multi-head graph attention and a dynamic gating mechanism, which produced learners’ knowledge state representations along the temporal dimension. These representations described learners’ mastery levels over different knowledge points. However, their feature space differed substantially from that of item attribute features, which made them difficult to use directly for joint modeling of answer prediction and personalized recommendation26. Therefore, a unified representation mechanism was required to map heterogeneous and multi-dimensional features into a shared latent space. To achieve unified modeling of multi-dimensional features, this study adopted a shared representation learning approach based on feature concatenation and a multilayer perceptron (MLP). The main idea of this approach was to first concatenate features from different sources in order to preserve their original semantic information. The concatenated high-dimensional features were then projected into a latent representation space with a unified dimension through nonlinear mapping using the MLP. In this way, features from different modalities and semantic levels were aligned and fused within a shared space, which supported subsequent dual-task collaborative optimization.

Dual-task joint prediction and personalized recommendation strategy

Within the shared representation space, a single prediction target could not simultaneously ensure answer performance prediction and exercise matching. Therefore, a dual-task collaborative optimization strategy was adopted. On one hand, it predicted learner performance on upcoming exercises. On the other hand, it estimated the matching degree between exercises and learner needs27. The recommendation module further integrated short-term interaction frequency, dwell time, and item-type preference features to improve the accuracy of personalized modeling. By combining prediction performance and matching score, the final personalized Top-K recommendation list was generated. For response prediction, the learner’s interaction sequence was modeled using LSTM:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{h}}_t}={\mathrm{LSTM}}({{\mathbf{u}}_t},{y_{t - 1}},{{\mathbf{h}}_{t - 1}})$$\end{document}

Based on the hidden state, the probability of the next exercise was predicted as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat {y}_{t+1}}=\sigma ({W_y}{{\mathbf{h}}_t}+{b_y})$$\end{document}

The function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma ( \cdot )$$\end{document} was the Sigmoid function. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat {y}_{t+1}}$$\end{document}represented the prediction accuracy rate. The matching score between question and learner state was calculated as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r_q}=\phi ([{{\mathbf{u}}_t}\parallel {{\mathbf{\tilde {v}}}_q}])$$\end{document}

The joint loss function was defined as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{L}=\lambda {\mathcal{L}_{KT}}+(1 - \lambda ){\mathcal{L}_{Rec}}$$\end{document}

The weight coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} balanced the two objectives. This joint optimization simultaneously improved prediction accuracy and ensured the rationality of recommendation. If recommendations only targeted unmastered concepts or coverage, they often ignored learner interest and short-term patterns. Therefore, difficulty progression and diversity constraints were added to the ranking of candidate exercises. This ensured that the recommendation set contained both challenging and weakly mastered exercises, thereby improving adaptability. The optimization objective was defined as:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop {\max }\limits_{S} \mathop \sum \limits_{{q \in S}} r_{q} - \beta Var(d_{q} )$$\end{document}

The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} controlled the balance between diversity and fairness. At the same time, short-term preference bias was adjusted as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w_q}=\alpha {r_q}+(1 - \alpha ){p_q}$$\end{document}

The score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w_q}$$\end{document} was the combined result. This optimization strategy guaranteed sufficient inclusion of weak concepts, maintained progressive difficulty and diversity, and improved the fairness and satisfaction of recommendations. To enhance personalization and interpretability, the study quantified learners’ interests through three behavioral signals. (1) Short-term interaction frequency: A sliding window was used to calculate the proportion of recent interactions in which the topic matched the current subject. Computational complexity analysis

Let the sequence length be T, the hidden dimension be d, the number of attention heads be K, and the number of graph attention layers be L. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\mathcal{V}|, |\mathcal{E}|$$\end{document} denote the numbers of nodes and edges used in each interaction, respectively. The main computational cost of a single forward pass comes from the hierarchical graph attention mechanism. Under sparse graph conditions, the complexity of attention coefficient computation and message aggregation is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(KL|\mathcal{E}|d)$$\end{document}, while the cost of linear transformations is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(KL|\mathcal{V}|{d^2})$$\end{document}. The additional cost introduced by the MLP in shared representation learning and the prediction head is approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T{d^2})$$\end{document}. If the recommendation module scores Mcandidate items, the scoring cost is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(TMd)$$\end{document}. Therefore, the overall time complexity for a single sequence is given by:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( {T\left( {KL|\mathcal{E}|d+KL|\mathcal{V}|{d^2}+{d^2}+Md} \right)} \right)$$\end{document}

The space complexity is mainly determined by intermediate node representations and attention weights, which is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(KL|\mathcal{V}|d+KL|\mathcal{E}|)$$\end{document}. In practical implementations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\mathcal{V}|, |\mathcal{E}|$$\end{document} usually correspond to a hierarchical subgraph extracted around the interacted knowledge points. This design keeps the computational cost within a manageable range.

Experimental settings and statistical validation

The experiments were conducted on a server equipped with an NVIDIA A100 40GB GPU, a 32-core CPU, and 128GB memory. The implementation was based on PyTorch 2.0, DGL, PyG, and FAISS. The optimizer was Adam, with a learning rate of 1e-3 and weight decay of 1e-4. Early stopping with patience was applied to avoid overfitting. All experiments were repeated 5 times, and the results were reported as mean and standard deviation with a 95% confidence interval. The datasets were randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 70%, 10%, and 20%, respectively. Validation and test sets were split sequentially to avoid information leakage. For each dataset, every model was trained and evaluated on 5 different random seeds, and the mean and standard deviation were reported. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 200, and early stopping was enabled.

Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics. AUC, F1, Recall, and Accuracy (ACC) were applied to measure prediction accuracy. NDCG@10 evaluated ranking performance. Coverage (COV) measured the proportion of recommended items over the entire set. Weighted Coverage (WC) considered the coverage of both concepts and items. Diversity measured the dissimilarity among recommended items. Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) assessed the rank position of the first relevant item. Hits at 10 (Hits@10) measured whether the correct item appeared in the top 10 results. Mastery Probability represented the predicted probability that the learner had mastered a concept, ranging from 0 to 1. Precision at 10 (Precision@10) calculated the proportion of relevant items among the top 10 recommended items. Recall at 10 (Recall@10) evaluated the proportion of relevant items covered in the top 10 recommendations. Local Prediction Consistency (LPC) is used to measure the reliability of model predictions for students’ responses within consecutive learning windows. Prediction Volatility reflects the model’s ability to stably capture changes in learning pace. Learning Continuity reflects the impact of the recommendation strategy on learning engagement from the perspective of behavioral responses.

The study used Spearman rank correlation and Pearson correlation to measure the relationships between three interest features and two outcome variables, including the correctness of the next response and the matching score. Partial correlation was further applied to control for item difficulty, learners’ mastery level, and session index. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method (q < 0.05). To assess the contribution of each feature group, an additional ablation test was performed. In this test, each interest feature was removed individually. The features included short-term interaction frequency, average dwell time, and item-type preference. The recommendation head was retrained, and the variations in Recall@10 and NDCG@10 (± 95% CI) were reported.

Comparative experiments

To verify the effectiveness of the proposed hierarchical knowledge tracing model across diverse educational datasets, experiments were conducted on three datasets and compared with seven baseline models. The results are presented in Table 2. The results showed that the proposed model achieved the best average performance across all datasets and evaluation metrics. Specifically, on EdNet, the AUC, F1, Recall, and ACC values reached 0.890, 0.800, 0.790, and 0.870, respectively, which were about 2%–4% higher than the best baseline AKT and DyGKT. Because this dataset is large in scale and has a clear hierarchical structure, the attention mechanisms in AKT and DyGKT mainly rely on similarity between items or connections in dynamic graph structures. As a result, they have limited ability to represent hierarchical relationships between chapters and knowledge points. HGAKT + PR calculated attention weights on the hierarchical knowledge graph by integrating node features and adjacency relations, which allowed it to capture cross-level dependencies more effectively. On ASSISTments 2017, the proposed model also achieved improvements of about 3%–4% compared with the baselines. This dataset contained many noisy labels and complex prerequisite relationships. Although DyGKT improves the modeling of temporal dependencies through dynamic graph learning, and CL-TSKT enhances the representation of learning participation by integrating temporal and spatial features, both methods have difficulty jointly balancing multiple feature channels under high-noise conditions. Unlike the static GKT model based on graph convolution, HGAKT + PR introduced a dynamic gating mechanism to adjust the importance of different feature channels over time. This mechanism maintained stable higher-order dependency modeling in noisy environments and significantly improved model robustness and generalization. On Junyi Academy, the proposed method achieved further improvements, with four metrics reaching 0.900, 0.810, 0.800, and 0.880. This dataset included Chinese knowledge labels and shorter learning sequences, testing the model’s adaptability across languages and curricula. Although DyGKT and CL-TSKT achieve certain improvements over traditional baselines on this dataset, HGAKT + PR unifies learners’ knowledge states and item feature distributions through shared representation learning. This design ensures effective semantic alignment, which allows the model to maintain prediction accuracy across different cultural and language contexts. Overall, HGAKT + PR performed consistently across three data types—large-scale, high-noise, and heterogeneous label systems. These results confirmed the strong generalization and robustness of its hierarchical graph structure, multi-head attention, and dynamic gating mechanisms across diverse educational contexts.

Scalability analysis

To evaluate the engineering feasibility of HGAKT + PR in large-scale database scenarios, this study analyzed GPU memory consumption and recommendation generation time from two major computational sources: hierarchical subgraph size and candidate item scale. As the knowledge graph continues to expand and the candidate pool grows, these two factors are regarded as key variables affecting model scalability. During the scalability experiments, all model parameters remained unchanged except for the variable under investigation. Peak memory usage was recorded through the GPU monitoring interface. The generation time was measured as the average latency of a single Top-K recommendation. Each configuration was executed five times, and the mean value was reported. This setup simulated the model behavior under continuously expanding database conditions.

Figure 8 presents the scalability results of HGAKT + PR. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the GPU memory usage increased approximately linearly with the growth of subgraph size, which was represented by the number of nodes |Vsub|. No exponential rise was observed. This behavior occurred because the model performed message passing only within the hierarchical subgraph centered on the target knowledge point. Consequently, the intermediate representation size was effectively constrained. The computational cost remained manageable under sparse graph conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 8(b), the single recommendation generation time rose approximately linearly with the candidate scale M. This trend is consistent with the theoretical complexity analysis. The main additional cost in the recommendation stage came from matching score computation over M candidate items. Since the scoring process for each candidate was independent, the overall time complexity was approximately linear with respect to M. Within the typical candidate range of online learning systems, the model still maintained millisecond-level response time. In summary, HGAKT + PR showed stable and predictable growth behavior when the database scale increased. These results verify the engineering scalability and practical deployment potential of the proposed method in large-scale intelligent education scenarios.

Ablation study

Ablation study of model components

To further validate the contribution of each module, ablation experiments were conducted and compared with five modified versions. These were defined as follows: HGAKT w/o Hierarchical Representation, which removed hierarchical representation and retained only the flat knowledge graph; HGAKT w/o Multi-Head Attention, which discarded multi-head attention and used only a single-channel attention mechanism; HGAKT w/o Dynamic Gating, which removed the dynamic gating mechanism; HGAKT + PR w/o LSTM Prediction, which excluded LSTM prediction in the collaborative task and used only static features; and HGAKT + PR w/o Preference Modeling, which omitted preference modeling in the recommendation strategy and relied only on knowledge mastery. The results were evaluated using MRR, Hits@10, Precision@10, and Recall@10.

As shown in Table 3, the complete model HGAKT + PR achieved the best performance on all four metrics across the three datasets. On the EdNet dataset, HGAKT + PR obtained an MRR of 0.612 and Hits@10 of 0.862, both higher than the modified versions. Recall@10 reached 0.545, which was about 0.03–0.04 higher than the versions without hierarchical representation or dynamic gating. On the ASSIST2017 dataset, the trend was consistent. HGAKT + PR achieved a Recall@10 of 0.534, which was nearly 0.04 higher than the version without preference modeling. On the Junyi dataset, HGAKT + PR reached 0.283 for Precision@10 and 0.552 for Recall@10, both significantly better than the baselines. Overall, the results indicated that hierarchical representation and dynamic gating contributed the most to model performance, while LSTM prediction and preference modeling also played an important role in improving precision and recall. These results confirmed the completeness and robustness of the proposed method.

Case study

To further demonstrate the practical performance of the proposed model in accurately characterizing learning states and delivering personalized learning resources at the individual learning trajectory level, a case study was conducted based on the EdNet (KT1) dataset. EdNet (KT1) was collected from a real online learning platform and records students’ continuous response behaviors during actual learning processes. It reflects changes in learning paths under real instructional settings. In this section, one student with relatively complete learning behavior and a learning path covering multiple chapters and knowledge points was selected as a representative case. This case was used to illustrate how the model captures changes in learning states during a typical learning process. It should be noted that this case study was conducted for qualitative analysis of the model’s prediction and recommendation behavior at the individual level. Its purpose was to explain the model mechanism rather than to provide statistical validation. AKT, DyGKT, and the proposed HGAKT + PR were selected for comparison. These methods represent sequence-based modeling and dynamic graph–based modeling approaches, respectively, and thus provide clear contrasts to highlight the effects of hierarchical knowledge graphs, multi-head graph attention, and dynamic gating–based collaborative modeling.

Given that the case study focused on intuitive evaluation at the individual level, LPC, Prediction Volatility, Hit@10, NDCG@10, and Learning Continuity were used to assess model performance from the perspectives of prediction accuracy, recommendation quality, and learning behavior. The results are reported in Table 6. Overall, the proposed method outperformed the comparison models across all case-level evaluation metrics. In terms of learning state modeling, HGAKT + PR achieved an LPC value of 0.80, while prediction volatility was reduced to 0.109. These results were superior to those of AKT and DyGKT, indicating that the proposed method more stably reflected students’ true learning states. For personalized recommendation performance, HGAKT + PR obtained the highest scores in Hit@10 (0.59) and NDCG@10 (0.53), which indicated better relevance and ranking quality with respect to students’ current learning needs. In addition, the learning continuity score reached 0.62, further suggesting that the recommendation strategy provided appropriate knowledge targeting and difficulty progression. These advantages mainly resulted from the integration of hierarchical knowledge graph modeling and shared representation learning, which enabled effective fusion of knowledge structure information and learning behavior features. Key hyperparameter sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the impact of key structural parameters on model performance, the sensitivity of the number of attention heads K in the multi-head graph attention mechanism and the number of graph attention layers L was analyzed. Experiments were conducted on the EdNet (KT1) dataset, while all other parameters were kept unchanged. AUC and F1 were used as evaluation metrics, and the results are shown in Fig. 10. As shown in Fig. 10(a), the model achieved its best performance when K = 6 (AUC = 0.889, F1 = 0.801). Increasing the number of attention heads beyond this value did not lead to further performance gains, indicating that neighborhood relationships could be sufficiently modeled with a moderate number of heads. In Fig. 10(b), the optimal performance was observed when L = 2 (AUC = 0.889, F1 = 0.803). When the network depth increased to four layers, performance decreased by 0.142, which may be attributed to noise accumulation in deeper graph structures. Overall, HGAKT + PR showed stable performance under different parameter settings, which supported the rationality of the model design.

Conclusion

This study addressed the limitations of existing knowledge tracing models in modeling hierarchical dependencies and providing targeted personalized recommendations. A hierarchical graph attention–based knowledge tracing and personalized recommendation method (HGAKT + PR) was proposed. By constructing a hierarchical knowledge graph with chapter–knowledge point–sub-knowledge point structures and integrating multi-head graph attention, dynamic gating mechanisms, and shared representation learning, the proposed method achieved collaborative optimization of knowledge state modeling and personalized recommendation.

Although the model achieved excellent performance across multiple datasets, several limitations remained. The experiments were based on public datasets and lacked online validation in real educational environments. Since the three datasets used in this study came from different educational platforms and hierarchical knowledge structures, the model exhibited strong generalization across domains and levels, partly mitigating the limitation of single-course scenarios. However, these datasets mainly focused on K-12 knowledge tracing and exercise recommendation tasks, without involving inter-course knowledge transfer or cross-subject content associations. Therefore, further studies are needed to verify the model’s performance in real cross-curricular recommendation. Future research can build on this work by incorporating online incremental learning and real-time recommendation mechanisms, introducing multimodal data to enrich feature representations, and integrating causal inference with reinforcement learning to optimize recommendation decisions. These directions will promote the practical application of the proposed model in intelligent education systems.
