Effects of chronic swimming exercise and cannabidiol on metabolic and i̇nflammatory gene expression in obese rats
Authors: Sezgin Hepsert, Lütfiye Kadioglu Dalkılıç, Emrah Özdemir, İbrahim Aydınlı, Fatih Mehmet Ugurlu, Semih Dalkılıç, Gülsüm Altuntaş, Yakup Kılıç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of exercise and cannabidiol on the expression of target genes involved in metabolic regulation and inflammation-related molecular pathways in rats made obese by a high-fat diet.Method The study with a randomized controlled experimental design was conducted on 40 male Sprague-Dawley rats aged 8 weeks. The rats were randomly assigned to 5 groups with 8 rats in each group (Control, Obese Control, Obese Exercise, Obese CBD, Obese Exercise CBD). The intervention protocol consisted of 6 weeks of swimming exercise (30 min/day, 5 days/week) and CBD (10 mg/kg/day/week) by oral gavage. The expression levels of the target genes examined in adipose tissue and liver tissues were determined by quantitative real-time PCR. SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) package program was used for data analysis. The data were analyzed using One Way Anova for the determination of significant differences and LSD test for the determination of significant differences between groups.Results Upon examination of the findings, significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of BMI (p=0.001) and blood sugar levels (p=0.001) after 6 weeks of exercise and cannabidiol application. Significant differences were observed in the LKB1 (p=0.045) and PERK (p=0.013) parameters examined in liver tissue, while no significant differences were observed in the SREB1 (p=0.209) target gene. Significant differences were observed in the LKB1 (p=0.009) and PERK (p=0.011) parameters examined in adipose tissue, while no significant difference was detected in the SREB1 (p=0.060) target gene.Conclusion In conclusion, it was determined that the 6-week chronic swimming exercise and cannabidiol application, particularly the combined intervention, resulted in improvement in the intervention groups compared to the OC group in the target genes examined. The absence of significant differences between the C group and the intervention groups indicates that complete improvement was not achieved. Based on these data, it is thought that the combined application of regular exercise and controlled doses of cannabidiol may be beneficial in improving metabolic health parameters in obese individuals. These results highlight the potential of integrating exercise with natural bioactive compounds in obesity prevention strategies.

Introduction

Obesity is recognized as one of the most common metabolic disorders threatening public health globally today, and its prevalence is steadily increasing. While traditional approaches define the fundamental cause of obesity as energy intake exceeding energy expenditure [1], this approach falls short of fully explaining the pathogenesis of the disease. Recent studies have revealed that genetic, epigenetic, metagenomic, environmental, cultural, and behavioral factors play a role in the development of obesity, either alone or in combination [2]. This multifactorial etiological structure not only increases the risk of obesity-related mortality but also plays a decisive role in the development of many secondary diseases associated with obesity, such as type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, chronic inflammation (CI), and oxidative stress [3]. In particular, CI has been reported to underlie obesity-related metabolic disorders. The effects of various lifestyle changes and therapeutic interventions on CI have been extensively studied in the literature; although some studies have yielded positive results, the long-term sustainability of these effects has often been limited [4]. In general, treatment approaches such as exercise, dietary modifications, and pharmacological agents have been shown to be effective in modulating inflammatory responses [5–7]. These studies feature various research designs in which interventions are applied both alone and in combination [8, 9]. However, the role of specific genetic markers is coming to the fore for a better understanding of obesity and related inflammatory processes at the molecular level. In this context, the Sterol Regulatory Element Binding Transcription Factor 1 (SREBF1), Liver Kinase B1 (LKB1), and Protein Kinase RNA-Like Endoplasmic Reticulum Kinase (PERK) genes have been reported to play important regulatory roles in the pathogenesis of obesity and inflammatory processes [10–12]. Evaluating the expression levels of these genes may shed light on the molecular basis of obesity and contribute to the development of more effective diagnostic, monitoring, and treatment strategies. In this study, the LKB1, PERK, and SREBF1 genes were evaluated together because they represent obesity-related metabolic regulation processes through distinct yet interconnected biological axes. LKB1 plays a central role in cellular energy sensing and AMPK-mediated metabolic adaptation, while PERK is one of the key regulators of the endoplasmic reticulum stress response. SREBF1, on the other hand, is critical in controlling lipogenesis and lipid homeostasis. The combined analysis of these genes allows for a more comprehensive interpretation of the potential holistic effects of exercise and cannabidiol administration on the interactions between energy balance, cellular stress response, and lipid metabolism.

Cannabidiol (CBD), a non-psychoactive component of the hemp plant, has seen growing interest among athletes in recent years. This trend has gained momentum, particularly since CBD was removed from the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) list of prohibited substances. Potential benefits such as supporting muscle regeneration and alleviating exercise-related pain are thought to be driving athletes’ use of CBD [13, 14]. Recent preclinical and clinical studies suggest that CBD may have positive effects not only on athletic performance but also on cardiometabolic health. In particular, CBD has been reported to have therapeutic potential in modulating processes involved in the development of metabolic disorders, such as insulin resistance and inflammatory responses [15]. Recent comprehensive reviews have highlighted the multifaceted role of cannabidiol in metabolic regulation, inflammation, and energy homeostasis. In particular, Botticelli et al. (2025) systematically summarized evidence indicating that CBD may modulate lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory signaling pathways, and appetite-related mechanisms through both central and peripheral actions. These findings suggest that CBD represents a biologically active compound with potential relevance for obesity-related metabolic dysfunctions [16]. In this regard, CBD is considered a complementary agent in the prevention of diseases such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. However, some studies conducted on animal models have shown that CBD also has promising effects in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases [17]. However, large-scale, randomized, and controlled clinical trials in humans are needed to establish the clinical validity of these findings.

A review of the literature reveals studies suggesting that CBD use may have regulatory effects on inflammatory processes associated with obesity [18, 19]. However, numerous studies have also reported that chronic exercise creates positive effects on obesity, contributing to the improvement of metabolic diseases [20, 21]. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the current literature contains an extremely limited number of studies evaluating the combined use of CBD and chronic exercise. This situation points to a significant gap, particularly in terms of developing multifaceted and integrated treatment strategies for systemic and multifactorial health problems such as obesity. On the other hand, the potential effects of CBD use on athletic performance and recovery processes are also attracting attention in the field of sports science. The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) removed the non-psychoactive L-isomer of CBD from its list of prohibited substances in 2018, thereby paving the way for athletes to use this compound legally [22]. This change has led to a marked increase in CBD use among athletes, despite the scientific evidence not yet being conclusive [23, 24].

In this context, the primary objective of the study is to experimentally evaluate the potential therapeutic effects of chronic exercise and CBD administration on obesity and to elucidate the effects of these approaches on molecular biomarkers based on the obtained data. The research seeks to answer the question: “Can the combined application of CBD and chronic exercise modulate metabolic- and inflammation-related gene expression in obese rats?” In this regard, the problem addressed by the study highlights the need for alternative or complementary strategies, considering the limitations of current methods in obesity treatment. The lack of scientific data in the literature on the combined use of different intervention forms, such as CBD and exercise, makes this research a unique study that could contribute to both clinical and sports applications.

Method

Experimental animals and housing conditions

The experimental animals used in this study were 8-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats obtained from the Fırat University Experimental Animal Production and Research Center (FÜDAM). The animals were housed in rooms maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, with a relative humidity of 35–40%, a noise level below 85 dB, and a light intensity of 40 lx, under environmental conditions with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark photoperiod. All rats were housed in wire-topped polypropylene cages with adequate ventilation; water and feed were provided ad libitum. All animals were selected to be of similar age, genetic background, and health status. In addition, the study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by Fırat University Animal Experiments and Local Ethics Committee (2024/26192).

Selection criteria for laboratory animals

Only male Sprague-Dawley rats were used in this study. At the start of the experiment, the rats were 8 weeks old, and individuals with normal basic physiological parameters (activity level, feed consumption, coat structure, etc.) and showing no signs of disease were included in the study. After obesity induction was completed, rats with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 1.00 g/cm² were included in the experimental groups as an indicator of successful obesity model establishment.

Rats that developed respiratory distress, significant weight loss, injury, or serious health problems before or during the experiment were excluded from the study. Additionally, individuals exhibiting significant behavioral or neuropsychological reactions such as lethargy, excessive aggressive behavior, or food refusal after CBD administration were also excluded from the evaluation. Rats that died during the study, from which tissue samples could not be obtained, or for which biological material of sufficient quality for molecular analysis could not be provided were not included in the analyses.

Experimental design

The study was planned based on a randomized controlled experimental model. Within this model, experimental animals were initially weighed using a sensitive scale, and after their body weights were measured, they were randomly assigned to experimental groups. The randomization process was carried out to minimize potential bias and ensure initial homogeneity between groups. The interventions were conducted according to predefined standard protocols, and group-specific interventions were applied systematically. This model, which has high internal validity within experimental research designs, allows for the comparative assessment of the effects of the independent variable (diet, exercise, and/or CBD application) on the dependent variables [25]. Within this framework, the assessment was made based on the assumption that the differences between the groups were solely due to the interventions applied (Fig. 1).

Sample size

The sample size to be used in the study was determined based on a power analysis performed using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 software (Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany). The Type I error level (α) was set at 0.05, the test power (1 − β) at 0.80, and the effect size at 0.60; based on these parameters, it was predicted that a minimum of eight animals per group would be sufficient to detect meaningful differences (total n = 40). At the start of the study, all rats were weighed using a sensitive scale to determine their body weights, then randomly assigned to five different experimental groups: Control (C), Obese Control (OC), Obese Cannabidiol (OC), Obese Exercise (OE), and Obese Cannabidiol Exercise (OCE) (Table 1).

Within the experimental protocol, five experimental groups were formed, each containing eight rats. Group C was fed standard laboratory chow in pellet form (2.65 kcal/g; 67.3% carbohydrates, 4.33% fat, 24.17% protein) throughout the study and received no pharmacological or physical intervention. The OC group was fed a high-fat diet (60% fat; 5.24 kcal/g; 20% carbohydrates, 60% fat, 20% protein) to create an obesity model and was then monitored without any treatment or intervention. The OCBD group was made obese with a high-fat diet and then subjected only to systemic CBD administration; no physical exercise protocol was applied. The OE group underwent an exercise protocol following the induction of obesity but did not receive CBD. Finally, the OCBDE group underwent both systemic CBD administration and a chronic exercise protocol following the induction of obesity with a high-fat diet. Thus, the experimental model was designed considering the diet type, obesity status, and variety of interventions applied to each group.

Establishment of the obesity model

At the beginning of the study, the body weights of all rats were measured with a precision scale in plastic containers with their weights recorded. These measurements were repeated regularly every Friday between 10:00 and 11:00 a.m. to monitor the development of obesity. To establish the obesity model, the animals were fed a special diet high in fat content (60% fat, 20% carbohydrates, 20% protein; 5.24 kcal/g) [26]. Rats with a BMI ≥ 1.00 g/cm² were classified as obese [27]. The high-fat diet was administered for 10 weeks, and at the end of this period, the BMI values of all rats were found to be above the obesity threshold value of 1.00 g/cm². Thus, the development of obesity was experimentally confirmed. Following the diagnosis of obesity, the animals were randomly assigned to five experimental groups (C, OC, OCBD, OE, OCBDE) and the relevant intervention protocols were initiated.

CBD application protocol

The CBD used in this study was dissolved in 100% pure corn oil prior to application to create a homogeneous solution. The dosage was determined as 10 mg/kg/day, taking into account the current body weight of each rat; this dose has been reported to be within the effective range in the literatüre. The selected dose was administered in a manner aimed at eliciting a molecular-level response without causing systemic toxicity. Within the study design, a single dose level was chosen to standardize the effects of the interventions, and all applications were performed according to the same protocol throughout the study period [28, 29].

Prior to administration, the rats’ body weights were measured using a precision scale, and the required amount of CBD for each individual was calculated based on these data. The prepared solution was administered orally via gavage using sterile syringes. To ensure experimental standardization, all administrations were performed by the same researcher, and a separate syringe was used for each rat.

CBD applications were planned for five days a week over a period of six weeks. To minimize circadian rhythm-induced biological variability, applications were performed between 12:00 p.m. and 2:00 p.m. each day. Throughout the experiment, rats were observed daily for possible complications or adverse effects, and all observations were systematically recorded. To control for the carrier effect, corn oil was administered orally via gavage to animals in the OC and OE groups in the same volume as the CBD-treated groups. This ensured that the gavage procedure and any potential metabolic effects associated with the carrier were equal across all groups.

Exercise protocol

The exercise program was designed to be implemented chronically after the development of obesity. The protocol was carried out over a total of 6 weeks (30 sessions) at a frequency of five days per week. Exercises were performed daily between 08:00 and 10:00 a.m. and in the same order to minimize biological differences that could arise from circadian hormone changes, particularly basal glucocorticoid levels.

The chronic swimming exercise protocol was performed in a pool filled with tap water, maintained at a constant temperature (22 ± 1 °C), 50 cm deep, and 120 cm in diameter. A one-week adaptation period was planned before the exercise to allow the rats to acclimate to the water and exercise environment. During this period, the swimming duration was started at 10 min on the first day and increased by 5 min each day up to 30 min. After the adaptation period was completed, each rat underwent 30 min of swimming exercise daily throughout the experiment [30]. Water temperature was monitored throughout the exercise using a digital thermometer and maintained at a constant 22 ± 1 °C during all swimming sessions. After the swimming exercise, the animals were removed from the pool, carefully dried with soft towels, and placed in temperature-controlled cages at an appropriate distance from a low-level heat source to maintain body temperature. To prevent possible metabolic effects associated with cold stress and hypothermia, no animal was allowed to remain wet after swimming.

Collection of tissues

Rats were euthanized under deep anesthesia. A combination of ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally for anesthesia. After deep anesthesia was achieved, euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation when reflexes were absent and consciousness was completely lost [30]. Liver and adipose tissue samples collected from rats were transferred to RNA Later solution to preserve RNA integrity. Tissue samples were incubated at 4 °C for 24 h, after which excess RNA Later solution was removed. Samples were stored at -20 °C for two weeks prior to isolation, after which total RNA was obtained using standard isolation protocols [31].

RNA Isolation from tissue samples

RNA isolation from samples was performed using the Invitrogen PureLink Total RNA Kit, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, 100 mg of tissue samples were crushed on ice and placed into 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. Each tube containing a sample was added 1000 mL of Trizol and 5 µL of DTT, and a measure of zirconium beads was added and homogenized by vortexing for 5 min. The samples were then vortexed at 5-minute intervals and incubated on ice for 15 min. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants containing 200 µL of chloroform were transferred to separate Eppendorf tubes and vortexed until the colors changed. After a 10-minute incubation on ice, a three-phase liquid was obtained (DNA in the bottom phase, protein in the middle phase, and RNA in the top phase). In each sample, the RNA portion was transferred to another Eppendorf tube and diluted with isopropyl alcohol at a 1:1 volume ratio. The solution was vortexed for 3–5 min and then incubated on ice for 10 min. The sample was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, all supernatant was removed from the pellet by vortexing after adding 1 ml of 75% ethanol. A final 5-minute centrifugation was performed at 14,000 rpm. The resulting precipitate pellet was removed and incubated on ice for 6 min with the caps open. Diethylpyrocarbamate (DEPC) was added according to the amount of RNA solution. The RNA sample solution was left on ice for 15–20 min [32].

RNA Quality control

Gels containing 1% agarose and 5 µl Red Safe dye were used to run the isolated RNA samples for 20–30 min. RNA degradation was examined by visualizing the RNA 18 S and 28 S bands on an Invitrogen IBright 750 UV gel imaging system. RNA samples found to be suitable were used for cDNA synthesis. Additionally, the concentrations of RNA samples were assessed using the Qubit HS RNA Assay Kit on a Qubit Fluorometer. Based on the results, an average value was determined for the concentrations, and cDNA synthesis was performed from this starting concentration. Complementary DNA was synthesized from RNA samples of suitable quality using Applied Biosystems High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kits (Lot:01096160 REF: 4368814) at 25 °C for 10 min, 37 °C for 120 min, 85 °C for 5 min, and 4 °C [33].

Quantitative Real-Time (qRT)-PCR

The GAPDH (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase) gene was used as a control (housekeeping) gene during Real-Time PCR for mRNA gene normalization. The mRNA gene expression level was determined by the qRT-PCR reaction after cDNA synthesis. The Applied Biosystem Step One Plus Real-Time device performed the PCR process on the synthesized cDNA samples. The conditions for qRT-PCR were 3 min at 95 °C, 15 s at 95 °C, 10 s at 54 °C, 15 s at 72 °C, and 20 s at 95 °C for 40 cycles. The cycle was set at 60 °C for 1 min and 95 °C for 15 s. The Relative Gene Expression algorithm was used to determine the expression level of each gene during qRT-PCR. Fold changes (Fc) were used to express changes in gene expression [33].

Statistical analysis of data

The obtained data were analyzed using the SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) software package. GraphPad Prism 8 was also used to prepare the figures. To determine the normality of the data, skewness and kurtosis values within the ± 2 range were set as criteria [34]. The One-Way ANOVA test was used for intergroup comparisons. The LSD test was used to determine significant differences between groups. This approach aims to sensitively identify biologically meaningful differences between interventions included in the experimental design.

Discussion

According to the findings of this study, the chronic swimming exercise program and CBD applications show potential for improvement in LKB1 and PERK mRNA levels in the liver and adipose tissues of rats compared to the OC group. Furthermore, analysis of the mean values for the groups reveals that the combined application of exercise and CBD produces a more pronounced effect than either intervention alone in both target tissues.

In the study, measurements taken after chronic swimming exercise and CBD interventions administered to rats revealed significant differences in BMI values between the OC group and the OE, OCBD, and OCBDE groups, respectively. This effect may be based on chronic swimming exercise increasing energy expenditure, leading to weight loss in rats [35], and the regulatory effects of CBD administration on appetite regulation and fat storage [36]. However, food intake and eating behavior were not directly and systematically evaluated quantitatively in this study. Therefore, the potential contributions of CBD to appetite regulation should be considered as a mechanistic explanation based on the literature and interpreted with caution. A review of the literature indicates that chronic CBD intervention at a dose of 5 mg/kg in rats has been reported to reduce body weight and improve BMI values [37]. Similarly, an 8-week chronic swimming exercise program administered to rats with obesity induced by a high-fat diet has been reported to have positive effects on BMI [38]. Although studies evaluating the combined effects of chronic swimming and CBD administration are limited, the findings of the present study indicate that the highest improvement effect was achieved when these two interventions were administered together, considering the average BMI values.

In the study, measurements taken after chronic swimming exercise and CBD interventions administered to rats revealed significant differences in blood glucose levels between the OC group and the OE, OCBD, and OCBDE groups, respectively. The underlying mechanism of the blood glucose-balancing effect of chronic swimming exercise is an increase in glucose uptake in skeletal muscles and the associated improvement in insulin sensitivity [39]. Furthermore, CBD administration is thought to contribute to improved blood glucose levels through its insulin-sensitizing effect [19]. The literature reports that rats that were floated for 30 min a day, five days a week for four weeks showed a significant decrease in blood glucose levels compared to groups that did not exercise [40]. Similarly, it has been reported that chronic oral gavage administration of CBD at a dose of 20 mg/kg induces a downward trend in blood glucose levels in rats. In conclusion, both chronic exercise and CBD application, when used alone, have been shown to have beneficial effects on blood sugar regulation. However, consistent with the glucose values presented in Table 5, the combined application did not provide greater improvement compared to the application of either intervention alone. This finding indicates that, under the current experimental conditions, the effects of exercise and CBD on glycemic balance are not complementary.

The study found that LKB1 mRNA levels in liver tissue differed significantly between groups. In particular, significant differences were observed between the OC group and the OE and OCBDE groups; this indicates that the combined use of chronic swimming exercise and CBD has a higher potential effect on the target gene. Similarly, LKB1 mRNA levels in adipose tissue were found to differ significantly between the OC group and both the C and OCBDE groups. The downregulation observed in the OC group can be considered an indicator of metabolic damage, while the upregulation detected in the OCBDE group can be interpreted as an indicator of improvement in terms of the target gene. It has been reported that the cellular energy deficit during exercise increases LKB1 activation, and this mechanism results in the liver gaining resistance to chronic diseases such as obesity [41] Additionally, it has been demonstrated that LKB1 mRNA levels were significantly increased in rats subjected to chronic running exercise compared to the control group [42]. These findings support the notion that different types of exercise have the capacity to increase LKB1 mRNA expression. No specific study directly examining the effect of CBD administration on LKB1 gene expression has been found in the literature. However, in the current study, the increase in target gene levels in liver tissue following both CBD alone and CBD combined with exercise may be related to CBD’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [43]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that exercise induces structural and functional changes in adipose tissue via LKB1, thereby exerting beneficial effects on obesity [44]. In this study, the fact that chronic swimming exercise alone did not cause a significant change in the target gene compared to the control group, but showed a stronger effect when combined with CBD, partially differs from some findings in the literature.

In the study, when looking at the mean values of SREB1 mRNA levels in liver tissue between groups, an increase was observed in the OCBDE group compared to the OC group, but this change was not found to be statistically significant. Similarly, a downward regulation was observed in the OCBDE group in adipose tissue, but this difference was not significant. The SREB1 gene plays a regulatory role, particularly in fatty acid and lipid synthesis processes. However, conflicting findings have been reported in the literature regarding exercise and CBD administration. In a study conducted on rats made obese with a high-fat diet, it was reported that SREB1 gene expression increased significantly after a 15-week dietary intervention [45]. In contrast, CBD administered via diet was found to decrease SREB1 gene expression in liver tissue [46]. In the current study, an increase in SREB1 gene expression was observed in the CBD-treated groups, contrary to the literature. This suggests that the effects of CBD on lipid metabolism may vary depending on the tissue, dose, or duration of administration. Furthermore, the limited number of studies on this topic highlights the need to clarify the mechanism of CBD’s effects on gene expression. Chronic exercise has been reported to regulate SREB1 gene expression and reduce lipid synthesis by inducing metabolic adaptation in liver tissue [47]. In the current study, the lack of significant changes in SREB1 mRNA levels with chronic exercise may be related to the type, intensity, or duration of exercise. Furthermore, factors such as dosage, pharmacokinetic properties, or insufficient accumulation in the target tissue may underlie the lack of significant effects of CBD in liver and adipose tissues.

The study found that PERK mRNA levels in liver tissue showed significant differences between the OC group and the OE and OCBDE groups. Similarly, significant differences were also detected in adipose tissue between the OC and OE groups and the OC and OCBDE groups. These findings indicate that CBD administration alone does not produce a significant effect, but when combined with exercise, its effect increases significantly. It is thought that CBD alone does not cause a significant change in the target gene due to the limited application dose or duration. Supporting these findings, the literature reports a decrease in PERK mRNA levels in rats subjected to treadmill exercise and dietary intervention (metformin) via oral gavage for eight weeks. In parallel with our study, it has been shown that the combined application of exercise and dietary intervention is more effective in suppressing PERK mRNA expression [48]. Furthermore, it has been reported that eight weeks of 60 min of swimming exercise per day in rats fed a high-fat diet caused a significant change in PERK mRNA levels through the activation of the AMPK pathway and that this mechanism is effective in preventing metabolic syndrome [49]. It has been suggested that CBD may have beneficial effects on PERK gene expression at different doses, particularly due to its antioxidant properties [50]. In the current study, although CBD alone showed a tendency to improve average values, it was determined that it was not sufficient on its own to produce a significant effect.

These findings may contribute to a broader understanding of how lifestyle-based interventions combining physical activity and phytochemical supplementation could aid in the prevention of obesity and metabolic disorders at the population level. From a public health perspective, the integration of exercise programs with naturally derived bioactive compounds such as CBD may represent a feasible, low-cost, and non-pharmacological strategy to mitigate metabolic risk factors associated with obesity. Although this study was conducted on an experimental animal model, the molecular targets examined are highly conserved across species and play critical roles in regulating energy homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and cellular stress responses. In this context, the transcriptional changes obtained are thought to provide insights into the underlying mechanisms of metabolic dysregulation associated with obesity. However, it is not appropriate to interpret these findings as direct evidence of clinical effect or therapeutic efficacy. The transferability of the obtained results to human physiology should be confirmed by controlled clinical studies evaluating exercise and cannabidiol administration together. Finally, while the combination of exercise and CBD intervention produced significant differences compared to the OC group, post-hoc comparisons showed that these responses were largely comparable to those observed in the exercise-only group. This indicates that the observed molecular adaptations primarily stem from chronic exercise and that cannabidiol supplementation did not provide any statistically significant additional benefit beyond exercise under the current experimental conditions.

Limitations

First, the sample size in each group (n = 8) may have limited statistical power and prevented reaching the significance level for changes in SREB1 genes. Second, the use of male rats only is a deliberate design choice aimed at ensuring experimental control by reducing biological variability. However, it is known that sex-specific hormonal and metabolic differences may be decisive in exercise and metabolic responses. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings to both sexes may be limited. The study used a single CBD dosage, failing to examine the mechanisms of action at different doses. The molecular findings obtained in this study were evaluated based on the mRNA expression levels of the target genes. While the changes observed at the transcriptional level provide important clues about the cellular responses to exercise and cannabidiol administration, it cannot be said that these changes directly translate to protein levels or functional outcomes. Therefore, the results obtained should be interpreted not as definitive indicators of biological activity, but as findings reflecting possible orientations of regulatory mechanisms. Future studies that address protein expression, enzyme activity, and functional assessments together will provide a clearer understanding of the physiological significance of the reported molecular changes.

Conclusion

In this study, the combined examination of the liver and adipose tissues, which interact with each other in terms of energy metabolism, has enabled a more comprehensive evaluation of the holistic effects of exercise and CBD applications on metabolic adaptation. The findings indicate that the combined application of chronic exercise and CBD has a stronger regulatory effect potential on target genes compared to interventions performed alone. However, although a statistically significant level of improvement was not achieved when compared to the control group, a clear trend toward improvement was observed. The parallel gene expression changes detected in the liver and adipose tissue suggest that CBD and exercise support metabolic regulation through similar molecular pathways. In conclusion, while chronic swimming exercise alone has the potential to regulate mRNA levels of target genes, its combined use with CBD may constitute a complementary strategy for alleviating obesity-related metabolic disorders. These findings support the idea that the combined use of exercise and phytochemical agents (especially CBD) may have a combined effect on metabolic health. Future research could conduct controlled clinical trials in human sample groups based on the results obtained from this study. Furthermore, the dose-response relationship of CBD, the effects of different doses and application durations, and its interactions with exercise type and intensity should be investigated in greater detail.
