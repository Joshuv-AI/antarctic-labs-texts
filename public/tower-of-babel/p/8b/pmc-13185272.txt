Acute effects of “exercise snacks” during prolonged sitting on hemodynamics and peripheral vascular function: a three-level meta-analysis
Authors: Hai Wang, Yuanbo Chang, Hao Wang, Kaiyue Diao, Shiqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProlonged sitting acutely impairs flow-mediated dilation (FMD) by reducing peripheral blood flow and vascular shear stress. Interspersing prolonged sitting with short bouts of physical activity (i.e., “exercise snacks” or sedentary breaks) has been proposed as a strategy to mitigate these adverse effects. However, previous meta-analyses have been limited by small sample sizes and by inadequate handling of the statistical dependency arising from multiple effect sizes within studies, thereby limiting the robustness of their conclusions.MethodsWe followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and registered the protocol with PROSPERO (CRD420261282295). We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, EBSCO, and the Cochrane Library (up to September 30, 2025) for randomized crossover or parallel controlled trials involving adults aged ≥ 18 years. Eligible studies compared the effects of sedentary breaks with prolonged sitting on acute hemodynamic and peripheral vascular outcomes. Data were pooled using a three-level random-effects model in R (metafor package) to account for statistical dependency, and the results were compared with those from traditional two-level models. We also reported 95% prediction intervals and conducted outlier diagnostics, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses, meta-regressions, and publication bias assessments.ResultsA total of 36 studies comprising 632 participants were included. Compared with prolonged sitting, sedentary breaks significantly improved flow-mediated dilation (Hedges’ g = 0.44 [95% CI, 0.06–0.81]; P = 0.02; GRADE: Moderate), increased peripheral blood flow (g = 0.46 [0.22–0.71]; P < 0.001; GRADE: Moderate), and reduced systolic blood pressure (MD = − 1.67 mm Hg [95% CI, − 2.81 to − 0.52]; P < 0.001; GRADE: Moderate). Shear rate increased in the primary analysis (g = 0.32 [0.09–0.54]; P = 0.008; GRADE: Very Low) but became non-significant after trim-and-fill (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{0}$$\end{document}) adjustment, suggesting potential small-study effects. Moderator and meta-regression analyses indicated that shear rate improvements were influenced by the type of break (stair climbing yielded the largest effect) and were positively correlated with break duration. Improvements in peripheral blood flow were associated with the total duration of sitting. Among the additional hemodynamic and vascular outcomes, heart rate increased slightly (GRADE: Very Low), whereas mean arterial pressure (GRADE: Moderate), diastolic blood pressure (GRADE: Moderate), and peripheral arterial diameter (GRADE: Moderate) showed no significant consistent changes.ConclusionsBreaking up prolonged sitting with short bouts of physical activity (“exercise snacks”) acutely improves flow-mediated dilation and peripheral blood flow, and is associated with a small but statistically significant reduction in systolic blood pressure. Mean arterial pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and peripheral arterial diameter did not show consistent significant changes. Findings for shear rate and heart rate were sensitive to bias correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Activity breaks involving large muscle groups (e.g., stair climbing), performed for 2–5 min every 30–60 min, may be particularly beneficial for vascular protection. Where feasible, reducing total sedentary time and avoiding prolonged uninterrupted sitting may also be important.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01120-5.

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This is the first three-level meta-analysis to synthesize 36 randomized controlled trials involving 632 adults, explicitly correcting for statistical dependency to provide robust estimates of the acute vascular effects of sedentary breaks.

“Exercise snacks” (short, frequent activity bursts) acutely improve flow-mediated dilation (FMD), peripheral blood flow, and shear rate (though the latter requires cautious interpretation due to potential bias), while modestly reducing systolic blood pressure.

Subgroup analyses identify that “stair climbing” and activity bouts lasting 2–5 min elicit the most consistent and potent hemodynamic benefits compared with other modalities.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Strength and conditioning professionals may consider ‘exercise snacks’ as a practical strategy to interrupt prolonged sitting and support acute vascular health, while recognizing that evidence regarding safety, feasibility, and real-world implementation remains limited.

For vascular protection, activities involving large muscle groups (e.g., stair climbing) performed for 2–5 min every 30–60 min may be a reasonable option.

Where feasible, reducing total daily sedentary time and avoiding prolonged uninterrupted sitting may also be beneficial. A graphical summary of the main findings and practical implications is shown in Fig. 1.

Introduction

Sedentary behavior is defined as any waking behavior characterized by an energy expenditure ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs) while in a sitting, reclining, or lying posture [1]. In recent years, the prevalence of sedentary time has risen globally. Data indicate that daily sedentary time has increased from 7.0 to 8.2 h in adolescents and exceeds 9 h in older adults [2]. Numerous longitudinal studies have established sedentary behavior as a risk factor for cardiometabolic disease, independent of physical inactivity [3, 4]. Furthermore, a significant dose-response relationship exists between sedentary time and cardiovascular disease (CVD): for every additional hour of sedentary time beyond 10 h per day, the risk of CVD increases by approximately 8% [5].

The detrimental effects of sedentary behavior on the cardiovascular system are not only cumulative over the long term but also manifest acutely. Research demonstrates that a single bout of prolonged sitting can significantly elevate postprandial cardiometabolic risk markers [6] and cause an acute decline in peripheral vascular endothelial function—specifically, a reduction in flow-mediated dilation (FMD) of approximately 2.12% [7]. FMD is a critical indicator of endothelial function and is closely correlated with coronary artery function. A 1% decrease in FMD is associated with a 13% increased risk of future cardiovascular events [8, 9]. Consequently, FMD impairment is considered a key precipitating factor and pathological basis for cardiovascular complications such as diabetes [10]. The primary mechanisms underlying sitting-induced FMD suppression include reductions in vascular shear stress and limb blood flow [11]. These hemodynamic alterations are critical for maintaining nitric oxide production, vasodilation, fibrinolysis, and anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic functions in endothelial cells [11]. Therefore, reducing sedentary time and increasing physical activity has become an international consensus for improving vascular function and preventing atherosclerosis [12, 13].

“Sedentary breaks” offer a feasible and effective strategy to reduce sedentary time and promote physical activity [1]. This intervention typically involves interspersing prolonged sitting with short bouts of non-sedentary behavior—often termed the “exercise snack” strategy—such as standing or light walking performed periodically. Acute experimental studies suggest that sedentary breaks may mitigate the physiological vascular impairment caused by prolonged sitting, thereby lowering cardiovascular risk. However, findings regarding their acute effects remain inconsistent. While meta-analyses by Zheng et al. [14] and Soto-Rodríguez et al. [15] reported that sedentary breaks significantly enhance peripheral blood flow, shear stress, and FMD, other studies have observed no substantial improvement in FMD [7, 16]. Notably, the aforementioned meta-analyses included a limited number of primary trials (only 6–12), resulting in insufficient statistical power and potentially compromising the robustness of their conclusions.

Moreover, existing systematic reviews have largely lacked in-depth explorations of moderating factors; variables such as participant characteristics, break modality and frequency, and the duration of the sedentary bout have not been fully considered. Current research has also predominantly focused on the acute effects of sedentary breaks on glucose and lipid metabolism, with relatively less attention paid to hemodynamics and vascular function. This evidence gap not only limits our comprehensive understanding of the acute cardiovascular benefits of sedentary breaks but also poses practical challenges for implementing these interventions in real-world settings (e.g., determining optimal frequency and activity type).

In this context, we employed a three-level random-effects meta-analysis to systematically integrate recent literature, assessing the acute effects of sedentary breaks on peripheral hemodynamics and vascular function in adults. Furthermore, we explored the moderating roles of participant characteristics and intervention protocols. We aimed to provide evidence with higher statistical power and greater robustness, clarify discrepancies in prior research, and provide a scientific basis for public health guidelines regarding “regularly interrupting sitting.” Ultimately, our findings facilitate the translation of “sedentary breaks” from a conceptual recommendation into a more actionable behavioral prescription, offering an implementable strategy to reduce sedentary-related cardiovascular risk.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [17]. The complete PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary material 2. The study protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420261282295).

Literature search

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, EBSCO, and the Cochrane Library to identify eligible studies. No restrictions were imposed regarding publication date, language, or participant characteristics; however, only full-text peer-reviewed articles were included. In practice, all studies that ultimately met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review were published in English; therefore, no translation of non-English full-text articles was required. To ensure comprehensive retrieval, we employed three “snowballing” strategies: (1) screening reference lists of included studies; (2) tracking forward citations of included articles; and (3) utilizing the “related articles” function in databases such as PubMed and Embase. The final search was updated on September 30, 2025.

Search strategy

To maximize retrieval sensitivity, the search strategy combined keywords related to “sedentary” and “interrupting.” The detailed search strings for each database are presented in Table S1.

Study selection

Study selection was performed independently by multiple investigators in two phases. First, duplicates were removed using EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics) by one investigator (HW). Subsequently, two investigators (QG and HW) independently screened titles and abstracts against the pre-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria to identify potentially relevant studies. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third investigator (QS). Full texts of potentially eligible articles were then reviewed independently by the same two investigators. Inter-rater reliability was high (Cohen's k = 0.85) [18]. Any disagreements during the screening process were resolved via discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer.

Additionally, to minimize omission, we manually screened the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and, based on the research team’s expert judgment, included additional studies that met the criteria but were not identified in the initial search.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were established based on the PICOS framework (Participants, Interventions, Comparisons, Outcomes, and Study design).

Inclusion Criteria: Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria:

Participants: Adults aged ≥ 18 years, including healthy individuals, those with cardiometabolic risk factors, or patients with specific conditions (e.g., obesity, type 2 diabetes), excluding those who were bedridden or had severe mobility impairments.

Interventions: Regular short bouts of physical activity performed during prolonged sitting, defined as “sedentary breaks” or “exercise snacks.” Modalities included, but were not limited to, standing, walking, bodyweight resistance (SRA), under-desk pedaling, or cycling. The intensity ranged from low to moderate, with durations of several minutes per bout.

Comparisons: Continuous uninterrupted sitting (e.g., quiet seated rest or a seated control condition) without active interruption or structured activity breaks.

Outcomes: Reported at least one acute hemodynamic or vascular function outcome, including flow-mediated dilation (FMD, %), shear rate (SR, s⁻¹), peripheral blood flow (PBF) (mL·min⁻¹), peripheral arterial diameter (PAD, mm), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mm Hg), diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mm Hg), mean arterial pressure (MAP, mm Hg), or heart rate (HR, beats·min⁻¹). Outcomes must have been assessed immediately or within the same day following the intervention relative to the control.

Study Design: Controlled intervention trials, including randomized crossover or parallel-group designs. Given the focus on acute effects, crossover designs were predominant.

Data extraction

Two investigators, trained in systematic review methodology, independently extracted data from eligible studies using a standardized data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel. The following information was extracted:

Study Characteristics: publication year, country, and a study identifier retained from the standardized extraction worksheet for study tracking and data management. In the supplementary tables, these study identifiers were used for consistency with the extraction dataset and do not represent formal citation labels.

Participant Characteristics: Sample size, sex distribution, mean age, and health status (e.g., weight, BMI, clinical condition).

Intervention Details: Type of sedentary break (e.g., walking, resistance exercise, standing, cycling, stepping, stair climbing), break intensity (when reported), duration per bout, frequency (e.g., every x minutes), total number of bouts, cumulative activity duration, and total sitting time under experimental conditions. In addition, we reviewed the descriptions of control conditions, including whether uninterrupted sitting involved explicit restrictions on lower-limb movement, fidgeting, or incidental movement, when such information was reported in the original studies.

Outcome Measures: Baseline and post-intervention values for SBP, DBP, MAP, HR, FMD, SR, PBF, and PAD. FMD was prespecified as the primary outcome. Shear rate, peripheral blood flow, and SBP were considered key secondary outcomes, while HR, MAP, DBP, and PAD were treated as additional hemodynamic and vascular outcomes.

Effect Data: Statistics required for effect size calculation, such as mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size. If only confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, SDs were estimated using the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:SD=\sqrt{N}\frac{{CI}_{high}-{CI}_{low}}{2t}$$\end{document};

If standard errors (SEs) were provided, they were converted using:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:SD=\sqrt{N}\times\:SE$$\end{document}

When data were not directly reported in the article, they were estimated from figures or requested from the corresponding authors whenever possible.

Data were cross-checked by the two investigators. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or adjudication by a third investigator. A pilot extraction was conducted prior to the formal process to ensure consistency. Inter-rater agreement for data extraction was 96.25%, and all differences were resolved prior to analysis.

Effect size calculation

We utilized Mean Difference (MD) and Standardized Mean Difference (SMD, specifically Hedges’ g) as effect size metrics, depending on the outcome characteristics [19]. For outcomes reported in uniform units (e.g., blood pressure in mm Hg, heart rate in beats·min⁻¹), MD was selected to reflect absolute changes. For outcomes with varying units or significant baseline differences (e.g., FMD, SR, PBF, PAD), Hedges’ g was used to standardize mean differences into a dimensionless metric, ensuring comparability across studies.

Statistical models and meta-analysis

In our dataset, several studies reported multiple outcome variables, introducing statistical dependency among effect sizes. To retain all effect sizes for maximizing statistical power while accounting for this dependency, we employed a multilevel meta-analytic approach rather than a multivariate approach, as most primary studies did not report the necessary inter-variable correlation coefficients [20, 21]. Specifically, we constructed a three-level random-effects model that accounts for three sources of variance:

Level 1: Sampling variance (variance of the observed effect sizes).

Level 2: Within-study variance (variance between effect sizes within the same study).

Level 3: Between-study variance (variance between different studies).

All analyses were performed in R version 4.2.0 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) using the metafor and metaSEM packages. P-values were reported to assess the statistical significance of effect estimates. Detailed calculation methods and the complete statistical workflow are provided in Supplementary Material Sect. 1.2 Statistical Analysis.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity and evaluate the consistency of intervention effects across different conditions, we conducted pre-specified subgroup analyses and meta-regressions. In the subgroup analyses, studies were stratified according to participant characteristics and intervention protocols. Pooled effect sizes within each subgroup were estimated using the three-level random-effects model, and differences between groups were assessed using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Q}_{between}\:$$\end{document}statistic. To ensure robust estimation, formal subgroup comparisons were not performed for categories containing fewer than 5 effect sizes; instead, descriptive summaries were provided for these subgroups.

Pre-specified categorical moderators included: (1) age group (young adults: 18–39 years; middle-aged adults: 40–59 years; older adults: ≥60 years); (2) sex composition (men only, women only, mixed-sex samples); (3) weight status (non-obese vs. obese); (4) health status (healthy populations vs. clinical populations with comorbidities); (5) interruption modality (walking, bodyweight resistance, standing, cycling, under-desk pedaling, stair climbing); and (6) interruption frequency (breaks every 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, 120, or 240 min). We reported pooled effect sizes with 95% CIs for each subgroup and determined statistical significance of between-group differences based on the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Q}_{between}$$\end{document} test.

Meta-regression analysis was employed to examine linear associations between continuous moderator variables and effect sizes. Continuous moderators included mean age, mean body mass index (BMI), duration per break, total number of breaks, total accumulated break time, and total sitting time under experimental conditions. For statistically significant associations (P < 0.05), regression coefficients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document}) and 95% CIs were reported to quantify the magnitude of effect change per unit increase in the moderator. Non-significant results were interpreted as “no observed linear association” within the current data range, rather than definitive evidence of no heterogeneity, to avoid overinterpretation.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by two investigators using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0) [22]. For crossover trials, the specific RoB 2 extension for crossover designs was utilized to address design-specific biases. Assessment domains included the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Studies were classified as having “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias. For crossover trials, particular attention was paid to carryover effects, period effects, and the adequacy of washout periods. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third investigator.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for primary outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) [23] approach. Two investigators independently assessed five domains: risk of bias (limitations), inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Each domain was graded for severity (not serious, serious, or very serious), potentially resulting in downgrading of the evidence quality. The overall certainty of evidence was categorized as High, Moderate, Low, or Very Low, and results were presented in a Summary of Findings table.

Results

Literature search and study characteristics

The initial search identified a total of 3526 records, comprising 3522 from electronic databases and 4 from other sources. After removing 1332 duplicates, 2190 records were screened by title and abstract. Subsequently, 327 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 36 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the quantitative synthesis (Fig. 2).

The included studies comprised a total sample of 632 adults (aged ≥ 18 years), with mean participant ages ranging from 21 years [24] to 69.4 years [25]. The studies were published between 2014 and 2025. Geographically, the studies were conducted across multiple regions: 2 in China [24, 26], 10 in the United States [27–36], 5 in the United Kingdom [37–41], 2 in Japan [42, 43], 6 in Australia [44–49], 3 in the Netherlands [50–52], 3 in Brazil [25, 53, 54], and 2 in Canada [55, 56]. Additionally, single studies were conducted in Germany [57], South Korea [58], and New Zealand [59]. Detailed study characteristics are provided in supplementary material Table S2\S3.

Primary outcome: flow-mediated dilation (FMD)

Flow-mediated dilation (FMD)

The pooled analysis for FMD included 12 studies (25 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model demonstrated that interspersing prolonged sitting with sedentary breaks significantly improved FMD compared with continuous sitting (Hedges’ g = 0.44 [95% CI, 0.06, 0.81]; 95% PI [− 0.88, 1.76]; P = 0.02; Fig. 3). Consistent results were obtained using a traditional two-level random-effects model (g = 0.48 [95% CI, 0.19, 0.76]; 95% PI [− 0.74, 1.69]; P = 0.002). Overall, these findings suggest that sedentary breaks acutely promote improvement in peripheral endothelium-dependent vasodilation.

Sensitivity Analysis: Influence analysis and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis revealed that no single study exerted a disproportionate impact on the pooled effect size, indicating good robustness of the overall estimate (Figure S20).

Heterogeneity and Variance Decomposition: Variance decomposition based on structural equation modeling (SEM) showed that level 1 sampling error accounted for 27.18% of the total variance, level 2 within-study variance accounted for 32.24%, and level 3 between-study variance accounted for 40.58%. Likelihood ratio tests (LRT) indicated that the level 3 variance component was non-significant (LRT = 1.76, one-tailed P = 0.09), whereas the level 2 variance component was significant (LRT = 5.22, one-tailed P = 0.01; Fig. 3). This suggests that a substantial source of variation in effect sizes stems from the dependency of multiple effect sizes within studies, while between-study heterogeneity signals are relatively weak. Therefore, employing a three-level model to explicitly model the within-study correlation structure and avoid underestimation of standard errors remains justified. Subsequent results are primarily reported using the three-level model.

Moderator and Meta-Regression Analyses: We further examined potential moderator variables, provided that the robust variance estimation (RVE) requirement of at least 5 effect sizes per category was met. The results showed no significant differences between subgroups for categorical moderators such as age group, weight status, health status, interruption modality, and interruption frequency (all P > 0.05; Table 1). Similarly, meta-regression analysis did not find significant moderating effects for continuous variables, including age, BMI, duration per break, total number of breaks, total accumulated break time, and total sitting time under experimental conditions on the FMD improvement effect (Figure S13).

Publication Bias: The funnel plot showed a slight trend of asymmetry (Figure S18), suggesting potential small-study effects, but the overall extent was limited. Egger’s regression test did not detect significant small-study effects (two-level: t(23) = 1.47, P = 0.15; multilevel: t(23) = 0.03, P = 0.97; Figure S18). Furthermore, the Duval and Tweedie [60] trim-and-fill (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{0}$$\end{document}) analysis indicated no missing studies on either side of the funnel plot, and the pooled effect size remained virtually unchanged after adjustment. Synthesizing this evidence, the improvement effect of sedentary breaks on FMD is unlikely to be primarily driven by publication bias, and the overall conclusion possesses a certain degree of robustness.

Key Secondary Outcomes (Shear Rate, Blood Flow, SBP)

Shear rate (SR)

The pooled analysis for shear rate included 12 studies (33 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model showed that sedentary break interventions significantly increased vascular shear rate compared with continuous sitting (g = 0.44 [95% CI, 0.10, 0.78]; 95% PI [− 0.64, 1.52]; P = 0.01). Consistent results were obtained using a traditional two-level model (g = 0.36 [95% CI, 0.20, 0.53]; 95% PI [− 0.19, 0.91]; P < 0.001), and the initial heterogeneity test indicated significant between-study heterogeneity (Q(32) = 52.43, P = 0.01).

Sensitivity Analysis and Heterogeneity Handling: Influence diagnostics identified 1 outlier (g = 2.73 [32]; Figure S20). After excluding this outlier, the pooled effect size in the three-level model decreased slightly but remained significant (g = 0.32 [95% CI, 0.09, 0.54]; 95% PI [− 0.29, 0.93]; P = 0.008; Fig. 3). The two-level model result also remained significant (g = 0.31 [95% CI, 0.17, 0.44]; P < 0.001), and the heterogeneity test became non-significant (Q(31) = 31.43, P = 0.44). Therefore, to ensure robustness and consistency, subsequent analyses were based on the dataset with the outlier excluded.

Heterogeneity and Variance Decomposition: Variance decomposition based on SEM showed that level 1 sampling error accounted for 64.86%, level 2 within-study variance was negligible (< 0.001%), and level 3 between-study variance accounted for 35.14%. Likelihood ratio tests indicated that the level 3 variance component was significant (LRT = 6.11, one-tailed P = 0.01), whereas the level 2 variance component was non-significant (LRT < 0.01, one-tailed P > 0.49; Fig. 3). This indicates that the overall variation mainly stems from between-study differences rather than the dependency of multiple effect sizes within the same study. Therefore, given the nested structure of effect sizes, employing a three-level random-effects model to model between-study heterogeneity and handle correlations remains justified. Subsequent results are primarily reported using the three-level model.

Moderator and Meta-Regression Analyses: Moderator analysis based on robust variance estimation (RVE) showed that only interruption modality had a significant moderating effect on the improvement of shear rate (F(2,27) = 3.48, P = 0.045; Table 2). The stair climbing mode yielded the largest effect size (g = 0.88 [95% CI, 0.38, 1.38]; P = 0.001; Table 2), which was significantly higher than other modes (pairwise comparison t(27) = 3.63, P = 0.01). Other categorical variables showed no significant differences between subgroups (all P > 0.05; Table 2). Meta-regression results suggested that only duration per break was significantly positively correlated with the magnitude of shear rate improvement, while continuous variables such as total number of breaks, total accumulated break time, and total sitting time under experimental conditions did not show significant associations (Figure S16).

Publication Bias: The funnel plot showed some degree of asymmetry (Figure S18), suggesting potential small-study effects. Egger’s test was approaching significance in the two-level model (t(30) = 2.03, P = 0.052), whereas the multilevel Egger’s test was non-significant (t(30) = 1.14, P = 0.26; Figure S18). Trim-and-fill (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{0}$$\end{document}) analysis further indicated that approximately 24 effect sizes might be missing on the left side of the funnel plot. After imputation, the pooled effect became non-significant (adjusted g = − 0.11 [95% CI, − 0.27, 0.05]; P = 0.18), which differs notably from the observed value (g = 0.32 [95% CI, 0.09, 0.54]; P = 0.008), suggesting a risk of effect overestimation for this outcome. Therefore, the impact of sedentary breaks on shear rate should be interpreted with caution.

Peripheral Blood Flow (PBF)

The pooled analysis for peripheral blood flow included 13 studies (34 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model showed that sedentary break interventions significantly increased peripheral blood flow compared with continuous sitting (g = 0.58 [95% CI, 0.25, 0.89]; 95% PI [− 0.48, 1.63]; P < 0.001). Consistent results were obtained using a traditional two-level model (g = 0.48 [95% CI, 0.30, 0.66]; 95% PI [− 0.28, 1.24]; P < 0.001). The initial heterogeneity test indicated significant between-study heterogeneity (Q(33) = 66.40, P < 0.001).

Sensitivity Analysis and Heterogeneity Handling: Influence diagnostics identified 1 outlier (g = 2.63 [32]; Figure S20). After excluding this outlier, the pooled effect size in the three-level model decreased slightly but remained significant (g = 0.46 [95% CI, 0.22, 0.71]; 95% PI [− 0.29, 1.21]; P < 0.001; Fig. 3), and the two-level model result also remained significant (g = 0.43 [95% CI, 0.27, 0.59]; 95% PI [− 0.12, 0.97]; P < 0.001). Although the heterogeneity test remained statistically significant (Q(32) = 48.24, P = 0.03), the direction and magnitude of the effect changed little before and after exclusion, and the prediction interval narrowed significantly, suggesting good robustness of the overall estimate. Therefore, subsequent analyses were based on the dataset with the outlier excluded.

Heterogeneity and Variance Decomposition: After excluding the outlier, variance decomposition based on the three-level model showed that sampling error accounted for 52.32%, within-study variance was negligible, and between-study variance accounted for 47.68%. Likelihood ratio tests indicated that the level 3 variance component was significant (LRT = 13.56, one-tailed P < 0.001), whereas the level 2 variance component was non-significant (LRT < 0.01, one-tailed P > 0.49) (Fig. 3). Therefore, the three-level model was primarily used to report results to model between-study differences and handle the nested structure of effect sizes.

Moderator and Meta-Regression Analyses: Moderator variable analysis showed that most potential moderators did not differ significantly between subgroups (all P > 0.05; Table 3). Meta-regression results suggested that total sitting time under experimental conditions was significantly correlated with the blood flow improvement effect: the longer the total sitting time during the experiment, the more significant the effect of sedentary breaks on improving blood flow relative to the control. Other variables, such as duration per break, total number of breaks, and total accumulated break time, did not show significant associations (Figure S14).

Publication Bias: The funnel plot showed slight asymmetry (Figure S18). Egger’s test suggested small-study effects in the two-level model (t(31) = 3.08, P = 0.004), while the multilevel Egger’s test did not reach significance (t(31) = 1.78, P = 0.08; Figure S18). Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP)

The pooled analysis for SBP included 20 studies (51 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model showed that sedentary breaks significantly reduced SBP compared with continuous sitting (MD = − 2.09 mm Hg [95% CI, − 3.13, − 1.05]; 95% PI [− 3.13, − 1.05]; P < 0.001). The traditional two-level random-effects model yielded consistent results (MD = − 2.09 mm Hg [95% CI, − 3.13, − 1.05]; 95% PI [− 3.13, − 1.05]; P < 0.001), suggesting that sedentary breaks can lead to an acute SBP reduction of approximately 2 mm Hg.

Sensitivity Analysis and Robustness: Influence diagnostics suggested 1 effect size might be an outlier (MD = − 4 mm Hg41; Figure S19). After excluding this outlier, the pooled effect size decreased slightly but remained significant (three-level: MD = − 1.67 mm Hg [95% CI, − 2.81, − 0.52]; 95% PI [− 2.81, − 0.52]; P = 0.005, Fig. 4; two-level: MD = − 1.67 mm Hg [95% CI, − 2.81, − 0.52]; P = 0.005). The heterogeneity test was non-significant both before (Q(50) = 20.65, P = 0.99) and after (Q(49) = 17.60, P = 0.999) exclusion, and the prediction interval narrowed further after exclusion, suggesting consistent effect direction and robust overall estimation. Therefore, subsequent analyses were based on the dataset with the outlier excluded.

Heterogeneity and Variance Decomposition: Variance decomposition showed that the total heterogeneity came almost entirely from sampling error (level 1 variance accounted for approx. 100%), while within-study (level 2) and between-study (level 3) variances were close to zero. Likelihood ratio tests also did not indicate that adding level 2 or level 3 variance components significantly improved model fit (both LRT < 0.01, one-tailed P > 0.49; Fig. 4). These results suggest that there is almost no additional heterogeneity in SBP effect sizes between studies. Although a two-level model would be sufficient to fit the data in this scenario, considering that some studies reported multiple effect sizes and the presence of a nested structure, we still adopted the three-level framework as the primary reporting model.

Moderator and Meta-Regression Analyses: Moderator variable analysis did not find significant differences between subgroups for categorical variables such as age, sex, weight status, health status, interruption modality, and interruption frequency (all P > 0.05; Table 4). Meta-regression also did not observe significant associations between continuous variables (age, BMI, duration per break, total number of breaks, total accumulated break time, or total sitting time under experimental conditions) and the magnitude of the SBP effect (Figure S9).

Publication Bias: The funnel plot showed slight asymmetry (Figure S17), but Egger’s regression test did not suggest significant small-study effects (two-level: t(48) = − 0.74, P = 0.15; multilevel: t(48) = − 1.45, P = 0.26; Figure S17). Additional hemodynamic and vascular outcomes (HR, MAP, DBP, PAD)

Heart rate (HR)

The pooled analysis for heart rate included 16 studies (42 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model showed that sedentary breaks could cause a small increase in heart rate compared with continuous sitting (MD = 3.46 beats·min⁻¹ [95% CI, 0.61, 6.32]; 95% PI [− 6.91, 13.84]; P = 0.02), and the two-level model conclusion was consistent (MD = 2.40 beats·min⁻¹ [95% CI, 0.78, 4.03]; 95% PI [− 5.76, 10.57]; P = 0.005). The heterogeneity test indicated significant differences between studies (Q(41) = 97.06, P < 0.001), indicating substantial variation in the magnitude of heart rate responses across studies.

Sensitivity Analysis and Outlier Handling: Influence diagnostics identified 2 potential outliers (MD = 20 beats·min⁻¹, MD = 16.8 beats·min⁻¹ [37,58]; Figure S19). After excluding outliers, the pooled effect size decreased but remained statistically significant (three-level: MD = 2.28 beats·min⁻¹ [95% CI, 0.04, 4.51]; 95% PI [− 4.09, 8.65]; P = 0.046, Fig. 4; two-level: MD = 1.52 [95% CI, 0.27, 2.78]; 95% PI [− 2.99, 6.03]; P = 0.02). Heterogeneity decreased slightly but remained present (Q(39) = 58.39, P = 0.02). Overall, the heart rate increase signal was still observable after excluding extreme values, but the effect magnitude was small.

Heterogeneity and Variance Decomposition: Variance decomposition based on SEM showed: level 1 sampling error accounted for 46.33%, level 2 within-study variance was negligible (< 0.001%), and level 3 between-study variance accounted for 53.67%. Likelihood ratio tests indicated that the level 3 variance component was significant (LRT = 17, one-tailed P < 0.001), whereas the level 2 variance component was non-significant (LRT < 0.01, one-tailed P > 0.49) (Fig. 4). This indicates that the variation in heart rate effect mainly comes from between-study differences rather than the dependency of multiple effect sizes within the same study. Therefore, employing a three-level model to model between-study heterogeneity and handle the nested structure remains justified, and subsequent results are primarily reported using the three-level model.

Publication Bias: The funnel plot showed clear asymmetry (Figure S17), suggesting potential small-study effects. Egger’s test reached significance under the two-level framework (t(38) = 2.39, P = 0.02), whereas the multilevel Egger’s test did not reach significance (t(38) = 0.368, P = 0.72) (Figure S17). The pooled analysis for MAP included 13 studies (27 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model showed that the effect of sedentary breaks on MAP did not reach statistical significance compared with continuous sitting (MD = − 0.66 mm Hg [95% CI, − 1.53, 0.21]; 95% PI [− 1.53, 0.21]; P = 0.13). Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP)

The pooled analysis for DBP included 19 studies (49 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model showed that the effect of sedentary breaks on DBP did not reach statistical significance compared with continuous sitting (MD = − 0.76 mm Hg [95% CI, − 1.59, 0.08]; 95% PI [− 2.55, 1.04]; P = 0.07). The two-level model yielded a smaller but significant overall effect (MD = − 0.88 mm Hg [95% CI, − 1.58, − 0.19]; 95% PI [− 1.92, 0.16]; P = 0.01), suggesting that a signal of mild DBP reduction might appear when the correlation of within-study effect sizes is not fully accounted for. Given the inconsistent results between the two models, further sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess robustness.

Sensitivity Analysis and Outlier Handling: Influence diagnostics identified 3 potential outliers (MD = − 1.81 mm Hg [47], MD = − 1.41 mm Hg [47], MD = − 3.00 mm Hg [41]; Figure S19). After excluding outliers, the pooled effect size further approached 0 and was no longer significant (three-level: MD = − 0.19 mm Hg [95% CI, − 1.07, 0.69]; 95% PI [− 1.07, 0.69]; P = 0.66, Fig. 4; two-level: MD = − 0.19 mm Hg; P = 0.66). Peripheral Arterial Diameter (PAD)

For the peripheral arterial diameter outcome, a total of 11 studies provided data (35 effect sizes). The three-level random-effects model showed that sedentary breaks had no significant effect on peripheral arterial diameter compared with continuous sitting (g = − 0.03 [95% CI, − 0.15, 0.09]; 95% PI [− 0.15, 0.09]; P = 0.60) (Fig. 3). The two-level model result was consistent (g = − 0.03 [95% CI, − 0.15, 0.09]; 95% PI [− 0.15, 0.09]; P = 0.60), suggesting that a single sedentary break intervention does not significantly change peripheral arterial caliber. The initial heterogeneity test did not suggest significant variation between studies (Q(34) = 8.69, P > 0.49).

Sensitivity Analysis and Robustness: Influence diagnostics and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis did not find any single study exerting an abnormal impact on the pooled effect size; excluding any study did not change the direction and significance of the result (Figure S20), indicating good robustness of the overall estimate. Discussion

Key findings and interpretation

This three-level meta-analysis provides robust evidence that interrupting prolonged sitting with short, frequent bouts of physical activity—conceptually defined as “exercise snacks”—elicits acute, favorable improvements in both peripheral vascular function and systemic hemodynamics. Specifically, compared with uninterrupted sitting (control condition), the exercise-snack intervention significantly enhanced flow-mediated dilation (FMD)—the gold-standard measure of endothelial function—concurrent with increases in peripheral blood flow (PBF) and modest reductions in systolic blood pressure (SBP). These benefits occurred alongside no clear changes in diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), or resting arterial diameter, suggesting that the acute vascular effects of exercise snacks were not accompanied by marked perturbations in these additional hemodynamic and vascular measures. While shear rate (SR) and heart rate (HR) also exhibited trends toward improvement, rigorous sensitivity analyses for bias suggest these specific results may be influenced by small-study effects and thus warrant cautious interpretation. Collectively, these findings support a framework wherein regular “micro-doses” of activity breaks facilitate a “hemodynamic reset,” thereby acutely preserving vascular health and mitigating the physiological burden of prolonged sitting.

Acute effects of sedentary breaks on vascular function and hemodynamic outcomes

Acute Effects on Flow-Mediated Dilation (FMD)

Compared with uninterrupted sitting (control condition), this study confirms that introducing regular activity breaks significantly improves FMD, suggesting that sitting-induced endothelial dysfunction is acutely reversible. Previous meta-analyses regarding whether sedentary breaks can acutely improve FMD have yielded inconsistent conclusions. Paterson et al. [7] included 8 studies and reported that sedentary breaks acutely increased FMD by 1.91% (SMD = 0.57); similarly, Soto-Rodríguez et al. [15] and Zheng et al. [14] observed improvements of approximately 1.5%–1.74%. Conversely, Taylor et al. [16] suggested that the acute effect of sedentary breaks on FMD was small and non-significant (SMD = 0.13), thereby creating controversy in the field.

Our findings provide stronger and more methodologically robust evidence helping to clarify this inconsistency. Compared with previous studies, we integrated updated primary trials and expanded the sample size, while employing a three-level random-effects model to explicitly model the statistical dependency of multiple effect sizes within the same study. This approach allows for more rational estimation of standard errors and confidence intervals without information loss. Under this more rigorous and conservative statistical framework, the acute enhancement of FMD by sedentary breaks remained observable, supporting an overall beneficial effect on endothelial function.

Regarding intervention protocols, we did not observe significant moderating effects of interruption modality or interruption frequency on FMD improvement, suggesting that several feasible activity-break strategies may be beneficial within the current evidence base. Specifically, walking, bodyweight resistance, standing, cycling, under-desk pedaling, and stair climbing were all identified as viable modalities; furthermore, interruption frequencies of both 30 and 60 min were confirmed to be effective. It must be re-emphasized that “no significant moderating effect” does not equate to “complete equivalence.” However, the absence of significant moderating effects should not be interpreted as definitive equivalence between modalities or frequencies, as some subgroup comparisons were based on a limited number of studies and may have been underpowered. Therefore, while the overall acute effect on FMD appears supported, further research is still needed to determine whether specific break modalities or frequencies yield differential effects under standardized prescription frameworks.

Collectively, our study supports a strategy of employing short activity breaks every 30–60 min during prolonged sitting to significantly improve FMD in adults. This finding has clear clinical relevance: a 1% decrease in FMD is associated with an approximate 13% increase in the risk of cardiovascular events [8, 9]; conversely, a 1% increase in FMD is generally considered to have potential cardiovascular preventive significance [8, 9]. Therefore, although the results of this study represent acute effects, they can be viewed as an early physiological signal with clinical direction, providing an evidence-based rationale for the immediate protection of endothelial function in sedentary settings through brief, easily implementable activity breaks that can be incorporated into routine sitting with minimal requirements for time, equipment, or supervision.

Acute effects of sedentary breaks on peripheral shear rate indices

Compared with uninterrupted sitting (control condition), this study indicates that introducing regular activity breaks can acutely improve peripheral shear rate indices. This suggests that the core value of “exercise snacks” extends beyond mere energy expenditure to the restoration of critical hemodynamic stimuli, thereby providing immediate endothelial protection. This finding is generally consistent with meta-analyses by Soto-Rodríguez et al. [15] and Zheng et al. [14], both of which reported increases in peripheral shear rate of approximately 7.58–12.70 s-1. Based on our primary analysis, shear rate increased significantly (g = 0.32, 95% CI 0.09–0.54; P = 0.008; GRADE: Very Low), implying that activity breaks may ameliorate the adverse hemodynamic environment by enhancing antegrade shear stimuli.

However, it is crucial to emphasize that interpretation of this outcome warrants caution. After bias correction using the trim-and-fill method (R0), the pooled effect for shear rate was no longer significant, suggesting that this finding may be influenced by small-study effects or publication bias. Given the existing heterogeneity in the measurement and reporting of shear rate across studies (e.g., inconsistencies in index definitions, measurement time windows, and directionality reporting), future high-quality research with larger sample sizes, pre-registered protocols, and standardized reporting is required to clarify the true effect size and certainty of evidence. Regarding moderator analyses, we observed no significant effects across age group, weight status, or interruption frequency, suggesting that the acute response to shear stimuli may possess a degree of universality across different ages and body weights. In contrast, a significant moderating effect was observed for interruption modality. Specifically, stair climbing elicited the largest effect size (g = 0.88, 95% CI [0.38, 1.38]; P = 0.001), which was significantly higher than other modes (pairwise comparison t(27) = 3.63, P = 0.01). This result is physiologically robust: stair climbing functions as a form of Vigorous Intermittent Lifestyle Physical Activity (VILPA). Compared with walking or bodyweight resistance, overcoming gravity requires the recruitment of larger lower-limb muscle groups (e.g., quadriceps, gluteals), eliciting higher metabolic demand and stronger circulatory mobilization. Consequently, this leads to a sharper rise in cardiac output and limb perfusion within a short timeframe, generating a more potent shear stimulus on the vessel wall.

Furthermore, our regression analysis indicated a positive correlation between duration per break and the magnitude of shear rate improvement, with the rate of benefit accelerating most rapidly within the 2–5-minute window. This “time-window advantage” likely reflects the threshold-saturation kinetics of endothelial mechanotransduction. Breaks shorter than approximately 1–2 min may only trigger transient blood flow increases, insufficient to stably alter shear patterns (particularly in suppressing retrograde/oscillatory shear) or fully activate endothelial mechanotransduction [61, 62]. Conversely, when duration reaches 2–5 min, repetitive muscle pump activity can more effectively disrupt venous stasis, increase antegrade flow, and establish distinct “shear spikes.” This provides the endothelium with a more sustained, regular laminar stimulus, promoting eNOS phosphorylation and NO bioavailability, thereby rapidly improving vasodilation and vascular tone balance [63, 64]. Beyond this duration, further increases in shear rate may enter a phase of diminishing marginal returns (e.g., approaching the saturation of endothelial mechanosensitivity or physiological limits of individual circulatory response), resulting in the observed steeper effect slope within the 2–5-minute interval. This explanation also aligns with the time-course for the onset of post-exercise hypotension and peripheral resistance reduction [65].

In summary, this study supports that interrupting sitting every 30–60 min can improve shear rate indices in adults. To maximize the shear stimulus, we recommend prioritizing high-intensity modes involving large muscle groups (e.g., stair climbing) with a duration of 2–5 min. This finding has potential clinical implications: exposure to higher, “healthier” shear stress helps maintain endothelial phenotype homeostasis and reduces the risk of endothelial dysfunction [11], potentially slowing the progression of atherosclerosis and reducing cardiovascular risk over the long term [66]. At the same time, given the reduced robustness of shear rate findings after bias correction, these prescription recommendations should be viewed as “directional support” based on current evidence, and further consolidation of certainty is needed through future high-quality research.

Acute effects of sedentary breaks on peripheral blood flow

Compared with uninterrupted sitting (control condition), incorporating short, regular physical activity breaks (exercise-snack condition) during sitting significantly enhances peripheral blood flow in the acute phase. This conclusion aligns with the direction of previous evidence. The meta-analysis by Soto-Rodríguez et al. [15] similarly reported that sedentary breaks increased peripheral blood flow by a mean of 12.08 mL/min, suggesting that “exercise snacks” exert a reproducible acute pro-perfusion effect. Notably, while Soto-Rodríguez et al. [15] included a limited number of studies (n = 6) restricted primarily to young healthy adults, the present study included 13 studies and expanded the population to include older adults and clinical cohorts. This validates the acute effect across a broader demographic spectrum, thereby enhancing the external validity and translational value of the results.

To elucidate potential sources of heterogeneity underlying the pooled effect, we further conducted subgroup and regression analyses. Subgroup analyses revealed that variables including age group, weight status, interruption frequency, and interruption modality did not exhibit statistically significant moderating effects. This suggests that, within the current scope of evidence, the acute effect of exercise snacks on promoting peripheral blood flow possesses a degree of consistency across populations and protocols. However, it must be emphasized that the limited number of studies in certain subgroups may result in insufficient statistical power; therefore, the “observation of no significant moderating effect” should be interpreted as an inconclusive finding under current evidence, rather than a definitive judgment of “complete equivalence” between protocols.

Meta-regression analysis further indicated a positive correlation between total sitting time under experimental conditions and the magnitude of blood flow improvement; that is, the longer the sedentary exposure, the more pronounced the elevation in blood flow induced by exercise snacks. This phenomenon has a plausible hemodynamic explanation: longer periods of uninterrupted sitting exacerbate lower limb venous stasis, muscle pump stagnation, and reduced limb perfusion, thereby creating a more profound “low flow–low shear” state [67, 68]. Consequently, when an exercise snack is introduced, the brief muscle contractions elicit a greater restoration of venous return and antegrade flow, leading to a more distinct functional hyperemic response (rebound hyperemia) [30, 44]. In essence, the greater the acute “deconditioning” caused by sitting, the larger the perfusion reserve released by the interruption stimulus, manifesting as a more significant improvement in peripheral blood flow.

In summary, interrupting sitting with short activity bouts every 30–60 min can significantly improve peripheral blood flow in adults. This finding is clinically meaningful, as higher blood flow is critical for maintaining peripheral vascular function and reflects the capacity and status of vascular supply to tissues [11]. Furthermore, where feasible, efforts should still be made to minimize total daily accumulated sedentary exposure and avoid prolonged uninterrupted sitting; for populations where sitting is unavoidable, regular activity breaks should be regarded as a scalable “micro-intervention” strategy.

Blood pressure and safety considerations

Compared with uninterrupted sitting (control condition), this study observed an acute reduction in SBP, while DBP and MAP remained generally unchanged. This pattern aligns with the physiological direction of previous evidence: prolonged sitting on a timescale of ≥ 1 h may induce a mild “blood pressure drift,” a phenomenon driven by lower-limb venous pooling and compensatory sympathetic activation [69–71]. Introducing activity breaks engages the skeletal muscle pump to facilitate venous return, improve peripheral perfusion, and optimize vascular regulation, thereby attenuating or even reversing the upward trend in SBP [69–71]. Although the magnitude of SBP reduction in this study was modest (approximately 1–2 mm Hg), it may still be relevant at the population level if sustained over time, given that cardiovascular risk reductions are generally proportional to the magnitude of SBP lowering [72].

Concurrently, the transient increase in heart rate observed during activity breaks represents a normal physiological response to exercise stimuli, particularly with moderate-intensity or resistance/strength-based modalities [73]. In the present meta-analysis, DBP, MAP, and PAD did not show consistent significant changes relative to uninterrupted sitting. However, orthostatic intolerance, symptom responses, and other safety-related outcomes were not prespecified for formal extraction and were not consistently reported across the included trials. Therefore, the present synthesis does not support firm conclusions regarding safety or tolerability. Future studies should systematically report adverse events, symptom responses, and implementation-related outcomes to better inform safety, acceptability, and real-world applicability.

Potential physiological mechanisms underlying improved vascular function

From a mechanistic perspective, prolonged sitting may create a deleterious hemodynamic environment characterized by reduced limb blood flow, diminished shear stimulus, and prolonged lower-limb joint flexion, all of which may adversely affect vascular function [74–76].

In contrast, sedentary breaks appear to interrupt this maladaptive state by inducing repetitive muscle contractions, increasing peripheral blood flow, and restoring a more favorable shear stress pattern [9]. These hemodynamic changes may in turn enhance endothelial responsiveness, potentially through greater nitric oxide bioavailability and reduced vasoconstrictor influence [4, 77].

In addition, the small acute reduction in SBP observed in the present review may reflect broader peripheral vascular effects, such as reduced sympathetic activity or lower total peripheral resistance [69, 71]. Overall, these findings support a plausible mechanistic pathway linking activity interruptions with acute vascular protection, although the underlying biological mechanisms still require confirmation in dedicated mechanistic studies.

Clinical implications and practical considerations

Preserving vascular endothelial function is of pivotal significance for reducing the morbidity and mortality risks associated with various chronic diseases, such as diabetes and hypertension [78, 79]. Sedentary breaks may represent a promising behavioral strategy for interrupting prolonged sitting in contexts such as workplaces. However, because the included studies were acute, experimentally controlled trials and did not systematically assess implementation-related outcomes such as adherence, acceptability, or protocol completion, the present review does not allow firm conclusions regarding safety, feasibility, or real-world implementation. Notably, the trials included in this study predominantly utilized highly accessible, lifestyle-embedded modalities—such as walking, stair climbing, bodyweight resistance, standing, and under-desk pedaling. These activities were primarily of low-to-moderate intensity and required only a few minutes per bout to yield appreciable acute vascular functional benefits.

More importantly, accumulating periodic, multiple daily bouts of short-duration activity has been shown to improve various cardiometabolic health markers (e.g., ) [80–82], with benefits comparable to traditional continuous or interval exercise under certain conditions [83, 84]. Furthermore, prospective cohort studies indicate that such high-frequency lifestyle activities are associated with lower rates of adverse cardiovascular events, reduced incidence of cardiovascular disease [85, 86], and lower mortality risk [87]. Based on our findings, several practical considerations may be cautiously suggested: For general adults, priority should be given to activity modalities engaging large muscle groups (e.g., stair climbing) during sedentary periods. Low-to-moderate intensity activity breaks of 2–5 min at a relatively high frequency (e.g., every 30–60 min) may represent a reasonable approach for interrupting prolonged sitting and mitigating its acute adverse effects on vascular function. Concurrently, where feasible, efforts should be made to minimize total daily accumulated sedentary time (e.g., aiming for a target of 6 h/day based on relevant guidelines) and to avoid prolonged continuous inactivity. These considerations may offer a practical reference for strength and conditioning professionals and other practitioners when translating movement-promoting guidance into context-specific, inclusive behavioral strategies.

Limitations and future directions

Our study has several limitations that merit acknowledgment. First, the included studies were exclusively acute, single-day, laboratory-based experiments. Consequently, it remains unknown whether these acute vascular and hemodynamic benefits translate into long-term structural vascular adaptations or sustained blood pressure improvements with chronic adherence. Long-term randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are urgently needed to bridge this evidence gap.

Second, despite our rigorous statistical approach, the robustness of findings for shear rate and heart rate was attenuated following bias correction. This highlights the necessity for future studies with larger sample sizes and pre-registered protocols to minimize publication bias and enhance the certainty of evidence.

Third, marked heterogeneity in intervention protocols remains, precluding the establishment of a precise “dose-response” relationship at present. In addition, the characteristics of the control condition were not uniformly reported across studies. In particular, whether uninterrupted sitting explicitly restricted lower-limb movement, fidgeting, or incidental postural adjustments was often unclear. This variability may have influenced hemodynamic responses and contributed to between-study heterogeneity, especially for outcomes such as shear rate, peripheral blood flow, and FMD. Future research should employ more standardized designs to systematically compare different interruption “doses” (i.e., intensity, duration, frequency, and accumulated volume) to identify optimal prescription parameters tailored to specific populations.

Finally, safety-related outcomes such as orthostatic intolerance, symptom responses, or other adverse events were not prespecified for formal extraction and were inconsistently reported across the included trials. Therefore, the present review does not allow a formal synthesis of safety or tolerability, and future studies should report these outcomes more systematically.

Conclusion

This three-level meta-analysis suggests that interrupting prolonged sitting with short, frequent bouts of physical activity acutely improves peripheral artery endothelial function (FMD), increases limb blood flow, and modestly lowers systolic blood pressure. Findings for shear rate and heart rate were less robust following bias sensitivity analyses and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Overall, these results support the potential vascular benefits of breaking up prolonged sitting, although further high-quality long-term studies are needed before firm practical recommendations can be made.
