Evaluating the performance of large language models versus human researchers on real world complex medical queries
Authors: Daphna Idan, Itamar Ben-Shitrit, Mark Volevich, Yair Binyamin, Raouf Nassar, Majd Nassar, Nitzan Abelson, Alexander Zlotnik, Sharon Einav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhether large language models (LLMs) can resolve real-world dilemmas faced by clinicians remains unclear and physician quality assessment is often used as a measure of LLM output quality. We compared reports - defined as answers to clinical queries generated by LLMs or written by human researchers - generated by GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0, and Claude Sonnet 3.5 in response to such dilemmas (n = 20) to reports written by trained human researchers and studied whether physician satisfaction correlates with objective report quality. Twenty human reports and fifty-six LLM-reports were analyzed. Human reports met physicians’ expectations more frequently (p = 0.044), were considered more reliable (p = 0.032), professionally written (p = 0.003), and time-saving (p = 0.003). Human reports cited more sources (p < 0.001) and while these were from lower ranking journals (median IF: 7 [3, 11] vs 14 [10, 27], p = 0.003), they were considered more relevant (p < 0.001). Unlike LLMs, human reports contained no hallucinated (p < 0.001) or unfaithful (p < 0.001) citations. However, no meaningful correlation was identified between physician satisfaction and objective measures of report quality. A meaningful gap remains between LLM and human capacity to respond reliably and in a relevant manner to real-life clinical dilemmas. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been shown to be capable of producing better responses than medical students to medical licensing questions1. Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4 (GPT-4), for instance, surpassed the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) passing score by 20 points2, while MED-Palm2 exhibited an accuracy of 86.5% when responding to USMLE-style queries within the MedQA dataset3. Furthermore, LLMs also seem to be able to produce accurate and correct responses to expert-level multiple choice questions in some medical professions4–6. Real life medical dilemmas are often multidisciplinary and many of these dilemmas are presented as open-ended questions. In responding to open ended questions LLMs seem to perform significantly worse7–9.

Claims are repeatedly being made that LLMs are approaching human capability to respond to real-world medical dilemmas4,10,11 - defined here as clinical management questions involving complex cases in which physicians must consult the medical literature while considering unique individual patient details. However, many evaluation processes have relied on subjective assessment methods, such as clinician ratings and satisfaction measures, without incorporating standardized objective assessments9,10,12. Additionally, whether LLMs are capable of responding to complex medical dilemmas raised by physicians during routine clinical work has rarely been assessed. Such dilemmas are often complex and require a deep understanding of medical concepts13 as well as consideration of the individual circumstances of the case at hand. If accurate and comprehensive answers to complex medical questions can be provided and objectively evaluated, LLMs could assist in decision making processes and considerably revolutionize how clinicians access information and make decisions in the future.

We conducted a study comparing the performance of GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0, and Claude sonnet 3.5, to a trained human researcher in responding to real-life complex medical treatment dilemmas put forward by clinicians during their daily work. The study aimed to assess whether LLMs match the ability of trained human researchers in providing responses that satisfy clinicians. To do so, we evaluated both subjective clinician satisfaction and objective metrics - including whether the medical literature cited in such responses exists, is relevant, cited accurately and of good quality. This study further evaluates the possible correlation between subjective clinicians’ satisfaction these objective response metrics.

Based on the existing literature, we hypothesized that if any differences between LLMs and humans exist these would be minor and possibly favor LLMs. We also hypothesized that physician satisfaction correlates with objective measures of information quality.

Methods

Study design

Prospective, single center study of responses to real-life medical dilemmas posed by clinicians in several medical disciplines, comparing the responses provided by three LLMs to a human researcher in subjective (satisfaction, relevance) and objective parameters.

This study is reported in accordance with STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations14.

Clinical setting

The study was conducted at the Soroka University Medical Center (SUMC), a tertiary medical center belonging to the Clalit Healthcare Service and affiliated with Ben Gurion University. The SUMC is a tertiary referral center with more than 1200 admission beds. It was selected as Clalit best medical center in 2022. The SUMC provides medical services to more than one million residents in the Negev region, including a large Bedouin population, accommodating > 250,000 visits to the Department of Emergency Medicine and approximately 84,000 inpatient admissions annually.

Ethics

The study protocol was submitted to the Soroka University Medical Center Institutional Review Board (SUMC IRB), which waived the need for ethical approval, as the reports were de-identified and involved no direct involvement of human participants or clinical interventions. The IRB also waived the requirement for informed consent, as the data were anonymized. Researchers had no access to full patient records, and the information in each query was minimal; the most detailed query included eight clinical data points and 91 words. All participating physicians provided written informed consent and signed a declaration confirming they would not base clinical decisions on the generated reports. All procedures were conducted following institutional guidelines and relevant ethical regulations, including those outlined by the Declaration of Helsinki.

Use cases (population)

Real-life dilemmas arising during patient treatment were used. The dilemmas were presented as queries by physicians providing service in four domains (Pediatrics, Pediatric endocrinology, Pediatric gastroenterology, and Obstetric anesthesiology). Each physician was allowed to submit up to five queries related to treatment. Physicians were chosen using purposive sampling, prioritizing mid-level physicians who treat complex cases daily but have no formal training with LLMs.

Included were queries regarding the clinical management of patients whose medical conditions presented a challenge for the physician. The queries were selected by each physician independently, based on their realization that in order to make a clinical decision they must refer to the medical literature taking into consideration unique individual case details.

Excluded were general queries on overarching topics unrelated to specific patient cases.

The answers generated by either LLMs or human researchers to each query are termed as reports and were classified as cases, and those generated by LLMs were classified as controls. Sample de-identified reports are presented in Supplementary D. The LLMs used for generating reports were GPT-4o (by OpenAI), Gemini 2.0 (by Google), and Claude Sonnet 3.5 (by Anthropic).

Variables and their sources

Before study initiation, the participating physicians were requested to complete a Resistance to Change Scale Questionnaire (Supplement A)15. While submitting each query, the physicians provided their impression of the case complexity (simple, moderate, complex). Data were collected on case characteristics. The number of iterations required by the physician to either achieve LLM generation of a satisfactory (based on their judgment) response, or discontinue attempting to do so, was documented. Report quality was assessed based on the existence of the cited sources, their quality (journal impact factor and quartile), the faithfulness of the citation to its source and the relevance of the citation to the case at hand (supplement B).

Main outcome measures

The primary study measure was clinician satisfaction with the reports generated by the LLMs versus those written by human researchers. The secondary outcome measures included the existence, quality, and relevance of sources cited by LLMs compared to those cited by trained human researchers and the correlation between physician satisfaction and these measures.

Data collection

Each physician submitted five queries to the research team. Examples to queries that were sent (The full list of queries is presented in Supplement Table 3):

Is growth hormone safe in a patient with a genetic VUS finding suspected to cause Autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome?

A 17 years old female patient drank alkalotic caustic substance intentionally two hours ago, admitted to the emergency room vomiting and drooling. What are the chances (in percentage) of developing esophageal stenosis within two months?

A 17 years old female patient drank alkalotic caustic substance intentionally two hours ago, admitted to the emergency room vomiting and drooling. What are the chances (in percentage) of developing esophageal stenosis within two months?

Each query was sent to a human researcher. At the same time, the physicians were granted access to the LLMs in order to submit the same query with the intent of generating their own report. Physicians underwent a brief training session on the use of the LLMs and were then allowed to run their queries on the LLMs either at work or at home. LLMs were accessed using the standard web interfaces. The physicians also determined the stopping point for their investigation. Finally, they reported their satisfaction with the individual LLM reports and the human reports using a standard Satisfaction Questionnaire (Supplement Table 4).

The quality of the reports was evaluated post-hoc by an independent researcher blinded to the source of the reports and to physician satisfaction. The existence of each source was verified through PubMed and Google Scholar searches or through a direct URL search for website sources. Journal impact factors and quartiles were obtained from journal official websites or, if unavailable, from Scimago or Clarivate. Citation accuracy was verified by reviewing the full text of the cited papers to ensure faithfulness to the original source. Citations were deemed relevant when the information provided in the full text aligned with the information required by the clinician to make a decision (Examples of non-aligned content are provided in supplement C). All data were entered using Microsoft Excel 2021.

Bias

Bias may be introduced using purposive sampling. We attempted to address preference bias through selection of physicians with no prior experience with LLMs (as these still probably represent much of the mid-level physician population) and training them in LLM use, in which they had interest. To examine the possibility of dissatisfaction with the LLM report stemming from a hesitation to adopt a new technology, physician resistance to change was assessed. Outcome bias was addressed through blinding of the secondary outcome assessment process.

Sample size

In this study, 20 cases (human reports) were paired with three comparators (different LLM reports). To determine the minimal detectable difference allowed by our sample size, we performed calculations based on matched pairs (1:1 ratio). Based on the assumption that differences in report quality followed a normal distribution with a broad standard deviation of 1 (based on Likert scale assessments), we calculated that the study would be able to detect a true difference in the mean satisfaction from paired reports of ± 0.584. With a narrower standard deviation of 0.5, the study would be able to detect a true difference in satisfaction from matched reports of ± 0.292. Both calculations were conducted with a power of 0.8 and a Type I error probability of 0.05 for a two-sided test of the null hypothesis that there would be no difference in physician satisfaction. A 1:3 ratio was used to increase the study power.

Handling of quantitative variables and statistical analyses

Missingness is reported. In the first stage, descriptive statistics were used for case and participant characteristics and for the resistance to change questionnaire. Categorical data (e.g., sex, department) are reported as numbers and proportions (%). Continuous variables (e.g., the main outcome measure of physician satisfaction) are reported as mean ± SD and median (Interquartile Range [IQR]). Clinician satisfaction was evaluated according to accepted practice16.

For the main outcome measure (satisfaction), a paired ordinal variable, the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used for comparisons. The correlation between physicians’ mean satisfaction score and their resistance to change and the correlation between satisfaction scores and objective quality metrics (e.g. impact factors, quartiles) were evaluated using the Spearman correlation coefficient.

For the secondary outcome measures with nominal variables (e.g. journal quartile), comparisons were performed between the cases (human reports) and controls (LLMs reports) using the Chi-square or the a-parametric Fisher exact test. Comparisons between cases and controls in secondary outcome measures with continuous variables (e.g. journal impact factor) were performed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test.

The proportions of human and LLM reports containing hallucinations, unfaithful and irrelevant citations are presented. The proportion of faithful citations was determined from citations that were not hallucinated, while the proportion of relevant citations was calculated from citations classified as faithful.

For post-hoc subgroup comparisons (i.e., each LLM alone vs. the human report) the a-parametric Wilcoxon matched pairs signed-rank test was used (e.g. for comparing citation hallucinations, faithfulness). All analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.4.1), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two-sided tests).

Results

Participants

Overall, 27 queries were selected by the physicians for their complexity and submitted for inclusion. Five of the queries were unrelated to a specific case and two were related to diagnoses rather than treatment and were therefore excluded. Four reports generated by Gemini were not captured due to a technical malfunction. The study therefore included twenty human reports and 56 LLM generated reports (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0, and Claude Sonnet 3.5).

The participating physicians were aged 37 years (median, 35–41 years old). Their resistance to change scores ranged between 33 and 55. The cases included patients with an age ranging between 8 weeks to 40 years. Most of the cases were female patients. The main diagnoses of the patients are presented in Supplement D. 20% of the cases were assessed as complex, 60% were classified as medium complexity, and 20% as simple. The median number of iterations clinicians used before truncating attempts to achieve a report with the LLMs was 3 (IQR [2.00, 9.00]). Overall, 456 citations were provided, 265 for human reports, and 191 for LLM reports.

Main outcome - physician satisfaction (Fig. 1)

Comparison of LLM reports to human reports: Physician satisfaction.

Cronbach alpha testing of the questionnaires yielded a value of 0.86. Human reports met physicians’ expectations more frequently than LLMs (75% vs. 48% respectively, p = 0.044), were assessed as being more reliable (85% vs. 50%, p = 0.032), as having better professional writing (85% vs. 57%, p = 0.003), and were assessed having saved the physicians more time (70% vs. 21.3%, p = 0.003). However, human reports were also assessed as being too lengthy (55% vs. 22%, p < 0.001). In terms of information novelty, report clarity, and relevance to the patient, human reports were assessed as equal to LLM reports.

A negative correlation was identified between physician satisfaction with LLM reports and resistance to change (Spearman’s R = -0.966, p = 0.034, 95% CI: [-1.00, -0.07]). No such correlation was identified with human reports. No significant correlation was found between physician satisfaction and objective report metrics (e.g., the impact factor of the cited sources).

Discussion

This study assessed physician satisfaction with reports generated by LLMs versus reports written by human researchers in response to complex real-world medical queries. Contrary to our hypothesis, the subjective evaluations of physicians favored human reports across most parameters. Lower physician satisfaction with LLM-generated reports correlated with resistance to change. Human reports were superior to LLM reports in objective parameters (hallucinations, faithfulness and relevance) despite their reliance on literature sources with lower rankings (impact factor and quartile). Human reports also outperformed standalone LLM reports in professional writing, readability, and time-saving, though both demonstrated similar levels of information novelty. Contrary to our expectations, physician satisfaction seemed unrelated to objective report quality.

Natural language processing (NLP) tools have been effectively integrated as clinical decision support tools. However, more advanced language models remain underutilized for direction of medical treatment due to concerns regarding their reliability17. Research on LLMs has focused primarily on diagnostic decision-making18–21, with limited evaluation of their role in the management of treatment. With the rise of multimorbidity and chronic illness22,23, treatment decisions are becoming more complex and nuanced24. One study has suggested that LLMs may improve physician reasoning in such circumstances25. However, another study evaluating the responses of ChatGPT and physicians to complex exam cases presented to primary care physicians in Sweden, graded the responses provided by the physician as superior26. There is also little data evaluating whether LLMs can manage real-world cases27. A study of GPT-4 treatment recommendations in the emergency department found that LLMs tended towards over-intervention, potentially straining resources and exposing patients to unnecessary care18.

This study assessed LLM reports to complex clinical queries derived from daily medical practice in a free-text format, directly comparing them to human reports. This methodology offers an authentic assessment of the applicability of LLM use in real-world clinical settings. In a letter to JAMA, Cabral et al. examined clinical reasoning using GPT-4 and highlighted the need for evaluations incorporating real-life medical scenarios27. Our study also adds information on Claude and Gemini, which have previously been less studied28–30. Furthermore, incorporation of both physician satisfaction ratings and objective performance measures covers multiple aspects of assessment.

A preference for human-generated responses rather than LLM responses has been demonstrated in previous studies. Singhal et al. showed that physicians preferred answers provided by other physicians over those generated by MED-PaLM2, a specialized medical LLM3. A study focused on diabetes that compared GPT-4 to endocrinologists found that while GPT-4 responses were reasonable in scenarios without clear right answers, they differed significantly from clinicians’ choices, particularly in metformin prescription patterns31. The physicians in the current study also largely favored human-generated reports. However, objective quality assessments indicated these reports relied on lower-tier literature sources. This seeming contradiction may be attributed to citation of case reports and series which were more like the cases presented by the physicians. Human researchers chose to address the complexity of these real-world cases through literature that is less focused on the disease and more focused on the individual which seems to align with physician preferences.

The absence of correlation between satisfaction ratings and objective quality measures raises important questions regarding the use of satisfaction measures looking forward. Ohde et al. highlighted this challenge and the associated burden for healthcare providers in their NEJM AI perspective32. The findings of this study further reinforce this concern, highlighting the need for a different evaluation process.

Our study, like prior studies, identified LLM hallucinations and unfaithful citations33,34. Emerging technologies, such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)35, seem to offer promising solutions to these issues. RAG enables LLMs to access and verify information from external, curated data sources, potentially reducing hallucinations and improving citation accuracy17,36. LLMs seem to prioritize meta-analyses and guidelines as sources, probably because these tend to be more often cited in the general literature and are often accessible in full text. Use of RAG could improve source selection by allowing for a more comprehensive approach based on query specificity. In practice, RAG can strengthen trust by grounding answers in reliable, up-to-date sources that are directly relevant to the user’s query. When paired with structured prompting, these benefits may be further enhanced, as prompting can guide the model to cite sources more explicitly and explain how they support its output. RAG augmented LLMs still require monitoring for potential biases in external knowledge sources, possible privacy infringement37, and source validation38. As LLM capabilities continue to develop, future studies should explicitly evaluate how features such as RAG, advanced reasoning38 and differences in writing style and flow39,40,41 influence output accuracy, physician workflow, and overall trust in these tools.

Strengths and limitations

The prospective design of this study, the comparative evaluation of several LLMs in real-world cases, and analysis of relationships between subjective and objective metrics are the strengths of this study. Medical dilemmas from diverse specialties were assessed to provide a true picture of a diverse clinical environment.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, physician satisfaction may have been influenced by the difference in report turnaround times: LLM-generated reports were available immediately, whereas human researcher reports could take up to 48 h. This discrepancy could have biased perceptions of time-saving and reliability in favor of the LLM, as faster availability might have been conflated with higher efficiency or dependability. Interestingly, in this study, human-generated reports were nevertheless rated as more time-saving, suggesting that factors beyond turnaround time contributed to these perceptions. Still, this discrepancy is important to highlight.

An additional important limitation to address is the inherent asymmetry in comparing LLM-generated responses to those written by human researchers. For example, the term “hallucination” is specific to LLMs and typically refers to the generation of information or citations that do not exist. In contrast, when similar inaccuracies appear in human-generated reports, they are more likely the result of misinterpretation, oversight, or - when based on faulty source material - fabrication, rather than the spontaneous generation of false information. This difference highlights a conceptual non-equivalence in the behaviors being measured, which makes direct comparisons inherently limited. Moreover, despite blinding, distinctive stylistic features of LLM output (e.g., consistent grammar, lack of typographical errors, formulaic structure) could have allowed the independent reviewer to infer report origin, introducing a potential source of bias. This observation points to a broader methodological challenge that extends beyond this study and highlights the need for the medical community to define standardized metrics for evaluating and comparing information generated by artificial and human intelligence alike. Moreover, physician satisfaction may be confounded by limited prompt-engineering skills. Educated prompts might improve LLM output but no standard training for such exists, and most clinicians lack this expertise.

Conclusion

In complex medical queries where no single simple correct answer exists, LLMs underperformed under the study’s conditions in both physicians’ subjective assessments and objective measures. Although LLMs efficiently accessed high-impact literature and guidelines, their source verification and citation accuracy remained limited. Emerging features such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and advanced reasoning may help address some of these shortcomings, though their real-world impact on accuracy, workflow, and clinician trust requires further study. Furthermore, the findings of this study suggest that the current regulatory model of keeping a human in the loop when using AI may be a fig-leaf at best. At worst, clinical implementation of LLM-generated output could become a new and lucrative source of liability suits against physicians if deployed without adequate safeguards and validation.
