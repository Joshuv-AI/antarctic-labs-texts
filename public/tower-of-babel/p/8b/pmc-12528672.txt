Use of deep learning and self-supervised methods to accelerate and improve the earth subsurface characterization
Authors: Vanessa Simoes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe propose a self-supervised deep learning methodology based on a one-dimensional adaptation of the Convolutional Visual Transformer (CVT) model for characterizing the Earth’s subsurface using well log data. Our foundation model is pre-trained on unlabeled multivariate sensor data across diverse basins and then fine-tuned for geological formation identification in the Williston Basin. The model achieves an average F1 score of 0.94 across six key formations, demonstrating faster convergence, increased robustness to missing inputs, and improved accuracy compared to baseline models, including U-Net, XGBoost, SVM, and KNN. We validate the model in geologically distinct basins, including the Groningen gas field, confirming its generalization to other fields. The framework applies to hydrocarbon exploration, carbon storage, geothermal, and groundwater reservoir characterization, supporting scalable and transferable AI solutions for the energy transition.Keywords: Transfer learning, Subsurface characterization, Sensor dataSubject terms: Carbon capture and storage, Fossil fuels, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

We explore the use of pre-trained models aligned with the concept of foundational models1 for wellbore log-related applications. We adopt self-supervised methods and unlabeled data (directly measured data) to train a deep learning model. The first intent when building the model is to reconstruct multiple wellbore log data with improved quality and high similarity with real acquired logs, and the second intent is to adapt the model for numerous downstream applications or regions (with different geological properties).

In this work, we propose Model FT, a deep learning model trained using self-supervised methodology and adaptable for multiple tasks, including regression and classification. This method aligns with the concept of foundation model, which was coined by researchers at Stanford University’s Center for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM) and the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI), as seen in1, and describes general-purpose large models trained on vast datasets and adaptable to various downstream tasks. This concept was initially proposed in the context of natural language and is being increasingly used in other sciences (see2).

The deep learning architecture used is an adaptation of the CVT model with the following transformed and normalized input and output wellbore log types: formation density (RHOB -), gamma ray (GR -gAPI), deep resistivity (RESD - ohm.m), neutron porosity (NEUT - v/v), photoelectric factor (PEF - b/e), spontaneous potential (SP - mV), compressional slowness (DTC - ), vertical depth (DEPT - ft), caliper (CALI - in), latitude (LAT - rad), and longitude (Long - rad) log types.

The following curves: RHOB, GR, RESD, NEUT, PEF, CALI, SP, and DTC represent distinct physical measurements acquired in situ by wireline tools or while drilling. These tools read the surrounding properties of the geological layers crossed by the wellbore. Those measurements are predominantly sensitive to specific formation properties, as seen in3. For example, the density measurement decreases in the presence of lower-density components, such as kerogen, or high-porosity formations, and increases in tighter formations or heavy minerals. Resistivity decreases in the presence of conductive materials, such as saltwater (brine) and shale. The caliper is a purely physical measurement acquired by arms that measures the diameter of the well in specific directions. It is mainly associated with the bit size used to drill the well and deviates from the bit size due to breakouts and washouts, for example.

We also use the location properties DEPT, LAT, and LONG as part of the input set. Ideally, those quantities should reflect the location of each point of the wellbore. If the well trajectory information is missing for a certain well, the well is assumed to be vertical, and the measured depth is considered to be the vertical depth. The latitude and longitude are based on the well’s top location.

Modeling these measurements presents several challenges, including varying sensitivity to different formation properties, tools with varying depths of investigation, tool failures at specific intervals, the influence of borehole fluid, missing logs, and inconsistencies between measurements.

To address some of those challenges, we built a base pre-trained deep learning model capable of handling input samples with varying levels of noise and missing inputs. The input samples are obtained from the original logs, which have been subjected to multiple distortions aligned with common data issues. The denoising autoencoder model reconstructs those logs with improved overall quality. When doing so, the model is exposed to various data challenges commonly found in the wellbore log data, and it learns multiple partial inter-dependencies between the log types and their sequential dependencies.

The second objective of the modeling is to fine-tune the pre-trained model for multiple downstream applications beyond log reconstruction. In this study, we focus on a segmentation task to identify the following formation types: Lodgepole (LP), Upper Bakken Shale (UBS), Middle Bakken (MB), Lower Bakken Shale Member (LBS), Upper Three Forks (UTF), and Lower Three Forks (LTF), as interpreted by domain experts. We used a subset of the input logs, which are widely available in the Williston Basin, and selected the formation types that are economically important for their large hydrocarbon production.

This objective aligns with the primary goal of this study, which is to test the hypothesis that the pre-trained model enhances training time and inference quality for downstream tasks not included in the original log set. The advantages of using a pre-trained model, including improved training time and increased accuracy, make it a key component in an automated workflow. The geological formation identification represents a good test because it goes beyond a single depth classification, as evidenced by the lack of continuity using traditional single-depth approaches, as exemplified in the Supplemental Material.

A typical scenario when performing log-related applications is the presence of missing log types for a well or interval; it is therefore important to understand how a method performs in this scenario. For additional comparison, we estimated the impact of missing logs on the final model’s performance. We concluded that Model FT is less impacted by missing logs compared to Model 0 (a model starting from random initialization), as exemplified in the Supplemental Material, confirming that the pre-trained step helps the model learn the interdependencies of the logs.

The third objective is to evaluate and compare the models using the validation and test wells. To compare the models and test our hypothesis, we use the F1 score per formation type and per well, along with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. We observe that the pre-trained model significantly improved the formation identification.

We reviewed the problem in the literature and noticed that most facies classification predictions are more influenced by lithology content than by age; therefore, the classification considered might be more related to a depth-by-depth analysis, see4–7 and8, and as a result, most of the facies classification solutions at a wellbore scale use single depth samples for both the features and the labels. To compare the proposed model with other ML methods, we selected XGBoost (see9), SVM (see10), and KNN (see11) as strong candidates. Additionally, a U-Net (see12) with the same training strategy as this work is used to compare the proposed architecture with an alternative method using a window-based approach. The CVT-based model achieves a higher F1 score for each of the formation types than all other methods (as indicated in the Supplemental Material) using the same training, validation, and test wells.

Methods

Our methodology involves pre-training a model using self-supervised methods and analytical functions derived from the following input logs: GR, RESD, RHOB, NEUT, PEF, DEPT, CALI, LAT, and LONG.

The pre-trained model is developed using unlabeled measured logs from public wells from the following datasets:

To improve data consistency, we harmonize the wellbore logs from these public datasets by identifying the best curves for each log type of interest and verifying and correcting the units to ensure they are appropriate.

In each well, the strategy described in Chapter Three of18 guided the filtering applied to the dataset. The first filter identifies out-of-limits values for each log type considered in the modeling. Specifically, it filters out values that are larger than a predefined maximum value or lower than a predefined minimum value from the dataset. The minimum and maximum values used are set to avoid filtering real data. In the Williston Basin, we used a maximum GR value of 900 gAPI to avoid deleting extremely high values in the Upper and Lower Bakken formations.

The second filter identifies intervals that have been synthetically extrapolated by a linear curve. This extrapolation frequently happens at the upper and lower intervals where data were logged and on the salt interval where some measurements are not acquired (example in the Supplemental Material).

This work analyses the prediction of the target curves in the test wells, but the goal is to create a model to be applied for the entire set of wells, and we cannot rely on manual cleaning or time-consuming processing for large datasets; therefore, the filters are applied automatically for all wells in the basin.

After filtering, we apply linear and non-linear transformations to improve numerical stability and better align the curves with the properties of interest. The non-linear transformations are the logarithm application to the curves RESD and DEPT to derive logRes and logDEPT, and the second derivative of CALI to extract the local variation of caliper (CALLDIFF), which highlights variations due to washouts and breakouts, and reduces dependence on bit size.

We selected this transformation over the minimum and maximum values because it is less sensitive to outliers, which are common in wellbore logs. For example, the GR log in the Williston Basin had values larger than 600 gAPI in the Bakken shale and approached 0 in the salt layers.

To build the pre-trained model, we focus on intervals and wells where at least 60% of the logs are available; this threshold appears to be a good balance between maintaining model convergence and considering enough wells from the different datasets.

The primary objective of the self-supervised methodology is to train the model to learn log cross-dependencies and sequence dependencies by modifying the model input, such as masking or altering the data. The second example is data alterations, which is associated with the method used by20 to improve log consistency by considering multiple input data simultaneously rather than performing shifts and gains on one curve at a time.

The first two tasks are aligned with understanding the log cross-dependencies, which is a desired property in the pre-trained model. The third example, missing interval tasks, is associated with sequence dependency and resembles the alterations used in natural language processing. Additionally, we incorporate other sources of noise and sequentially combine multiple alterations to enhance the variety during model training.

The model architecture is based on the CVT model in21, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This is a three-stage deep learning neural network method that incorporates convolutional layers into the vision transformer approach. In this architecture, the main parameters that we varied when building the pre-trained model are the depths which indicates the number of layers in each encoder block, the number of heads which represents the number of attention heads for each attention layer in each block of the Transformer encoder, and the dropout ratio which defines the dropout ratio for the patch embeddings probabilities.

We explored multiple combinations of hyperparameters, and based on these experiments, we selected the combination that yielded the smallest loss in the validation set during the self-supervised method, along with a small difference between the training and validation losses. The chosen architecture has an input and output length of 256 samples, three stages with the first stage with one head and depth equal to two, the second stage with three heads and depth equal to two, and the third stage with ten heads and depth equal to ten, we selected the dropout ratio equal to 0.1, and we kept the other values as presented in the original paper.

This architecture yielded promising results in the self-supervised stage and the proposed downstream application. The model is implemented using PyTorch, an optimized tensor library for deep learning in Python.

The pre-trained model can be tuned for various tasks, and in this work, we will focus on predicting the formation type. The adaptation of the model, as shown in Fig. 2, is performed using the base model as an initial state, freezing part of the model, and adding new heads (or layers of neural networks) for the downstream task. In the case presented, we have a new head to predict the six formation types simultaneously in segmentation tasks.

Scheme of CVT-based method (adapted from21) fine-tuned to the new tasks of formation type prediction. Example of model input and output samples for the downstream task of multiclass classification.

To train the classification, we organized a set of training and validation samples. These samples contain intervals from the training and validation wells. The input is the available subset of GR, RESD, RHOB, NEUT, PEF, DEPT, CALI, LAT, and LONG logs. The outputs are the formation types.

For training both the pre-trained and the Model FT, we use separate training and validation wells to ensure the resulting model generalizes to other wells in the reservoir and that it is not simply memorizing samples. We use AdamW optimizer, a stochastic gradient descent method based on adaptive estimation of first-order and second-order moments, with an added method for weight decay22.

To evaluate and compare the classification models, we provide the MSE (mean squared error) and the BCE (binary cross-entropy) for the samples from the validation set. We also apply the classification models to fifteen training wells, where domain experts have labeled the formation types. Based on these interpreted curves, we provide the accuracy, precision, and recall, as well as the average accuracy, precision, and recall for each of the six formation types.

For the final evaluation of the classification model, we use a test set formed by wells that were not seen during the training and validation processes. And for each class of interest, we treat the problem as a binary classification. We calculate accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score (see Eq. 2), for each well in the test set, as well as the aggregate metrics for the entire test set. Among the various metrics, we prioritize the F1 score when comparing the models, as it is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, making it more relevant for imbalanced datasets and sensitive to both false positives and false negatives. If the domain expert defines the weight for each type of error or identifies which metrics are more relevant, other metrics or averages between precision and recall can be used as a final metric.

We test for a statistically significant difference between the paired samples using the Wilcoxon test, a non-parametric test that does not require the assumption of a standard distribution. We classify the samples based on the difference between the F1 scores of Model 0 and Model FT, then rank them by their absolute values and calculate the sum of positive and negative ranks.

We use a one-tailed Wilcoxon test, where the null hypothesis is that the performance of Model FT is equal to or smaller than that of Model 0; this translates to the sum of the positive ranks being equal to or smaller than the sum of the positive ranks. The alternative hypothesis is that Model FT outperforms Model 0, which translates to the sum of the positive ranks being significantly larger than the sum of the negative ranks.

To obtain the p-value, we calculate Z according to . In a given set of paired n samples without ties and zeros, is equal to the sum of positive ranks, , and is the expected standard deviation of the sum of positive values. With the obtained Z, it is possible to calculate the p-value using the standard normal distribution and test if the null hypothesis can be rejected.

Field Applications and Results

To test the hypothesis that the pre-trained deep learning model contributes positively to solving downstream tasks, we use a segmentation task based on identifying formation types in the Bakken formation at Williston Basin in North Dakota. The formation types are from the most recent (shallower) to the oldest (deeper): Lodgepole (Seal), Upper Bakken Shale, Middle Bakken, Lower Bakken Shale Member, Upper Three Forks, and Lower Three Forks. The definition of formation is aligned with the definitions of23, and those formations are key components in the petroleum system, see24.

The overlying Lodgepole Formation and younger strata provide the necessary burial and thermal maturation for hydrocarbon generation in the Bakken source rocks25.

The Upper and Lower Bakken shales are the primary source rocks in this system, functioning as seals due to their low permeability, which traps hydrocarbons within and adjacent to them (26;23). They are organic-rich, containing Type II kerogen, which is highly prone to generating oil upon thermal maturation (25,27,28; and26). The reservoir facies within the Bakken and Three Forks formations are diverse, reflecting different depositional environments.

Input preparation

We perform log harmonization on the input logs to obtain spliced curves for the model input information: (GR (gAPI), Deep Resistivity (ohm.m), RHOB (), NEUT (v/v), PEF (b/e), DEPT (ft), CALIPER (in), LATITUDE (rad), and LONGITUDE (rad)) for both training, validation and test sets. In this dataset, DTC and SP were not broadly available and were not present in the training set, and we treated these curves as missing inputs.

Input samples and label preparation

We used three labeled wells, which had a total of 38,510 ft of labeled intervals and contained the six formation types, as the training set. In total, we obtained 2,318 ft of Lodgepole formation, 60 ft of Upper Bakken Shale formation, 120 ft of Mid Bakken Shale formation, 85 ft of Lower Bakken Shale formation, 265 ft of Upper Three Forks formation, and 372 ft of Lower Three Forks formation. Most of the intervals were not associated with any formation of interest for this study. They were included in the experiment to provide a classification model that would apply to entire wells in this reservoir with low amounts of false positives.

The class imbalance is exemplified by the training set, where the Upper Bakken Shale has a total thickness of 60 ft and the Lower Bakken Shale has 85 ft, representing uncommon formation types that account for less than 0.5% of the total thickness (38,510 ft). Lodgepole is the most common formation type considered in this task, with a total thickness of 2,318.

When preparing the training and validation samples, we use the transformed input logs, as described in 2, aligned with the formation types. We randomly select intervals of 256 depth reference (127.5 ft). In this dataset, we selected 7000 random intervals from the training wells and 2100 samples from the validation wells.

To ensure that all formation types are represented in both the training and validation sets, we randomly substitute 983 samples from the training set and 700 from the validation set with intervals containing each of the formation types of interest. This step addresses segmentation issues with uncommon classes, improves classification metrics for classes with lower availability, and enhances performance stability across different runs.

Compared to Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), a traditional oversampling technique that has been successfully applied to subsurface data, see29 and8, our technique has the advantage that the alterations are performed in the entire training set, and those alterations have been inspired in common log problems to prepare the model to work well in presence of input data quality issues (e. g. missing input logs and intensity shift). We have tested the impact of this approach by deactivating this step and concluded that the number of true positives increased for the minority classes and remained similar to the dominant classes.

Model comparison

We load the pretrained CVT method, which contains 55 million parameters, and add new layers (heads) associated with the multi-output problem in this scenario. We freeze the first layers in the Model FT, leaving 15 million parameters free to be optimized for new tasks and regions using AdamW.

For a fair comparison, Model 0 contains the same CVT model architecture, and it is trained with the same optimizer.

We applied both Model 0 and Model FT to a set of 15 blind test wells within the same reservoir as the training and validation wells. We then obtained the predicted formation types and compared these predictions with the labels provided by an expert.

Conclusion and future development

We developed a pre-trained model using unlabeled data for multivariate one-dimensional wellbore measurements to perform self-supervised tasks, such as predicting missing logs and missing intervals. This pre-trained model can be used to improve and accelerate convergence when applying deep learning methods to downstream applications, leveraging the concepts of foundational models and transfer learning.

We focus on the task of geological formation type identification in the Williston Basin and use the Wilcoxon test to conclude that Model FT (using weights from the pre-trained model) outperforms Model 0 (a model trained with a similar strategy and architecture but starting with random weights). We confirmed this improvement in the Groningen field in the Netherlands.

Predicting geological formations is a challenging and time-consuming task that can be applied to hydrocarbon exploration, carbon storage, and the characterization of groundwater and geothermal reservoirs. A method for accelerating this interpretation is highly valuable in multiple applications.

Model FT exhibited faster convergence, an improved F1 score, and greater stability in the presence of missing logs. These enhancements make the Model FT a valuable component in workflows that consider user interaction to improve final accuracy for formation-type predictions in heterogeneous, complex scenarios.

For future work, we aim to further enhance feature extraction from the pre-trained model to accelerate model convergence during the fine-tuning stage and improve model performance. We also aim to improve the model uncertainty estimation and provide a quality indicator curve alongside the geological formation predictions. We view model uncertainty as a valuable indicator for identifying wells or geographical locations where the model requires more labels or additional information to improve its predictions.

One limitation is that the model requires a high-quality training and validation set of labeled classes, which serve as examples from which the model learns; obtaining these classes is time-consuming for the domain expert. Further reducing the required number of examples would add value to the application.

One risk of using pre-trained models with frozen layers is that the layers might filter out useful information for the downstream tasks. In this case, the model might overcorrect or delete important features, resulting in worse results than starting from random initialization. To reduce this risk, we add multiple self-supervised tasks that vary in nature and complexity, and we expose the pre-trained model to diverse mineral, porosity, and fluid types.
