Impact of 2018 allocation system change on heart transplantation outcomes in china: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Zhiyuan Zhu, Shanshan Zheng, Jie Huang, Zhongkai Liao, Xianqiang Wang, Wei Feng, Zhe Zheng, Sheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn 2018, China implemented a nationally standardized urgency-based heart allocation system, replacing the previous region-based prioritization approach. However, its impact on waitlist dynamics and post-transplant outcomes remains unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed heart transplant recipients treated at Fuwai Hospital between 2015 and 2022. The data were obtained from the China Heart Transplant Registry (CHTR). A total of 731 consecutive recipients were stratified into prereform (Era 1) and post-reform (Era 2) cohorts. Era 2 recipients were further classified as urgent or nonurgent. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression identified risk factors for 1-year mortality.ResultsAfter implementation, urgent status transplants increased from 25.7% (2019) to 36.5% (2022), while the waitlist-to-transplant ratio declined from 1.41 to 1.10. Despite COVID-19-related disruptions that peaked waitlist mortality at 15.2% in 2021, urgent recipients had significantly shorter waiting times than nonurgent patients (15 vs. 89 days, P < 0.001). Notably, 1-year survival was similar between eras (92.15% vs. 94.57%, P = 0.181) and urgency groups (93.1% vs. 95.2%, P = 0.324), despite urgent recipients presenting with greater preoperative acuity (71.6% vs. 11.1% IABP dependence), more postoperative complications (74.1% vs. 21.8% IABP support), and longer ICU stays (6 vs. 6 days, P = 0.040). Multivariate analysis identified preoperative dialysis (aHR = 5.67, P = 0.001), elevated bilirubin (aHR = 1.01, P = 0.007), and prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time (aHR = 1.01/hour, P < 0.001) as predictors of mortality.ConclusionsOur findings provide preliminary evidence that China’s urgency-based allocation system prioritizes critically ill recipients while maintaining comparable 1-year survival. These findings provide initial validation of the policy’s clinical effectiveness in balancing equity and survival benefits. However, larger and more comprehensive datasets are needed for further confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03314-8.

Background

Heart transplantation (HTx) remains the definitive therapy for end-stage heart failure [1]. Despite increasing waitlist volumes, persistent donor shortages necessitate an equitable and efficient allocation system [2–6]. In response, major transplant programs, including those in the United States and United Kingdom, adopted revised urgency-based allocation frameworks in 2018 to prioritize critically ill candidates [2, 3].

International evidence indicates important clinical trade-offs associated with urgency-based allocation. These systems increase transplantation rates and reduce waiting times but may limit opportunities for weaning from mechanical circulatory support (MCS) because of earlier transplantation [7]. Although urgent status recipients show reduced waitlist mortality, they experience higher perioperative complication rates, which may offset early survival benefits [8]. Notably, long-term survival remains comparable between urgency status [9], raising critical questions about whether reduced waitlist mortality compromises longitudinal outcomes.

In 2018, China introduced a nationwide acuity-based heart allocation policy, stratifying candidates as urgent (requiring MCS, ventilatory support, or qualifying pediatric indications) or nonurgent status. This framework replaced regional prioritization by granting national allocation priority to urgent status candidates, while nonurgent candidates follow sequential geographic tiers (transplant center, provincial, adjacent provincial, and national levels). The system also incorporates age-matching priorities, graded ABO compatibility protocols, and weighted waiting-time calculations.

Six years after its implementation, the clinical impact of China’s heart allocation system has not been systematically evaluated. Here, we present an analysis from a high-volume transplant center to: (1) compare outcomes between the pre-reform (2015–2018) and post-reform (2019–2022) eras, and (2) assess survival differences between urgent and non-urgent recipients under the current allocation framework.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Recipient data were prospectively recorded in the China Heart Transplant Registry (CHTR), which contains comprehensive inpatient records for all transplant recipients, as well as limited donor information. Waiting list data were obtained from the China Organ Transplant Response System (COTRS), a centralized platform jointly established by the Ministry of Health and the Red Cross Society of China to manage heart allocation nationwide. Comprehensive waiting list data have been available only since COTRS became fully operational in 2019. For this retrospective analysis, data extracted up to December 31, 2023, included heart transplant recipients from two eras: Era 1 (January 1, 2015-December 31, 2018) and Era 2 (January 1, 2019-December 31, 2022). Recipient and donor data were obtained from the CHTR and previous medical records, whereas waiting time data were extracted from the COTRS.

For all recipients, demographic data (age, weight, diabetes), surgical variables [donor ischemic time, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) duration, and aortic cross-clamp (ACC) duration], perioperative outcomes [postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support, mortality, mechanical ventilation (MV) duration], intensive care unit (ICU) stay, total postoperative hospitalization, endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) findings, and follow-up information were collected from institutional medical records and registries.

Population and study design

This retrospective, single-center study was performed at Fuwai Hospital, the National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases in China. All consecutive patients who underwent HTx at our center between 2015 and 2022 were included as part of this real-world analysis. Recipients were stratified by urgency status (urgent vs. nonurgent) as documented at the time of transplant registration. Preoperative demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between Era 1 and Era 2 cohorts, and between urgent and nonurgent groups within Era 2, using data available at the time of transplantation. The primary endpoint was 1-year post-transplant mortality, and secondary endpoints included in-hospital outcomes.

All grafts were voluntarily donated and procured from brain-dead donors. The study protocol was approved by the Fuwai Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2023-1992), and written informed consent was obtained from all recipients.

Urgency status definition

The highest medical urgency level for heart transplant candidates is urgent status. The definition for urgent status is provided in Additional file 1.

Donor-recipient match definition

Donor-recipient matching was defined as follows: donors aged ≥ 55 years were classified as Aged donors, whereas those younger than 55 years were considered normal donors [10]. For weight matching, a donor-recipient weight difference exceeding ± 30% was considered mismatched, whereas differences within ± 30% were deemed matched [11]. For sex matching, donor-recipient pairs of the same sex were considered matched, whereas pairs of different sexes were considered mismatched. For blood type matching, donor-recipient pairs with identical ABO blood groups were classified as matched, whereas those with different ABO types were considered mismatched.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Stata (www.stata.com). Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables are summarized as frequencies with percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with normal distribution were compared using Student's t test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Waitlist mortality was analyzed using a competing-risk model, with death as the primary event and other outcomes(e.g. HTx) as a competing event. Cumulative incidence rates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify risk factors for 1-year mortality, with variables showing P < 0.1 in univariate analysis included in the multivariate model. The proportional hazards assumption was checked using Schoenfeld residuals. The results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and statistical significance was defined as P ≤ 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Study population and baseline demographics

The volume of heart transplantation in China showed a steady increase over the study period (Fig. 1). Of the 731 recipients enrolled, 344 underwent HTx in the pre-reform era (Era 1: 2015–2018) and 387 in the post-reform era (Era 2: 2019–2022). Under the revised allocation policy, 116 recipients (29.9%) in Era 2 were classified as urgent status and 271 (70.1%) as nonurgent status. Urgent status recipients (n = 116) were predominantly male (81.9% vs. 72.3%, P = 0.046) and had higher pre-HTx ALT (26 vs. 21 μmol/L, P = 0.010) and N-terminal pro-B-type Natriuretic Peptide(NT-proBNP) levels (3059 vs. 2159 pg/mL, P = 0.002), but lower PRA positivity (10.3% vs. 23.2%, P = 0.003). The underlying etiologies differed significantly between groups (P = 0.037). Urgent recipients required substantially more mechanical circulatory support, including IABP (71.6% vs. 11.1%, P < 0.001), ECMO (6.9% vs. 0.4%, P < 0.001), and LVAD (4.3% vs. 0%, P < 0.001). They received younger donor hearts (39 vs. 42 years, P = 0.013), fewer older donors (1.7% vs. 6.6%, P = 0.045), and experienced longer ischemic times (3.83 vs. 3.50 h, P = 0.036). Urgent status recipients also had a higher proportion of type O donors and fewer type A, B, and AB donors (P < 0.001), together with a higher rate of ABO mismatches (28.4% vs. 10.7%, P < 0.001).

Baseline data on pediatric recipients are presented in Additional File 2. Although the small sample size precludes definitive conclusions, these data provide valuable insight into the pediatric transplant population.

Postoperative characteristics and in-hospital outcomes

Era 2 recipients had significantly higher rates of postoperative IABP support (37.5% vs. 17.2%, P < 0.001; Table 2) and secondary thoracotomy (9.6% vs. 5.5%, P = 0.041). Era 1 recipients required longer mechanical ventilation (29 vs. 22 h, P = 0.018) but had shorter ICU stays (4 vs. 6 days, P < 0.001) and overall hospitalization (15 vs. 19 days, P < 0.001). urgent status recipients required markedly more postoperative IABP support (74.1% vs. 21.8%, P < 0.001) and were more likely to receive continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT; 7.8% vs. 2.2%, P = 0.018). They also had higher rates of secondary thoracotomy (14.7% vs. 7.4%, P = 0.026), longer mechanical ventilation (25 vs. 21 h, P = 0.018), and longer ICU stays (median 6 vs. 6 days, P = 0.040). Mortality in the first year after transplantation

The overall 1-year survival rates were 92.15% in Era 1 and 94.57% in Era 2, with no statistically significant difference between the two eras (P = 0.181; Fig. 3A). Comparable outcomes were also observed among adult recipients (P = 0.079; Fig. 3B) and pediatric recipients (P = 0.243; Fig. 3C). Similarly, no survival difference was detected among recipients requiring pretransplant ECMO support (P = 0.306; Fig. 3D). In contrast, recipients with pretransplant IABP support had significantly better survival in Era 2 than in Era 1 (P < 0.001; Fig. 3E). Among Era 2 recipients, urgent status patients achieved a 1-year survival rate of 93.1%, comparable to 95.2% in nonurgent patients (P = 0.324; Fig. 3F). The leading causes of 1-year mortality were infection (18.4%) and graft failure (18.4%), together accounting for 36.7% of deaths (Fig. 4). Risk factors associated with first year mortality in HTx recipients

Multivariate analysis identified several independent predictors of 1-year mortality. Elevated preoperative total bilirubin (adjusted HR 1.01; 95% CI 1.00–1.03; P = 0.007) and prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time (adjusted HR 1.01 per hour; 95% CI 1.01–1.02; P < 0.001) were significantly associated with higher mortality risk (Table 3). Preoperative dialysis was strongly associated with increased mortality (adjusted HR 5.67; 95% CI 2.00–16.07; P = 0.001). Preoperative life support showed a trend toward increased mortality, although this did not reach statistical significance (adjusted HR 1.78; 95% CI 0.91–3.50; P = 0.093). No significant associations were found between 1-year mortality and preoperative creatinine, left ventricular ejection fraction, donor ischemic time, or aortic cross-clamp duration. Schoenfeld residual testing (global P = 0.6896; all individual variables P > 0.05) and time-dependent coefficient plots confirmed no violation of the proportional hazards assumption for any covariates in the Cox model (Additional File 4). Discussion

The burden of advanced heart failure in developing countries is compounded by large populations and severe donor shortages. Thus, a simple, efficient, and equitable heart allocation system based on objective evidence is urgently needed. In 2018, China revised its heart allocation policy, replacing the previous region based model with a national framework guided by medical urgency. The reform introduced a Medical Urgency Score derived from clinical data to categorize candidates as urgent or nonurgent, with mandatory reassessments every 14 days to capture changes in disease severity. The key features included giving priority to urgent recipients over geographic considerations, applying a waiting time algorithm that favors patients with prolonged critical illness, and implementing an age based allocation strategy that prioritizes donors younger than 18 years for pediatric recipients. Although China’s system is less complex than those in the United States or the United Kingdom, its streamlined design may be more adaptable in settings where transplantation programs are still developing [12].

In this single-center study, we evaluated two primary outcomes following the policy change. First, despite substantial changes in allocation practice, the one year survival after heart transplantation remained stable (Era 1: 92.15% vs. Era 2: 94.57%, P = 0.181). Second, urgent status recipients achieved one-year survival comparable to nonurgent recipients (93.1% vs. 95.2%, P = 0.324), despite requiring more MCS and experiencing higher perioperative morbidity. These findings contrast with international reports showing increased postoperative mortality after the implementation of urgency-based allocation systems [8]. We attribute this discrepancy to three features of China’s model: (1) retention of geographic tiering preserved access for nonurgent candidates; (2) urgent recipients were matched with younger donors (median age 39 vs. 42 years, P < 0.001), which offset the risk of longer ischemic times (3.83 vs. 3.50 h, P = 0.036); and (3) extensive experience in MCS management and patient selection, particularly with IABP support [13–15], which significantly improved survival among MCS-bridged recipients (P < 0.001). The impact of donor factors on 1-year survival was not observed, which may reflect the limited sample size of this study.

Beyond survival outcomes, perioperative morbidity and resource utilization under the urgency-based allocation system also warrant attention. In our cohort, postoperative use of IABP increased, likely reflecting higher baseline risk and greater preoperative severity among recipients, which necessitated continued reliance on mechanical circulatory support to maintain hemodynamic stability. Similarly, the utilization of CRRT also increased in urgent status recipients, consistent with data from the United States [16]. In addition, the rate of secondary thoracotomy increased, potentially related to device weaning, postoperative bleeding, or other surgical complications. Although ventilator duration was shorter in Era 2, likely reflecting advances in perioperative respiratory management, ICU stay was significantly longer in Era 2, likely reflecting the higher baseline risk and greater perioperative morbidity of urgent status recipients, compounded by older donor age and longer ischemic times. By contrast, other countries reported that ICU stay showed no major differences either between eras or between urgent and nonurgent recipients, suggesting that perioperative morbidity may vary across countries depending on allocation practices and patient characteristics [13, 17]. Collectively, these findings highlight that while urgency-based allocation improves access for critically ill patients, it also places a greater burden on perioperative management and postoperative care.

Waitlist outcomes did not improve as anticipated, and their interpretation should be approached with caution. The number of active candidates fluctuated during the post-reform period (2019–2022), reaching its lowest point in 2021, which coincided with China’s most stringent COVID-19 containment measures. At the same time, waitlist mortality peaked at 15.2% in 2021 despite the reduced candidate pool. This phenomenon was primarily driven by constraints related to the pandemic along both ends of the transplant pathway: barriers to urgent hospitalizations on the candidate side and restrictions on interprovincial transport that critically impeded the mobility of organ procurement teams. Together, these factors disproportionately increased mortality risk despite fewer listed candidates. By 2022, both the number of waitlist candidates and waitlist mortality showed recovery as pandemic controls eased, further supporting this association. Although overall waiting times increased annually for both groups in the post-reform era, the lack of pre-reform data prevents causal attribution to policy changes. In this study, the observed waitlist mortality in 2022 was 9.5%, which is comparable to contemporary international data. For example, the United States reported a waitlist mortality of approximately 7.0% between 2011 and 2017 [18], while in the United Kingdom, waitlist mortality was 7% for nonurgent candidates and 8% for urgent candidates [19].

Despite these challenges to overall waitlist metrics, the urgent status policy clearly achieved its intended efficacy. Under the revised allocation policy, the proportion of urgent status transplants increased steadily from 25.7 to 36.5%, representing 29.9% of all procedures. At the same time, the waitlist-to-transplant ratio declined from 1.41 to 1.10 (2019–2022), suggesting improved donor utilization efficiency. Waiting times varied substantially across different years; however, the median waiting time for nonurgent recipients in our cohort remained shorter than that reported in the United Kingdom (788 days) and in UNOS (198 days) [18, 19]. Interestingly, overall waiting times increased for both groups, likely reflecting COVID-19 transport delays, accumulation of nonurgent candidates, and progression of disease leading to urgent reclassification [20]. Importantly, urgent status recipients consistently had significantly shorter waits than nonurgent recipients (P < 0.05), validating the policy’s effectiveness in prioritization [6]. Baseline profiles confirmed greater disease severity in urgent recipients, reflected by higher NT-proBNP levels and greater preoperative MCS use. Postoperatively, these patients required more frequent hemodynamic support and renal replacement therapy, and had longer ICU stays [21, 22]. Nevertheless, equivalent 1-year survival was achieved, largely attributable to timely transplantation preventing decompensation and effective MCS bridging [23].

The optimized candidate selection process ensures that urgency criteria reliably identify recipients most likely to benefit from prioritized organ allocation. Within the urgency cohort, more than half qualified for urgent status due to IABP support exceeding 14 days. The 1-year survival of recipients bridged with IABP was higher in Era 2 than in Era 1, likely due to prioritization of IABP supported candidates under the new policy and overall improvements in perioperative care during Era 2 [24, 25]. Multivariate analysis identified preoperative dialysis and elevated bilirubin as independent predictors of mortality. These factors likely reflect irreversible end-organ damage and should be considered when listing critically ill candidates for urgent status. Multivariate analysis also confirmed prolonged CPB as an independent predictor of mortality. Prolonged CPB duration likely reflects greater surgical complexity in high-risk transplants, such as those involving congenital heart disease or repeat operations. Notably, urgent status recipients receiving nationally allocated organs face extended donor ischemic times [26], which, when combined with complex surgeries, may necessitate prolonged postoperative mechanical support. Consequently, urgent status designation for such candidates requires careful assessment of donor transport distance to avoid compounded ischemic injury [27].

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability. Subgroup analyses at multiple levels were not performed because of limited sample size. Second, the absence of waitlist data before the allocation reform precluded longitudinal assessment of waitlist mortality and urgent status transitions. In addition, the dataset lacked detailed donor information, which prevented analysis of associations between donor characteristics and prognosis. Moreover, follow-up was limited to one year, and longer observation is needed to validate the long-term effects of the urgency-based allocation policy. The COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2022) significantly disrupted transplant logistics through travel restrictions and organ transport delays, potentially confounding evaluation of allocation efficiency. In addition, long-term functional outcomes and cost-effectiveness of MCS bridging strategies were not evaluated. Therefore, future multicenter studies with extended follow-up are needed to validate policy impacts in nonpandemic settings, assess long-term survival and quality-of-life outcomes, and further quantify the cost–benefit balance of prolonged MCS versus urgent status.

In summary, at our center, although urgent status recipients presented with more severe preoperative conditions and experienced higher rates of perioperative complications, their survival outcomes were comparable to those of nonurgent recipients. Our study provides preliminary evidence that China’s national urgency-based allocation policy has improved access to transplantation for critically ill recipients while maintaining 1-year survival rates. These findings provide initial validation of the clinical effectiveness and operational feasibility of the policy within China’s developing transplantation system, although multicenter studies are warranted to confirm its efficacy.
