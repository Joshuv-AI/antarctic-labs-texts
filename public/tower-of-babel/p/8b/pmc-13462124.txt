Fluid-mediated nuclearity control in heterogeneous polyolefin catalysis
Authors: Zaitian He, Jingyuan Sun, Qingyuan Zheng, Wei Li, Lynn F. Gladden, Jingdai Wang, Yongrong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The nuclearity of active sites in heterogeneous Ziegler-Natta catalysts fundamentally governs polyolefin microstructure, yet its dynamic regulation during polymerization remains challenging. Here, we report a liquid containing polymerization strategy that directs the formation of dinuclear titanium species through periodic wetting-drying of inert n-hexane on the catalytic particles. Operando spectroscopy reveals how this unique fluid environment promotes the formation of dinuclear centers, in contrast to the monomeric species dominant in conventional gas- or slurry-phase systems. These dinuclear sites substantially enhance comonomer incorporation efficiency, yielding polyethylene with more uniform short-chain branching (SCB) distribution. Diffusion-ordered NMR spectroscopy further confirms that the capillary condensation of inert n-hexane in catalyst pores during liquid containing polymerization can enrich 1‑octene concentration by over twofold compared to the bulk slurry environment, thereby effectively promoting the SCB content in the synthesized polyethylene. This work establishes reactor fluid dynamics as a powerful tool for in-situ active-site engineering, opening avenues for selective control of polymer microstructure without modifying catalyst composition.

Introduction

A defining challenge in polyolefin science is the selective control of molecular microstructure to tailor mechanical properties and other functionalities1,2. While such structural homogeneity has been successfully achieved with single-site catalysts, it remains a significant challenge for multi-site Ziegler-Natta catalytic systems3. The enrichment of branches within the low-molecular-weight fractions represents a key drawback of Ziegler-Natta catalytic systems, which, despite accounting for about half of global polyethylene production, yield materials with limited performance4,5.

Classical Ziegler-Natta catalysts are typically synthesized by immobilizing transition metal species (e.g., TiCl4) onto the unsaturated lattice sites of MgCl2 support to achieve high dispersion of active centers6. Two main types of active sites have been identified based on titanium aggregation, i.e., isolated monomeric TiCl4 and dinuclear Ti2Cl8 species bridged by chloride ligands7,8. The catalyst enriched with monomeric Ti species presents highly catalytic activity and considerable copolymerization ability, but concentrates the short-chain branch distribution (SCB) in the low-molecular-weight fractions. While spectroscopic and kinetic analyses indicate that the catalysts enriched with either dinuclear titanium species or monomeric dormant active species facilitate a more uniform SCB distribution9,10. Thus, regulating the nuclearity of active species represents the state-of-the-art strategy in tailoring polymer chain microstructures.

Recent studies have confirmed the inherent dynamic mobility of titanium species, providing a new foundation for tailoring active-site nuclearity11. Such mobility allows for the enrichment of dinuclear titanium species, achieved either by increasing local titanium concentration or by attenuating their interaction with the MgCl2 matrix12,13. For instance, complexation of TiCl4 species with ligands such as ethyl benzoate enhances titanium mobility by reducing its binding strength to the MgCl2 surface, thereby promoting titanium aggregation14. Furthermore, the formation of dinuclear titanium species, occurring both during catalyst synthesis and its activation, likely results from the aggregation of reduced Ti(OR)3-xClx species. This process is promoted by Lewis acids and preferentially takes place at the exterior of catalyst particles15. Additionally, beyond modulating the microscopic coordination environment of active centers, research on industrial condensed-mode polymerization has demonstrated that the introduction of inert condensing agents (ICAs) effectively elevates the local concentration of ethylene around the active centers, thereby influencing the polymer chain growth process16. This condensed-mode strategy can be realized in industry with the assistance of modeling studies in fluidized bed reactors17,18. In light of the dynamic behaviour of active centres and the significant influence of the mass transfer environment on the copolymerization reaction, a fundamental question emerges: Can nuclearity regulation be achieved not only through catalyst design, but also via polymerisation engineering? Affirmative proof would shift the paradigm beyond static catalyst design, toward the in-situ engineering of active sites for SCB regulations.

In this study, we introduce a liquid containing polymerization mode that diverges from conventional catalytic methodologies to actively tailor the nuclearity of active species. The moderated n-hexane was introduced to transfer the catalytic particles into the high-frequency cycles of drying-wetting. This periodic phase transition not only enriches 1-octene within the catalyst particles but also promotes the formation of dinuclear titanium, thereby enhancing comonomer insertion efficiency and enabling uniform distribution of SCB in the synthesized polyethylene. To probe the mass transport within the catalyst particles and the evolution of active-site nuclearity under this liquid containing polymerization mode, nuclear magnetic resonance imaging and operando spectroscopic analysis are applied, respectively.

Results

Catalytic activation in the different phases

Operando activation and polymerization were performed in a Harrick Praying Mantis cryogenic cell under three phase environments, i.e., a gas-phase mode with dry reactants, a slurry-phase mode with continuous solvent wetting, and a liquid containing mode where periodic wetting-drying cycles were regulated by gas-liquid hydraulic equilibrium (Fig. 1a and details in ESI section II). The oxidation state and coordination structure transformations of the activated catalyst were first probed by UV-vis spectroscopy (Fig. 1b). The spectrum of the pre-catalyst is dominated by the prominent absorption band located around 40,700 cm−1, which can be ascribed to the Cl → Ti charge transfer (CT) transitions associated with six-coordinated Ti4+, as well as the O → Ti CT transitions linked to four-coordinated Ti4+ (Fig. S1a)19,20. Similar spectra were acquired for the pre-catalyst under three different phases, indicating the robust stability of titanium species before the activation (Fig. S1a–c). Interestingly, the absorption band gradually undergoes a red shift during the catalyst activation in slurry phase (37,400 cm−1), gas-phase (36,800 cm−1), and liquid containing phase (33,800 cm−1), suggesting an increased reduction extent of TiCl4 (Fig. 1b)21. Furthermore, the nuclearity of Ti3+ is analyzed through the deconvolution of UV spectra, where the bands around 23,000 cm−1 are assigned to the monomeric Ti3+ species (alkylated Ti5c3+) and the broad absorption around 16,000 cm−1 is attributed to the resonance of dinuclear Ti3+ species15,22–24. It is shown that the alkylated Ti5c3+ in the catalyst activated by the gas-phase is more than that of slurry-phase activation. This is consistent with the normalized Ti K-edge XANES characterization spectra (Fig. S2). The catalyst activated via the gas-phase exhibits the most intense pre-edge peak at approximately 4971.74 eV, which suggests the higher abundance of low-symmetry coordination Ti species and demonstrates the predominant formation of monomeric Ti5c3+ 25,26. The unpaired electrons of Ti3+ species after the activation were further investigated by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Fig. 1c). Two distinct signals, i.e., g = 1.948 (Ti-1) and 1.977 (Ti-2) are both found in the catalyst activated in the gas phase and slurry phase, where the former is attributed to Ti3+ ions in a chlorine environment, and the latter corresponds to alkylated Ti3+ species28–31. Quantitative analysis reveals a much higher proportion of alkylated Ti3+ in the gas-phase system (i.e., 55.1%), compared to the slurry-phase case (39.3%, see Table S1). This aligns with UV-vis spectroscopy results, indicating TiCl4 species are more prone to being reduced under the gas-phase activation. In contrast, the catalyst activated by the liquid containing phase exhibits a broad feature at g = 1.930 (Ti-1), which indicates the formation of chlorine-bridged Ti3+-Ti4+ dinuclear species8,32. A detailed discussion of the DFT simulations supporting this signal assignment is provided in Fig. S3. Moreover, the resonance at g = 2.005 (Ti-3) corresponding to the dormant active site is present in this system, accounting for up to 21.1% of the spectral composition10,33.

EXAFS far-edge region characterization was further conducted to address the coordination environment of the active species, where the wave function fitting results are transformed into R-space spectra (Fig. 1d and Table S2). The imaginary component of the Fourier transform of kΔχ (k) with the fitting is shown in Fig. S4. The Fourier-transformed R-space EXAFS spectrum (Fig. 1d) demonstrates that the coordination environment of the active Ti species consists predominantly of Ti-O and Ti-Cl scattering paths34,35. Quantitative fitting results reveal a higher coordination number (C.N.) of Ti-Cl bonds with a shorter bond distance (Ti-Cls, R = 2.05 Å) in the gas-phase activated catalyst (Table S2), consistent with the dominant formation of monomeric Ti5c3+ species. Notably, an additional longer Ti-Cl bond (Ti-ClL, R = 2.36 Å) is shown in the liquid containing activated catalyst with a higher C.N. compared to Ti-Cls. These results suggest that the mobility of active centers after activation promotes structural rearrangement, facilitating the formation of dinuclear titanium species36.

The mobility of active species in the liquid containing activation

To elucidate the origin of dinuclear titanium species formation during the liquid containing phase activation, the frequency of the wetting-drying cycles is focused and modulated by varying the bubbling rate. At a low bubbling frequency (e.g., 0.53 Hz), insufficient drying time maintains the catalyst in a persistently wetted and slurry-like state. In contrast, an excessively high frequency (e.g., 1.63 Hz) limits adequate solvent contact, shifting the system toward a gas-phase-dominant environment. Intermediate frequencies sustain a dynamic liquid containing state with periodic transitions (Movies S1, S2).

EPR spectra of the activated catalyst upon the variation of bubbling frequency are shown in Fig. 2a and Table S3. As the frequency increases from 0.53 Hz to 1.01 Hz, the weighting factor of dormant Ti3+ species (Ti-3, g = 2.001) increases significantly from 4.5% to 21.1%, demonstrating enhanced activation of dormant sites. Concurrently, the dinuclear titanium active center (Ti-1, g = 1.930, resonance for chlorine-bridged Ti3+-Ti4+ species) becomes activated at frequencies ≥1.01 Hz, as evidenced by signal broadening in the up-field region. The alkylated Ti3+ species reaches its highest concentration at the maximum frequency of 1.63 Hz, indicating that TiCl4 reduction is facilitated under conditions where the particles approach the gas-phase state. Complementary DR UV-vis spectra further support these findings (Fig. 2b). The absorption at ∼16,000 cm−1, assigned to dinuclear titanium species, intensifies with increasing bubbling frequency and maximizes at 1.01 Hz, underscoring the key role of periodic wetting-drying cycles in promoting dinuclear titanium formation. The structural evolution of the MgCl2 support under varying bubbling frequencies was probed by X-ray diffraction. As shown in Fig. 2c, all activated catalysts exhibit characteristic peaks between 18–23°, 27–34°, and 48–52°, consistent with the presence of disordered δ-MgCl211. As the bubbling frequency increases, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peaks at 35° (assigned to the (104) plane) and 50.5° (corresponding to the (110) plane) first increases and then decreases, reaching a maximum broadening at 1.01 Hz. This reversible peak broadening reflects a reduction in the structural ordering of MgCl2 lamellae induced by high-frequency wetting-drying cycles37,38. Further insight was gained from low-temperature CO adsorption DRIFT spectra (Fig. S5). The maximum θCO curves, normalized to the resonance around 2183 cm−1 (the reflection of Mg4c2+ species on the MgCl2 (110) plane)39, are shown in Fig. 2d. The absorption peak at ca. 2161 cm−1 is assigned to the resonance of Mg5c2+ species on the MgCl2 (104) plane40. As the bubbling frequency rises, the intensity of the absorption peak at ca. 2161 cm−1 first decreases and then increases, reaching the minimum at the frequency of 1.01 Hz (Fig. 2e). This decrease in intensity indicates progressive occupation of Mg5c2+ sites by active Ti species41. The most significant decrease at 1.01 Hz aligns with the highest degree of structural disorder induced by wetting-drying dynamics, which facilitates the aggregation of active species and promotes the formation of dinuclear titanium species anchored on the (104) plane.

Catalytic performance in ethylene polymerization

As shown in Table S4, ethylene-1-octene copolymerization was performed under gas-phase, slurry, and liquid containing conditions. With increasing temperature, the slurry-phase polymerization exhibited a gradual increase in catalyst activity, whereas the gas-phase process showed a steady decline. This contrast is primarily attributed to the superior heat transfer of the inert solvent in slurry polymerization, which minimizes localized hot spots and prevents thermal deactivation at elevated temperatures42. Under liquid containing conditions, catalyst activity was generally intermediate between those of the gas-phase and slurry systems. However, when the temperature exceeded 80 °C, limited heat transfer led to a decrease in polymerization activity.

Inspired by the effect of wetting-drying cycles on the variance of active species, the switching between slurry polymerization and liquid containing polymerization should be focused. By reducing the volume of n-hexane to one-third of that used in conventional slurry polymerization, we induced a transition from an initial slurry state to a liquid containing environment within the catalytic particles. During the early polymerization stage, low polymer yield maintained the system in the slurry phase. The SCB content of polyethylene synthesized by the slurry-phase polymerization gradually decreased over time and stabilized after 40 mins (Fig. 3b). The SCB was predominantly concentrated in the low molecular weight fraction (Fig. S6). When the n-hexane volume was reduced to 100 mL, a slight SCB content increase was observed during the initial stage (≤20 mins), attributable to elevated comonomer concentration. Notably, after 40 mins of polymerization, the SCB content increased abruptly by more than 2.5-fold (Fig. 3b, detailed NMR spectrum in Figure S7). This transition was accompanied by a significant redistribution of SCBs, i.e., decreasing in low-molecular-weight regions while increasing markedly in high-molecular-weight fractions (Fig. 3c). Comparative polymerization experiments conducted in gas, slurry, and liquid containing phases across different temperatures consistently demonstrates that the polyethylene synthesized in the liquid containing system shows substantially higher SCB content than the other two systems (Fig. S8). The observed increase in SCB corresponds with a decrease in molecular weight, as shown in Table. S5. The liquid containing phase produces a lower Mw (344549 g/mol) than the gas phase and slurry phase, which suggests a phase-dependent shift in the reactivity ratios of 1-octene and ethylene during copolymerization. Furthermore, the gas phase yields the broadest molecular weight distribution (PDI = 13.49), indicating pronounced mass transfer limitations. To achieve short-chain branching (SCB) levels comparable to those obtained with liquid containing polymerization, the volume of 1-octene was increased approximately 20-fold, from 10 mL to 200 mL. This increase resulted in SCB levels, as measured by 13C NMR, rising from 2.6 to 18.7 per 1000 carbons in the copolymer (Fig. 3d, Fig. S9). The SCB values of copolymers synthesized via liquid containing phase polymerization were taken as the benchmark. Consequently, by regulating the 1-octene concentration, specifically by using an initial adding amount in the slurry phase that is 20 times higher (200 ml) than that in the liquid containing phase, the resulting polymer (SLURRY-20 fold) from the slurry phase achieved a molecular weight comparable to that from the liquid containing phase, as shown in Table S5. However, Fig. 3e indicates that while the 1-octene content in the slurry-phase polymer is enhanced, it remains preferentially distributed in the low-molecular-weight region. As shown in Fig. S10, the DSC second-heating curves reveal a clear trend in thermal properties across the three polymerization phases. With increasing 1-octene incorporation, both melting temperature and crystallinity decrease in the same order: slurry phase (134.8 °C, 52.9%) > gas phase (133.7 °C, 50.2%) > liquid containing phase (131.4 °C, 37.1%). More notably, when the 1-octene concentration in the slurry phase is elevated 20-fold to achieve an SCB content comparable to that of the liquid containing phase, the resulting polymer exhibits a melting point of 132.3 °C and a crystallinity of 46.9%, both higher than those of the liquid containing phase. Comparing the polymer products obtained from the liquid containing phase with those from the slurry-20 fold sample, the more uniform SCB distribution also influences the mechanical properties: the tensile strength increases from 33.6 to 42.4 MPa, the elongation at break increases from 742% to 1030%, and the Young’s modulus decreases from 231 to 211 MPa, as shown in Fig. S11. The polyethylene synthesized by the liquid containing phase also shows a pronounced content of SCB in the long chains and a reduced SCB content in the short chains, compared with those of the other two phases (Figs. S6, S12).

To elucidate the mechanism behind the enhanced distribution of SCB in the liquid containing mode, operando ethylene polymerization was first conducted in the slurry phase, and then transferred into liquid containing phase after 6 mins (Fig. 3d–f). The intensity of the ultraviolet absorption peaks gradually decreases with prolonged polymerization time, due to the generated polyethylene progressively encapsulating the catalyst particles and shielding the ultraviolet light (Fig. 3d). The absorption bands of the operando slurry polymerization are predominantly observed at 37,400 cm-¹ and 23,000 cm-¹, indicating that mononuclear Ti species serve as the primary active centers. Notably, upon switching to the liquid containing phase, a broad and intensified absorption emerges near 16,000 cm−1, signifying the formation of dinuclear titanium species. Operando CO adsorption DRIFT spectra further reveal changes in MgCl2 defect sites during this transition (Fig. 3e, f). As polymerization shifts from slurry to liquid containing mode, peak intensities at 2184 cm-¹ and 2204 cm-¹ increased substantially, reflecting a higher population of MgCl2 sites with lower coordination numbers40,41. Consistent with these observations, when the catalyst is first treated under liquid containing conditions in the cell followed by slurry polymerization in the reactor, neither high SCB content nor uniform SCB distribution is achieved in the synthesized polyethylene (Fig. S13a). In contrast, pre-activating the catalyst in the operando cell under the slurry phase, followed by liquid containing polymerization in the reactor, yields polyethylene with significantly elevated SCB content and more uniform SCB distribution (Fig. S13b). Correspondingly, the characteristic resonance of dinuclear titanium species (~16,000 cm⁻¹) in the operando UV spectrum does not intensify after the transfer from liquid containing mode to the slurry mode (Fig. S14). These results collectively demonstrate that sustaining the liquid containing environment during the polymerization is essential for promoting dinuclear titanium species formation and enabling effective SCB regulation.

To further demonstrate the reason for the promoted SCB content of the polymer synthesized in the liquid containing polymerization, vapor-liquid equilibrium established within the pores of catalyst particles is probed by the operando magnetic resonance techniques (Fig. 4). When the partial pressure of the hydrocarbon mixture PHC is lower than its boiling point PHC,b, liquid is only present within the catalyst pores by adsorption and capillary condensation, as confirmed by the MRI imaging of the sample tube (Fig. 4a and Fig. S15). In contrast, bulk liquid condensation in the sample tube is observed at PHC/PHC,b > 1 (Fig. 4a). It is shown in Fig. 4d that the 1‑octene/ethylene ratio in the capillary condensate is approximately twice that in the gas phase or in the bulk liquid of slurry polymerization. The enrichment of 1-octene concentration in pores is observed at all hydrocarbon pressures (Fig. S16), which is likely to occur in the liquid containing conditions. When further considering mass-transfer limitations in slurry systems, the effective 1‑octene concentration accessible to active sites in the liquid containing mode is expected to exceed that in slurry polymerization by more than twofold. Discussion

This work demonstrates that the nuclearity of active titanium species in Ziegler-Natta catalysis is not an intrinsic catalyst property, but a dynamic feature that can be engineered through reactor fluid dynamics. By establishing a liquid containing polymerization regime with periodic wetting-drying cycles of inert n-hexane on the catalytic particles, we achieve in-situ formation of dinuclear titanium sites, which significantly enhance comonomer incorporation efficiency and short-chain branching distribution in polyethylene. Operando spectroscopic studies coupled with diffusion-ordered NMR confirm that this strategy simultaneously promotes titanium aggregation and enriches 1-octene concentration within catalyst pores. The findings shift the paradigm from static catalyst design toward dynamic active-site engineering via polymerization medium control, offering a versatile pathway to tailor polyolefin microstructure without modifying catalyst composition. This fluid-directed approach opens new possibilities for regulating active-site nuclearity in heterogeneous catalysis.

Methods

Operando activation in different phases

A Harrick Praying Mantis cryogenic reaction cell, featuring a 6 mm diameter and 6 mm depth, was used as the operando reactor to enable catalyst activation and polymerization for operando measurement (Fig. S17). The outlet of the reactor was sealed with a bubbler. Approximately 10 mg of reference compound (e.g., BaSO4 or KBr) was first introduced into the reaction cell to serve as the background reference for spectroscopic measurements. The 1-2 mg of catalyst was deposited onto the reference surface for operando UV-Vis and infrared spectroscopy conducted in diffuse reflectance mode, respectively. During the operando process, nitrogen was introduced at 20 sccm during catalyst activation, while a premixed gas of ethylene and nitrogen at a 1:4 volumetric flow ratio was simultaneously supplied at a total flow rate of 20 sccm for the operando ethylene polymerization.

The operando operations in the gas phase, slurry phase and liquid containing phase were conducted as follows. (i) For the operando operation at the gas-phase mode, dry gas components were introduced into the operando reactor, with no liquid introduced. (ii) For the operando operation at the slurry-phase mode, the catalyst was first immersed in 1 mL of n-hexane. Then, 6 mL of n-hexane was added every 6 mins via a tube immediately preceding the reactor inlet to ensure the catalyst particles remained consistently wetted by the solvent. (iii) For the operando operation at the liquid containing mode (Fig. S18), the gas (N2, or N2/C2H4 mixtures) was introduced at 20 sccm at room temperature. A hydraulic head was established by introducing 20 mL of n-hexane through a side arm connected to the reactor outlet and positioned 45 cm above the reactor. This hydrostatic pressure was required for initial catalyst bed wetting. To maintain a stable liquid level in the side arm, n-hexane was supplemented in 6 mL aliquots at 6-minute intervals. The system sustained a continuous flow of gas, which progressively displaced the n-hexane solution from the catalyst bed owing to the gas accumulation. This dynamic gas-liquid interaction established a cyclic equilibrium governed by the balance between gas pressure and liquid hydraulic head, thereby inducing periodic wetting-drying transitions within the catalyst bed. In particular, each bubble formation corresponded to one switching cycle (Movies S1, S2). The bubbling interval was calibrated to slightly exceed the catalyst drying time through the control of gas flow rate, system pressure, and n-hexane hydrostatic head (detailed calculations are shown in ESI and the calculated parameters are provided in Table S6). This ensured that the catalyst particles underwent repeated dry-wet transitions during each operational cycle. The frequency of wetting-drying cycles was therefore directly controlled by the bubbling frequency, which in turn was regulated by the gas flow rate.

Operando spectrum measurement

The operando spectrum measurement with the gas distribution system is shown in Fig. S17 a. All the samples were prepared in the glovebox and then transferred to the operando reactor. For catalyst activation, TEA was added dropwise to the catalyst with an Al/Ti molar ratio of 10. The operando spectrum measurement of catalyst activation was carried out at 80 °C with nitrogen purging maintained at 20 sccm. All operando ethylene polymerizations were conducted at 80 °C and 2 bar pressure. 1-Octene was vaporized via the heated intake duct prior to entering the reactor. After activating the catalyst, a mixed gas of 16 sccm nitrogen and 4 sccm ethylene was introduced into the operando reactor to start the polymerization.

Diffuse reflection ultraviolet-visible (DR UV-vis) spectroscopy

Diffuse reflection ultraviolet-visible (DR UV-vis) spectra were obtained utilizing a Lambda 1050 + spectrophotometer from PerkinElmer, USA. The catalysts were loaded into the operando reactor inside the glovebox, which was then sealed and transferred to the UV-vis spectrometer. A pristine BaSO₄ plate was used to obtain the reference background spectrum. The spectra were collected in reflectance mode (R%) and subsequently transformed into normalized Kubelka-Munk F(R) units for analysis.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded at room temperature, using a Bruker Elexsys E500 spectrometer operating at the X-band frequency of 9.42 GHz. The catalyst was activated under the specified state conditions (i.e., gas, slurry, and liquid containing) in the operando reactor, which was then transferred into a quartz tube in the glovebox. The settings of the X-band continuous wave (CW) EPR measurements were as follows: 25 dB attenuation, 0.6325 mW microwave power output, 100 kHz modulation frequency, and 0.5 mT modulation amplitude. After the acquisition, the experimental EPR spectra were analyzed using a global least-squares fitting procedure with the EasySpin toolbox to process all CW EPR spectra.

DFT computational methods

Local cluster models representing symmetric Ti–Ti pairs, asymmetric Ti–Cl–Ti bridges, five-coordinate Ti sites, six-coordinate Ti sites, and MgCl2-perturbed interfacial motifs were extracted from the parent MgCl2/TiCl4 framework and treated within a scalar-relativistic exact-two-component (X2C) Hamiltonian in order to preserve the ligand-field splitting of the open-shell Ti centers while avoiding an over-delocalized nonrelativistic description. The reference wave functions for the doublet states were generated from restricted open-shell Hartree-Fock determinants, after which the principal g values were evaluated from a coupled-cluster response treatment. In practice, CCSD was used for the ground-state linear response, the low-lying excited-state manifold entering the paramagnetic spin-orbit contribution was generated by EOM-CCSD, and perturbative triples corrections were used to refine the energy denominators governing the magnitude of the g shifts. Spin–orbit coupling was introduced at the mean-field X2C/Breit–Pauli level and folded into the effective spin Hamiltonian, from which the anisotropic g tensor was assembled through the standard sum-over-states response expression. Powder EPR spectra were then reconstructed by orientational averaging of field-derivative absorption profiles using mixed Lorentzian/Gaussian broadening functions, so that each structural unit was represented by a physically constrained set of principal g values, anisotropic linewidths, and population weights rather than by unconstrained peak fitting. The final simulated spectra were obtained as weighted superpositions of the inactive symmetric Ti pair, the EPR-active asymmetric Ti–Cl–Ti bridge, the MgCl2-associated perturbed component, and a weak broad background contribution. In this way, the emergence of the g ≈ 1.89 signal, the shoulder-to-main-feature ratio, and the residual contraction upon inclusion of the asymmetric bridge motif were all evaluated within a single electronic-structure-to-lineshape workflow43.

Ti K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy

Ti K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopic (XAS) analysis was conducted with a 100 W (33.3 kV, 3 mA) easyXES150 spectrometer. 10 mg of catalyst was mixed with 50 mg of hexagonal boron nitride (BN), which was further compacted into a 1 mm pellet in diameter. This pellet was loaded into the operando reactor after being activated with TEA and was further treated under gas, slurry, and liquid-containing states. The operando reactor was quickly transferred to the measurement chamber at the beamline for further characterization. The polychromatic X-ray beam was refined using a Si (4 0 0) monocrystalline bent crystal. For XAS analysis, scans were performed from −100 to +150 eV relative to the Ti K-edge (XANES) and up to k = 12 Å⁻¹ (EXAFS). The benchmark was established using the first derivative of a Ti metal foil standard. The XANES spectral normalization and alignment were performed using Athena software, while the EXAFS data fitting was conducted with Artemis software44. The k2χ(k) functions were processed through Fourier transformation within the Δk interval of 3.0-11.0 Å−1.

Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy

Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFT) spectroscopy was performed using a Perkin-Elmer 2000 spectrometer. The spectrometer was enclosed within a glove box to ensure a stable and controlled characterization environment. The samples were carefully placed and sealed inside a Harrick Praying Mantis cryogenic reaction cell, which allowed for precise control over experimental pressure and temperature. Low-temperature CO adsorption experiments were conducted to investigate the unsaturated positions on the MgCl2 plane after the catalysts were processed in a certain state (e.g., gas state, slurry state, or liquid-containing state). The cell was cooled to −150 °C by circulating liquid nitrogen through its jacket. Carbon monoxide (CO), used as a probe molecule with a 99.9% purity and diluted to 10% in helium, was introduced into the cell until the pressure reached 300 mbar. The adsorption peaks were recorded after waiting for 30 minutes. CO desorption was initiated by gradually decreasing the reactor pressure from 300 mbar to <10 mbar. The spectra for tracing the desorption process were performed at 20 scans with 8 cm−1 resolution.

X-ray diffraction spectroscopy

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted by a Bruker D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer, configured at an operational voltage of 20 kV and a current of 100 mA. The energy resolution of the instrument was ascertained to be superior to 380 eV at the energy level of 8 keV. The experimental setup employed a 1D detection system using a copper target to produce a high-resolution Kα1 monochromatic beam (λ = 1.5406 Å). The catalyst samples were carefully prepared in a glovebox and subsequently loaded onto the sample holder to prevent exposure to moisture and oxygen during the transfer. The diffractometric scans were conducted across a 2θ angular range spanning from 10° to 60°, employing a scan rate of 2° per minute.

Ethylene/1-octene copolymerization

Ethylene/1-octene copolymerization was conducted in a 500 mL Buchi stainless steel autoclave reactor, where the traditional gas-state and slurry-state polymerization were performed as the benchmark.

For the gas-phase polymerization, 200 g of dried NaCl was added as a seedbed before the reaction, and the reactor was degassed under vacuum at 95 °C for 3 hours to remove water and oxygen. For slurry polymerization and liquid containing polymerizations, 300 ml and 100 ml of n-hexane were directly fed into the reactor via a pipeline, respectively. After the reactor temperature was adjusted to the desired temperatures (60, 70, 80, and 85 °C), 20 mg of catalyst was injected into the reactor with the dispersion in a solution consisting of 10 ml of n-hexane and 10 ml of 1-octene. Then, triethylaluminum was added to the reactor, where the nominal loading of the Al/Ti molar ratio was maintained at 100. The reaction was initiated immediately as ethylene was fed into the reactor at a pressure of 8 bar. The reactor was quickly vented after stirring at 500 rpm for 1 h. Finally, nascent polyethylene was obtained by immediate washing with acidified ethanol and vacuum drying at 60 °C for at least 24 h.

Short-chain branch distribution

The measurement of short-chain branch distribution was performed at 160 °C using a PolymerChar GPC-IR instrument (Valencia, Spain), equipped with an IR5-MCT detector and a 200 μL sample loop. The samples were dissolved in HPLC-grade 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene (TCB) as a mobile phase at a concentration of 0.5 mg/ml for 2 h at 160 °C. The samples were injected into the system via an autosampler. The GPC-IR system was calibrated using narrow-dispersity polystyrene calibration sample for molecular weight and molecular weight distribution (MWD) determination, along with ethylene/1-hexene copolymer calibration sample for short-chain branching (SCB) distribution analysis. The calibration sample were predetermined by quantitative ¹³C NMR spectroscopy. The calibration curve (CH₃/CH₂ ratio vs. SCB/1000 C) exhibited excellent linearity (R² > 0.99) over the concentration range relevant to our samples. Using the calibrated curve, the branching degrees of a series of polymer samples obtained under gas and slurry phases at various polymerization times were measured and compared with ¹³C NMR data. The agreement between GPC-IR and ¹³C NMR is further demonstrated in Fig. S19, which presents SCB/1000 C data for slurry and liquid containing phase samples collected at polymerization times ranging from 10 to 80 mins.

13C NMR spectroscopy for branching degree

The branching degree of the synthesized polyethylene was investigated by 13C NMR spectroscopy (Bruker Advance III 300 MHz NMR spectrometer). 40 mg of the synthesized polymers were dissolved in 0.5 mL of deuterated o-dichlorobenzene and heated to 120 °C to ensure complete dissolution. Each sample was subjected to 5000 scans to obtain a high-resolution nuclear magnetic resonance spectrum. The branching degree was calculated according to the ref. 45.

Tensile testing method

Tensile testing was performed using a universal testing machine equipped with a 10 kN load cell. Polymer samples containing 0.6 wt% Irganox 1010 were hot-pressed at 190 °C and 15 MPa for 30 min, then cold-pressed at 25 °C for 30 min to form dumbbell-shaped specimens. The specimen had a rectangular cross-section (width: 3.5 mm; thickness: 2.0 mm) and an initial gauge length L0 of 20 mm.

The specimen was subjected to uniaxial tension at a constant crosshead speed until fracture. Engineering stress was calculated by normalizing the measured load by the original cross-sectional area, and engineering strain was determined as the ratio of displacement to the initial gauge length.

From the resulting stress–strain curve, the Young’s modulus was derived from the slope of the initial linear (elastic) region. Tensile strength was defined as the maximum stress sustained by the specimen, and elongation at break was taken as the strain at failure.

NMR measurements for capillary condensation in catalyst pores

The NMR measurements were performed on a Bruker AV 300 spectrometer equipped with a vertical super-wide-bore 7.1 T superconducting magnet and a 3-axis gradient set providing a maximum gradient strength of 0.77 T m−1 in all 3 directions. A birdcage r.f. A coil of diameter 66 mm was used and was tuned to a resonance frequency of 300.88 MHz for the 1H signal. A typical 1H 90° pulse duration of 76 μs was applied throughout the experiment.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to determine the liquid distribution within the sample tube. The images were acquired using a rapid acquisition with relaxation enhancement (RARE) sequence46 with the field of view (FOV) of 80 (z) ´ 25 (x) mm2, in-plane resolution of 313 (z) ´ 195 (x) mm2 and a slice thickness of 25 mm to enhance signal to noise ratio. The images were acquired with 8 scans, a recycle time of 1 s and a RARE factor of 2.

The diffusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) measurement was performed to obtain the NMR spectrum of intra-particle liquid. A pulsed field gradient (PFG) sequence with stimulated echo (PGSTE)47. The pulsed gradient was applied along the x direction, perpendicular to the axial direction of the ceramic vessel, with a gradient stabilization time of 1 ms and an r.f. pulse spacing time of 3.3 ms. A gradient pulse duration of 1 ms and a diffusion observation time of 50 ms were applied with the pulsed gradient strength varied from 0.1 to 77 G cm−1 in 32 logarithmic steps. The diffusion observation time was applied to ensure fast exchange between liquid and vapor phases; further increase in this time does not affect the results. The measurement was performed using a recycle time of 1 s and 64 scans. The recycle time is comparable to the T1 relaxation time of the system. To measure diffusion coefficients at local positions of the sample tube, a pulse sequence consisting of the PGSTE sequence followed by a 1D frequency encoding sequence48 was applied, providing diffusion measurement with 1D spatial resolution. The setup for PGSTE section is the same as previously described. The 1D spatial encoding has a FOV of 200 mm and a spatial resolution of 0.78 mm.

Operando experiments in NMR

The sample tube packed with catalyst powders was placed in a ceramic vessel in which the temperature and pressure are under control as described previously49. Ceramic vessel was then placed into the magnet of the NMR spectrometer with the catalyst layer located at the center of the radio frequency (r.f.) coil. The sample tube was first heated to 120 °C with N2 flow rate of 10 NL h−1 and maintained at the temperature overnight to dry the water on the solid surface. NMR signal was acquired continuously to confirm a dry surface. The temperature was then reduced to the desired value of 80 °C and the background NMR signal was acquired. The gas mixture of 1-octene, n-hexane and N2 was then flowed into the ceramic vessel and pressurized the system to total pressure of 8 bar. The gas mixture was formed by flowing the liquid mixture of 1-octene and n-hexane with the 1-octene composition of 8 mol% (same as the liquid concentration used in the liquid containing polymerization) using a HPLC piston pump at a constant flow rate of 8 mL h−1. The liquid mixture was vaporized at 120 °C and mixed up with N2 in the feed line prior to entering the ceramic vessel. With constant total pressure of 8 bar, the partial pressure of hydrocarbon mixture PHC was first increased to 1.1 bar by flowing N2 at a flow rate of 8.8 NL h−1. The NMR signal intensity of the system was monitored to confirm that the system had reached vapour-liquid equilibrium. After conducting magnetic resonance (MR) measurements at the steady state, PHC was gradually increased to 1.3 bar in 5 steps by reducing the N2 flow rate and MR measurements were performed at each step. The variation of PHC corresponds to operating at a relative pressure of PHC/PHC,b = 0.8-1, where PHC,b is the bubble point pressure of hydrocarbon species calculated as 1.31 bar at 80 °C. This indicates that bulk condensation of hydrocarbon liquid mixture does not occur during the experiment, as confirmed by MRI images (Fig. S15).

NMR data processing and analysis

The MRI imaging data were Fourier transformed to obtain the 2D images of the system during condensation. For the DOSY data, the time-domain data acquired at each pulsed gradient strength were Fourier transformed to produce a spectrum. For each value of chemical shift, a PFG signal decay was extracted and then inversely Laplace transformed to produce a distribution of diffusion coefficients. The inverse Laplace transform was performed using the method of Tikhonov regularization50 using a smooth parameter of 0.1. In this way, a 2D spectrum correlating chemical shift d and diffusion coefficients D was obtained. For the diffusion data with spatial resolution along the sample tube, the spatial dimension of the data was first Fourier transformed to obtain a 1D image with diffusion weighting as shown in Fig. S20a, b. The signal from the catalyst layer and above the layer was integrated to yield two PFG decay datasets. These datasets were then processed to obtain diffusion distributions corresponding to the two local regions in the same way as for DOSY data processing.

In the 2D d-D spectra obtained in the DOSY measurements, three diffusion components are generally observed in the D range of 10−6, 10−8 and 10−9 m2 s−1. The diffusion data with spatial resolution along the sample tube were used to assist signal assignment for the 3 diffusion components observed in DOSY. Figure S20 c presents the diffusion distributions measured from the catalyst layer and the region above the layer. It is observed that only one diffusion component with a D value at around 10−6 m2 s−1 was identified from the region above the catalyst layer where only gas signal is detected, indicating that the diffusion component of 10−6 m2 s−1 is associated with the gas present in the system. In the catalyst layer, the diffusion components at 10−9−10−8 m2 s−1 are dominating. While the diffusion component of 10−9 m2 s−1 is typical for hydrocarbon liquid, the component with a D value of 10−8 m2 s−1 between the typical D of liquid and gas species is assigned to the liquid species in fast exchange with gas51. This fast exchange results in an apparent diffusivity which is a linear combination of the diffusivities of liquid and gas Dapp = plDl + pgDg where pl and pg (pl + pg = 1) are the signal populations in the liquid and gas phases, respectively. It is estimated from the magnitudes of Dapp, Dl, and Dg that the signal in the liquid phase is dominating with pl ~ 0.97. Therefore, the diffusion component of 10−8 m2 s−1 is identified as the liquid present in partially saturated pores where liquid and gas are in equilibrium and fast exchange.
