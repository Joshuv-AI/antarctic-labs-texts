Metabolic phenotypes and pulmonary function in aging: the mediating roles of cognitive function and depression in CHARLS
Authors: Xuefei Zhang, Zhiyue Liang, Licong Su, Zihao Wang, Yu Shi, Guiyuan Cai, Zhenan Deng, Shengli An, Wen Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic phenotypes, defined by weight status and metabolic health, influence various physiological outcomes, yet their impact on pulmonary function in aging populations remains unclear. This study examines the longitudinal association between metabolic phenotypes and peak expiratory flow (PEF), considering the mediating roles of cognitive function and depression.MethodsWe analyzed longitudinal data (2011–2015) from 4,393 participants (mean age 58.9 years; 56.7% female) in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Participants were categorized into four metabolic phenotypes: metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW), metabolically healthy overweight/obese (MHO), metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW), and metabolically unhealthy overweight/obese (MUO). PEF was assessed via linear mixed model, adjusting for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors. Chain mediation analyses evaluated cognitive function (composite score) and depression (CES-D scale), with sensitivity analyses verifying robustness.ResultsAmong middle-aged and older women, MHO was associated with modestly higher PEF versus MHNW (middle-aged: β = 7.410, P < 0.001; older: β = 10.358, P = 0.038), and MUO with higher PEF in older women (β = 14.990, P < 0.001). No significant associations were found in men. Chain mediation showed metabolic phenotypes indirectly influenced PEF via cognition→depression (total indirect effect: 0.008, P = 0.001; 17% of total effect). Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness, though interpretations are cautious given PEF’s limitations as a proxy for full lung function.ConclusionsMetabolic phenotypes significantly may influence pulmonary function in aging populations, partially mediated by cognitive and mental health, with distinct sex- and age-specific patterns. However, higher PEF in overweight/obese groups warrants caution, potentially reflecting mechanical effects rather than overall respiratory benefit. Background

With the accelerating global aging population, the incidence of respiratory diseases among middle-aged and older populations has risen significantly, posing a major public health challenge worldwide. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a representative condition of pulmonary dysfunction, has a prevalence rate of 13.7% among individuals aged 40 and above in China, ranking as the third leading cause of death. This not only severely impairs patients’ quality of life but also imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden [1]. In recent years, growing evidence has suggested a potential pathophysiological link between metabolic abnormalities and respiratory function, highlighting the importance of exploring the relationship between metabolic characteristics and lung function for public health.

Metabolic phenotypes, as an emerging biomarker system, integrate multiple metabolic indicators such as obesity, blood glucose, blood pressure, and lipid levels to classify populations into subtypes including metabolically healthy, metabolically unhealthy obese (MUO), and metabolically unhealthy non-obese (MUNO) [2]. This classification method overcomes the limitations of traditional single-metric studies and provides a more comprehensive and accurate reflection of the body’s metabolic status. Basic research has shown that metabolic abnormalities may lead to lung tissue damage through mechanisms such as chronic inflammation and enhanced oxidative stress, thereby affecting pulmonary function [3]. However, existing epidemiological studies have predominantly focused on the association between single metabolic indicators [e.g., Body mass index (BMI) or fasting glucose] and lung function, with samples largely drawn from Western populations, lacking an examination of the overall characteristics of metabolic phenotypes. Moreover, previous studies have often overlooked the unique metabolic-respiratory interactions in middle-aged and older populations and have inadequately controlled for confounding factors (e.g., physical activity, air pollution exposure), resulting in significant heterogeneity in conclusions [4].

Prior work links metabolic syndrome to reduced lung function (e.g., FEV1/FVC) via inflammation [1, 3], and obesity to airflow limitation [17], yet some report an “obesity paradox” with higher PEF in obese groups due to thoracic stiffening [16, 18–20]. These inconsistencies underscore the need for integrated phenotype analyses in Asian cohorts. To address these gaps, this study utilizes longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) from 2011 to 2015, including 17,705 middle-aged and older individuals. We systematically investigated the association patterns between different metabolic phenotypes and pulmonary function decline by constructing linear mixed models integrating metabolic phenotypes with on-site pulmonary function test indicators (peak expiratory flow rate, PEF), while controlling for the effects of demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and environmental exposures. This study is the first to integrate multi-dimensional indicators of metabolic syndrome to construct phenotype classifications in an Asian middle-aged and older population. Its findings will provide critical scientific evidence for the early identification and precise intervention of metabolic-related pulmonary dysfunction.

Methods

Study participants

The data for this study were derived from the CHARLS, a nationally representative study conducted among community-dwelling adults aged 45 and older in China [5]. To date, five waves of follow-up surveys have been completed, with data collection occurring in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 [6]. The detailed research methodology of CHARLS has been described in previous publications [7]. In this study, participants were drawn from three waves of CHARLS (2011, 2013, and 2015) due to the availability of relevant pulmonary function indicator data. The baseline sample (2011) comprised 17,705 participants, from which individuals satisfying the following exclusion criteria were removed: (1) absence of available data on BMI, hypertension, gender, alcohol consumption, smoking status, diabetes, dyslipidemia, age, asthma, and lung disease (n = 11,915), (2) under the age of 45 (n = 99), (3) participants with asthma and lung disease at baseline (n = 687), (4) participants who had no measurement of PEF across the three waves (n = 459) and PEF outliers (n = 152). A total of 4,393 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The CHARLS study received ethical approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection

Previous studies have indicated that PEF is a reliable indicator of respiratory function in patients with COPD [8], with PEF defined as the maximum flow rate during the most forceful exhalation starting from maximum lung inflation. Higher PEF values are indicative of better pulmonary function. In CHARLS, PEF was measured using a peak flow meter equipped with a disposable mouthpiece [9] (Shanghai Everpure Medical Plastic Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China) with measurements recorded in liters per minute (L/min). The same type of instrument was used across the three waves (2011, 2013, and 2015). Prior to the test, participants were provided with standardized instructions by the researchers. Participants were instructed to stand up, take a deep breath, place their lips around the mouthpiece, and exhale as forcefully as possible. Researchers conducted three PEF (L/min) measurements at 30-second intervals. The highest value among the three tests was adopted for the purposes of this study.

Definitions

Participants were categorized into BMI groups [10]: normal weight (BMI < 25 kg/m²) and overweight/obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²). The metabolic status was defined as follows: (1) dyslipidemia; (2) hypertension; (3) diabetes. Participants were considered metabolically healthy if they did not have any of the three conditions, otherwise, they were classified as metabolically unhealthy. Consequently, based on BMI and metabolic status, we distinguished four metabolic phenotypes: (1) metabolically healthy with normal weight (MHNW); (2) metabolically unhealthy with normal weight (MUNW); (3) metabolically healthy with overweight or obesity (MHO); (4) metabolically unhealthy with overweight or obesity (MUO).

Cognitive function was measured by the CHARLS Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (CHARLS-HCAP). Specifically, overall cognitive function is composed of episodic memory and mental status, as detailed in prior research [9, 11–13]. A higher overall cognitive score indicates better cognitive function among participants.

Depression was assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), which has been widely used in population-based studies and has demonstrated good validity and reliability in CHARLS. The scale evaluates depressive symptoms over the past week through the following 10 items [14]: (1) bothered by minor matters, (2) difficulty concentrating, (3) feeling depressed, (4) everything is an effort, (5) feeling hopeless, (6) feeling fearful, (7) restless sleep, (8) feeling unhappy, (9) feeling lonely, and (10) unable to move forward. Items 5 and 8 are reverse-scored. Each item is rated as follows: 0 (rarely or none of the time, < 1 day), 1 (some or a little of the time, 1–2 days), 2 (occasionally or a moderate amount of the time, 3–4 days), and 3 (most or all of the time, 5–7 days). The total CESD-10 score ranges from 0 to 30. In this study, a CESD-10 score of ≥ 12 was considered positive for depression, while other scores were considered negative. Higher scores indicate more severe depression.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Data that are not normally distributed are expressed as median and interquartile range and analyzed by the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical data are described by counts and percentages and assessed using the chi-square test.

This study employed a linear mixed-effects model to examine the longitudinal association between metabolic phenotypes and pulmonary function, with PEF as the outcome variable and metabolic phenotypes as the main predictor. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted (only included metabolic status and survey wave as fixed effects, with random intercepts for individuals to account for repeated measurements); Model 2 expanded the base model by adjusting for sex (male, female) and age (50–59, 60–69, 70–79, ≥ 80 years); Model 3 further adjusted for smoking (non-smoker, ex-smoker, current- smoker), alcohol consumption (yes, no), and marital status (divided, married, never married). Additionally, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to diagnose multicollinearity issues, with VIF values less than 5 indicating that multicollinearity is not severe in the models, suggesting robustness of the results.

This study also conducted subgroup analyses by gender and age subgroups (middle-aged women [45–59 years], older women [≥ 60 years], middle-aged men [45–59 years], older men[≥ 60 years]) and employed mediation analysis to explore the mediating role of cognitive level and depression status between metabolic phenotypes and pulmonary function.

Finally, we performed the following sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings: (1) re-analyzed the data using more stringent PEF outlier exclusion criteria (the exclusion of the extreme 5% of baseline PEF outliers [15]); (2) additionally adjusted for cognitive function and depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted model; and (3) re-estimating models with altered reference groups for metabolic phenotypes.

All statistical analyses were completed using R software (version 4.3.3), with a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The 4,393 participants had a mean age of 58.92 ± 8.64 years, with 56.7% being female. Baseline characteristics of the remaining indicators are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Significant differences were observed among the groups divided by metabolic status in terms of age, gender, prevalence of chronic diseases, lifestyle, and biochemical indicators. The prevalence of hypertension was 42.1%, diabetes 7.9%, dyslipidemia 24.9%, and cardiovascular diseases 12.0%. Smokers accounted for 28.9%, with the highest rate in the MHNW group (36.2%) and the lowest in the MHO group (14.9%) (P < 0.001). Drinkers made up 32.3%, with the highest rate in the MHNW group (35.6%) and the lowest in the MHO group (25.5%) (P < 0.001).

Associations between metabolic phenotypes with pulmonary function

The analysis was conducted using the linear mixed model, and the results are presented in Table 1. Model 1 (unadjusted for covariates) showed no statistically significant associations between other metabolic phenotypes and pulmonary function compared to the MHNW reference group (all P > 0.05). After adjusting for age and gender (Model 2), both the MHO and MUO groups demonstrated significantly better pulmonary function than the MHNW group (MHO: β = 8.109, P < 0.001; MUO: β = 8.606, P = 0.001). These advantages remained statistically significant in Model 3 after further adjustment for marital status, smoking, and drinking (MHO: β = 7.711, P < 0.001; MUO: β = 8.410, P = 0.002), while no significant difference was observed for the MUNW group across all models. These results suggest that, after controlling for various potential confounding factors, both MHO and MUO individuals showed higher PEF values, whereas there was no significant difference in PEF values between MUNW and MHNW. This may imply that overweight or obesity has a positive effect on the maximum expiratory flow rate of lung function. Subgroup analysis

To further explore the association between metabolic phenotypes and pulmonary function, we conducted subgroup analyses based on age and gender. The distribution of different metabolic phenotypes across subgroups is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. PEF was generally higher in males than in females. Middle-aged individuals had higher PEF than older individuals. PEF was also generally higher in individuals with MHO and MUO phenotypes compared to the other two phenotypes. As shown in Table 2, subgroup analyses revealed significant sex and age specific associations. Among middle-aged women, the MHO individuals exhibited significantly better pulmonary function than the MHNW group (β = 7.410, P < 0.001). Notably, elderly women demonstrated even more pronounced benefits, with both MHO (β = 10.358, P = 0.038) and MUO (β = 14.990, P < 0.001) phenotypes showing superior pulmonary performance. These findings suggest a potentially protective effect of obesity on lung function in middle-aged and elderly female populations. In contrast, none of the metabolic phenotypes reached statistical significance in male subgroups (all P > 0.05).These findings suggest that the impact of metabolic phenotypes on pulmonary function may vary by gender and age.

Chain mediation analysis between metabolic status and PEF by cognition and depression

The chain mediation model results showed that metabolic status significantly predicted cognition (β = 0.044, P < 0.001). Cognition not only directly promoted PEF (β = 0.141, P < 0.001) but also indirectly enhanced PEF (β =−0.160, P < 0.001) by alleviating depressive symptoms (β = −0.089, P < 0.001). Although the direct impact of metabolic status on depression was insignificant (β = −0.005, P = 0.393), the chain mediation path from metabolic status to cognition, then to depression, and finally to PEF was statistically significant (β = 0.001, P = 0.002). The total indirect effect was 0.008 (P = 0.001), accounting for 17.0% of the total effect, indicating that the improvement in PEF by metabolic status was partially achieved through the chain mediation of cognition and depression.

Sensitive analysis

Initially, scatter plots (Supplementary Fig. 2A) and distribution plots (Supplementary Fig. 3A) revealed notable outliers in the raw data. To assess robustness, We redefined outliers as values in the extreme 5% of the PEF distribution and refitted the linear mixed model (Model 3) with these adjusted criteria, including collinearity diagnostics. The revised scatter plot (Supplementary Fig. 2B) and distribution plot (Supplementary Fig. 3B) illustrates the distribution of PEF following the exclusion of outliers, with the revised sample size amounting to 4,307. The sensitivity analysis confirmed that the effects of each metabolic phenotype on PEF remained consistent (Supplementary Table 2). After adjusting for cognitive function and depressive symptoms in Model 3 (Supplementary Table 3), cognitive function showed a significant positive association with PEF (β = 2.807, P < 0.001), indicating better pulmonary function in individuals with higher cognitive ability. Conversely, depressive symptoms demonstrated a significant negative association with PEF (β= −18.299, P < 0.001), suggesting potential detrimental effects of depression on lung function. Importantly, the effects of age, sex, marital status, smoking, and drinking on pulmonary function remained consistent across models, confirming the robustness of these associations. After changing the original reference group (MHNW), we re-constructed the linear mixed model based on Model 3, and the results are presented in Supplementary Table 4. When MHO was the reference group, MHNW was significantly negatively associated with PEF (β=−7.711, P < 0.001); when MUO was the reference group, both MHNW and MUNW were significantly negatively associated with PEF (MHNW: β=−8.410, P = 0.002; MUNW: β=−5.127, P = 0.003); when MUNW was the reference group, MUO was significantly positively associated with PEF (β = 5.127, P = 0.003). These results consistently indicate that obesity may favorably impact lung function health, aligning with our previous conclusions.

Discussion

Our longitudinal analysis of CHARLS data revealed that MHO and MUO phenotypes were associated with modestly higher PEF compared to MHNW, particularly in older women, though these findings should be interpreted cautiously as they may reflect short-term mechanical advantages (e.g., adiposity-induced thoracic stiffening enhancing expiratory force [16, 17]) rather than true respiratory health benefits. No significant associations emerged in men, and chain mediation indicated partial indirect effects via cognition and depression (17% of total effect). These align with the obesity paradox in lung function [16, 17, 19, 20] but contrast single-metric studies showing negative metabolic impacts [1, 3, 21], highlighting phenotype integration’s value in Asian aging cohorts.

Our results revealed that obese patients showed higher PEF, which contrasts with obesity’s typical association with restrictive lung patterns. This paradox may be explained by the “thoracic stiffening effect”where excess adipose tissue increases intrathoracic pressure during forced expiration, enhancing expiratory force [16, 17]. In women, estrogen-mediated adipocyte distribution (e.g., hip and thigh fat) may protect the lungs from restrictive pulmonary mechanics by maintaining diaphragmatic activity, while in men, visceral fat may temporarily increase expiratory force by raising intra-abdominal pressure [18, 19]. For the older, age-related sarcopenia may amplify the relative contribution of fat-driven thoracic stiffness to PEF, as muscle loss reduces elastic recoil resistance [20]. Notably, the absence of pulmonary impairment in MUNW aligns with the “obesity survival paradox,” where metabolically unhealthy normal weight individuals lack cardiorespiratory reserves conferred by adipocyte-mediated anti-inflammatory pathways [21]. These findings underscore the necessity of employing gender and age stratification methods when evaluating metabolic-lung interactions.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to identify that cognitive function and depression levels have a partial mediating effect between metabolic phenotypes and PEF. The potential mechanisms by which metabolic phenotypes affect lung function through cognitive function and depression levels may involve multiple biological and psychological processes. First, metabolic abnormalities (such as insulin resistance and dyslipidemia) may lead to cerebral microvascular damage and neuroinflammation [22, 23], thereby affecting cognitive function and emotional regulation [24]. For instance, insulin resistance has been shown to be associated with hippocampal dysfunction and an increased risk of depression [25]. Second, depression and cognitive decline may indirectly affect lung function through behavioral mechanisms [26–28], such as reduced physical activity and weakened respiratory muscle function. Additionally, chronic inflammation and oxidative stress may serve as bridges between metabolic abnormalities, cognitive decline, and lung function impairment [29, 30]. Previous studies have shown that systemic inflammatory markers (such as CRP and IL-6) mediate the relationship between metabolic abnormalities and cognitive decline [31], and our research further highlights their potential impact on lung function.

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, this study is based on middle-aged and older adults in China and needs further validation in other ethnic and age groups. Secondly, the present study can only establish the correlation between metabolic phenotypes and pulmonary function and cannot reveal the causal relationship between the two. Thirdly, despite efforts to control for confounding variables, it is possible that some confounding factors were not considered, and thus more comprehensive analyses are still needed. Last limitations include PEF’s effort-dependence and role as a partial proxy for lung function (e.g., insensitive to obstruction vs. FEV1/FVC); CHARLS lacks full spirometry, potentially overestimating obese group benefits. Future research should validate with comprehensive measures and explore mechanisms like inflammation. Nonetheless, our robust sensitivity analyses support integrated interventions targeting metabolic, cognitive, and mental health for respiratory outcomes.

Conclusions

This study reveals paradoxical links between overweight/obese metabolic phenotypes (MHO/MUO) and preserved pulmonary function in aging adults, mediated by cognitive benefits and depressive attenuation. Sex-specific patterns—consistent protection in females versus age-dependent effects in males—highlight biological heterogeneity. Findings suggest adipose-related metabolic reserve mechanisms may mitigate lung function decline, advocating integrated management of metabolic and neuropsychological health. Future research should explore body composition dynamics and inflammatory pathways to inform targeted interventions for respiratory resilience in aging populations.
