Cognitive biotypes identified through ECG-derived workload and behavioral accuracy
Authors: Sarah Conklin, Golshan Kargosha, Jenny Tu, Kanika Bansal, Quang Dang, Justin Brooks, Murat Kucukosmanoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividual differences in physiological effort during cognitive workload, which we define as mental effort during task execution, are well established, yet self-reports often fail to reflect actual physiological effort. We hypothesized that combining cognitive performance with ECG-derived workload would reveal distinct biotypes of performance and physiological effort and that these biotypes would differ in how closely subjective appraisals align with objective measures. Participants completed cognitive tasks while ECG data were analyzed in real time using a validated workload classification algorithm. Clustering based on standardized performance accuracy and cognitive workload revealed three biotypes: (1) high performers with low workload, (2) average-to-high accuracy with high workload, and (3) low performers with variable workload. These biotypes exhibited distinct patterns of perceptual bias: Clusters 1 and 3 showed smaller discrepancies between subjective and objective workload, while Cluster 1 notably underestimated task success relative to their actual performance. These findings demonstrate that clustering behavioral and physiological data can reveal meaningful cognitive stress response profiles and suggest that subjective-objective misalignment may serve as a potential marker of cognitive resilience or poor interoception. Introduction

Individual differences in the reflexive physiological reactivity to cognitive challenges, perceived threats, and stressors contribute to disease risk and resilience1–5. Acutely, enhanced stress reactions may lead to symptoms such as fatigue and anxiety, yet can also enhance task performance in the short term6. Over time, however, exaggerated reactions, whether in magnitude or duration, compromise performance and increase the subsequent risk for high blood pressure, burnout, heart disease, and other indices of poor health1,7–10. In laboratory research, exaggerated reactions to the paced serial addition test (PASAT), a cognitive challenge task, have been shown to predict coronary heart disease (CHD) mortality over a 16-year period11. These findings highlight the need to better characterize individual reactivity profiles that span both behavioral performance and autonomic responses, particularly under increasing cognitive load.

Measurement of stress is a challenge and is approached by a variety of methods. In laboratory settings, paradigms such as the Trier Social Stress Task, the cold pressor test, and the PASAT are used to induce social, physical, or cognitive stress, respectively12–14. Physiological responses to these laboratory stress-inducing tasks are typically recorded and analyzed in phases (baseline, stress exposure, recovery)15,16. Both exaggerated and blunted reactions to laboratory stress have been related to psychopathology, cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, traumatic experiences, patterns of health behavior, and social and demographic variables1, 2,10,11.

In medical settings, serum cortisol is commonly used as a purely physiological measure of stress17. Cortisol is often assessed as an awakening response with the area under the curve calculated across several morning measurements17,18. Finally, in the wild, wearable devices use proprietary algorithms on photoplethysmography sensor data to estimate stress load or periods of stress. Although these measures provide brief insights into stress, they do not reflect real-time performance or autonomic physiological responses. Understanding how a person performs while under active stress or challenge, beyond just measurement, could inform precision medicine or lifestyle changes to influence health outcomes associated with heightened stress reactivity2, 19.

It is well established that cognitive demand triggers reflexive physiological responses via the autonomic nervous system (ANS), linking rapid decision-making and frequent shifts in attention to measurable changes in heart rate and other autonomic markers20–22. Using this known relationship, Brooks et al. developed an algorithm using electrocardiogram (ECG) and electroencephalogram (EEG) data that generalizes across multiple cognitive tasks and participant groups. The algorithm makes second-by-second predictions of cognitive depletion, a construct that is sensitive to multitasking, mental and temporal demand, and task difficulty23. Building on this framework, we leveraged the previously validated ECG-based cognitive workload model, which quantifies cognitive demand from autonomic cardiac dynamics, to evaluate whether these continuous workload estimates could characterize consistent patterns of behavioral accuracy and physiological responsiveness across cognitive tasks.

Additional supporting literature has examined how workload and performance relate across diverse cognitive contexts. Heard et al. (2018) provide a comprehensive survey of workload assessment algorithms, detailing both physiological and machine-learning approaches used to infer mental workload from autonomic signals24. Louis et al. (2023) further demonstrate that cognitive-task performance covaries systematically with mental workload across difficulty levels and showed that clustering based on subjective workload can reveal meaningful subgroups25. Although neither study jointly clustered objective workload and objective accuracy, both lines of work conceptually may support the premise that combining physiological and behavioral data can reveal underlying response profiles.

Our approach aligns with the neuroergonomics framework, which emphasizes the measurement of neural and physiological processes during naturalistic, task-embedded human performance. Prior neuroergonomic work, particularly EEG-based assessments of mental effort26, has demonstrated the value of wearable neurophysiology for tracking cognitive workload. However, while EEG and electrodermal activity (EDA) offer rich information about neural activation and arousal, they require greater instrumentation and are more susceptible to motion and environmental artifacts. In contrast, ECG provides a robust, low-burden, and field-deployable measure of autonomic workload that is well suited for operational and mobile environments. By leveraging a validated ECG-based workload model, the present study demonstrates how a scalable physiological signal can support the identification of cognitive-efficiency profiles in applied settings.

Individual differences in physiological responses to cognitive challenges and stressors are crucial in psychological research, as it provides insight into why people react differently to similar situations3,7,27. Stress response research is used in our study to better understand these individual differences. Genetic, psychological, and sociocultural factors can influence these differences. For example, some individuals may have a heightened stress response due to genetic predispositions, leading to increased magnitude and/or duration of physiological reactions (blood pressure, cortisol, heart rate dynamics, skin conductance)28. Others might exhibit more resilient physiological responses, which can buffer against the negative effects of stress29. These variations can impact mental health outcomes, such as susceptibility to anxiety, depression, and other stress-related disorders30.

Despite advancements in neuroimaging technologies, the gold standard assessment of psychological experience remains self-reports, typically gathered through structured or semi-structured interviews, questionnaires, or state probes31,32. However, recent research has highlighted the need for more integrative frameworks that connect subjective state appraisals with objective measurements of physiological and/or behavioral function33. Studies have shown that healthy individuals and those with psychiatric conditions, such as depression, insomnia, schizophrenia, and fibromyalgia, often exhibit significant discrepancy between their subjective reports and objective assessments34–36. For example, many people with insomnia report long sleep-onset latency or sleep duration, even though polysomnography typically shows normal sleep. This discrepancy may prevent proper diagnosis and reduce treatment effectiveness33. Conversely, in other work, discordant, positively biased perceptions were linked to more positive outcomes, such as reduced morbidity and mortality3, 37,38. The contrasting forms of discrepancy lead to markedly different outcomes, underscoring the importance of understanding how similar mismatches in other health domains, such as perceived workload, could inform treatment pathways. Therefore, identifying the subjective-objective discrepancy (the difference between perceived and ECG-measured workload) for cognitive ability may help develop interventions to improve performance under workload or stress.

The concept of “biotypes” has emerged in psychology and psychiatry as a promising approach to provide a more global assessment of functional type, capturing the interplay between cognitive, physiological, and/or psychological processes that less integrated approaches would not capture. This framework is increasingly being applied to inform precision medicine approaches to treatment. For example, in psychiatry, biotypes have been used to identify subgroups within conditions like depression, addiction, insomnia, and schizophrenia, improving the prediction of treatment outcomes with a personalized approach of prevention and intervention strategies39,40. In this context, we invoke the use of biotype to express a conceptualization of high dimensional cognitive performance and reflexive cardiovascular physiology. Specifically, the cognitive battery used in this study engages various executive function networks across a range of difficulty levels in addition to a machine learning model that utilizes several features of autonomic physiology to generate an estimate of cognitive workload. Recent multivariable biotyping efforts, such as multisystem stress-response biotypes in adolescence39, neural-circuit biotypes predicting transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) responsiveness in depression41, multimodal psychiatric biotypes in major depression40 and cognitive biotype of depression in large-scale clinical trials42, typically integrate high-dimensional neural, behavioral, and autonomic measures. In contrast, the present framework represents a compact exploratory biotype structure derived from composite cognitive performance and reflexive cardiovascular workload.

We expected that a workload-integrated approach to cognitive challenge would reveal distinct cognitive biotypes, characterized by both performance and associated physiological responses. We anticipated systematic variation in cognitive task accuracy and workload, leading to four distinct phenotype clusters: (1) average-to-high accuracy, low workload; (2) average-to-high accuracy, high workload; (3) average to low accuracy, low workload; and (4) average to low accuracy and high workload. This expectation is grounded in the continuous nature of the workload algorithm. Objectively measured workload varies along a spectrum from high to low, reinforcing the idea that both accuracy and workload serve as primary determinants of biotypical differentiation. Although four clusters were hypothesized, analysis revealed a stable three-cluster solution, indicating that low-performing individuals did not stratify by workload. We also expected that accurate self-reported appraisals of cognitive function would covary with the performance–workload phenotypes described above, using ECG-derived workload as the ground truth. In particular, we expected Cluster 1 to show the least discrepancy between subjective ratings and objective measures, reflecting a more accurate or self-aware phenotype.

To determine whether demographic factors may influence cluster membership, we compared race, gender, and age across the three mode-cluster groups. The racial composition of the clusters was relatively even: Cluster 1 contained 37.5% Asian, 20.8% Black/African-American, 6.2% Hispanic/Latino, and 35.4% White participants. Cluster 2 was similarly composed (37.5% Asian, 28.1% Black/African-American, 9.4% Hispanic/Latino, 25.0% White), and Cluster 3 consisted of 41.7% Asian, 16.7% Black/African-American, 25.0% Hispanic/Latino, and 16.7% White participants. Gender composition appeared similar across Clusters 1 (F = 14.6%) and 2 (F = 18.8%), but females were overrepresented in Cluster 3 (F = 41.7%). Mean age was also similar across groups (Cluster 1: M = 20.96; Cluster 2: M = 21.81; Cluster 3: M = 21.00). Chi-square tests showed gender (χ²(2) = 4.50, p = 0.106) and race (χ²(6) = 5.65, p = 0.464) were not significantly associated with cluster membership. A multinomial logistic regression including race, gender, and age likewise failed to predict cluster assignment (LLR p = 0.437; pseudo-R² = 0.056; all individual predictors p > 0.05). These findings indicate that the clusters were not driven by demographic differences.

An important observation from this clustering approach is that Cluster 3 was overrepresented in tasks with hard difficulty levels. This suggests that participants who were classified into Cluster 3 did not solely struggle with hard tasks but also demonstrated lower accuracy even at easy and medium difficulty levels. This pattern suggests that participants in Cluster 3 may have adopted a disengagement strategy or blunted physiological reactivity compared to Cluster 1, which had low workload but high task accuracy, particularly as task difficulty increased, leading to their overall lower workload values.

We used the Coefficient of Variation (CV) to assess variability in three key metrics: accuracy, workload, and their interaction (calculated as accuracy × workload). CV was computed as the standard deviation divided by the mean, multiplied by 100. The CV values were 30.13% for accuracy, 68.55% for workload, and 85.79% for the interaction term. The higher CV for the interaction indicates that combining accuracy and workload introduces greater variability than either metric alone.

Subjective-objective comparisons will vary by cluster

To test the hypothesis that Cluster 1 would show less discrepancy between subjective state probes and objective measures than the other clusters, we examined the discrepancy metrics for mental demand, temporal demand, and success across the three modal clusters (Clusters 1, 2, and 3). Assumption checks showed that the clusters had similar variances for all metrics (Levene’s ps ≥ 0.59), but some of the variables did not meet normality assumptions based on the Shapiro–Wilk tests (ps < 0.05). Therefore, we used Kruskal–Wallis tests for the temporal demand and success discrepancies, and a one-way ANOVA for the mental demand discrepancy, which met normality based on the Shapiro–Wilk test (ps ≥ 0.087). The residual degrees of freedom come from the total number of valid data rows (each row representing one trial across 3 tasks × 3 difficulty levels). Although the study design would yield 900 rows (100 participants × 9 trials), some trials were missing or dropped due to poor data quality, leaving 887 usable data points in the dataset. In a one-way ANOVA with 3 modal clusters, the residual degrees of freedom was calculated as N − k, so 887 − 3 = 884. For workload-based discrepancies, 2 trials had missing workload values, giving a maximum of 882 usable rows. Overall, 13 rows were missing from the original 900 due to missingness or quality filtering, which explains why the final degrees of freedom values differ across discrepancy variables.

The results revealed a significant effect of Biotype on the Subjective Temporal Demand–Objective Workload discrepancy metric, χ²(2) = 80.2, p < 0.001. Dunn post hoc tests showed that Cluster 2 had greater discrepancy than both Clusters 1 and 3 (ps < 0.05), while Clusters 1 and 3 did not differ (p = 0.078) (see Fig. 4). Post hoc comparisons using the Tukey HSD showed the same pattern, Cluster 2 had more discrepancy than Cluster 1 and 3. Finally, the effect of Biotype on the Subjective Success–Objective Accuracy discrepancy metric was significant, χ²(2) = 16.2, p < 0.001. Dunn post hoc tests indicated that Cluster 1 had more discrepancy on the success metric than both Clusters 2 and 3 (ps < 0.05).

Discussion

To identify cognitive biotypes, we clustered participants based on performance and ECG-derived cognitive workload dynamics and examined discrepancies between subjective and objective ratings of temporal demand, mental demand, and success during the tasks. We found support for the concept of cognitive biotypes through the identification of three, not four, distinct performance x cognitive workload groups. The hypothesis that the highest-performing and most efficient cluster would show the least discrepancy was partially supported. The findings provide insights into cognitive task appraisal, physiological cost, and perceptual bias, offering both theoretical and practical implications for stress and workload assessment and research.

The results generally supported the hypothesis that distinct phenotypes emerge based on cognitive performance and physiological workload. Using K-Medoids clustering, three clusters were identified, reflecting variability in accuracy and workload profiles across participants43. These clusters demonstrate the value of integrating behavioral and physiological measurements in addition to subjective and objective comparisons of the same phenomena in understanding cognitive task performance and associated stress. For example, Cluster 1 participants exhibited high accuracy and low workload, aligning with a more efficient, or “cognitively fit”, phenotype44–46. Conversely, Cluster 3 showed lower accuracy and variable workload, suggesting a blunted response of physiological activity and indicating a less efficient and potentially maladaptive response to cognitive challenges. The finding of three clusters, rather than the expected four, suggests that low-performing individuals do not split into distinct subgroups based on workload levels. This suggests that below average performance may reflect a general difficulty adapting to cognitive challenges, regardless of workload intensity, warranting further study47.

Research on cognitive phenotyping suggests that maladaptive responses may cluster around limited adaptability or inefficient resource allocation, which could explain the lack of further differentiation among low-performing individuals45–48. The observed workload stabilization across difficulty levels suggests that participants may have reached a self-imposed effort threshold, limiting additional cognitive resources as tasks became harder. This suggests that interventions targeting low performers may benefit from addressing broader cognitive and physiological adaptability rather than workload-specific patterns49–53.

Additionally, the findings revealed significant objective-subjective discrepancies in workload appraisals across clusters. Specifically, participants in Cluster 1 reported a large discrepancy between the subjective state probe for task success and the objective accuracy performance, indicating they underestimated their success. This finding is consistent with the Dunning-Kruger effect where low and high performing individuals over and underestimate their performance capabilities respectively54–56. This cognitive bias may offer an explanation to the observed discrepancy through the lens of interoception, which is the ability to sense the internal state of the body57. High performers on cognitive tasks may struggle to accurately perceive their capability, suggesting a disconnect between their cognitive workload and actual performance. Similarly, Clusters 2 and 3 underestimated their success as well but to a lesser extent. Evidence of poor interoception in Cluster 2 is seen in the larger temporal and mental demand discrepancies compared to the other clusters, indicating they may not have accurately perceived bodily signals during high cognitive workload despite performing well. This negative perception may reflect higher cognitive workload, lower efficiency, or other factors like mood or poor interoception57.

A predictive-processing view of interoception (EPIC) suggests that subjective feelings of effort or stress reflect top-down predictions about bodily state that are only partly constrained by incoming signals, so perceptual bias can emerge when expectations outweigh sensory evidence58. Consistent with this, a systematic review of daily-life studies found that only ~ 35% of analyses showed the expected association between self-reported stress and cardiovascular indices, with substantial heterogeneity across methods and measures59. Likewise, a recent study relating perceived stress to cortisol reported inconsistent associations overall60. Together, these findings highlight why discrepancies between subjective and objective workload/stress measures are not only common but theoretically expected, and why contextual and mechanistic explanations (e.g., interoceptive precision, measurement specificity) are essential when interpreting such mismatches.

These findings contribute to a growing body of evidence on individual differences in performance under stress1,61–65. The identification of functionally distinct phenotypes highlights the importance of personalized approaches to studying workload and task performance. By integrating ECG-based workload estimation with subjective state probes, this study addresses the limitations of unitary measures of stress or performance, offering a more nuanced understanding of task appraisal dynamics. For example, a recent meta-analysis found that age-related declines in cognitive flexibility were linked to both decreased and increased activation across several brain networks66. This suggests that variability in cognitive flexibility could contribute to differences in task performance and workload management among individuals and warrants further investigation.

Beyond their theoretical relevance, these exploratory biotypes could have important implications for adaptive systems and human-machine interface design. Real-time ECG-derived workload estimates could be integrated into training platforms, decision-support tools, and operational environments to dynamically adjust task pacing, information flow, or assistance levels. Individuals showing high performance but elevated workload (e.g., Cluster 2) may benefit from adaptive systems that modulate task demands during periods of increased effort. Conversely, individuals with low performance and low-to-moderate workload (Cluster 3) may require systems that increase engagement or provide targeted support to maintain performance. By linking behavioral performance with physiological workload, these exploratory biotypes offer a framework for tailoring interfaces and adaptive automation to individual cognitive response patterns, supporting safer, more resilient, and more efficient human-machine teaming.

The current study underscores the utility of physiological workload models, particularly ECG-based algorithms, in assessing cognitive workload. Compared to more invasive or logistically complex methods like EEG, ECG data provide a practical and scalable approach to monitoring real-time cognitive workload in various settings, including workplace, military, and educational environments. Our approach has potential applications in designing personalized interventions to enhance performance and mitigate stress-related health risks over time. For example, a recent study by Tozzi et al. identified a cognitive biotype of depression marked by treatment resistance, impaired cognitive control, and dysfunction in cognitive control brain circuits41. Individuals with weak baseline connectivity improved following TMS, while those with strong connectivity did not, indicating that cognitive biotypes may differ in treatment response. Our Cluster 3 individuals, characterized by low performance with a range of low to moderate workload, reflect patterns identified by Tozzi et al. and warrant further investigation for more targeted performance-enhancing interventions. Emerging research suggests that the future of psychological interventions aimed at improving performance and maximizing functionality may lie in the application of neurostimulation techniques67. For example, studies have demonstrated that transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) can improve cognitive flexibility68and working memory69, while TMS has shown promise in enhancing attention and reducing symptoms of depression70 and insomnia71. Future investigation could explore the effectiveness of TMS and the three identified exploratory biotypes of cognitive workload.

The difficulty levels in each task were designed to increase the amount of mental effort required. In the Enumeration task, this was done by shortening the stimulus display time and increasing the number of items to count. In the Task Switching task, the response time was reduced as the task became more difficult. In the working memory task, difficulty increased as the sequences became longer, requiring participants to remember more items. As shown in Fig. 1b, these manipulations were reflected in modest, consistent increases in workload probability from easy to hard, suggesting that higher task demands were experienced as more challenging. Both exaggerated and blunted cognitive workload responses have been linked to adverse outcomes, including psychopathology and cardiovascular risk1,11,72. By characterizing how individuals respond to cognitive challenges at varying levels of difficulty, this study provides a foundation for understanding how workload-related phenotypes or biotypes may inform precision health interventions39,40,65,73,74. These exploratory biotypes suggest that participants in Cluster 3 could benefit from targeted strategies to improve task efficacy and reduce workload-related stress, whereas participants in Cluster 2 would benefit mostly from workload reduction under challenge9,75.

Conversely, Cluster 1 participants, who demonstrate efficient performance and accurate self-appraisal for mental and temporal demand but underestimate success, could serve as a model for interventions aimed at enhancing resilience and adaptive task engagement. These findings align with prior work showing similar subjective–objective discrepancies in both healthy individuals and clinical populations34–36.

A major strength of this study is the integration of subjective and objective measures, which provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating cognitive workload. The use of ECG-based workload estimation offers a practical and non-invasive approach to assessing physiological responses, while state probes capture subjective perceptions of stress, challenge, and success. This integrated approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of the interplay between perceived and actual levels of workload. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study sample consists of college students who were predominantly young, and male which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. While our sample was predominantly young and male, prior work shows that autonomic reactivity, heart-rate dynamics, and perceived workload can vary meaningfully across age and gender76. Therefore, the exploratory biotypes identified in this study may not generalize fully to older individuals or to more gender-balanced groups, underscoring the need for replication in more diverse cohorts. Future research should explore these dynamics across more diverse demographic groups across the lifespan with equal representation across genders77. Additionally, while the K-Medoids clustering methods proved effective in identifying distinct biotypes, the stability of these over time remains to be determined.

Future research should build on these findings by examining the longitudinal implications of workload-related biotypes on health and performance outcomes39,40. Future work could test whether the higher autonomic cost seen in Clusters 2 and 3 predicts greater long-term risk for stress-related conditions, including cardiovascular disease, stroke, diabetes, gastrointestinal disorders, mood and anxiety disorders, autoimmune disease, or chronic pain, compared with Cluster 19,30,78,79. Similarly, interventions aimed at reducing workload under challenge like exercise, sleep, mindfulness, paced breathing under load could be differentially “prescribed” based on cluster assignment39,46,80,81,82. Similarly interventions aimed at reducing perceptual discrepancies such as biofeedback, cognitive behavioral therapy could be prescribed for individuals with notable perceptual bias35,83–87. Finally, for individuals with Cluster 3 performances that fall below average interventions such as neurostimulation including photobiomodulation could be evaluated for their effectiveness in “resetting” brain networks involved with cognitive control and flexibility74,88–91.

Another promising direction is the application of these findings to real-world settings. For example, integrating ECG-based workload monitoring into wearable devices could enable continuous assessment of cognitive demands, providing insights for individuals in high-stress and high-demand occupational environments22,27,92– 94.

Method

Participants

Participants were young adults (N = 100; M Age = 21.38, SD = 3.27), racially diverse (37% Asian, 31% White/Caucasian, 21% Black/African, 8% Hispanic), and mostly male (76%). An overview of the study design and analysis pipeline is presented in Fig. 5. Participants who did not follow study instructions, such as failing to complete tasks (n = 1), failing to remain seated (n = 1), or falling asleep (n = 3) during testing, were excluded from the analysis.

Study Design and Analysis Pipeline. The illustrated study protocol shows participants following the baseline phase to the testing phase in the same session where objective cognitive workload (ECG data) was continuously measured. In the analysis phase, task accuracy and workload metrics were clustered into three biotypes, and perceptual discrepancy was calculated as the difference between subjective state probes and objective measures. Color coding: yellow represents baseline-specific tasks; blue represents continuous objective workload monitoring; orange represents active participant behavior such as cognitive task performance and responding to subjective probes.

Participants were recruited from the University of Maryland, Baltimore County through flyers and social media advertisements. Eligibility criteria included prior experience with e-sports games; although e-sports performance data were collected, these results will be reported in a separate publication. All participants were at least 18 years of age and proficient in speaking, reading, and writing English. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All study procedures were reviewed and approved through the University of Maryland, Baltimore County Department of Human Research Protections and Integrity (HRPI), which supports the Institutional Review Board (IRB). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants received monetary compensation for their time.

Procedure

After providing consent, participants completed a 60-minute laboratory session (Fig. 5). First, brief study questionnaires (demographic information, and questions about video game history (not reported here)) were completed on a desktop computer. Following this, participants were instructed to remain seated, at the same computer, for a 5-minute, baseline recording.

Following the baseline period, participants completed three cognitive tasks (< 15 min each) on the same computer, in random order, while cardiovascular activity was recorded. For the first round of each cognitive task, there was a practice round that could be repeated to ensure each participant understood the task. Each cognitive task was organized into blocks that consisted of three varying levels of difficulty (easy, medium, and hard). Task difficulty was manipulated by reducing the amount of time to view the stimulus or increasing the number of stimuli to remember. More detail on difficulty manipulation for each task is explained in their corresponding section below. Participants completed a set of state probes that asked them to separately indicate how challenging, successful, stressful, mentally demanding, and hurried the level or block was after completion. Participants repeated this until all difficulty levels were completed for the one task. They repeated these steps until all three tasks were completed. Participants completed nine total levels, three for each task, in randomized order.

Equipment

Electrocardiography sensors

Heart activity was recorded using a custom-built ECG device created by our team. The device consisted of an Arduino box (Lakewood, NJ, USA) connected to a set of wired electrodes with three leads. Each lead was attached to a disposable sticker placed below the left and right clavicles (near the shoulders) and on the lower left abdomen. The box was installed under the table, allowing participants to manually connect the electrode wires after placing the adhesive stickers. This setup provided a secure and minimally invasive connection for data collection.

Recording, signal processing, workload calculation

The ECG signals were collected using a 3-channel configuration at a sampling rate of 250 Hz. In total, approximately 62.5 h of usable ECG data were obtained. Although the theoretical maximum duration was higher, several trials were missing or discarded due to poor signal quality, participant movement, or incomplete task sessions.

The data were segmented into 30-second windows with a 1-second increment. All signals were filtered using a zero-phase 0.25–30 Hz bandpass filter (butter + filtfilt, scipy.signal) from scipy.signal in Python 3.1195. Signal quality was assessed by computing the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for each window; windows with SNR < 4 dB were removed. R-peaks were detected using the Engzeemod2012 algorithm in NeuroKit2 (v0.2.2)96. Key ECG features, which include mean, maximum, and minimum heart rate as well as heart rate variability, were extracted for each valid 30-second window using NeuroKit2. These features were standardized relative to each participant’s baseline to reduce inter-individual variability and ensure physiologically comparable inputs across participants.

Workload model development

Previously, we developed models to detect cognitive resource depletion or workload using physiological features23. Two versions of the model were evaluated: one integrating both EEG and ECG data and another relying solely on ECG data. For the present study, we selected the ECG-only model due to its practicality, minimal instrumentation requirements, and demonstrated reliability in detecting cognitive workload.

Model inputs and physiological feature extraction

The ECG-only workload model used in this study operated on four autonomic features derived from the ECG waveform: mean heart rate, maximum heart rate, minimum heart rate, and heart-rate variability (RMSSD). These features capture sympathetic-parasympathetic balance and reflect well-established cardiovascular correlates of cognitive demand. To ensure physiologically valid inputs, we applied constraints excluding heart rates below 25 bpm or above 175 bpm, as well as RMSSD values exceeding 300 ms; invalid values were replaced with the most recent valid observation to maintain continuity. These preprocessing steps ensured that only clean, physiologically plausible signals contributed to feature calculations and model inference.

Model architecture, training framework, and optimization

The classifier consisted of two fully connected hidden layers with 20 units each, using ReLU activations to model nonlinear relationships between autonomic signals and workload. The final layer used a sigmoid activation to generate continuous 0–1 probabilities of high workload. To promote generalization and reduce overfitting, we applied L1 (0.003) and L2 (0.01) regularization. The loss function was a weighted binary cross-entropy in which false negatives were penalized twice as strongly as false positives, reflecting the higher cost of failing to detect true high-workload states. Hyperparameters and regularization strengths were optimized using learning-curve diagnostics. To guarantee strict subject-level independence, training followed a Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) protocol: each participant served once as a held-out test case, while the model was trained exclusively on all remaining participants. This approach eliminates subject-level leakage and provides robust out-of-sample performance estimates.

Model performance

In the DualTask experiment, the objective was to determine, on a second-by-second basis, whether the participant was in the dual-task state or not. Across LOSO folds from 45 subjects, the ECG-only workload model achieved an accuracy of 0.84, and a Class-1 (high-workload) precision of 0.47, recall of 0.25, F1-score of 0.33, and MCC of 0.26. High-workload periods comprise only ~ 14% of the physiological time series, making this an imbalanced and challenging detection problem. Further, because the model outputs continuous autonomic responses, predictions will never follow the rectangular shape of the target annotation; instead, probability traces rise and fall gradually as physiological state evolves. Under this class distribution, the expected F1-score from a random classifier is approximately 0.14, meaning the model’s observed F1-score of 0.33 is more than twice chance and therefore reflects meaningful physiological structure rather than random fluctuations. The model produces continuous, second-by-second workload probability, with values above 0.5 considered high workload. As illustrated in Fig. 6, prediction traces from three representative participants show low false-positive activity during baseline segments and clear probability increases during the annotated high-workload interval (blue box).

Example second-by-second workload probability traces for three representative participants. Each panel shows the model’s continuous probability estimates of high workload (0–1) across the trial. The blue shaded region denotes the high-workload segment. In all subjects, the model exhibits a clear probability increase during the high-workload interval, demonstrating appropriate physiological reactivity.

However, the comparatively modest F1 and MCC values reflect the strictness of these metrics when used with dense physiological time-series. Even small temporal misalignments, such as the prediction rising slightly before the annotated workload onset or decaying shortly after, are treated as full false positives or negatives. These brief offsets are common in continuous physiological recordings and do not indicate poor model behavior, making F1 and MCC conservative relative to the temporal prediction patterns shown in Fig. 6. For example, in Subject C, the probability trace rises sharply throughout the high-workload segment despite the modest MCC and F1-score value (~ 0.30 at both).

Threshold-based workload separation

To quantify the model’s sensitivity to cognitive demand, we calculated the proportion of samples in which the predicted workload probability exceeded the 0.5 threshold during low-workload (P0) and high-workload (P1) intervals. Shapiro-Wilk normality tests indicated that neither distribution was normally distributed (P0: W = 0.893, p = 0.001; P1: W = 0.892, p = 0.0008), so we used a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, which revealed a significant difference between the conditions (p = 0.004). As shown in Fig. 7, above-threshold outputs increased from 0.042 ± 0.011 during P0 to 0.249 ± 0.073 during P1, representing nearly a sixfold increase. This marked shift indicates that the model produces substantially higher workload activations during cognitively demanding periods compared with baseline.

Permutation-based feature importance

To further assess interpretability, we quantified the contribution of each physiological feature using permutation-based importance analysis97, 98. For each LOSO test fold, we replaced one feature at a time with random noise drawn from the empirical distribution of that feature, and we measured the resulting decrease in F1-score on the held-out subject. Larger drops indicate features that the model relies on more strongly for workload discrimination. This analysis shows that mean heart rate produced the largest reduction in F1 (ΔF1 = 0.14), followed by minimum heart rate (ΔF1 = 0.05), heart-rate variability (RMSSD) (ΔF1 = 0.05), and maximum heart rate (ΔF1 = 0.03).

Baseline acclimation period and ECG

To collect resting baseline data, 5 min was recorded while the participant watched a relaxing video of a lava lamp available on YouTube (https://youtu.be/h_lQ2tMgLVM). Before the recording, participants were instructed to adhere three adhesive ECG electrodes: one below the clavicle near the right shoulder, one below the clavicle near the left shoulder, and one on the lower left abdomen. Participants were asked to keep both feet on the floor and remain seated.

Cognitive tasks and physiologic cost assessment

The cognitive tasks were developed from an open-source cognitive battery published by Dr. Helen Sauzeon and her colleagues at the University of Bordeaux99. We adapted their battery into an abbreviated set integrated with an ECG sensor and expanded their code so that task difficulty was manipulated into blocks of easy, medium, and hard levels randomized on each administration. The ECG signal was processed by a machine learning algorithm developed by our group that estimates cognitive workload from ECG data and predicts NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) workload and temporal demand23. Completion of each of the three tasks generated a raw accuracy score for each level of task difficulty (easy, medium, and hard for Tasks 1, 2, and 3). Cognitive Biotypes were generated for overall performance that characterizes the physiologic cost of performance (ECG reaction/cognitive depletion) on the cognitive battery across all tasks and difficulty levels. Proportional variation in gender and race across Cognitive Biotypes was examined in Python using cross-tabulations and Chi-square tests (scipy), and multinomial logistic regression (statsmodels) was used to evaluate whether gender, race, or age predicted biotype membership.

Enumeration task

Enumeration measures counting ability99,100. Trials began with a fixation cross in the center of the screen. Participants were instructed to look at the center of the screen, count the number of circles (varied between 5 and 9), and answer using a mouse click. Difficulty was manipulated across three conditions by decreasing the display stimulus time (easy = 500 ms, medium = 250 ms, hard = 50 ms). Each trial resulted in a binary outcome: success or failure. A trial was considered successful if the participant’s response matched the number of circles presented. Accuracy was computed as the number of successful trials divided by the total number of trials. Each participant completed 20 trials per stimulus condition, for a total of 60 Enumeration trials.

Task switching (TS) cognitive task

TS measures the flexibility of selective attention and shifting attention between different goals99,101. In this task, participants were asked to indicate if the target digit was odd/even or if the target digit was higher/lower than five as a function of the color of the task cue. Digits 1–4 and 6–9 were used for target stimuli. Tasks changed as a function of the color and shape of the task cue. Trials were either an even/odd task or a high/low task. When task cues were red and square, participants answered whether a target digit was odd/even by pressing a specific key. When cues were blue and diamond-shaped, a different key was pressed. Task difficulty was manipulated across three conditions (easy, medium, and hard) by reducing the time participants had to respond. Participants had a longer response window of 3000 ms in the easy condition, a moderate response window of 2000 ms in the medium condition, and a limited 1000 ms response window in the hard condition, making it increasingly difficult to process the cue and respond accurately before the deadline.

Each trial resulted in a binary outcome: success or failure. A trial was considered successful if the participant provided the correct response of the tasks. A failure outcome occurred either due to an incorrect response or no response within the reaction time deadline. Accuracy was computed by dividing the number of successful trials by the total number of trials. Each participant completed 80 trials per stimulus condition, for a total of 240 TS trials.

Spatial working memory task

This task assesses visual-spatial short-term memory capacity99,102,103. For each trial, 1 of 16 squares were colored red briefly (other squares are gray) in a sequential pattern. A new grid of non-colored squares was shown and participants were asked to “replay” the pattern of red squares by clicking on the squares colored red in order on the prior display. Task difficulty was manipulated across three conditions (easy, medium, and hard) by increasing the number of red squares in the sequence, 4 for easy, 6 for medium, and 8 for hard, which made it progressively more difficult to recall and accurately reproduce the full sequence. Each trial had a percentage outcome ranging from 0 to 1, represented by the number of squares answered in the correct order divided by the total number of squares. Accuracy was calculated as the average score across all trials. A score of 0 indicated that all responses were incorrect, while a score of 1 indicated that all responses were correct. Each participant completed 20 trials per stimulus condition, for a total of 60 Spatial Working Memory trials.

State probes

To capture individual differences in state appraisal during the tasks, at the end of each level, the screen paused, and the participant was prompted to complete several brief questions about their level of perceived stress and frustration, challenge, temporal, and mental demand that best represented their state during the level immediately preceding the prompt. These dimensions are derived from the NASA-TLX to assess subjective experience of workload23,104. The response option for each state probe was a Likert scale of 0–20 with 0 conveying no stress, challenge, or mental demand and 20 conveying the highest level. State probe responses were used to generate metrics of subjective-objective discrepancy/perceptual bias to test the second hypothesis.

To test the hypothesis that discrepancies between subjective-objective assessments during tasking would covary with biotypes we generated the following metrics using the difference between normalized (subjective) state probe responses and the corresponding (more objective) measure for three comparisons (1) the success discrepancy metric was the state success probe minus objective task accuracy, (2) the cognitive workload discrepancy metric was calculated as the subjective workload (mental demand) state probe minus workload from the ECG algorithm, and (3) the temporal demand discrepancy metric included the temporal demand probe minus cognitive workload from the algorithm. For each metric, positive values indicated over-estimation. Negative values indicated underestimation. We decided not to calculate discrepancy metrics for the stress or challenge state probes to avoid overlapping analyses of related measures, which can make the results harder to interpret. Instead, we focused on mental and temporal demand, as these items have consistently been linked to cognitive fatigue during task performance in previous research23. Brooks et al. found that higher perceived mental and temporal demand were associated with reduced task accuracy and signs of cognitive depletion. Focusing on these measures helped keep the analysis aligned with our goal.

Modeling individual workload dynamics

To analyze workload dynamics, we employed a clustering-based analytic approach. We computed workload and accuracy metrics for all participants, with each participant contributing nine data points (three cognitive tasks × three difficulty levels). These data were clustered using the K-medoids algorithm. The model generated three clusters, which is the optimal number of clusters. Each cluster shows unique characteristics and statistically meaningful differences from the others.

Clustering model

The clustering model incorporated two feature sets: workload and accuracy. For the workload data, we obtained raw workload values from the workload model, which were then baseline-corrected by subtracting each participant’s baseline workload from their in-task workload. This produced the change of workload relative to baseline, capturing task-related behavioral shifts. Then, we standardized across the entire dataset to ensure equal weighting with accuracy for clustering approach.

For the accuracy data, z-score standardization was applied within each task type to account for performance variability across tasks. These standardized accuracy values were then rescaled to match the scale of the workload data.

We employed the K-Medoids algorithm for clustering due to its robustness in handling noise and outliers. Unlike K-Means, K-Medoids identifies actual data points as cluster centers, providing more interpretable outcomes by minimizing dissimilarities rather than squared distances43. Clustering was implemented using the scikit-learn-extra package105 in Python 3.11.

To determine the optimal number of clusters, we conducted:

Silhouette Analysis: We computed the mean Silhouette Coefficient of all samples for each k. The 3-cluster solution yielded a silhouette coefficient of 0.33, reflecting moderate but expected overlap for behavioral and physiological data. Silhouette values near + 1 indicate well-separated clusters, values near 0 indicate overlapping clusters, and negative values suggest misclassification.

Centroid proximity index (CPI)

CPI measures the average distance of points within a cluster to its centroid, with smaller distances indicating stronger clusters. Among the three clusters, the one in the upper-left quadrant of Fig. 2, characterized by higher accuracy and lower workload, had the smallest CPI.

Statistical analysis plan

Each participant was assigned to a mode cluster based on their most frequently occurring cluster assignment. However, their ratings for mental demand, temporal demand, and success were recorded separately for each of the three games and three difficulty levels, leading to multiple data points per participant. For analysis, we used the mode cluster to process all data points, ensuring that each rating was categorized according to the participant’s dominant cluster while preserving the variability across task conditions. All statistical tests were performed in R (version 4.4.1), with one-way ANOVAs run using the aov() function from the base stats package. Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed using the kruskal_test() function from the rstatix package. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn tests for the Kruskal-Wallis analyses and Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test for the ANOVAs. Effect sizes (η²) were calculated using the eta_squared() function from the effectsize package, with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Since η² ranges from 0 to 1, the effectsize package truncates upper confidence interval limits that exceed 1.00, which is why the reported CIs show 1.00 as the upper bound. In the package output, this appears as ‘One-sided CIs: upper bound fixed at [1.00],’ indicating that any CI value above 1.00 is capped at the maximum allowable value106.
