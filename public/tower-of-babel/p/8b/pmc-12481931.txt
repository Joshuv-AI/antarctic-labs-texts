High-intensity interval training improves cognitive dysfunction in chronically stressed mice through alleviating homocysteine-induced transcriptional repression of Cldn5
Authors: Zhao-Xin Sun, Mao-Yang Zhou, Jin-Shan Li, Yun Zhao, Fang Xie, Xue Wang, Hong Feng, Zhao-Wei Sun, Ling-Jia Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic stress-induced blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction contributes to neurological disorders, with homocysteine (HCY) as a key risk factor. Considering pharmacotherapy limitations, non-invasive interventions like high-intensity interval training (HIIT) are promising. To determine whether HIIT improves stress-induced BBB dysfunction and cognitive impairment, we first established a chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model and assigned mice into four groups: Control (Ctrl), CUMS, HIIT, and HIIT + CUMS. Here, we found that HIIT significantly ameliorated cognitive impairment in male CUMS mice, as evidenced by reduced escape latency in morris water maze and increased memory performance in novel object recognition test. HIIT also preserved BBB integrity by ameliorating the tight junction disruption and BBB hyper-permeability in stressed mice. Subsequently, to clarify the role of HCY in the HIIT-mediated effects, we established an HHCY model and divided mice into four groups: Ctrl, HIIT, HHCY, and HIIT + HHCY. The results showed that HIIT normalized the plasma and hippocampal HCY levels by restoring the expression of related metabolic enzymes including CBS, MTHFR and MS, and alleviated HHCY-induced cognitive decline and BBB damage. Further, HIIT reversed HCY-induced Claudin-5 downregulation by inhibiting H3K27me3 enrichment at the Cldn5 (the encoding gene of Claudin-5) promoter region. In addition, HIIT restored the expression of ETS1, one of the transcriptional activators of Cldn5, to facilitate the transcription of Cldn5 gene and the stabilization of BBB. Collectively, these findings reveal that HIIT improves chronic stress-induced cognitive impairment via eliminating the disruptive effects of HHCY on the BBB integrity, offering a non-pharmacological intervention potential for stress-related cognitive deficits.Keywords: HIIT, Stress-induced cognitive dysfunction, BBB damage, HCY, Cldn5 transcriptional repression

Introduction

Chronic stress has been widely recognized as a key factor contributing to cognitive impairment and is closely associated with the onset and progression of various neurological disorders(McEwen, 2007). Accumulating evidence have shown that chronic stress induces neuronal damage, reduces synaptic plasticity, and leads to hippocampal atrophy, which in turn triggers neuropsychiatric conditions such as anxiety and depression(Li et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2022). Moreover, chronic stress significantly affects learning and memory abilities, which is not only linked to neuronal damage but also involves alterations in the brain microenvironment. Recently, blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction has been identified as a critical link between chronic stress and cognitive impairment(Dion-Albert et al., 2022; Matsuno et al., 2022). The BBB is composed of endothelial tight junctions (TJs), pericytes, and astrocytic end-feet, and its integrity is essential for maintaining brain homeostasis(Chen et al., 2024; Zlokovic, 2008). Studies have shown that chronic stress is sufficient to compromise BBB integrity such as disrupting TJs to facilitate the infiltration of peripheral inflammatory factors and neurotoxic substances into the brain parenchyma, resulting in the neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction(Dion-Albert et al., 2022; Menard et al., 2017).

Under physiological conditions, methionine metabolism proceeds via the transsulfuration and transmethylation pathways, mediated by key enzymes such as MS, CSE, CBS, MTHFR, and BHMT (the latter being absent in the brain), as well as various cofactors. This process is involved in the methylation of DNA, RNA, proteins, and lipids, playing a crucial role in regulating gene expression, maintaining neural homeostasis, and controlling cell proliferation and apoptosis(Li et al., 2020). When the methionine cycle is disrupted, for example due to abnormalities in key enzymes, homocysteine (HCY) levels may increase, potentially resulting in hyperhomocysteinemia (HHCY). HHCY can contribute to the development of neurological disorders such as cognitive decline and dementia by inducing oxidative stress and aberrant DNA methylation(Jan et al., 2021). Studies have shown that Hcy levels rise significantly under stress conditions(Wang et al., 2022). Notably, upregulated HCY levels are implicated in the brain endothelial dysfunction and BBB damage(Beard et al., 2011; Kamath et al., 2006). Thus, modulating HCY levels may provide a promising therapeutic strategy for alleviating stress-induced cognitive impairment.

Current pharmacological interventions for stress-associated neurological disorders such as antidepressants and antioxidants are often limited by their efficacy and high financial costs, driving the exploration of more economical, effective, and non-invasive alternatives. Among non-pharmacological strategies, exercise therapy has gained significant attention due to its safety and multi-target effects. High-intensity interval training (HIIT), a time-saving and efficient exercise modality, has demonstrated remarkable neuroprotective effects. For example, clinical trials have shown that 6-months HIIT can significantly improve hippocampus-dependent memory function and sustain cognitive benefits for up to 5 years(Blackmore et al., 2024). Additionally, HIIT can alleviate symptoms of cerebrovascular diseases such as vascular dementia and stroke as well(Anjos et al., 2022; Crozier et al., 2018). However, despite its promising neuroprotective potential, the precise mechanisms through which HIIT mitigates cognitive decline and regulates neurovascular function are not fully understood.

Herein, we reveal the regulatory role of HIIT in stress-induced hyperhomocysteinemia and BBB epigenetic dysregulation, aiming to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms by which HIIT improves cognitive function. To this end, we first established a chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model to investigate whether HIIT alleviates stress-induced BBB damage and cognitive impairment, with mice randomly assigned into four groups: Control (Ctrl), CUMS, HIIT, and HIIT + CUMS. We found that HIIT significantly improved cognitive impairment and stabilized BBB integrity in male CUMS mice. Moreover, to clarify the role of HCY in these effects of HIIT, we established an HHCY model and divided mice into four groups: Ctrl, HIIT, HHCY, and HIIT + HHCY. HIIT could successfully reduce the plasma and hippocampal HCY levels by restoring the expression of related metabolic enzymes including CBS, MTHFR and MS, and attenuated HHCY-induced cognitive decline and BBB damage. Further, HIIT blocked the recruitment of H3K27me3 at the Cldn5 promoter region to restore its transcription. Also, HIIT could enhance the expression of ETS1, one of the transcriptional activators of Cldn5, to promote BBB integrity. Taken together, the findings will provide theoretical insights and experimental evidence for developing novel non-invasive intervention strategies to mitigate stress-induced cognitive impairment.

Materials and methods

Animals

6-weeks-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of the Academy of Military Sciences (AMS) and randomly divided into different groups. All mice were bred and maintained according to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 8023). Experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the AMS (IACUC-DWZX-2022-738).

Experimental groups and sample sizes

Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups as follows: for the CUMS model, groups included Ctrl (n = 14), CUMS (n = 14), HIIT (n = 14), and CUMS + HIIT (n = 14); for the HHCY model, groups were Ctrl (n = 14), HHCY (n = 14), HIIT (n = 14), and HIIT + HHCY (n = 14). Behavioral experiments (e.g., Morris water maze, open field test) were conducted using the same cohort of mice (n = 14 per group). Following the behavioral assessment, mice from each group were randomly allocated into different subgroups for tissue analyses to ensure unbiased sampling. Sample sizes for biochemical and histological analyses (e.g., ELISA, Western blot, immunofluorescence) are specified in the figure legends. Independent biological replicates were performed as described.

Chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS)

The chronically stressed mice models were established using the CUMS paradigm because CUMS mimics the complexity and unpredictability of stressors encountered in daily life, thereby preventing habituation and producing robust, reproducible phenotypes(Shi et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2025). Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that exposure to CUMS for 6 weeks is sufficient to produce stable alterations in hippocampal structure and function, and the impairment of BBB integrity, while minimizing excessive physiological deterioration or mortality(Dandekar et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2024). In our experiment, CUMS groups were exposed to one of the following chronic unpredictable mild stressors: swimming in cold water (4 °C) (2 min), food and water deprivation (12 h), restraint (8 h), foot shock (2 min), soiled cage (with water), cage tilt, and breaking circadian rhythms. These stressors were administered randomly daily over a period of 6 weeks in. To avoid the mice being accustomed to the same kind of stimulation, the same stimulus was not performed for two consecutive days. Non-stressed animals were left undisturbed in their home cages except during housekeeping procedures such as cage cleaning.

HHCY mouse models

To investigate the impact of Hcy on cognitive function, the mice were fed a diet containing 1 % methionine (purchased from Beijing Sped BioTech Co., Ltd.) to induce an HHCY state (HHcy group).

HIIT paradigm

Following previous research with minor modifications(Fernando et al., 1993; Liu et al., 2022), we used an intermittent treadmill running protocol in mice to model the high-intensity interval training (HIIT) paradigm used in human studies. An initial 1-week acclimation period preceded the formal exercise. Each daily session consisted of a 5-min warm-up at 10 m/min, followed by 2 min of running at 17 m/min. The running speed increased by 1 m/min each week. Each session consisted of 10 sets of 2 min running followed by 2 min of rest, with a treadmill incline of 0°. Treadmill training was conducted between 8:30 a.m. and 12:00 a.m., 5 days a week, for a total of 11 weeks.

Morris water maze (MWM)

A large metal pool filled with opaque water was maintained at a controlled temperature of 24 ± 2 °C. The water was rendered opaque by the addition of non-fat milk and was separated into four equal quadrants. There was a hidden square platform (10 cm in diameter), which was located 1 cm below the water surface in the center of quadrant three. In the acquisition trials, a mouse was put into the tank to find a hidden platform within 180 s (one trial). Two trials per day were performed for three days in total, and the latency for mice to find the platform was recorded. Each mouse was allowed to stand on the platform for 20 s at the end of each trial. To mitigate potential fatigue and thermoregulation alterations, the mice were gently removed from the pool after each trial and carefully dried using a towel. The mouse body tracks were recorded by EthoVision tracking system.

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

The mice were acclimated for a duration of 15 min in an enclosure with black walls and a white floor. Each mouse was subsequently returned to the same chamber containing two identical objects placed in the corners and allowed to freely explore until spending 30 s exploring both objects. After a period of 1 h, object recognition was assessed by substituting one of the objects with a novel object, which was counterbalanced within the chamber. Novel object preference was quantified as the percentage of time spent exploring the novel object compared to the cumulative time spent exploring both objects.

BBB permeability

Mice were intraperitoneally injected with sodium fluorescein salt (NaFI, 20 mg per 1 ml of PBS, 0.5 ml/animal) on the final day of CUMS. Following 45-min circulation, the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (80 mg⋅kg−1, i.p.). Blood samples were then obtained via cardiac puncture prior to transcardial perfusion with 0.9 % saline. The hippocampus was dissected, weighed and homogenized in 1 mL PBS. The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. An equal volume of 2 % trichloroacetic acid was added to the supernatant, and the mixture was stored overnight at 4 °C. After further centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, the clarified supernatant was combined with 0.05 M borate buffer in a 1:1 ratio. Finally, standards (at concentrations of 0, 0.00390625, 0.0078125, 0.015625, 0.03125, 0.0625, and 0.125 μg/μL) and samples were added sequentially to a 96-well plate, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (excitation wavelength for NaFI: 480 nm, emission wavelength: 538 nm). BBB permeability was determined by computing the fluorescence intensity ratio between brain tissue and serum, with results expressed as fold changes compared to control group values.

Western blotting

The hippocampal tissue samples and cell pellets from mice were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (C1053+, Applygen) containing a proteinase inhibitor cocktail (B14001, Bimake). The samples were then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. The protein concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. The samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5 % non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1 % Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were probed with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and was quantified using Image-Pro Plus software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After collecting mice blood, the samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min to obtain the supernatant. The HCY in serum was measured with the HCY ELISA KIT (A104903,Fusheng) according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

The frozen tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 for 20 min and blocked at room temperature for 1 h. Then, the sections were incubated with the primary antibodies against Claudin-5 (1:100,Thermo Fisher Scientific, 35–2500) and H3K27me3(1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, 9733)overnight at 4 °C. Following washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 AffiniPure donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L), 1:200, Jackson, 715-545-150; Cy3 AffiniPure donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), 1:200, Jackson, 711-165-152) overnight in darkness at 4 °C and stained with Hoechst (1:500, C0031, Solarbio). The images were captured via a confocal laser–scanning microscope and quantified using Image-Pro Plus software.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-qPCR

Samples for ChIP experiments were prepared using Simple ChIP Plus Enzymatic chromatin IP kit (Cell Signaling Technology, #9005) according to the manufacturer's protocol. ChIP experiments were performed with anti-H3K27me3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #9377). ChIP signals were quantified by qPCR analysis with a real-time PCR system. Values obtained for immunoprecipitated samples (percent (%) input DNA) were normalized to values for respective normal IgG. The primers for the Cldn5 promoter region were described in Table S1.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Hippocampal tissues from mice were carefully dissected and immediately fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2 h. Then the samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and subsequently post-fixed in 1 % osmium tetroxide for 2 h at room temperature. Tissue dehydration was performed using an ascending ethanol series followed by propylene oxide treatment. The samples were infiltrated with epoxy resin and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm thickness) were cut using an ultramicrotome and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, the prepared sections were examined under a transmission electron microscope for ultrastructural analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

bEnd.3 cells, the mouse brain microvascular endothelial cell line, were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. The cells were cultured in bEnd.3-specific medium (CM-0598,Procell, China). The incubator conditions were set to 37 °C, 5 % CO2 in a humidified environment. The HCY (Sigma Diagnostics, H4628) was dissolved in 0.9 % saline and diluted with sterile phosphate–buffered saline (PBS) to the indicated concentrations. The cells were collected for further analysis after treatment for 24 h.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at 70 % confluency followed transfection with the luciferase reporter gene plasmid and the trans-acting factor gene plasmid. The DNA and transfection reagent were prepared as follows: for each well in the 96-well plate, the ratio of Firefly luciferase vector (Firefly) to Renilla luciferase vector (Renilla) to transfection reagent was 0.1 μg: 0.005 μg: 0.5 μL (with a total of 0.1 μg of transcription factor plasmid or control plasmid). The DNA and transfection reagent were then diluted separately and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The diluted DNA and transcription factor plasmid were mixed with the transfection reagent and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Then, 50 μL of culture medium was removed from each well, and 50 μL of the plasmid-transfection reagent mixture was added to each cell sample. After transfection for 6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. After 48 h of plasmid co-transfection, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed once with 100 μL PBS. Then, 50 μL diluted passive lysis buffer (PLB) was added to each well, and the plate was shaken at room temperature for 15 min to lyse the cells. Next, 5 μL supernatant was transferred to each well of an opaque white 96-well plate. Then, 50 μL pre-mixed LAR II was added, and data were recorded after 2 s. Subsequently, 50 μL pre-mixed Stop & Glo Reagent was added to each well, and after a 2-s incubation and data were recorded again.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1. The normality assumption was assessed with the D'Agostino-Pearson omnibus normality test. Non-parametric tests were applied to data that did not pass the normality test. Results were expressed as mean ± SEM. Differences between two groups were compared using corrected t-tests, and interactions among multiple factors were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

HIIT alleviates CUMS induced-cognitive impairment and BBB disruption in mice

To evaluate the effect of HIIT on CUMS-induced cognitive impairment, we conducted the MWM and NORT, and the timeline of the HIIT intervention is shown in Fig. 1A. The results showed that compared to the Ctrl group, the CUMS group exhibited reduced body weight as well as impaired learning abilities during the training trial of MWM test (Fig. S1). The CUMS group also displayed significantly increased escape latency and decreased target entries in the probe trial of MWM test (Fig. 1B–D), and a reduced cognitive index in the NORT (Fig. 1E). However, compared to the CUMS group, the CUMS + HIIT group demonstrated a significantly decreased escape latency and increased platform crossings (Fig. 1B–D) in the MWM, and an improved cognitive index in the NORT (Fig. 1E). These findings indicate that HIIT can mitigate the cognitive impairment caused by chronic stress.

Considering previous findings that exercise exerts a protective effect on the BBB(Liang et al., 2023), we evaluated the protective role of HIIT on the BBB function. The CUMS mice exhibited a significant increase in the numbers of discontinuous TJs and permeability to NaFI, whereas the HIIT + CUMS group showed a reduction in discontinuous TJs and decreased permeability to NaFI (Fig. 1F–H). Overall, the results indicate that HIIT can restore chronic stress-induced BBB structural damage and hyper-permeability.

HIIT reduces HCY levels via normalization of its metabolic enzymes

Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated HCY levels are closely associated with cognitive impairment and vascular perturbation (Wang et al., 2022). In this study, to identify the regulatory pathway through which HIIT modulates chronic stress-induced cognitive dysfunction, we measured HCY levels in the hippocampus of CUMS mice using ELISA. The results showed that HIIT intervention reversed the abnormal increase in HCY levels in the hippocampus induced by CUMS (Fig. 2A). Since the brain contains complete HCY metabolic pathways, we measured hippocampal expression of four HCY-metabolizing enzymes to explore HIIT-induced regulation mechanisms. The results revealed that CUMS reduced the expression of three key enzymes including CSE, CBS and MS, while HIIT reversed the expression of these enzymes (Fig. 2B–F). These results suggest that the regulation of HCY levels in the hippocampus by HIIT may be achieved by maintaining the normal HCY metabolic pathway.

Additionally, we examined the effect of HIIT on plasma HCY levels in CUMS mice. The results showed that HIIT could decrease plasma HCY levels induced by CUMS, and the HIIT intervention alone had no effect on plasma HCY levels in the mice as well (Fig. 3A). As peripheral HCY metabolism primarily occurs in the liver, we further evaluated the protein expression of key enzymes in the HCY metabolic pathway. HIIT could blocked the inhibitory effects of CUMS on three key enzymes in the liver including MTHFR, CBS and MS (Fig. 3B–F). These findings demonstrate that HIIT can ameliorate stress-induced HHCY via concurrently modulating both cerebral and peripheral HCY metabolic pathways.

HIIT reverses HHCY-induced cognitive deficit and BBB damage in mice

To verify the role of HCY in HIIT-mediated prevention of stress-induced cognitive dysfunction, we used an HHCY mouse model involving administration of a high methionine diet as described in previous studies(Wang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2016). The elevation of HCY levels in the plasma and hippocampus, and the decreased body weight of the mice with high methionine diet confirmed the successful establishment of the HHCY model (Fig. 4A and B; Fig. S2A). Moreover, the results showed that in the HIIT + HHCY group, both plasma and hippocampal HCY levels, and body weight were restored to the normal control levels (Fig. 4A and B; Fig. S2A), highlighting the regulatory effect of HIIT on HCY levels.

Subsequently, we performed the MWM and the NORT to elucidate the influence of HIIT on cognitive function in HHCY mice. The HHCY group exhibited obvious learning and memory impairment similarly to those caused by CUMS, whereas HIIT improved theses behavioral phenotypes (Fig. 4C–F; Fig. S2B). HHCY mice with HHIT intervention also showed significant improvements in discontinuous TJs and hyper-permeability of BBB compared with those in the HHCY group (Fig. 4G–I). The data indicate that HIIT can reverse BBB dysfunction and cognitive impairment by regulating HCY levels.

HIIT inhibits the recruitment of H3K27me3 to enhance Claudin-5 expression in CUMS and HHCY mice

TJ proteins are crucial for maintaining the integrity of TJs and the low permeability of the BBB(Zhao et al., 2015). Among them, Claudin-5 is highly expressed in brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) and is recognized as a direct regulator of BBB function(Nitta et al., 2003; Zhao et al., 2015). To investigate the molecular basis on which HIIT reverses chronic stress-induced BBB damage and cognitive impairment in mice, we first measured the protein expression level of Claudin-5 in the hippocampus using Western blot analysis. The suppression of Claudin-5 expression was observed in the CUMS and HHCY group, while HIIT intervention enhanced the protein level of Claudin-5 (Fig. 5A–D). Our findings suggest that HIIT may protect the BBB integrity and cognitive function from CUMS-induced HHCY by affecting Claudin-5 expression in the hippocampus.

We further explored the potential mechanism by which HIIT regulates Claudin-5 expression. Previous research has indicated that the transcription of Cldn5 is suppressed with increased levels of H3K27me3 in stressed mice(Dudek et al., 2020). Given the close link between HCY metabolism and methylation, we sought to decipher the potential mechanism of HIIT in restoring Claudin-5 expression by examining the enrichment of H3K27me3 at the Cldn5 promoter region. The results showed that HIIT inhibited the enhancement of H3K27me3 deposition at the Cldn5 promoter induced by CUMS and HHCY (Fig. 5E–J). Although the specific methylation sites regulated by HIIT were not entirely consistent between the CUMS and HHCY models, these findings nevertheless demonstrate the therapeutic role of HIIT in stress-induced cognitive impairment by reversing HCY-mediated Cldn5 hypermethylation.

ETS1 is involved in the HIIT-induced transcriptional activation of Cldn5

ETS1 has been shown to directly bind to various endothelial cell-specific genes, leading to transcriptional changes and playing a role in vascular function (Chen et al., 2017; Harel et al., 2021; Zurawska et al., 2024). Therefore, we speculated that ETS1 might participate in the HIIT-mediated epigenetic regulation of Cldn5 transcription. First, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay to verify the binding between ETS1 and Cldn5 (Fig. 6A). The results showed that ETS1 could bind to the Cldn5 promoter and facilitate its expression (Fig. 6B). To explore whether HCY exerts an inhibitory effect on ETS1 as well, the bEnd.3 cells were treated with HCY and the expression of ETS was measured. We found that high levels of HCY could reduce the expression of ETS1 successfully (Fig. 6C and D). The inhibitory effect of HCY on ETS1 was consistent in vivo, while HIIT increased the ETS1 levels in the hippocampus of both CUMS and HHCY mice (Fig. 6E–H). In summary, these results suggest that HIIT may reverse HCY-induced suppression of ETS1 to restore Cldn5 transcription.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that HIIT alleviates CUMS-induced BBB damage and cognitive impairment via the normalization of HCY metabolism. Further, our findings indicate that HIIT-mediated reduction of HCY levels not only hampers H3K27me3 enrichment at the Cldn5 promoter region but also promotes the expression of ETS1, a transcriptional activator of Cldn5 (Fig. 7). Our findings provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying HIIT-induced neuroprotection and suggest that HIIT may be a promising non-pharmacological intervention for stress-related neurological disorders.

The proposed hypothesis for the mechanisms by which HIIT attenuates chronic stress-induced BBB dysfunction and cognitive deficits. HIIT alleviates CUMS-induced BBB damage and cognitive impairment in the CUMS mice. Mechanistically, HIIT restores stress-induced HHCY via the normalization of HCY metabolic enzymes. Moreover, HIIT-mediated reduction of HCY levels not only hampers H3K27me3 enrichment at the Cldn5 promoter region but also promotes the expression of ETS1, a transcriptional activator of Cldn5, together enhancing Claudin-5 expression to repair BBB damage.

Substantial evidence indicates that BBB disruption plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of stress-induced cognitive impairment (Sun et al., 2024; Welcome and Mastorakis, 2020). During the development of cognitive dysfunction, BBB damage leads to increased vascular permeability and destabilization of the brain microenvironment, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates BBB dysfunction. Both our previous research and that of others have indicated that epigenetic mechanisms play a significant role in stress-induced BBB damage. For instance, DNA methylation and histone modifications can regulate the expression of although the regulatory mechanisms underlying BBB maintenance remain incompletely understood, therapeutic interventions targeting BBB integrity have shown promise in ameliorating cognitive deficits(Bien-Ly et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2023).

As confirmed in our current study, CUMS significantly elevated HCY levels in both plasma and hippocampus. Moreover, extensive research has demonstrated the role of HCY in epigenetic regulation. Abnormal HCY metabolism perturbs the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)/S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) ratio, thereby disrupting DNA and histone methylation states(Dai et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). For example, HCY accumulation enhances H3K27me3 modification, leading to transcriptional repression of related genes in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (Xiaoling et al., 2016). Our results extend these observations by demonstrating that HCY increases H3K27me3 enrichment at the Cldn5 promoter region, to inhibit Claudin-5 expression. Interestingly, in addition to increasing H3K27me3 levels, HCY also suppresses the expression of the transcription factor ETS1, further reducing its transcriptional activation of Cldn5 and exacerbating BBB damage. These findings provide novel insight into how HCY contributes to BBB breakdown during stress. While our study focused on these epigenetic mechanisms, HCY may also impair BBB function through alternative pathways, such as oxidative stress induction(Liang et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024) and glial cell modulation(Skovierova et al., 2015; Tripathi et al., 2016), warranting further investigation.

HIIT has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological intervention for various neurological disorders including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and stroke(Blackmore et al., 2024; Crozier et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2023; McEwen, 2007).

Our study confirms that HIIT significantly improved CUMS-induced cognitive dysfunction, reinforcing its neuroprotective effects in stress-related neural damage. The hippocampus appears to be particularly sensitive to exercise intervention. This sensitivity may be attributed to its pronounced synaptic plasticity, dense distribution of glucocorticoid receptors, and its critical role in memory formation, which collectively make it highly vulnerable to stress-induced damage while also highly responsive to neuroprotective stimuli. Multiple studies have demonstrated that physical activity can induce neuroplastic changes in the hippocampus through various mechanisms, thereby modulating cognitive functions such as learning, memory, and emotion(Blume and Royes, 2024; Dong et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). However, stress-related cognitive and emotional impairments are not solely determined by hippocampal alterations. Other stress-sensitive brain regions, such as the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hypothalamus, also make significant contributions to these beneficial effects. Indeed, accumulating evidence indicates that physical activity can improve or preserve cognitive and emotional functions via modulation of the PFC(Yi et al., 2025)and hypothalamus(Liu et al., 2022). These findings suggest that the beneficial effects of HIIT may result from coordinated, network-level modulation involving both the hippocampus and other stress-responsive brain regions.

Interestingly, the impact of exercise on HCY levels remains controversial. Some studies report that intermittent treadmill running reduces HCY levels(Choi et al., 2014; Leao et al., 2021), while other physical activities suggest no significant effect(Herrmann et al., 2003; Zhong et al., 2023). The observed discrepancies across studies regarding the effects of physical activity on HCY levels likely reflect heterogeneity in intervention protocols. Our findings clearly indicate that the specific HIIT protocol employed here effectively counteracts CUMS-driven HCY elevation. Notably, previous studies have shown that the HIIT's neuroprotective effects are mediated through epigenetic reprogramming(Keiser et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2022). Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrates HIIT reversed HCY-induced H3K27me3 hypermethylation at the Cldn5 promoter while concurrently restoring ETS1 expression. This dual mechanism synergistically enhances Cldn5 transcription, thereby preserving BBB integrity. Our findings establish HIIT as a modulator of HCY metabolism under stress conditions and uncover HIIT's capacity to coordinate both histone modification and transcription factor regulation to maintain BBB function. However, factors such as exercise duration, modality and intensity may influence its efficacy. Future research should focus on optimizing and standardizing HIIT intervention protocols to determine its most effective application under different pathological conditions and among diverse populations. Establishing these parameters will provide a more robust theoretical basis for HIIT's application in neurological disorders.

A limitation of the present study is that only male mice were used. This decision was made to reduce biological variability associated with the estrous cycle in females, which can influence stress responses. While this approach minimized potential confounding factors and maintained statistical power within the available resources, it does not address potential sex-specific differences. Previous rodent studies have consistently demonstrated pronounced sex differences in stress reactivity, particularly in hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation, the modulatory role of sex hormones, and both behavioral and neurobiological phenotypes, with females often showing greater stress sensitivity and stronger HPA axis responses compared to males(Handa et al., 2022; Krolick and Shi, 2022; Qiao et al., 2025). Future studies will include female cohorts to determine whether the HIIT–HCY–BBB regulatory axis identified here operates similarly across sexes. In addition, although the present study employed imposed exercise, existing evidence suggests that both voluntary and forced exercise can lead to improvements in spatial learning, memory, and hippocampal neuroplasticity through largely overlapping biological mechanisms(Alomari et al., 2013; Rafie et al., 2023). For example, studies in rodents have shown that either voluntary wheel running or forced swimming exercise enhances cognitive performance and increases neurotrophic factor expression in the hippocampus(Alomari et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2021). While certain behavioral outcomes, such as anxiety-like behaviors, may differ between the two modalities(Leasure and Jones, 2008), the core neuroprotective and cognitive benefits appear to be comparable. Therefore, voluntary exercise could also produce similar beneficial effects. Future studies comparing voluntary and imposed exercise within the same experimental framework will be valuable for confirming this hypothesis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence that HIIT can reverse the detrimental effects of HCY on cognitive function under stressful conditions by epigenetically regulating BBB integrity, offering a promising avenue for the development of exercise-based interventions to prevent and treat stress-related neurological disorders. To maximize the translational potential of HIIT's neuroprotective effects, further investigations are needed to optimize HIIT parameters for stress-related cognitive disorders, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between physical activity and brain health.
