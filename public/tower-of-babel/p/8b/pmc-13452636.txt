Myxoid Fibroadenoma of the Breast With Radiologic-Pathologic Correlation: A Case Report
Authors: Despoina Milonaki, Ioannis Provatas, Nikoleta Sinou, Natalia Sinou, Helen Trihia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myxoid fibroadenoma is a rare histologic variant of conventional fibroadenoma characterized by prominent hypocellular myxoid stromal change. Owing to its overlapping morphologic features with other myxoid breast lesions, including mucinous carcinoma and phyllodes tumor, it may pose a significant diagnostic challenge, particularly in limited biopsy specimens.We report the case of a 47-year-old woman presenting with a non-palpable breast mass, initially evaluated with imaging and core needle biopsy. Preoperative histopathologic assessment demonstrated a fibroepithelial lesion; however, definitive diagnosis remained uncertain because of the prominent myxoid stromal component and limited sampling. Surgical excision was therefore performed, and complete histopathologic examination established the diagnosis of myxoid fibroadenoma. Microscopic evaluation revealed a well-circumscribed benign biphasic fibroepithelial lesion composed of epithelial and stromal components with abundant hypocellular myxoid stroma, without cytologic atypia, stromal overgrowth, or increased mitotic activity.Myxoid fibroadenoma is an uncommon benign breast lesion that may mimic malignant or borderline breast neoplasms, particularly in limited biopsy specimens. Awareness of this rare entity and careful radiologic-pathologic correlation are essential to avoid misdiagnosis as an aberration of the normal development and involution (ANDI).

Introduction

Breast fibroepithelial lesions comprise a heterogeneous group of biphasic tumors, including fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumors, which may present diagnostic challenges due to overlapping clinical, radiologic, and histopathologic features [1,2].

Myxoid fibroadenoma is a rare histologic variant of conventional fibroadenoma, characterized by abundant hypocellular myxoid stromal change [2,3]. Although benign, its unusual morphology may lead to diagnostic uncertainty, particularly in distinguishing it from other myxoid breast lesions such as phyllodes tumor and mucinous carcinoma [4].

Myxoid fibroadenoma has occasionally been described in association with Carney complex; however, most cases occur sporadically. Carney syndrome is characterized by cardiac myxomas, cutaneous myxomas (including angiomyxomas), spotty skin pigmentation (lentigines), blue nevi, and endocrine abnormalities (such as Cushing syndrome and acromegaly). Therefore, the identification of a myxoid fibroadenoma may warrant further systemic evaluation [5,6].

The diagnosis of fibroepithelial breast lesions relies on the integration of clinical examination, imaging findings, and histopathologic evaluation. However, the assessment of limited tissue samples, particularly core needle biopsies, may be challenging because of overlapping morphologic features among different entities [7].

Although less commonly used today, fine-needle aspiration (FNA) may still be employed in selected cases. However, core needle biopsy is generally preferred because preservation of tissue architecture is essential for diagnosis. In addition, FNA has been proven to provoke secondary changes, such as infrarctions of adenomas [8]. In such cases, radiologic-pathologic correlation plays a critical role in achieving an accurate diagnosis and guiding appropriate management.

The differential diagnosis of myxoid fibroadenoma includes benign breast lesions, such as myofibroblastomas and benign myofibroblastic/epithelial lesions with minimally infiltrative margins. In addition, we should exclude malignant transformations, such as low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma that arises from fibroadenomas [9].

From a molecular perspective, myxoid fibroadenomas appear to differ from conventional fibroadenomas, as they typically lack the MED12 mutations commonly identified in classic fibroadenomas. This finding supports the notion that myxoid fibroadenomas, particularly those associated with Carney complex, may represent a distinct biological entity. Due to the wide differential diagnosis of myxoid fibroadenomas, an interdisciplinary approach and analytical assessment are necessary [10].

We present the case of a 47-year-old woman with myxoid fibroadenoma of the breast. The aim of this report is to highlight the evaluation of the characteristic imaging and histopathological findings to diagnose myxoid fibroadenoma of the breast on time and emphasize the importance of its distinction from other benign and malignant breast lesions.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old female patient presented to the breast clinic for routine annual screening. She reported no pain, nipple discharge, or nipple retraction. Her family history was positive for breast cancer in her mother. Her past medical history included chronic asthma and a hysterectomy performed three years earlier due to uterine fibroids, with no drug or allergy history.

On clinical examination, no palpable abnormalities were detected. Her laboratory results (tumor markers and CBC) were within normal limits. A bilateral digital mammography was performed, which revealed moderately dense breasts, with micronodular depiction of the fibrogranular elements (American College of Radiology (ACR) 2 parenchymal pattern). A smooth, well-circumscribed nodular opacity is revealed in the upper inner quadrant of the right breast, in a peripheral location, with a maximum diameter of 18 mm. No architectural distortion is observed, and no suspicious clustered microcalcifications are noted (Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) IVA) (Figure 1).

Subsequently, the patient underwent breast magnetic resonance imaging, which demonstrated mild background parenchymal enhancement following intravenous administration of gadolinium-based contrast material. A lobulated lesion was identified in the right breast at the 1 o’clock position, containing hypovascular linear bands, possibly corresponding to internal septations, showing progressive enhancement with a plateau kinetic pattern, measuring 17 x 13 x 13 mm, and located at a distance of 12.5 cm from the nipple (BI-RADS III) (Figure 3).

Thereafter, the patient underwent an ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy (16G cutting needle) for the lesion located at 1 o’clock of the right breast, measuring 15 mm in maximum diameter. Four core tissue samples were obtained and submitted for histopathological examination. The procedure was performed under local anesthesia, and because the lesion was non-palpable, a magnetic seed (Magseed) was placed following biopsy for preoperative localization [11]. Core needle biopsy histopathology demonstrated a benign fibroepithelial lesion consistent with fibroadenoma, with no evidence of cellular atypia or malignancy.

Despite the benign findings on core needle biopsy, the patient underwent lumpectomy to obtain a definitive diagnosis through complete histopathological examination. As the mass was non-palpable, intraoperative localization was performed using the Magseed detection system. Following the excision, specimen mammography was performed to confirm the removal of the targeted lesion.

Subsequently, layered wound closure was carried out, followed by an intradermal skin suture. The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged within the first 24 hours in excellent condition, with appropriate postoperative instructions.

Additionally, we may perform immunohistochemistry to rule out epithelial (mucinous) neoplasms when there are tumor cells with carcinomatous morphology or suspicious for malignancy. Histological findings

Τhe specimen consisted of a well-circumscribed fibroepithelial lesion measuring 18 mm, arising within breast parenchyma, showing background involutional changes. Histologically, the lesion demonstrated an intracanalicular and pericanalicular growth pattern (Figure 3). The epithelial component consisted of bilayered ductal structures (Figure 4) composed of luminal epithelial and myoepithelial cells, lacking cytologic atypia or architectural complexity. The stromal component was diffusely hypocellular and exhibited marked myxoid degeneration (Figure 5), forming an abundant basophilic extracellular matrix in which scattered spindle-shaped to oval stromal cells were embedded. Importantly, there was no evidence of stromal hypercellularity, cytologic atypia, mitotic activity, stromal overgrowth, or infiltrative margins. Surgical excision was complete with clear margins.

Discussion

Myxoid fibroadenoma is a rare histologic variant of conventional fibroadenoma, characterized by prominent hypocellular myxoid stromal change within an otherwise benign fibroepithelial lesion. While fibroadenomas are among the most common benign breast tumors in premenopausal women, the myxoid subtype remains distinctly rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature [13].

Although myxoid fibroadenoma appears to occur predominantly in premenopausal women, its true epidemiologic distribution remains uncertain because of its rarity. In the present case, the patient's age exceeded the commonly reported range, adding to the diagnostic challenge, as myxoid breast lesions in older patients may raise greater suspicion for malignancy or other uncommon neoplastic entities. This finding further expands the reported age spectrum of myxoid fibroadenoma and highlights that this uncommon entity should also be considered in the differential diagnosis of myxoid breast lesions in older patients. This rare entity presents a diagnostic challenge because the prominent myxoid stroma may mimic other breast lesions with similar morphologic features, most notably mucinous carcinoma and phyllodes tumor [9].

Accurate distinction is essential because these entities differ substantially in their biological behavior and clinical management. Unlike mucinous carcinoma, which is characterized by malignant epithelial cell clusters suspended within extracellular mucin, myxoid fibroadenoma retains the benign biphasic architecture typical of fibroepithelial lesions, without cytologic atypia, infiltrative borders, stromal overgrowth, or increased mitotic activity [14].

Likewise, phyllodes tumors generally demonstrate increased stromal cellularity, stromal overgrowth, and the characteristic leaf-like architectural pattern, features absent in myxoid fibroadenoma [15].

Because of the rarity of myxoid fibroadenoma, its imaging characteristics have not been extensively described in the literature. Available evidence suggests that imaging findings may overlap with those of conventional fibroadenoma, typically presenting as a well-circumscribed breast mass on ultrasound or mammography. Nevertheless, the prominent myxoid stromal component may occasionally result in atypical radiologic appearances, potentially complicating preoperative assessment and raising concern for other fibroepithelial or malignant lesions. Therefore, careful radiologic-pathologic correlation is essential, particularly in cases where core biopsy findings are limited or inconclusive. Recognition of this uncommon entity may help avoid diagnostic misinterpretation and unnecessary aggressive management [3,16].

Preoperative diagnosis may be particularly challenging on core needle biopsy specimens, where limited sampling may fail to adequately capture the overall architecture of fibroepithelial lesions. Extensive myxoid stromal change may further obscure the characteristic morphology of conventional fibroadenoma, increasing diagnostic uncertainty. In such cases, close correlation between histopathologic findings and imaging characteristics is essential to avoid misinterpretation.

Previous reports have also highlighted the potential impact of prior diagnostic procedures on lesion morphology. Vargas and Merino [17] described a case of infarcted myxoid fibroadenoma following FNA, emphasizing the possibility of histologic distortion after interventional manipulation. Although the present case was preceded by core needle biopsy rather than FNA, awareness of procedure-related architectural alterations remains important during pathologic evaluation [18].

Given the rarity of this entity, accurate recognition of myxoid fibroadenoma is important to avoid overdiagnosis of malignancy and unnecessary aggressive treatment. Although most reported cases are sporadic, recognition of this entity remains important because of its potential to mimic more aggressive breast lesions. Recent case reports, including that by Al-Darwish et al. [13], suggest that definitive diagnosis often requires complete histopathologic evaluation following surgical excision. A core needle biopsy provided a preliminary diagnosis; however, a definitive diagnosis was established only after a complete histopathological examination of the excised lesion. To our knowledge, only a limited number of myxoid fibroadenomas have been reported in the literature. Reporting additional cases may improve recognition of this uncommon variant, broaden the currently available clinicopathologic spectrum, and facilitate its distinction from malignant and borderline breast lesions.

The role of immunohistochemistry (IHC) in differential diagnosis

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) serves as an invaluable tool when evaluating fibroepithelial lesions with extensive myxoid stromal change, particularly in limited core biopsy specimens. A targeted IHC panel is highly beneficial to definitively rule out malignant mimics and phyllodes tumors, such as differentiating from mucinous carcinoma and differentiating from phyllodes tumors and sarcomas.

Limitations

This case report presents many limitations. First, it is a single-case study; the clinical presentation, imaging kinetics, and histopathologic findings may not fully represent the entire spectrum of myxoid fibroadenomas, limiting the generalizability of our conclusions. Second, formal IHC validation (such as p63 or Ki-67 staining) was not performed, while the definitive diagnosis was confidently established using standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) morphology on the complete surgical specimen. Finally, this study lacks long-term clinical and radiological follow-up data post-excision.

Conclusions

Myxoid fibroadenoma is a rare benign fibroepithelial breast lesion that may pose significant diagnostic challenges because of its overlapping radiologic and histopathologic features with other myxoid breast neoplasms, including phyllodes tumor and mucinous carcinoma. This case highlights the limitations of core needle biopsy in lesions with extensive myxoid stromal change, where complete histopathologic evaluation of the excised specimen may be required to establish the final diagnosis.

Careful radiologic-pathologic correlation and multidisciplinary evaluation are essential to avoid diagnostic pitfalls and inappropriate management. When diagnostic uncertainty persists, complete surgical excision remains the gold standard for accurate histopathologic assessment and exclusion of more aggressive lesions.
