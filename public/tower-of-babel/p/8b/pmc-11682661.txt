Cross-species striatal hubs: Linking anatomy to resting-state connectivity
Authors: Xiaolong Peng, Lucas R. Trambaiolli, Eun Young Choi, Julia F. Lehman, Gary Linn, Brian E. Russ, Charles E. Schroeder, Suzanne N. Haber, Hesheng Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corticostriatal connections are essential for motivation, cognition, and behavioral flexibility. There is broad interest in using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) to link circuit dysfunction in these connections with neuropsychiatric disorders. In this paper, we used tract-tracing data from non-human primates (NHPs) to assess the likelihood of monosynaptic connections being represented in rs-fMRI data of NHPs and humans. We also demonstrated that existing hub locations in the anatomical data can be identified in the rs-fMRI data from both species. To characterize this in detail, we mapped the complete striatal projection zones from 27 tract-tracer injections located in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), ventrolateral PFC (vlPFC), and dorsal PFC (dPFC) of macaque monkeys. Rs-fMRI seeds at the same regions of NHP and homologous regions of human brains showed connectivity maps in the striatum mostly consistent with those observed in the tracer data. We then examined the location of overlap in striatal projection zones. The medial rostral dorsal caudate connected with all five frontocortical regions evaluated in this study in both modalities (tract-tracing and rs-fMRI) and species (NHP and human). Other locations in the caudate also presented an overlap of four frontocortical regions, suggesting the existence of different locations with lower levels of input diversity. Small retrograde tracer injections and rs-fMRI seeds in the striatum confirmed these cortical input patterns. This study sets the ground for future studies evaluating rs-fMRI in clinical samples to measure anatomical corticostriatal circuit dysfunction and identify connectional hubs to provide more specific treatment targets for neurological and psychiatric disorders.

Introduction

There has been an explosion of interest in linking psychiatric illnesses to circuit dysfunction measured by resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Moreover, recent advances in neuroimaging combined with network science (graph theory) offer a quantitative approach to understanding brain regions central to integrative processing across brain networks and referred to as ‘hubs’ (Crossley et al., 2014; Fornito et al., 2015, 2016; Gordon et al., 2018). Cortico-basal ganglia connections mediate a wide range of behaviors and are essential for behavioral flexibility. Circuit dysfunction in these connections is linked to several psychiatric disorders, including, but not limited to, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), major depressive disorder (MDD), and addiction. Although animal tracing studies have formed the basis for understanding cortico-striatal connections and their function, imaging has taken the lead, specifically identifying abnormalities linked to diseases (Haber et al., 2020).

Prefrontal cortex (PFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) projections terminate most densely in the rostral striatum (anterior to the anterior commissure) (Averbeck et al., 2014; Calzavara et al., 2007; Haber, 2003; Haber et al., 2006b; Haber and Knutson, 2010, 1995; Selemon and Goldman-Rakic, 1985). Here, they occupy the majority of the striatum and are organized in an overall dorsoventral and mediolateral topography. Orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and ACC projections occupy the ventral and medial striatum, while projections from lateral PFC regions occupy a more lateral striatal position (Haber, 2003; Haber and Knutson, 2010; Haber et al., 1995; Selemon and Goldman-Rakic, 1985). Cortico-basal ganglia connections were once considered to be organized in parallel, segregated loops (Alexander et al., 1986). However, based on detailed anatomic studies (Averbeck et al., 2014; Calzavara et al., 2007; Haber et al., 2006b), supported by behavioral and physiological studies (Apicella et al., 2011; Buch et al., 2006; Pan et al., 2014; Ravel et al., 2003; Williams and Eskandar, 2006; Yamada et al., 2004) in monkeys and imaging studies in humans (Draganski et al., 2008; Greene et al., 2020), we now know that this system is highly integrated across functional domains. Thus, PFC/ACC fibers overlap extensively in the striatum. These overlapping projections are in a position of potentially integrating information across functional domains, forming hubs (Averbeck et al., 2014; Calzavara et al., 2007; Choi et al., 2017b; Draganski et al., 2008; Haber, 2003; Haber et al., 2006a, 2006b; Haber and Knutson, 2010; van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2013). fMRI-based functional connectivity data show that these overlapping zones also exist in humans (Choi et al., 2017b; Draganski et al., 2008; Greene et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021a). However, a key question is whether these overlapping connections inferred from the indirect rs-fMRI human data represent direct, monosynaptic connections forming striatal hubs that integrate information from specific cortical regions.

Previous studies provided insights into the similarities of cortico-striatal organization in NHPs and humans by comparing tract-tracing data with human rs-fMRI (Choi et al., 2017b, 2012) or rs-fMRI from NHPs and humans (Liu et al., 2021b; Xia et al., 2023). However, conclusions from these approaches are limited by directly comparing different species and modalities (NHP tracer data vs. human fMRI data) or not considering the anatomical information (NHP and human fMRI). Here, we use a cortico-striatal hub previously identified in the dorsomedial caudate (Averbeck et al., 2014) as a test case to propose a pipeline to validate the reliability of cortico-striatal connectivity comparisons across species (NHPs and humans) and modalities (tract-tracing and fMRI). First, based on the anterograde tracer data from Averbeck et al. (2014), we placed seeds in rs-fMRI scans of the NHP cortex. We matched those seed locations with our anatomic tracer injection sites to determine how well the rs-fMRI connections compare to the tracing results in the same species. Second, we placed seeds in homologous PFC/ACC regions in the human brain to identify the cortico-striatal connectivity patterns and hub location in the human brain. Finally, using the retrograde tracer data from an injection in the NHP caudate nucleus, we validated the location and strengths of the inputs to the striatal hub using rs-fMRI of NHP and humans.

Our results show that the spatial distribution of functional connectivity observed in the rs-fMRI data in NHPs and humans is consistent with spatial patterns of anatomical frontocortical-striatal projections. Using these data, we identified the caudate hub across species and modalities likely to be strongly monosynaptically connected. This approach provides a new systematic pipeline to evaluate cross-species relationships between cortico-striatal anatomy and functional connectivity.

Methods

Monkey anatomic tracing

Using data from anterograde tracer injections in the OFC, vmPFC, dorsal ACC (dACC), vlPFC, and dlPFC from our previously published studies, we identified their projection zones in the striatum (Averbeck et al., 2014).

Surgery and tissue preparation

We used data from twenty-eight adult male macaque monkeys (20 Macaca nemestrina and 8 Macaca fascicularis, ages: 3 – 6 years, weights: 2.7 – 9.8 kg) for anatomic tracing analysis. Experiments and animal care followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the University of Rochester’s University Committee on Animal Resources. Surgeries followed the same procedures as previously described (Averbeck et al., 2014; Heilbronner and Haber, 2014). Briefly, animals were tranquilized with intramuscular injection of ketamine (10 mg kg−1) and maintained with isoflurane. Using a 0.5 l Hamilton syringe, 40nl of tracer was pressure injected over 10 min. The tracers used were Lucifer Yellow (LY), Fluororuby (FR), or Fluorescein (FS) conjugated to dextran amine (Invitrogen), or tritiated amino acids (AA) (100 nl, 1:1 solution of [3H]-leucine and [3H]-proline in dH2O, 200 mCi/ml; NEN). Regions of interest included six injections in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC – two injections in area 14, two in areas 14/25, one in area 25, and one in area 32), seven in the dorsal prefrontal cortex (dPFC – one in areas 10/9, four in area 9/46, one in area 9 and one in areas 9/8A), six in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC – one in area 10, one in area 11, one in areas 11/12, one in areas 11/13, one in area 13, and one in area OP), five in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC – one in areas 32/24, one in area 24b, one in areas 24b,c, and two in area 24c), and three in the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC – one in area 47/12, one in areas 45/8A, and one in area 44) (Averbeck et al., 2014) (Fig. S1–S5) Twelve to fourteen days after surgery, animals were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital and perfused with saline, followed by a 4 % paraformaldehyde/1.5 % sucrose solution. Brains were post-fixed overnight and cryoprotected in increasing sucrose gradients (10, 20, and 30 %) (Haber et al., 2006a). We performed immunocytochemistry on one of every eight free-floating 50 μm sections to visualize tracers, as previously described (Choi et al., 2017b; Heilbronner and Haber, 2014). Sections were mounted, dehydrated, defatted in xylenes, and coverslipped with Permount.

Anterograde cortical projection mapping

Using dark field microscopy under 1.6X, 4X, and 10X objectives, we traced outlines of dense, focal projection patches in the striatum. We charted one in every eight sections throughout the entire striatum for each case. Dense projections corresponded to terminal fields visible at 1.6X with discernible boundaries (Choi et al., 2017b; Trambaiolli et al., 2022). For each case, we created outlines from each slide using the Neurolucida software (MicroBrightField, Williston, VT), resulting in a stack of 2-D coronal sections. After importing this stack into IMOD (3-D rendering program (Kremer et al., 1996)), we reconstructed in 3-D the dense projection fields for each case separately. Later, we merged all cases into a standard brain (MR261) with a linear transformation in IMOD based on anatomic landmarks (e.g., gyri, sulci, white, and gray matter structures). We manually adjusted the medial–lateral (M-L), dorsal-ventral (D-V), and anterior–posterior (A-P) placements and relative terminal size to ensure the accuracy of registration (Averbeck et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2017b; Haber et al., 2006a).

Retrograde cell charting

Using light field microscopy under 20X objective, we identified retrogradely labeled cells, as previously described (Choi et al., 2017a, 2017b; Trambaiolli et al., 2022). We used the StereoInvestigator software (MicroBrightField Bioscience, U.S.A) to stereologically label cells in one of every 24 sections (1.2 mm interval).

Monkey functional MRI data collection and preprocessing

Monkey MRI dataset

We used data from nine macaque monkeys (Macaca mulatta, 3 males, age ages 6–7 years, weight distribution 2.5–6.75 kg) acquired at the Nathan Kline Institute (NKI). Two of the nine macaque datasets are available online as part of the NHP data-sharing project PRIME-DE (http://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/PRIME/nki.html). Each macaque received 2–7 anesthetized scanning sessions with monocrystalline iron oxide ferumoxytol (MION) as the contrast agent. Each session consisted of 4–8 functional scans (8-min per scan). See Table S1 for details. The NKI Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approved all methods and procedures.

Monkey MRI data acquisition

MRI data were acquired on a 3.0 Tesla Siemens Tim Trio scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using an 8-channel surface coil, except for five scanning sessions of one macaque (Majom), which used a knee coil adapted for monkey head scanning. Structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted sequence (TR=2500 ms, TI=1200 ms, TE=3.87 ms, FA= 8°, 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5-mm voxels). Functional data were acquired using a gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR=2000 ms, TE=16.6 ms, FA= 45°, 1.5 × 1.5 × 2-mm voxels, 32 slices, FOV=96×96 mm). A monocrystalline iron oxide ferumoxytol (MION) solution was injected at iron doses of 10 mg kg−1 IV before functional scanning. Each monkey received sedation with an initial dose of atropine (0.05 mg kg−1 IM), dexdomitor (0.02 mg kg−1 IM), and ketamine (8 mg kg−1 IM) intubated and maintained at 0.75 % isoflurane anesthesia during scanning.

Monkey MRI data preprocessing

Structural data preprocessing included the following steps: (1) spatial noise removal and bias field correction using ANTs (http://stnava.github.io/ANTs/), (2) brain extraction and segmentation into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid using FSL (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/FsFast) and FreeSurfer (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/), and (3) reconstruction of native white matter and pial surfaces using FreeSurfer.

We preprocessed functional data according to the following steps (Trambaiolli et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2018), using FSL, FreeSurfer, and ANTs: (1) removal of the first 5 frames of BOLD data, as well as the constant offset and linear trend over each run, (2) regression of six motion correction parameters using a rigid body registration algorithm, (3) spatial smoothing with a full-width half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel of 2 mm, (4) normalization for global mean signal intensity, (5) band-pass filtering of BOLD signal to only retain frequencies between 0.01 and 0.1 Hz, (6) removal of head motion, average whole-brain signal, ventricular and white matter signals through linear regression, (7) registration of the preprocessed fMRI data to the NIMH Macaque Template (NMT) template (Seidlitz et al., 2018) and down-sampling to 1-mm resolution for further analysis.

Human functional MRI data collection and preprocessing

Human MRI dataset

We used a human dataset consisting of 30 healthy young adult participants (15 males; age 24 ± 2.4 years) recruited as part of the Consortium for Reliability and Reproducibility Project by Hangzhou Normal University (CoRR-HNU). Each participant underwent ten rs-fMRI scans (10 min per scan) and a structural MRI scan across a period of one month. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with guidelines set by the Institutional Review Board of Hangzhou Normal University. This dataset is publicly available online (http://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/CoRR/html/hnu_1.html).

Human MRI data acquisition

Human MRI data were acquired on a GE MR750 3T MRI scanner (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA). Structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted Fast Spoiled gradient-echo sequence (TR = 8.1 ms, TI = 450 ms, TE = 3.1 ms, FA = 8°, 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0-mm voxels, and FOV = 256). Functional data were acquired using an EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FA = 90°, 3.4 × 3.4 × 3.4-mm voxels, FOV = 220). Participants received instructions to keep their eyes open and relax during the rs-fMRI scans.

Human resting-state MRI data preprocessing

We preprocessed rs-fMRI using the same preprocessing pipeline described in a prior report (Peng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021) that included: discarding the first four volumes, slice timing correction with SPM2 (Penny et al., 2011), head motion correction with FSL 5.0.6 (Jenkinson et al., 2002; Smith et al., 2004), global mean signal intensity normalization, bandpass temporal filtering (0.01 – 0.08 Hz), regression of signals from head motion, ventricular, white matter, and global brain. We preprocessed structural MRI data using the “recon-all” pipeline from FreeSurfer version 5.3.0 software package (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). We registered the preprocessed fMRI data to the FreeSurfer volume template and “fsaverage6” cortical surface template (40, 962 vertices in each hemisphere). Then, we down-sampled fMRI data to 2-mm resolution in the volume space, and applied spatial smoothing with a 6-mm FWHM kernel in both the volume and surface space.

Estimating the convergence of connections within the striatum

Estimation of convergence using NHP tracing data

To estimate the convergence zones from tracer data, we grouped cortical injections into five prefrontal regions, including the vmPFC, dPFC, OFC, dACC, and vlPFC (Fig. 1a & d). We merged and binarized projection patterns derived from the injections belonging to the same prefrontal region within the striatal mask. For each voxel within the striatum, we calculated the number of prefrontal regions (range from 0 to 5, with 5 corresponding to the highest number of overlapping regions) from that the voxel receives projections.

Estimation of convergence using NHP functional connectivity data

To estimate the patterns of convergency in the NHP functional connectivity data, we manually placed 27 spherical seeds (3-mm radius, following the human-NHP proportions defined by Trambaiolli et al. (2022)) in the prefrontal cortex virtually at the same locations as the injection sites (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figures 1–5). We defined seeds on the NMT with 1-mm resolution according to anatomical landmarks (i.e., gyri, sulci, white and gray matter structures) and the NHP brain atlas from Paxinos (2009). The cortico-striatal functional connectivity of these ROIs was then calculated. Following the same strategy from the tracer data, we merged and binarized (threshold of r = 0.03) within the striatum the functional connectivity maps from the ROIs belonging to the same prefrontal regions. Finally, we overlapped the striatal maps from the five prefrontal regions to obtain the functional connection convergence distribution.

Estimation of convergence using human functional connectivity data

To estimate the convergence of functional connectivity patterns in the human data, we manually placed 27 spherical seeds (5-mm radius, following the human-NHP proportions defined by Trambaiolli et al. (2022)) in the prefrontal cortex (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figures 1–5). Seed placement followed cytoarchitectonic and morphological homologies with NHPs defined in the atlases from Mai et al. (2015) and Petrides (2018), respectively, and other anatomical landmarks (i.e., gyri, sulci, white matter tracts, and subcortical structures). To ensure consistency of results across species, we first calculated the cortico-striatal functional connectivity for each ROI. Then, we grouped ROIs into five prefrontal regions (i.e., vmPFC, dPFC, OFC, dACC, and vlPFC) following the same cytoarchitectonic groupings used in the NHP data. We binarized and overlapped the striatal functional connectivity maps using the steps from the NHP analysis and the thresholds from Figs. 2–3.

Estimating cortical connections from the striatal hub region

We placed one retrograde tracer injection in the dorsomedial caudate to evaluate the input strengths from different frontocortical regions to the hub location. We retrogradely labeled cortical cells and quantified the anatomical connectivity strengths as the percentage of total labeled cells that project from each of the five brain regions to the injection site (Tang et al., 2019; Trambaiolli et al., 2022). We placed fMRI seeds in the NHP and human striata for the functional connectivity analysis. We used the cortical parcellations from Calabrese et al. (2015) to calculate the functional connectivity strengths in the macaque brain. For the human analysis, we used the cortical parcellation from Tang et al. (2019) (manually defined parcels following the description and criteria from Petrides et al. (2012) to maximize architectonic correspondence between human and NHP prefrontal areas). Both human and macaque parcellations are highly consistent with the anatomical divisions in the atlas from Paxinos (2009). The functional connectivity strength was defined as the average of R-values within all the parcels corresponding to each of the five brain regions of interest for this study.

Results

Delineation of striatal connections with different frontocortical regions

We first compared the results from rs-fMRI functional connectivity in NHPs and humans with those from the anatomic tracing data previously reported by Averbeck et al. (2014). Fig. 2 shows the ventral PFC and striatum connectivity profiles at different anterior-posterior levels. vmPFC axons projected to the ventral and medial anterior striatum (Fig. 2a), with terminals extending dorsally along the striatal medial wall. The rs-fMRI connectivity in NHPs showed the strongest connectivity in the ventralmost part of the striatum and extended dorsally along the medial wall of the caudate nucleus. Similarly, the human rs-MRI results also demonstrated the strongest connectivity in the ventromedial striatum. However, these patterns did not extend dorsally along the medial border of the caudate nucleus, as observed in the NHP tracing and rs-fMRI. vlPFC-striatal fibers terminated dorsally in the anterior caudate and extended diagonally through the caudate nucleus and putamen at the more posterior levels of the anterior striatum (Fig. 2b). The connectivity with the anterior levels of the caudate was absent in the NHP data, but present in the human data. Both NHPs and humans showed rs-fMRI connectivity crossing the caudate and putamen in a similar pattern as the one observed in the tracer data at the posterior slices.

OFC projections terminated ventrally in the striatum, extending to the medial wall (Fig. 2c). A similar pattern was observed in the rs-fMRI connectivity maps of NHPs and humans in the anterior level of the striatum. However, the human connectivity in more posterior levels was mainly located in the ventral striatum, while the NHP patterns extended to the caudate as the observed pattern in the tracer data.

Fig. 3 shows the dorsal PFC and dACC connectivity profiles to the striatum at different anterior-posterior levels. The dPFC terminals mostly terminated in the caudate nucleus, with lesser terminal fields at the mid portion of the putamen (Fig. 3a). NHP and Human rs-fMRI showed connectivity patterns in the caudate were similar to those observed in the tracer data. rs-fMRI connectivity with the putamen was only observed in the human data.

Inputs from the dACC were widespread in the striatum, with larger terminal fields at the anterior level and scattered terminal fields at the posterior level (Fig. 3b). The NHP rs-fMRI data presented connectivity profiles mostly consistent with the tracer data, with higher functional connectivity in the anterior portion and the absence of voxels in the posterior level of the ventral striatum. The human functional connectivity was also widespread, with peak values centered at the ventral striatum.

Identification of zones with converging cortical inputs in the striatum

To estimate the convergence of frontocortical inputs within the striatum, we overlapped all the previous region-specific connectivity maps and calculated the number of cortical regions projecting to each voxel within the striatum. We evaluated where inputs from different functional cortical areas converged and how many cortical regions connected with each voxel. Fig. 4 shows the convergence maps from anatomical tracing data (left) and rs-fMRI connectivity in NHPs (center) and humans (right) at different anterior-posterior levels.

These convergence maps showed high spatial consistency across species and modalities. Voxels at the dorsomedial caudate nucleus showed anatomical and functional connectivity with all the frontal regions. The anterior levels of the caudate also showed high convergence of inputs, where three to four regions connected with the same voxels. On the other hand, the dorsolateral putamen did not show connections with many of the frontal areas. The main difference across species was the higher number of voxels with inputs from all frontocortical regions in the human rs-fMRI data.

Validation of connectivity patterns with retrogradely labeled cortical inputs

Finally, we evaluated the diversity of inputs in striatal locations with high convergence of cortical inputs. For this, we compared the rs-fMRI cortical connectivity maps following seed placement in these striatal regions with the distribution of retrogradely labeled cells in the cortex after tracer injections at similar locations (tracer data previously reported by Choi et al. (2017b)).

The tracer injection in the medial caudate showed cell labeling in the medial and ventral surfaces of the frontal cortex (Fig. 5 top). Specifically, the densest labeling in the vmPFC occurred in areas 14o and 25, in the vlPFC in areas 47, 44, and 45, in the OFC in areas 11 and 13, in the dPFC in areas 9/46v, 9 L and 9/32, and in the dACC in area 24. The rs-fMRI seed in NHPs showed high labeling in the vmPFC (areas 14 and 25), vlPFC (44 and 45), OFC (11 and 13), dPFC (areas 9 L, 9/32 and 46) and dACC (32 and 24) (Fig. 5 middle). In humans, the medial caudate seed showed high connectivity with most of the frontal cortex (Fig. 5 bottom), with the strongest patterns in vlPFC areas 47 and 44, OFC area 13, dPFC areas 9 and 46, and ACC areas 32 and 24. The connectivity strengths (radar charts) show that vlPFC, OFC, and dPFC inputs are consistently the strongest across modalities and species, although the human fMRI also showed higher dACC strengths compared to those from the NHP data.

As control analyses, we evaluated the cortical functional connectivity maps derived from seeds located in different parts of the striatum and symmetrically in the left and right striatum and identified consistent connectivity patterns in the cortex (Fig. S6). For example, seeds placed in the high overlapping zones of the caudate in both hemispheres showed stronger connectivity maps with the vmPFC, vlPFC, OFC, dACC, and dPFC. However, seeds placed in low overlapping zones (putamen) showed weaker connectivity maps in the frontal cortex but stronger maps in more posterior cortical regions (e.g., parietal cortex). We also evaluated the effects of variations in the seed size in the striatum. We did not find significant differences in the spatial distribution of cortical connectivity patterns, with consistent results obtained using seeds with 2 mm and 3 mm diameters for NHPs, and 4 mm and 6 mm diameters for humans (Fig. S7).

Discussion

Cortico-striatal connectivity maps across species and modalities

This study proposed a pipeline where: i. we identified anatomical connections using NHP tract-tracing data, ii. compared these results with NHP rs-fMRI data, and iii. translated these results to human rs-fMRI data. Rs-fMRI striatal connectivity maps from seeds placed in frontocortical regions from both species were consistent with those observed in the NHP anterograde tracer data. The main differences were observed in the anterior portions of the ventral striatum and caudate. The test case - the dorsomedial caudate hub (Averbeck et al., 2014) with overlapping inputs from all frontocortical regions - was identified in both species and modalities. Validating these results, seeds placed in the medial caudate in both species presented cortical rs-fMRI connectivity patterns similar to the anatomical labeling observed after a retrograde tracer injection at a similar location in NHPs. The proposed pipeline can be expanded to explore the existence of potential hubs in other striatal locations (e.g., those described by Choi et al. (2017a) and Choi et al. (2017b)).

Bridging the gap for multimodal cross-species research

Humans and NHPs share a close evolutionary relationship (Preuss and Wise, 2022), having a homologous cytoarchitectonic organization of the frontal cortex (Petrides et al., 2012) and inter-areal anatomical and functional connectivity patterns (Neubert et al., 2014; Sallet et al., 2013; Yeterian et al., 2012). Since tract-tracing methods are impossible in humans, NHP tracing data are considered the gold standard method for investigating anatomical connections in primates. Results from these experiments can then be used to guide human imaging (Haber et al., 2020). Cross-species research comparing NHP tract-tracing data and rs-fMRI connectivity shows high quantitative similarity between these modalities within and across species (Hori et al., 2020b; Miranda-Dominguez et al., 2014). A similar approach was also used to characterize the anatomical hard-wiring underlying large-scale functional networks (Liu et al., 2019; Trambaiolli et al., 2022) and evaluate connectivity patterns from specific cortical locations (Kelly et al., 2010; Trambaiolli et al., 2022). Importantly, most of these studies focused on rs-fMRI connectivity between regions with reciprocal (bidirectional) anatomical connections. In this study, we focused on cortico-striatal connections, which are unidirectional. Although the functional connectivity strengths of unidirectional connections are less stable over time (Shen et al., 2015), our results suggest that the overall spatial distribution of these connections is consistent across modalities and species.

The pipeline proposed in this study is relevant to identifying potential differences across species or limitations due to comparisons across modalities. For instance, previous studies comparing rs-fMRI connectivity in NHPs and humans reported different cortico-caudate organization across species (Liu et al., 2021b; Xia et al., 2023). On the other hand, a study comparing human rs-fMRI with NHP tracer data showed similarities in cortico-caudate hubiness (Choi et al., 2017b). In our data, the anterior levels of the vlPFC-caudate connectivity identified with NHP tract-tracing data are not present in the NHP rs-fMRI data but present in the human rs-fMRI. One possible explanation is the anesthesia effect on the NHP rs-fMRI recordings. Previous research shows that isoflurane has a dose-dependent effect on cortical rs-fMRI connectivity in NHPs (Giacometti et al., 2021; Hori et al., 2020a; Hutchison et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2019) and in cortico-striatal connectivity in other mammal species (Grandjean et al., 2014; Stenroos et al., 2021). Since vlPFC is a hub region for the attention networks (Trambaiolli et al., 2022), the data from anesthetized animals may explain the lack of vlPFC-striatum connections in the NHP rs-fMRI. Similarly, differences in rs-fMRI connectivity between anesthetized NHPs and awake humans may explain the cross-species differences observed in other studies in the literature (Liu et al., 2021b).

Potential differences may also be caused by using MION contrast in the macaque dataset but not in humans. Although MION increases the contrast-to-noise ratio, it has a few differences from the traditional BOLD signal, such as slower decay, a higher relationship with blood volume, and inverse signals (Leite et al., 2002). All these factors may play a role in the differences observed in some of the rs-fMRI patterns across species. Another possible explanation for the observed differences is the influence of preprocessing methods in the cortico-striatal functional connectivity patterns. Prior studies have demonstrated that although smoothing methods improve the signal-to-noise ratio, they can also introduce artifactual activity shifting (Choi et al., 2017b; Geissler et al., 2005; Jo et al., 2008; Sacchet and Knutson, 2013). For instance, Sacchet and Knutson (2013) described that larger smoothing kernels can systematically shift the localization of task-based fMRI activity in the nucleus accumbens in a caudal direction. Choi et al. (2017b) later demonstrated that data smoothing caused a rostral shift in caudal connectivity from human rs-fMRI compared to patterns observed in the NHP tracer data. Thus, variations of the connectivity maps in the NHP and human rs-fMRI data may also have influences from the smoothing kernel.

Finally, it is essential to highlight that identifying seed locations in humans that are homologous to the NHP injection sites is not trivial. In this study, we used the parcels from Yeo et al. (2011) as seeds for the human connectivity analysis. Regions such as the vlPFC, dPFC, and dACC included parcels from multiple networks as seed patches. For this reason, some of the results reported here differ from those reported in previous studies describing the striatal parcellation of the human brain according to the different cortical networks (Choi et al., 2012). Therefore, future investigations comparing individual parcels of the human brain with individual injections and seeds of the macaque brain may provide a detailed map of the macaque striatal parcellation similar to those described by Choi et al. (2012).

Multiple overlapping regions in the striatum

We demonstrated that the test case hub in the dorsomedial caudate stands out with overlapping inputs from all the frontocortical regions included in this study in both species and modalities. These results align with the findings reported by Choi et al. (2017b) using an independent human rs-fMRI dataset. Moreover, it is also consistent with recent findings suggesting that the caudate presents clusters of connections with multiple large-scale networks in the human brain (Kosakowski et al., 2023).

Our results also demonstrate that other striatal regions have high overlapping of frontocortical inputs but with different connectivity strengths. The striatum has a smaller volume than the cortex, and dense terminal fields cover larger striatal volumes than predicted by the projecting areas’ relative cortical volume (Haber et al., 2006b). Thus, projections from regions outside the frontal cortex will likely overlap with frontocortical terminal zones, composing multiple zones of convergence in the striatum. For example, the dorsal caudate receives dense projections from the inferior parietal (Cavada and Goldman-Rakic, 1991; Choi et al., 2017b; Yeterian and Pandya, 1993) and superior temporal areas (Choi et al., 2017b; Maunsell and van Essen, 1983; Saint-Cyr et al., 1990; Yeterian and Pandya, 1997). The ventral striatum receives dense projections from the rostral and medial parts of the temporal cortex (Friedman et al., 2002; Van Hoesen et al., 1981; Webster et al., 1993), agranular insula (Chikama and Haber, 1995; Chikama et al., 1997), amygdala, and hippocampus (Friedman et al., 2002; Fudge et al., 2002; Russchen et al., 1985). Thus, the striatum will likely have multiple hubs with overlapping inputs associated with different functional interactions (Choi et al., 2017a). The proposed pipeline implemented in this study can be used in the future to investigate additional hubs in other striatal locations.

Implications for psychiatry

Abnormal corticostriatal connections are observed in various psychiatric disorders, including addiction, obsessive-compulsive disorder, depression, and schizophrenia (Davis et al., 1991; Ersche et al., 2012; Goodman et al., 2010; Kegeles et al., 2010; van den Heuvel et al., 2010; Weinberger et al., 1992). For example, substance abuse involves alterations in the normal functioning of connectional zones within the ventral and dorsal striatum, which are involved in reward processing and reinforcement learning (Koob and Volkow, 2010). Similarly, depression and OCD are thought to be associated with abnormal functioning of ventral striatal locations involved in processing emotions, values, and habits (Admon and Pizzagalli, 2015; Burguiere et al., 2015).

Recognizing the importance of striatal connectional hubs in mental health has led to the development of new therapeutic approaches to target these regions with projection overlaps. The striatum is a common target for invasive (e.g., deep-brain stimulation – DBS (Greenberg et al., 2010; Malone et al., 2009) and non-invasive (e.g., real-time neurofeedback (Greer et al., 2014; Kirsch et al., 2016; Xiaolong Peng, 2024) neuromodulation. However, 20–60 % of patients do not respond to the current neuromodulation technologies (Barcia et al., 2019; Greenberg et al., 2010), as targets may not be optimized. Some authors argue that neuromodulation effectiveness may be improved by targeting regions with high connectional overlapping (Bassett and Khambhati, 2017; Haber et al., 2021). The precise translation of connectional convergence zones helps to guide future trials targeting these striatal locations.
