Strategies for engineering domesticated and undomesticated human microbes
Authors: Dake Liu, Meng-Lun Hsieh, Yousong Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human-associated microbes hold immense therapeutic potential, yet most species remain undomesticated due to cultivation barriers and limited genetic tools. Recent advances in genetic engineering are overcoming these challenges, enabling the precise manipulation of both domesticated and previously intractable microbes. This review highlights established strategies for engineering domesticated strains, such as Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 and lactic acid bacteria, for diverse therapeutic applications. We also discuss emerging tools, including optimized transformation protocols for skin commensals and genome-editing approaches for Clostridium species and undomesticated E. coli, that address key barriers in non-model microbes and expand the potential of engineered microbiome therapeutics.

Introduction

The human microbiome is a dynamic ecosystem composed of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea that inhabit various body sites, significantly influencing health and disease states [1]. These microbial communities participate in critical physiological processes, such as nutrient absorption, immune regulation, and neurodevelopment. Dysbiosis, or microbial imbalance, has been implicated in numerous disorders, including inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, diabetes, neurological conditions, and cancer [1]. Consequently, elucidating and harnessing host–microbe interactions have emerged as critical objectives in biomedical and biotechnological studies.

Considerable effort has been made to translate microbiome knowledge into therapies. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), which transfers fecal material from healthy donors to patients, is one notable approach (Figure 1a). In 2022, the FDA approved Rebyota, the first FMT product, for recurrent and refractory Clostridium difficile infection [2]. Probiotics (e.g. Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) and prebiotics also modulate microbiota (Figure 1b) but often yield inconsistent results due to complex, poorly understood human–microbe interactions [3]. This unpredictability underscores the need for targeted, mechanism-based therapies. Recent advances in genome sequencing and synthetic biology propel the engineering of microbes with novel functions or reprogrammed pathways, offering a promising path toward predictable and controllable microbiome-based therapies (Figure 1c).

Central to microbial engineering is the delivery of genetic material, primarily through conjugation, transformation, or transduction (Figure 1c) [4]. Conjugation involves the direct transfer of genetic materials (typically plasmids) between bacterial cells via physical contact. Transformation entails the uptake of free DNA from the environment, a process requiring the recipient to be naturally competent or rendered through chemical or physical treatments. Transduction utilizes bacteriophages to deliver DNA into host cells, integrating genetic material either randomly or at specific genomic sites adjacent to phage integration loci.

The success of microbiome engineering strategies largely hinges on whether target microbes are domesticated or undomesticated. Domesticated strains, such as certain Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, and Escherichia coli strains, have long been cultivated for research and industrial use due to their stable growth, well-annotated genomes, and ease of genetic manipulation [5]. In contrast, undomesticated microbes make up most microbial diversity and often exhibit poor cultivability, low transformation efficiency, and unknown defense mechanisms, posing major engineering challenges. Despite recent notable progress, over 70% of gut microbial species remain uncultured [6]. These ‘microbial dark matter’ organisms are expected to possess unique metabolic traits compared with domesticated strains. Furthermore, most culturable bacteria (over 6600 species) are not naturally competent [7], requiring customized transformation protocols. As such, undomesticated microbes represent a rich but underexplored reservoir of functional diversity with high potential for uncovering novel biology and developing new microbiome-based therapies. Importantly, advances in genetic engineering and synthetic biology are now accelerating the engineering of these strains for basic, translational, and biotechnological applications [8,9]. Using representative examples, this review highlights recent progress in microbial engineering, emphasizing novel approaches to engineer previously undomesticated human microbiome strains for the development of therapeutic applications.

Engineering domesticated human microbes for therapeutic applications

Certain human-associated microbes have been domesticated over decades, with their genetic traits finely tuned for specific therapeutic functions, such as delivering therapeutic payloads, modulating host immunity, and detecting disease-related signals (Figure 2). These microbes are amenable to standard genetic engineering techniques. Strategies for engineering domesticated human-associated microbes have been extensively reviewed in recent years [10–12]. For detailed discussions, readers are referred to these references. In this section, we highlight two representative strains to illustrate recent advances in their genetic manipulation and therapeutic applications.

Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) has emerged as a leading probiotic chassis for microbiome engineering, with extensive research focused on enhancing its therapeutic efficacy and expanding its functional capabilities [13–19]. EcN is primarily engineered through transformation and conjugation, enabling diverse therapeutic applications (Figure 2).

Both plasmid-based and genome-integrated expression systems have been successfully established in EcN. Plasmid-based systems offer tunable expression by varying copy numbers and regulatory elements. For example, Gurbatri et al. used plasmids of varied copy numbers to express checkpoint blockade nanobodies and the small molecule salicylate in EcN for colorectal cancer immunotherapy and adenoma tracking (Figure 2) [13,20]. Similarly, Vincent et al. utilized an Axe/Txe toxin-antitoxin system-stabilized plasmid to express synthetic chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) targets in EcN, enabling targeted guidance of CAR-T cells [14]. Other plasmid-based applications include tyrosinase for melanin-based immunotherapy [21] and serotonin for gut-targeted delivery (Figure 2) [17].

To improve genetic stability, genome-integrated systems have been increasingly employed. For example, the Synchronized Lysis Circuit (SLIC) was chromosomally inserted into the ϕ80 site via the Conditional-Replication, Integration, and Modular (CRIM) method [20], while Canale et al. inserted a feedback-resistant argA into an argR-deleted locus to drive sustained L-Arginine production [18]. Conjugation methods have further expanded EcN’s engineering capacity by facilitating the transfer of large or complex constructs. Gong et al. used conjugation to implement the type zero secretion system (T0SS) and a protein-packaging mechanism for local delivery of multiple enzymes, such as L-Phenylalanine deaminase StlA, catalase KatE, lactate oxidase Lox, and uricase Uox, via outer membrane vesicles [16]. Our group leveraged the CRAGE (Chassis-independent Recombinase-Assisted Genome Engineering) system developed by Wang et al. to stably integrate fluorescent/luminescent reporters and biosynthetic gene clusters into EcN (Figure 2) [15,22]. Collectively, these advances underscore EcN’s versatility as a therapeutic chassis, and conjugation-based genome engineering will play an increasingly pivotal role in enabling the stable integration of complex genetic programs.

Beyond EcN, lactic acid bacteria (LAB), particularly Lactococcus and Lactobacillus species, have long histories in food and health applications, supported by strong safety records [23]. Among them, Lactococcus lactis stands out for its genetic tractability, supported by robust transformation protocols and genome engineering tools. Consequently, L. lactis has been extensively engineered to produce a wide range of therapeutics, from small molecules such as the antioxidant lycopene [24] and the neurotransmitter GABA [25] to complex proteins including the hormones G-CSF and GLP-1 (Figure 2) [26]. These engineering efforts primarily rely on plasmid-based expression systems and genome integration strategies delivered through transformation or conjugation (Figure 2), which have also been successfully adapted for other LAB species with therapeutic potential.

Despite these advances, current engineering efforts remain focused on a limited set of cultivable and genetically tractable strains, capturing only a small portion of the ecological and functional diversity within the human microbiome. Developing broadly applicable genetic tools to access this untapped diversity will be essential for unlocking the next generation of microbiome-based therapeutics.

Engineering undomesticated human microbes for therapeutic applications

Despite the promising therapeutic potential of the human microbiome, most of its members remain genetically intractable due to poor DNA uptake, strong defense systems, and a lack of dedicated toolkits. Recent advances, however, are beginning to overcome these barriers. Modified conjugation and transformation strategies have enabled genetic manipulation. Emerging tools ranging from CRISPR-Cas and homologous recombination to allelic exchange delivered via plasmids or genome integration are now facilitating the stable expression of therapeutic genes in these microbes (Figure 3). In the following section, we highlight specific engineering strategies applied to several representative previously undomesticated strains, including Staphylococcus epidermidis, Cutibacterium acnes, Clostridium spp., and undomesticated E. coli (Figure 3). Of note, recent progress in engineering many other key taxa, particularly Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, and Lactobacillus, have offered valuable conceptual and technical insights [27,28]. Extending these advances to the broader human microbiome will be critical to unlocking its full therapeutic potential.

Optimized transformation for skin bacteria

S. epidermidis, a Gram-positive commensal bacterium, is a dominant colonizer of skin and mucosal surfaces. It contributes to barrier immunity by outcompeting pathogenic organisms, producing antimicrobial compounds, and modulating host inflammatory responses, yet it can also act as an opportunistic pathogen [29]. Genetic manipulation of primary S. epidermidis isolates has long been hampered by strain-specific restriction systems and inefficient DNA uptake via electroporation. To overcome these barriers, researchers developed an optimized engineering pipeline involving plasmid passage through E. coli DC10B (Δdcm) to evade host restriction systems, washes with sorbitol or glycerol to transiently increase membrane permeability, and a final combination of heat shock in 10% glycerol and electroporation (Figure 3a). This strategy enabled targeted genetic modifications in 13 out of 17 S. epidermidis strains tested and was further extended to other Gram-positive skin-associated bacteria, including S. aureus [29] and Corynebacterium amycolatum [30]. This work provides a powerful template for overcoming host-specific barriers to DNA delivery in previously intractable microbes.

C. acnes is another dominant member of the human skin microbiota. Genetic manipulation of C. acnes has been historically limited by inefficient, labor-intensive, and poorly reproducible transformation protocols, largely due to intrinsic barriers such as low DNA uptake, restriction-modification (R-M) systems, and weak gene expression. To overcome these limitations, researchers developed a systematic approach to enhance DNA delivery and bypass endogenous defense systems [31]. Key advancements included optimized electroporation buffer compositions and plasmid methylation mimicry to evade R-M systems (Figure 3b). Additional improvements were achieved through the use of freeze-thawed cells, cell wall weakening with Glycine or penicillin G, and the adoption of minicircle vectors from methylation-deficient E. coli strains. Recoded antibiotic resistance cassettes further enhanced homologous recombination, collectively enabling a robust, high-efficiency transformation pipeline for C. acne. Building on this foundation, recent efforts have established a comprehensive genetic toolbox that positions C. acnes as a promising chassis for microbiome-based therapeutics [32]. These developments exemplify how systematic barrier-by-barrier optimization can unlock the genetic potential of previously intractable microbes.

Engineered commensal skin bacteria have emerged as powerful tools for probing host–microbe interactions and developing novel therapeutic strategies. Recent applications include inducing tumor-specific T-cell responses [29], boosting mucosal immunity via surface modifications [33], modulating skin volatiles to reduce mosquito attraction [30], secreting therapeutic protein to modulate cutaneous sebum [31], and protecting keratinocytes from UV-induced oxidative stress [32]. Collectively, these advances highlight the versatility of engineered skin commensals as programmable living therapeutics. However, several abundant and clinically relevant skin commensals, such as Staphylococcus hominis and Corynebacterium tuberculostearicum [34], remain genetically intractable, with no published reports of successful engineering to date. Building on recent advances, including strategies to bypass R-M systems and optimize competent cell preparation, will be key to unlocking the therapeutic potential of these currently inaccessible taxa.

Genome engineering strategies for Clostridium species

Clostridium, a genus of Gram-positive obligate anaerobe, has garnered significant interest in biotechnology and pharmaceutical research, particularly as a tumor-targeting vector due to its selective germination and proliferation in hypoxic tumor regions [35,36]. Although transformation and conjugation methods have been established to introduce plasmids into certain Clostridium species [37,38], these approaches often encounter challenges, including plasmid instability and segregational loss. Consequently, genome engineering has emerged as a preferred strategy due to enhanced genetic stability and reliability.

A key method for Clostridium genome engineering is Allele-Coupled Exchange (ACE) (Figure 3c), enabling precise, scarless chromosomal integration via double-crossover homologous recombination [39]. ACE for Clostridium commonly targets the pyrE locus, conferring uracil auxotrophy and resistance to 5-fluoroorotic acid, facilitating efficient counterselection. For instance, Mowday et al. employed ACE to engineer non-pathogenic C. sporogenes expressing Neisseria meningitidis type I nitroreductase (NmeNTR) for prodrug activation in Clostridium-Directed Enzyme Prodrug Therapy (CDEPT) [40]. Combining the engineered strain with the prodrug PR-104 and the vascular disrupting agent vadimezan significantly inhibited tumor growth and improved survival compared to controls. Additionally, Donnelly et al. utilized ACE to integrate a dual transcriptional reporter system into C. difficile, allowing visualization of toxin and sporulation gene expression via fluorescent reporters mNeonGreen and mScarlet [41]. Furthermore, ACE or similar allelic exchange methods have been adapted for other genetically recalcitrant bacteria, particularly anaerobic or spore-forming organisms, including Fusobacterium nucleatum [42], Bacillus subtilis [43], and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron [44]. Successful implementation typically requires species-specific optimization of DNA delivery methods (transformation or conjugation), as well as careful selection and calibration of counterselection markers and recombination systems.

Another genome-editing method is the ClosTron system (Figure 3d), utilizing a mobile group II intron from Lactococcus lactis for targeted gene disruption and insertion [45]. Guided by base pairing and assisted by intronencoded proteins (IEP), ClosTron mediates precise genetic insertions at specific genomic sites via host DNA repair mechanisms [46]. This system has been successfully applied across various Clostridium strains [47–50]. Recently, Wang et al. enhanced ClosTron efficiency in Ruminiclostridium papyrosolvens by coupling the system with an endogenous xylan-inducible promoter (Pxyl) and a uracil phosphoribosyltransferase (Upp)-based counterselection system, thereby improving both precision and selection stringency [51]. Although originally optimized for Clostridium, mobile group II introns have also been successfully adapted for gene knockouts and insertions in a range of other undomesticated bacteria, including S. aureus, B. subtilis, and Streptomyces [52,53], owing to their high targeting efficiency without the need for selection markers.

The CRISPR-Cas systems have transformed Clostridium genome editing. Classical CRISPR-Cas9 has been applied in several species like C. difficile [54]. However, its broader use is hindered by Cas9-associated toxicity, low transformation efficiency, inefficient homology-directed repair, and off-target effects [55]. Cas12a (Cpf1) offers a promising alternative with lower toxicity, compatibility with the AT-rich Clostridium genomes with the T-rich PAMs recognition, and intrinsic RNase activity that supports multiplex editing (Figure 3e) [56]. Despite its lower toxicity compared to Cas9, Cas12a expression can still impose a burden on cells. To mitigate this, Zhang et al. developed a tetracycline-inducible CRISPR-Cas12a system employing two Cas12a variants in C. butyricum and C. sporogenes, achieving 85–100% editing efficiency and enabling near-infrared imaging through the expression of infrared fluorescent protein 2.0 (IFP2.0) [57]. Similarly, Patinios et al. employed a xylose-in-ducible CRISPR-FnCas12a system for single and multiplex gene deletions in C. beijerinckii NCIMB 8052 [56]. Inducible Cas9 or Cas12a systems decouple nuclease expression from transformation and early cell recovery, thereby reducing cytotoxicity by preventing premature double-strand breaks before repair pathways are active. Furthermore, the expression of heterologous recombination proteins like RecET, along with optimized delivery strategies, will be key to expanding CRISPR’s reach across genetically recalcitrant Clostridium strains.

Collectively, these advancements underscore the substantial progress and versatility of genome engineering methods in many previously intractable Clostridium strains, expanding their potential for diverse therapeutic and biotechnological applications. Continued optimization of these tools will likely broaden the scope of Clostridium-based solutions, facilitate more effective and precise microbiome interventions, and support their use in engineering other undomesticated microbial taxa.

Engineering undomesticated E. coli by phage-mediated transduction and CRAGE method

While E. coli strains like EcN and MG1655 are widely used in genetic engineering, many human- and environment-associated strains remain challenging to manipulate. The gut-native strain EcAZ-1, isolated from a conventionally raised mouse, is an undomesticated E. coli isolate that can stably colonize the gut for over six months without perturbing the native microbiota [58], making it a promising chassis for bacterial diagnostics and therapeutics. Owing to its transformation resistance, Russell et al. used P1vir phage transduction, with MG1655-derived lysate (Figure 3f), to introduce genes like bile salt hydrolase bsh and gfp into EcAZ-1 [58]. To enable stable integration of larger constructs, our group employed CRAGE [22] to successfully integrate biosynthetic gene clusters for natural products such as naringenin and mycosporine-like amino acids directly into the EcAZ-1 genome (Figure 3g) [15]. Notably, CRAGE has been applied to engineer ~60 non-model strains, mostly from Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria [22,59]. The success of phage-mediated transduction and CRAGE in EcAZ-1 highlights their potential to overcome long-standing barriers in engineering other undomesticated, physiologically relevant E. coli strains, and, more broadly, to extend synthetic biology into the wider landscape of human-associated microbes.

Outlook

Microbiome engineering holds immense potential for therapeutic delivery, immune modulation, and disease prevention. While model strains like EcN and Lactococcus lactis have provided crucial proof-of-concept, the vast majority of human-associated microbes remain genetically intractable. Recent advances, such as CRISPR-Cas (notably Cas12a), ACE, CRAGE, ClosTron, and optimized transformation approaches, are beginning to overcome these barriers, enabling precise engineering in diverse non-model organisms (Figure 3) and expanding their applications to tumor targeting, immune modulation, and pathogen control. Looking forward, progress will depend on the integration of synthetic biology with high-throughput culturing, multiomics analyses, and computational modeling to design programmable, multi-strain consortia capable of dynamic responses to host and environmental cues. Equally critical will be the development of standardized toolkits, strain-specific genetic resources, and rigorous biosafety frameworks to support clinical translation. Bridging the gap between model and non-model microbes is, in our view, the next frontier for unlocking the full therapeutic potential of the human microbiome.
