Radiomic clustering using graph network techniques coupled with unbalanced optimal transport
Authors: Jung Hun Oh, Aditya Apte, Harini Veeraraghavan, Jiening Zhu, Amita Shukla-Dave, Joseph O. Deasy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Radiomic data analysis frequently requires identifying major sub-groups within high-dimensional datasets. However, many datasets are limited in size, hindering the effective application of machine learning methods. We adopted a regularized network model coupled with the extended Bayesian information criterion to identify sub-networks and developed a graph network-based k-means clustering algorithm in connection with unbalanced optimal transport to group samples. Survival analysis and CIBERSORT analysis with tumor immune cell abundance were conducted between the identified sub-groups. This approach was applied to two cancer types: head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), using radiomic features extracted from computed tomography (CT) scans and RNA-Seq gene expression profiles. For HNSCC, high and low-risk groups were identified from the largest sub-network using the proposed method. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a statistically significant difference in progression-free survival between the high and low-risk groups (p = 0.0202). In NSCLC, Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a statistically significant difference in overall survival between the high and low-risk groups identified from the second largest sub-network (p = 0.0007). The NSCLC radiomic data were assessed on the HNSCC network, validating statistical significance with p = 0.0007. In CIBERSORT analysis for HNSCC, neutrophils showed a statistically significant difference between the high and low-risk groups (p = 0.0221). In NSCLC, resting dendritic cells and activated mast cells showed statistically significant differences with p = 0.0126 and 0.0046, respectively. We demonstrated that closely related tumor radiomic characteristics can effectively identify radiophenotypes with distinct prognoses. Our specific findings suggest that these image characteristics may be associated with varying tumor-immune interactions.

Introduction

Radiomics is a method for extracting numerous quantitative features from radiological images. It is frequently employed to characterize inter-patient heterogeneity, which may be associated with variations in treatment response [1], [2], [3]. A number of studies have demonstrated the potential of machine learning with radiomic inputs to predict diagnosis and prognosis [4], [5], [6], [7]. However, this field still faces several challenges. The high dimensionality and multicollinearity in radiomic data impede identifying a key set of features associated with treatment outcomes. In particular, supervised machine learning approaches that require massive parameter fittings may cause an overfitting problem in modeling and hamper the validation of previously developed models [8]. Furthermore, while machine learning models trained with many features can be powerful, they often come at the cost of interpretability.

To address these issues, we propose a novel unsupervised graph network-based method for analyzing radiomic data represented as a form of network that is constructed based on partial correlation coefficients of radiomic features [9]. In this approach, a regularized network model coupled with the extended Bayesian information criterion was utilized to sparsify and break down the network into sub-networks [10]. The sparse network enhances the interpretability of the radiomic model by visualizing dependency among features. Sample clustering was then conducted on the resulting sub-networks to identify sub-groups of patient samples, using an integrated mathematics and machine learning approach, namely the unbalanced optimal transport-based k-means algorithm called UOTK.

In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), we investigated whether the proposed network-based analysis technique using radiomic data can identify patients at high risk of poor prognosis and further explored whether the survival difference between the identified sub-groups is associated with tumor immune infiltrates, using tumor immune cell abundance quantified on RNA-Seq gene expression profiles. This radiogenomic analysis enhances our understanding of biological findings derived from radiomic analysis by correlating with genomic phenotypes [11], [12], [13].

Materials and methods

Data

In the current radiogenomic study, radiomic features derived from pre-treatment computed tomography (CT) scans and CIBERSORT scores computed on RNA-Seq gene expression profiles were analyzed for two cancer types: HNSCC and NSCLC. For radiomic analysis, image biomarker standardization initiative (IBSI) compliant 397 radiomic features, consisting of first order statistics, shape-based features, and higher order texture features, were extracted from primary tumors manually contoured on CT scans, using our in-house computational environment for radiotherapy research (CERR, version 56183a7) radiomics toolbox [14]. For HNSCC, CT imaging data for 77 patients were downloaded from The Cancer Imaging Atlas (TCIA) [15]. A radiation oncologist manually contoured primary tumors and the delineation was independently confirmed by a neuroradiologist as described in our previous study [5]. For NSCLC, CT imaging data, along with contoured tumor regions of interest (ROIs) for 117 patients, were downloaded from the TCIA [16], [17].

For genomic analysis, RNA-Seq gene expression profiles for the matched 77 The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-HNSCC cases were downloaded from the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org) [18]. For NSCLC, RNA-Seq gene expression profiles for the matched 117 NSCLC cases were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE103584). Using the R package immunedeconv (version 2.1.0) [19], CIBERSORT scores that quantify the abundance of 22 immune cell types employing a support vector regression-based deconvolution method were estimated for HNSCC and NSCLC cohorts [20].

Radiomic data extraction

Radiomic features were extracted using the CERR software following IBSI definitions. These included the scalar radiomics (IBSI1) and image filters (IBSI2). IBSI1 features consisted of (i) first order statistics, (ii) shape-based features, and (iii) higher order textures. Higher order texture features included gray level cooccurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), neighborhood gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM), and neighboring gray level dependence matrix (NGLDM). For texture features, the intensity values in each CT scan were discretized between −1000 and 300 using a bin width of 5 Hounsfield units (HU). This ensured that the first bin starts at −1000 HU for each CT scan. Scans were resampled to an isotropic resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm. IBSI2 features consisted of first order statistics from Laplacian of Gaussian, Laws, and Gabor-filtered images.

Regularized radiomic networks

In general, radiomic features tend to be highly correlated with each other, which may hamper identifying a key set of imaging markers associated with the endpoint of interest. To handle this issue, we adopted a Gaussian graphical model applied to partial correlation coefficients of radiomic features represented as a form of network. More specifically, each node in a network indicates a radiomic feature and each link between two connected nodes represents a partial correlation coefficient computed between the two features after conditioning on all other features [10]. This approach tends to reduce the complexity of network structure and enhance the interpretability of data analytics by visualizing relationships between radiomic features. However, the resulting network still may have spurious links that represent false positive relationships between the radiomic features. To remove such likely spurious links, we employed the graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) that further shrinks partial correlation coefficients [21], [22]. In the current study, we utilized the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) to optimize the fit of the regularized network constructed using the graphical LASSO [10]. This analysis was conducted using the R package qgraph (version 1.9.8) [23] with a hyperparameter γ set to 0.5 (the default value), as suggested by Foygel and Drton [24].

Unbalanced optimal transport

Suppose that μ and ν are two probability measures on X=Rn and c(x,y) is the transportation cost to move one unit of mass from x to y. The original optimal mass transport (OMT) problem seeks a transport map T: Rn→Rn:x↦T(x) such that the total transportation cost ∫Rnc(x,Tx)μ(dx) is minimized over the set of all transport maps T. This original non-linear OMT problem can be relaxed by the Kantorovich’s formulation that replaces transport maps T with couplings π:infπΠμ,ν∫Rn×Rncx,yπdx,dy,where Π(μ,ν) is the set of all joint probability measures between two marginals, μ and ν.

In the current study, we employed the unbalanced optimal transport (UOT) technique, which is an unbalanced extension of optimal transport to compute distances between a pair of samples, not restricted to probability distributions [25]. Let C and T (C, T∈Rn×n) be the cost and transport plan matrices, respectively, for a given connected network, consisting of n radiomic features. For cx,y, the shortest path between radiomic features on the given network was used, which was computed employing the Dijkstra's algorithm. Network-based k-means clustering

k-means clustering is an unsupervised machine learning technique that clusters data into a k number of groups by assigning each data point to a cluster such that the sum of the squared distance between each data point and the centroid in a cluster is minimized [26]. Here, we propose a novel clustering method in which the cost function in the conventional k-means algorithm is replaced with the Wasserstein distance metric computed using the UOT in a radiomic network, as shown in the following equation:argmin∑i=1k∑xj∈kiW1xj,mi,

where W1 is the Wasserstein distance computed using the UOT and mi is the centroid of cluster i.

More specifically, given a radiomic network, the Wasserstein distance W1 from each centroid in k clusters to samples is computed and each sample is assigned to a cluster such that the within-cluster sum of Wasserstein distance is minimized. During the process of k-means clustering, each centroid in k clusters is sequentially updated. This process is iterated until the centroids do not change within a tolerance of 1E-05. We call the k-means method coupled with the UOT-based Wasserstein distance the UOTK algorithm.

Statistical analysis

Using our proposed UOTK method, sub-groups of samples were identified. The silhouette score was used to determine the optimal number of sub-groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis with a log-rank test was then conducted to compare the survival difference between the identified sub-groups. Statistical analysis was performed to compare differences in clinical variables and CIBERSORT scores between the identified sub-groups, using a two-sample t-test and a Fisher’s exact test for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Fig. 2 illustrates the radiogenomic data analysis pipeline.Fig. 2Radiogenomic data analysis pipeline. Results

Radiomic network analysis

Tumor volume is a key prognostic factor and many radiomic features are inherently highly correlated with tumor volume, providing redundant information. Therefore, prior to radiomic network analysis, we filtered out those features highly correlated with tumor volume with a threshold of Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.8. Radiomic features remained unnormalized, with the exception of those containing negative values; these were transformed between 0 and 1 using min-max normalization before computing the Wasserstein distance. Weak connections in the network were further removed with a threshold of partial correlation coefficient of 0.15. Fig. 3 illustrates the resulting sub-networks for HNSCC, wherein the largest connected sub-network consisted of 65 radiomic features. For NSCLC, the largest and second largest connected sub-networks consisted of 68 and 56 radiomic features, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1). Interestingly, between the largest sub-network in HNSCC and the second largest sub-network in NSCLC, 50 radiomic features were overlapping, which consisted of tumor volume and 34 and 15 first order statistics derived from Laws and Gabor-filtered images, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). The maximum distance (number of hops) between any two nodes was 17 for both the largest sub-network in HNSCC and the second largest sub-network in NSCLC. Survival analysis

Using the UOTK method, sample clustering was performed for individual resulting sub-networks with 2–4 clusters. In the HNSCC cohort, when two clusters were used, Kaplan-Meier analysis conducted on the largest sub-network showed the most significant p-value in progression-free survival (log-rank p = 0.0202, hazard ratio [HR] = 1.88, 95 % confidence interval [CI] = 0.80–4.39; Table 1 and Fig. 4). The two clusters were defined as a high-risk group and a low-risk group based on survival rates. Diseases-specific survival and overall survival analyses resulted in borderline significance between the high and low-risk groups with p = 0.0559 and 0.1387, respectively (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3). In the NSCLC cohort, Kaplan-Meier analysis conducted on the largest sub-network did not show a statistically significant difference in overall survival between the identified sub-groups, whereas a statistically significant difference in overall survival was found in Kaplan-Meier analysis conducted on the second largest sub-network; the most significant p-value was found when two clusters were used (p = 0.0007, HR = 2.86, 95 % CI = 1.51–5.40; Table 1 and Fig. 5). Again, the two clusters were defined as a high-risk group and a low-risk group based on survival rates. In addition, the silhouette score test determined two clusters as the optimal partition (Supplementary Table S2) in both HNSCC and NSCLC datasets. Comparison with benchmark methods

Our proposed network-based k-means coupled with UOT (UOTK) was compared with the network-based k-means coupled with balanced optimal transport and traditional k-means with 2–4 clusters. Note that for balanced optimal transport, all features in a sample are converted to probability measures, summing up to 1. As shown in Table 1, UOTK using two clusters yielded the most significant log-rank p-values in Kaplan-Meier analysis for both HNSCC and NSCLC.

CIBERTSORT analysis

Tumor immune cell abundance quantified using the CIBERSORT method was compared between the high and low-risk groups in the HNSCC cohort. Neutrophils showed a statistically significant difference (t-test p = 0.0221) with the low-risk group having higher abundance compared to the high-risk group. For the NSCLC cohort, resting dendritic cells and activated mast cells showed statistically significant differences between the high and low-risk groups with p = 0.0126 and 0.0046, respectively (Fig. 6). Discussion

Radiomic analysis of tumors often involves extracting numerous quantitative features (radiomic features) for prognostic or predictive modeling [1], [2]. Modeling approaches for radiomic analysis include low-dimensional methods (e.g., regularized regression), machine learning (e.g., random forests), and deep learning, often with survival as the endpoint [27], [28], [29]. Supervised machine learning modeling on radiomic data faces several challenges, including overfitting due to the 'curse of dimensionality', high feature correlation (multicollinearity), and limited model interpretability.

To handle these issues, we propose a novel unsupervised modeling method for radiomic data analysis. Our network-based analysis pipeline consists of two steps on a radiomic network constructed based on partial correlation coefficients of radiomic features: (1) sparsify and break down a network into sub-networks using the graphical LASSO and (2) cluster samples using the UOT-based k-means method applied to the resulting sub-networks. This approach refines the network by removing spurious connections, leading to a more interpretable representation that facilitates the identification of clinically relevant associations with survival outcomes in downstream analysis. Our method's key strengths, compared to alternative approaches such as graph neural networks, are its enhanced interpretability and unsupervised, parameter-free design. Conversely, graph neural networks depend on many parameters, require large sample sizes, and are more prone to overfitting noise or data-specific biases. Compared to balanced optimal transport, which strictly requires equal total mass between the source and target distributions, UOT offers greater flexibility in handling marginal constraints. Moreover, while balanced optimal transport is highly sensitive to noise or outliers, UOT can simply discard the mass associated with them during its process.

Sample clustering in HNSCC was conducted using the UOTK method on the resulting largest sub-network, consisting of 65 radiomic features. Survival analysis revealed a significant difference in progression-free survival between the high and low-risk groups identified by the clustering. Although disease-specific survival (Supplementary Figure S2) and overall survival p-values (Supplementary Figure S3) were not statistically significant, they showed borderline significance. Survival analysis in NSCLC revealed a significant difference in overall survival between the high and low-risk groups identified by the clustering on the second largest sub-network, which consisted of 56 radiomic features. Intriguingly, there was an overlap of 50 radiomic features between the HNSCC and NSCLC sub-networks. Notably, applying the HNSCC model to the NSCLC dataset resulted in a statistically significant difference in overall survival between the high and low-risk groups in NSCLC, indicating the model's robustness and its capability to stratify patients effectively.

While no clinical variables showed significant differences between the high and low-risk groups in HNSCC, T and M stages differed significantly in NSCLC. Although this study focuses on radiomic analysis, future work will explore integrating radiomic features with clinical variables for improved modeling.

The link between survival outcomes and the tumor immune microenvironment was investigated. In HNSCC, our analysis found that the low-risk group had a higher abundance of neutrophils. A study in HNSCC reported that the high-risk group had a higher abundance of neutrophils in CIBERSORT analysis, but a lower abundance in xCell analysis [30]. Future studies should focus on exploring the relationship between radiomic features and the tumor immune microenvironment with more datasets. In NSCLC, the high-risk group was found to have a higher abundance of resting dendritic cells and activated mast cells in our analysis. These findings were consistent with previous lung cancer studies; Hu et al. reported that the high-risk group had a higher percentage of resting dendritic cells [31] and Kurbatov et al. reported that the high-risk group had increased activated mast cell fraction [32].

Our proposed method (UOTK) was compared with other alternative methods, including the network-based k-means coupled with balanced optimal transport and traditional k-means with 2–4 clusters. UOTK using two clusters resulted in the most significant difference in survival curves between the high and low-risk groups for both HNSCC and NSCLC. Notably, while the traditional k-means method also showed similar results for NSCLC, it did not effectively separate the high and low-risk groups in HNSCC.

The current study has several limitations that need to be discussed. First, it is difficult to identify the optimal network size. While a larger network can capture more complex relationships in radiomic data, potentially leading to better predictive performance until some point, increasing network size beyond a certain point also brings challenges: (1) false positive information can be added, degrading predictive performance and (2) modeling a large network requires more computational cost and resources. Supervised learning can help find the optimal network size. Secondly, in the UOT analysis, α was used as a cost for injection and extraction of mass. The optimal value of α can be dependent on the network complexity. Our test with several different values of α showed that it had little to no effect on the clustering results and α = 1 was used in this study. Lastly, the sample size in both cohorts is relatively small. However, it is important to note that our radiomic network model utilizes an unsupervised approach that requires no parameter fitting. To ensure robustness, we used two distinct cancer datasets and performed external validation by training the model on one dataset and testing it on the other. We plan to apply the network-based radiomic analysis approach to different imaging modalities beyond the one used in the current study. This will broaden the applicability of the proposed method.

Conclusion

In the current study, we demonstrated the potential of using a graph network-based approach for radiomic analysis. Our proposed UOTK clustering method results in an embedding that can detect subtle differences or similarities between images, thus providing a robust tool to detect radiomic subtypes associated with poor prognosis while avoiding parameter fitting. Further analysis on tumor immune cell abundance suggests that poor survival in the identified sub-group is likely to be associated with the tumor immune microenvironment.
