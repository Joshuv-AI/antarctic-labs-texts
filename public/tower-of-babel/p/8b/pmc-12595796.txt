Women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding perimenopausal syndrome in China
Authors: Wei Li, Wen Tan, Xiuhua Que
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of perimenopausal syndrome has increased among women in China, leading to considerable physical and emotional distress. This study aimed to assess women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding perimenopausal syndrome. Unlike previous studies, this research provides a comprehensive analysis of KAP among a broad age range of Chinese women and explores the influence of sociocultural factors on their experiences.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at The Affiliated People’s Hospital of Ningbo University, Ningbo, China, between April and August 2024. A self-developed questionnaire was used to collect demographic information, KAP data, and life satisfaction scores using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS).ResultsA total of 403 valid questionnaires were analyzed; 7.69% of participants had been diagnosed with perimenopausal syndrome. The mean SWLS score was 22.60 ± 5.27 (range: 5–35). The mean knowledge score was 9.86 ± 1.25 (range: 0–12), the mean attitude score was 29.49 ± 2.56 (range: 8–40), and the mean practice score was 32.30 ± 5.25 (range: 9–45). Correlation analysis showed positive associations between knowledge and attitude, attitude and practice, attitude and SWLS, and practice and SWLS (r = 0.112–0.344, P < 0.001). Knowledge was negatively correlated with SWLS (r=-0.133, P = 0.007). Multivariate logistic regression found that both attitude (OR = 1.304, 95% CI: 1.180–1.448, P < 0.001) and SWLS (OR = 1.080, 95% CI: 1.028–1.135, P = 0.002) were positively associated with practice. Structural equation modeling further revealed indirect associations between SWLS and attitude (β=-0.018, 95% CI: -0.032 to -0.009, P = 0.004) and between knowledge and practice (β = 0.366, 95% CI: 0.202–0.519, P = 0.009).ConclusionWomen in China demonstrated good knowledge but only moderate attitudes and practices regarding perimenopausal syndrome. This study highlights the need for educational and behavioral interventions that account for sociocultural influences to improve women’s health during the perimenopausal transition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25063-3.Keywords: Perimenopausal syndrome, Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Women in china, Cross-sectional study

Background

Perimenopause is a natural transition period before menopause, marked by changing and eventually declining levels of estrogen and progesterone as ovarian follicles are gradually depleted and the hormonal axis shifts [1, 2]. This stage is part of normal female aging rather than a disease. However, it is often linked to a range of symptoms that can seriously affect daily life and well-being. These symptoms commonly include irregular menstruation, which can last for several years before periods stop completely [3]. The most frequent symptoms are hot flashes and night sweats (vasomotor symptoms), mood swings, anxiety, depressive symptoms (psychological changes), and physical complaints such as sleep problems, joint pain, and changes in sexual health [4, 5]. These issues arise from changing hormone levels, which can affect many body systems, including the nervous, heart, and musculoskeletal systems [1, 6].

Globally, menopause-related symptoms are very common but vary in frequency. For example, a recent meta-analysis found that formication affected 20.5% of women, while joint and muscle discomfort affected 65.4% [1, 4]. In China, the rate of perimenopausal syndrome among women aged 40–65 years has increased to 61.0%, and in some regions, such as Southwest China, rates are as high as 71.3% [3, 5].

Unmanaged perimenopausal symptoms can lead to more than discomfort. They also increase the risk of developing metabolic problems, osteoporosis, and heart disease. Effective management is vital during this important stage of life [7, 8]. In addition, psychological distress and reduced physical activity due to symptoms like fatigue or joint pain can further worsen overall health and reduce social and work functioning [9]. Stigma and misunderstandings about menopause can make it harder for women to seek help or talk openly about their experiences, especially within certain cultural contexts [10].

Early recognition and diagnosis of perimenopausal symptoms are very important. Timely intervention can relieve symptoms, prevent long-term complications, and improve overall quality of life [11]. Still, outcomes among women vary greatly. This variation reflects not only biological differences but also differences in healthcare access, health literacy, and cultural attitudes [12, 13]. In China, traditional beliefs, limited awareness, and low use of healthcare services may further complicate how women manage perimenopausal health [14, 15]. Therefore, there is a need for a comprehensive study of women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) about perimenopausal syndrome in the Chinese context.

The KAP model is widely used in public health to understand how information and beliefs affect health-related behaviors [16]. According to this model, increased knowledge about a health issue can lead to more positive attitudes, which in turn encourage healthier practices [17]. Many studies confirm that these factors are linked: health education can improve knowledge, change attitudes, and ultimately improve health behaviors [18]. Understanding the relationships among knowledge, attitudes, and practices is essential for designing effective interventions for women during perimenopause.

Previous research on KAP related to perimenopausal syndrome has found many gaps. For example, studies in Saudi Arabia reported low knowledge and little healthcare-seeking among women with perimenopausal symptoms [13]. Similarly, research in India and some areas of China found that women often lacked adequate knowledge and did not follow recommended health practices during this transition [15, 19]. However, most previous studies in China have only focused on specific issues—such as sleep habits or hormone therapy—rather than providing an overall picture of KAP about the causes, prevention, and management of perimenopausal syndrome. In addition, the impact of cultural and healthcare system factors on women’s experiences remains largely unexplored.

To address these gaps, our study (1) includes a broad age range of Chinese women, (2) incorporates the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) to assess life satisfaction, and (3) applies structural equation modeling (SEM) to explore both direct and indirect associations among KAP and SWLS. By integrating these methods, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of how knowledge, attitudes, practices, and life satisfaction interact within the unique sociocultural context of China.

We hypothesized that greater knowledge would lead to more positive attitudes and healthier practices, and that attitudes would be associated with better health behaviors. This evidence can help guide the development of targeted interventions to support women during the perimenopausal transition.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at The Affiliated People’s Hospital of Ningbo University, Ningbo, China, from April to August 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of The Affiliated People’s Hospital of Ningbo University (approval number 2024-032), and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Eligible participants were women aged 20 to 70 years who had resided in mainland China for an extended period and were attending gynecology clinics at the study site. Women aged 20 years and above were intentionally included, as early childbearing and increasing rates of ovarian insufficiency in younger women are common in China. This broader age range enables the assessment of KAP regarding perimenopausal syndrome among both perimenopausal and non-perimenopausal women, thereby supporting early intervention and education. All participants were required to have the cognitive and communicative ability to understand and complete the questionnaire.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) women aged 20–70 years; (2) long-term residency in mainland China; (3) ability to provide informed consent; (4) no history of major diseases affecting vital organs; (5) no diagnosed psychiatric disorders. Participants were not required to be currently perimenopausal; reproductive status and relevant gynecological history were collected for subgroup analysis. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe allergic diseases; (2) pregnancy or current breastfeeding; (3) undergoing special medical treatments (e.g., anticancer therapy); (4) inability to complete the survey independently.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire was developed based on national and international guidelines for diagnosing and managing perimenopausal syndrome [20, 21], as well as relevant KAP research literature [22]. Content validity was confirmed by review from two chief physicians and two associate chief physicians in gynecology, one with additional qualifications in psychological counseling and nutrition. The instrument was revised for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness based on their feedback.

A pilot test with 27 participants was conducted to assess clarity and reliability. The overall Cronbach’s alpha for the final questionnaire was 0.765, indicating good internal consistency.

The final questionnaire (Chinese-language; English translation provided as an Appendix) included five sections: (1) Demographic and reproductive history. (2) Knowledge: 13 questions assessing understanding of perimenopausal syndrome (concept, symptoms, risks). Two trap questions (items 4 and 5) identified inconsistent answers; only 11 items counted toward the total knowledge score (range: 0–11). Each correct answer scored 1; incorrect or “don’t know” scored 0. (3) Attitude: 8 items on a 5-point Likert scale from “Strongly Agree” (5 points) to “Strongly Disagree” (1 point), with items 3–6 reverse scored. Total attitude score range: 8–40 [23]. (4) Practice: 9 items, 5-point Likert scale from “Strongly Consistent” (5 points) to “Strongly Inconsistent” (1 point), total score range: 9–45. (5) SWLS: Validated 5-item measure of global life satisfaction on a 7-point Likert scale, total score 5–35 [24, 25].

KAP levels were classified as “good” (80–100%), “moderate” (60–79%), and “poor” (< 60%), following established conventions based on Bloom’s cut-off points [26]. This approach facilitates meaningful comparison across studies and is supported by Bloom’s taxonomy, which groups performance into three levels.

Data collection and quality control

Data were collected using the Wenjuanxing electronic survey platform, distributed via WeChat, QQ groups, and in-person clinics. Participants accessed the survey through a QR code or hyperlink. Trained research assistants (physicians) supervised recruitment and data collection, ensuring completeness and logical consistency of responses. Submissions with errors in trap questions, incomplete answers, or uniform responses were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and n (%) for categorical variables. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and homogeneity of variance by Levene’s test [27]. For group comparisons, the independent-samples t-test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normal distributions. For comparisons across three or more groups, one-way ANOVA (with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons) was used for normal data, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normal data. Pearson’s correlation was used for normally distributed variables; Spearman’s correlation was used otherwise. Internal consistency reliability was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha for the overall questionnaire. For multivariate analysis, binary logistic regression was performed with the practice score (dichotomized at the 75th percentile) as the dependent variable [28]. Variables with P < 0.2 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model, reducing omitted variable bias [29]. Details of the sample size calculation and full statistical formulas are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Results

A total of 610 questionnaires were collected. After exclusions—six respondents who declined consent, two respondents under 18 years or with abnormal responses, 140 due to logical errors in trap questions, 32 with a BMI over 40, and 27 with unreasonable answers—403 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis. The mean age of participants was 38.92 years (SD = 9.70). Most participants lived in urban areas (74.19%), were married (79.65%), and held an associate or undergraduate degree (72.95%). Over half (53.85%) reported having both social and commercial health insurance, and 76.67% had given birth. Regarding reproductive health, 48.64% had experienced miscarriage, 50.87% had undergone gynecological surgery, 32.26% reported uterine fibroids or ovarian cysts, and 7.69% had been diagnosed with perimenopausal syndrome (Table 1).

Characteristics of participants and their KAP score (N = 403)

The mean SWLS score was 22.60 (SD = 5.27). Married women (M = 23.09, SD = 5.25) reported higher life satisfaction than single/divorced/other women (M = 20.67, SD = 4.91), with a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.48, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Women with a history of childbirth also had significantly higher SWLS scores (M = 23.13, SD = 5.32) than those without childbirth experience (M = 20.85, SD = 4.75, d = 0.44, P < 0.001). Positive agreement rates for individual SWLS items ranged from 27.54% (“would change nothing if given another chance”) to 64.76% (“satisfaction with life”), and 55.58% felt they had achieved what was most important in life (Table S1).

The mean knowledge score was 9.86 (SD = 1.25). Urban participants had higher scores (M = 10.00, SD = 1.18) than rural participants (M = 9.44, SD = 1.35; d = 0.44, P < 0.001). Participants with an associate or undergraduate degree (M = 10.00, SD = 1.20) scored higher than those with middle school or below (M = 8.90, SD = 1.27; d = 0.96, P < 0.001). Knowledge scores also varied significantly by occupation (η² = 0.04, P = 0.001), type of health insurance (η² = 0.02, P = 0.032), history of gynecological surgery (d = 0.20, P = 0.038), and history of uterine fibroids or ovarian cysts (d = 0.39, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Accuracy for individual knowledge items ranged from 67.25% to 99.75%. The highest accuracy (99.75%) was for recognition of mood fluctuations and memory impairment as symptoms (K6), while the lowest (67.25%) was for the misconception about vaginal dryness and genitourinary atrophy (K7) (Table S2).

The mean attitude score was 29.49 (SD = 2.56). Urban women (M = 29.68, SD = 2.61) had more positive attitudes than rural women (M = 28.94, SD = 2.33; d = 0.29, P = 0.011). Higher education was associated with more positive attitudes, with those holding a master’s degree or above (M = 30.40, SD = 2.53) scoring higher than those with middle school or below (M = 27.87, SD = 1.89; d = 1.08, P < 0.001). Significant differences were also found by history of miscarriage (d = 0.26, P = 0.007) and gynecological surgery (d = 0.26, P = 0.011) (Table 1). Agreement rates for attitude items ranged from 5.21% to 95.54%. The highest was for recognizing the importance of understanding perimenopausal syndrome (A1), and the lowest was for lack of concern about its impact on life quality (A3). Additionally, 19.85% reported difficulty discussing perimenopausal syndrome (A6) (Table S3).

The mean practice score was 32.30 (SD = 5.25). Married women (M = 32.76, SD = 5.11) had higher practice scores than single/divorced/other women (M = 30.48, SD = 5.43; d = 0.43, P < 0.001). Practice scores also differed significantly by occupation (η² = 0.03, P = 0.042), type of health insurance (η² = 0.02, P = 0.043), and childbirth history (d = 0.46, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Adherence rates for individual practice items ranged from 31.76% to 87.34%. The highest adherence (87.34%) was for following prescribed treatment plans (P9), while the lowest (31.76%) was for maintaining a consistent exercise routine (P4). Only 36.23% regularly tracked menopausal symptoms and management effectiveness (P6) (Table S4).

Correlation analysis indicated several significant positive relationships: knowledge and attitude (r = 0.260, P < 0.001), attitude and practice (r = 0.344, P < 0.001), SWLS and attitude (r = 0.112, P = 0.025), and SWLS and practice (r = 0.292, P < 0.001). In contrast, there was a small but statistically significant negative correlation between SWLS and knowledge (r = −0.133, P = 0.007), indicating that women who reported higher life satisfaction tended to have slightly lower knowledge scores. This unexpected pattern suggests a complex relationship between subjective well-being and health information, warranting further exploration (Table 2).

Discussion

This study investigated knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to perimenopausal syndrome among women in China, revealing overall good knowledge but only moderate attitudes and practices. Significant interrelationships among KAP components were observed, and structural equation modeling (SEM) further illuminated both direct and indirect pathways—including a notable negative association between life satisfaction (SWLS) and knowledge. These findings underscore the need for targeted, culturally sensitive educational and behavioral interventions to enhance women’s health during the perimenopausal transition.

Our results align with previous research from Iran, where women aged 45–60 years exhibited relatively high knowledge and positive attitudes toward menopausal health [30]. In contrast, several Chinese studies have reported lower knowledge and less active management regarding specific aspects of perimenopausal health, such as sleep hygiene and hormone therapy [15, 19]. Studies from Saudi Arabia and India have similarly noted insufficient knowledge and low healthcare-seeking behaviors [13, 19]. Unlike most prior research, our study included a broader age range (20–70 years), assessed overall life satisfaction with the SWLS, and used SEM to capture both direct and indirect relationships among KAP components. This integrative approach provides a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of women’s experiences and the sociocultural context shaping them.

A particularly important behavioral gap was identified in the low rates of regular exercise (31.76%) and symptom tracking (36.23%) among participants. These findings are concerning given the well-established benefits of physical activity and self-monitoring in symptom control, mental health, and long-term disease prevention [31]. Barriers in the Chinese context may include lack of motivation, competing responsibilities, or inadequate access to resources, as well as urban-rural disparities in access to fitness facilities and health information [31, 32]. The limited proportion tracking their symptoms raises concerns about self-management practices among Chinese women [33]. Barriers may include lack of knowledge regarding the importance of monitoring symptoms, the absence of accessible tools such as mobile applications or regular healthcare consultations, and limited engagement with primary care [33]. Cultivating supportive groups and encouraging healthcare consultations may enhance women’s confidence in self-management practices. Recent pilot programs using WeChat-based support groups and digital health records have shown promise for improving self-management among middle-aged women in China [33].

Multivariate analysis confirmed that neither knowledge nor demographic factors alone were sufficient predictors of positive practice; rather, attitudes and life satisfaction emerged as more robust determinants. This underscores the importance of multifaceted interventions that not only educate but also address motivation, perceived barriers, and the provision of practical tools (such as digital symptom trackers or tailored exercise programs) to support behavioral change [32].

The SWLS results indicated that only about half of participants felt they had achieved what was most important in life, and nearly one-third would choose to change aspects of their lives if given another chance. These findings echo research linking life satisfaction to psychosocial factors such as age, health status, and social support [34]. The perimenopausal transition may exacerbate feelings of unfulfillment and vulnerability, particularly in the context of physical and emotional challenges [35]. Healthcare providers should not only address the physical aspects of menopause but also promote psychological resilience and self-acceptance. Initiatives that facilitate peer support and empowerment can help women articulate their goals and navigate feelings of dissatisfaction [35].

Most participants were aware of the cognitive and emotional challenges associated with perimenopause. This increased awareness may stem from recent educational efforts surrounding women’s health and increased media coverage of perimenopausal experiences [36, 37]. Nevertheless, a significant proportion of women incorrectly believed that symptoms like vaginal dryness and genitourinary atrophy are not typically associated with perimenopausal syndrome. These symptoms can be caused by multifaceted factors, such as decreased estrogen levels, aging, reduced blood flow, and vulvovaginal inflammation [38, 39]. Therefore, healthcare providers should proactively encourage conversations about sexual health, offering information and resources to empower women during this transitional phase.

In the attitude dimension, the majority of participants recognized the importance of understanding perimenopausal syndrome. Consistent with previous studies, women who were more informed about menopausal health tended to report better health outcomes and improved quality of life [40]. Conversely, the lowest proportion expressed no concerns about the detrimental impacts of perimenopausal syndrome, suggesting heightened anxiety toward the condition. Moreover, several participants indicated discomfort discussing perimenopausal syndrome, highlighting a significant barrier to effective healthcare delivery. Cultural stigmas and societal taboos surrounding menopausal health have been identified as substantial obstacles to open dialogue [37]. Reportedly, women may feel ill-prepared to articulate their experiences, particularly regarding sensitive issues like sexual health, vaginal dryness, or emotional changes [38]. Healthcare professionals should be trained to initiate these discussions sensitively, provide reassurance, and foster an atmosphere where women feel comfortable expressing their concerns.

The majority of participants showed adherence to prescribed treatment plans, possibly due to increased awareness of the implications of perimenopausal syndrome. However, less than half maintained consistent exercise routines. Regular physical activity is widely recognized for its benefits in alleviating menopausal symptoms, improving mood, and enhancing overall quality of life [31]. The low engagement in exercise can be influenced by various factors, including lack of motivation, or inadequate access to resources. Integrating discussions about lifestyle changes into routine consultations is required for women to adopt healthier practices. The limited proportion who tracked their symptoms and management effectiveness further highlights barriers to self-management [33]. One potential barrier is a lack of knowledge regarding the importance of monitoring symptoms, while the absence of accessible tools such as mobile applications and regular healthcare consultations can further inhibit this practice [33]. Cultivating supportive groups and encouraging healthcare consultations may enhance women’s confidence in self-management practices.

In line with the health belief model, our results suggest that educational interventions aimed at increasing knowledge can enhance attitudes, which may further translate into improved health practices [39]. Interestingly, the negative association between SWLS and knowledge was observed—an unexpected finding. Women who are more satisfied with life may perceive less need to seek out detailed knowledge about perimenopausal syndrome, possibly due to a lower perceived health threat. This phenomenon has been observed in other contexts, where higher subjective well-being is sometimes associated with lower engagement in preventive health behaviors or information seeking, perhaps due to lower perceived vulnerability [41]. Additionally, psychological constructs such as self-efficacy, as well as cultural factors like harmony-seeking and reluctance to discuss illness, may contribute to this association in the Chinese context [14, 37]. SEM also confirmed the indirect association between SWLS and attitude, which may be mediated by other psychosocial factors like self-efficacy or social support. Despite high perceived life satisfaction, women may lack the urgency to address perimenopausal concerns, suggesting that educational initiatives are needed to stimulate awareness. Similarly, the indirect association between knowledge and practice suggests that improving knowledge not only has a direct effect on behaviors but also enhances attitudes, which in turn influences practices.

To address observed behavioral gaps, future interventions should be concrete and multifaceted. Quasi-experimental studies could evaluate the effectiveness of targeted educational materials, health talks, or digital health campaigns tailored to women with low baseline knowledge. Comparing KAP scores before and after such interventions would provide evidence for best practices in health promotion. Furthermore, integrating mobile health applications for symptom monitoring, exercise tracking, and peer support—such as WeChat groups or community health clubs—could help overcome current barriers to self-management [32, 33]. Policy initiatives should aim to integrate perimenopausal health into routine primary care, develop culturally appropriate resources, and train healthcare providers in sensitive, proactive support.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-hospital setting and mixed-mode sampling may limit the generalizability of findings. Second, social desirability bias may have led to overreporting of positive attitudes or practices. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding KAP and life satisfaction relationships. Fourth, the relatively young mean age of participants may not fully capture the experiences of older, perimenopausal women. Future research should employ longitudinal, multicenter designs, stratified by age and region, to better elucidate the causal pathways and contextual factors influencing women’s experiences with perimenopausal syndrome.

Compared to previous KAP studies, this study’s broader age range, inclusion of SWLS, and use of SEM to model direct and indirect relationships distinguish its contribution. By illuminating the complex interplay between psychosocial well-being, knowledge, attitudes, and practices—in the context of China’s unique sociocultural landscape—this research provides new insights for policy, intervention, and future research.

Conclusion

Women in China exhibited good knowledge, moderate attitudes, and moderate practices regarding perimenopausal syndrome. Interventions should prioritize educational programs that enhance KAP, particularly concerning disease symptoms, open discussions, exercise routines, and regular symptom tracking.
