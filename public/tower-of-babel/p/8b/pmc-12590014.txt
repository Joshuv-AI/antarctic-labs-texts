Retinitis & optic neuropathy as a complication of Brucellosis: A case report
Authors: Hytham Al-Hindi, Alec Curtis, James Pratt, Mohannad Al-Hindi, Nitin Nigam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe describe the clinical and diagnostic features of a patient with ischemic retinitis and optic neuropathy secondary to brucellosis.ObservationsA 47 year old Caucasian man presented with acute loss of vision secondary to retinitis affecting the macula and optic neuropathy after recent exposure to wild hogs. Workup revealed positive serology for brucella antibodies. Macular optical coherence tomography and fluorescein angiography revealed obliterative retinitis in the macula. The patient developed no light perception vision soon after presentation despite treatment with rifampin and doxycycline.Conclusions and ImportanceThis case highlights a rare, retinal complication of acute brucella infection as well as the importance of clinical suspicion for the disease in those with known risk factors.

Introduction

Brucellosis ranks among the most widespread zoonotic diseases globally.1 It is caused by Brucella bacteria, a highly adaptable group of pathogens, with Brucella melitensis and Brucella abortus being among the most virulent species and primary causes of human infection.2,3 The pathophysiology of brucellosis involves the bacteria's ability to invade and replicate within macrophages, ultimately causing a systemic disease characterized by a range of clinical manifestations, including arthralgia, fever, malaise, sweating, lethargy, myalgia, and tremor.4,5

The primary modes of infection are direct contact with infected animals or their products, consumption of unpasteurized dairy products, and inhalation of contaminated aerosols.6, 7, 8 Occupational exposure is a significant risk factor, particularly for individuals working in agriculture, veterinary medicine, or animal husbandry. Additionally, certain socio-demographic factors, such as education level and professional activities, have been linked to higher seroprevalence rates of Brucella antibodies among at-risk populations.9

The clinical symptoms of Brucellosis are predominantly systemic, and focal involvement is most commonly seen in the osteoarticular system. The most common clinical findings include fever and hepatomegaly. While it is rare, ocular involvement is also possible. Complications such as optic neuropathy, uveitis, cataracts, vitreous changes, optic atrophy, glaucoma, and neovascularization leading to tractional retinal detachment have been documented in cases of Brucellosis.4,10, 11, 12 Understanding the mechanisms of Brucella infection and its potential ocular manifestations is crucial for timely diagnosis and management, as the disease can harm the eye through autoimmune reactions, antigen-antibody complex formation, toxin release, and the development of septic thrombi in ocular tissues.12 This case report describes a rare acute presentation of ocular brucellosis involving optic nerve head edema and retinal necrosis.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old Caucasian male presented to the emergency department due to sudden vision loss in his right eye for two days. He also reported intermittent blurred vision in the left eye. Review of systems was remarkable for headaches with temporal pain, intermittent fever, body aches, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and chills. The patient was recently hunting and trapping wild hogs. Besides alcohol consumption he denied any other drug use and had a past medical history of anxiety, post traumatic stress disorder and migraines.

Ophthalmic examination revealed near visual acuity in the right eye of hand motion eccentrically and, in the left eye, Snellen acuity of 20/20. A relative afferent pupillary defect was noted on the right. Anterior segment exam was unremarkable. Dilated fundus exam revealed diffuse whitening and edema of the macula and optic nerve head in the right eye with attenuation of retinal vessels at the macula in the left eye (Fig. 1). C reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 12.7 mg/L (elevated) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was within normal range. The patient was afebrile at presentation and remained so throughout admission. Given the concern for pallid disc edema on exam the patient was subsequently started on high-dose solumedrol 250 mg IV every 6 hours for one day until a temporal artery biopsy was performed, which was negative thus ruling out Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA). MRI orbits only revealed mild prominence of optic nerve sheaths bilaterally (Fig. 5). Infectious disease was consulted due to the patient's history of insect bites and exposure to wild hogs. Serology was significant for positive serum IgM for Brucella. Hypercoagulable workup was also notable for decreased protein S activity and elevated factor VIII activity. Of note CSF analysis was not obtained, however given the positive Brucella IgM, clinical picture and recent exposure to wild hogs a presumptive diagnosis of ocular brucellosis was made. Based on World Health Organization protocol, patient was started on a 45-day course of oral rifampin 300 mg twice daily and oral doxycycline 100 mg twice daily. Two weeks after hospital discharge, vision remained hand motion in the right eye. Fundus evaluation revealed whitening of the retina and peripapillary tissues, suggestive of retinitis and optic neuritis. Corresponding optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated inner layer hyperreflectivity and thickening of the macula, suggestive of retinitis (Fig. 2). Vision and exam in the left eye remained stable throughout follow up.Fig. 2Optical coherence tomography of macula with inner layer hyperreflectivity two weeks after discharge from hospital.Fig. 2

One month after his initial follow up, visual acuity remained hand motion in the right eye. Fundus examination demonstrated loss of the foveal reflex, glistening of the macula, and sheathing of the surrounding arteries, including the optic disc. Repeat OCT showed severe thinning and disorganization of all retinal layers (Fig. 3). Fluorescein angiography (FA) revealed central macular ischemia with complete blockage and enlargement of the foveal avascular zone (FAZ), absent central foveal perfusion, and attenuation of perifoveal vessels (Fig. 4). Flow within the arterial and venous arcades was preserved. Three months later at last follow up his vision had decreased to no light perception (NLP). Discussion

We describe an atypical presentation of ocular brucellosis on a patient with a recent known exposure to wild boars. After conducting a literature review on 9/24/25 using PubMed and the keywords “ocular brucellosis,” “retinitis,” and “ischemic optic neuropathy,” we did not find any prior reports of an acute occlusive macular retinitis and optic nerve head ischemia causing permanent loss of vision from brucellosis. Most cases of ocular brucellosis reported in the literature have had visual compromise in the chronic phase of the disease. Several pathologic processes have been described that explain the ophthalmic consequences of brucellosis, particularly the neurotoxic effects of the infection. Brucella is thought to reach the nervous system through the bloodstream or lymphatic system, where the pathogen attacks Schwann cells resulting in axon demyelination. The pathogen is also believed to cause a pronounced vascular inflammatory process via infective endarteritis, bacterial antigen-antibody immune complex deposition, and endotoxins, each of which provokes a profound vascular and perivascular inflammatory response.15, 16, 17 These pathophysiologic mechanisms can explain the severe occlusive retinitis seen in our patient, as well as previous documented ocular manifestations of brucellosis such as those of uveitis, optic neuritis and cystoid macular edema among others. Our patient's OCT testing demonstrated hyperreflectivity of the inner retinal layers suggestive of severely attenuated macular perfusion with subsequent OCT testing revealing diffuse ischemia with disorganization of outer and inner retinal layers. These findings likely developed as a result of direct vascular bacterial invasion leading to a clinical picture most consistent with occlusive retinitis.

Also of interest was the unilateral nature of his disease despite the underlying systemic process. This may be explained by differences in the microvascular and blood brain barrier environment between the two eyes. Analogously, most cases of endogenous endophthalmitis are unilateral despite the systemic nature of the infection in these cases. Our patient also was found to have decreased protein S activity and elevated factor VIII activity which confounded the diagnostic workup. Our theory is that the direct inflammatory effects of the brucella pathogen on the retinal vasculature at the macula, combined with patient's already susceptible prothrombotic microvascular environment resulted in the novel macular and optic nerve ischemia seen in our patient.

Of note, there were several limitations in the workup of our patient. We did not obtain an aqueous sample for testing, as this is not a routine diagnostic test given the broad differential of the presentation and the lack of a laboratory to process an aqueous sample. Furthermore, a CSF analysis was not done for brucella antibodies which would have provided further confirmatory evidence of brucellosis causing the clinical picture seen in the patient. In addition, there was a delay in obtaining fluorescein angiography at initial ophthalmic evaluation due to unavailability of this imaging modality at the location where the patient presented.

Our patient was started on a course of doxycycline and rifampin, and his systemic symptoms improved; however, given the focal macular ischemia, he had profound visual loss with no improvement. This case highlights the importance of considering brucellosis in the differential diagnosis of occlusive retinitis affecting the macula in an acute setting, particularly in those at higher risk for exposure. It also highlights the potential for prothrombotic risk factors to potentiate the ischemic complications of brucella infection.
