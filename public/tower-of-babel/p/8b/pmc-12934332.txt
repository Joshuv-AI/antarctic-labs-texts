Motivational factors for general practice training and career establishment: A cross-sectional study in Flanders, Belgium
Authors: Lotta E. Coenen, Madieke Maseman, Janique Lobbestael, Guy Gielis, Ann Gils, Johan Vansintejan, Inès Van Rossem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Flanders, Belgium, the 3-year specialised training in General Practice includes a specialty training rotation, for which trainees may choose their training site. After graduation, General Practitioners (GPs) decide where to establish their practice.ObjectiveThis study explored the factors influencing GP trainees’ choice of training location, reasons for settling in a specific region after graduation, and motivations for remaining in or leaving a practice.MethodsIn May 2024, the Interuniversity Centre for Education of General Practitioners distributed an anonymous online survey with closed- and open-ended questions to all GP alumni graduating between 2014 and 2023. Quantitative data were analysed using linear regression; qualitative data underwent content analysis.ResultsOf 772 alumni respondents (26.7% response rate), 93.4% were still practising as a GP. Most were female (70.9%), and 37.2% engaged in additional professional roles. Key influences on training location choice and practice settlement included colleague connections, proximity to home, and opportunities to work in group practices. Decisions to stay in or leave a practice were shaped by interpersonal relationships, practice organisation, and work–life balance. Working as a substitute GP was a frequent reason for changing practices, especially among recent graduates, to explore varied working environments.ConclusionBoth interpersonal and logistical factors strongly influence training practice choice and later GP practice settlement. High-quality training practices and supportive work environments are essential to promote sustainable GP careers and achieve a more balanced geographical distribution of GPs.

Introduction

General Practitioners (GPs) play a central role in community-based healthcare and are a cornerstone of the healthcare system [1–4]. As the first point of contact for patients, GPs provide continuous, high-quality care and foster long-term relationships based on trust. [1,3] Multiple studies have demonstrated that GP job satisfaction is closely linked to the quality of care delivered, making the presence of motivated GPs essential for a strong healthcare system [2,5]. Therefore, a well-organised primary care system and a sufficient number of motivated GPs are crucial [2–6]. This issue is especially urgent given the growing mismatch between the demand and supply of GPs. Many countries face GP shortages—whether widespread or region-specific—caused by an ageing population, an increasing chronic disease burden, and high hospital care costs. These challenges highlight the need to strengthen primary care [1,3,4,6–8]. Strong evidence shows that job satisfaction is important for remaining in a primary care career path as GP [2,5,6,8]. The key elements for GP job satisfaction have been studied in multiple European countries to attract more medical students to general practice and to retain GPs [2–5,8,9].

A coordinated strategy is required, involving (1) attracting more medical graduates into general practice, (2) enhancing job satisfaction to achieve longer GP careers, and (3) guiding GPs towards underserved regions [2,3,6–9].

In Belgium, the Interuniversity Centre coordinates specialty training in general practice for Education of General Practitioners (ICHO), a collaboration among the four Dutch-speaking medical faculties in Belgium. The program consists of a three-year, full-time curriculum. The key component is the specialty training rotation during which trainees spent 2.5 years in a general practice and six months in a hospital setting. Trainees are free to choose their training site from an approved list of GP practices, enabling them to select a setting that matches their personal and professional preferences. Between 2014 and 2023, a total of 2,706 GPs graduated from a Dutch-speaking university in Belgium.

Many European countries face a general shortage of GPs. The main arguments for not pursuing a career in General Practice vary considerably between countries, but commonly include a lack of workforce support, the absence of targeted specialty training for GPs, and the overall devaluation of the profession [4,5,10–12]. Qualitative research has shown that key factors for attracting and retaining GPs include support for the GP’s family installation and their social network [11,13,14]. In Belgium exists a general shortage of GPs exists, except in areas near medical universities [8,15]. In response, policy initiatives introduced in 2012 offered financial incentives (such as grants and rent-free loans) to encourage settlement in underserved areas. These efforts, however, had limited long-term effectiveness and were eventually discontinued [16]. To date, little is known about the motivational factors that influence where GPs choose to practice or why they remain in—or leave—a particular GP practice.

While previous research has examined the determinants of GP job satisfaction in various European contexts [2–5,8,9], the published evidence relating to GP recruitment and retention is limited internationally and non-existent for Belgium [17]. The current study addresses this gap by exploring what factors influence GP trainees’ selection of a training site and whether this is related to their settlement after graduation as GPs.

These findings may support future policy initiatives aimed at improving a balanced geographical distribution of GPs across Belgium and strengthening general practice delivery.

Methods

To gain a general understanding of the motivations and distribution of GP trainees and GPs, an online survey was conducted, initiated by ICHO. The survey (Appendix A) was developed based on existing literature and the expertise of a steering committee on GP education research consisting of a GP trainee, a GP trainer, GP education researchers, a university staff member and the director of ICHO. It was reviewed and checked for understanding by four GP alumni. The survey comprised three sections: (1) demographic characteristics and information on current career, (2) the motivation for choosing a General Practice education and career, and (3) motivation for the current and previous GP practice location. Each section included several closed-ended questions (rated 0–10) and two or three open-ended short-answer questions. The open-ended questions aimed to provide additional insights in the quantitative data and broaden the scope found in the literature. It was created in Qualtrics and distributed via e-mail to all alumni of the specialised training in General Practice from Dutch-speaking universities in Belgium who graduated between 2014 and 2023. Recently graduated GPs were targeted because their decisions on training location were recent and relevant for future (re)allocation measures. The survey remained open for four weeks in May 2024, with one reminder sent after two weeks.

All collected data were analysed anonymously. Respondents who did not answer the demographic questions were excluded from the analysis, as they provided no content-related responses. Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS version 29.0.2.0, applying descriptive statistics and frequency tables to explore the demographics. The normal distribution of the quantitative data was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test, and subsequently further analysed via linear regression, with the significance level set at p = 0.05.

Qualitative data were analysed using content analysis by JL, LC and MM independently applying an inductive approach. Secondly, the authors jointly defined the themes to ensure a comprehensive understanding [18,19]. Finally, triangulation of the quantitative and qualitative data was performed to create a better understanding of the dataset as a whole and detect convergent and additional information [20,21]. The study design was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ghent University, BUN-number ONZ-2024-0444.

Results

The questionnaire was sent to 2,706 alumni. A total of 866 respondents started the online survey, of which 772 (26.7%) answered at least the demographic questions. Almost three-quarters of respondents were female (70.9%), and nearly all respondents (93.4%) were still working as a GP. However, 16 respondents had never worked as GP after their graduation (2.1%). Most active GPs worked in Flanders (88.0%) and Brussels (2.2%). The longer it had been since graduation, the more likely respondents were no longer active as GP (p = 0.008). While 55.7% of respondents worked as many hours as they wish as GP, 30.5% report working more than preferred. Additionally, 59.1% worked over 40 h per week. Furthermore, 287 respondents (37.2%) engaged in other professional activities besides their GP work.

Content analysis

On average, respondents reported spending 0.5–1.0 day per week on additional professional activities. These most often involved preventive healthcare organisations, teaching medical students and GP trainees, professional health training, and research. Other respondents served as board members of health or well-being organisations.

For newly graduated GPs, these factors remained important, though they were rated somewhat lower in priority compared to during their traineeship. Additional factors considered very important by the respondents included the GP practice location, the GP’s domicile and the possibility of joining a group practice. Furthermore, the partner’s workplace, family’s domicile, and an interesting vacancy were rated as important with a significant difference compared to the traineeship preferences (p < 0.001).

Respondents working fewer hours preferred group practices and community health centres (both p < 0,001), and prioritised locations matching their family’s residence (p = 0,021; p < 0,001) for both training and settlement decisions.

Content analysis.

When changing from one practice to another, the mentioned reasons included work conditions or personal circumstances. The primary work-related argument for leaving a GP practice was a difference in vision regarding organisation or medical practice. Conflicts with colleagues and financial considerations were also cited.

Personal reasons for changing workplaces included the partner’s professional activities, relocation, or children. Several respondents made a positive choice for a different GP practice or other profession.

Some respondents deliberately opted for short-term contracts as substitute physicians, which require frequent workplace changes. Recent graduates exploring different work settings before settling mainly preferred this.

Respondents who did not wish to change workplaces reported similar themes as determinants to stay in their current GP practice. Work-related motivations included good contact and collaboration with colleagues, practice organisation and favourable arrangements allowing time for other professional activities. Additionally, relationships with patients were also important motivators. Financial considerations – mostly related to investments in practice premises –were mentioned as well. Personal reasons primarily involved GP practice location, with respondents preferring proximity to their home and daily life activities.

Discussion

This study explored the factors influencing GP trainees’ choice of training location, the reasons graduated GPs settle in a particular region, and the motivations for remaining in or leaving a general practice. The results identify the strong influence of both interpersonal and logistical factors on these decisions.

A key finding is that the decision-making process for training placement is primarily influenced by the perceived ‘connection’ with future colleagues, particularly their personality, values, and vision of practice [10,11,14,22]. Notably, none of the respondents mentioned the pedagogical or educational capacities of GP training practices as a factor when applying, possibly because this aspect was not addressed in the closed-ended questions. Interpersonal factors continued to play a significant role in decision-making after graduation; however, practical considerations such as proximity to home and the professional situation of a partner or family became more prominent when deciding on long-term settlement. Holloway et al. mention the importance of family unit considerations when choosing a working location [13]. However, review studies point out the even higher impact of interhuman interactions with patients and colleagues as well as workplace organisation [11]. This aligns with our findings that features of practice organisation gained more importance. Studies have shown these elements are all important for job satisfaction [3,5,6,8]. Whether related to location or practice structure, this evolution reflects the need to balance a career in general practice with personal life and, potentially, other professional pursuits [5,8]. This is reflected as well in the preference for more organised types of working environments by those working fewer hours. Therefore, it is remarkable that more than half of our respondents (59.1%) works more hours per week as GP than the European average working week of 36.0 h [23].

The finding that many respondents remain in their first post-training GP practice or report a high likelihood of staying underscores the long-term impact of initial placement choices. This suggests that early career decisions, can have enduring consequences for professional trajectories and workforce distribution.

On the other hand, motivations for leaving a GP practice often involved mismatches in professional vision, interpersonal conflicts, or limited flexibility, whereas decisions to stay were largely driven by positive relationships with colleagues and patients, effective organisational structures, and proximity to relatives and family. These findings align with existing literature and highlight the importance of both relational and structural factors in retention of GPs in their current practice [11,13,14].

Interestingly, a subset of recent graduates deliberately chose short-term substitute positions to explore different work environments before committing, allowing them to gain work experience and make more informed career decisions.

Implications for policy and practice

First, as training location may influence later practice settlement, it could be beneficial to strategically promote training locations in underserved areas—particularly when supported by structured mentorship and high-quality training environments [11,12,24]. Currently, ICHO implements TOEKAN, a program which should attract GP trainees to high-quality training practices, prioritising pedagogical criteria [25].

Second, promoting GP group practices with a clear vision, strong team dynamics, and opportunities for diversified roles beyond the core GP work package (e.g. education, prevention, or research) may enhance both recruitment and long-term retention of GPs [5,6,16]. As females prioritise these (inter)personal factors stronger than men and 70% of the Belgian GPs are female, this emphasises the importance of good practice organisation.

Furthermore, policy measures aimed at improving retention should include enhanced task delegation and improved communication strategies and better support for work–life balance [3,6,26]. Reducing GPs’ administrative burden—through practice-based support staff, and streamlined digital systems, could enhance the attractiveness and sustainability of GP work [5,8]. Governments and universities should support management skills development amongst GPs as well as hands-on support in associations.

Finally, policies aimed at improving a balanced geographical distribution of GPs should consider not only financial incentives but also social and professional motivators—such as collegiality, flexibility, and alignment with personal values—which appear to be more influential in the long term [17].

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the comprehensive approach, combining quantitative and qualitative data from a large sample of GP alumni over 10 years. The triangulation of findings provides a first, nuanced understanding of the factors influencing key career decisions [19]. However, this study also has limitations. The reasonable response rate (26.7%), raises the possibility of response bias as those with stronger or more reflective experiences may have been more likely to participate [27]. As there were only a few additional respondents after the first reminder, additional reminders were waived [28]. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Lastly, although the survey was based on expert input and literature, self-reported motivations are inherently subject to interpretation and recall bias.

Future research

Qualitative research using in-depth interviews could provide deeper insight into specific turning points in GP career trajectories, particularly among those who leave the profession or move frequently. Such studies could create more understanding of causal relationships in settlement decision-making processes and identify which policy measures might have the greatest impact.

Further research should explore regional differences in greater detail and assess the long-term impact of specialty training location on career satisfaction and practice sustainability.

Conclusion

This study investigated the factors influencing workplace choice among newly graduated GPs in Belgium. Personal factors such as connection with colleagues, shared vision, and proximity to one’s domicile were most important. For GPs to remain in a workplace, additional motivators emerged, including practice organisation, workload, doctor-patient relationships and financial investments. In contrast, personal conflict, relocation of family or partner, or new professional opportunities was major reasons to leave a GP practice. Further research is needed to fully understand which of these factors can be influenced by policy and society, while recognising that the most important drivers are often personal. Therefore, a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach will not succeed in (re)locating GPs to underserved regions and flexible, multifaceted interventions will be required.
