Use and Outcomes of Cost-Effectiveness Analyses and Additional Considerations in Pharmaceutical Reimbursement in the Netherlands
Authors: Diederik Brokkelkamp, Vivian Reckers-Droog, Lonneke Timmers, Pieter van Baal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveIn the Netherlands, the National Health Care Institute (ZIN) assesses pharmaceuticals and advises the Minister of Health, Welfare, and Sport (MoH) on their reimbursement. Since 2015, two policy reforms—the introduction of willingness-to-pay reference values and the lock procedure, which temporarily withholds high-budget hospital pharmaceuticals from reimbursement pending assessment and price negotiations—have changed the decision-making process and expanded the role of cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA). This study provides a comprehensive overview of the use and outcomes of CEAs in Dutch pharmaceutical reimbursement over the past decade. Additionally, we identify how additional considerations beyond cost-effectiveness were used in reimbursement recommendations.MethodsWe analysed reimbursement dossiers and recommendations published by ZIN between 2015 and 2024. Descriptive methods were used to provide insight into characteristics of assessments, CEA outcomes, and recommendations. For a subset of pharmaceutical indications with a CEA and a recommendation to negotiate, we performed a content analysis of ZIN’s appraisal and recommendation letters to identify how additional considerations influenced the reimbursement recommendation alongside cost-effectiveness.ResultsZIN advised the MoH on 330 unique reimbursement dossiers covering a total of 350 pharmaceutical indications in the timeframe. CEAs were conducted for 86 indications, primarily for hospital pharmaceuticals (n = 49), for which assessment became mandatory following the introduction of the lock procedure. Of the 86 indications with CEAs, 27 (31%) were deemed cost-effective and 42 (49%) were not, and cost-effectiveness was classified as unknown for 17 (20%), mostly because these CEAs were judged methodologically insufficient by ZIN. Many cost-ineffective pharmaceutical indications exceeded their reference value substantially. Mean incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) reported by ZIN for pharmaceutical indications associated with reference values of €20,000, €50,000, and €80,000 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) were €97,300, €81,400, and €278,900 per QALY, respectively. CEA results informed proposed price reductions, by relating the ICER to the relevant reference value. ZIN also frequently based its proposed discount on a more plausible scenario analysis or invoked additional arguments, such as uncertainty in costs or effects, anticipated indication expansions, or ample revenue from earlier indications, to determine a societally acceptable price.ConclusionsIn Dutch pharmaceutical reimbursement decision-making, CEA outcomes directly inform price negotiations, and explicit reference values function primarily as anchors for negotiations rather than as strict cut-off points for reimbursement. Additional considerations, such as uncertainty about long-term effects and anticipated changes in the treatment landscape, were frequently invoked alongside cost-effectiveness to argue for further price reductions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40273-026-01637-w.

Key Points for Decision Makers

The introduction of the lock procedure for expensive hospital pharmaceuticals increased the number of indications assessed on cost-effectiveness in the Netherlands. Most pharmaceuticals assessed over the past decade were not cost-effective at the list price. In such cases, price negotiations were initiated rather than reimbursement being denied outright.In Dutch reimbursement decisions, reference values are not applied as strict thresholds for yes/no decisions: they primarily serve as anchors for price negotiations, translating incremental cost-effectiveness ratios into explicit discounts to attain.Additional arguments, such as uncertainty in long-term effects, anticipated indication expansions, or high budget impact, are routinely taken into account when determining further price reductions, reflecting a policy framework that balances cost-effectiveness with broader economic and societal considerations.

Introduction

Background and Objectives

Over the past few decades, healthcare expenditure has risen substantially in almost all high-income countries [1]. In many cases, increases in healthcare expenditure have outpaced economic growth, and are projected to do so in the future, thereby posing a risk of crowding out funding for other public services, such as education and social security [1–4]. While population ageing is a significant contributor to rising expenditures, evidence suggests that the main driver is the introduction and adoption of new medical technologies [4–6]. In particular, prices of novel pharmaceuticals have increased considerably in recent years, pushing up pharmaceutical expenditure both in absolute terms and as a share of total healthcare spending in many countries [7, 8]. This trend raises concerns about the sustainability of healthcare financing and the potential displacement of other types of care within constrained budgets [9, 10].

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) (broadly including cost-utility analysis) has gained prominence as a policy tool to facilitate an optimal allocation of available resources, so far mostly used to inform reimbursement decisions on new pharmaceuticals [11, 12]. CEAs offer a structured approach to evaluate the incremental costs and health effects of new pharmaceuticals in comparison with the current standard of care (SoC). The methodology of and guidelines for performing CEAs have been developed, refined, and expanded upon in the past decades, and CEAs are now firmly embedded in Health Technology Assessment (HTA) in several countries [13]. Nevertheless, questions remain about how, and to what extent, CEAs are used in decision-making, with substantial cross-country variation in their formal role and in how their outcomes are weighed against other criteria and considerations [13–17].

This study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the use and outcomes of CEAs and their relationship with additional considerations in pharmaceutical reimbursement recommendations in the Netherlands. It focuses on the period following policy reforms introduced in 2015 that increased the prominence of CEAs, namely the lock procedure1 for high-cost pharmaceuticals and the introduction of maximum willingness-to-pay reference values [19, 20]. We analysed all reimbursement dossiers and recommendations produced by the National Health Care Institute (Zorginstituut Nederland, ZIN) between 2015 and 2024. By examining how CEAs are applied in practice and combined with other considerations, the study provides insight into the role of CEA in price negotiations and contributes to ongoing policy discussions on the transparency and consistency of pharmaceutical reimbursement decisions. To aid interpretation of our findings, Sect. 1.2 provides background on the Dutch pharmaceutical reimbursement system, including the aforementioned policy changes, ZIN’s role, the decision criteria applied, and the procedures through which reimbursement recommendations are formulated.

The Dutch Pharmaceutical Reimbursement System

In the Netherlands, ZIN advises the Minister of Health, Welfare, and Sport (MoH) on whether pharmaceuticals should be included in the basic benefit package (BBP), which defines the services covered under the statutory health insurance [21]. The MoH ultimately decides on their inclusion, and this decision is informed by and usually aligned with ZIN’s reimbursement recommendation [11]. ZIN assesses all reimbursement applications for outpatient pharmaceuticals, that is, medicines dispensed through community pharmacies. Since the introduction of the lock procedure in 2015, ZIN also assesses a selection of hospital pharmaceuticals, which are financed through hospital budgets and may be administered or taken in inpatient or outpatient settings. The lock allows ZIN to assess hospital pharmaceuticals and the MoH to negotiate prices before inclusion in the BBP [18, 19]. The introduction of the lock followed from concerns over rapidly increasing expenditures on hospital pharmaceuticals [19, 22]. Currently, hospital pharmaceuticals are placed in the lock if expected gross pharmaceutical expenditure upon reimbursement exceeds €20 million per year (€40 million per year before July 1, 2023), or €10 million per year, with annual costs per patient upwards of €50,000 for a single indication [18].

ZIN’s reimbursement recommendation follows from the assessment of four key decision criteria: effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, necessity, and feasibility [23, 24]. Figure 1 provides an overview of the assessment and appraisal process described in the following paragraphs. First, the effectiveness criterion is assessed by comparing the clinical effectiveness of a new pharmaceutical with that of the current SoC. To be eligible for inclusion in the BBP, a pharmaceutical must be considered effective meaning that it needs to meet the criterion of established medical science and medical practice (Stand van de Wetenschap en Praktijk, SWP). This requires an added or equal therapeutic benefit when compared to the SoC, supported by evidence considered adequate under evidence-based principles [25]. The cost-effectiveness criterion is typically assessed through a CEA, performed by the manufacturer and subsequently reviewed by ZIN. Manufacturers are required to conduct a CEA if (1) they claim added therapeutic value compared with the SoC, and (2) the yearly expected gross expenditure upon reimbursement exceeds a certain limit. Specifically, CEAs for outpatient pharmaceuticals are mandatory when the expected gross expenditure in any of the first 3 years after reimbursement exceeds €10 million per year, or €1 million per year if annual costs per patient exceed €50,000 [23]. Before 2019, this limit was €2.5 million per year [21]. A waiver for conducting a CEA can be requested for pharmaceuticals falling below these thresholds.2 Hospital pharmaceuticals are assessed on cost-effectiveness when subject to the lock procedure and have a claim of added therapeutic value. When a CEA is required, manufacturers must adhere to ZIN’s pharmacoeconomic guidelines, with costs and health effects quantified over the lifetime of the targeted patient population [24, 27]. Health effects must be expressed in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), and because the guidelines mandate a societal perspective, cost categories such as informal care and productivity losses are to be included [24, 27].

The necessity criterion comprises several domains. One is intrinsically linked to cost-effectiveness and is operationalised in terms of disease severity, using the proportional shortfall approach [28]. Proportional shortfall is calculated as the number of QALYs lost with the disease divided by the number of QALYs that an individual of the same age and sex without the disease would be expected to accrue [28]. A higher proportional shortfall therefore reflects a greater relative health loss and corresponds to a more severe disease. The greater the proportional shortfall of patients for whom the pharmaceutical is indicated, the higher the reference value against which its incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) is evaluated, reflecting a greater societal willingness to pay per QALY gained for more severe diseases. Currently, three proportional shortfall categories are distinguished: 0.10–0.40, 0.41–0.70, and 0.71–1, corresponding to maximum reference values of €20,000, €50,000, and €80,000 per QALY gained, respectively [20, 28]. Reimbursement of pharmaceuticals indicated for patients with a proportional shortfall of 0.00–0.09 is, in principle, not recommended [20, 28]. The current reference values were made explicit in a 2015 report by ZIN and do not represent definitive thresholds for reimbursement, as ZIN notes that there may be arguments to justify higher or lower costs per QALY gained [20]. Beyond disease severity, the necessity criterion also considers whether costs should be publicly reimbursed through inclusion in the BBP or can be paid out-of-pocket by patients [29].

Lastly, the feasibility criterion considers the extent to which a pharmaceutical can be reimbursed from standpoints like societal support, organisational constraints, and affordability [30]. It is considered whether there will be potential indication expansions for the pharmaceutical in the future and if certain shortages (e.g. healthcare personnel required for administration) may arise [30]. A budget impact analysis is typically conducted as part of the assessment on feasibility [30].

The effectiveness criterion is the only criterion laid down by law and is, therefore, referred to as a knock-out criterion [29]. Pharmaceuticals that do not meet the effectiveness criterion are, by definition, ineligible for inclusion in the BBP. In all other instances, ZIN’s reimbursement recommendation is based on an integral consideration of the evidence on the four decision criteria [24].

In its assessment and subsequent appraisal, ZIN is advised by two independent committees: the Scientific Advisory Board (Wetenschappelijke Adviesraad, WAR) and the Insured Package Advisory Committee (Adviescommissie Pakket, ACP). The WAR evaluates whether the conclusions reached by ZIN are adequately supported by the available evidence and advises on adjustments needed to ensure the robustness of the evidence as presented in the reimbursement dossier. In addition, the WAR advises ZIN on whether a pharmaceutical meets the effectiveness criterion and on the most plausible ICER. This typically takes place over two meetings. Prior to the first meeting, the manufacturer submits its initial CEA model, which is reviewed by the WAR. During this meeting, the WAR provides feedback on modifications required to improve the methodological robustness of the model and its alignment with real-world outcomes. The manufacturer may then revise the model before the second meeting, after which the WAR issues its final recommendation to ZIN on whether the CEA is of sufficient methodological quality for decision-making.

Some dossiers, particularly those with major societal impact (e.g., pharmaceuticals that are cost-ineffective, with high budget impact or whose long-term consequences are uncertain) are subsequently appraised by the ACP [31]. The ACP uses the four criteria as a basis for an argumentative deliberation during a public meeting and may incorporate additional considerations, resulting in a recommendation to ZIN on the societal acceptability of reimbursement. Typically, in cases of high-cost pharmaceuticals, the ACP also advises ZIN on potential price discounts needed for inclusion in the BBP for a societally acceptable price [31].

Following assessment and appraisal, ZIN’s reimbursement recommendation to the MoH typically takes one of three forms: a positive, negative, or negotiation recommendation [24]. A positive recommendation indicates that the pharmaceutical can be included in the BBP at the proposed price. A negative recommendation means that either the pharmaceutical does not meet the effectiveness criterion or is considered unsuitable for reimbursement after integral consideration of the four criteria [24]. The pharmaceutical will not be included in the BBP. A negotiation recommendation is issued when reimbursement is advised to be conditional on successful negotiations with the manufacturer. Such negotiations are typically aimed at price reductions to improve cost-effectiveness and reduce expected budget impact. Negotiations are carried out by the Bureau of Financial Arrangements (Buro Financiële Arrangementen, BFAG), a team within the Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport. If negotiations are successful, the pharmaceutical is included in the BBP under a financial arrangement for a specified period; if they fail, it remains excluded unless new negotiations lead to an agreement.

Three previous studies have examined reimbursement recommendations produced by ZIN, all focusing on dossiers that included a CEA. The first study, a review covering dossiers from 2005 to 2008, assessed their methodological quality and found frequent deviations from ZIN’s pharmacoeconomic guidelines [32]. The second study, a review covering 2005–2011, compared assessments in the Netherlands and Sweden and found that the relative importance of cost-effectiveness in pharmaceutical reimbursement in the Netherlands was “more modest than would generally be expected”, largely due to the numerous waivers granted for conducting a CEA based on low expected gross expenditure [33]. The most recent study, covering 2012–2020, examined whether the introduction of the reference values in 2015 affected subsequent reimbursement recommendations. The authors reported that most (65%) pharmaceutical indications assessed on cost-effectiveness after the introduction of the reference values fell into the highest disease severity category, corresponding to the reference value of €80,000 per QALY. They also observed a higher frequency of cases in which negotiations were initiated compared with the period before 2015 [34]. However, they cautioned that these findings could not be interpreted as evidence of causality due to the limited number of CEAs and the exploratory nature of the study [34].

Since the introduction of the lock procedure and the reference values in 2015, the decision-making process has changed and the scope of cost-effectiveness has expanded in the Netherlands. Against this backdrop, the present study reviews reimbursement dossiers to examine the role of CEA in the current Dutch pharmaceutical reimbursement landscape, their use and outcomes, and how these inform reimbursement recommendations alongside additional relevant considerations.

Methods

Data Collection

We collected dossiers from ZIN’s website if they concerned (outpatient or hospital) pharmaceuticals for newly registered indications seeking reimbursement, published between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2024 [35]. The dossiers typically consist of (1) a letter with the reimbursement recommendation from ZIN to the MoH, (2) a pharmacotherapeutic assessment and, for selected cases, (3) a pharmacoeconomic assessment, and (4) an appraisal by the ACP. Some dossiers comprised multiple indications for the same pharmaceutical, with potentially different CEA outcomes and reimbursement recommendations; in such cases, each indication was recorded separately. Dossiers concerning removal of pharmaceuticals from the BBP, conditional reimbursement of pharmaceuticals with evidence development [36], and vaccines were excluded to ensure comparability. Conditional reimbursement involves temporary inclusion of pharmaceuticals in the BBP while evidence on their effectiveness is being collected, and CEAs are therefore not conducted for such indications [36]. Vaccines were also excluded because they typically fall outside the standard reimbursement pathway in which cost-effectiveness is systematically assessed.

Data extraction was conducted manually by the first author (DB) for each reimbursement dossier using a predefined list of variables, which included the pharmaceutical name, indication, administrative setting (outpatient or hospital), and whether the effectiveness criterion was met. For indications meeting the effectiveness criterion, we further extracted the assessed therapeutic benefit (added or equal compared with the SoC), whether a CEA was performed (yes/no), and whether the manufacturer received a waiver or was exempted from performing a CEA for another reason (yes/no).

For dossiers including a CEA, we extracted the disease severity classification, the corresponding reference value for evaluating the ICER, and the deterministic ICER—both as reported by the manufacturer and as deemed most plausible by ZIN. When ZIN reported the most plausible ICER as a range, the midpoint was recorded. We also recorded ZIN’s assessment of the methodological quality of the CEA. CEAs were classified as methodologically sufficient, insufficient, or sufficient after adaptation. The latter category applied when ZIN judged the CEA methodologically sound but identified a different ICER as most plausible compared to the one reported in the manufacturer's base case, reflecting cases in which ZIN considered the manufacturer’s base-case ICER overly optimistic, biased, or insufficiently transparent. ZIN adopted a more plausible scenario analysis or recalculated the ICER using alternative inputs in such cases. Finally, we recorded whether ZIN judged the pharmaceutical cost-effective at the list price (yes/no/unknown) and the final reimbursement recommendation to the MoH (positive, negative, or negotiation). Cost-effectiveness was categorised as unknown when the CEA was deemed methodologically insufficient, or when ZIN did not explicitly classify the indication as cost-effective or cost-ineffective for reasons other than methodological insufficiency. In a small number of cases where extraction of a numeric value or classification was ambiguous, the case was discussed within the author group to reach a consensus on the appropriate classification. A complete overview of the data extracted from the reimbursement dossiers is provided in the Electronic Supplementary Material (Online Resource 1). Cases where extraction was ambiguous are annotated in the dataset with the rationale for the recorded value.

For all 60 indications with a CEA and a negotiation recommendation, we also extracted the additional considerations beyond cost-effectiveness that the ACP and ZIN invoked in their respective documents. The full text of the ACP appraisal and ZIN's recommendation letter to the MoH was read by the first author (DB), and considerations or arguments that influenced the recommendation to negotiate or that informed how negotiations should be conducted were identified and recorded.

Data Analysis

We summarised key variables using descriptive statistics, stratified by outpatient and hospital indications. We then examined in greater detail the dossiers that included a CEA. For CEAs judged of methodologically sufficient quality, we analysed their outcomes by plotting ICERs, reported by ZIN as the most plausible scenario, against the corresponding reference values, and by reporting their means and medians. The additional considerations extracted from the ACP appraisals and ZIN recommendation letters were categorised through a content analysis combining deductive and inductive elements. The deductive element was the overarching research question, defined a priori: to identify the considerations beyond cost-effectiveness that influenced ZIN's negotiation recommendations and to characterise how these considerations were used. The inductive element was the development of the categories themselves. The grouping of considerations into 11 thematic categories was developed iteratively through multiple rounds of discussion within the author group and with pharmacoeconomic advisors at ZIN.

Results

The review comprised 330 reimbursement dossiers, covering a total of 350 pharmaceutical indications. A total of 313 dossiers covered a single indication, 14 covered two indications with distinct CEA outcomes or recommendations, and three covered three indications. A total of 86 CEAs were performed, of which 49 concerned hospital pharmaceutical indications and 37 concerned outpatient pharmaceutical indications. An overview of all pharmaceutical indications with a CEA is provided as Electronic Supplementary Material (Online Resource 3). Most pharmaceuticals were indicated for patients in the highest disease severity category, and their ICERs were therefore evaluated against a reference value of €80,000 per QALY. Regarding methodological quality, ZIN judged 71 of the 86 CEAs to be sufficiently robust for decision-making. For 32 of these, ZIN adjusted the manufacturer’s proposed base-case ICER prior to formulating its recommendation, either by selecting a more plausible scenario analysis from the dossier as the preferred base case or by recalculating the ICER using alternative inputs. The remaining 15 CEAs were judged to be of methodologically insufficient quality. In these cases, manufacturers were typically invited to resubmit an updated dossier with a revised CEA model (n = 13). However, two hospital pharmaceutical indications received a negotiation recommendation despite a methodologically insufficient CEA. In both cases, concerns about uncertain long-term effects led to a recommendation for a pay-for-performance scheme, with stepwise payment conditional on demonstrated effectiveness. Neither dossier explicitly stated whether the methodologically insufficient CEA would be used as input for these proposed schemes.

In total, 27 (31.4%) pharmaceutical indications were considered cost-effective at the list price, while 42 (48.8%) were deemed cost-ineffective when their ICERs were compared with relevant reference values. For the remaining 17 indications (19.8%), no definitive conclusion on cost-effectiveness could be drawn: 15 due to methodological insufficiency, one because the most plausible ICER range overlapped the reference value, and one because the confidential price of the comparator was considered too influential.

Among the 86 assessments that included a CEA, 12 pharmaceutical indications received a positive reimbursement recommendation outright, all of which had an ICER below their relevant reference value. For the majority (60 out of 86), ZIN advised negotiations with the manufacturer. Notably, negotiations were recommended for 15 of the 27 cost-effective pharmaceuticals, indicating that considerations beyond cost-effectiveness, such as uncertainty or treatment landscape dynamics, influenced ZIN’s recommendation to initiate negotiations.

Subsequent analyses focused on the 71 pharmaceutical–indication combinations with a methodologically sufficient CEA. Of these, four were deemed dominant by ZIN (more effective and less costly than the comparator), one yielded no incremental QALYs, and for one, no deterministic ICER could be reported, because it depended heavily on the confidential price of the comparator. This left 65 pharmaceutical indications with QALY gains at additional costs. It should be noted that when ZIN advised the MoH to initiate negotiations with manufacturers, this was typically, but not exclusively, informed by a CEA indicating that the pharmaceutical indication was cost-ineffective at the list price. ZIN often specified a percentage discount or discount range for the MoH to pursue, but these proposals were not based on the ICER alone. In practice, reference values have primarily functioned as anchors for price recommendations since their introduction, with the objective of reducing the ICER to just below the applicable reference value. Price discount recommendations following a CEA therefore typically begin with a calculation based on the ICER relative to the reference value, for example: “Osimertinib has an ICER of €135,000 per QALY. To bring the ICER below the relevant reference value of €80,000 per QALY, the price of osimertinib must decrease by about 35%” [37].

Additional considerations listed in Table 3 were often invoked alongside these calculations, usually leading to higher proposed discounts. Uncertainty in cost-effectiveness estimates was the most frequently cited additional argument and could substantially influence the final recommendation. In such cases, ZIN based its advice on an ICER range rather than a single point estimate, as illustrated by the following example: “Due to structural uncertainty regarding the long-term effects of sacituzumab govitecan on overall survival, ZIN reports an ICER range of €197,000–€241,000 per QALY. When compared against the relevant reference value of €80,000 per QALY, achieving cost-effectiveness would require price reductions of 65–75%, depending on the scenario considered. ZIN considers the most conservative scenario, a price reduction of at least 75%, appropriate. During negotiations, anticipated indication expansions and the expected entry of competing treatments could be taken into account” [38].

Negotiations were also recommended for some pharmaceuticals considered cost-effective at the list price when additional considerations were deemed relevant. For example: “While ipilimumab plus nivolumab is expected to be cost-effective, with an estimated ICER of €60,000 per QALY compared with the reference value of €80,000 per QALY, there are nonetheless arguments to initiate price negotiations. These include remaining uncertainty about long-term effects, the expected entry of multiple competing therapies, and the fact that the cost-effectiveness of the comparator treatment, costing €250,000 per patient, has never been established” [39]. Taken together, these examples illustrate that ICERs typically translated into explicit percentage discounts, whereas additional considerations unrelated to cost-effectiveness were generally invoked qualitatively and were not accompanied by quantified discount adjustments.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to provide a comprehensive overview of the use and outcomes of CEAs and the relationship with additional considerations in pharmaceutical reimbursement in the Netherlands since the introduction of the lock procedure and reference values in 2015. Our findings show that hospital pharmaceuticals targeted at patients with a high disease burden have become the primary domain for cost-effectiveness assessments, reflecting a policy shift towards greater focus on high-cost pharmaceuticals within hospital care. The methodological quality of most CEAs (71 out of 86) conducted was deemed sufficient for decision-making. Nonetheless, upward adjustments of base-case ICERs by ZIN were common, either by selecting a more plausible scenario analysis as the preferred base case or by recalculating ICERs using alternative inputs. These adjusted estimates then formed the basis for reimbursement recommendations. Of the 69 indications for which a definitive conclusion on cost-effectiveness could be drawn, 27 (39%) were considered cost-effective at the list price, with many ICERs substantially exceeding their associated reference values. When pharmaceuticals were deemed cost-ineffective, price negotiations were initiated, rather than recommending to deny reimbursement outright. In such cases, CEA results informed minimum discounts to attain in negotiations by the MoH. Additional considerations, such as high budget impact and changes in the treatment landscape, also influenced recommendations but were rarely translated into quantitative discount targets. Uncertainty about long-term effects or costs was the most frequently cited additional consideration following a CEA.

Compared with earlier reviews of Dutch reimbursement dossiers, our results indicate that the methodological quality and adherence to guidelines of manufacturer-submitted CEAs have improved over the past 2 decades. Between 2015 and 2024, 83% of CEAs were considered methodologically robust for decision-making, compared with 38% in 2005–2008 [32] and 48% in 2005–2011 [33]. Although the observational nature of this comparison does not allow causal interpretation, possible contributors to this improvement include the gradual clarification and tightening of ZIN’s CEA guidelines, increased experience among manufacturers and assessors, and the institutionalisation of HTA requirements in the Dutch reimbursement process. However, adaptations by ZIN to the resulting CEA outcomes, such as selecting a more plausible scenario or re-estimating ICERs with alternative inputs, were common in the assessments included in the present study. Pharmaceuticals exempted from a CEA still outnumber those subjected to full economic evaluation, consistent with findings from 2005–2011 [33]. Also in line with the most recent review of reimbursement dossiers from 2012–2020 was that the majority of pharmaceutical indications fell into the highest disease severity category, suggesting this trend has continued between 2020 and 2024 [34]. The present study adds to the literature by providing a structured overview and classification of the additional considerations relevant in reimbursement recommendations, as well as insight into the difference between the manufacturers’ proposed ICERs and the ICERs ZIN deemed most plausible, from which recommendations actually followed.

It is useful to place the Dutch findings in international context. A particularly relevant comparator is Sweden, which also uses a societal perspective in CEAs, and reimbursement decisions are taken by a comparable agency that also weights willingness to pay by disease severity. A recent review of Swedish reimbursement decisions between 2017 and 2024, similar to the present study, reported that ICERs were on average 30.6% below the relevant threshold and that no treatments were reimbursed above it, suggesting that thresholds in Sweden function effectively as price ceilings [40]. An important difference, however, is that the Swedish ICERs reflect prices after confidential discounts have been incorporated, whereas the ICERs reported by ZIN in the present study reflect manufacturer list prices before negotiation. The two systems are similar in their use of severity-varying willingness to pay, but differ in how strictly the resulting limits are enforced: the Netherlands publishes explicit reference values that are not applied as strict reimbursement thresholds, while Sweden does not publish formal thresholds but appears to apply them as effective ceilings.

The main strengths of this study lie in its comprehensive coverage of reimbursement dossiers over an extended period, the combined use of quantitative and qualitative approaches to describe system outcomes, and the systematic documentation of how CEA results inform pricing recommendations. Some limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. First, although the policy reforms introduced in 2015 motivate examining the role of CEAs in the Netherlands, this study is descriptive in nature, which precludes making causal claims. For example, we could not establish whether any observed changes in reimbursement recommendations are attributable to the increased use of CEAs, the policy reforms themselves, or any other contemporaneous developments. Second, our analysis relies on publicly available dossiers, which may not fully capture informal deliberations or implicit considerations influencing final recommendations. Third, the identification and categorisation of additional considerations was performed by a single researcher, with iterative discussions held within the author group and with pharmacoeconomic advisors at ZIN. While these discussions served as a validation procedure, there is a degree of interpretive judgment involved in grouping thematically similar considerations.

Beyond these study-specific limitations, there are also broader system-level constraints inherent to analysing the role of CEAs in reimbursement decision-making, primarily related to the confidential nature of price negotiations and financial arrangements. Confidentiality precludes verification of whether cost-effectiveness criteria are ultimately met when negotiations succeed, and investigation into the precise influence of specific considerations on final pricing outcomes. Although individual discounts remain undisclosed, the MoH’s annual letters to the House of Representatives on central financial arrangements provide aggregate insights, including average discounts achieved, total savings realised, and hypothetical savings had only ZIN’s minimum advised discounts to bring ICERs below the reference value been obtained. For all available years (2019–2022), the letters show that realised savings exceeded the hypothetical benchmarks, suggesting that negotiations are, on average, effective in safeguarding cost-effectiveness [41, 42]. While the MoH acknowledges that ZIN’s proposed discounts are not achieved in every individual case, it is emphasised that, in aggregate, BFAG delivers greater savings than those implied by ZIN’s minimum advice [41]. This may indicate that additional arguments used by ZIN beyond cost-effectiveness, contribute to higher negotiation power, although the confidentiality of agreements prevents this from being empirically verified.

This study demonstrates that the reference values in the Netherlands are primarily used as anchor points for formulating price recommendations, typically in combination with additional arguments that justify larger price reductions, such as uncertainty about long-term effects and anticipated changes in the treatment landscape. This approach reflects a policy framework aiming to combine efficiency with broader economic (e.g. promoting competition between products) and societal (e.g. managing uncertainty) considerations. While such a framework allows context-sensitive decision-making, it may also challenge predictability and consistency across pharmaceutical indications. For instance, there is currently no explicit guidance on how large additional price reductions should be in circumstances when there is structural uncertainty about long-term effectiveness, the anticipated budget impact is considered disproportionately high, or when pharmaceuticals have previously been reimbursed for other indications. Nonetheless, the arguments identified in this study and the overview of CEA outcomes provide a useful basis for broader reflection on pharmaceutical pricing and reimbursement in the Netherlands. In particular, further discussion is needed on how cost-effectiveness and additional considerations are jointly weighed in negotiations and decision-making processes. These questions are also addressed in a recently published report from the Societally Acceptable Expenditure on Medicines (Maatschappelijk Aanvaardbare Uitgaven Geneesmiddelen, MAUG) programme, presented to the MoH and developed by a collaboration of three independent governmental bodies, including ZIN [43]. The report argues that a cost-effective price, defined as one at or below the maximum reference value, is not always equivalent to a societally acceptable price, and proposes six guiding principles for determining where below the reference value an acceptable price should sit. Several of these principles correspond directly to the considerations identified in this study, including the role of uncertainty in effectiveness and cost-effectiveness, the macro-level budget impact, and the degree of innovation (e.g. indication expansions). The report also explicitly calls for further quantification of these principles to update the assessment framework [43].

Building on these ongoing policy discussions and the need for greater specificity in applying additional considerations, future research should examine how the arguments identified in this study can be translated into more explicit and operational price-adjustment guidelines. Such efforts could support more transparent decision-making and contribute to financially sustainable pharmaceutical reimbursement policies.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive overview of how CEAs are used in pharmaceutical reimbursement decision-making in the Netherlands. Our findings show that explicit reference values primarily function as anchors for price negotiations rather than as strict yes/no thresholds, reflecting the central role of negotiation in the Dutch reimbursement process. Recommendations to negotiate are often informed by additional considerations, such as uncertainty, treatment landscape dynamics and expected budget impact, reflecting an assessment framework that seeks to balance efficiency with broader economic and societal considerations. The frequently high ICERs at the list price and the regular use of considerations beyond cost-effectiveness suggest that, in the dossiers reviewed here, cost-effectiveness has informed price negotiation rather than determined reimbursement on its own. Together, these findings raise broader questions about the alignment between prevailing pharmaceutical pricing practices and societal value.
