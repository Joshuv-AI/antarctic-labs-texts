Brain-wide mapping of acute hypoxia-induced neuronal activation in mice: A c-Fos immunofluorescence study
Authors: Xingyao Yu, Pai Pang, Tao Liu, Xiufang Jiang, Jiayi Zhang, Xiangpei Yue, Jianjun Guo, Xiang Cheng, Tong Zhao, Yongqi Zhao, Zhonghua Dai, Lingling Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, defined as rapid ascent to high altitude with brief sojourn, triggers profound physiological adaptations while increasing risks of acute mountain sickness (e.g., gastrointestinal distress, pulmonary/cerebral edema). Beyond these somatic manifestations, cognitive deficits frequently emerge under hypoxic stress, yet the neural substrates mediating these impairments remain poorly mapped. To systematically characterize hypoxia-induced brain activation patterns, we conducted whole-brain mapping of neuronal activity changes in mice exposed to acute hypobaric hypoxia versus normoxic controls using c-Fos immunofluorescence, a robust marker of neuronal activation. Our functional analysis focused on key brain regions governing: cardiorespiratory homeostasis, attention, memory, emotional processing, motivation and reward. Quantitative c-Fos mapping revealed increased neural activity in several brainstem nuclei and repressed neural activity in higher-order forebrain after exposure to 24 h-6000 m hypobaric hypoxia. These datas establish the first brain-wide map of hypoxia-responsive neural networks, providing mechanistic insights into physiological adaptation and cognitive vulnerability after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure.Keywords: Hypobaric hypoxia, Cognition, Cardiorespiratory function, c-Fos

Introduction

High-altitude environment induces significant physiological challenges, primarily due to hypobaric hypoxia—a critical determinant of systemic adaptation and maladaptation (Millet and Debevec, 2020, West and Richalet, 2013). As altitude increases, the decline in partial oxygen pressure (PO2) of environment reduces hemoglobin oxygen saturation, triggering compensatory cardiorespiratory responses while increasing risks of acute mountain sickness (AMS), including cerebral edema and cognitive dysfunction (Luks et al., 2017, Storz and Bautista, 2022). Cognitive impairments—particularly in attention, memory, emotion, motivation and reward processing—are increasingly recognized as hallmark consequences of acute hypoxia exposure (McMorris et al., 2017, Zhang et al., 2024). Given the brain’s exquisite sensitivity to oxygen deprivation (Kalogeris et al., 2012, Kristián, 2004), neuronal hypoactivation caused by energy metabolism disruption may underlie these deficits (Lee et al., 2018, Rodriguez-Alvarez et al., 2015, Song et al., 2023), yet the spatial patterns of hypoxia-sensitive neural networks remain unmapped.

The elevation in respiratory rate and heart rate following exposure to hypoxia at high altitudes represents the predominant physiological response. The brainstem nuclei, for example, the dorsal vagal complex (DVC), integrating visceral inputs via vagal afferents, orchestrates cardiorespiratory adaptation to hypoxia (Duan et al., 2017, Joyce and Wang, 2022). Concurrently, higher-order cognitive networks exhibit differential vulnerability: The changes of attention are primarily characterized by prolonged reaction time and decreased accuracy (Pun et al., 2018, Steinman et al., 2021). Additionally, the memory function is affected with decreased learning and consolidation abilities, as well as dysfunction of working memory (Champod et al., 2013, Malle et al., 2013). As altitude increases, individuals tend to experience negative emotions such as anxiety and depression (Yu, 2024, Zhai et al., 2023), along with a decline in motivation (Çelik et al., 2022). However, it remains unclear how the neuronal activity changes in the brain regions associated with above cognitive dysfunctions after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure.

Thus, systematically evaluating the sensitivity of brain regions that are closely involved in the modulation of various cognitive functions to hypobaric hypoxia exposure may facilitate a deeper understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive decline following hypoxic exposure. Here, we screened some key brain regions related to concerned cognitive functions as objects, including DVC, ventral respiratory group (VRG), lateral parabrachial nucleus (LPB) for cardiorespiratory function; the subthalamic nucleus (STN), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), insular cortex (IC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), locus coeruleus (LC), dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) and the paraventricular thalamus (PVT) for attention; ventral and dorsal dentate gyrus (DG), cornu ammonis area 1 (CA1) and cornu ammonis area 3 (CA3) for memory; basolateral nucleus (BLA) and the central nucleus (CeA) for emotion and caudate putamen (Cpu), the substantia nigra (SN), the ventral tegmental area (VTA), and the nucleus accumbens (NAc) for motivation and reward. Besides that, we also employed c-Fos immunofluorescence—a robust neural activity marker—to establish the first brain-wide atlas of neuronal activation following 24 h-6000 m hypobaric hypoxia. By quantifying activation changes across cardiorespiratory, attentional, memory-related, emotional and reward-related brain regions, this work identifies vulnerable neural substrates and provides a framework for understanding hypoxia-induced cognitive decline.

Methods

Animal

Male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8 w and weighing between 18 g and 20 g, were purchased from SiBeiFu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China, Beijing) [SCXK(Beijing) 2019-0010]. The mice were housed at a room temperature of (23 ± 2) °C with a humidity range of 50–70 %, and exposed to a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. They had access to water and food ad libitum.

Acute hypobaric hypoxia treatment

The C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to two groups, (n = 6): the control group and the hypobaric hypoxia group. The mice in the hypobaric hypoxia group were placed inside a simulated high-altitude environment chamber (model DYC-DWI, manufactured by Guizhou Fenglei Aviation Ordnance Co., Ltd.). Within this chamber, the humidity was maintained between 50 % and 60 %, the temperature was kept at (23 ± 1)°C, and the altitude was increased at a rate of 30 m/s. Once the altitude reached 6000 m (corresponding to an oxygen partial pressure of 9.5 ± 0.1 KPa), a timer was started and the mice were continuously exposed to these conditions for 24 h (Fig. 1A). The mice exited the chamber on the second day, descending at a rate of 30 m/s. The mice in the control group, on the other hand, were housed in the same room but without exposure to the simulated high-altitude conditions.

DVC: dorsal vagal complex, VRG: ventral respiratory group, LPB: lateral parabrachial nucleus.

Immunofluorescence staining

After acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, the mice were anesthetized by 1 % pentobarbital sodium (6 μL/g, i.p.) and perfused with 4 °C paraformaldehyde (PFA). After post-fixed in 4 % PFA for 6 h, the gradient of dehydration was performed to transfer the brains from 20 % to 30 % sucrose solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C. Dehydrated brains were embedded in OCT and coronal brain sections (40 μm) were prepared using frozen slicer at −23 °C. Based on the varying sizes of different brain regions, 6–15 slices were collected for each brain region of interest from each mouse. Then, 3 slices were randomly selected along the anatomical sequence from the caudal part to the rostral part for staining. Floating slices were rinsed three times (3 × 10 min) in PBS and blocked in PBS with 2 % Triton X-100 and 10 % normal goat serum (NGS) for 2 h at room temperature (RT). After the samples were rinsed three times, incubation with primary antibody (rabbit anti-cFos, 1:1000, Abcam, ab190289) in 5 % NGS and PBS solution was performed at 4 °C overnight. After another three times rinsed, slices were incubated with a secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor® 594, donkey anti-rabbit, 1:500, Thermo Fisher, A30678) for 2 h at RT. After washing, slices were transferred onto pathological-grade slides and imaged with fluorescence microscopy.

Tissue image

The images were acquired using a digital slide scanning system (Olympus, VS200) at a 20 × magnification. The emission filter for the red light was set at 561 nanometers. OLYVIA software was utilized to generate high-resolution snapshots of coronal sections.

Cell counting and statistical analysis

The boundaries of brain regions were manually delineated using OLYVIA software based on The Mouse Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates (4th Edition). Additionally, the area of regions of interest was also calculated using OLYVIA software. The images were later employed in Fiji ImageJ's automated cell counting feature to quantify the number of c-Fos+ cells. Finally, we calculated the density of c-Fos+ cells in DVC, VRG, LPB, mPFC, ACC, IC, STN, LC, PVT, DRN, BLA, CeA, NAc, Cpu, SN, VTA, ventral and dorsal DG, CA1, CA3. Regions of interest were chosen based on a review of the literature on regions involved in cognitive and physiological functions. Subsequently, the density of c-Fos+ cells in these areas (i.e., the number divided by the area of the region) was calculated as an indicator of cellular activation in that region. The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 and presented as the arithmetic mean ± SEM. Statistically significant differences between groups were determined using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. For all analyses, *, P < 0.05, **, P < 0.01, ***, P < 0.001, considered statistically significant.

Results

Hypersensitivity of cardiorespiratory nuclei underlies autonomic compensation

Mice were divided into hypobaric hypoxic group (HH) and control group (Con) randomly. After acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure for 24 h, coronal brain sections (40 μm) were prepared for c-Fos immunofluorescent staining and image analysis (Fig. 1A). The c-Fos protein expressed after cell activation, which is commonly used as an indicator of activation levels in a specific brain region (Perrin-Terrin et al., 2016).

Acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure can induce compensatory increases in respiration and heart rate. To identify whether the activity of brain regions related to respiration and heart rate changes after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, we focus on the brainstem area, including dorsal vagal complex (DVC), lateral parabrachial nucleus (LPB), and ventral respiratory group (VRG) for investigation, which play crucial roles in maintaining normal respiratory and heart rates. The results revealed that the density of c-Fos+ cells in DVC, LPB and VRG in HH group were increased significantly compared with control group (Fig. 1C, D and F), with Fig. 1B and E showing representative images for DVC, VRG and LPB. These observations suggest that the activation of these brain regions after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure may be the neural basis for inducing compensatory increases in respiration and heart rate.

Hypoactivation of attentional circuits predicts attention decline

Executive control

Previous studies showed that acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure could induce executive control impairment during attention process (Altbäcker et al., 2019; Zani et al., 2023). In order to investigate the activation of brain regions related to executive control after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, four brain regions—the subthalamic nucleus (STN), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), insular cortex (IC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)—were selected for neural activity test. Fig. 2A, D and F show representative images for ACC, IC, mPFC and STN. The results showed that there was a significant decrease in the density of c-Fos+ cells in mPFC, IC and ACC compared with the control group (Fig. 2B, C, E). However, no difference was observed in the density of c-Fos+ cells in STN between two groups (Fig. 2G). These results suggest that the decreased activity of the mPFC, IC and ACC may participate in promoting impairment of executive control after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure.

Vigilance

Vigilance refers to the ability to sustain focused attention over prolonged periods while maintaining a state of readiness to detect and respond to rare, unpredictable, or behaviorally relevant stimuli in the environment (Pershin et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). As the altitude increases, the individual's vigilance level shows a downward trend (An et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025). To test how the activity of brain regions related to vigilance changes after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, three brain areas were selected: dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN), the locus coeruleus (LC), and the paraventricular thalamus (PVT). Fig. 3A, C and E shows representative images for DRN, LC, and PVT. The results revealed that compared with control group, the density of c-Fos+ cells increased in DRN (Fig. 3B), while decreasing in LC (Fig. 3D). However, the density of c-Fos+ cells in PVT remained consistently high levels both before and after acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure. No statistically significant differences in c-Fos+ cell density were observed between the HH group and the control group (Fig. 3F). These results imply that the neural activity of LC and DRN may serve as critical regulators in the decline of vigilance levels following acute hypobaric hypoxia exposure.

Reduced hippocampal activation underlies memory suppression

The deficits in memory formation and retrieval represent the primary manifestations of memory impairment induced by high-altitude environmental exposure (Bao et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2024). To investigate the alterations in the activity of brain regions associated with memory following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, we focused on examining three subregions of both the dorsal and ventral hippocampus: dentate gyrus (DG), cornu ammonis area 1 (CA1) and cornu ammonis area 3 (CA3), as it has been shown that they are associated with memory acquisition and retrieval functions. Fig. 4A and C show representative images for dDG and vDG. The results revealed that the density of c-Fos+ cells in the HH group decreased significantly compared with control group in both the dorsal and ventral DG (Fig. 4B, D), while there was no notable change in the density of c-Fos+ cells in dorsal and ventral CA1 (Fig. S1B, E) and CA3 brain regions (Fig. S1C, F) following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure. (Fig. S1A and S1D shows representative images for dorsal CA1, CA3, and ventral CA1, CA3, respectively.) These findings suggest dorsal and ventral DG may play an important role in inducing memory impairment following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure.

Maladaptive responses in limbic networks link to emotional abnormalities

Exposure to the hypoxic environment at high altitudes is capable of inducing negative emotions, such as anxiety, depression, and despondency (Verma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2024). Moreover, as the altitude ascends, the generation of negative emotions intensifies increasingly (Ma et al., 2024; Zegarra-Rodríguez et al., 2022). To investigate the changes in activity of brain regions related to emotion following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, we focused on the amygdala, which plays a central role in emotion regulation. We further assessed the neural activity alterations in its two key subregions: the basolateral nucleus (BLA) and the central nucleus (CeA). Fig. 5A shows representative images for BLA and CeA. The results showed that compared with control group, the density of c-Fos+ cells in CeA increased significantly in HH group (Fig. 5B), while there was no difference in the density of c-Fos+ cells between two groups in BLA (Fig. 5C). These findings indicate that the CeA may play a critical role in the induction of negative emotions following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure.

Blunted motivation-related neural activity reflects reward processing deficits

Rewards and motivation constitute one of the primary intrinsic driving forces underlying individual behavior (Mehta et al., 2024; Morris et al., 2022). At high altitude environment, individuals may demonstrate a decrease in motivational drive or a diminished preference for rewards (Kaneko et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2021; Sheth et al., 2018). To further investigate the alterations in activity of brain regions associated with reward and motivation following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure, we identified four brain regions that are most strongly linked to motivational and reward processing: the caudate putamen (Cpu), the substantia nigra (SN), the ventral tegmental area (VTA), and the nucleus accumbens (NAc). Fig. 6A and B show representative images for Cpu, NAc, SN and VTA. The results indicated that the density of c-Fos+ cells in Cpu and NAc in HH group decreased significantly compared with control group (Fig. 6C and D), while there was no difference in the density of c-Fos+ cells in SN and VTA between the two groups (Fig. 6E and F). These findings imply that the decline in motivation and reward-related functions following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure may be significantly associated with the diminished activity of the Cpu and NAc brain regions.

Discussion

Acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure induces significant alterations in neural activation patterns across key brain regions, reflecting both adaptive physiological responses and maladaptive cognitive decline. c-Fos, the expression product of the immediate early gene (IEG), serves as a robust marker for neuronal activity. The peak of c-Fos protein expression occurs 60–90 min subsequent to neurons being exposed to external stimuli (Perrin-Terrin et al., 2016). Despite the fact that hypoxia exerts an influence on protein synthesis, there remains a lack of direct evidence to reveal that acute hypoxia exposure (24 h) specifically suppresses c-Fos synthesis within the brain. Moreover, c-Fos immunofluorescence staining has been utilized as a robust tool to investigate the alterations in brain region activity following hypoxic exposure (Brown et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2025; Moya et al., 2020). Our whole-brain c-Fos mapping revealed distinct spatial profiles of neuronal hypoactivation and hyperactivation following 24 h hypobaric hypoxia (6000 m equivalent altitude), providing mechanistic insights into the neural substrates underlying hypoxia-induced physiological compensation and cognitive deficits.

The ventral respiratory group (VRG), lateral parabrachial nucleus (LPB) and dorsal vagal complex (DVC) exhibited marked hyperactivation, consistent with their established roles in coordinating cardiorespiratory adaptation to oxygen deprivation (Bateman and Levitt, 2023; Chen et al., 2020; Díaz-Casares et al., 2012; Krowicki et al., 1997). Notably, the heightened activity in DVC likely mediates compensatory tachycardia and tachypnea through vagal efferent modulation (Yao et al., 2023), while potentially contributing to gastrointestinal symptom generation via gut-brain axis signaling (Xie et al., 2022). Intriguingly, DVC activation may also regulate hypoxia-induced inflammatory homeostasis, given its documented capacity to suppress peripheral immune responses (Jin et al., 2024), as minimal central inflammation was observed in our previous experimental results (Geng et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2017). These multifunctional properties position the DVC as a critical hub for systemic hypoxia adaptation.

As an essential constituent of the ventral respiratory column (VRC), the VRG integrates chemical signal inputs from both central and peripheral sources through interactions with other nuclei, including the dorsal respiratory group (DRG) and the parafacial respiratory group (pFRG), thereby establishing a stable respiratory rhythm (Bush and Ramirez, 2024; Silva et al., 2019). In the streptozotocin (STZ)-induced Alzheimer's disease (AD) rat model, following acute normobaric hypoxia treatment, there was a marked reduction in c-Fos expression within the nucleus tractus solitarii (NTS) and the rostral VRG. This reduction subsequently inhibited the increase in respiratory frequency that would normally occur in response to hypoxic stimulation (Brown et al., 2019). In contrast, mice that had previously undergone no prior treatment exhibited heightened neural activity in the VRG brain region following acute exposure to high-altitude hypoxia in our experiment, indicating a potential compensatory upregulation of ventilation in the body.

The LPB serves as a crucial link between respiratory control and the arousal state, with its glutamatergic neurons receiving chemosensory signals from the medulla oblongata, including those indicating hypoxia and elevated CO₂ levels (Kaur et al., 2013; Kaur and Saper, 2019; Li et al. 2025). Given the functional heterogeneity of the LPB, two distinct subtypes of glutamatergic neurons exert different effects under hypoxic conditions. The FoxP2 (Forkhead Box protein 2)-positive neurons in the LPB modulate respiratory frequency and pattern through their connections with respiratory rhythm-generating regions, including the pre-Bötzinger complex (Kaur et al., 2024). Once the CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide)-positive neurons in the LPB detect signals of hypoxia or hypercapnia, they activate the cortical arousal system, such as the thalamus and the basal forebrain, thereby triggering a rapid arousal response (Kaur et al., 2017). This mechanism may play a protective role in alleviating the adverse effects of oxygen deficiency and cognitive decline in the body following rapid ascent to high altitude.

Conversely, prefrontal-limbic networks displayed widespread hypoactivation correlating with cognitive-emotional deficits. The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), insular cortex (IC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) showed particularly activity reductions, potentially disrupting executive attention through impaired top-down control mechanisms (Ma et al., 2023; Sridharan et al., 2008). This finding aligns with clinical observations of attentional decline at altitudes > 1500 m (Burtscher et al., 2024), suggesting prefrontal hypoactivation as a neural correlate of hypoxia-induced executive dysfunction. Parallel decreases in locus coeruleus (LC) activation may compound these deficits by reducing noradrenergic modulation of vigilance (Liebe et al., 2024; Maness et al., 2022), creating a dual neural mechanism for attentional impairment. Here, it is crucial for us to recognize that the mPFC and the ACC exhibit a notably elevated level of activity under normobaric normoxic conditions. The rationale behind this observation lies in the fact that decision-making processes (Mugan et al., 2024), encompassing choices like opting between water intake and food consumption, or deciding between activity and rest, along with social interaction behaviors (Chen et al., 2024), are pervasive throughout the daily behaviors of mice.

The dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) serves as the primary source of forebrain 5-hydroxytryptamine/serotonin (5-HT), projecting serotonergic fibers to multiple brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex, thereby modulating vigilance and attentional behaviors across various species (Jing et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Activation of serotonergic neurons in the DRN enhances active wakefulness and suppresses rapid eye movement (REM) sleep in mice (Moriya et al., 2021). However, contrary to the observed decline in vigilance and attention following acute high-altitude exposure (Steinman et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025), our findings reveal that acute hypobaric hypoxia promotes neural activity in the DRN region. A possible explanation is that the DRN may be compensatorily activated to mitigate the issue of abnormally low arousal levels in individuals following acute hypobaric hypoxic exposure.

Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that after acute normobaric hypoxia, serotonergic terminals increase in brainstem nuclei regulating respiratory rhythms in mice, and these nuclei express 5-HT2A receptors (Morinaga et al., 2016; Raul, 2003), suggesting they may receive serotonergic modulation from the DRN. DRN 5-HT neurons are capable of responding to the signals of elevated CO2 levels in the brain (Smith et al., 2018) and regulate ventilatory responses during hypercapnia by projecting to the retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN), which is situated in the brainstem (Leirão et al., 2021). Additionally, activation of serotonergic neurons in the DRN reduces the incidence of apnea induced by seizures in mouse models, with DRN- pre-Bötzinger complex (pre-BötC) projections mediating seizure-induced respiratory arrest (Ma et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2018). These provide plausible mechanisms for DRN serotonergic neurons to regulate respiration. We therefore hypothesize that activation of DRN serotonergic neurons following acute hypobaric hypoxia likely represents a compensatory response to hypoventilation.

The LC and DRN regulate wakefulness, attention, and emotion through the norepinephrine (NE) and 5-HT systems, respectively, but they maintain the balance between cognition and emotion through synergistic effects. For instance, the rapid-acting antidepressant ketamine elevates both NE and 5-HT levels in the synaptic cleft to counteract depressive behaviors (López-Gil et al., 2019). Moreover, the loss of noradrenergic and serotonergic neurons serves as the basis for early emotional disorders in Alzheimer's disease (AD) (Khan et al., 2023). Interestingly, after acute exposure to hypobaric hypoxia, the LC and DRN exhibit opposite activation states. The activation of the LC is inhibited, while that of the DRN is elevated. NE acts as a stress hormone, and excessive NE can induce negative emotions such as anxiety and evasive behaviors (McCall et al., 2015). The 5-HT system projects to regions like the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, inhibiting anxiety-like behaviors and promoting emotional stability (Chen et al. 2024; Ren et al., 2018). Therefore, the distinct activity states of the LC and DRN following acute hypoxia essentially represent a dynamic equilibrium achieved through inhibiting the stress-related effects of NE and enhancing the emotion-stabilizing effects of 5-HT. This compensatory mechanism contributes to the maintenance of the body's psychological adaptability in high-altitude environments.

The paraventricular thalamic nucleus (PVT) is a critical thalamic nucleus governing arousal, with its glutamatergic neurons maintaining high activity levels during wakefulness and during the recovery period from anesthesia (Ren et al. 2018; Wang et al., 2023). Given that mice sustain a certain level of arousal both before and after acute hypobaric hypoxia exposure, the PVT exhibits consistently elevated neural activity. When it comes to the maintenance of wakefulness, the roles played by the LC and the PVT are in alignment (Carter et al., 2010). Consequently, prior to acute exposure to hypobaric hypoxia, the LC exhibits a comparatively elevated activation state.

Hippocampal subregions exhibited differential vulnerability, with dentate gyrus (DG) showing significant hypoactivation while no change in CA1 and CA3 activation. DG, a neurogenic niche critical for pattern separation and spatial encoding, exhibits heightened metabolic demands due to ongoing neurogenesis and dense granule cell connectivity, potentially rendering it more susceptible to oxygen deprivation. The observed DG hypoactivation may reflect disrupted neurogenesis or impaired synaptic integration of newborn neurons under hypoxic stress, and the mechanisms previously linked to memory encoding deficits in hypoxia models (Dai et al., 2023). The DG-CA1 interaction is critical for pattern separation and spatial encoding (Li et al., 2022), while the DG-CA3 circuitry is involved in working memory and memory consolidation (Twarkowski et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023). The selective vulnerability of these subregions may underlie characteristic memory deficits in encoding and consolidation under hypoxia (Ando et al., 2023; Nation et al., 2017), suggesting discrete hippocampal microcircuits as therapeutic targets for cognitive preservation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that moderate hypoxia, such as intermittent hypoxia (IH), can promote neurogenesis in the DG subregion through the HIF-1α–Notch signaling pathway, thereby alleviating impairments in spatial learning and memory (Li et al., 2021; Li et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2005). In contrast, more severe hypobaric hypoxia not only damages the morphological structure and synaptic plasticity of hippocampal neurons(Gao et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2022) but also inhibits DG neurogenesis, as evidenced by a reduction in immature neurons, neural progenitor cells (NPCs), and immature neurons (Chauhan et al., 2022). Currently, there is a lack of direct evidence indicating the expression of c-Fos protein or mRNA in neural precursor cells or neural stem cells. Consequently, our study on the c-Fos protein did not observe the impact of acute hypobaric hypoxia exposure on neurogenesis in the DG subregion. In other words, we cannot explain the reduction in neural progenitor cells or immature neurons observed in previous studies based on the decrease in c-Fos+ neurons within DG subregion.

Our findings reveal distinct patterns of limbic and reward circuit dysregulation following acute hypobaric hypoxia, providing novel insights into the neural substrates of hypoxia-induced emotional dysregulation and motivational deficits. The observed hyperactivation of the central amygdala (CeA) coupled with preserved basolateral amygdala (BLA) activity suggests a dissociation in amygdala subregion responses to hypoxia. The CeA, a critical node in fear and stress processing, exhibits heightened activity under acute hypoxia, potentially reflecting maladaptive activation of defensive circuits in response to oxygen deprivation. This aligns with clinical reports of increased anxiety-like behaviors and negative emotional states at high altitudes (Dos Santos Quaresma et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2015; Li et al., 2000), where CeA hyperexcitability may amplify threat appraisal and emotional distress (Wilson et al., 2019). In contrast, the unaltered BLA activity implies preserved encoding of contextual emotional information, indicating selective vulnerability of amygdala microcircuits to hypoxic stress. This dissociation may disrupt coordinated emotional processing, as BLA-CeA cross-talk is essential for balancing affective valence (Bolmont et al., 2000), though further circuit-level analyses are required to identify this hypothesis.

Reward-related circuits exhibited blunted activation, with significant hypoactivity in the caudate putamen (Cpu) and nucleus accumbens (NAc), key hubs for motivation and reward valuation. The Cpu, integral to habit formation and action selection, showed reduced c-Fos+ density, suggesting impaired striatal plasticity critical for goal-directed behavior. Similarly, NAc hypoactivation may reflect diminished dopamine-dependent reward anticipation, as NAc integrates dopaminergic inputs from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) to drive motivated behavior (Mohebi et al., 2019). Notably, the preserved activity in VTA and substantia nigra (SN) implies intact dopaminergic neuron survival but potential dysregulation of downstream signaling pathways. This dissociation between midbrain integrity and striatal hypoactivation mirrors patterns observed in preclinical models of anhedonia, where disrupted striatal dopamine receptor signaling uncouples motivation from reward perception (Fung et al., 2021). The observed reward circuit dysfunction may thus underlie the reported declines in motivation and hedonic tone during hypoxia exposure (Çelik et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2023). It should be pointed out that the Cpu and the NAc manifest a pronounced activated state under normobaric normoxia conditions. This activation can be attributed to the fact that the Cpu and the NAc play a pivotal role in the routine behavioral patterns of mice, specifically in their daily endeavors of water intake and food procurement (London et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2022).

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the acute 24-hour exposure model may not fully recapitulate acute altitude adaptation processes. Second, only male subjects were selected as the research objects because the estrous cycles of female animals are inherently unstable and difficult to control, which could potentially introduce confounding variables affecting the final outcomes. Notably, gender-based differences emerge in the decline of emotional or cognitive functions in mice following exposure to hypobaric hypoxia. After chronic (3-month) exposure to hypobaric hypoxia at an altitude of 4250 m, male mice exhibit a higher susceptibility to cognitive decline compared to females, with a more pronounced degree of decline (Zhu et al., 2022). Given that estrogen plays a role in mitigating cognitive decline under such conditions, incorporating experiments with female animals into future research endeavors is essential to ensure the robustness and reliability of our conclusions. Third, c-Fos mapping provides robust activity snapshots, a single timepoint at the end of exposure to hypoxia. Compelling evidence has emerged indicating that following 1-hour exposure to 16 % O2, the pyramidal neurons within the CA1 and CA3 subregions of the rat hippocampus exhibit a heightened level of activity (Hencz et al., 2023). Our experimental findings reveal that the activities of both CA1 and CA3 remained at extremely low levels before and after 24-hour hypobaric hypoxia exposure. This implies that during 24-hour hypobaric hypoxia exposure, the activities of CA1 and CA3 might exhibit a pattern of initial elevation followed by a decline that ultimately returned to the baseline. Regrettably, these dynamic fluctuations failed to be detected throughout our research. Consequently, complementary real-time monitoring techniques (e.g., multi-channel extracellular recording or calcium signal fiber photometry recording in vivo) could better provide characterize temporal dynamics of neural coding alterations. Fourth, the observation of brain region activity in this experiment falls under resting-state research. We did not combine any behavioral paradigms to modulate the activity of the brain regions of interest, so as to observe the impact of acute hypoxia on cognitive function. Fifth, the molecular mechanisms driving observed activation changes remain to be elucidated, particularly regarding oxygen-sensing pathways and metabolic adaptation cascades.

Conclusion

Our brain-wide activation atlas identifies brainstem nuclei hyperactivation as central drivers of physiological compensation, while prefrontal-hippocampal-limbic hypoactivation underlies cognitive-emotional deficits during acute hypobaric hypoxia. These findings establish a neuroanatomical framework for understanding altitude-related morbidity and highlight potential targets for therapeutic interventions to preserve cognitive function in high-altitude environments.
