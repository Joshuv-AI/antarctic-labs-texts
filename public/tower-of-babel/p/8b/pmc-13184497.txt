Periprosthetic joint infection as failure of containment: A causal and probabilistic framework
Authors: Jiri Gallo, Zeynep Bal, Stuart B Goodman
Published: 2026

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a major complication of arthroplasty surgery [1]. The dominant biological narrative remains contamination, adhesion, biofilm formation, and clinically manifest PJI [2]. That sequence is valid, but as a general explanation, it leaves a central paradox unresolved. Microbial challenge to the peri-implant environment is probably common, whereas overt PJI remains rare [1,3].

Arthroplasties remain exposed over years to recurrent hematogenous episodes, spread from adjacent tissues, and other routes of microbial access [[4], [5], [6]]. Any satisfactory framework must therefore explain not only how PJI occurs, but also why long-term containment persists despite repeated opportunities for challenge.

Recent ecosystem-oriented accounts have enriched the field by showing that implant-related infection is not simply a microbial event, but a context-dependent interaction among biomaterials, host immunity, and microbial behavior [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, this still leaves a question insufficiently answered: if the relevant ecological ingredients are common, why is overt PJI so uncommon?

Complexity alone is not yet an explanation [[11], [12], [13]]. A framework that lists interacting determinants does not by itself explain why most challenged systems remain clinically silent while only a minority cross into sustained pathology. The unresolved issue is therefore not ecological complexity itself, but the logic of rare transition.

We propose that many clinically relevant forms of PJI are better understood as failure of containment at the host–implant interface. Here, containment means preservation of local host control: a state in which microbial challenge may occur, persist transiently, and even adapt, yet remains buffered and clinically silent. Overt PJI emerges when containment fails and the peri-implant system crosses a threshold from constrained challenge to self-sustaining pathology. This concept complements ecological interpretations by explaining why disturbance is usually absorbed and only rarely becomes disease.

Containment is needed because prevailing accounts remain vulnerable to a frequency problem [14,15]. If microbial contact, adaptive bacterial responses, immune fluctuation, metabolic stress, and repeated perturbation are all plausibly frequent around implants, then any satisfactory theory must explain why the final clinical event remains so infrequent. Otherwise, mechanistic plausibility risks outrunning epidemiological plausibility.

The problem becomes more provocative when a long-term perspective is adopted. If one considers not only perioperative contamination but also recurrent bacteremia [6,16], possible extension from adjacent tissues [5], or other potential routes of delayed access [17], the number of lifetime opportunities for peri-implant challenge becomes far greater than the number of clinically evident PJIs. The key biological question is therefore not simply how microorganisms reach the implant, but why most such encounters fail to culminate in overt disease.

Failure of containment addresses this problem by shifting the explanatory target from exposure to loss of control at the tissue-implant interface [10,18,19]. In most arthroplasties, equilibrium appears to be preserved despite repeated challenges. Overt PJI should therefore not be treated as the expected consequence of ordinary microbial challenge, but as the relatively rare outcome of threshold failure in a previously buffered system [10,20,21].

Containment does not mean sterility, complete exclusion of microbes, or absence of transient challenge [19]. It refers to a regulated peri-implant state in which host defense, tissue integrity, vascular support, mechanical conditions, and microbial burden remain sufficiently coordinated to prevent challenge from becoming self-propagating pathology. In this sense, containment is an active property of the host–implant system, not a passive absence of microorganisms [7,18]. Therefore, containment should be understood as a durable system's property that may persist for years despite recurrent perturbation. This definition is especially relevant in arthroplasty because implants create a biologically altered niche [1,10,22]. Surface properties, conditioning films, wear byproducts, local hypoxia, nutrient gradients, altered immune cell performance, microdamage, and revision burden can all reshape the interface at which host and microbe interact. The peri-implant environment is therefore not neutral ground, but a stressed ecological site whose stability depends on continual regulation [7,19,22]. Failure of containment occurs when this regulated state can no longer be maintained. At that point, microbial challenge is no longer merely present; it becomes biologically consequential. Overt PJI is thus not simply microbial presence on an implant; it is the breakdown of a locally constrained equilibrium (Fig. 1).

Exposure, persistence, and disease should not be treated as interchangeable states [19,23]. Microbial access may occur without accommodation, accommodation without durable persistence, and persistence without overt PJI. Even biofilm-associated survival need not equal clinically manifest PJI if the broader system still contains it [7,21].

Conceptually, one may think of the trajectory as access to the peri-implant niche, accommodation within that niche, constrained persistence, destabilization of host control, and overt PJI [19,24]. Movement between these states is conditional rather than automatic. Disease appears when progression succeeds across several gates that usually remain closed. This perspective is particularly important for delayed and late presentations [25]. Long after implantation, the relevant issue may be less a single dramatic inoculation than the cumulative failure of a system repeatedly exposed to low-grade challenge [7,21]. Whatever the route, opportunities for challenge appear more frequent than clinically overt PJI. The explanatory burden therefore lies not only in mechanisms of access, but in why containment usually endures.

From a causal perspective, no single proximal factor explains PJI across its heterogeneous forms [11,12]. What matters is conditional causation within a structured vulnerability system [18,19,26]. Microbial inoculation is necessary, but rarely sufficient. Likewise, host frailty, wound problems, revision burden, or biofilm persistence may each contribute, but their importance depends on timing, interaction, and system state [7]. The causal question is not which factor caused PJI in isolation, but under what conditions the system lost its capacity to contain the challenge.

That logic is inherently probabilistic [27]. In practice, clinicians do not infer PJI from one datum alone. They revise their judgment depending on wound history, inflammatory markers, aspiration results, imaging, implant age, prior surgery, soft-tissue condition, and systemic vulnerability and other factors accumulate (Fig. 2). Bayesian reasoning is therefore not an optional embellishment of this framework; it is its bedside expression [28]. When disease is a conditional transition rather than an inevitable sequel of exposure, probability must be updated in context [29].

Formally, this logic can be represented as a sum over biologically plausible progression paths to clinical PJI, with each path expressed as a product of conditional gate probabilities. This perspective helps avoid two opposite errors. The first is over-attribution, in which any microbial finding is treated as direct proof of disease. The second is under-recognition, in which subtle but persistent signs are dismissed because they do not fit a simplistic linear model. A containment-based view encourages proportionate interpretation. The key question is whether the evolving evidence suggests that local control has been lost.

Prediction should shift from isolated risk factors toward estimation of transition risk [[29], [30], [31]]. The goal is not simply to identify exposure, but which host–implant systems are contributing to loss of containment. Repeated wound disturbance, poor soft tissues, impaired perfusion, revision burden, persistent inflammatory drift, or metabolic fragility may together indicate a system nearing destabilization.

Diagnosis may also be sharpened by asking not only whether microbes are present, but whether containment is eroding [[32], [33], [34]]. Current criteria remain indispensable, but future progress may come from detecting early signs of reduced resilience, chronic low-grade inflammatory disequilibrium, or persistent wound vulnerability.

Prevention extends beyond reduction of inoculum [20,21,35]. Asepsis and antibiotic prophylaxis remain foundational, but prevention also includes preserving buffering capacity through soft-tissue protection, hematoma control, adequate local perfusion, metabolic optimization, and avoidance of repeated local destabilization.

Therapy is perhaps the most important translational extension [1]. If PJI is viewed only as microbial persistence, treatment naturally centers on antibiotics and surgery. If it is also viewed as failure of containment, therapy can additionally target the transition itself by stabilizing tissues, improving perfusion, reducing mechanical or inflammatory amplification, modulating host responses, and designing biomaterial interfaces less permissive to loss of control [35,36]. Antibiotics remain essential but need not define the full therapeutic frame.

In conclusion, recent ecosystem-oriented theories correctly show that implant-related infection is more than a germ-centered event. However, a final challenge remains: explaining why clinically overt PJI remains so rare if many relevant ecological perturbations are common over the lifetime of an implant. Failure of containment addresses that challenge. It does not replace ecological thinking; it gives it a sharper endpoint.

Therefore, PJI should be understood not simply as microbial presence, nor even as ecological complexity, but as a rare, state-dependent transition in which the host–implant system loses its capacity to buffer challenge and crosses into self-sustaining pathology. Its strength is that it explains both sides of the paradox: why microbial challenge may be common over the lifetime of an implant, yet clinically overt PJI remains uncommon. In this way, this concept provides a translational framework for prediction, diagnosis, prevention, and therapy beyond antibiotics alone.
