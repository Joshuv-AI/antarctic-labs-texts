Letter to the Editor regarding “Comfort Feeding in Hospitalised People with Dementia: A Retrospective Study of Survival Following Comfort Feeding Recommendations”
Authors: Rui He, Lingjia Xu
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We recently reviewed the article titled “Comfort Feeding in Hospitalised People with Dementia: A Retrospective Study of Survival Following Comfort Feeding Recommendations [1].” This study investigates the outcomes of comfort feeding in patients with advanced dementia, focusing on survival rates, readmission rates, and the association between dysphagia severity and recommended feeding consistency. A retrospective analysis of 163 hospitalized dementia patients who received comfort feeding recommendations revealed that 42% of the patients died during their hospital stay, with a median survival of just 13 days. The 30-day and 90-day survival rates were 25% and 15%, respectively. The study further found that fluid consistency was significantly associated with patient survival: patients recommended thicker fluids or thickened fluids with ice chips had relatively longer survival times, whereas those advised thin fluids or restricted to ice chips had the shortest survival, reflecting the close link between feeding strategies, swallowing difficulty severity, and prognosis.

The findings of this study underscore the critical role of comfort feeding as an approach aligned with palliative care principles, emphasizing quality of life and patient comfort over aggressive interventions. In clinical practice, comfort feeding offers a humane and patient-centered option for those with severe swallowing difficulties, allowing patients to continue eating and drinking in a manner that prioritizes their comfort and dignity. These insights highlight the need for healthcare providers to have candid discussions with families about expected outcomes, helping them make informed decisions that prioritize the patient’s comfort.

Despite the valuable insights provided, the study has some limitations that warrant further consideration. A significant drawback is the lack of comprehensive control for potential confounding factors such as comorbidities, nutritional status, and levels of cognitive impairment [[2], [3], [4]]. These factors are known to significantly influence survival outcomes and could introduce bias in interpreting the results [5]. Without adequately adjusting for these variables, isolating the specific impact of comfort feeding from the overall health condition of the patients becomes challenging, potentially leading to overestimation or underestimation of its effects.

Additionally, while the study focused on survival rates and readmission, we suggest that future research could include assessments of how comfort feeding impacts patients' quality of life, such as measures of comfort, satisfaction, and overall well-being. These factors are crucial for a comprehensive evaluation of the benefits of comfort feeding beyond clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, while this study provides valuable data on comfort feeding in patients with advanced dementia, we recognize the authors’ significant contributions to this important area of research. Our suggestions aim to enhance the robustness and applicability of the findings, not to question the study’s validity. Addressing the highlighted limitations, such as better controlling for confounding factors and incorporating quality of life assessments, would provide a more nuanced understanding of comfort feeding's impact on patient care. We commend the authors for their work and encourage further studies that continue to refine and optimize care strategies for this vulnerable population.
