Macrolide-induced hypersensitivity reactions in the pediatric population
Authors: Silvia Caimmi, Lucia Liotti, Sara Riscassi, Greta Rosset, Paolo Bottau, Annamaria Bianchi, Giuseppe Crisafulli, Fabrizio Franceschini, Francesca Mori, Francesca Saretta, Rocco Luigi Valluzzi, Enrico Vito Buono, Gian Luigi Marseglia, Carlo Caffarelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Macrolides are widely prescribed antibiotics in pediatric practice and are commonly used as alternatives in children with suspected or confirmed beta-lactam allergy. Although generally considered safe and associated with low allergenic potential, hypersensitivity reactions to macrolides do occur and may lead to inappropriate drug avoidance. This review critically appraises the current evidence on macrolide-induced hypersensitivity reactions in the pediatric population, focusing on clinical manifestations, diagnostic approaches, cross-reactivity, and management strategies. Hypersensitivity reactions to macrolides are estimated to occur in 0.4–3% of treatments, with immediate reactions such as urticaria and angioedema being the most frequently reported. Anaphylaxis is exceedingly rare, while delayed reactions, particularly maculopapular exanthema, represent the most common non-immediate presentations. Severe cutaneous adverse reactions, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis, and fixed drug eruptions, have been reported but remain exceptional in children. The diagnostic workup of suspected macrolide allergy is challenging due to the limited sensitivity and poor predictive value of skin tests and the lack of validated in vitro assays. Current evidence supports the use of drug provocation tests as the gold standard for diagnosis, particularly in children with non-severe, skin-limited reactions. Recent guidelines increasingly favor direct oral challenge without prior skin testing in low-risk pediatric patients. Studies consistently demonstrate that most children with suspected macrolide allergy tolerate re-exposure, confirming that true allergy is uncommon. Cross-reactivity among macrolides appears to be infrequent in children but may occur, especially in severe reactions. Desensitization protocols, although not standardized, have been successfully applied in selected pediatric cases when macrolides are indispensable. Overall, accurate diagnosis through appropriate testing is essential to avoid unnecessary antibiotic restrictions, ensure optimal antimicrobial therapy, and reduce the burden of incorrect allergy labeling in pediatric patients.

Introduction

Macrolides are characterized by the presence of a macrolactone ring consisting of 12 to 16 carbon atoms (14 in erythromycin and clarithromycin, and 15 in azithromycin). This ring is typically substituted with one to three sugar residues, most commonly desosamine and cladinose, which are generally attached at the C3 and C5 positions. Such a structural organization underlies their pharmacological activity, as it facilitates binding to specific target sites within the ribosome [1, 2]. They mediate bacteriostatic and bactericidal activity by inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis through binding to a specific region of the 50S ribosomal subunit, known as the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET). The NPET constitutes the channel through which newly synthesized polypeptides exit the ribosome. Several studies [3, 4] have demonstrated that macrolides selectively interfere with the synthesis of certain proteins, while enabling the translation of others [2–4]. In addition to their antibacterial properties, macrolides have also been shown to exhibit anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities [5]. Macrolides constitute up to 30% of all oral antibiotics that are prescribed. Erythromycin, clarithromycin and azithromycin are the most used. They are generally considered safe with low allergenic activity, and therefore, used as alternative drug in children with proven beta-lactam allergy [6]. Indeed, macrolide hypersensitivity reactions are rarely reported, though clinically significant events have been described [1, 7–12]. However, self-reported reactions to macrolide were the most frequent among non-beta-lactam antibiotics in children [13, 14] and adults [15]. Hypersensitivity reactions to macrolides have an estimated frequency between 0.4 and 3% of treatments [10, 16–18]. These rates are considerably lower compared to those reported for other antibiotic classes, such as penicillins, cephalosporins, sulfonamides, and fluoroquinolones [1, 9, 10, 19]. Of note, the true prevalence may be lower than self-reported rates, especially in children [13, 18, 20]. The mechanisms underlying macrolide-induced hypersensitivity reactions remain unclear [21]. The current review critically appraises key studies related with macrolide hypersensitivity in the pediatric population and consider practical approaches to their management.

Clinical manifestations

Adverse reactions to macrolides may include nausea, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and vomiting. Rare side effects include QTc prolongation, sensorineural ototoxicity, and hepatotoxicity. In a study of drug-induced liver injury (DILI), although azithromycin appears overrepresented among cases of DILI in patients with pre-existing chronic liver disease compared with those without underlying hepatic conditions, it does not constitute the most frequent causative agent within this population [22]. In another investigation, amoxicillin–clavulanate, nitrofurantoin, and minocycline emerged as the agents most frequently associated with DILI, both in the general population and among patients with underlying chronic liver disease. Although azithromycin accounted for a relatively higher proportion of cases within the chronic liver disease subgroup, this increase was insufficient to make it the leading cause in this population [23]. Macrolides also interact with many drugs, through the affinity of 14C macrolides for CYP450 and the effects of clarithromycin on CYP3A metabolism.

In particular, when the immune system is elicited, it is called hypersensitivity reactions to macrolides, that can occur either immediate (occurring within 1–6 hours after drug intake) or non-immediate (delayed) reactions [1, 8, 10, 19, 24]. Interestingly, HLA-A*02:07 allele is associated with clarithromycin induced cutaneous adverse drug reactions in Han Chinese patients [25]. The most frequently described reactions include immediate urticaria and/or angioedema [19]. Although anaphylactic reactions to macrolides are extremely rare, some have been documented and confirmed through positive allergy tests, particularly for azithromycin, clarithromycin, and erythromycin. There are fewer than 20 published cases in the literature [1, 19]. Furthermore, one case of anaphylaxis was later found to be caused by the carmine dye in the tablet coating rather than by the azithromycin itself [19]. Among macrolides, one study reported a higher incidence of anaphylaxis with azithromycin (30%) than with clarithromycin (15.5%). However, the incidence of anaphylaxis to clarithromycin in pediatric studies is extremely low, at one case per million children per year [8]. Among delayed hypersensitivity reactions, maculopapular exanthema (MPE) and unspecified rashes are the most common [1, 8–11, 19, 24, 26]. In more than 40 clinical trials in children who received azithromycin for infections, a total of 29 of the 2655 children (1.1%) developed skin eruptions [27]. It is remarkable that 1% is often the rate of skin eruption to placebo. MPE is the most common type of rash and is characterised by an acute, generalized eruption of erythematous macules and papules without superimposed desquamation. Differentiating MPE from a viral rash can be complex. The cutaneous eruption usually occurs within 7 to 10 days (range 5 to 21 days) after treatment, starting from the trunk and proximal extremities with cranio-caudal progression. Drug-induced exanthema typically involves itching, the confluence of the rash in gravity-affected areas of the body, face involvement and purpuric lesions on the lower extremities. It is also not associated with prodromal symptoms, such as fever, conjunctivitis, or rhinorrhea, myalgia and arthralgia [28]. Spiramycin and clarithromycin have been associated with other skin reactions, including Henoch-Schönlein purpura and leukocytoclastic vasculitis [17]. Serious reactions such as Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS), Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) [29], and Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS) syndrome have been associated with macrolides, including azithromycin and clarithromycin. SJS/TEN are rarely acute severe, potentially life-threatening, mucocutaneous hypersensitivity reactions characterized by epidermal necrosis and detachment. There is little data in the literature on paediatric cases of SJS/TEN. Some case series have reported that antibiotics are the most common cause in children [30–32], while others anticonvulsivants [33, 34]. Among antibiotics, the most frequent culprit is penicillin. Macrolides can also induce SJS/TEN [30, 31, 34]. The mortality rate is variable but generally lower than in adults [30, 35, 36]. The most common causes of death in children are sepsis and multiple organ failure [30]. Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis (AGEP) and fixed drug eruptions (FDE) are also reported to be caused by macrolides [17, 30].

Skin testing and in vitro tests

Non-irritating concentrations have been determined for skin prick test erythromycin (5 mg/mL), azithromycin (100 mg/mL), and clarithromycin (50 mg/mL) and for intradermal test (IDT), erythromycin (0.05 mg/mL), azithromycin (0.01 mg/mL), and clarithromycin (0.5 mg/mL) [17, 29, 37, 38]. However, skin prick tests and IDT are not fully validated for macrolides, and their utility is often considered uncertain. Some studies indicate low sensitivity of skin testing for immediate reactions [39]. In children with history of hypersensitivity reactions, skin prick test to clarithromycin generally resulted negative [11, 40, 41]. At a maximum concentration of 0.05 mg/mL [40], IDT to clarithromycin resulted positive in 45% of children but none of children with positive IDT reacted to clarithromycin provocation test, while 18% of children with negative IDT had a positive challenge to clarithromycin. Sensitivity and positive predictive value were 0, specificity was 50%, negative predictive value was 82%. At a maximum concentration of 0.5 mg/mL [11], IDT to clarithromycin was positive in 32% of cases. None of them had a positive challenge, while oral challenge was positive in 11% of children with negative IDT. Sensitivity and positive predictive value were 0, specificity was 73%, negative predictive value was 92%. On the other hand, one study showed that at a maximum concentration of 0.05 mg/mL, IDT to clarithromycin had a 75% sensitivity, 90% specificity, and 98% negative predictive value although still showing a low positive predictive value (33%) due to low allergy prevalence [41]. Along this line, Yildirim [42] showed that in immediate reactions, at a concentration of 0.05 mg/mL, IDT to clarithromycin had a low sensitivity of 33%, a specificity of 92%, a low positive predictive value of 40.0% and a negative predictive value of 90%. At the higher concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, the sensitivity was 100%, the specificity was 78%, the positive predictive value was 33%, and the negative predictive value was 100%. Moreover, in delayed reactions, at a concentration of 0.05 mg/mL, IDT to clarithromycin resulted negative in all cases. At the concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, IDT was positive in 9/26 (34%) children, and the subsequent oral provocation test was positive in 1/9. Oral provocation test was positive in 1/17 who had negative IDT results. Delayed IDT readings at 24, 48, and 72 h. resulted negative in all children. Several case reports have investigated in vitro assays. Basophil activation test and specific IgE antibodies to macrolide have been studied for immediate reactions. The lymphocyte transformation test has been studied for delayed reactions. Such laboratory tests lack validation and are not made commercially available [27].

Drug provocation test

Drug provocation test is recommended when skin testing results are negative. Taking into account, the very low rate of macrolide anaphylaxis and the low reliability of skin testing, and the possibility of anaphylaxis in children who perform clarithromycin drug provocation test (DPT) without prior skin testing [18]. Many experts recommend performing challenge test without routine skin testing for macrolide allergy evaluation in patients with a history of non-anaphylactic reactions, such as rash, urticaria, and angioedema [9, 15, 39]. Skin testing should be reserved for anaphylaxis or to reassure patients [39]. When skin tests are negative, a drug provocation test can be performed to establish the diagnosis when appropriate. Usually drug challenges should not be performed in the following conditions [39]: Severe cutaneous adverse drug reactions (SJS/TEN), DRESS, AGEP, Drug-induced neutrophilic dermatosis, Drug-induced autoimmune diseases (Bullous pemphigoid, Pemphigus vulgaris, Linear IgA bullous disease, Drug induced lupus), Generalized bullous FDE, Exfoliative dermatitis, Severe drug anaphylaxis, Organ-specific drug reactions (Cytopenias, DILI, Nephritis, Pneumonitis, Meningitis, Pancreatitis), Drug-induced vasculitis. It has been shown that drug challenge with macrolides with daily doses for 4 days after the challenge day, had a NPV (Negative predictive value) of 97.4% [9]. Many protocols for macrolide drug challenge have been used, as depicted in Table 1. In patients who have reported mild skin reactions to macrolides (e.g. FDE or urticaria) 1-step or 2-steps drug challenge are suitable. In these cases, macrolides can be safely reintroduced in 95% of patients [15, 40, 41, 44]. Recently, the Joint Task Force on Practice Parameters (JTFPP) [39] suggested to use 1-step or 2-steps drug challenge for non-anaphylactic reactions to macrolides without a previous skin test. Single full-dose challenge can be performed in patients with low risk and without important comorbidities. In case of doubtful reactions, placebo-controlled challenges can be considered to confirm or refute allergy. Desensitization to macrolides

Drug desensitization is a procedure aimed at inducing temporary tolerance to medication, allowing a safe administration when no therapeutic alternatives exist. For macrolides, desensitization is considered an effective strategy, particularly when a macrolide is the optimal or only available treatment option for a patient with a confirmed hypersensitivity reaction [9, 24, 45]. Positive skin tests, anaphylaxis and severe cutaneous allergic reactions (SCAR) are indications for desensitization.

While robust, standardized protocols for macrolide desensitization are limited in the literature compared to beta-lactams, reported case series have demonstrated successful outcomes [46]. These protocols are often adapted from penicillin desensitization regimens [46]. For instance, three reported cases of clarithromycin desensitization in the literature were successful, and one of these patients also successfully completed azithromycin desensitization [45]. A 68-year-old female with a history of anaphylaxis to erythromycin and bronchospasms to roxithromycin successfully underwent oral clarithromycin desensitization [16]. Another 68-year-old female, who experienced anaphylaxis to azithromycin and urticaria/bronchospasms to clarithromycin, underwent successful desensitization to oral clarithromycin [45]. Notably, her positive skin prick tests (SPT) to both clarithromycin and azithromycin reverted to negative six weeks post-desensitization, suggesting an immunological shift. An 11-year-old pediatric patient with a history of diffuse urticaria to clarithromycin successfully underwent clarithromycin desensitization [26]. This was particularly relevant as an earlier attempt at azithromycin desensitization in the same patient resulted in a delayed urticarial rash [46].

Self-reported and true hypersensitivity reactions in the pediatric population

Studies specifically evaluating macrolide allergy in children are scarce, yet crucial, given that macrolides often replace beta-lactams in children with reported hypersensitivity reactions, which can lead to lifelong avoidance of suspected antibiotics [20, 47]. Establishing true antibiotic allergy is vital to ensure the selection of the most appropriate, safe, and cost-effective antibiotic, as mislabeling can increase the risk of antibiotic resistance, healthcare costs, and poorer concordance with prescribing guidelines. Several studies provide insights into the true prevalence of allergic reactions to macrolides in children by using skin testing and if negative or not performed, drug provocation test to confirm or exclude macrolide allergy (Table 2) [8, 9, 11, 13, 14, 40, 41, 43]. Cavkaytar et al. [40] found that in 48 children with history of hypersensitivity reaction to clarithromycin only 2 (4%) had a positive provocation test to clarithromycin and none of them had positive IDT. Mori F. et al. [41] showed that 16% of 64 children had confirmed clarithromycin allergy. Similarly, Barni et al. [8] studied 77 children with suspected macrolide allergy. They found that azithromycin was more allergenic than clarithromycin. Allergy was confirmed in 15.5% of reactions to clarithromycin (9 out of 58) and 47.3% of reactions to azithromycin (9 out of 19). This study suggests that azithromycin, with its longer half-life, might cause more allergic reactions and facilitate sensitization. Süleyman et al. [11] enrolled 61 children with suspected macrolide allergy and confirmed the diagnosis in 9.8% of patients, with 2 out of 39 (5.1%) for clarithromycin and 4 out of 22 (18.2%) for azithromycin. There was no significant difference between clarithromycin and azithromycin. Guvenir et al. [13] showed that among 65 pediatric patients with non-severe reaction histories to macrolides, hypersensitivity was confirmed in 2 (3%) children. IDTs to clarithromycin were positive in 1 patient, and clarithromycin provocation test was positive in 1 child. Grinlington et al. [14] found in 77 children with history of hypersensitivity reaction to macrolides that challenge to macrolides was positive to erythromycin in 8/52 children, children with delayed reactions to erythromycin had delayed positive challenge 6/16 (37%) cases, those with history of immediate reactions had immediate reactions at challenge in 2/36 (5.5%) cases. Recently, larger studies have been conducted. According to Karaatmaca et al. [9] clarithromycin allergy was confirmed in 8 (5%) out of 160 children (median age 48 months) with a suspicion of macrolide allergy, all of whom had mild index reactions. Clarithromycin was the most frequently suspected agent, reported by 151 patients (94.4%). Moreover, one patient with negative clarithromycin challenge reported cutaneous reactions after reuse and he was invited to be reevaluated. A second clarithromycin challenge showed urticarial lesions. Sillcox et al. [43] evaluated 112 children with a suspected macrolide allergy (clarithromycin in 81 (72.3%) patients and azithromycin in 31 (27.7%.), undergoing direct graded oral challenges. They showed that 90.2% of children tolerated the challenge, while 8.0% had an immediate reaction and 1.8% had a nonimmediate reaction. Children older than 12 and with other antibiotic allergies had a higher rate of positive drug challenge results, while a lower rate was found in those with a known food allergy comorbidity. In 102 patients with history of hypersensitivity reaction to clarithromycin and azithromycin, Yıldırım et al. [42] confirmed clarithromycin allergy in 8/88 (9%) children and azithromycin allergy in 1/14 (7.1%) children. It may be possible that unconfirmed hypersensitivity reactions to macrolides are due to rash caused by viral-macrolide interactions as seen in children with mononucleosis [48]. Cross-reactivity among macrolides

The concept of cross-reactivity among macrolides remains controversial [8, 24, 37]. Some earlier studies suggested that macrolide allergies were unlikely to be class allergies [7] and indicated that cross-reactivity appears to be more frequent among macrolides possessing a lactone ring with the same number of carbon atoms, suggesting a potential role of this structural feature [17]. More recent studies documented that cross-reactivity can indeed occur between macrolides with lactone rings of varying carbon atom counts [24]. Accordingly, the allergic determinants of macrolide molecules have not been identified, so the mechanisms of crossreactivity among macrolides is not clear. Cross-sensitization among roxithromycin, erythromycin, and clarithromycin has been documented [49]. A study reviewing pharmacovigilance data found cross-reactivities in 30 out of 253 explorations (11.9%) among macrolides [24]. So far, some studies in children showed that crossreactivity rarely occurs. A study in 160 children with suspected macrolide allergy, included only a child with history of reactions to both azithromycin and clarithromycin that not confirmed by challenge [9]. Another study included 1 child out of 77 with suspected macrolide allergy, who had a positive challenge result to erythromycin but tolerated roxithromycin [14]. However, case reports of crossreactivity have been reported. A study reported 1 child with anaphylaxis to both azithromycin and clarithromycin (a 14-membered macrolide) out of 3 cases of anaphylaxis to azithromycin (a 15-membered macrolide) [17]. Indeed, cross-reactivity seems to be more commonly reported for severe reactions like anaphylaxis. In a study, 2/6 patients with macrolide hypersensitivity had both clarithromycin and azithromycin allergy [11]. There is a need of studies that perform challenges in close temporal association to different macrolides to convincingly assess cross-reactivity.

Prevalence of macrolide hypersensitivity in patients allergic to beta-lactams

Despite their relative rarity, macrolide allergies may be more likely to occur in patients with a prior history of beta-lactam allergy. A study reported that among children with suspected clarithromycin allergy, a history of hypersensitivity to beta-lactams was present in 40% of cases [11]. However, hypersensitivity reactions to clarithromycin were confirmed in 2/35 children with previous beta-lactam allergy and in 4/19 who were switched to clarithromycin after a rash while receiving amoxicillin-clavulanic acid/amoxicillin. Furthermore, among children treated with clarithromycin following the onset of rash due to amoxicillin-clavulanate, the rate of positive clarithromycin oral challenges was significantly higher (9%) compared to children who received clarithromycin without previous beta-lactam exposure (3%) (p < 0.001). This finding suggests a potential role of pre-existing inflammation or a non–immune-mediated mechanism underlying the phenomenon. A second study published in 2025, conducted among 60 children with confirmed allergy to beta-lactams, reported a significantly lower rate (1,4%) of positive oral challenge for clarytromycin among these patients, thus confirming macrolides, and especially claritromycin, as a safe alternative in pediatric patients allergic to beta-lactams [6].

Conclusions

Macrolide allergies should be considered a sporadic event, unremarkable in children with beta-lactam allergy. For allergists, the approach to suspected macrolide allergy necessitates a diagnostic pathway that is less standardized than the one employed for beta-lactam allergy. Confirming the true allergic status through provocation test, if there are no contraindications, remains key to ensure that patients can safely receive these essential antibiotics when needed. Nowadays, in pediatric patients with non-severe, skin-limited symptoms, skin testing should be avoided, and direct drug challenge is a reliable, effective strategy for diagnosing hypersensitivity to macrolides [9, 43]. Moreover, the available evidence, also from few pediatric case reports, underlines that desensitization to macrolides is a feasible and successful intervention, often necessary given the drug’s importance and the potential for cross-reactivity. Finally, physicians managing patients with hypersensitivity reactions to macrolides should be aware of a rare risk of cross-reactivity among the macrolides. It is reasonable to consider an oral challenge to an alternative macrolide before recommending it.
