MMR and varicella immunization during dupilumab therapy

Introduction

Dupilumab is a mAb that is currently approved for treatment of moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD) in infants aged 6 months and older as well as for treatment of other conditions.1 Currently, the US Food and Drug Administration recommends deferring administration of live vaccines during dupilumab therapy owing to lack of data on safety and efficacy. Because of its action being limited to blocking IL-4 and IL-13, dupilumab acts as an immunomodulator rather than as immunosuppressive therapy.2

Understanding the safety profile of measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) and varicella vaccine administration in children undergoing dupilumab therapy is important to promote appropriate vaccination according to US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and World Health Organization guidelines. Our study reports on (1) the safety of MMR and varicella immunizations during treatment with dupilumab and (2) the impact of dupilumab therapy on MMR and varicella immunization rates in early childhood.

A single-center retrospective review of children younger than 9 years who were undergoing dupilumab therapy and had available vaccination records was performed with institutional review board approval (institutional review board approval no. 19-016617). Children were excluded from analysis for incomplete vaccine or dupilumab history, contradiction of live vaccines for an alternate medical condition, prior enrollment in a dupilumab clinical trial, or allergic reaction to dupilumab (Fig 1). The primary outcome measures were vaccine-associated infections (VAIs) or vaccine-associated adverse events (VAEs), which were defined as vesicular rash, granulomatous lesion, or fever within 21 days of MMR of varicella immunization and rate of MMR or varicella immunization. To evaluate for delayed VAEs, all charts were further reviewed for 90 days after immunization.

Results and discussion

Of the 362 patients younger than 9 years who were identified as taking dupilumab, 284 were included in the final analysis (Fig 1). The average population age was 5.5 years (range 1.4-8.8 years), and the average age of initiation of dupilumab therapy was 4.8 years for those with available data (range 0.92-8.79 years) (n = 252). Dupilumab therapy was initiated for AD (n = 262), asthma (n = 16), eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) (n = 4), EoE and asthma (n = 1), and EoE and AD (n = 1).

In all, 9 patients began receiving dupilumab within 4 weeks of receiving their MMR or varicella vaccination (Fig 1). Initiation of dupilumab therapy for these patients occurred an average of 15 days after they had received their MMR or varicella vaccination. There were no safety events, including VAEs or VAIs, in these patients. A total of 19 patients received their MMR or varicella vaccination after initiation of dupilumab therapy (Fig 1). Administration of dupilumab to these patients was initiated an average of 246 days (range 28-579 days) before varicella vaccination and an average of 248 days (range 28-579 days) before MMR vaccination. There were no VAIs; 1 VAE (a fever of 38.3°C and an erythematous nodule at the injection site on day 3) was reported in a patient from whom dupilumab was withheld only before administration of MMR and varicella vaccination. In the 9 patients from whom dupilumab was withheld both before and after immunization, 4 reported a flare of AD within 21 days of immunization. Of the 284 patients included in the final analysis, 280 received dose 1 of varicella vaccine. Of those patients, 216 were eligible for varicella dose 2 on the basis of their age (≥4 years) at the time of chart review. Varicella dose 2 was given to 190 patients. In 21 of the 26 patients who did not receive varicella dose 2, the delay was attributable to dupilumab. Of the 284 patients included in final analysis, 281 received MMR dose 1. Of those patients, 217 were eligible for varicella dose 2 on the basis of age (≥4 years) at the time of chart review. MMR dose 2 was given to 187 of them. In 25 of the 30 patients who did not receive MMR dose 2, the delay was attributable to dupilumab (Fig 2).

MMR and varicella vaccination.

The average age of vaccination for varicella and MMR dose 1 was 1.17 years (n = 280), with no difference between the before–dupilumab administration (n = 279) and after–dupilumab administration (n = 1) groups. The average age of vaccination for dose 2 of varicella was 4.30 years in the before–dupilumab administration group (n=165) compared with 4.55 years in the after–dupilumab administration group (n = 23) (P = .023). Similarly, the average age of vaccination for dose 2 of MMR vaccine was 4.29 years in the before–dupilumab administration group (n = 162) compared with 4.55 years in the after–dupilumab administration group (n = 23) (P = .029).

These data suggest that MMR and varicella immunization may be safe to administer to children receiving dupilumab therapy and that pausing dupilumab for administration of live vaccines may lead to flare of underlying AD. In our small study, there were no severe VAEs or VAIs. There was 1 reported VAE on day 3 after immunization. Fever and injection site reaction are commonly reported adverse effects of the MMR and varicella vaccines; thus, whether this is truly attributable to dupilumab is unclear.3 Additionally, in 4 of the 9 patients from whom dupilumab was withheld both before and after administration of MMR or varicella vaccine, AD flared.

Because of our study’s retrospective observational design, we are unable to evaluate the efficacy of MMR or varicella vaccination in patients taking dupilumab. Further study limitations include the fact that in the majority of eligible patients, dupilumab therapy was initiated for AD; thus, our findings are most robust for children receiving dupilumab for this indication. It is additionally important to note that given the elimination half-life of 19 to 32 weeks in pediatric patients, the children from whom dupilumab was withheld at the time of vaccination likely had therapeutic concentrations of dupilumab.4

These data further suggest that dupilumab therapy in early childhood may result in decreased and/or delayed vaccination rates for MMR and varicella. In our small sample, 21 varicella boosters (9.7%) and 25 MMR boosters (11.5%) were delayed because the children were taking dupilumab. There was a significant delay in the age of MMR and varicella boosters when before– and after–dupilumab administration rates were compared.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate rate of vaccination during dupilumab therapy and the third study to evaluate the safety of live vaccines during dupilumab therapy.2,5 Collectively, 74 patients have received a live vaccine while receiving dupilumab therapy.2,5 Of those 74 patients, 2 have reported nonserious VAEs (ie, 1 patient with body pain, malaise, and dizziness and our patient, who reported fever and injection site reaction).2 Reassuringly, there have been no severe VAEs or MMR or varicella VAIs in any of the aforementioned individuals.2,5

Further prospective study of MMR and varicella immunization for young children while they are receiving dupilumab therapy will be needed to validate these preliminary findings and evaluate efficacy. Our findings support the recent recommendation that immunizations not be delayed while a pediatric patient is receiving dupilumab, especially in light of the recent measles outbreaks.6

Clinical implications

These data suggest that MMR and varicella immunizations may be safe to administer to children undergoing dupilumab therapy and that pausing dupilumab for administration of live vaccines may lead to flare of underlying AD.
