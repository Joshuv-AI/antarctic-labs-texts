Hypothalamic endoplasmic reticulum stress drives pubertal precocity due to early-onset obesity in female rodents
Authors: Elvira Rodríguez-Vázquez, Álvaro Aranda-Torrecillas, Manuel Jiménez-Puyer, Oscar Freire-Agulleiro, María J. Sánchez-Tapia, Miguel Ruiz-Cruz, Violeta Heras, María López-Sancho, Luisina Ongaro, Cecilia Perdices-López, Daniel J. Bernard, Rafael Pineda, Francisco Gaytan, Miguel López, Juan M. Castellano, Manuel Tena-Sempere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceChild obesity is a worrying condition, of escalating prevalence, with adverse health consequences along lifespan. Child obesity is associated with pubertal disorders; yet, the mechanisms whereby obesity can perturb pubertal maturation remain ill-defined. Stress to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) at central hypothalamic pathways is involved in the pathogenesis of obesity. We report here that central ER stress also contributes to obesity-induced accelerated puberty in female rodents. This mechanism is proposed to involve a circuit including the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus, where Kiss1 neurons, as key elements in pubertal maturation, seemingly contribute. Our findings uncover a pathophysiological pathway for disordered puberty due to obesity, and may set the basis for more personalized therapies of pubertal disorders in children suffering obesity.

Obesity is a major health problem worldwide, bound to multiple comorbidities and increased mortality burden (1). In line with the view that obesity is primarily a brain disease, functional perturbation of neuronal circuits within the hypothalamus, as a key center for metabolic and body weight homeostasis, can cause overweight (2). Accumulating evidence points out the involvement of hypothalamic endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in the pathogenesis of metabolic alterations and obesity (3–6). As key pillars for cellular homeostasis, proteins are subjected to precise regulatory mechanisms, centered around the ER, a dynamic organelle responsible for their proper assembly, folding, and secretion (7). Proteins that fail to fold correctly are normally degraded in the cytosol. Yet, conditions that compromise ER homeostasis can lead to accumulation of misfolded proteins, resulting in ER stress (8, 9), which activates the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR), as a mechanism directed to restore protein balance (8, 9). In mammals, the UPR is primarily mediated by three transmembrane sensors located in the ER: i) Activating Transcription Factor 6α (ATF6α), ii) Protein Kinase RNA-like Endoplasmic Reticulum Kinase (PERK), and iii) Inositol-Requiring Enzyme 1α (IRE1α). These sensors remain inactive while bound to the ER chaperone, GRP78 (for glucose regulated protein 78 kDa, aka BiP). When ER stress occurs, GRP78 becomes attached to and sequestered by misfolded proteins, which triggers the release and subsequent activation of UPR sensors and downstream cascades (9–12).

The contribution of central ER stress to body weight control and obesity was initially disclosed by studies showing that adult overnutrition induces hypothalamic ER stress, with subsequent impairment of central insulin and leptin signaling (13). Supporting its pathogenic role in obesity, central infusion of the ER stress inducer, thapsigargin, disrupted hypothalamic leptin and insulin signaling, resulting in increased appetite and body weight (14). In contrast, administration of taurourso-deoxycholic acid (TUDCA), a well-known ER stress reliever, enhanced hypothalamic leptin signaling, leading to a reduction in food intake in conditions of obesity (5, 13, 15, 16). Elevated levels of various ER stress markers have been documented in the hypothalamus of rodent models of both adult and early-developmental obesity (3, 5, 13, 15, 17–19), indicating that ER stress may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of obesity and its comorbidities across the lifespan (20, 21). Alleviation of hypothalamic ER stress in such conditions has been associated with improved metabolic phenotypes (3, 6, 17, 18, 21, 22).

Childhood obesity is a particularly worrying condition, as its prevalence is escalating alarmingly, with 20% of children and adolescents being obese; most children suffering obesity are likely to remain obese as adults (23). Among its multiple comorbidities, childhood obesity is associated with pubertal disorders; obesity being regarded as a major contributing factor for the trends of earlier puberty onset worldwide (24, 25). This phenomenon is particularly prominent in girls, although possibly happening also in boys (26). This association reflects the tight connection between body energy status and pubertal timing; in fact, the nutritional and metabolic status is one of the most important modifiers of the age of puberty (27, 28). Pubertal awakening of the reproductive axis is driven by the concerted activation of the neurosecretory activity of hypothalamic neurons producing gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). However, since GnRH neurons are mostly devoid of receptors for the main metabolic regulators of puberty and the reproductive axis, their modulation takes place mainly via intermediary afferent pathways, sensitive to the regulatory actions of metabolic signals. Among them, neurons producing kisspeptins, named hereafter Kiss1 neurons, have emerged as master regulators of puberty, with a relevant role in transmitting metabolic information to GnRH neurons to modulate pubertal timing (29, 30). In the rodent hypothalamus, Kiss1 neurons are found mainly in two distinct populations, in the arcuate nucleus (ARC) and the anteroventral periventricular area (AVPV) (31). Kiss1 neurons, especially in the ARC, play a major role in the metabolic control of puberty (26, 32, 33), either by transmitting the metabolic effects of key hormones or sensing changes in energy status, via factors such as AMPK and SIRT1 (30).

The eventual role of hypothalamic ER stress in the metabolic regulation of puberty and its potential contribution to obesity-induced precocious puberty remains totally unexplored. However, fragmentary evidence suggests the involvement of central ER stress in specific metabolic-reproductive disorders. Hypothalamic ER stress has been documented in an adult rat model of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), showing also altered hypothalamic kisspeptin (34). In addition, we have recently shown that activation of ceramide pathways from the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) has a major role in the pathogenesis of obesity-induced precocious puberty. Hypothalamic ER stress and ceramide signaling are tightly bound in adulthood (35), and brain ceramides have been proposed to mediate the increase in hypothalamic ER stress associated with high-fat diet (HFD) and obesity in adult rodent models (6, 35). We present herein a comprehensive series of expression and functional analyses that explore the involvement of hypothalamic ER stress in the metabolic regulation of female puberty, its putative sites of action and contribution to obesity-induced pubertal alterations.

Results

Hypothalamic Levels of ER Stress Markers Change during Juvenile–Pubertal Transition and in Early Obesity.

The protein levels of representative ER stress markers were analyzed in the mediobasal hypothalamus (MBH) of female rats across the infantile–juvenile–pubertal transition (Fig. 1A). While content of GRP78, a chaperone that allows alleviation of misfolded protein aggregation, remained unaltered during the infantile-to-pubertal maturation, other ER stress markers displayed clear trends for suppressed protein content in the MBH of maturing female rats (Fig. 1B). ATF6α protein levels tended to be reduced, albeit without reaching statistical significance, while the content of p-IRE1α, p-PERK and p-eIF2α were significantly suppressed in the MBH of peripubertal female rats. Yet, the developmental profiles of these factors were somewhat different, so that p-IRE1α levels remained elevated in late juvenile age but massively dropped to nearly undetectable levels in peripubertal rats, while the reduction in hypothalamic levels of p-PERK and p-eIF2α was already detectable during the infantile-to-juvenile transition (Fig. 1B). In line with previous reports, expression levels of Kiss1, encoding kisspeptins, as a major driver of pubertal maturation, was increased during the infantile-to-pubertal transition in the MBH of female rats, with maximal levels at the peripubertal stage (Fig. 1C).

Given the role of hypothalamic ER stress in the pathogenesis of obesity, protein levels of ER stress markers were assessed also in the MBH of a validated model of early-onset obesity (36), due to postnatal overnutrition (ON), linked to accelerated puberty in female rats, that mimics clinical observations in girls with obesity (Fig. 1D). In line with our previous data (33, 37), female rats subjected to ON exhibited increased body weight (Fig. 1E), which was detectable already before weaning and persisted during the pubertal transition, together with elevated food (energy) intake (Fig. 1F), along with early onset of puberty, as evidenced by advanced ages of vaginal opening (Fig. 1G) and first estrus (Fig. 1H), as well as higher relative uterus weight (Fig. 1I). This phenotype of obesity and earlier puberty was associated with changes in the hypothalamic expression of ER stress markers, which included a substantial increase in the hypothalamic levels of p-PERK and p-eIF2α, while the content of ATF6α was decreased (Fig. 1J), without significant global changes in GRP78 or p-IRE1α levels at the MBH of obese female rats with advanced puberty.

Central Induction of ER Stress in Lean Female Rats Mimics Obesity-Induced Precocious Puberty.

Given the activation of key ER stress markers in conditions of early obesity, pharmacological experiments were conducted to assess the putative causative role of central ER stress induction in pubertal acceleration. To this end, a validated protocol of central pharmacological stimulation of ER stress was applied to prepubertal lean female rats (Fig. 2A). Chronic intracerebroventricular (icv) administration of Thapsigargin (THAP), a well-known ER stress inductor, resulted in precocious puberty onset, without changes in body weight (Fig. 2B) or food (energy) intake (Fig. 2C). Pubertal advancement was denoted by the earlier ages of vaginal opening (Fig. 2D) and first estrus (Fig. 2E) in rats treated with Thapsigargin (NN+THAP), as well as by higher follicular maturation scores and ovulatory rates at postnatal day (PND) 34: 67% NN+THAP vs. 44% in the group injected with vehicle, NN+VEH (Fig. 2I). Yet, no changes were detected in terminal uterus weight or serum LH and FSH levels in NN+THAP rats at PND34 (Fig. 2 F–H). In line with the capacity of icv THAP to induce ER stress in adult animals (3), the hypothalamic content of key markers, such as ATF6α and p-eIF2α, was significantly increased in lean, THAP-treated female rats at puberty (Fig. 2J).

Pharmacological Alleviation of Central ER Stress Has a Marginal Effect on Obesity-Induced Precious Puberty.

Conversely, we analyzed the impact of pharmacological alleviation of central ER stress on puberty onset in our model of ON female rats that display accelerated puberty (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). ON female rats chronically icv injected with vehicle (ON+VEH) showed higher body weight and food (energy) intake than their corresponding controls (NN+VEH) along pubertal maturation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). Early overfeeding resulted also in accelerated puberty onset, as denoted by earlier ages of vaginal opening and first estrus (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E), as well as higher uterus weights (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F) and detectable ovulation in 60% of ON rats at PND30, an age-point when no control lean (NN) female rat had undergone ovulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I). Chronic icv administration of TUDCA, as a pharmacological agent with proven capacity to alleviate central ER stress (3), to ON female rats (ON+TUDCA) did not change body weight and feeding profiles during pubertal maturation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C), and had rather modest effects in terms of pubertal maturation, denoted only by a moderate reduction in the % of rats displaying complete vaginal opening at the end of the treatment, which dropped from >75% in ON+VEH rats to 50% in the ON+TUDCA group on PND30 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). Other parameters, including first estrus, uterus weight, serum LH levels, or ovulatory rates, were not modified by TUDCA administration in obese rats (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E–G and I). However, FSH levels tended to rise in ON rats, while TUDCA significantly suppressed FSH concentrations, in line with its moderate effect in delaying vaginal opening in obese rats (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H).

ARC Expression of GRP78, an ER Stress Chaperone, Is Altered in Obesity-Induced Precocious Puberty.

The somewhat discordant effects of pharmacological activation vs. alleviation of central ER stress on pubertal indices, prompted us to conduct anatomical analyses to map changes within the pubertal hypothalamus of key markers of ER stress. To this end, we conducted immunohistochemical (IHC) detection of GRP78, which is amenable for IHC analysis, as a pivotal component of the chaperone machinery activated upon induction of unfolded protein responses linked to ER stress, in the hypothalamus of lean (NN) and obese (ON) female rats at PND30, i.e., during the juvenile to pubertal transition. Our results revealed a significant increase in the hypothalamic content of GRP78 in ON female rats, as compared to NN rats. Interestingly, this rise in GRP78 content in obese female rats at puberty was prominently detected in the ARC and the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), where its levels were significantly increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B), in contrast with other hypothalamic nuclei, where a similar phenomenon was not detected.

Virogenetic Amelioration of ER Stress in the ARC, But Not PVN, Normalizes Obesity-Induced Advanced Puberty.

In order to dissect out the putative contribution of ARC vs. PVN ER stress in obesity-induced pubertal alterations, we applied virogenetic tools to alleviate ER stress in a nucleus-specific manner, using protocols previously validated in adult rats (6). To this end, we used a viral probe to further overexpress the chaperone, GRP78, in the hypothalamus of ON female rats, either at the ARC or the PVN, during the pubertal transition, following protocols of stereotaxic targeting and viral injections optimized previously in our group (37). First, immature ON female rats were stereotaxically injected into the ARC on PND20 with an adenovirus containing a vector for GRP78 overexpression (Ad-GRP78); this adenovirus was coinjected with fluorescent beads to independently validate the place of the injection. ON female rats stereotaxically injected with an adenovirus containing GFP (Ad-GFP) served as controls (ON+GFP) (Fig. 3A). Successful targeting of the ARC was documented by bead and GFP detection upon termination of experiments on PND30, as well as by immunohistochemical detection of increased GRP78 signal in ON+GRP78 animals (Fig. 3B). Notably, overexpression of GRP78 in the ARC was associated with a significant reduction in the number of immunoreactive cells for the ER stress markers, p-IRE1α and p-eIF2α, denoting alleviation of ER stress at this site, while the levels of p-PERK were not altered (Fig. 3C); for representative images of immunohistochemical analyses of the three signals, see SI Appendix, Fig. S3. While the protocol of ON was effective in causing a substantial (>45%) increase in body weight vs. reference values in pair-aged rats with normal nutrition, ON+GRP78 rats displayed values of body weight (Fig. 3D) and food (energy) intake (Fig. 3E) that were similar to those of ON+GFP controls. Yet, overexpression of GRP78 in the ARC largely normalized the accelerated pubertal timing in ON female rats, as evidenced by deferred ages of vaginal opening (Fig. 3F) and first estrus (Fig. 3G). This was associated with a partial decrease in the indices of follicular maturation, even if uterus weights and serum LH levels were not changed, and pubertal scores in terms of completed ovulation were only marginally reduced in ON+GRP78 rats (Fig. 3 H, I, and K). However, in line with the partial delay in puberty onset, ON+GRP78 rats displayed significantly reduced FSH levels (Fig. 3J).

A similar protocol was applied to drive GRP78 overexpression in the PVN in ON pubertal female rats. Stereotaxic injection of Ad-GRP78 into the PVN was conducted using previously validated protocols (37), in immature (PND20) female rats (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). ON+GRP78 animals were coinjected with fluorescent beads for tracing proper PVN targeting, while ON rats similarly injected with Ad-GFP served as controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). No differences in body weight or food (energy) intake were detected between control ON+GFP and ON+GRP78 groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). In contrast to ARC targeting, overexpression of GRP78 in the PVN did not normalize the accelerated puberty onset caused by ON in immature female rats. Actually, a trend for slightly advanced age of vaginal opening (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E) and first estrus (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F) was detected in ON+GRP78 rats. No changes were observed either for uterus weights or serum LH and FSH levels, and indices of follicular maturation were grossly similar between ON+GRP78 and control ON+GFP rats (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 G–J).

Changes in ER Stress Marker Expression in Kiss1ARC Neurons in Peripubertal Female Mice with Obesity.

Considering that amelioration of ER stress, specifically in the ARC, largely normalized obesity-induced pubertal acceleration, we analyzed changes in the gene expression profiles of key markers of ER stress pathways in Kiss1ARC neurons. For comparative purposes, Kiss1AVPV neurons, primarily involved in the ovulatory surge, that is also mandatory for the first ovulation, were also analyzed. To this end, we carried out isolation of Kiss1 neurons, from these two main hypothalamic sites, namely ARC and AVPV, using fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACS), in the Kiss1Cre/−::tdTomatoloxP/− mouse line, validated in our group (38). Kiss1 neurons were sorted from lean (NN) and early-overfed (ON) Kiss1Cre/−::tdTomatoloxP/− female mice, at two ages during pubertal transition, PND 25 and 32 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). This protocol allowed separation of the tdTomato positive cell fraction containing Kiss1 neurons, from the tdTomato negative fraction, enriched in cells other than Kiss1 neurons.

As per internal validation, the protocol of early ON in female mice caused a significant increase in body weight at the two age-points (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), and this was associated with a significant advancement of the age of vaginal opening, as denoted by the larger % of female mice displaying vaginal canalization on PND32 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Validation of FACS-isolated Kiss1 neurons was conducted in PND25 mice by assessing the expression levels of Kiss1, as well as Tac2 (that codes for NKB) and Pdyn (that codes for Dynorphin), as these are differentially coexpressed with Kiss1 in Kiss1ARC and/or Kiss1AVPV neurons (31). In addition, expression of Pomc was assessed in cell populations isolated in the ARC, to exclude contamination with the prominent population of neurons producing proopiomelanocortin (POMC) at this site. In the ARC, prominent expression of Kiss1, Tac2, and Pdyn was detected in the tdTomato-positive fraction of FACS, putatively containing Kiss1ARC neurons, which did not express detectable levels of Pomc (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). In contrast, expression of Kiss1 and Tac2 was negligible in the negative fraction, in which Pomc expression was high and Pdyn expression was clearly detectable, denoting the existence of non-Kiss1 cells producing dynorphin (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). In the AVPV, Kiss1 mRNA was detectable only in the tdTomato-positive fraction of FACS-isolated cells, whereas no expression was found in the negative fraction. Negligible expression of Tac2 was found, whereas Pdyn mRNA was clearly detected in the positive and negative cellular fractions of the AVPV, suggesting expression of this transcript in non-Kiss1 cells at this hypothalamic site (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D).

Expression analyses of ER markers in FACS-isolated Kiss1ARC and Kiss1AVPV neurons, namely the tdTomato-positive fractions from the MBH and preoptic area, respectively, revealed significant changes in the expression of the mRNAs encoding key ER stress markers selectively in Kiss1ARC neurons. In detail, Ire1α and Perk expression was significantly increased in ON female mice compared to NN female mice at the prepubertal stage. In addition, the Xbp1s/u expression ratio, considered an additional ER stress marker, was significantly enhanced during the prepubertal to peripubertal transition in ON female mice (Fig. 4A). Gene expression of other ER stress factors, including Grp78, Atf6α, Eif2α and Gadd34, was not altered in Kiss1ARC neurons of pre/peripubertal ON mice. Of note, expression profiles of ER stress markers remained unaltered in the tdTomato-negative fraction encompassing the ARC of ON female mice, either at PND25 or PND32, except for an increase in the levels of eIF2α in ON females at PND32 (Fig. 4A) No significant alterations in gene expression of any of the ER stress markers under analyses were observed in the tdTomato-positive fraction of the rostral hypothalamus, containing Kiss1AVPV neurons (Fig. 4B). Likewise, the tdTomato-negative fraction of this hypothalamic area did not display any detectable changes in ER stress marker expression at any of the two ages analyzed, except for an increase in Grp78 mRNA levels during the transition between PND25 and PND32 (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Hypothalamic ER stress is known to play a prominent role in the central control of body weight homeostasis and the pathogenesis of obesity and its complications (6, 13, 16, 39–41). Yet, eventual changes in hypothalamic ER stress markers during the pubertal transition and early-onset obesity remained basically unexplored, and the relevance, if any, of central ER stress in obesity-induced pubertal alterations had not been studied. Our data reveal a previously unnoticed drastic suppression of key components of ER stress signaling pathways in the MBH during normal postnatal maturation and pubertal transition, especially prominent for p-PERK/p-eIF2α. Admittedly, our initial protein analyses lacked cellular resolution and hence did not ascertain whether such change affects puberty-controlling neuronal populations within the MBH. Yet, the magnitude of this suppression is indicative of a concerted shutdown of ER stress across the mediobasal hypothalamus during pubertal maturation, which is likely to involve different cell types and circuits. A tenable explanation is that this phenomenon may play a permissive role for the active protein synthesis that is likely required for intense pruning and reorganization of hypothalamic circuits that take place before and during puberty, including the dramatic reshaping of Kiss1-GnRH neuronal networks (29, 42). It is well known that activation of eIF2α by the kinase, PERK, leads to global translational suppression (43, 44). Hence, reduction of p-PERK/p-eIF2α at the MBH already during the infantile–juvenile transition might be a prerequisite for active protein synthesis needed for the dynamic hypothalamic changes preceding normal puberty onset. Alternatively, ER might experience considerable stress during the early postnatal period, when very active tissue remodeling and protein synthesis occur, which may lessen as puberty onset approaches, with the consequent reduction of these ER stress markers. Yet, the fact that other components of ER stress pathways, such as IRE1α and, to a lesser extent, ATF6α, were also suppressed, albeit in a deferred manner during pubertal transition, suggests subtle differences in the eventual contribution of various central ER stress factors to normal pubertal development. Of note, induction of ER stress has been reported to impair axon outgrowth from ARC explants ex vivo (45), further supporting the putative permissive role of dampening hypothalamic ER stress for the plastic changes occurring before and during puberty.

In contrast to observations during normal postnatal maturation, analyses in a well-validated model of early-onset obesity in female rats demonstrated that, in line with previous data in adult rodents (46), exposure to an obesogenic nutritional regimen during lactation and after weaning can induce markers of ER stress at the hypothalamus of peripubertal female rats. This phenomenon was associated with an acceleration of puberty, suggesting that a delicate balance between activation and inactivation of ER stress factors is needed for proper pubertal timing. The mechanistic relevance of central ER stress induction in driving pubertal acceleration in conditions of obesity was further documented by the observation that central administration of the ER stress inducer, THAP, was sufficient to advance puberty onset and ovarian follicular maturation in lean female rats, therefore mimicking the impact of obesity on pubertal timing, despite no changes in body weight. Intriguingly, ER stress activation induced by obesity was not consistent across its major pathways, with a marked increase in p-PERK/p-eIF2α contents, but a significant suppression of ATF6α, and no changes in GRP78 or p-IRE1α levels; in the latter case, acceleration of puberty in obese female rats might have masked an increase of p-IRE1α, since its hypothalamic content dramatically drops between late-juvenile and pubertal ages (Fig. 1B). A grossly similar profile was detected after central THAP administration, except for a significant increase of ATF6α, which might reflect the rather global stimulus for ER stress caused by the pharmacological agent, in contrast with more specific effects of early-onset obesity, which might have also brought about compensatory responses. In any event, comparison of data from protein profiles and pharmacological experiments suggests that activation of the hypothalamic p-PERK/p-eIF2α pathway may contribute to obesity-induced pubertal acceleration.

In the above scenario, it was intriguing that central administration of TUDCA to obese peripubertal female rats, in a protocol known to alleviate ER stress in adult rat models of obesity (15), not only failed to change body weight and food intake during pubertal transition, but caused also rather marginal effects in terms of normalization of accelerated puberty, with only a modest improvement (in terms of delay) of the advanced age of vaginal opening and reduction of FSH levels in obese female rats, without changes in other pubertal indices. The fact that TUDCA did not cause major changes in body weight in pubertal animals, despite its well-proven effects in adulthood, is compatible with our previous observations indicating that puberty, as a period of accelerated body growth and weight accrual, is to some extent refractory to the metabolic effects of different signals (e.g., ghrelin) known to influence body weight in adulthood (47). On the other hand, the lack of more robust effects in terms of modulation of pubertal timing in immature female rats could be due to the widespread actions of TUDCA after icv injections, which does not permit selective targeting of specific hypothalamic regions, eventually leading to a “dilution” of its effects for the suppression of ER stress selectively at the areas involved in pubertal acceleration.

To ascertain the eventual anatomical substrate for the putative effects of ER stress on pubertal alterations linked to obesity, we first mapped the expression of GRP78, within the hypothalamus, in early obesogenic conditions. GRP78, also known as BiP, is a chaperone located upstream of the UPR pathways, with capacity to buffer ER stress responses (10). Despite the inherent difficulty to detect the expression of components of the ER stress pathways with spatial resolution (48), immunohistochemical analysis of GRP78 has been proposed as a proxy marker of ER stress in human brain tissue sections (49). Our analyses comparing lean and obese conditions in peripubertal female rats documented clear induction of GRP78 in two prominent hypothalamic areas, namely the ARC and the PVN, with relevant roles in the control of puberty and metabolism (29, 37), but apparently not in others, such as the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH), where a key role of ER stress in the control of adult body weight and thermogenesis has been proposed (41). Of note, such induction of GRP78 immunostaining did not translate into global changes in total GRP78 content, as detected by Western blot in MBH preparations, suggesting the restricted nature of these changes to specific hypothalamic nuclei. The ARC-PVN pathway was previously suggested as major conduit for the impact of obesity on puberty onset, likely via a circuit involving projections of Kiss1ARC neurons and de novo ceramide synthesis in the PVN (37); ceramide accumulation had been previously connected with ER stress induction in the context of adult obesity (6).

On this basis, we implemented virogenetic overexpression of GRP78 selectively at the ARC or PVN of immature female rats with early-onset obesity, based on analogous protocols for viral-driven genetic overexpression of this chaperone in the VMH of adult rodents (41). The rationale for such approach, despite the detected increase of endogenous GRP78 in early obesity, is based on the fact that GRP78 is induced upon activation of UPR, as a means to alleviate ER stress, while overexpression of GRP78, selectively in specific hypothalamic nuclei, as the VMH, can alleviate ER stress and reduce body weight in a model of HFD-induced obesity in adult male rats (6, 41). Effective virogenetic targeting of a vector harboring GRP78 coding sequence to the ARC not only enhanced its content and reduced the expression of downstream ER stress markers, such as p-IRE1α and p-eIF2α, in this hypothalamic nucleus, but largely normalized indices of pubertal maturation and delayed follicular maturation in obese female rats, which per se displayed an acceleration of puberty onset due to obesity. This phenotype is in striking contrast with the effect of a similar virogenetic approach in the PVN that, despite effective targeting and GRP78 overexpression at this nucleus, did not normalize any marker of puberty; if any, a modest advancement of vaginal opening and first estrus was detected. Taken together, these findings indicate that although induction of GRP78, as a general marker of ER stress, occurs at both the ARC and PVN of obese pubertal rats, only the former (ARC) seems to play a major mechanistic role for pubertal acceleration in conditions of obesity. Notably, chemical chaperones are being considered for pharmacological management of human diseases, including metabolic disorders (50, 51). Our data support that, if properly targeted to the ARC, chemical or genetically driven chaperones, as GRP78, may also be used for more efficient management of pubertal disorders due to early obesity.

Ample literature has suggested a bidirectional interplay between ceramide accumulation and ER stress in central and peripheral tissues, as a putative mechanism for metabolic disorders. In the brain, ceramide-associated lipotoxicity in the hypothalamus has been suggested to induce ER stress, as a pathway for the generation and/or progression of obesity and its comorbidities (6). Given that ceramide accumulation in the PVN in obese female rats occurs downstream Kiss1 afferents from the ARC (37), our current data strongly suggest that ER stress induction in the ARC is proximal to eventual changes in ceramide signaling in the PVN, as a potential output circuit for accelerated ovarian maturation driven by the sympathetic nervous system (37). This working model does not exclude that ceramide accumulation in the PVN might also induce local ER stress responses in peripubertal obese female rats, as denoted by enhanced PVN content of GRP78. Yet, our virogenetic studies strongly suggest that such induction is less determinant for pubertal acceleration than ER stress activation in the ARC.

In addition, expression analyses in isolated Kiss1 neurons indirectly supported a discernible role of ER stress phenomena in the ARC in obesity-induced pubertal alterations. Here, we capitalized on our capacity to isolate Kiss1 neurons, at their two main hypothalamic locations, i.e., ARC and AVPV, using fluorescence-activated cell sorting, in a mouse line that allows selective tagging of Kiss1 neurons with tdTomato (38). In keeping with the so-called KNDy hypothesis (31, 52), the positive FACS fraction from the MBH containing Kiss1ARC neurons was highly enriched in Kiss1 and Tac2 (encoding NKB), together with Pdyn (encoding dynorphin) expression. The negative fraction, in turn, was enriched in Pomc expression, documenting the presence of non-Kiss1 neurons with key roles in metabolic and pubertal control (53, 54). Expression of Perk and Ire1α was significantly increased in Kiss1ARC neurons from obese female mice at the juvenile (PND25) period, suggesting the activation of these two main ER stress pathways in conditions of obesity; this response appeared to be specific as it was not detected in the negative fraction containing non-Kiss1 cells. Admittedly, activation of key components of such ER stress circuits takes place at the posttranscriptional level, e.g., by changes in phosphorylation, which cannot be ascertained in FACS-isolated Kiss1 neurons, due to the scarcity of the biological material. Yet, although not providing conclusive mechanistic but rather correlative evidence, elevation of mRNA levels of the above sensors is highly indicative, and can be considered a proxy marker of induction of ER stress in Kiss1 neurons in the ARC following early obesity, at an age-point (PND25) that immediately precedes pubertal activation in obese but not lean female mice. Further confirmation of such activation came from the demonstration of a moderate but significant increase in the Xbp1 spliced/unspliced ratio during the transition between PND25 and PND32 in obese female mice. The factor Xbp1 is first transcribed as an unspliced Xbp1-u isoform that, following ER stress activation, is transformed into the spliced Xbp1-s variant, which is active and contributes to the induction of UPR (55). Hence, this mRNA ratio is a proxy marker of enhanced IRE1α activity and serves as quantitative index of ER stress levels. Altogether, these mRNA changes point out the activation of ER stress in Kiss1ARC neurons in conditions of obesity in pubertal mice, which is in line with the observed induction of some ER stress markers (e.g., p-PERK and its downstream, p-eIF2α) in the MBH of obese female rats with accelerated puberty. Interestingly, the changes in ER stress markers detected in Kiss1ARC were not observed in Kiss1AVPV neurons. This is congruent with the contention that Kiss1 neurons in the AVPV are likely less sensitive than Kiss1ARC neurons to metabolic distress (31, 56). Admittedly, the contribution of ER stress in other key neuronal populations, such as GnRH neurons, to obesity-induced pubertal acceleration cannot be discarded based on our data. In addition, given the correlative nature of our expression data, the effector mechanism whereby activation of ER stress in Kiss1ARC neurons may lead to accelerated puberty is yet to be defined and warrants future investigation. Notably, maternal obesity has been reported to induce hypothalamic ER stress in the early postnatal period in the offspring, affecting the development of melanocortin circuits (18). Given the proven interplay between melanocortin (POMC) and Kiss1ARC neurons in the metabolic control of puberty (53), it is plausible that at least part of the effects of hypothalamic ER stress activation on the Kiss1 system in conditions of early obesity may stem from perturbation of such POMC-Kiss1 neuronal interplay.

Early obesity is a worrying condition linked to long-term adverse health outcomes, such as cardiometabolic, musculoskeletal, gynecological, and psychological disorders, as well as increased risk of some forms of cancers and reduced life expectancy (26, 57). Hence, elucidation of the underlying mechanisms is mandatory in order to design more appropriate strategies for prevention and treatment. Together with previous data pointing out the hypothalamic roles of specific cell energy sensors and lipid mediators, such as ceramides, in conveying the effects of obesity on pubertal timing (33, 37, 58), our current study uncovers a previously unsuspected role of hypothalamic ER stress, which contributes to pubertal acceleration in conditions of obesity; a phenomenon which is centered mainly at the ARC and might involve, at least in part, Kiss1 neurons. Given the recent approval of pharmacological agents with capacity to alleviate ER stress for treatment of human diseases (9), and recent developments on the possibility of genetic manipulation of brain targets using extracellular vesicle technology in vivo (59), our data pave the way to exploring the potential use of ER stress-modifying agents in the personalized management of some forms of precocious puberty in children suffering obesity.

Female Wistar rats and genetically modified C57BL/6J female mice, bred in the vivarium of the University of Cordoba, were used. Experimental procedures and animal protocols were approved by the Ethics Committees of the University of Córdoba and Junta de Andalucía. Animal experimentation strictly adhered to the European Union normative for the use and care of experimental animals (Directive 2013/63/UE).

Pharmacological Compounds.

Thapsigargin (THAP, ER stress inducer; Ref: T9033) and sodium Tauroursodeoxycholate (TUDCA, ER stress inhibitor; Ref: 580549) were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). For in vivo experiments, THAP was dissolved in 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Ref: 1096780100), and TUDCA was dissolved in physiological saline (NaCl 0.9%). All drugs were administered through intracerebroventricular (icv) injections, in a final volume of 5 µL.

Experimental Designs.

1. Hypothalamic expression of ER stress markers along postnatal/pubertal maturation

To evaluate putative changes of markers of ER stress during postnatal maturation and pubertal transition, mediobasal hypothalamic (MBH) tissue was obtained from female rats at three postnatal developmental stages: mid-infantile (PND15), late juvenile/prepubertal (PND29), and peripubertal (PND36); sample size, n = 4/group. The protein content of selected ER stress markers was analyzed by Western blot (SI Appendix). For reference purposes, Kiss1 mRNA levels in MBH tissues at different stages of postnatal maturation (namely, infantile -PND10-, juvenile -PND20-, prepubertal -PND25-, and peripubertal stage -PND35-) were also assessed.

2. Hypothalamic expression of ER stress markers in a model of ON rats with accelerated puberty

To assess the impact of early-onset obesity on hypothalamic ER stress at puberty, the protein levels of selected ER stress markers were measured in the MBH of ON female rats at the pubertal transition. Thus, within a large cohort of ON female rats, followed up to PND32 (when pubertal maturation is expected to be completed in ON rats), a set of animals (n = 6/group) were sampled at PND29 for Western blot analyses; pair-aged NN female rats served as control. For validation purposes, body weight (BW), food intake (FI), vaginal opening (VO), and first estrus (FE; by vaginal cytology) were monitored daily during the pubertal progression in the reference ON female rat cohort and their controls, and uterus weights (UW) were recorded at the termination of the experiment on PND32.

3. Pubertal maturation after icv injections of THAP to induce central ER stress in lean female rats

To evaluate the impact of activation of ER stress on pubertal maturation, central ER stress was pharmacologically induced in prepubertal NN female rats by two daily icv injections of Thapsigargin (0.1 µg/day), between PND25 and PND34. NN female rats icv injected with vehicle (0.1% DMSO/rat) served as controls. BW, FI, VO, and FE were monitored daily throughout the treatment in all experimental groups. On PND34, animals were euthanized 60 min after the last injection, and tissues of interest were collected.

4. Pubertal maturation after icv injections of TUDCA to alleviate central ER stress in ON female rats

A validated pharmacological protocol of suppression of central ER stress (3) was applied to prepubertal ON female rats by two daily icv injections of TUDCA (5 µg/day), between PND24 and PND30. NN and ON female rats icv injected with vehicle (saline) served as nutritional model and treatment controls, respectively. BW, FI, VO, and FE were monitored daily throughout the treatment in all experimental groups. On PND30, animals were euthanized 60 min after the last injection, and tissues of interest were collected. Note that differential age-windows of treatments were applied in experiments using lean rats (THAP; NN) and obese rats (TUDCA; ON); this is justified by differences in pubertal timing under the two different nutritional conditions, with accelerated pubertal maturation being observed in ON rats (36). This allows proper targeting of similar maturational events in both nutritional models.

5. Pubertal maturation after virogenetic overexpression of GRP78 in the ARC or PVN in ON female mice

GRP78 protein was virogenetically overexpressed at the arcuate (ARC) or paraventricular (PVN) nucleus in immature early-overfed female rats. To this end, ON female rats were stereotaxically injected bilaterally at the nuclei of interest with an adenoviral vector containing the Grp78 gene for overexpression of GRP78 protein. ON female rats injected bilaterally with an adenoviral vector encoding green fluorescence protein (GFP) served as controls. Coinjection was performed in both groups with fluorescent beads (0.2 μL; Red Retrobeads™, LumaFluor Inc.) to independently validate the place of the injections. BW, FI, and VO were monitored daily in all experimental groups from the beginning of the stereotaxic injections (PND20), as well as the occurrence of first estrus, as assessed by vaginal cytology. At PND30, animals were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine and perfused. Intracardiac blood, hypothalamus, and ovary samples were collected, and uterus weight was recorded.

6. Expression of ER stress markers in FACS-isolated Kiss1 neurons from lean and obese mice

RNA expression analyses were conducted in Kiss1 neurons isolated from the ARC and AVPV. Cell isolation was carried out by fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACS) from normal-fed (NN) and early-overfed (ON) female Kiss1Cre/−::tdTomatoloxP/− mice. Sampling was conducted at two age-points; prepubertal (PND25) and peripubertal (PND32). BW and VO were monitored before animals were euthanized. For description of the procedures of FACS-isolation of Kiss1 cells, see SI Appendix.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism software (GraphPad Prism 9.0 for Macintosh, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, www.graphpad.com). Data distribution was examined to ensure the selection of appropriate statistical methods. Normality tests were performed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Student t test for parametric or Mann–Whitney for nonparametric data was used to compare the two groups. The differences between several groups were analyzed by One-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey multiple comparison test for parametric or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test for nonparametric data. Two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was performed to compare the influence of two different independent variables. A minimal group size of n = 7 animals per group was established (unless otherwise indicated for operational reasons), since these sample sizes should provide at least 80% power to detect effect sizes. For physiological experiments, group sizes usually exceeded this threshold. However, based on standard procedures, while phenotypic and hormonal analyses were applied to all available samples, more complex molecular and histological analyses were implemented in a representative subset of randomly assigned samples. Hormonal determinations were performed in duplicate with a minimal total number of 6 to 9 determinations per group, RNA analyses were performed in duplicate from at least five independent samples per group, and WB analysis was conducted in at least 5 to 6 samples per group. As general rule, no data were excluded from the analysis, unless in rare occasions when objective scientific (outlier identification based on Grubbs tests or statistical GraphPad Prism software) or animal welfare reasons demanded exclusions. Outliers are identified in data source files. All data are expressed as mean ± SEM; a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
