Dobutamine-assisted exclusion of left atrial appendage thrombus in atrial fibrillation patients with sludge
Authors: Kentaro Minami, Tomoko Machino-Ohtsuka, Hiro Yamasaki, Bryan J Mathis, Yu Yamada, Naoto Kawamatsu, Kimi Sato, Masayoshi Yamamoto, Tomoko Ishizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDense spontaneous echo contrast (SEC) and sludge in the left atrial appendage (LAA) complicate differentiation between sludge and thrombi during transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE). We prospectively evaluated the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of dobutamine to reduce sludge and improve thrombus detection in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF).Methods and resultsThis was an open-label, single-arm, single-center clinical study. A total of 20 patients with LAA sludge on TOE received intravenous dobutamine infusion, starting at 5 μg/kg/min and increasing to 10 and 20 μg/kg/min at 3-min intervals until sludge resolution. SEC grade and thrombus presence were reassessed at the maximum dose. All patients had LAA sludge at baseline, which improved to SEC grade 2 in 5% of patients, grade 1 in 75%, and completely resolved in 20%. Peak dobutamine doses were 5 μg/kg/min in one patient, 10 μg/kg/min in six patients, and 20 μg/kg/min in 13 patients. Dobutamine significantly increased LAA emptying velocity (median: 13.8 to 18.7 cm/s, P < 0.001), emptying fraction (12.8% to 28.9%, P < 0.001), medial and lateral LAA wall velocities (6.6 to 8.4 cm/s, P = 0.012; 5.8 to 7.3 cm/s, P = 0.007, respectively), and left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (51.8% to 60.0%, P < 0.001). Median heart rate increased from 78 to 89 bpm, while median systolic blood pressure briefly dropped from 116 to 105 mmHg before recovering to 123 mmHg three minutes post-infusion. No severe adverse events or hemodynamic instability occurred.ConclusionBy enhancing LAA and LV function, dobutamine infusion during TOE reduces sludge and facilitates LAA thrombus exclusion in patients with AF.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Dobutamine, Left atrial appendage, Spontaneous echo contrast, Transoesophageal echocardiography

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a growing global health concern primarily driven by worldwide aging, with prevalence increasing by nearly 57% between 2010 and 2019 [1], [2]. Critical treatment options, such as electrical cardioversion, catheter ablation or percutaneous left atrial appendage (LAA) closure, require careful evaluation of the LAA thrombi prior to the procedures to mitigate thromboembolic complications [2]. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) is the gold standard for identifying thrombi in the LAA; however, AF-induced blood stasis can lead to dense spontaneous echo contrast (SEC) or sludge within the left atrium (LA) and LAA [3]. This dense, smoke-like echo may obscure the interior of the LAA, making it difficult to accurately assess the presence of thrombus in the setting of severe blood stasis [3], [4]. Reliability in discriminating between a thrombus and sludge on TOE is a necessity to prevent intervention-related thromboembolism.

Isoproterenol, a β-adrenergic receptor agonist, has been tested for its ability to reduce sludge through increased cardiac contractility and blood velocity, showing high effectiveness and safety [5], [6], [7]. However, its vasodilatory nature often causes hypotension during infusion and it carries a risk of proarrhythmic effects [8]. Dobutamine (DOB), a selective β1-adrenergic agonist derived from isoproterenol, was developed to enhance myocardial contractility while minimizing chronotropic stimulation, arrhythmias, and vascular side effects [9], [10]. Due to its minimal β2- and α1-adrenergic receptor stimulation, dobutamine has limited impact on blood pressure and is associated with a lower risk of arrhythmia [9], [10]. These characteristics have established DOB as a key agent in the management of heart failure and in stress echocardiography for assessing myocardial ischemia, valvular disease severity, and myocardial viability [10], [11], [12].

Considering its similar efficacy to isoproterenol and its more favorable hemodynamic profile, DOB has shown potential in differentiating sludge from LAA thrombi. Furthermore, evidence in similar patient settings is limited to retrospective analyses of a few cases [6], [13] and an isolated case report [14]. To supplement the limited existing evidence, we conducted a preliminary open-label, single-arm, single-center study to explore the potential safety and efficacy of DOB infusion in attenuating sludge, thereby facilitating the exclusion of LAA thrombus in patients with AF.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This single-arm prospective study, conducted between December 2022 and December 2024, was designed to evaluate DOB efficacy and safety in the diagnosis or exclusion of LAA thrombi in AF patients with sludge. Written, informed consent for research was obtained from each patient prior to the commencement of the study. We conducted the present study in accordance with the Clinical Trials Act in Japan (jRCT number: s031220544) because of the off-label DOB usage. The study was performed according to the principles of Declaration of Helsinki after obtaining approval from the University of Tsukuba Clinical Research Review Board (TCRB 22-027).

The main inclusion criteria were as follows: patients with AF undergoing TOE; those receiving appropriate anticoagulation therapy; those with dense SEC and sludge (as defined below) within the LA and LAA; and those in whom exclusion of LAA thrombus was challenging. The main exclusion criteria were: history of LAA occlusion or resection; obvious LAA thrombus without DOB infusion; systolic blood pressure (BP) ≤ 80 mmHg or ≥180 mmHg or heart rate (HR) ≥ 100 bpm before the commencement of TOE; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with significant left ventricle (LV) obstruction; and/or disorientation, quadriplegia, dysarthria, and/or visual field impairment before TOE. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the Supplementary methods.

Finally, 20 patients were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1). All patients received adequate anticoagulation therapy for more than three weeks prior to TOE [2]. Details regarding anticoagulation therapy, AF, and the definition of congestive heart failure used in this study are provided in the Supplementary methods.

Transthoracic echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using commercially available ultrasound systems (General Electric Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI) within 3 months prior to TOE. Current guidelines [15] were followed to obtain images. LA maximum and minimum volumes were measured using the biplane method of disks at mitral valve opening and closure, respectively, and indexed to body surface area. LA emptying fraction was calculated as [(LA maximum volume - LA minimum volume)/LA maximum volume] × 100. Peak early (E) transmitral flow velocity was measured via pulsed wave Doppler in the apical view, while early diastolic velocity (E') was obtained using tissue Doppler imaging at the septal and lateral mitral annulus. The E/E' ratio was averaged. For two-dimensional speckle tracking of the LV and LA, non-foreshortened apical views were analyzed using automated functional imaging software (EchoPAC 204, GE Vingmed Ultrasound) [15], [16]. After endocardial border detection, the software tracked speckle motion and calculated LV global longitudinal systolic strain and LA global longitudinal reservoir strain [16]. Each measurement was averaged using data from five consecutive beats.

Transesophageal echocardiography

TOE was performed using the EPIQ CVx/X8-2t ultrasound system (Philips Medical Systems, Andover, MA, USA) after at least 6 h of fasting, with topical anesthesia and intravenous midazolam sedation. Cine loops were recorded for analysis in a 45° to 60° longitudinal LAA view and emptying blood flow velocity was measured with pulsed Doppler, placing the sample volume 1 cm below the LAA orifice [17]. Peak LAA emptying and filling velocities were measured within each RR interval. LAA wall velocity was assessed using tissue Doppler imaging, with the sample volume placed at the proximal third of the medial and lateral LAA sidewalls [5]. Peak upward and downward LAA wall velocities were recorded within each RR interval. Maximum and minimum LAA areas were measured by tracing the endocardial border from the ridge of the left upper pulmonary vein. The LAA emptying fraction was calculated as (maximum LAA area – minimum LAA area)/maximum LAA area × 100 [5]. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was determined using the biplane method of disks from midesophageal four- and two-chamber views at 0° to 20° and 80° to 100°, respectively [5]. TOE measurements, averaged over five consecutive beats, were performed during AF in all patients.

SEC was graded from 0 to 4, with gain settings carefully adjusted to minimize background noise. The grading was as follows: 0 = none (no echogenicity); 1 = mild (minimal echogenicity in the LAA or sparsely distributed in the LA, detectable only transiently); 2 = mild-moderate (denser swirling than grade 1 but similarly distributed); 3 = moderate (dense swirling in the LAA, constant throughout the cardiac cycle); 4 = severe (intense echodensity with slow swirling in the LAA and LA) [5], [18], [19]. LAA sludge is defined as dynamic, viscous, layered, echo-dense material, without evidence of a discrete mass [3], [19]. The severity of LAA blood stasis was evaluated using a six-point scale incorporating SEC grades 0 to 4, with sludge representing the most severe category. The SEC grade, sludge, and LAA thrombus were assessed by K.M. (Observer 1) during TOE. Subsequently, T.MO (Observer 2), blinded to clinical data, independently evaluated the same parameters using stored TOE images and offline software. Biplane LAA images from 0° to 135° were used to comprehensively assess SEC grade, sludge, and thrombi. LAA thrombi were defined as round, oval, or irregularly shaped, uniformly echo-dense masses within the LAA, distinct from the endocardium, and visible in multiple imaging planes [3.5]. Special care was taken to differentiate thrombi from pectinate muscles.

Dobutamine infusion and embolic risk management

After baseline TOE, dobutamine (Dobutamine Hydrochloride Continuous Intravenous Infusion 50 mg syringe [KKC], Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd., Nagoya, Japan) was infused through the antecubital vein using an infusion pump at gradually increasing doses of 5 μg/kg/min, 10 μg/kg/min, and 20 μg/kg/min at 3 min intervals. To minimize the risk of embolic events during DOB infusion, continuous visualization of the LAA was maintained throughout TOE. The infusion was immediately discontinued if a thrombus was identified, if the heart rate increased by 20% from baseline, after dose escalation to 20 μg/kg/min for 3 min, or when the LAA interior became adequately visible to rule out thrombus. We reassessed SEC grade, the presence of thrombus, and quantitative parameters within 1 min of DOB stoppage. DOB doses and durations were based on previous studies [6], [10], [11]. The observation period was based on a half-life of 2 min and set from the DOB infusion to 20 min after stopping [10], [12]. Continuous electrocardiography was performed to monitor for arrhythmias other than AF. Blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR) were recorded at baseline, every minute during DOB administration and for 5 min after discontinuation, and then every 5 min during the subsequent observation period. After the observation period, patients were assessed for impaired consciousness, quadriplegia, dysarthria, and visual field disturbances. In the event that any neurological abnormalities were identified, prompt evaluation and management were to be conducted in coordination with stroke specialists and cardiologists (as arranged prior to study initiation) to address potential cerebral or systemic embolic events.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on the expected changes in the severity of LAA blood stasis before and after DOB infusion. Severity was assessed using a six-point scale, with SEC graded from 0 to 4 and sludge considered as a distinct category representing the most severe finding beyond grade 4. We assumed post-infusion changes in severity scores of −3, −2, −1, 0, and +1 would occur at rates of 20%, 50%, 20%, and 10%, respectively. Fifteen patients had a power > 90% based on the Wilcoxon signed rank test, with a significance level of α = 0.05. Considering a roughly 20% dropout rate, we set the sample size as 20.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range while categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed to compare the severity of LAA blood stasis and other TOE parameters before and after DOB infusion. Inter-observer agreement for the severity of LAA blood stasis was assessed using the concordance rate and the kappa coefficient. We used a linear mixed-effects model with unstructured covariance for random effects to evaluate repeated measurements of systolic BP and HR. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 28.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Clinical characteristics and echocardiographic findings

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A total of 20 patients (17 male, 3 female) with a mean age of 72 years (range: 67–82 years) were included. All patients exhibited sludge in the LAA at baseline, representing cases in whom exclusion of LAA thrombus was challenging. More than half of the patients had a history of stroke or thromboembolic events despite receiving appropriate anticoagulation therapy. The median CHADS2 and CHA2DS2-VASc scores were 3 [2], [4] and 4 [3], [6], respectively. A history of congestive heart failure was noted in 16 patients (80%), accompanied by elevated BNP levels. Additionally, 3 patients (15%) had non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy while 2 patients (10%) were diagnosed with severe mitral valve stenosis, and none had cardiac amyloidosis. Regarding the parameters of transthoracic echocardiography, reduced global longitudinal strain reflecting LV systolic dysfunction and a high E/E' ratio indicative of diastolic dysfunction were observed, along with severe LA dilatation. Furthermore, reduced LA reservoir strain and LA emptying fraction, suggestive of advanced LA dysfunction, were also noted.

Cardiac function and hemodynamics under dobutamine infusion

Peak DOB doses were 5 μg/kg/min, 10 μg/kg/min, and 20 μg/kg/min for one, six, and 13 patients, respectively. Fig. 2 depicts the effects of DOB on LAA and LV function. DOB significantly increased both the emptying velocity of LAA flow from 13.8 cm/s to 18.7 cm/s (P < 0.001) and the filling velocity from 13.8 cm/s to 22.6 cm/s (P < 0.001), as well as LAA emptying fraction from 12.8% at baseline to 28.9% (P < 0.001). It also enhanced wall motion velocities of the LAA medial side, including upward velocity from 6.6 cm/s to 8.4 cm/s (P = 0.012) and downward velocity from 5.4 cm/s to 7.6 cm/s (P < 0.001). Similarly, the lateral wall velocities increased, with upward velocity improving from 5.8 cm/s to 7.3 cm/s (P = 0.007) and downward velocity from 5.9 cm/s to 7.2 cm/s (P < 0.001). Additionally, DOB boosted LVEF from 51.6% to 60.0% (P < 0.001), highlighting its overall positive impact on LAA and LV function.

Fig. 3 depicts the changes in HR and BP caused by DOB infusion. During infusion, HR increased from a mean of 78 bpm at baseline to 89 bpm at the peak while systolic BP transiently decreased from a mean of 116 mmHg at baseline to 105 mmHg and then increased to 123 mmHg after DOB infusion at 20 μg/kg/min. Although these changes were statistically significant, they did not cause hemodynamic instability as both HR and BP remained elevated for only a few minutes after the cessation of DOB infusion, with rapid normalization over time. These findings highlight the dose-dependent hemodynamic effects of DOB and the transient nature of its short half-life.

Reduction in LAA blood stasis severity with DOB infusion

Two observers independently assessed the severity of LAA blood stasis, finding a nearly perfect agreement (κ = 0.86, p < 0.001). The detailed grading results for each observer are shown in Supplemental Table 1. Fig. 4 shows the distribution of blood stasis severity as evaluated by the first observer. At baseline, all patients (100%) exhibited sludge in the LAA, indicating severe blood stasis. DOB infusion significantly improved blood stasis in all patients, with 100% achieving a SEC grade of ≤2: 75% reached grade 1, 5% grade 2, and 20% showed complete resolution (grade 0). No LAA thrombi were identified in any patient. The median blood stasis severity score significantly decreased from sludge to grade 1 after DOB administration (p < 0.001).

Adverse events and clinical outcomes

No major adverse events related to the study were encountered. During DOB infusion, single ventricular premature contractions (<5 beats/min) were observed in four patients; however, hemodynamics were unaffected. After DOB, no novel neurological abnormalities or visual field disturbances were observed. Furthermore, no life-threatening complications or side effects were encountered, nor was specific treatment needed to counter any such effects. Among the 20 patients in whom LAA thrombus was excluded after DOB infusion, six underwent catheter ablation for AF, five underwent percutaneous LAA closure, two underwent electrical cardioversion, and one underwent percutaneous mitral commissurotomy within 24 h. None of the patients experienced thromboembolic complications related to these procedures. During a mean clinical follow-up of 31 ± 6 months, no patient developed symptomatic cardiogenic cerebral embolism or other clinically apparent thromboembolic events.

Discussion

This is the first prospective study in Japanese AF patients to examine the efficacy of DOB infusion in reducing the sludge seen during TOE and providing adequate visualization into the LAA. In this study, DOB infusion significantly enhanced both LAA and LV function while significantly ablating sludge within the LAA to permit confirmation of the absence of LAA thrombus in all participants. No major side effects requiring specific treatment were observed. Although no LAA thrombi were identified after DOB infusion, the value of this approach lies in improving visualization when conventional TOE cannot reliably distinguish thrombus from sludge, thereby facilitating clinical decision-making before interventions requiring exclusion of LAA thrombus.

Patients with sludge in the present study were older and exhibited significant structural remodeling and functional impairment of the LA and LAA. Additionally, LV longitudinal systolic dysfunction and diastolic dysfunction were also anticipated to adversely affect blood stasis in the LA and LAA. A previous study using simultaneous catheterization and echocardiography demonstrated that DOB reduces LA pressure and stiffness in patients with AF or HF. In that study, DOB administration decreased LV tau, improved LV relaxation, and subsequently reduced LA afterload [20]. Considering that blood stasis in the LAA is associated with elevated LA pressure [21], DOB may alleviate sludge by improving LV relaxation and enhancing LAA blood flow since the early diastolic upward velocity of the LAA wall has been reported to correlate with LV relaxation [5], [22], a conclusion supported by our results. Furthermore, the positive inotropic effects of DOB improved the systolic parameters of both the LAA and LV, which is consistent with previous studies [10], [12], [20]. As the LAA and LV lateral basal wall are anatomically and functionally connected [5], this resulted in increased LAA blood flow velocity and sufficient visualization to accurately screen for LAA thrombi.

Diastasis lies between the early and late diastolic phases when atrial blood flow to the ventricle slows down or progressively halts [23]. As extended diastasis, commonly associated with bradycardia, can lead to blood stasis and the formation of dense SEC [23], the positive chronotropic effects of DOB (increased HR and reduced diastasis duration) may contribute to reducing SEC grade. Support for this putative mechanism can be found in a reported case of sinus bradycardia (40 bpm) due to sick sinus syndrome, where sludge in the LAA resolved following ventricular pacing at 80 bpm [24]. In this case, the LAA blood flow velocity remained unchanged before and after pacing [24], suggesting that the reduction in stasis was likely due to the shortened diastasis rather than enhanced contractility. This suggests that, particularly with bradycardia, increasing HR through pharmacological measures such as DOB or pacing can help reduce blood stasis and potentially resolve sludge.

In this study, DOB infusion at a maximum dose of 20 μg/kg/min successfully reduced sludge in all cases. In contrast, previous reports involving small cohorts treated with DOB at 5–40 μg/kg/min documented cases in which SEC or sludge persisted, preventing exclusion of LAA thrombus [6], [13]. This discrepancy may be explained by the presence of atrial cardiomyopathy—characterized by atrial myocyte loss and interstitial fibrosis—which commonly coexists with dense SEC or sludge in the LAA. Atrial cardiomyopathy frequently progresses with aging, persistent AF, and heart failure [2], [25]. The extent of atrial myocardial damage varies among individuals, and inotrope responsiveness is generally inversely related to the degree of injury [26]. Thus, it is possible that patients in the present study had less advanced atrial cardiomyopathy than those in previous studies. In cases of severe atrial cardiomyopathy, higher doses of DOB than those used in this study may be necessary to resolve sludge.

Additionally, the observed efficacy and safety of DOB in reducing sludge appear consistent with previous reports on isoproterenol, although a direct comparison was not conducted in this study [5], [6], [7]. Worsening blood stasis in LAA is usually accompanied by a variable degree of bradycardia or impairment of atrial contraction (or both) [27] and DOB predominantly enhances myocardial contractility while exerting minimal effects on HR and peripheral vascular tone compared to isoproterenol [9], [10]. Therefore, in cases where prolonged diastasis caused by bradycardia is the main factor contributing to blood stasis, isoproterenol may be more effective. Conversely, when weak myocardial contractility is the primary cause of dense SEC or sludge, DOB may be the preferred option to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Tailoring the choice of medication based on the patient's underlying condition could improve the precision of diagnostic evaluations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a small, single-arm study involving only 20 patients, limiting generalizability and precluding definitive conclusions regarding the safety and efficacy of DOB. The results should be considered preliminary and serve as a foundation for optimizing DOB dosing and methodology. Larger studies are needed to validate these observations and establish individualized diagnostic strategies.

Second, although no new neurological abnormalities were observed after DOB infusion, brain magnetic resonance imaging was not performed; thus, asymptomatic embolic events cannot be completely ruled out. Given the high embolic risk associated with LAA sludge, DOB infusion should be undertaken with careful preparation and multidisciplinary planning, as in the present study.

Finally, although no serious arrhythmias or hypotension occurred in this study, the dose-dependent risks of DOB, including life-threatening arrhythmias, are well documented. Careful patient selection and continuous monitoring to ensure safety in broader clinical use.

Despite these limitations, this pilot study suggests that DOB infusion during TOE may serve as a useful approach for excluding LAA thrombi obscured by sludge, particularly in settings where accurate differentiation is hindered by the unavailability of ultrasound contrast agents. If thrombus can be ruled out, this strategy may enable timely intervention and ultimately help reduce embolic risk. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

DOB infusion at 5–20 μg/kg/min during TOE effectively reduced LAA sludge and facilitated the exclusion of thrombi in patients with AF, without significant adverse events. These findings support the potential utility of this approach in clinical decision-making, although larger studies are warranted to confirm its efficacy, safety, and optimal application.
