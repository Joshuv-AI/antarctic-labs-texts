Efficacy and Safety of Ca2+ Channel α2δ Ligands for the Treatment of Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathic Pain: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis
Authors: Hui Guo, Yi Wang, Hongjun Lv, Bingyin Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiabetic peripheral neuropathic pain (DPNP), a chronic complication of diabetes mellitus that impacts quality of life, is treated with Ca2⁺ channel α2δ ligands such as mirogabalin (MGB), pregabalin (PGB), and gabapentin (GBP), which are recommended as first-line treatments. However, direct comparative evidence among 3 Ca2⁺ channel α2δ ligands efficacy and safety remain limited. This study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of MGB, PGB and GBP for DPNP through a systematic review and network meta-analysis.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science on July 1, 2024 from inception, to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the efficacy and safety of MGB, PGB, and GBP at various doses for DPNP. Non-RCTs, observational studies, reviews, and case reports were excluded. Eligible studies with data on pain relief, sleep interference, and adverse events were selected. The Cochrane risk of bias tool was used for quality assessment, and a Bayesian hierarchical fixed-effects model was applied using Stata and R software. Primary outcomes were the comparative efficacy of MGB, PGB, and GBP, assessed by changes in average daily pain score (ADPS) and pain response rates (≥ 30% and ≥ 50% pain reduction). Secondary outcomes included comparative effects on were patient global impression of change (PGIC), sleep interference score (SIS), adverse event (AE) rates, and withdrawals due to AEs (WDAE). For continuous outcomes, results were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs), and for dichotomous outcomes, as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CrIs within a Bayesian network meta-analysis framework.ResultsIn total, 34 RCTs involving 8630 patients and 13 dosing regimens met the inclusion criteria. The sample size ranged from 40 to 834 and the included RCTs were conducted across multiple countries. MGB 30 mg significantly reduced ADPS compared to PGB 300 mg (Mean Difference (MD) − 0.29; 95% credible interval (CrI) − 0.53 to − 0.05) and MGB 15 mg (MD − 0.32; 95% CrI − 0.64 to − 0.01) and achieved higher pain response rates (≥ 30% and ≥ 50%) than GBP 1800 mg (odds ratio (OR) 2.33; 95% CrI 1.01–5.48 and OR 2.87; 95% CrI 1.14–7.81, respectively). MGB 30 mg and GBP 3600 mg were superior to PGB 300 mg and MGB 15 mg in PGIC scores. MGB 30 mg also demonstrated better SIS improvements over GBP 1800 mg and PGB 300 mg. Fewer AEs occurred with MGB 15 mg, PGB 300 mg, and placebo compared to PGB 600 mg, with MGB 15 mg having the lowest WDAE rates. No significant inconsistency or publication bias was observed.ConclusionsMirogabalin, especially at 30 mg showed superior or comparable pain relief, improved sleep interference, and favorable tolerability for diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain treatment. Ca2⁺ channel α2δ ligands demonstrated consistent efficacy in pain reduction and functional improvement, supporting their role as first-line therapies for DPNP.Trial RegistrationPROSPERO Identifier: CRD42024584773.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40122-025-00789-5.Keywords: Diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain, Ca2+ channel α2δ ligands, Systematic review, Network meta-analysis

Key Summary Points

DPNP severely impairs quality of life, yet head-to-head evidence on first-line Ca2⁺ channel α2δ ligands (mirogabalin, pregabalin, gabapentin) is scarce.We assessed, across 34 RCTs (n = 8630), how different doses of these drugs compare for pain relief, sleep, and safety.Mirogabalin 30 mg provided superior pain reduction, better sleep improvement, and comparable tolerability versus pregabalin 300 mg and gabapentin 1800/3600 mg.Mirogabalin 30 mg may be the preferred first-line choice for DPNP, but real-world studies are needed to confirm these trial-based benefits.

Introduction

Diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain (DPNP) is a common chronic complication of diabetes mellitus [1], with studies reporting prevalence rates from 10 to 70% among patients with diabetes [2–9]. This wide variability is influenced by factors such as age, duration of diabetes, and type 1 or 2 diabetes [9–12]. DPNP significantly impacts patients’ quality of life and imposes substantial economic burdens on individuals, their families, and healthcare systems [13, 14].

To address these challenges, current guidance recommends a multimodal approach in which first-line pharmacologic options for DPNP. First-line pharmacologic options include Ca2+ channel α2δ ligands pregabalin (PGB), gabapentin (GBP), mirogabalin (MGB); and amitriptyline, duloxetine. Second-line or adjunctive options include topical capsaicin and device-based interventions, which are considered in selected cases. Glycemic optimization, foot care, and rehabilitation strategies further support comprehensive management. PGB and GBP are widely recommended as first-line treatments for DPNP in international and Chinese guidelines [15–17]. Although GBP has not been approved by FDA for DPNP, it is widely used in the US owing to its safety profile and minimal drug interactions [18]. PGB, a second-generation α2δ ligand, received approval in Europe in 2004 for treating peripheral neuropathic pain and in the US in 2005 for managing neuropathic pain linked to diabetic peripheral neuropathy and postherpetic neuralgia [19]. In China, both PGB and GBP are administered off-label for treatment of DPNP. These agents are associated with central nervous system adverse events such as dizziness, ataxia, somnolence, and headache [20–22]. In recent years, MGB, a third-generation α2δ ligand that selectively binds to and modulates the α2δ-1 subunits of voltage-gated calcium channels, has been developed and approved in Japan for peripheral neuropathic pain in 2019 and for neuropathic pain in 2022 [23–26]. It was also approved in China for DPNP in June 2024.

Systematic reviews indicate significant pain reductions with PGB, GBP, and MGB compared to placebo and treatments like duloxetine [27–30]. However, these conventional meta-analyses have primarily focused on individual medications, with limited comparisons among different agents or across dosing regimens. By synthesizing the highest-level evidence to date, this network meta-analysis equips physicians and patients with a rigorously validated reference for selecting the most effective Ca2⁺-channel α2δ ligand, thereby optimizing pain control and minimizing tolerability trade-offs in diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain.

Study Objective

This study aims to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety and optimal dosing of MGB, PGB, and GBP in treating DPNP using a network meta-analytical approach. By integrating direct and indirect evidence, it aims to provide healthcare providers with robust data to enhance DPNP management and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2015 extension statement for network meta-analysis (NMA) [31]. The protocol has been registered at PROSPERO (CRD42024584773). This study is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Data Sources and Literature Search

PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were searched from inception to July 1, 2024 with MeSH terms included ‘Diabetic Neuropathies’, ‘Pregabalin’, ‘Gabapentin’, ‘Mirogabalin’, free-text terms and synonyms. No restrictions were placed on publication years, languages, or publication status (Supplementary 1). To avoid omitting important articles, we manually searched ClinicalTrials.gov, International Clinical Trials Registry Portal, CenterWatch (including taiwanclinicaltrials.tw), Global Index Medicus, and reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews on PubMed published in the last 5 years for further eligible trials.

Study Selection

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) designed as randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Non-RCTs, observational studies, reviews, and case reports were excluded. The restriction to RCTs was applied during the search strategy, and this was further confirmed during manual screening of the retrieved records; (2) enrolled adult patients aged 18 years or older diagnosed with DPNP as defined by original studies; (3) examined the efficacy and safety of the Ca2+ channel α2δ ligands GBP, MGB, and PGB, in comparison with each other, placebo, or no treatment. Outcome measures were selected to assess multiple dimensions recommended by Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) for chronic pain trials [32]. The primary outcomes were the change in ADPS from baseline, measured by the numeric rating scale (NRS, where 0 = no pain to 10 = worst possible pain), and the pain response rate, defined as 30% or 50% pain reduction from baseline. Secondary outcomes included: (1) the patient global impression of change (PGIC, a 7-point NRS scale), specifically the percentage of patients with an overall status of “minimally improved” or better (scores ranging from 1 to 3) and “much improved” or better (scores of 1 and 2); (2) the change in sleep interference score (SIS, a 11-point NRS scale where 0 = does not interfere with sleep and 10 = pain completely interferes with sleep or unable to sleep); (3) the proportion of patients experiencing adverse events (AEs); and 4) the percentage of patients who withdrew from the study due to the occurrence of AEs (WDAE). All scales have been validated for reliability and validity [33–35]. There was no restriction on treatment durations or study settings. Two reviewers independently screened articles for eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with assistance from a third reviewer if necessary.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers extracted the following data from each study into a pre-defined data extraction form: general study characteristics (sample size, publication year), study design, participants’ baseline characteristics (age, sex, type of diabetes), treatment design, and outcomes. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with assistance from a third reviewer.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

An NMA was performed using a fixed-effects model with a Bayesian hierarchical approach, employing the network package of Stata 15.1 and R Studio 4.0 software. The network relationships between intervention measures were visualized through network plots.

For dichotomous variables, such as pain response rate, effect sizes for the results were based on the odds ratio (OR) and 95% credible interval (CrI). For continuous variables, such as change in ADPS from baseline, effect sizes for the results were based on the mean difference (MD) and 95% CrI. League tables, namely square matrices consisting of all pairwise comparisons within a meta-analysis, were created to enable readers to directly compare the direction and magnitude of the treatment effect. Ranking probabilities were calculated by the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), with a higher SUCRA ranking indicating a better intervention effect and a greater likelihood of the intervention being the optimal choice [36]. In the included studies reported the baseline and endpoint values, the change value could be calculated. For the critical missing data, we would contact the corresponding authors via email to obtain the information.

The presence of inconsistency was assessed by the node-splitting method to detect disagreement between direct and indirect comparisons of the interventions [37]. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with I2 > 50% predefined as substantial. When significant heterogeneity was present, we explored its possible sources by examining patient characteristics, treatment modalities, and outcome definitions among the included studies. Sensitivity analyses were conducted whenever clinical or methodological heterogeneity was identified [38]. Forest plots were generated to visualize the magnitude and uncertainty of treatment effects (presenting mean differences for continuous outcomes and odds ratios for dichotomous outcomes, with 95% credible intervals). Funnel plots were generated to evaluate publication bias for primary outcomes when the analysis included 10 or more studies.

Quality Assessment

The quality of the included RCTs was assessed by two independent reviewers using the Cochrane risk of bias tool (RoB version 1.0) [39]. This tool evaluates potential biases across several key domains, specifically selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other biases. Each study was rated as having a low, unclear, or high risk of bias in each domain. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, with assistance from a third reviewer if necessary.

Results

Literature Search and Characteristics of Included Studies

The initial search retrieved a total of 1992 articles. After removing duplicates, 1177 unique articles from databases and 194 manually searched gray literature sources remained. Following the screening of titles and abstracts, 148 articles were deemed eligible and retrieved for full-text review. After further full-text screening, a total of 34 RCTs [15, 40–72] met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

Flowchart illustrating the selection of studies

A total of 8630 patients, 57.61% of them men, were included in the 34 RCTs. The sample size varied from 40 to 834. According to diagnosis, 481 patients had type 1 diabetes and 5235 had type 2 diabetes. The average age of patients was over 50 years. Of the 34 RCTs, 26 compared two interventions and eight compared multiple interventions. A total of 13 different dosing regimens were identified among the three drugs of interest: gabapentin at daily doses of 900, 1800, 3000, and 3600 mg (GBP 900, GBP 1800, GBP 3000, and GBP 3600), pregabalin at daily doses of 75, 150, 300, 600, and 675 mg (PGB 75, PGB 150, PGB 300, PGB 600, and PGB 675), mirogabalin at daily doses of 15, 20, and 30 mg (MGB 15, MGB 20, and MGB 30), and placebo (PBO) (Table 1).

Quality of Included Studies

Regarding the quality of studies assessed by RoB 1.0, 11 studies (32.35%) were rated as having concerns of risk of bias, mainly due to performance, attrition, and reporting bias; 23 studies (67.65%) were rated as having an unclear risk, mainly due to selection and detection bias (Fig. S1).

Change in Average Daily Pain Score (ADPS)

The NMA of 17 RCTs [15, 40, 42, 47, 48, 50–54, 56, 58, 60, 61, 63, 69, 73] with 5852 patients compared the efficacy of ten different interventions in reducing ADPS, with 19 direct and 26 indirect comparisons (Fig. 2A) (I2 = 23%). MGB 30 significantly reduced ADPS compared to PGB 300 (MD, − 0.29; 95% CrI − 0.53 to − 0.05) and MGB 15 (MD, − 0.32; 95% CrI − 0.64 to − 0.01, Table 2). GBP 3000, GBP 3600 (Fig. 3B) (I2 = 26%), and PGB 600 showed significant reductions than PGB 300 (MD, − 0.79 to − 0.46). PGB 600 reduced ADPS more effectively than all MGB doses (MD, − 0.75 to − 0.43), PGB 75 and PGB 300 (MD, − 0.92 to − 0.72). All interventions except MGB 15, PGB 150, and PGB 75 significantly reduced ADPS compared to PBO (Fig. 3C). Node-splitting identified the direct comparison of PGB 600 versus PGB 300 as the main source of inconsistency (p = 0.0047). After excluding this single trial from the network, the inconsistency was markedly reduced and no longer statistically significant. SUCRA rankings are presented in Fig. S2.

30% and 50% Pain Reduction

Fourteen RCTs [15, 40, 42, 44, 51–54, 58–61, 63, 73] with 4772 patients compared 8 interventions for achieving 30% pain reduction, with 15 direct and 13 indirect comparisons (Fig. 2B). MGB 30 had a significantly higher likelihood of achieving 30% pain reduction than GBP 1800 (OR, 2.33; 95% CrI 1.01 to 5.48). MGB 15, MGB 20, GBP 3600, and PGB 600 also exhibited a higher likelihood of achieving 30% pain reduction (OR, 2.52–4.21) than GBP 1800 (Fig. 4A). MGB 15 was more likely to achieve 30% pain reduction than PGB 300 (OR, 2.20), and PGB 600 was more likely than MGB 30 (OR, 1.78, Fig. 4B) and PGB 300 (OR, 2.16, Fig. 4C). With the exception of GBP 1800, all interventions significant improvements in achieving 30% pain reduction compared with PBO (Fig. 4D, Table 3). Figure S3 shows the SUCRA ranking result.

Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC)

The NMA of 14 RCTs [15, 42, 43, 45, 48, 52–54, 59, 61, 63, 67, 69, 73] with 4764 patients compared 9 interventions for achieving a PGIC score of 1–2 (Fig. S5) (I2 = 18%). MGB 30 had a higher likelihood of achieving a score of 1–2 on PGIC than PGB 300 (OR, 1.47; 95% CrI 1.10–1.97), MGB 15 (OR, 1.62; 95% CrI 1.13–2.32), and MGB 20 (OR, 1.39; 95% CrI 1.02–1.90). GBP 3000 exhibited a higher likelihood of achieving a score of 1–2 on PGIC than both PGB 300 and MGB 15 (OR, 2.21–2.43). GBP 3600 (OR, 1.65 to 1.92) and PGB 600 (OR, 1.89–2.20) demonstrated a higher likelihood than MGB 15, MGB 20, and PGB 300. All interventions, except MGB 15, showed statistically significant improvements over PBO (Table S1). The ranking of interventions based on SUCRA analysis is presented in Fig. S6 (I2 = 0%).

For the percentage of patients scored 1–3 on PGIC, 12 RCTs [15, 42, 43, 49, 52–54, 59, 60, 63, 69, 73] with 4336 patients compared eight interventions (Fig. S7). MGB 30, PGB 300, and PGB 600 demonstrated a statistically significant likelihood of achieving a score of 1–3 on PGIC compared to PBO, with no significant difference between other interventions (Table S2). Figure S8 shows the SUCRA ranking result.

Change in Sleep Interference Score (SIS)

Thirteen RCTs [15, 40, 42, 47, 48, 51, 52, 58, 60, 65, 69, 71, 73] with 4503 patients provided data on changes in SIS (Fig. S9) (I2 = 12%). MGB 30 significantly reduced SIS compared with GBP 1800 (MD, − 1.99; 95% CrI − 2.57 to − 1.41), PGB 300 (MD, − 0.43; 95% CrI − 0.68 to − 0.18), MGB 15 (MD, − 0.32; 95% CrI − 0.64 to − 0.01), and MGB 20 (MD, − 0.38; 95% CrI − 0.65 to − 0.11, Table S3). GBP 3000 was also superior to PGB 300 (MD, − 0.77). Notably, GBP 1800 was found to be less effective than all other interventions, including PBO, in terms of SIS reduction. Figure S10 shows the SUCRA ranking result.

Adverse Events (AEs)

In a comparison of AEs from 20 RCTs [15, 40, 42, 44, 47–49, 51–54, 56, 58–60, 64, 66, 69, 72, 73] (6280 patients, Fig. S11) (I2 = 29%), MGB 30 (OR, 0.22; 95% CrI 0.04–0.91), MGB 15 (OR, 0.17), MGB 20 (OR, 0.17), PGB 300 (OR, 0.17), and GBP 1800 (OR, 0.14) had fewer reported AEs than GBP 900. MGB 15 (OR, 0.57), MGB 20 (OR, 0.60), and PGB 300 (OR, 0.59) had a significantly lower incidence of AEs than PGB 600. PBO had fewer AEs than all interventions except MGB 15, GBP 1800, and GBP 3000 (Table S4). The SUCRA ranking result is shown in Fig. S12.

Withdrawal Due to Adverse Events (WDAEs)

The incidence of WDAEs was analyzed across 24 RCTs [15, 42–45, 47–50, 52–54, 56, 58, 60, 61, 63, 66–69, 72–74] (7318 patients, Fig. S13) (I2 = 22%). The NMA results indicated a lower incidence of WDAEs for MGB 15 than for MGB 30 (OR, 0.32; 95% CrI 0.09–0.9), PGB 300 (OR, 0.33), and PGB 600 (OR, 0.18). In addition, GBP 3600 (OR, 0.5), PGB 75 (OR, 0.22), PGB 150 (OR, 0.29), and PGB 300 (OR, 0.54) showed lower WDAE rates than PGB 600. PBO showed a lower incidence of WDAEs than PGB 300 and PGB 600 (Table S5). The SUCRA ranking is shown in Fig. S14.

Inconsistency and Publication Bias

Results of inconsistency tests and publication bias are shown in Figs. S15–S30 and indicate no notable inconsistency across the networks or significant publication bias.

Discussion

Summary of Key Findings

This study involved a comprehensive systematic review and Bayesian NMA of 34 RCTs to evaluate the efficacy and safety of three key Ca2+ channel α2δ ligands (GBP, PGB, and MGB) in treating DPNP in adult patients. The results demonstrated that all three agents show clinically meaningful efficacy in reducing pain and improving patient outcomes, with MGB emerging as a viable and effective alternative to the more established agents, particularly GBP and PGB.

Interpretation of Results and Comparisons with Previous Research

Persistent pain is a key symptom in patients with DPNP. According to our analysis, MGB 30 significantly outperforms PGB 300 in reducing ADPS (MD, − 0.29; 95% CrI − 0.53 to − 0.05), indicating effective pain relief at its recommended maximum dose. MGB 30 also demonstrates superior outcomes compared to GBP 1800 in both 30% and 50% pain reduction (OR, 2.33 and 2.87, respectively). This suggests that MGB may have similar performance to PGB and GBP in terms of pain relief, and at the clinical maximum dose, MGB 30 may demonstrate greater efficacy in reducing pain. Its unique pharmacokinetic properties, including higher α2δ-1 subunit selectivity and a longer half-life, allow for prolonged analgesic effects, thus reducing the dosing frequency and positively impacting patient adherence [75, 76]. Although our results revealed that higher doses of PGB (600 mg) and GBP (3000 or 3600 mg) showed better effects on ADPS than MGB 30, high doses of these agents are often associated with a higher risk of adverse effects, limiting their clinical applicability [77–79].

High doses of PGB and GBP are often linked to side effects such as dizziness and somnolence [80], while MGB, due to its higher α2δ-1 subunit selectivity, may cause fewer central nervous system side effects [75]. Clinically, PGB is often dosed at 150 mg/day [81], which may be suboptimal for some patients but is used to balance effectiveness and adverse effects [82]. According to our analysis, MGB 15 has a lower incidence of AEs than most other interventions, including PGB 600 and GBP 900 (OR, 0.16 to 0.57). While MGB 30 has a higher AE rate than MGB 15, it remains lower than that PGB 600 (OR, 0.59). MGB 30 also shows a relatively low rate of WDAEs, indicating good tolerance for long-term use. Thus, MGB may be a suitable alternative for patients experiencing AEs with high doses of PGB or GBP.

MGB 30 mg was significantly more likely than PGB 300 mg to achieve a PGIC score of 1–2 (“much” or “very much improved”; OR 1.47, 95% CrI 1.10–1.97). The finding suggests that MGB 30 mg can enhance treatment satisfaction and may support better long-term adherence in patients with DPNP. Additionally, patients with DPNP often have sleep issues, with pain disrupting sleep and vice versa, impacting daily functioning and overall quality of life [83, 84]. Our results indicate that MGB 30 significantly reduces SIS compared with GBP 1800 and PGB 300, suggesting it has potential clinical advantages in improving sleep quality. In addition, a recent economic analysis using a Markov model evaluation in Taiwan suggests that MGB 30 is a cost-effectiveness treatment option for DPNP [85].

Mechanistic Interpretation of Outcome Differences

The observed differences in efficacy and tolerability across MGB, PGB, and GBP can be explained by their distinct pharmacological profiles. MGB demonstrated superior reductions in ADPS and higher pain response rates compared with PGB 300 mg and GBP 1800 mg, which is consistent with its high affinity and slow dissociation from the α2δ-1 subunit, the primary target mediating analgesia in neuropathic pain [24, 76]. Improvements in PGIC and SIS with MGB 30 mg further reflect its ability to sustain inhibition of abnormal neuronal excitability, particularly during the night, thereby enhancing both pain control and sleep quality. In contrast, GBP’s weaker binding affinity and non-linear absorption may account for its comparatively limited efficacy at lower doses, while high-dose GBP (3600 mg) achieved better PGIC at the expense of increased adverse events [18]. Although all three agents act as Ca2⁺ channel α2δ ligands, MGB shows slower dissociation from α2δ-1 relative to α2δ-2, whereas PGB does not exhibit this selectivity, which may contribute to its lower central nervous system adverse effects [86]. Tolerability outcomes also align with the underlying mechanisms: MGB’s weaker binding to the α2δ-2 subunit minimizes central nervous system adverse effects, explaining the lower AE and WDAE rates observed at 15 mg, whereas PGB and GBP showed higher rates of dizziness and somnolence at escalated doses. These mechanistic distinctions provide a biologically plausible explanation for the differential clinical outcomes observed in this review.

Clinical Significance

Our network meta-analysis provides a comprehensive comparison of MGB, PGB, and GBP across multiple doses for DPNP, highlighting differences in efficacy, sleep interference, and tolerability. Most guidelines recommend α2δ ligands (PGB, GBP, and now MGB in some regions) as first-line therapy, which aligns with our evidence supporting the efficacy of these agents. Our findings further offer clinicians practical guidance for treatment selection: for instance, MGB 30 mg showed superior pain relief and improved sleep interference compared to commonly used doses of PGB and GBP, supporting its consideration as a preferred option for patients who can tolerate this dose. Cost considerations and local availability should also inform decision-making, as higher doses of GBP (e.g., 3600 mg) may be less feasible due to tolerability concerns. For researchers, our study identifies key knowledge gaps, including the need for head-to-head trials and real-world studies, and provides a methodological framework for future comparative effectiveness research. All included evidence came from RCTs, which may not fully reflect routine clinical practice; factors such as patient comorbidities, adherence, and long-term outcomes were not captured. Prospective observational studies or pragmatic trials are warranted to validate these findings in everyday care. For patients, the findings clarify treatment options that may balance pain relief with tolerability, ultimately contributing to improved quality of life.

Strengths and Limitations

This analysis benefits from its thorough methods, use of high-quality RCTs, and comparison of different gabapentinoid dosages for DPNP treatment. It fills evidence gaps and provides insights into pain reduction, quality of life, and side effects. However, several limitations must be noted and critically considered. First, heterogeneity in outcome definitions, measurement timing across included studies, and variations in cutoff times —such as variations in ADPS calculation, responder rate definitions, and follow-up intervals—may limit direct comparability and generalizability. Such variation can introduce differential misclassification, inflate between-study heterogeneity, and challenge the transitivity assumption underlying indirect comparisons, potentially contributing to global or local inconsistency in the network. To address this, we prespecified methods to explore sources of heterogeneity when substantial variability was observed. In practice, however, no significant global or local inconsistency was detected, and overall heterogeneity was low (I2 < 50% for key outcomes), suggesting that these differences did not meaningfully affect the robustness of the network estimates and reducing the necessity for formal meta-regression analysis. Second, our exclusive reliance on RCTs, while enhancing internal validity, inherently limits the inclusion of real-world data and long-term follow-up, restricting the ability to assess the sustainability of pain relief, safety, and generalizability to routine clinical practice. This focus may introduce selection bias, as RCT populations often differ from real-world patients in terms of comorbidities, adherence, and baseline characteristics. Although we attempted to mitigate this by including a broad range of multicenter trials and carefully evaluating study populations for representativeness, prospective observational studies are still needed to confirm the applicability of our findings in real-world settings. Third, key effect modifiers such as sex distribution and diabetes duration, as well as other potential confounders like baseline pain severity or comorbidities, were not analyzed due to incomplete or inconsistently reported data across studies. This may introduce residual confounding, potentially affecting effect estimates. Although meta-regression could in principle explore these factors, it was not conducted because the small number of trials per covariate would risk overfitting and unreliable results. Additionally, some studies lacked sufficient detail on randomization procedures, which may reduce the reliability of the evidence. Future studies with richer, harmonized datasets can test whether dose, region, or baseline pain modifies treatment effects.

Conclusions

Our network meta-analysis indicated that all three Ca2⁺ channel α2δ ligands including mirogabalin, pregabalin, and gabapentin are effective in reducing pain and improving patient-reported outcomes in diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain, with variation in efficacy, sleep improvement, and tolerability across agents and doses. Mirogabalin 30 mg provides comparable or superior pain relief, improved sleep quality, and better tolerability than pregabalin 300 mg and gabapentin 1800 mg for diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain. Pregabalin demonstrated dose-dependent efficacy, with higher doses (e.g., 600 mg) providing substantial pain relief and patient global impression of change improvements, though associated with increased adverse events. Gabapentin showed moderate efficacy at standard doses, with higher doses (e.g., 3000–3600 mg) improving pain and patient global impression of change outcomes but often at the expense of tolerability. Owing to its selective binding to the α2δ-1 subunit and favorable safety profile, mirogabalin may represent a more balanced, suitable therapeutic option. Future studies should assess its long-term real-world effectiveness, safety, and applicability across diverse patient populations, as well as its potential role in combination treatment strategies.
