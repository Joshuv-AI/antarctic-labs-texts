Introduction

The sacral spinal gangliogliomas poses a neuroradiological challenge in surgery. These comparatively uncommon WHO grade I tumors, which form out of neuronal and glial components, are often misunderstood and show symptoms like lower back pain, sciatica, or bladder dysfunctions due to the localization adjacent to critical nerve roots [1,2]. Typically, the diagnosis is done examining a MRI image and ensuring the diagnosis is confirmed by a biopsy. Today the surgical resection is the main treatment application with the purpose of maximal tumor removal with lowest possible risk to neurological function. Nonetheless, total resection could prove to be challenging resulting from the intricacy of sacral spine anatomy not to mention the closeness to essential structures [[3], [4], [5]]. Alongside adjuvant therapies like radiation, surgical or medical treatment can also be explored for any residual or recurrent tumors. The treatment results vary in the long run with resection and tissue sample type, and patient's age. Although gangliogliomas often grow more slowly and their survival prognosis is considered better in comparison with other spinal tumors, it is critical to follow them up regularly due to the risk of recurrence. Interdisciplinary approach including participation of neurosurgeons, oncologists, and rehabilitation specialists is essential for achieving both the best medical results as well as ensuring the best possible life quality of the patients [4]. Concentration of sacral spine ganglioglioma cases in general population is rare, therefore, exact figures about the epidemiology rate are difficult to report. On the other hand, they only constitute a minimal portion of all spinal tumors; the percentage is reported to be less than 1 % of all the cases. Therefore, the proportion of gangliogliomas in sacral spine is less than of the others, even though it is a small fraction of all spinal tumors. This is just the one why personalized approach to therapy and the further studies being done to describe these tumors better will be so important to patients outcomes. In this report, we present the case of a female patient who presented for left sided sciatica and was ultimately diagnosed with a left s1 ganglioglioma.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old female patient came to the outpatient clinic for left lower limb pain with weakness. The history of her complaint goes back to several years of insidiously increasing lower back pain without radiation to the lower limb. Few weeks prior to presentation, the patient reported new onset left lower limb pain that starts at the anterior thigh and continues into the calf with difficulty to ambulate. No drop foot was noted. The patient reported normal micturition and defecation. The patient scored 51 % on the Oswestry disability index and 6 on the visual analogue scale (VAS) [5,6].

A lumbosacral magnetic resonance imaging revealed an intradural foraminal and extraforaminal lesion at the left S1 root. The patient was scheduled for surgery. Under general anesthesia, in prone position and through a 10 cm linear incision over the midline of the low lumbar spine till the s2 vertebra. Unilateral laminectomy and foraminotomy of the S1 vertebra and root respectively. Under microscopic guidance and with continuous neurostimulation of the sacral plexus nerve roots, durotomy was done and the lesion was dissected in a piecemeal fashion of the S1 nerve root. Near total resection as done. The tumor was whitish-greyish in color and adherent to the nerve root compressing the later against the roof of the foramen. Postoperatively, the patient was pain free, and ambulatory and was discharged 48 h of the procedure. The pathology study revealed that the lesion presented a ganglioglioma (Fig. 3A, Fig. 3B, Fig. 3C, Fig. 3D, Fig. 3E). The sections of the tumor show a proliferation of cells arranged in small short fascicles of varied density and having schwannian appearance. The cells have angular and spindle nucleus and show some nuclear dystrophies, without significant atypia or mitotic activity. These cells are admixed with mature ganglion cells. The immunostaining reveals that the tumor cells are positive for synaptophysin and PS100. There are negative for smooth muscle actin. The proliferation index MIB-1 is moderate. Discussion

The presentation of the patient was suggestive of a disc herniation with unilateral nerve compression. MRI revealed a lesion suggestive of a S1 root schwannoma. Differentiation of spinal schwannoma from other lesions such as meningiomas are evident in the literature [1]. MRI feature, clinical assessment and epidemiology aid in the categorization of these tumors (Table 1) [[7], [8], [9]]. However, studies have not established basis for comparison of schwannomas against gangliogliomas. Usually, gangliogliomas of the spine typically involve multiple segments (up to 8 vertebrae) and can show cystic changes in 46 % [2,3]. During surgical excision, the tumor was intradural and intramedullary. The adherence of the tumor to the neural tissue was less suggestive of a benign schwannoma. Gross total resection (GTR) was not feasible due to adherence and invasiveness into the neural tissue even while using spinal neuromonitoring. The literature reports 27 % recurrence rate even with GTR [2]. However, neither radiotherapy (Rx) nor chemotherapy (Cx) have been indicated for the treatment of low grade gangliogliomas. In fact, except for anaplastic grade III gangliogliomas for which Rx is an adjuvant treatment, the latter can cause malignant transformation of low-grade lesions [4,11].

Conclusion

Sacral spinal gangliogliomas are rare and challenging tumors to treat due to their proximity to critical nerve structures. Successful management requires precise surgical intervention and a multidisciplinary approach to optimize patient outcomes. This case illustrates the importance of accurate diagnosis and careful surgical planning in achieving significant postoperative improvement while minimizing neurological risks. Continued research and case documentation are essential for advancing treatment strategies and improving long-term prognoses for patients with these uncommon tumors.
