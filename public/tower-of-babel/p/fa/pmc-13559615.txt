PSMB8: an immune-related prognostic marker for low-grade gliomas
Authors: Xinyu Yang, Zhuofan Li, Luyang Xu, Waner Wei, Zewei Tu, Xiaoyan Long, Jia Liu, Jingying Li, Xingen Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioma is the most common primary intracranial tumor in adults. As a subunit of immune proteasome, proteasome subunit beta type-8 (PSMB8) may regulate the progression of glioma via participating in degradation and presentation of tumor antigenic peptides, but its prognostic and clinical applicant usage is under investigation. Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the prognostic significance of PSMB8 in low-grade glioma (LGG) and to elucidate its association with the tumor immune microenvironment and potential as a predictor for immunotherapy response.MethodsTranscriptome data were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA), Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repositories. The correlations between PSMB8 expression and the clinicopathological features of LGG were investigated in our study, and the prognostic role of PSMB8 in LGGs was assessed fully and comprehensively. Furthermore, we evaluated the correlation between PSMB8 expression and LGG immune environment via the experiments and bioinformatic analysis.ResultsOur results indicated that, PSMB8 were highly expressed in most tumor tissues, including LGG. Lower expression of PSMB8 was significantly correlated with lower World Health Organization (WHO) grade and isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status. Moreover, PSMB8 showed a promising prognostic ability for LGG patients via nomogram model and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Association analysis showed that PSMB8 expression was associated with immune cell infiltration in a variety of tumors, including LGG. Our experiments validated the positive correlation between PSMB8 expression and M2-macrophage infiltration level in clinical LGG tissues and invasive ability of LGG cell.ConclusionsPSMB8 could be used as one of the prognostic indicators of LGG and it could regulate the LGG cell migratory and invasive ability. Besides, PSMB8 is expected to be a promising biomarker of cancer immunotherapy.Keywords: Low-grade glioma (LGG), proteasome subunit beta type-8 (PSMB8), immune-microenvironment, immunotherapy

Highlight box

Key findings

• Proteasome subunit beta type-8 (PSMB8) is overexpressed in low-grade glioma (LGG) and predicts poor overall survival.

• High PSMB8 correlates with higher World Health Organization grade, isocitrate dehydrogenase wild-type, and M2 macrophage infiltration.

• PSMB8 knockdown suppresses LGG cell migration, invasion, and proliferation.

• Recombinant PSMB8 promotes M2 macrophage polarization.

What is known and what is new?

• PSMB8 participates in antigen presentation and glioma progression.

• PSMB8 is a robust prognostic biomarker for LGG and actively drives M2 polarization, suggesting tumor-immune crosstalk.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PSMB8 may serve as a prognostic indicator and therapeutic target. Its link to immune checkpoints hints at guiding immunotherapy, but clinical validation is needed.

Introduction

Glioma is intracranial tumor that originates from glial cells and is also the most common primary tumor in the human brain (1). The classification of glioma is often determined by staining pathological sections during clinical diagnosis and treatment. According to the latest [2021] World Health Organization (WHO) classification standard of the central nervous system (2), adult-type diffuse gliomas include astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, and glioblastoma (GBM), and they are divided into four grades (WHO grade I, II, III and IV), in which grades II and III are generally defined as low-grade glioma (LGG) and grade IV is GBM according to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) definition. The current conventional treatments for gliomas include surgical resection combined with radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy (e.g., temozolomide) (3), but the therapeutic effect is far from satisfactory. Scientists are developing novel glioma therapies that target the tumor immune microenvironment (4,5), which showed robust anti-tumor effects in laboratory investigations. Notwithstanding these findings, it is necessary to actively search for new glioma targeting molecules, which contribute to tumor development and the poor prognoses of patients.

The ubiquitin-proteasome system dominates the degradation of proteins in cells and can regulate specific proteins and remove misfolded protein structures. Proteasome degradation pathways are involved in many cellular biological processes, such as the cell cycle, growth, apoptosis, and oxidative stress reactions (6). As an important part of the immunoproteasome, proteasome subunit beta type-8 (PSMB8) was found to participate in antigen-dependent immune editing of cancer cells (7). Researchers discovered that the overexpression of PSMB8 contributed to the higher survival rate and greater response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in patients with melanoma, and it positively correlated with the infiltration of CD8+ T cells in patients with bladder cancer (8,9). However, PSMB8 was found to induce angiogenesis in GBM multiforme through increasing expression of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and its receptors, which subsequently promoted tumor growth (10). Meanwhile, the overexpression of PSMB8 promoted glioma proliferation and migration via activating extracellular signal regulated kinase (ERK)1/2 and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT signaling pathways (11). These results indicate that PSMB8 has different effects in different tumor types. Thus, how the expression of PSMB8 influences the prognosis of patients with LGG remains a problem to be explored.

In this study, we used the resource of public glioma dataset to analyze the expression of PSMB8 in patients with LGG, and measured its expression in cells and tissues derived from our clinical samples. In this study, we aimed to investigate the expression, prognostic value, and potential immunobiological role of PSMB8 in LGG. Subsequently, based on the results of enrichment analysis, we determined that PSMB8 plays a crucial role of in migration and invasiveness of LGG cells in vitro. Furthermore, pan-cancer analysis also concluded that PSMB8 is highly associated with the tumor microenvironment (TME) of tumors, and it can effectively predict the immunotherapy response of cancer patients. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and TRIPOD reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0607/rc).

Methods

Data and sample sources

Relative mRNA expression profiles and patient clinical information of pan-cancer cohort and six LGG cohorts, including TCGA-LGG, CGGA-seq1, CGGA-seq2, GSE16011, GSE61374 and Rembrandt cohorts, were obtained from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn/) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds) (12-14). The mRNA expression data of PSMB8 in normal human tissues from genotype-tissue expression (GTEx) datasets were obtained from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC, https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) Xena database (15,16). For LGG-specific isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) wildtype analysis, only WHO grade II/III IDH-wildtype gliomas were included. WHO grade IV tumors (including IDH-wildtype GBM and H3 G34-mutant diffuse hemispheric glioma) were excluded from LGG-specific analyses but were included in the pan-cancer and GBM-specific analyses (e.g., TCGA-GBM dataset). The clinical information of GEO cohorts was downloaded from the previous publications (17-19). The WHO grades II and III glioma tissue samples were collected from the inpatients, who received surgical therapy in the Department of Neurosurgery of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (No. D-MREC-2022-01). Informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Data processing

Original data derived from TCGA and CGGA datasets were standardized and translated to the format of Transcripts Per kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (TPM). The data downloaded from the GSE16011, GSE61374, and Rembrandt datasets were normalized via the robust multiarray averaging (RMA) method, which was performed by the R packages “affy (version 1.70.0)” and “simpleaffy (version 2.66.0)” (20,21). Batch effects existing between CGGA cohorts or among GEO microarray cohorts were adjusted by the “ComBat” function of “sva” (22) R package (version 3.40.0). We finally obtain three LGG cohorts including TCGA-LGG cohort (n=474), CGGA-LGG cohort (n=413), GEO-LGG cohort (n=379).

Differential expression analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

R package “limma (version 3.48.3)” was used to perform the differentially expression analysis between different LGG subgroups (23). R package “clusterProfiler (version 4.0.5)” was used to perform the GSEA, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis, and the enrichment analysis results were visualized by the same package. The 50 hallmark gene sets of the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) represent distinct characteristic biological effects were prepared to performed GSEA (24). We further used the hallmark gene sets as templates to analyze the correlation between signaling pathways and PSMB8 expression levels in the TCGA pan-cancer cohort. P<0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) <25% represented that the enrichment results were significant and reliable, respectively.

Prognostic analysis

Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression models were used to show the prognostic role of PSMB8 in cancer cohorts by R package “survminer (version 0.4.9)” and “survival (version 3.2-11)”. The optimal cut-off value for PSMB8 expression was determined using the “surv-cutpoint” function of the “survminer (version 0.4.9)” package in R, applied to each cohort separately. This function identifies the cut-off that most significantly stratifies patients into different prognostic groups based on survival analysis. Subsequently, the LGGs were categorized into subgroups of high and low PSMB8 expression, based on this most significant cut-off value. This approach ensures a data-driven and statistically robust method for defining PSMB8 expression thresholds in the context of LGG prognosis.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI)

PPI network related with PSMB8 was established using the data downloaded from the Compartmentalized Protein-Protein Interaction Database (comPPI) (25), and visualized by “ggplot2 (version 3.3.5)” R package.

Clinicopathological correlation analysis of PSMB8 in LGGs

The correlations between PSMB8 expression and clinical characteristics in LGG patients, including O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) methylation status, 1p/19q codeletion status, and IDH mutation status in LGGs, were visualized via R package “ggpubr (version 0.4.0)”. The heatmaps were produced by R package “pheatmap (version 1.0.12)”.

Establishment of PSMB8-related prognostic model in LGG

The nomogram model was established via R packages “rms (version 6.2-0)” and “survival (version 3.2-11)” using the TCGA-LGG cohort, which was used to predict the OS of patients with LGG based on PSMB8 expression. The calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) model were also produced by R package “rms (version 6.2-0)” to evaluate the prognostic predictive ability and benefit of PSMB8 expression levels in patients with LGG. In addition, the time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was established via R package “timeROC (version 0.4)” to assess the prognosis-predictive value of PSMB8 in patients with LGG.

Correlation analysis between PSMB8 expression and immune microenvironment of LGG tissues

Three algorithms, including “CIBERSORT” and “ESTIMATE” were used to assess the immune cell infiltration levels of LGG patients, statistical correlations between PSMB8 expression and infiltration of immune cells in LGG tissues were analyzed by Spearman correlation analysis. The first algorithm was performed by the webtool CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). The latter algorithm was performed by R packages “ESTIMATE”. The different level of immune-related factors, including infiltration levels of immune cells, infiltrating components, and potential immune checkpoints between PSMB8 expression, were analyzed via R package “limma (version 3.48.3)” The data analyzed were all downloaded from the TCGA-LGG cohort. All results were visualized via R packages “ggplot2 (version 3.3.5)” and “pheatmap (version 1.0.12)”.

The predictive value of PSMB8 in cancer immunotherapy

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between the expression of PSMB8 and the classic immune checkpoint molecules. Models of tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) were established to evaluate the associations with PSMB8 expression in pan-cancer. The IMvigor210 cohort (urological cancer, treated with atezolizumab) and GSE91061 cohort (melanoma, treated with nivolumab) were used to assess the prognostic value and immune-checkpoint inhibitor response of PSMB8 on anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)/programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) immunotherapy.

Cell culture

SW1088 and SW1783 LGG cell lines were purchased from American Type Culture Collection. Both cell lines were cultured in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin) at normal air at 37 ℃.

The human monocyte cell line THP-1 were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and cultured in 1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), respectively. For M0 macrophage polarization, 5×107/mL THP-1 cells were incubated with 50 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, HY-18739; MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA, USA) for 24 hours. For M1 macrophages, PMA-stimulated THP-1 was stimulated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; HY-D1056, MCE, USA) and 20 ng/mL recombinant human interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; 300-02, PeproTech, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cranbury, NJ, USA), while recombinant human interleukin (IL)-4 (200-04, MCE, USA) and recombinant human IL-13 (PeproTech, 200-13, USA) were used to stimulate M2 macrophages (26,27); 100 ng/mL PSMB8 (Human) Recombinant Protein (H00005696-P01, Abnova, Taipei, China) was used to study the effect on the polarization of M0 macrophage.

PSMB8 siRNA transfection

SW1088 and SW1783 LGG cells were transfected with 50 nM pre-designed siRNA targeting PSMB8 and control siRNA (Sheweisi Companyw, Tianjin, China) with the Lipo3000 agent (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). The sequences of siRNA targeting PSMB8 are obtained from a previous publication (25) and shown as: sense: 5’-ACAAGGUGAUUGAGAUUAAUU-3’; antisense: 5’-UUAAUCUCAUCACC-UUGUUU-3’. After 24 h of transfection, the culture medium was replaced with Leibovitz’s L-15 medium containing 10% FBS before subsequent experiments.

Cell immunofluorescence staining

SW-1088 and SW-1783 cells were cultured on the surface of a cell climbing slice placed into a 6-well plate chamber. The anti-PSMB8 (1:100) antibody was then used to label glioma cells at 4 ℃. After the primary antibody was removed and the cells were washed with PBS, the cells were incubated with goat anti-rabbit antibodies (1:500) with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) fluorescence. Finally, the nucleus was labeled with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) dye for 15 min. The cell staining sections were observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Fluorescent immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunofluorescence histochemical staining was used to detect the protein expression of CD163 and PSMB8 in LGG tissues. Five LGG samples were obtained from the biological specimen bank of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. The tissues were first treated by paraffin embedding, then cut the tissue wax into thin slices of 4–6 µm thickness. The slides loaded with slices were subjected to deparaffinization protocol (xylene 15 min two times, 100% ethanol for 10 min, 85% and 75% ethanol for 5 min). Then, the slides were immersed in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer (PH 8.0) for antigen recovery; 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was used to block non-specific binding for 30 min. The primary antibodies, anti-CD163 (1:100, human reactivity, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and anti-PSMB8 (1:100, human reactivity, Proteintech, China) antibodies, were added to the slides and incubated overnight at 4 ℃. After the residual primary antibody was cleaned with PBS buffer, the secondary antibodies [Cy3 (1:300) and AF 488 (1:400) dyes, purchased from Proteintech] were added to the slides and incubated in dark condition at room temperature for 50 min. DAPI dye was used to stain the nucleus. The slides were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from macrophages using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (R1200, Solarbio, Beijing, China), and RNA quality was verified by measuring the A260/A280 ratio using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For cDNA synthesis, 1000 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed using RT Master Mix for qPCR II (HY-K0510A, MCE, USA). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System via primers listed in Table 1, and the thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 ℃ for 15 seconds, annealing at 60 ℃ for 30 seconds, and extension at 72 ℃ for 30 seconds. Relative expression of target genes versus the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Protein extraction and Western blotting (WB)

We collected three cases of WHO grade II glioma samples, four cases of WHO grade III glioma samples, and the corresponding para-cancer tissues from The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. No patients received radiotherapy or chemotherapy before undergoing surgical treatment; Precut tissue samples were incubated in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with a 1% protease inhibitor mixture. After the samples were processed with a tissue homogenizer for 15 min, the tissue suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm at 4 ℃ for 15 min. The supernatant was collected for subsequent experiments.

Total protein concentrations of the tissue extracts were determined with the bicinchoninic acid assay. An equal amount of protein was added to each well in a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gel. After protein electrophoresis was performed, the gel was transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. We blocked nonspecific binding with a 5% solution of skim milk powder, and the PVDF membranes were incubated with anti-PSMB8 (Proteintech, human reactivity, China) or anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, human reactivity, China) primary antibody (1:1,000) at 4 ℃ overnight. The PVDF membrane was incubated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:2,000) for 4 h. Finally, the results were observed with Tanon 5200 multi developer unit.

Transwell assays

For the Transwell cell invasion assay, Matrigel-coated filters assessed LGG cell invasiveness. PSMB8 knock-down and control LGG cells were seeded in serum-free media in the top chamber, with L-15 medium plus 10% FBS in the bottom chamber. Post 48-hour incubation, top chamber cells were removed, and the membrane was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then stained with crystal violet. Four fields were imaged for invasion analysis. Conversely, for migration assays, Transwell chambers without Matrigel quantified cell migration, following a similar protocol minus the Matrigel, focusing on migration through the membrane pores.

Cell proliferation assay

To assess the proliferation of negative control and PSMB8 knockdown LGG cells, the 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay was employed. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Post-adherence, cells were treated with EdU (10 µM) for 2 hours. Following EdU treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. The incorporated EdU was detected using a Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 Imaging Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI to quantify total cell numbers. Fluorescence microscopy was used to visualize and count EdU-positive (proliferating) and DAPI-stained (total) cells. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated to determine the proliferation rate of both negative control and PSMB8 knockdown LGG cells.

Single-cell cloning assay

For assessing proliferation and growth capacity, negative control and PSMB8 knockdown LGG cells were seeded at 1,000 cells per 10 cm dish. Cultures were maintained at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 environment, with medium refreshed every 3 days. After 14 days, cell colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and manually counted. Only colonies with over 50 cells were considered for analysis. The assay was performed in quintuplicate to ensure data reliability.

Statistical analyses

Differences between the two groups were determined using the Wilcox rank sum test, including the comparison in PSMB8 expression among normal/glioma tissues, WHO II/III grade gliomas, IDH mutation (−/+), MGMT methylation (−/+), and 1p19q codeletion (−/+). The Pearson test was used to analyze the correlation between PSMB8 promoter methylation and its mRNA expression. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to detect the prognosis difference between PSMB8 high- and low-expression subgroups in LGG and the log-rank test was used to determine the statistical significance. Concordance index (C-index) was applied to evaluate the accuracy of the multiple-Cox regression model. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare the difference between groups with high and low expression of PSMB8 in the analysis of immune cell infiltration. The R language program (version 4.1.1) and the GraphPad prism (version 8.0). Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test was used for differential analysis of gene expression. All the aforementioned experiments were conducted a minimum of three times under the same conditions. P value <0.05 was considered statistically significantly.

Results

PSMB8 is highly expressed in LGG tissues

Figure 1 showed the workflow of this study. We firstly analyzed PSMB8 mRNA expression in 31 types of tumor tissues using TCGA cancer and GTEx normal tissue data. Compared with normal human tissues, PSMB8 expression was significantly increased in 27 tumor tissues (Figure 2A). By analyzing TCGA-LGG, TCGA-GBM and GTEx datasets on the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) 2 website, we affirmed that PSMB8 is highly expressed in tumor tissues. Furthermore, the results revealed that PSMB8 expression in GBM tissues was even higher than that observed in LGG tissues (Figure 2B). We then performed the PPI analysis centered on PSMB8. The results revealed that PSMB8 interacts with the other proteasome submits such as PSMB2, PSMC2, and PSMA8, indicating that PSMB8 plays an important role in the activity of the proteasome complex. In contrast, PSMB8 may also interact with transporter 1 ATP binding cassette subfamily B member (TAP1), TAP2 and L-antigen 3 (LAGE3). The former participates in the formation of multidrug resistance and the latter belongs to LAGE gene family; they were clearly activated in cancer tissues and functions as an important factor in TME formation and shaping (Figure 2C). According to the network interaction mapping, nuclear receptor coactivator 2 (NCOA2) and estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) also may participate in the biological processes related to PSMB8. NCOA2 takes part in the gene fusion process in various types of cancers, and ESR1 is closely related to the development of breast cancer and endometrial cancer (28-33).

Moreover, according to the gene methylation data in TCGA repository, the mRNA expression level of PSMB8 was negatively correlated with the degree of methylation of its promoter (Figure 2D). This indicates that the expression of PSMB8 was highly regulated by promoter methylation. In our vitro experiments, we labeled the PSMB8 molecule with FITC dye, and a distinct FITC fluorescence signal was detected in the cytoplasm of SW-1088 and SW-1783 human LGG cells (Figure 2E). Moreover, we collected three cases of WHO grade II glioma tissues, four cases of WHO grade III glioma tissues, and their corresponding adjacent brain tissues to quantify the PSMB8 protein levels. The results of WB displayed that the protein content of PSMB8 was significantly increased in glioma tissues compared with the adjacent tissues (Figure 2F,2G).

The expression of PSMB8 LGG tissues was associated with the mutation status of IDH and WHO grade

To understand the associations between PSMB8 and LGG molecular and clinical features, we then analyzed the relationships between PSMB8 expression and the gene mutation status of IDH, 1p/19q codeletion, and MGMT promoter methylation. The results indicated that the expression of PSMB8 was significantly related to WHO grade, IDH mutation status. However, the correlations between PSMB8 expression and other factors like 1p/19q co-deletion status and MGMT promoter methylation status were not significant (Figure 3A). In LGGs, the PSMB8 mRNA expression in grade III glioma tissue was higher than that in grade II glioma tissue (Figure 3B). For the correlations between 1p/19q codeletion status and PSMB8 expression, the transcription data derived from the public glioma datasets displayed that the difference of PSMB8 expression between 1p/19q non-codeletion and codeletion groups was not significant in most cohorts (Figure 3C). According to the transcriptome data in LGG tissues from TCGA/CGGA repositories, the expression level of PSMB8 in IDH wildtype was higher than that in IDH mutant tissues, but the difference of PSMB8 expression between IDH wildtype and mutant tissues was not significant according to the data of the GEO database (Figure 3D), Of note, the IDH-wildtype group in this LGG-specific analysis consists exclusively of WHO grade II/III IDH-wildtype gliomas; it does not include WHO grade IV IDH-wildtype tumors such as GBM or H3 G34-mutant gliomas. Moreover, the TCGA dataset analysis results showed that PSMB8 expression in tissues with unmethylated MGMT was higher than that in tissues with methylated MGMT (Figure 3E). Previous studies have shown that IDH mutation indicated a better prognosis in patients with LGG (34,35). Our analysis showed that high expression of PSMB8 was related to the IDH wildtype in glioma, which implied that PSMB8 may be a prognostic risk factor for LGG.

Expression level of PSMB8 could be used as a prognostic indicator for patients with LGGs

We extracted the clinical prognostic data of patients with LGG from TCGA, CGGA, and GEO repositories and analyzed the relationship between prognosis and PSMB8 expression. The results of log-rank tests indicated that high expression of PSMB8 were related to low OS with patients with LGG, a finding that was verified using all three databases. According to the distribution map of patient survival status, LGG patients with low PSMB8 expression had longer overall survival (OS) times compared with those in the high PSMB8 expression group (Figure 4A-4C). Notably, the prognostic significance of PSMB8 was not universal; it was associated with poor OS in only six of the 33 tumor types analyzed (LGG, LUAD, PAAD, TGCT, THYM, and UVM), indicating a cancer-type-specific prognostic role. Similar results were verified using the Cox regression model. Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were performed to identify various potential factors that influence survival, including PSMB8. The results suggested that patients with LGG with high PSMB8 expression had a higher risk of death than the low expression group [hazard ratio (HR) >1, P value <0.05] (Table 2). In addition, we used the ROC curve to evaluate the predictive value of PSMB8 for LGG prognosis. The results showed that the values of ROC area under the curve (AUC) calculated from three databases were all greater than 0.6. The AUC value in using TCGA approached 0.7 and the AUC value using CGGA and GEO approached 0.6 (Figure S2), indicated that PSMB8 could be useful as an indicator for predicting the prognosis of patients with LGG.

We also used multivariable ROC curves to compare the predictive efficacy of PSMB8 and commonly used clinical biomarkers (such as age, WHO grade, IDH status, and 1p19q status). Analysis results based on TCGA-LGG dataset indicated that PSMB8 had good predictive ability for the 1-, 3- and 5-year survival of patients with grade II/III glioma (AUC ≈0.69), and it was a risk factor for reduced survival in patients with glioma. The predictive efficacy of PSMB8 was close to that of age (AUC ≈0.69), glioma grade (AUC ≈0.68), and IDH mutation (AUC ≈0.76) (Figure S3A-S3C). According to the analysis of the CGGA and GSE16011 datasets, PSMB8 still had relatively reliable prediction efficacy (AUC ≈0.6 in CGGA, AUC >0.6 in GSE16011 for predicting 1-year survival). However, the predictive ability of WHO grade and 1p/19q was higher than that of PSMB8 (Figure S3D-S3I). Because of the higher quality of TCGA’s data, we considered PSMB8 a stable and valuable independent index that could be used to predict the survival of glioma patients.

The nomogram model with PSMB8 factors could be used as a tool to predict the OS of patients with LGG

Based on the results of multivariate Cox regression analysis, we integrated several predictors including age, WHO grade, and PSMB8 to establish the nomogram model that could predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS of patients with LGG according to the weighted scores based on each predictor (Figure 5A). According to the status of predictors, they could obtain corresponding points from the top of the nomogram and the total points could be calculated via combining the scores for each predictor. Based on the total points, the corresponding OS rate for each patient with LGG could be determined from the bottom lines of the nomogram (Figure 5A).

We then used the calibration curves and DCA to evaluate the effectiveness of the nomogram model. Calibration curves were used to test the predictive power of the nomogram model for OS. The DCA could be used to evaluate the effectiveness of the model in clinical decision making, and it could analyze the net benefit for patients within a certain risk threshold (36). According to TCGA-LGG cohort, our nomogram model predicted the OS rate of patients with LGG (Figure 5B) and had good clinical benefit rate of 3- and 5-year survival for patients with LGG (Figure 5C,5D). These results were validated using the same model in the datasets from CGGA and GEO (Figure 5E-5J).

Gene enrichment analysis of PSMB8 in LGG

Firstly, GSEA of PSMB8 was performed according to the expression level of PSMB8 in LGGs. The specific method was to divide LGGs into high and low expression groups according to the expression of PSMB8. The GSEA results showed that, compared with the LGGs with lower PSMB8, multiple immune response pathways (including the process of cytokine production and non-specific defense response) were down-regulated in the LGGs with PSMB8 high expression. Moreover, the high expression of PSMB8 was also related to the activation of antigen processing and presentation (Figure S4). These results suggested that PSMB8 may be a potential tumor immune microenvironment regulator of LGG.

We used R package “limma” to obtain the genes differentially expressed between LGGs with different PSMB8 expression levels. The differential expression genes (DEGs) were used to perform GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis via R packages “clusterProfiler” (37-39). The GO analysis results revealed that the main biological processes involving the differentially expressed genes were immune responses including predominantly IFN-γ-mediated signaling pathways and lymphocyte reactions. According to the results of the cellular components and molecular functions analysis in GO analysis, these differentially expressed genes were related to the biological functions of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), vesicle transport, and membrane-associated receptor proteins (Figure 6A). It suggested that PSMB8 participates in the antigen presentation process mediated by the MHC complex and the signal transduction mediated by the interactions between membrane receptor proteins and their ligands.

GO (A) and KEGG (B) analysis in DEGs between high and low PSMB8 expression groups in LGG tissues. BP, biological process; CC, cellular component; DEG, differentially expressed gene; ECM, extracellular matrix; GO, Gene Ontology; KEGG, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes; LGG, low-grade glioma; MF, molecular function; MHC, major histocompatibility complex.

The KEGG analysis revealed that the main signaling pathways enriched in differentially expressed genes included transplantation-related immune rejection reactions, antigen presentation, cell adhesion, and immune networks mediated by IgA production and Th17 cell differentiation (Figure 6B). These results showed that the DEGs, included PSMB8, may participate in the immune response in the progress of LGG and regulate the glioma immune microenvironment, especially antigen presentation mediated by macrophages.

Relationships between PSMB8 expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cells

Given the significant implications of PSMB8 in cancer immunity related GO terms, we further investigate the associations between PSMB8 expression and LGG microenvironment. The tumor immune microenvironment, consisting of T cells, macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, and dendritic cells, participates in tumor formation and progression. Based on single-cell sequencing of glioma tissue, macrophages were found to be the main immune cells infiltrating high-grade gliomas (40). However, the types of immune cells infiltrating LGG tissues remained to be explored. We used two algorithms to evaluate the relationship between PSMB8 expression and the degree of immune cell infiltration in LGG. Based on the CIBERSORT method (41), the high expression of PSMB8 was related to the high degree of infiltration of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and the M1 and M2 subtypes of macrophages. According to the single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) algorithm (42,43), the infiltration degree of most immune cells, which included in CD4+/CD8+ T cells, macrophages, monocytes, and NK cells, was correspondingly increased with the high expression of PSMB8 (Figure 7A). The ESTIMATE algorithm was then used to assess the general degree of infiltration of immune system and matrix components in LGG tissues (44,45). The results showed that the infiltration scores of immune system and stromal components were higher in the PSMB8 high expression group than that in the low expression group. The estimate score, combined with immune and stromal scores, also displayed that the infiltration of these two components was higher in the PSMB8 high expression group (Figure 7B). With increased infiltration of immune and matrix components, the purity of LGG tissues might decrease, an effect confirmed with our analysis. The results showed that the LGG tissue purity was lower in the PSMB8 high expression group than that in low expression group (Figure 7B). In addition, the difference in immune cell infiltration between the PSMB8 high and low expression groups was statistically significant, except for the infiltration of mast cells (Figure 7C).

T cells play a pivotal role in the glioma immune microenvironment, contributing significantly to the modulation of tumor growth and immune response dynamics. As shown in Figure 7D, in LGG tissues with high expression of PSMB8, the expression of T cell-related immune checkpoint genes is also significantly elevated. For example, CD276 is a membrane surface receptor that was overexpressed in a variety of tumor cells. Researchers found that it could inhibit the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells to inhibit the immune response against tumors (46). The cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) is a transmembrane receptor located on the surface of T cells. When it combines with the B7 family protein on the surface of tumor cells, the anti-tumor effect of T cells is significantly inhibited. Similarly, the programmed cell death 1 (PDCD1) protein is an inhibitory receptor located on the surface of effector T cells that is involved in tumor immune escape responses (47). The above results suggested that PSMB8 was involved in the process of the immune response against LGG and may be related to the reactions of immune escape. The specific regulatory mechanism needs to be further explored.

Relationship between PSMB8 expression and NK cell activation and macrophage polarization

Wei et al. discussed the influence of NK cells on tumor immune-environment (48). Therefore, we briefly explored the relationship between PSMB8 expression and NK cell activation. The results indicated that there were differences in the expression of most NK cell-related genes between the high and low expression groups of PSMB8 (Figure S5A,S5B). Moreover, high expression of PSMB8 was positively corelated with the low expression of NK cell-associated activation receptors [NKG2C, NKG2D, DNAX accessory molecule (DNAM-1), and NKp30] and overexpression of inhibitory factors (TGFB1 and IDO1) (Figure S6A,S6B). These correlative findings raise the hypothesis that PSMB8 expression is associated with a profile of NK cell-related genes suggestive of suppressed activation, but this requires functional validation. However, the expression of the secretory proteins with cytotoxic effects (perforin and granzyme) was positively correlated with PSMB8 (Figure S6A,S6B), which may be due to the increased infiltration of T lymphocytes (Figure 7A).

Fluorescent IHC was employed to investigate the correlation between PSMB8 expression and the infiltration of M2-type macrophages, identified by their classical marker molecule CD163, in three cases of LGG tissues. Our findings revealed a significant association between PSMB8 expression and M2-type macrophage infiltration in these LGG tissues (Figure 8A). M2-type macrophage infiltration was higher in LGG tissues with higher PSMB8 expression. These results are basically consistent with the previous bioinformatic analysis, which indicated that there is a correlation between the high expression of PSMB8 and the infiltration of tumor-promoting M2 macrophages.

The expression of PSMB8 was examined during the induction of M1 and M2 polarization of THP-1 in vitro. THP-1 were treated with LPS and IFN-γ to induce an M1 macrophages or IL-4 and IL-13 to induce M2 macrophages. High expression of M1-like phenotype markers CD86, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-12β and CD40 was observed compared with M0 cells, which indicated successful induction of MI macrophages. Similarly, M2-like phenotype markers IL-10, SIRP2, CHIL3 and CD206 were also highly expressed, which indicated that M2 macrophages were also successfully induced. Then the expression level of PSMB8 in M1 or M2 macrophages was detected, and it was found that PSMB8 was highly expressed in M2 macrophages compared with M0 macrophages (Figure 8B,8C).

Then we explored whether PSMB8 recombinant protein would affect the polarization of M0 cells. It was found that whether it was M0 or M1 macrophages, the phenotype of M1 macrophages did not increase significantly after receiving PSMB8 treatment. Interestingly, the M2 macrophage phenotype of M0 and M2 macrophages was significantly upregulated after PSMB8 treatment. Additionally, to rule out the interference of endogenous PSMB8 expression, we confirmed that PSMB8 treatment did not affect PSMB8 mRNA levels (Figure 8D,8E), suggesting a potential positive feedback loop where extracellular PSMB8 may promote M2 macrophage polarization, and M2-polarized cells in turn express high levels of PSMB8. Further experiments, including direct measurement of PSMB8 in culture supernatants and siRNA-mediated knockdown of PSMB8 in THP-1 cells, are warranted to conclusively demonstrate this mechanism.

Inhibition of PSMB8 damage the migratory, invasive, and proliferation ability of LGG cells

As the results of KEGG pathway analysis of PSMB8 in LGGs, we found PSMB8 was significantly associated with the terms of cell adhesion molecules, ECM-receptor interaction and Focal adhesion, which are reported to be crucial in the regulation of cancer cell migration and invasion. Thus, we inhibited the PSMB8 expression in LGG cell lines using siRNA to test the migratory and invasive capabilities of SW1088 and SW1783 cells via transwell assay (Figure 9A). The results found that the migratory and invasive abilities of LGG cell lines were strongly defected when PSMB8 expression was inhibited (Figure 9B,9C), which confirmed our hypothesis on the role of PSMB8 in LGG cell migration and invasion. Moreover, as shown in Figure 9D-9G, LGG cells with PSMB8 knockdown exhibited significantly reduced EdU incorporation and colony formation efficiency compared to the negative control, indicating that PSMB8 downregulation inhibited LGG cell proliferation.

The GSEA and prognostic role of PSMB8 in pan-cancer

According to the GSEA results based on the hallmark gene sets of MSigDB, the PSMB8 high expression group was significantly positively correlated with the signaling pathways activated by immune factors in 33 tumor types (Figure S7A). These factors included IFN-γ/α, IL-6, IL-2, and inflammatory cytokines. Notably, the prognostic significance of PSMB8 was not universal; it was associated with poor OS in only six of the 33 tumor types analyzed (LGG, LUAD, PAAD, TGCT, THYM, and UVM), indicating a cancer-type-specific prognostic role (Figure S7B).

The results of Spearman analysis revealed that high expression of PSMB8 was related to the upregulation of immune checkpoints, including lymphocyte activating 3 (LAG3), galectin 9 (LGALS9), and PDCD1 in most types of a total of 33 tumors examined (Figure S8A). In addition, the results of the TMB and MSI models suggested that the expression of PSMB8 was significantly correlated with the degree of mutations in the genome. Among various tumor tissues, including LGG, colon adenocarcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, and prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), those with higher PSMB8 expression were more likely to produce genomic variation. However, in other tumors, like THYM, PRAD, and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), PSMB8 expression was negatively related to genomic variation (Figure S8B,S8C). The high degree of TMB and MSI usually meant better immunotherapy outcomes. Therefore, the expression level of PSMB8 may be useful as a predictor to assess the effectiveness of immunotherapy on most tumors including LGG.

Higher PSMB8 expression is associated with improved survival and immunotherapy response in patients treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors

To explore the correlation between PSMB8 expression and cancer immunotherapy response, we intended to seek the transcriptome data of LGG tissues with the interventions in immune-related pathways, but such data sets were temporarily lacked. Based on the transcriptome data of urinary tumor tissues with anti-PD-L1 intervention (46), the patients with high expression of PSMB8 had the higher OS rate and longer OS time (Figure S8D). We classified the urinary tumor tissues into three categories based on their immunophenotypes. We found that the PSMB8 expression in tumor tissues dominated by the inflammatory response was significantly higher than that in two other types of tissues with immune inhibitor reactions and non-immune infiltration (Figure S8E). The patients in PSMB8 high expression group had higher tumor response rates and lower tumor progression rate than the PSMB8 low expression group (Figure S8F). These results revealed that PSMB8 may participate in the activation of inflammatory response in urinary tumor tissues with the intervention of anti-PD-L1 therapy, which may contribute to improved clinical outcomes.

According to the analyses derived from the melanoma immunotherapies with anti-PD-1 interventions (49), high expression of PSMB8 was related to higher OS and longer progression-free interval (PFI) in patients with melanoma (Figure S8G,S8H). Moreover, we found that the tumor response rate in the PSMB8 high expression group was significantly higher than that in the low expression group. Likewise, tumor progression/stabilization rate in the PSMB8 high expression group was lower than that in the PSMB8 low expression group (Figure S8I).

Discussion

PSMB8 is an immune proteasome with a circular 20S core β subunit. Unlike the normal proteasome complex, the immune-related proteasome could cleave antigenic peptides via ATP/ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation pathways. These peptide segments after processing could be presented to antigen-presenting cells (APCs) by the MHC I complex. APCs then transmit antigen information to specific immune effector cells, including T cells, thus regulating the body’s immune functions. However, the role of PSMB8 in the LGG immune microenvironment is not known. An important question is the cellular origin of PSMB8 within the LGG TME. Our data indicate a dual source. First, PSMB8 is expressed intrinsically by LGG tumor cells, as demonstrated by immunofluorescence in cell lines and WB in patient tissues. Second, PSMB8 is also highly expressed by infiltrating immune cells, particularly M2-like macrophages, as shown by our fluorescent IHC co-staining and qPCR analysis of polarized macrophages. This suggests that both the tumor and the tumor-associated macrophages contribute to the overall PSMB8 signal, which may correlate with the observed poor prognosis.

Our study revealed that PSMB8 was overexpressed in LGG tissues compared with normal brain tissues. Moreover, the low expression of PSMB8 was closely related to MGMT methylation status, IDH mutant status, and the improvement of OS in patients with LGG. Furthermore, our analysis is limited to adult-type diffuse gliomas (WHO grades II and III). The expression and role of PSMB8 in rare, pediatric-type LGGs, such as Diffuse astrocytoma, MYB- or MYBL1-altered (WHO grade 1), remain unknown and warrant investigation in dedicated pediatric cohorts. The nomogram model with the PSMB8 indicator could predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS of patients with LGG. According to the enrichment analysis, differentially expressed genes, including PSMB8, were involved in the immune response to LGG. The overexpression of PSMB8 was closely associated with a high degree infiltration of multiple immune cells, including macrophages and T cells. Moreover, our study found that the expression of immune checkpoint inhibitors was also higher in patients with LGG with higher PSMB8 expression. These results suggested that PSMB8 could be used as a prognostic indicator for patients with LGG and may participate in the immunosuppression of LGG.

Type M2 is a macrophage subtype associated with the activation of immunosuppressive signals, and it was found to be significantly associated with poor prognosis in patients with glioma (48). According to our bioinformatics analysis, the high expression of PSMB8 was closely related to the infiltration of M2 macrophages and the overexpression of immune checkpoint-related genes. Furthermore, the fluorescent IHC indicated that the expression of PSMB8 seems to be positively correlated with the infiltration of M2 macrophages in LGG tissues. Our analysis results further revealed that overexpression of PSMB8 may be related to the inhibition of NK cell activity and exhaustion of T cell. It indicated that PSMB8 may show immunosuppressive effect on the whole in LGG immune microenvironment. Our in vitro study found that M2 macrophages highly express PSMB8, and PSMB8 protein can induce M0 macrophages to differentiate into M2 macrophages. Since M2 macrophages are intertwined with tumor cells in LGG immune microenvironment, we speculate that tumor cells may communicate with M2 macrophages through PSMB8, but further experiments are needed to prove this. While our data suggest a relationship between PSMB8 and M2 polarization, we acknowledge that the exact source and mechanism require further investigation. Future studies should quantify secreted PSMB8 in culture supernatants of polarized macrophages and employ genetic knockdown approaches (e.g., using siRNA targeting PSMB8 in THP-1 cells) to definitively establish its functional role in driving macrophage polarization.

According to the correlation analysis of PSMB8 expression and survival status in pan-cancer patients with anti-PD-1/anti-PD-L1 immunotherapies, high expression of PSMB8 was related to the higher survival rates in patients with melanoma and urologic neoplasms. These results may be caused by differences in the tumor immune microenvironment. The analyses of single-cell sequencing of glioma tissues showed that macrophages/microglial cells were the main immune component in glioma tissues, with a low degree of T lymphocyte infiltration (40). However, the infiltration of T lymphocytes into other solid tumors was generally higher than that in glioma tissues, and the anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies were mainly used to activate the function of effector T cells in tumor tissues. Therefore, we suspected that PSMB8 might play different roles in different immune cell-mediated responses. On the one hand, PSMB8 might participate in the immunosuppressive effect mediated by tumor-associated macrophages. In contrast, PSMB8 might be related to early inflammatory responses and cytotoxic effects mediated by tumor-associated T lymphocytes.

The prognostic factors of glioma include ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs), N6-methyladenine (m6A) RNA methylation regulators, lipid metabolism genes, noncoding RNAs, and the cationic channel protein Piezo1 (50-53). Compared to previous studies, the greatest advantage of PSMB8 as a prognostic factor for glioma was its association with the tumor immune microenvironment. Our study detailed the positive association of PSMB8 with common immune checkpoints and immune cell infiltration in LGG tissues. In addition, we analyzed the association between PSMB8 expression and immune system activation in pan-cancer tissues. The results revealed that high expression of PSMB8 was significantly positively correlated with the activation of the IFN-γ pathway and the overexpression of various immune checkpoints in pan-cancer, which indicated that the interaction of PSMB8 with the tumor immune microenvironment may be similar in different tumors. While PSMB8 is not a glioma-exclusive biomarker, its prognostic value appears to be tumor-type specific. In contrast to LGG, high PSMB8 expression did not correlate with poor prognosis in cancers such as breast or colon cancer in our analysis (Figure S7A). This suggests that the impact of PSMB8 on tumor progression may depend on the unique composition of the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly the dominant immune cell types present.

A variety of analytical models for predicting the prognosis of patients with glioma have been reported in the literature. Qu et al. established a nomogram model containing seven major pathological parameters to predict the prognosis of patients with high-grade glioma (54) and evaluated the influence of m6A methylation-related genes on the prognosis of patients with glioma (55). Wang et al. reported that alternative splicing signatures could be independent prognosis factors for patients with LGG (grade II/III gliomas) (56). Zhang et al. reported that the risk score based on immune-related genes could also be used as an independent prognostic index for patients with LGG (57). The main similarities between our research and previous studies were that the nomogram model and ROC curves were used to test the predictive efficacy of the corresponding indicators. The main difference between our model and those of others was research thinking. The above studies were designed to screen gene groups according to the corresponding characteristics (such as m6A and immune-related genes). A survival model for glioma patients was then established with the selected target gene group. In contrast, our research started with a single gene (PSMB8) and discussed in detail the effectiveness of the survival model established by it and its correlation with the tumor immune microenvironment. Because our model contained only one main index, it could reduce the cost and time of clinical detection and be more easily accepted by patients.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that PSMB8 is significantly overexpressed in LGG and serves as an independent prognostic factor associated with poor OS, higher WHO grade, and IDH wild-type status. Mechanistically, PSMB8 not only promotes tumor cell migration, invasion, and proliferation but also correlates with M2 macrophage polarization and immune checkpoint upregulation, implicating its role in reshaping the immunosuppressive TME. Furthermore, PSMB8 shows potential for predicting immunotherapy response in pan-cancer settings. Collectively, our findings suggest that PSMB8 is a promising prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target for LGG, warranting further clinical validation and mechanistic exploration.
