Recurrent pediatric vesical lymphangioma: The first case report of early relapse after transurethral resection and successful endoscopic management
Authors: Chadi Bourimi, Reda Tariqi, Saad Mesbahi, Mohamed Yassine Gounni, Josseline Kibanzala, Amal Fekkar, Meryem Rais, Imad Boualaoui, Ahmed Ibrahimi, Yassine Nouini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bladder lymphangioma, a rare benign vascular malformation in children, often mimics malignancy on imaging and endoscopy, challenging diagnosis and management. A 10-year-old girl presented with gross hematuria. Ultrasound and Computed tomography revealed a 13 mm left lateral wall polypoid lesion. Cystoscopy showed a friable reddish mass. Histology confirmed dilated lymphatic channels without atypia. Initial complete transurethral resection was followed by 2-month recurrence, successfully treated by deeper re-resection to muscularis propria. Twelve-month follow-up showed no relapse. Pediatric vesical lymphangioma requires complete histopathological assessment and meticulous resection. Structured postoperative surveillance is crucial to detect and manage early recurrence, especially in children.

Introduction

Bladder masses in children are rare and typically raise suspicion for malignancy, particularly rhabdomyosarcoma or urothelial carcinoma. Among benign entities, vascular malformations such as lymphangiomas are exceptionally rare in the pediatric bladder and may mimic malignant tumors clinically. Considered congenital lymphatic malformations rather than true neoplasms, these lesions carry a risk of local recurrence if incompletely resected.1 Reports of pediatric bladder lymphangioma remain sporadic, with no established management guidelines. Herein, we present a rare case of bladder lymphangioma in a 10-year-old girl, presenting with gross hematuria initially treated with transurethral resection but complicated by early recurrence requiring repeat surgery. This case underscores the diagnostic pitfalls of pediatric bladder vascular lesions and emphasizes the necessity of complete endoscopic excision and vigilant follow-up to detect and treat early recurrence.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 10-year-old girl presented with a one-week history of painless gross hematuria without associated lower urinary tract symptoms, fever, dysuria, or prior urinary tract infections. Physical examination was normal.

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a well-defined lesion on the left lateral bladder wall. Computed tomography demonstrated 9 mm segmental wall thickening extending over 3 cm with a 13 mm enhancing polypoid intravesical component (Fig. 1A and B). Repeat cystoscopy confirmed recurrence, treated by complete deep transurethral resection. Histopathology reaffirmed lymphatic malformation without atypia.

Surveillance at 3, 6, and 12 months showed no recurrence on ultrasound and cystoscopy. The patient remains asymptomatic, with planned annual ultrasonographic and cystoscopy follow-up for two years given the early recurrence.

Discussion

Pediatric bladder tumors are exceedingly rare, spanning malignant and benign entities. Lymphangiomas, exceptionally uncommon benign vascular anomalies, are frequently overlooked due to nonspecific presentation.1 Histopathologically, lymphangiomas are defined as hamartomatous proliferations of malformed lymphatic vessels arising from congenital dysgenesis and aberrant lymphatic development rather than true neoplastic processes. They are further subclassified into capillary, cavernous, and cystic types, based on the size and architecture of the lymphatic channels.1 Clinically, vesical lymphangioma may present with intermittent hematuria, dysuria, or irritative voiding symptoms.2 To our knowledge, only six cases of vesical lymphangioma have been documented in the English-language literature since 1983, including merely two pediatric patients (Table 1). Review of published cases shows a predominance of adult patients (57.1%), mainly females, with gross hematuria as the leading symptom (85.7%) and preferential involvement of the lateral wall or dome (85.7%). Among the few pediatric reports, the 8-year-old girl described by Niu et al. shared similar features but exhibited no recurrence.3 Our case is therefore distinguished by being the first to demonstrate histologically confirmed early relapse in a child.

Preoperative imaging presents major diagnostic challenges, as ultrasonography and computed tomography (CT) often yield nonspecific findings. In our patient, ultrasonography showed a well-circumscribed lesion in the left bladder wall, while CT revealed a 9 mm parietal thickening with an enhancing 13 mm polypoid component—features indistinguishable from rhabdomyosarcoma or papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential.1,2 Similar ambiguous “mass-like” or “wall-thickening” patterns have been consistently reported in all published cases, frequently prompting early cystoscopic assessment.2

Cystoscopy with histopathologic evaluation remains the cornerstone for definitive diagnosis. The 1.5 cm reddish, friable, and vascular lesion observed in our patient echoed prior descriptions, including Niu et al.’s “bright-red” mass and Moradi et al.’s “strawberry-like” appearance.1,3 Such vascular fragility often raises suspicion for hemangioma, yet biopsy is essential to distinguish lymphatic from blood-filled channels.

Histopathology confirms the diagnosis, showing dilated, thin-walled lymphatic channels lined by flattened endothelial cells within a fibro-edematous stroma, devoid of atypia or mitoses, and containing lymphoid aggregates. This morphology is characteristic of lymphangioma, demonstrating proteinaceous lymphatic spaces devoid of erythrocytes—findings that differentiate it from hemangioma.1,2

While immunohistochemistry (IHC) for markers such as D2-40, PROX1, and CD31 remains the gold standard for lymphatic identification, it was not performed in our case due to limited tissue, a limitation shared by several earlier reports.1,4 Nonetheless, the classical histologic features were sufficient for diagnosis. Differential diagnoses such as hemangioma or low-grade urothelial neoplasia were ruled out by the absence of hemorrhage, atypia, and tissue invasion.

Complete surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment to prevent recurrence. Historically, partial cystectomy was favored for deeply infiltrating lesions (57.1% of reported cases), often at the bladder dome or involving extensive wall thickening.2,5 However, for small, superficial tumors, minimally invasive treatments—including Holmium:YAG laser, complete transurethral resection (TUR) represents a bladder-sparing and function-preserving alternative, particularly suitable in children.1 Our patient underwent an initial TUR deemed complete, followed by a second, wider resection after early recurrence. This two-time endoscopic course ultimately achieved full excision extending into the muscularis, with favorable recovery. Collectively, these findings support a tailored strategy in which conservative endoscopic management is appropriate for pediatric cases, with prompt re-intervention warranted upon early recurrence to prevent progression or bleeding.

Although considered benign with low recurrence risk after complete resection, prior cases lacked long-term follow-up (median 6–12 months), potentially underestimating relapse potential (Table 1). Our case challenges this assumption by demonstrating an early recurrence two months after an apparently complete initial TUR. This finding suggests that residual microscopic disease within the submucosal lymphatic network may persist after endoscopic resection, a limitation of endoscopic techniques given the bladder's complex, multilayered anatomy. Open surgery better facilitates true “en bloc” excision of deep components.

This behavior parallels pediatric lymphangiomas in other regions, such as the retroperitoneum and mesentery, where recurrence is strongly associated with incomplete resection.6 By analogy, a limited TUR confined to mucosal and superficial submucosal layers may leave behind deeper lymphatic channels—an “iceberg” phenomenon of residual disease. In our case, a second, deliberately deeper resection achieved a gratifying outcome consonant with “en bloc” successes.7 We advocate structured surveillance: ultrasonography and selective cystoscopy at 3, 6, and 12 months, then annually for 2–5 years, balancing recurrence risk against procedural morbidity.

Conclusion

Bladder lymphangioma is an exceptionally rare benign lesion in children, but it can closely mimic malignant bladder tumors and therefore must be considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric hematuria with a focal bladder mass. In our 10-year-old patient, histopathologic confirmation after endoscopic resection, followed by early but successfully treated recurrence, underlined the diagnostic importance of tissue sampling and highlighted that a deeper, wide resection can achieve durable remission, suggesting that thorough but bladder-sparing endoscopic management, combined with structured medium-term imaging and endoscopic surveillance, represents an appropriate strategy for these rare lesions in children.
