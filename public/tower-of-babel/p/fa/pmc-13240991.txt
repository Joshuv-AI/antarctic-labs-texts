Cohort Study: Risk of Gallstones and Biliary Complications With Glucagon‐Like Peptide‐1 Receptor Agonists in Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Mohamed H Eldesouki, Omar Alkasabrah, Mohammed Kloub, Hazem Abosheaishaa, Khaled Elfert, Arpit Sharma, Sherif Andrawes, Nikki Duong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGlucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) are widely prescribed for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) because of their benefits in glycemic control, weight reduction, and cardiovascular outcomes. This study evaluated the risk of gallbladder disease among diabetic patients treated with GLP‐1 RAs.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was performed using the TriNetX research network. Adults ≥ 18 years with T2DM who received GLP‐1 RAs were identified and compared with matched controls. Propensity score matching (1:1) was used to balance baseline characteristics. Outcomes included cholelithiasis/choledocholithiasis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), and cholecystectomy. Subgroup analyses were performed according to GLP‐1 RA agents.ResultsA total of 156,376 patients were included; 43,077 patients were in the GLP‐1 RA cohort and 113,299 patients were in the control group. After matching, 39,140 patients remained in each group. At two years, patients with GLP‐1 RA use had higher rates of cholelithiasis/choledocholithiasis (aOR 1.44, 95% CI 1.24–1.65), whereas rates of cholecystectomy were non‐significant. By three years, GLP‐1 RA use remained associated with higher rates of cholelithiasis/choledocholithiasis (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.24–1.63), cholecystitis (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.14–1.83), and cholecystectomy (aOR 1.54, 95% CI 1.17–2.02). There were no significant differences in the rates of pancreatitis or ERCP. Semaglutide and dulaglutide demonstrated higher cholelithiasis, whereas liraglutide and exenatide were not associated with a significant increase.ConclusionGLP‐1 RAs use in patients with T2DM was associated with modestly higher rates of cholelithiasis/choledocholithiasis, cholecystitis, and cholecystectomy, whereas there were no significant differences in rates of pancreatitis or ERCP.Keywords: biliary complications, choledocholithiasis, cholelithiasis, gallbladder disease, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

The development of Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) followed the discovery of the incretin‐like effect of physiological GLP‐1 in 1996 [1]. Exenatide was the first FDA‐approved GLP‐1 RA for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in 2006, though its use was limited due to its short half‐life requiring twice daily injections [2]. Subsequently, liraglutide emerged as a single‐dose daily GLP‐1 and was approved in 2010. This was followed by a weekly injection of exenatide in 2011, and eventually other agents such as dulaglutide, albiglutide, and semaglutide, which enabled weekly dosing [3, 4]. GLP‐1 RA use increased considerably between 2014 and 2022, because of their benefits in controlling diabetes, cardiovascular effects, and weight loss [5].

Despite these therapeutic benefits, reports have emerged linking GLP‐1 RAs to gallbladder and biliary adverse events, including cholelithiasis and cholecystitis [6]. Possible explanations include biliary stasis, hypomotility and altered bile acid composition [7, 8]. In Europe, a drug surveillance study suggested a potential association between severe cholecystitis cases and GLP‐1 RAs [9]. The first population‐based study from the UK found that GLP‐1 RAs increased the composite risk of gallbladder and biliary diseases compared with conventional diabetic medicines [10]. Similarly, another cohort from Taiwan reported a link between GLP‐1 RAs use and higher incidence of biliary‐related problems compared with matched controls [11]. A U.S. Veterans Affairs cohort reported a higher overall incidence of gastrointestinal complications, including gallstones, among GLP‐1 RA users [12]. Most recently, a meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials reported an increased risk of cholelithiasis associated with GLP‐1 RA use in clinical trial settings [13].

While these studies provide important insights, data from large real‐world populations with extended follow‐up and detailed evaluation of individual gallbladder and biliary outcomes with downstream interventions remain limited. Real‐world studies are important to further inform the clinical relevance of these risks in routine practice. Accordingly, we conducted a large multicenter real‐world cohort study to quantify the individual risks of cholelithiasis, choledocholithiasis, cholecystitis, and pancreatitis among adults with T2DM initiating GLP‐1 RAs compared with propensity‐matched controls, with follow‐up through three years. We additionally performed agent‐specific analyses of semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, and exenatide to assess heterogeneity in the gallbladder and biliary risk. Furthermore, we uniquely conducted a multivariate regression analysis to identify baseline independent patient‐level risk factors for gallbladder and biliary disease prior to GLP‐1 RA initiation.

Methods

Study Design

This is a retrospective cohort study of patients with T2DM who received GLP‐1 RAs with other oral antidiabetic drugs versus those who received only oral antidiabetic drugs. Data were obtained from the TriNetX (Cambridge, MA, US) US Collaborative Network, a global federated research network that provides real‐time access to electronic health records of about 115 million patients within 67 healthcare organizations in the United States. All data were derived from electronic health records and through a built‐in natural language processing system that extracts variables from clinical documents. TriNetX provides only aggregate counts and statistical summaries to protect patient health information, ensuring that the data remain de‐identified. This study is exempt from Institutional Review Board approval, as it involved deidentified data. The deidentification process, as stipulated by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule, is meticulously executed at the network level by a qualified expert within the TriNetX framework. This study adheres to the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE) reporting guidelines (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Study Cohorts

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using real‐time data from the U.S. Collaborative Network within the TriNetX research platform, with analyses completed through December 2025. Cohorts were defined using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) diagnosis codes, Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes, and RxNorm medication identifiers (Table S2). We excluded patients with primary biliary cirrhosis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, sickle‐cell disorders, and neoplasms of the gallbladder, liver, and biliary tracts. Pregnant women and those with polycystic ovarian syndrome or gestational diabetes mellitus were excluded. Patients with Human immunodeficiency virus infection and taking antiretroviral medications, fenofibrate, or ursodeoxycholate were excluded. In addition, we excluded patients on insulin, as their use is indicative of advanced diabetes. All patients who developed gall stones or biliary disease before the index event were excluded. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a recorded diagnosis of T2DM were included. Because GLP‐1 RAs received U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval for diabetes management in 2006, and the study inclusion period started from January 2007 through December 2022.

Two cohorts were identified: Cohort 1 included patients prescribed GLP‐1 RAs in addition to at least one other antidiabetic medication (including thiazolidinediones, biguanides, sulfonylureas, or sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors). Cohort 2 included control patients treated with two or more non–GLP‐1 RA antidiabetic medications. This comparator was selected to represent patients with T2DM requiring treatment intensification beyond monotherapy, thereby approximating the clinical context in which GLP‐1 RAs were commonly prescribed during most of the study period. In addition, because the study population was restricted to adults with T2DM between 2007 and 2022, treatment selection occurred primarily within the context of diabetes management rather than an obesity‐only indication. This cohort construction was informed by prior large pharmacoepidemiologic studies evaluating incretin‐based therapies in routine clinical practice [10].

To enhance the validity of medication exposure ascertainment, inclusion required at least two documented prescriptions of GLP‐1 RA, thereby increasing the likelihood of sustained use and adherence. All diagnostic, procedural, and medication codes used for inclusion and exclusion are provided in Supporting Information S1: Table S2.

The index date was defined as the date of the qualifying prescription of the index medication (GLP‐1 RAs for Cohort 1 or qualifying non–GLP‐1 RA antidiabetic therapy for Cohort 2). Follow‐up began on the index date and continued until the earliest occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, last recorded clinical encounter, or the end of the study period (December 2025), whichever occurred first. Because discontinuation dates and treatment switching are not consistently captured across healthcare systems within the TriNetX platform, patients were analyzed according to their initial treatment exposure using an intention‐to‐treat‐like approach based on the index medication. Patients were not censored at the time of medication switching or discontinuation because of the lack of reliable longitudinal medication tracking across sites.

To further evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with T2DM and body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to this cohort using the identical index date definition.

Baseline Characteristics, Comorbidities, and Outcomes

Baseline variables were identified within 1 year prior to the index date including demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity), comorbidities (hypertension, liver cirrhosis, alcoholic liver disease, tobacco use, hyperlipidaemia), medication use (atorvastatin, estrogens, and opioids), and laboratory values (BMI, and haemoglobin A1C). The primary outcomes were the rates of incident cholelithiasis or choledocholithiasis, cholecystitis, and pancreatitis. Secondary outcomes were procedural interventions including abdominal imaging (ultrasound, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging/magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography [MRI]), endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), and cholecystectomy. Additional secondary outcomes included composite gallbladder and biliary events, blindness, and all‐cause mortality. All outcomes were assessed at 2‐year and 3‐year intervals following the index event.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the TriNetX software, a browser‐based real‐time analytic feature. Baseline characteristics of cohorts were described using means, standard deviations (SDs), and proportions. Covariates based on demographics, comorbid diseases, laboratory parameters, and medication use were identified. One‐to‐one (1:1) propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to balance the following covariates between groups including: age, gender, race, ethnicity, comorbidities, medication use, and BMI. The TriNetX platform utilizes input matrices of the user‐identified covariates to conduct logistic regression analysis to obtain propensity scores for all individual subjects. The propensity scores generated are used to match patients using greedy nearest‐neighbor algorithms with a calliper width of 0.1 pooled SDs. TriNetX randomizes the order of rows to eliminate bias resulting from nearest‐neighbor algorithms. Standardized mean difference after propensity‐score matching indicates the success of matching a covariate between the 2 cohorts. A standardized mean difference < 0.1 indicates that the difference between the cohorts for the covariate is small. After PSM, the risk of each outcome was calculated and expressed as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The t‐test was used for continuous variables. Two‐sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Subgroup Analyses

We conducted a subgroup analysis in patients with T2DM and concurrent obesity, using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the main cohort and evaluated outcomes over three years. Second, to assess whether the observed associations differed by specific GLP‐1 RAs medications, we conducted individual medication‐level analyses including semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, and exenatide. Each medication cohort was compared against their matched controls.

Multivariate Analysis

To identify clinical factors independently associated with gallbladder and biliary events, we performed a multivariable regression analysis using Cox proportional hazard models. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and availability within the database and included haemoglobin A1c, hyperlipidaemia, hepatic steatosis/steatotic liver disease, alcohol abuse, tobacco use, and obesity. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was defined as a p value < 0.05. In TriNetX, HRs are calculated using R's survival package, and the proportional‐hazards assumption is tested using the generalized Schoenfeld approach. Because death could preclude the occurrence of biliary outcomes, we additionally performed a competing risks analysis using the Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence method, treating death as the competing event.

Results

After PSM, there were a total of 39,140 patients in each cohort. The percentage of females in the GLP‐1 RA and control groups was 51.5% and 50.3%, respectively. Comparison of Outcomes Between GLP‐1 RAs and Control Cohorts

At two years follow‐up, the GLP‐1 RAs cohort recorded higher rates of cholelithiasis/choledocholithiasis (aOR 1.44, 95% CI 1.24–1.65; p < 0.001) when compared with controls. The rates of cholecystitis, cholecystectomy, or acute pancreatitis were not statistically significant. ERCP was not statistically different. Utilization of imaging was higher in patients receiving GLP‐1 RAs (aOR 1.46, 95% CI 1.38–1.53; p < 0.001). The rate of composite gallbladder complications was higher among GLP‐1 RA users (aOR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.50; p < 0.001) (Table 2). By three years follow‐up, patients receiving GLP‐1 RAs continued to exhibit significantly higher rates of cholelithiasis/choledocholithiasis (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.24–1.63; p < 0.001), and cholecystitis (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.14–1.83; p = 0.002). Cholecystectomy rates were higher in the GLP‐1 RAs group (aOR 1.54, 95% CI 1.17–2.02; p = 0.002). Utilization of imaging continued to be significantly higher in the GLP‐1 RAs (aOR 1.60, 95% CI 1.52–1.67; p < 0.001). ERCP was not significantly different showed a non‐significant trend (aOR 0.93, 95% CI 0.59–1.46; p = 0.59). Similarly, no significant association was observed with acute pancreatitis at three years (aOR 0.82, 95% CI 0.63–1.04; p = 0.11). The composite gallbladder complication rates were significantly higher in GLP‐1 RAs group compared with controls (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.29–1.62; p < 0.001) (Table 2). There were no differences in the incidence of blindness/ischemic optic neuropathy, or mortality between the two groups at two or three years.

Sensitivity Analysis

In the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with both T2DM and obesity, 21,008 patients were included in the GLP‐1 receptor agonist cohort, while 38,458 patients were identified in the control group. After propensity score matching, 19,256 patients remained in each group (Supporting Information S1: Table S3). By three years, GLP‐1 RAs use was associated with a higher risk of the composite gallbladder and biliary outcomes (aOR 1.59, 95% CI 1.34–1.86; p < 0.001). This association was primarily driven by cholelithiasis/choledocholithiasis (aOR 1.64, 95% CI 1.34–1.98; p < 0.001), cholecystitis (aOR 1.69, 95% CI 1.31–2.34; p = 0.002), and cholecystectomy (aOR 1.95, 95% CI 1.31–2.88; p < 0.01). There were no higher rates of pancreatitis or ERCP between the two cohorts (Supporting Information S1: Table S4).

Subgroup Analyses

Medication‐subgroup analyses at three years demonstrated heterogeneity across individual agents. Semaglutide was associated with an increased composite risk (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.28–1.78; p < 0.01), mainly driven by the higher risk of cholelithiasis (aOR 1.46, 95% CI 1.20–1.76; p < 0.01). There were no significant differences in choledocholithiasis (aOR 1.52, 95% CI 0.92–2.52; p = 0.10), cholecystectomy (aOR 1.23, 95% CI 0.84–1.81; p = 0.29), or pancreatitis (aOR 1.46, 95% CI 0.97–2.20; p = 0.07) (Table 3). Similarly, dulaglutide showed an increased composite risk (aOR 1.58, 95% CI 1.36–1.85; p < 0.01), with higher risk of cholelithiasis (aOR 1.52, 95% CI 1.27–1.84; p < 0.01) and choledocholithiasis (aOR 2.35, 95% CI 1.39–3.97; p = 0.01), while cholecystitis (aOR 1.37, 95% CI 0.98–1.91; p = 0.07), cholecystectomy (aOR 1.18, 95% CI 0.81–1.73; p = 0.39), and pancreatitis (aOR 1.14, 95% CI 0.75–1.73; p = 0.54) were not significantly different (Table 3). In contrast, exenatide and liraglutide were not associated with a significant increase in the composite gallbladder and biliary outcome (exenatide: aOR 1.21, 95% CI 0.91–1.62; p = 0.19; liraglutide: aOR 1.11, 95% CI 0.88–1.41; p = 0.38). These findings were consistent across individual gallbladder and biliary endpoints, with no significant differences observed in key complications such as cholelithiasis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, or procedural outcomes when compared with controls (Table 3).

Multivariate Analysis

In multivariate modelling, hepatic steatosis/steatotic liver disease (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.13–1.90; p = 0.003), alcohol use (HR 2.23, 95% CI 1.26–3.95; p = 0.005), and obesity (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.14–1.48; p < 0.001) were independently associated with higher risk of gallbladder and biliary events. In contrast, haemoglobin A1c, hyperlipidaemia, and tobacco use were not significantly associated with higher risk (Table 4). In a supplementary single‐cohort competing‐risks analysis using the Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence method and treating death as the competing event, the cumulative incidence of gallbladder and biliary events at the end of follow‐up was 2.24%, whereas the cumulative incidence of death was 0.91% (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1).

Discussion

Our real‐world study demonstrated that GLP‐1 RA use in patients with T2DM was associated with a higher risk of developing gallbladder disease such as cholelithiasis, choledocholithiasis, and cholecystitis compared with matched controls, although the absolute rates were low. There were no significant differences in the incidence of pancreatitis or the need for ERCP. In agent‐specific analyses, semaglutide and dulaglutide were associated with a higher risk of cholelithiasis, whereas liraglutide and exenatide were not, highlighting potential heterogeneity in biliary risk across individual agents. These findings add real‐world observational evidence to prior randomized controlled trials and meta‐analytic evidence linking GLP‐1 RAs to gallbladder disease by providing drug‐specific risk estimates derived from a large, contemporary real‐world population with longitudinal follow‐up [13, 14, 15]. Notably, in the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with both T2DM and obesity, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with increased odds of cholelithiasis, cholecystitis and cholecystectomy without significant changes in the rates of pancreatitis or ERCP.

Our study results align with a recent pooled meta‐analysis of clinical trials, suggesting an association between GLP‐1 RA use and gallbladder disease, particularly with longer duration of use, higher doses, and rapid weight loss [13, 14, 15]. Possible mechanisms include delayed gallbladder emptying, bile stasis and changes in bile composition leading to cholesterol supersaturation and gallstone formation [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. However, He et al. suggested that the risk may be more pronounced in individuals with preexisting gallbladder disease than in the general population [14]. Conversely, Zhang et al. proposed that liraglutide may protect against gallstones by modulating the balance of bile acids [8]; however, supporting evidence is limited.

Interestingly, the shift in cholecystitis from non‐significance at two years (aOR 1.10, p = 0.46) to clear significance at three years (aOR 1.45, p = 0.002) suggests that the risk of biliary complications may become more apparent with longer duration of GLP‐1 RAs exposure; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously given the observational study design and low absolute event rates.

Our study results demonstrated significantly higher rates of cholecystectomy in the GLP‐1 RA cohort (aOR 1.54, at three years); however, the absolute number of events remained low. The rates of ERCP were not significant between the study groups, despite a numerically higher number of cholecystectomies in the GLP‐1 RAs cohort. To further explore this observation, we evaluated the utilization of abdominal imaging between groups. Patients receiving GLP‐1 RAs demonstrated higher rates of abdominal imaging, compared with control patients. This difference in imaging utilization may have increased the likelihood of incidental gallstone detection and contributed to the observed increase in cholelithiasis diagnoses. However, the concurrent increase in cholecystitis and cholecystectomy suggests that the observed associations are unlikely to be explained solely by detection bias and may reflect clinically relevant biliary disease. Similarly, the LEADER trial reported an increased incidence of acute gallbladder and biliary events, including higher rates of cholecystectomy [22]. We did not find a significant association between GLP‐1 RAs use and acute pancreatitis at either two or 3 years, aligning with the previous meta‐analyses [13, 23, 24]. Overall, these findings should be interpreted as observational associations rather than evidence of causal relationships.

At 3 years, the observed associations appeared to vary across individual agents, with semaglutide and dulaglutide associated with higher composite risk, predominantly driven by cholelithiasis, while exenatide and liraglutide were not significantly associated with the composite outcome. Prior randomized trials further suggest that biliary risk varies across individual agents and is amplified by higher doses, longer treatment duration, and greater weight loss, providing a plausible explanation for the stronger associations observed with more potent agents such as semaglutide [13, 14]. In large obesity and cardiovascular outcome trials, semaglutide has been associated with higher rates of gallbladder‐related disorders, largely attributable to cholelithiasis, while rates of cholecystitis and pancreatitis have generally remained comparable to placebo [25, 26]. Dulaglutide has similarly demonstrated signals for increased gallbladder and biliary adverse events in trial‐level analyses and regulatory safety reviews, though without consistent increases in severe biliary complications or procedural interventions [14]. In contrast, exenatide has generally not been associated with a significant increase in biliary risk, and although liraglutide showed increased gallbladder and biliary events in post hoc analyses of the LEADER trial, these findings were most evident in higher‐dose or weight‐loss contexts, suggesting that differences in dose intensity, population characteristics, and outcome ascertainment may account for variability across studies [14, 22, 27]. These agent‐specific findings should be interpreted cautiously, as further stratification by agent reduces effective sample size and may increase the risk of type II error, particularly in the setting of low absolute event rates and imbalances in subgroup size. In addition, differences in dosing regimens, treatment adherence, cumulative exposure, and prescribing indications could not be fully standardized within the database, which may have further contributed to variability across agent‐specific outcomes.

As a retrospective cohort study, our study is subject to residual confounders from unmeasured lifestyle factors such as diet, and exercise. Additionally, due to the observational retrospective design, this study can identify associations but cannot establish causal relationships. The absence of standardized baseline imaging across patients may have contributed to variability in disease detection, and differential imaging frequency between groups may have introduced detection bias.

The TriNetX platform itself carries certain inherent constraints: data are aggregated from multiple healthcare systems with varying documentation and coding practices. This may affect outcome reliability and contribute to heterogeneity across sites. Additional sources of bias include diagnostic omissions, variable temporal coding, incomplete documentation, and inconsistencies associated with the transition from ICD‐9 to ICD‐10 coding [28]. Another important limitation relates to medication exposure dynamics. The TriNetX platform does not reliably capture medication discontinuation dates, treatment duration, or switching between therapies across all participating institutions. Consequently, patients were analyzed according to their initial treatment exposure using an intention‐to‐treat–like approach, and subsequent treatment changes could not be fully accounted for. This limitation may have introduced exposure misclassification and potentially biased risk estimates toward the null. Although we used an active comparator cohort and propensity score matching to reduce measured confounding, residual confounding by indication and minor differences may persist between groups. Also, detailed measures of diabetes duration, obesity severity, weight trajectory, and the precise reasons underlying GLP‐1 RA prescribing were not fully captured in TriNetX. The effects of various GLP‐1 RAs, which include treatment duration, dose intensity, medication adherence, and potential mediating variables such as rapid weight loss, should be investigated in future studies. Furthermore, the database primarily reflects insured patients receiving care in academic or large health systems, which may limit the generalizability of findings to broader or underserved populations. Finally, subgroup analyses by individual GLP‐1 RA agents may have been underpowered due to low absolute event rates and smaller subgroup sample sizes, and these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. However, our study has several strengths. This provides real‐world evidence on the long‐term safety of GLP‐1 RAs. The large diverse dataset improves generalizability across different clinical settings. Furthermore, it offers important insights into the relationship between GLP‐1 RAs and gallbladder disease in clinical practice. Propensity score matching reduces confounding by measured variables, and the large sample size ensures adequate statistical power. Longitudinal follow‐up enabled multiple analyses at several time points and a more robust risk assessment.

In summary, among adults with T2DM, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with higher rates of cholelithiasis, choledocholithiasis, cholecystitis, and cholecystectomy compared with matched controls, whereas no significant differences were observed in pancreatitis or ERCP. Given the low absolute event rates and the observational nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the results are clinically reassuring, supporting the favorable safety profile of GLP‐1 RAs with respect to severe biliary complications, while highlighting the importance of clinical vigilance for biliary symptoms during therapy.
