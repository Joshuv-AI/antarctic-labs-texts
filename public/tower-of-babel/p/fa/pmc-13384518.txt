Social determinants of health and stroke care and outcomes: SEQUINS lecture 2025
Authors: Moira K Kapral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe past decades have brought significant advances in stroke care through new therapies and coordinated stroke systems. However, these advances have not been experienced equally across all populations. Social determinants of health, defined as the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age … are associated with variations and inequities in stroke incidence, care processes, and outcomes. This paper provides an overview of the effect of factors such as sex and gender, socioeconomic status, rural residence, race and ethnicity, and immigration status on stroke care and outcomes, highlights structural drivers, and outlines directions for the future.Keywords: Social determinants of health stroke

Introduction

The Society for Equity Neuroscience (SEQUINS), founded in 2024, aims to “enhance brain health around the world by improving equitable nervous system care and outcomes through research ”. This paper summarizes key points from a lecture delivered at the 2025 SEQUINS Annual Meeting on the topic of social determinants of health and their impact on stroke care and outcomes.

Background

Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, with an estimated cost of over US$700 billion [1]. The late 1990s and early 2000s brought major breakthroughs in stroke therapy, with randomized controlled trials demonstrating the effectiveness of antithrombotic therapy, thrombolysis, endarterectomy, stroke unit care, thrombectomy, and other interventions [2, 3]. This era also saw the creation of organized stroke systems to coordinate care across regions, and of large clinical registries to measure and monitor the quality of stroke care [4]. In the United States, the AHA/ASA Get With The Guidelines–Stroke (GWTG-Stroke) registry enrolled over 8 million patients from over 2500 hospitals over two decades, and demonstrated sustained gains in acute treatment and secondary prevention performance measures; in Canada, the implementation of stroke systems of care was associated with improvements in care and outcomes such as mortality [5, 6].

Despite these advances on a population level, inequities in health and variations in stroke incidence, care, and outcomes have been documented based on social determinants of health, defined as “the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age … shaped by the distribution of money, power and resources ” [7]. Social determinants of health include but are not limited to socioeconomic status, health care access based on geography and other factors, neighbourhood and the built environment, gender, racism, and immigration status [8]. These factors are increasingly being recognized as necessary targets for interventions to improve stroke outcomes at a population level. As noted in an American Heart Association Scientific Statement, “the most significant opportunities for reducing death and disability from CVD … lie with addressing the social determinants of cardiovascular outcomes ” [8].

This paper provides a brief overview of the impact of selected social determinants of health on stroke care and outcomes. It is not intended as a comprehensive or systematic review but rather to provide examples that highlight work in this field and provide ideas for future research and directions.

Socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic status (or socioeconomic position) may be variably defined as income, wealth, occupation, education, housing status, health insurance status, geographic location, or aggregate measures that incorporate multiple dimensions into a composite score. It may also be measured at either the individual level, which captures personal resources, or the area level, which captures neighborhood resources and contextual influences [9]. Each of these measures has its own strengths and limitations; for example, income level may not accurately reflect the status of students or retired individuals, and occupation type may not reflect income or education.

Socioeconomic gradients in overall health are well documented; Marmot’s work in England demonstrated striking life expectancy differences by neighborhood deprivation, highlighting the social gradient across the lifespan [10]. In the context of stroke, most of the published literature has focused on measures of education, income, occupation, and area-based proxies such as neighborhood deprivation [11, 12]. Based on any of these measures, there are consistent associations between lower socioeconomic status and greater stroke incidence, lower quality of care, greater disability, and higher case-fatality [11–19].

Potential mechanisms for these associations are multifactorial and operate through interrelated pathways that may act before disease on-set, at presentation, during care, and after treatment. Lower socioeconomic status is associated with a higher prevalence of risk factors such as hypertension, obesity, hyperlipidemia, smoking, and exposure to air pollution [20]. A higher prevalence of psychosocial stress may further accelerate cardiovascular disease through inflammatory pathways [21, 22]. Lower socioeconomic status has also been associated with delays in presentation and greater stroke severity, which are in turn associated with worse outcomes, although findings are not consistent across all studies [23–25]. Post-stroke outcomes are further shaped by disparities in access to acute treatments, secondary prevention care, and rehabilitation services, and community-level features that affect cardiovascular health such as neighborhood resources and walkability [12, 14]. Addressing these mechanisms requires both upstream policies (for example, reducing poverty, improving education, ensuring equitable healthcare access) and downstream interventions (for example, enhancing stroke awareness).

Rural residence and geography

North American rural residents, compared to urban ones, have a higher prevalence of vascular risk factors such as smoking and obesity, are less likely to be screened and controlled for diabetes and hyperlipidemia, and experience higher rates of incident stroke and mortality [26–28]. Rural patients are less likely than urban ones to receive hyperacute interventions and more likely to experience delays because of geography, fewer stroke centers, longer EMS transport times, lower availability of specialists, and transfer logistics [29–32]. This is especially relevant to mechanical thrombectomy, which is highly time-dependent and geographically concentrated [33–35]. Finally, rural residents may face reduced access to rehabilitation services and stroke secondary prevention clinics, although other aspects of secondary prevention care are similar [31, 32, 36]. Collectively, this literature suggests that rural–urban stroke disparities arise from the combined effects of higher baseline risk, delayed or limited acute care, and constrained recovery environments. Beyond optimizing transport and hub-and-spoke configurations for EVT, rural stroke equity requires augmenting primary care, prevention, telemedicine, and community resources, and addressing nonmedical determinants such as transportation, broadband, and socioeconomic opportunities [37].

Sex and gender

Although sex is a biologic variable rather than a social determinant, sex and gender are frequently studied together or not distinguished from each other in the existing literature. Across the life course, women experience stroke differently than men, reflecting the interplay of biological sex, gendered social roles, and structural determinants of health [38, 39]. Although men have a higher incidence of stroke, women experience a greater absolute number of strokes and account for a higher proportion of stroke-related deaths [39, 40]. This difference is influenced by demographic factors such as age, as women tend to be older at the time of stroke and often have lower pre-stroke functional status [41, 42]. These factors, together with an increased prevalence of comorbid conditions such as atrial fibrillation and dementia in women, influence eligibility for acute therapies as well as recovery trajectories. Of note, some studies suggest that stroke incidence is higher in younger women compared to younger men, although the findings are not consistent [43–45]. Although stroke severity appears to be higher in women compared to men, this difference is attenuated after adjustment for age and no longer significant after additional adjustment for pre-morbid functional status [46, 47].

Women face unique stroke risk factors, including pregnancy-related complications such as pre-eclampsia, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, and pre-term delivery, which increase long-term cardiovascular risk [48–53]. They are also likely to serve as caregivers for stroke survivors, a role that can lead to significant social and economic burdens [54–56]. While stroke symptoms are broadly similar across sexes, women may present with non-focal symptoms more frequently, which can complicate diagnosis and lead to non-stroke diagnoses and missed treatment opportunities [57–59]. Women appear to be less likely than men to receive anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation, screening and treatment for hyperlipidemia, prompt neuroimaging, stroke unit care, or thrombolysis, but are more likely to undergo endovascular therapy [39, 60]. The findings vary depending on the study population and methodology, with some studies finding minimal differences in therapy after adjustment for age and other factors.

Outcomes after stroke are generally worse for women, with higher rates of disability, depression, care needs, and admission to long-term care facilities, and poorer health-related quality of life [61–64]. Importantly, however, these differences are largely explained by age, comorbidities, and social support gaps rather than inherent biological factors [62, 63, 65]. Although one study found that case fatality was higher in midlife women compared to men [66], overall stroke case fatality tends to be similar in women and men after adjustment for age [46]. A pragmatic implication is to focus less on sex differences as endpoints, and more on modifiable targets to reduce stroke incidence and severity (e.g. through management of atrial fibrillation and sex-specific risk factors), improve pre-morbid functional status, address social supports and care needs for women, and improve functional recovery and quality of life for women [67, 68]. Research gaps persist, as women remain underrepresented in clinical trials and sex-specific analyses are often lacking [69–72]. The Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines can be used to ensure appropriate reporting of sex and gender information in manuscripts [69].

Race and ethnicity

Substantial racial and ethnic disparities in stroke incidence, care, and outcomes have been observed in the United States and in other high-income countries. In the United States, for example, Black individuals experience the highest stroke mortality of any racial or ethnic group [73]. In Canada, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, Indigenous populations have a higher incidence of stroke than non-Indigenous populations, while in the United Kingdom, stroke incidence is higher among Black and South Asian individuals than among White individuals [74–78].

Historically, these inequities were sometimes solely attributed to innate biological differences or individual behaviors, treating race as a biological variable rather than a social construct [79]. Some biological differences in stroke risk factors and mechanisms do exist: hypertension is more common in Black populations, contributing to earlier stroke on-set, small vessel disease, and intracerebral hemorrhage; diabetes and metabolic syndrome are more common in Hispanic and South Asian populations, contributing to intracranial atherosclerosis; extracranial carotid stenosis and cardioembolic stroke are more common in White populations, possibly related to older age and increased detection of atrial fibrillation in this group [80–82]. However, it is also important to recognize that the excess burden of vascular risk factors, stroke, and stroke-related mortality in Black and Indigenous communities is in part the downstream consequence of policies, laws, and institutional practices that have produced unequal access to economic opportunity, education, health care, and other social resources [83, 84]. Accordingly, racial health inequities in stroke should be seen as driven not by race itself but by structural racism, underscoring the need to interpret observed disparities within this broader context [79, 85].

Immigration status

Over 300 million people worldwide are considered international migrants, whose health risks and needs may differ from host populations [86]. Large administrative-data cohorts from Canada and Europe, as well as systematic reviews and meta-analyses, demonstrate a 20–35% lower risk of incident stroke or TIA among immigrants overall [87]. This “healthy immigrant effect ” is not uniform: immigrants from some regions (parts of the Caribbean, Latin America, and sub-Saharan Africa) show attenuated or absent protection, and heterogeneity across studies is substantial [88]. In addition, the apparent healthy immigrant effect can erode with greater time spent in the host country. Work using the “proportion of life spent in Canada ” as a proxy for acculturation found that stroke incidence and outcomes vary with that exposure, consistent with convergence toward host-population risk over time [89].

Evidence for stroke mortality is less consistent; while overall stroke-related mortality among immigrants is often similar to that of host populations, lower incidence does not reliably translate into lower mortality [87]. In ischemic stroke survivors, mortality differences between immigrants and long-term residents attenuate after adjustment, with effect modification by age and ethnicity, pointing to complex interactions among baseline risk, care access, and social context over time [90].

In a Danish study, immigrants were less likely than Danish-born residents to receive stroke unit care, dysphagia screening, physiotherapy, and occupational therapy [91]. In a Canadian study of people with acute ischemic stroke or TIA, process measures were broadly similar between immigrants and long-term residents: immigrants with ischemic stroke were more likely to receive thrombolysis yet had a higher adjusted risk of disability at discharge [92]. These findings raise the possibility of residual differences in stroke severity, pre-stroke health, language or cultural barriers affecting presentation and rehabilitation, or unmeasured social determinants influencing recovery.

Importantly, most studies define immigration status by country of birth rather than legal or residency category, limiting inference about refugees, asylum seekers, or undocumented populations. Overall, the literature supports lower stroke incidence among immigrants in many settings, while highlighting important heterogeneity, incomplete characterization of immigration status, and gaps in understanding long-term functional outcomes and post-acute recovery.

Intersectionality

Social determinants of health do not occur in isolation, and intersectionality theory provides a framework for understanding how multiple intersecting social identities interact to shape health experiences and outcomes [93, 94]. For example, Black patients in rural areas of the US receive reperfusion therapy at lower rates than both White patients and urban Black patients, illustrating how race and geography interact to amplify disparities in care, and suggesting that interventions must address barriers related to both race and rurality[95]. As noted earlier, the risk of stroke in immigrants varies with country of origin, indicating that prevention efforts can be targeted towards the highest risk communities. Intersectionality theory can thus guide research questions that disentangle how various factors interact to produce health disparities and help us develop tailored solutions to address them.

Conclusions and next steps

Social determinants of health exert a pervasive influence on stroke risk, care, and outcomes across the life course. The health effects from these factors may be multiplicative, reflecting people’s intersecting social identities [96]. Explanations for the impact of social determinants of health on stroke outcomes are multifactorial, including unequal exposure to chronic stress, adverse built and food environments, environmental pollutants, and differential access to care.

Future research should continue to identify and quantify disparities in care based on social determinants of health, with priority given to studies that seek to understand the underlying causes and clarify how multiple adverse social and structural factors interact to elevate risk and hinder recovery. Studies should also focus on identifying and testing health system interventions to address inequities across the continuum of care, from prevention to acute care to long-term recovery. For example, interventions that improve timely access to stroke treatment, enhance community-based rehabilitation, and strengthen caregiver and social support networks are needed. In the long term, a population health equity framework can be used to shift attention beyond individual risk factors and behaviors to the broader policy, system, and resource-allocation decisions that systematically confer advantage on some groups while producing avoidable and unjust health inequities in others [97]. Achieving meaningful improvement will require research and actions that extend beyond healthcare settings and the individual patient to policies and structural reforms that address population-level systems and environmental factors [98].
