Chilaiditi Syndrome: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Maryam Amanahou, Zakaria Abide, Abdennasser El Kharras, Hicham Naji Amrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Chilaiditi sign refers to the interposition of a bowel segment, most commonly the colon, between the liver and the right hemidiaphragm. The term Chilaiditi syndrome is reserved for cases in which this radiographic finding is accompanied by clinical manifestations that can reasonably be attributed to it. Respiratory or thoracic presentations are uncommon. We report the case of an 80-year-old woman with hypertension and knee osteoarthritis who presented with a persistent dry cough, right-sided lower chest pain, and progressive fatigue, without active gastrointestinal symptoms at presentation. The pleuropulmonary examination was unremarkable, oxygen saturation was 97% on room air, and laboratory testing showed no significant abnormality, with a C-reactive protein level of 3 mg/L. Chest radiography demonstrated apparent elevation of the right hemidiaphragm and a right subdiaphragmatic lucency containing colonic haustral markings. Contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal computed tomography confirmed interposition of the hepatic flexure and transverse colon between the liver and the right hemidiaphragm, without digestive complications or associated pleuropulmonary abnormalities. CT therefore confirmed an uncomplicated Chilaiditi sign. In the absence of another thoracic abnormality that could explain the symptoms and given their complete resolution following conservative treatment, Chilaiditi syndrome was considered the most likely diagnosis. However, a causal relationship with the cough could not be established with certainty, particularly because pulmonary function testing was not performed. The Chilaiditi sign should be considered when a right subdiaphragmatic lucency containing colonic haustral markings is identified. CT confirms bowel interposition, assesses for complications, and helps exclude the main radiographic differential diagnoses. In uncomplicated cases, management is conservative, and clinical follow-up with reassessment if symptoms recur is appropriate.

Introduction

The Chilaiditi sign refers to the temporary or permanent interposition of a bowel segment, most commonly the colon, between the liver and the right hemidiaphragm. This radiographic abnormality was first described in 1910 by the radiologist Demetrius Chilaiditi, who reported three cases of bowel interposition between the liver and the diaphragm [1]. Chilaiditi sign, which is usually asymptomatic and incidentally detected, should be distinguished from Chilaiditi syndrome, in which this anatomical finding is associated with clinical manifestations that can reasonably be attributed to it [2,3].

The prevalence of the Chilaiditi sign is estimated to range from 0.025% to 0.28% on conventional chest and abdominal radiographs and appears to be higher on computed tomography [2,4]. Its prevalence increases with age. A male predominance is classically reported, with a male-to-female ratio of up to 4:1 [2,3,5]. Predisposing factors include dolichocolon, megacolon, chronic constipation, colonic distension, laxity of the suspensory ligaments, reduced liver volume, and certain diaphragmatic abnormalities [2,4,6].

The manifestations of Chilaiditi syndrome are most commonly gastrointestinal and include abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation, and abdominal distension [2,5]. Predominantly respiratory or thoracic presentations, including dyspnea, respiratory distress, chest pain, and cough-predominant presentations, are relatively uncommon [7-9]. Such cases may be diagnostically challenging, particularly when active gastrointestinal symptoms are absent.

The major clinical significance of this condition lies in its differential diagnosis with pneumoperitoneum, a surgical emergency with which it may be confused, potentially leading to unnecessary surgical intervention [3,10]. Recognition of the Chilaiditi sign is also important in patients undergoing percutaneous liver biopsy because of the risk of bowel perforation [4].

Case presentation

An 80-year-old woman was referred to the pulmonology clinic for a persistent dry cough that had been present for five weeks and was associated with right-sided lower chest pain and progressively worsening fatigue.

Her medical history included longstanding hypertension, which was well controlled with a calcium channel blocker, and bilateral knee osteoarthritis under rheumatology follow-up. She was not taking an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor. She denied heartburn, acid regurgitation, rhinitis, rhinorrhea, and postnasal drip. Her obstetric history included four pregnancies and four deliveries (G4P4). She reported no history of thoracic or abdominal surgery, known chronic respiratory disease, active smoking, or significant secondhand-smoke exposure. There was no history of liver disease or significant recent weight loss. Although the patient had no formally diagnosed chronic functional bowel disorder, she reported a previous history of constipation and abdominal bloating. Neither symptom was present at the time of presentation.

The symptoms developed insidiously. The cough was dry, nonproductive, predominantly daytime, and not accompanied by hemoptysis or sputum production. She described the right-sided lower chest pain as a sensation of heaviness that was unrelated to breathing, did not radiate, worsened in the supine position, and was partially relieved by sitting. Progressive fatigue accompanied the symptoms and prompted medical consultation.

On examination, the patient was in good general condition, alert, oriented, and afebrile, with a temperature of 37.1°C. Blood pressure was 135/80 mmHg, heart rate was 76 beats/minute, and oxygen saturation was 97% on room air. Her body mass index was 32 kg/m².

The pleuropulmonary examination was unremarkable. Breath sounds were symmetrical and clearly audible in both lung fields, without crackles, wheezing, or pleural rub. Percussion was normal. Cardiovascular examination was unremarkable. The abdomen was soft, nondistended, and nontender, without palpable organomegaly. There were no signs of right- or left-sided heart failure.

Laboratory testing showed no significant abnormality, including a C-reactive protein level of 3 mg/L (Table 1).

The frontal chest radiograph, obtained with the patient standing, showed apparent elevation of the right hemidiaphragm associated with a right subdiaphragmatic lucency projected over the lower thoracic zone (Figure 1).

The presence of colonic haustral markings within this image raised suspicion of hepatodiaphragmatic colonic interposition. The main differential diagnoses included a subpulmonic pleural effusion, right lower-lobe atelectasis, diaphragmatic eventration or hernia, and a subphrenic collection.

Contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal CT was performed to confirm the gastrointestinal origin of the radiographic finding and to assess for complications. It demonstrated the interposition of the hepatic flexure and transverse colon between the right hepatic lobe and the right hemidiaphragm (Figure 2). There was no evidence of bowel obstruction, volvulus, mesenteric ischemia, or perforation. The lung parenchyma showed no consolidation, pulmonary nodules, or infiltrative abnormalities. No pleural effusion or mediastinal abnormality was identified. CT confirmed an uncomplicated Chilaiditi sign. Chilaiditi syndrome was considered the most likely clinical diagnosis based on the association between the hepatodiaphragmatic colonic interposition and the patient’s symptoms, in the absence of another identifiable thoracic abnormality. However, the causal relationship between the anatomical finding and the respiratory symptoms remained presumptive.

Given the patient’s clinical stability, normal oxygen saturation, unremarkable cardiopulmonary examination, and the absence of pleuropulmonary abnormalities or complications, such as obstruction, volvulus, ischemia, or perforation on computed tomography, conservative management was considered appropriate. No additional respiratory testing was performed at that time.

Conservative treatment was initiated, including adequate hydration, increased dietary fiber, and an osmotic laxative.

At the one-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of the dry cough, right-sided lower chest pain, and fatigue. No routine radiological follow-up was scheduled, and she was advised to seek reassessment if symptoms recurred.

Discussion

This case illustrates an uncommon respiratory presentation of Chilaiditi syndrome in an older woman without active gastrointestinal symptoms at presentation. Several aspects warrant discussion.

Unusual clinical presentation

The most commonly reported manifestations of Chilaiditi syndrome are gastrointestinal and include abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation, and abdominal distension [2,5]. Respiratory or thoracic symptoms, such as dyspnea, chest pain, and respiratory distress, have been described but remain uncommon [7-9,11,12]. In the present case, the presentation was dominated by dry cough and right-sided lower chest pain, without active gastrointestinal complaints at presentation.

The mechanism linking colonic interposition to thoracic symptoms remains uncertain. Mechanical elevation of the hemidiaphragm or diaphragmatic irritation may contribute to pain and cough. However, no pulmonary function testing or specific assessment of diaphragmatic function was performed; therefore, a mechanical or restrictive effect could not be objectively demonstrated.

Chilaiditi sign has also been reported during the evaluation of a patient presenting with cough; however, concomitant chronic respiratory disease made it difficult to attribute the symptom directly to colonic interposition [13].

Predisposing factors in this patient

Advanced age and a previous history of constipation and abdominal bloating were considered possible predisposing factors in this patient. Aging may be associated with laxity of ligamentous structures and changes in abdominal anatomical relationships that favor bowel interposition [2,4,6]. Chronic or recurrent constipation may contribute to colonic elongation or distension [2]. The patient was also multiparous (G4P4); however, the exact contribution of multiparity to the development of the Chilaiditi sign remains insufficiently documented and cannot be established in this case.

Diagnostic approach

In an older patient presenting with a persistent dry cough and right-sided lower chest pain, the main differential diagnoses included infectious pneumonia, pleural disease, basal atelectasis, bronchopulmonary disease, a diaphragmatic disorder, and a cardiovascular cause. The unremarkable pleuropulmonary examination, normal oxygen saturation on room air, and normal laboratory results, including a C-reactive protein level of 3 mg/L, made an acute infectious or inflammatory process less likely, although they did not formally exclude it.

Chest radiography represented the diagnostic turning point. The presence of a right subdiaphragmatic lucency containing colonic haustral markings suggested colonic interposition. The radiological differential diagnoses included a subpulmonic pleural effusion, basal atelectasis, diaphragmatic eventration or hernia, and a subphrenic collection. Pneumoperitoneum is the main differential diagnosis when the finding appears as subdiaphragmatic free air [3,10].

Thoracoabdominal CT is the reference examination when radiographic findings are equivocal [4]. It precisely identifies the interposed bowel segment, evaluates the lung parenchyma, detects complications such as volvulus, ischemia, or perforation, and helps exclude the main radiographic differential diagnoses [4,10].

Differential diagnoses

In this case, the main differential diagnoses were a subpulmonic pleural effusion, right lower-lobe atelectasis, diaphragmatic eventration or right phrenic nerve palsy, diaphragmatic hernia, and a subphrenic collection. CT did not support the diagnoses of subpulmonic pleural effusion, basal atelectasis, diaphragmatic hernia, or subphrenic collection and confirmed the colonic origin of the radiographic finding. No specific dynamic assessment of diaphragmatic function was performed.

Management

Management of uncomplicated Chilaiditi syndrome is generally conservative [2,4,5]. It may include hydration, dietary measures, laxatives or enemas, and nasogastric decompression in patients with marked distension. Treatment should be tailored to the symptoms and radiological findings. In this patient, adequate hydration, increased dietary fiber, and an osmotic laxative were prescribed.

Surgical intervention is indicated only in the presence of complications, including volvulus, mesenteric ischemia, perforation, or intestinal obstruction that does not respond to conservative treatment [2,5]. Reported procedures include colopexy, hepatopexy, and colonic resection, with increasing use of minimally invasive approaches such as laparoscopy and robot-assisted surgery [2,5,14].

Limitations

No pulmonary function testing or specific assessment of diaphragmatic function was performed. Although the patient was not taking an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and denied symptoms suggestive of gastroesophageal reflux or upper airway cough syndrome, a systematic evaluation of all alternative causes of the subacute cough was not performed. Therefore, the relationship between the respiratory symptoms and Chilaiditi syndrome remains presumptive. Pulmonary function testing and targeted evaluation for other causes of cough would have been appropriate if the symptoms had persisted or recurred.

Conclusions

The Chilaiditi sign is an uncommon radiological finding characterized by the interposition of the colon between the liver and the right hemidiaphragm. It should be considered when a right subdiaphragmatic lucency containing haustral markings is identified on chest radiography, and computed tomography can confirm the anatomical interposition and exclude complications. In the present case, Chilaiditi syndrome was considered on the basis of the associated clinical presentation and the absence of another identifiable thoracic abnormality. Although complete symptom resolution following conservative management supports a possible association, it does not establish a definitive causal relationship between the colonic interposition and the respiratory symptoms. Careful clinical and radiological assessment is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary intervention.
