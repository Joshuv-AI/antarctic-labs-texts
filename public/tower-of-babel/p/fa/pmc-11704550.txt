This is for the RESILIA tissue (Edwards Lifesciences) and the Avalus valve in the PERIGON trial. And those are trials that are large, and we learn a lot from them as these are now the seventh-year outcomes. And the DEDICATE trial (Randomized, Multicenter, Event-Driven Trial of TAVI versus SAVR in Patients with Symptomatic Severe Aortic-Valve Stenosis) is now 1 year, so that takes a while. In the PERIGON trial, the percentage of patients with aortic regurgitation (AR) mixed was about half the COMMENCE trial. So, it's a bit different. I assume, going back to the previous discussion, that many of these patients actually had bicuspid valves. And so that's what I want to go back to. Is this really the same outcome for bicuspid patients? As there was less concomitant surgery, I assume they have less coronary artery disease. Still, they have the same outcomes. So, do you think that bicuspid problems are intervening in these outcomes?

Dr Vinod Thourani(Atlanta, Ga). Yeah, that was a good question. We did not break out into the bicuspid valve phenotype. That's something we probably should look into. But we have not broken out into the bicuspid versus not. But a lot of these patients, like you said, are younger, so it'd be interesting to look at. Obviously, the number—the power—is low. So, I'm not sure that we'll be able to break those out and have a meaningful result. Even if there are differences, I think the statistics on that would be relatively difficult to determine differences.

Dr Klautz. One of the problems in defining bicuspid with echocardiogram is it's difficult to see. On computed tomography (CT), it's much better to see. But a small raphe is sometimes very difficult to see on echocardiogram.

Dr Thourani. No, I agree. And we did not prescribe a CT like we did in all the transcatheter aortic valve replacement trials. And again, in the next trials that we design looking at surgical valves, we need a mandated CT in those patients so we can answer some of the questions you're asking.

Dr Klautz. Absolutely agree. Do you think the guidelines are adequate for mixed problems in valves? AR, aortic stenosis (AS), combinations, aren't they more malignant for the ventricle than we think they are?

Dr Thourani. So, it's interesting. When you go back and look at the guidelines and what Robert is talking about for those of you who've been hiding in a cave for the last couple of years, written by Catherine Otto, they have basically 1 half of a column on mixed aortic valve disease. And they say treat it like AS because they said, “There's no data to change that.” Which actually is probably not correct, quite honestly. And if you look at the pressures that a ventricle has from AR, and then you look at the pressures that the ventricle has on AS, they're radically different. And so, what we're finding—and so nobody has really looked at that carefully and I think that we probably need to combine some of our surgical data so that we can have a larger pool of patients to advise the guidelines. But the guidelines are absolutely clueless on what to do because we haven't provided them with data on it.

Dr Klautz. My final observation was that there is a very small trend to an increased gradient over time. Do you think that's typical for pericardial valves that they stiffen over time or is that just an error?

Dr Thourani. I don't know the right answer. Even if you look at the transcatheter aortic valve replacement trials, they're all seeing that gradient of maybe a millimeter going up in a year. We haven't had great adjudicated data after 5 to 7 years. And unfortunately, most of the articles are a reintervention, not echocardiogram-based. But I think we'll continue to see if pericardial for the PERIGON and everything else we'll see that 1- to 2-mm gradient go up—mean gradient goes up every year. As long as it stays under 20 for me, I'd stay pretty happy about that. So, it'll be interesting to see how we follow these patients for at least out to 10 years. And I know the COMMENCE trial—we have a commitment from Edwards I think to follow these patients for 10 years to kind of look at that data.

Dr Klautz. Well, thank you for your answer.

Dr Thourani. Thanks, Robert.
