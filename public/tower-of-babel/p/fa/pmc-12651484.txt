Impact of a nudge-based intervention on hand soap usage in a Japanese Hospital: interrupted time series analysis
Authors: Tetsuji Morishita, Masayoshi Monji, Masao Kuwabara, Shin Lee, Makoto Hayashi, Hisashi Tsurumi, Hidetoshi Matsunami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of nudge-based messages on hand soap usage in a Japanese hospital setting.MethodsInterrupted time series analysis was conducted using monthly hand soap usage data from April 2022 to March 2024. The following nudge messages were placed in restrooms: “Are your neighbors washing their hands with soap?” (men) and “Water does not disinfect; soap works!” (women). A generalized least squares model with harmonic terms was used to analyze the data, accounting for seasonality and autocorrelation.ResultsThe intervention was associated with an immediate increase of 147.85 units/month in hand soap usage (95% confidence interval [CI]: 61.87–233.83; P = 0.003). However, usage gradually decreased by 11.47 units/month thereafter (95% CI: –27.10 to 4.15; P = 0.166). Sensitivity analysis using Poisson regression confirmed the robustness of these findings. The intervention was associated with a 38.5% increase in hand soap usage (incidence rate ratio [IRR] = 1.385, 95% CI: 1.265–1.515, P < 0.001), but there was a subsequent 1.9% monthly decrease (IRR = 0.981, 95% CI: 0.965–0.997, P = 0.019).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that nudge-based interventions can be effective in promoting hand hygiene in Japanese health-care settings, but the effect may wane over time.Keywords: Hand hygiene, Nudge, Behavioral economics, Interrupted time series analysis, Hospital, Infection control

Introduction

Health-care-associated infections pose a significant threat to patient safety and contribute to increased morbidity, mortality, and health-care costs [1]. However, achieving consistent adherence to hand hygiene guidelines remains a substantial challenge.

Behavioral economics offers insights into human decision-making processes and provides a framework for designing interventions that “nudge” individuals toward desired behaviors without restricting their freedom of choice [2]. Nudge interventions have been successfully applied in various health-care settings to promote medication adherence, vaccination uptake, and healthy lifestyle choices [[2], [3], [4]].

Several studies have investigated the use of nudge interventions to improve hand soap usage [[5], [6], [7]]. For example, a study conducted in highway service areas in the UK found that simple messages placed in restrooms increased hand soap usage by 11%–12% [8]. The most effective messages were “Are your neighbors washing their hands with soap?” for men and “Water does not disinfect; soap works!” for women.

Despite the accumulating evidence supporting the effectiveness of nudge interventions, limited research has been conducted in Japanese health-care settings, where cultural and linguistic differences may influence the impact of such interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of nudge-based messages on hand soap usage in a Japanese hospital. We hypothesized that the intervention would lead to an immediate increase in hand soap usage, but that the effect would diminish over time.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a retrospective interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) to assess the impact of a nudge-based intervention on hand soap usage. ITSA is a quasi-experimental method that enables the evaluation of the effects of an intervention by examining changes in a time series before and after an intervention [9,10].

Setting

The study was conducted at Matsunami General Hospital, a community hospital in Gifu, Japan. Matsunami General Hospital is a private, regional core hospital located in Kasamatsu-cho, Gifu Prefecture, with a total of 501 beds. In the 2023 fiscal year, it accommodated a monthly average of approximately 20,000 outpatients, and it functions as a core medical institution in the region with 29 clinical departments. The hospital also employs approximately 1,400 staff members. Furthermore, our hospital is designated as a community-based hospital and a disaster base hospital.

Intervention

While point-of-care hand hygiene is crucial for healthcare providers, restrooms are frequently used by all hospital users—including staff, patients, and visitors—and are an ideal setting to test a broad-reaching public health intervention. The WHO and UNICEF documents Water, sanitation and hygiene in health care facilities and WASH FIT explicitly state that handwashing facilities in restrooms are an important component of a hospital's WASH program and are essential for comprehensive infection control for all facility users. This approach is aimed at improving infection control from a broader public health perspective, not just within the clinical care setting. Our study demonstrates the important role of the restroom environment in infection control, supported by previous research [11,12] which showed that nudges can improve public handwashing behavior. The intervention consisted of placing posters with nudge-based messages in restrooms throughout the hospital. The messages were adapted from a previous study conducted in the UK [8] and translated into Japanese. The messages were as follows:

Men's restrooms: “Are your neighbors washing their hands with soap?”

Women's restrooms: “Water does not disinfect; soap works!”

The posters were displayed for 9 months, from July 2023 to March 2024.

The messages were designed to align with a nudge, defined as an intervention that steers individuals toward a desired behavior without restricting their freedom of choice [2,13]. Our study, consistent with prior research [12] that has successfully used nudges to target handwashing, demonstrates that handwashing is a behavioral target that responds to such interventions. Our posters fit this definition because they do not force hand washing but subtly encourage it by appealing to social norms and awareness. Furthermore, the nudge was designed in line with the principles of the EAST checklist: Easy, Attractive, Social, and Timely.

The messages were chosen because they effectively targeted key behavioral domains identified in previous studies. Specifically, the male-oriented message, “Are your neighbors washing their hands with soap?”, leverages the social norms domain, while the female-oriented message, “Water does not disinfect; soap works!”, addresses the information provision domain. The effectiveness of nudges is known to vary by culture and context, particularly for nudges based on social norms, which can be influenced by cultural differences in behavior. Therefore, validating the effects of messages used in prior research within a Japanese cultural context is an important contribution to the field of nudge studies. Our study is based on the Intervention stage of the OECD's BASIC framework and the Social element of the UK Behavioral Insights Team's EAST checklist. Our study aimed to test the effectiveness of these messages in a different cultural and linguistic context, specifically a Japanese hospital.

Data collection

The intervention was deployed by placing the posters in all restrooms across the hospital. This ensured that the intervention was applicable to all user groups, including staff, outpatients, and inpatients.

Monthly hand soap usage data were collected from April 2022 to March 2024. The hand soap usage data was collected from the hospital's central inventory management system, which tracks dispensed bottles for use in public areas such as restrooms, not for point-of-care use. In this study, one unit of hand soap usage corresponds to one bottle of hand soap dispensed.

Statistical analysis

ITSA was performed using a generalized least squares (GLS) model to account for autocorrelation and seasonality. The following regression model was used:

where Yt is the monthly hand soap usage at time t, β0 is the intercept, β1 is the coefficient for time (trend before intervention), T is time (month), β2 is the coefficient for level change (immediate effect of intervention), Xlevel is the dummy variable indicating the intervention period (0 before, 1 after), β3 is the coefficient for trend change (change in trend after intervention), Xtrend is the time since intervention, harmonic(month,2,12) represents the harmonic terms to account for seasonality, and εt is an error term.

The model included harmonic terms to account for seasonality. A seasonality variable was also included in the model to account for typical fluctuations in soap usage that occur throughout the year, such as those related to seasonal illnesses or changes in hospital occupancy. Autocorrelation was assessed using the Durbin–Watson test supplemented by visual inspection of autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation function plots. An autoregressive moving average model structure was selected based on these diagnostic evaluations. Sensitivity analysis was performed using Poisson regression to confirm the robustness of the findings [9,10]. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Matsunami General Hospital ethics committee [No. 635]. As this was a retrospective study using aggregated data, informed consent was waived. The opt-out mechanism was a passive process. We posted a notice on the hospital's website informing individuals about the study and giving them the option to object to their aggregated data being used. This is a common ethical practice for retrospective studies using de-identified, aggregated data.

Results

ITS analysis of changes in hand soap usage before and after the nudge-based intervention

The GLS model revealed a significant immediate increase in hand soap usage following the intervention for the first month (Figure 1). The level change was 147.85 units/month (95% confidence interval [CI]: 61.87–233.83; P = 0.0032). However, the trend change was negative, indicating a gradual decline in hand soap usage after the intervention (–11.47 units/month; 95% CI: –27.10 to 4.15; P = 0.1664). Following the initial increase, hand soap usage showed a gradual decline over time, with the decline becoming clearly observable from October 2023.

ITS analysis of changes in hand soap use before and after a nudge-based intervention. Hand soap usage per month is plotted on the y-axis, and the date (year and month) is shown on the x-axis. Pink dots show the hand soap usage in units per month. Solid lines show the predicted trends in hand soap usage before (red) and after (blue) the intervention based on the seasonally adjusted regression model. Dashed lines represent the predicted values without the intervention (red) and with the intervention (blue). The vertical dashed line denotes when the intervention was implemented (July 2023).

Sensitivity analysis using Poisson regression confirmed these findings. The intervention was associated with a 38.5% increase in hand soap usage (incidence rate ratio [IRR] = 1.385, 95% CI: 1.265–1.515, P < 0.001); however, this was followed by a 1.9% monthly decrease (IRR = 0.981, 95% CI: 0.965–0.997, P = 0.019).

Discussion

The results of the present study indicated that nudge-based messages in restrooms in a Japanese hospital were effective in increasing hand soap usage. The immediate increase in hand soap usage suggests that the messages successfully raised awareness of hand hygiene and prompted behavior change.

The observed decline in hand soap usage may be attributed to habituation. While we observed a large initial effect (38.5% increase), the subsequent decline in hand soap usage is consistent with the principle of habituation and the novelty effect, which are well-documented in the behavioral science literature. The need for developing nudges that have long-term and stable effects, highlighting that the decay of a nudge's impact is a recognized challenge. A similar pattern was observed in a study on the popular game Pokémon GO, where a large initial increase in physical activity returned to baseline over a few weeks [14]. This phenomenon suggests that as the posters became a familiar part of the restroom environment, their influence gradually waned. This finding is further supported by studies on hand hygiene compliance in clinical settings, where continuous feedback or real-time monitoring was required to maintain high compliance rates [15]. Our results thus provide a compelling real-world example of how a static nudge can effectively trigger a behavior change initially, but that this effect can decay over time without dynamic reinforcement. This points to a need for future interventions that incorporate elements of a “booster nudge” or continuously evolving messages to sustain the behavioral change.

The findings of this study are consistent with the UK study conducted in highway service areas [8], which also found that simple messages in restrooms increased hand soap usage. However, the magnitude and duration of the effect may vary depending on the context and population. Furthermore, the hospital setting of our study, which includes a large number of healthcare professionals, is a potential factor contributing to the greater effect. Healthcare professionals, who generally have a higher awareness of health and hygiene and a strong sense of altruism as part of their profession, may have responded more strongly to the social norms and information-based nudges than a general population.

We selected product volume measurement to evaluate hand hygiene compliance, which has a number of key advantages. This method has the advantage of avoiding the “Hawthorne effect” that can occur with direct observation and allows for continuous monitoring of people's behavior. It also fully protects the privacy of participants, as it uses aggregate data without identifying individual actions. On the other hand, we recognize that this method has certain limitations, such as the inability to track how many times a user washed their hands with only water, variations in individual soap usage, and the inability to distinguish the behavior of individuals who enter the restroom without using the facilities (e.g., cleaning staff). Despite these limitations, this approach remains a robust and ethical method for large-scale public health intervention studies, and it provides a strong foundation for our findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we were unable to obtain data on gender-specific hand soap usage, which limited our ability to explore the effects of the intervention on men and women separately. Second, we did not have information on the characteristics of the restroom users, such as whether they were outpatients, inpatients, or staff. Therefore, we could not determine whether the intervention had differential effects on these subgroups. Third, our study was conducted in a single hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Further research is needed to explore strategies for sustaining the effect of these interventions, such as using a variety of messages or implementing reminder systems.

Conclusion

Nudge-based interventions can be effective in promoting hand hygiene in Japanese health-care settings. However, this effect may wane over time.
