Inhibition of Airway Epithelial CTSC Attenuates Type 2 Inflammation: Implications for Eosinophilic Chronic Rhinosinusitis with Nasal Polyps
Authors: Xiayan Li, Zhenchao Zhu, Junlu Zhu, Bingqi Lin, Suizi Zhou, Ruofan Yang, Yitong Liu, Yang Peng, Qianhui Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCathepsin C (CTSC) has been identified as a key pathogenic gene in chronic rhinosinusitis. This study aimed to investigate the specific function and subcellular localization of CTSC within the nasal mucosal epithelium.Patients and MethodsA total of 34 patients with eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (eNP), 32 with non-eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (neNP), and 16 control subjects were enrolled. Airway epithelial morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy. The expression, localization, and function of CTSC in epithelial cells were assessed using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), immunohistochemistry, quantitative real-time PCR, Western blot, and an siRNA-mediated knockdown approach in an IL-4/IL-13-stimulated 16HBE cell line model.ResultsscRNA-seq revealed that CTSC was primarily localized to goblet cells and basal cells within the nasal epithelium, with its expression significantly upregulated in eNP patients compared with those in control subjects and neNP. These findings were confirmed at both the RNA and protein levels in clinical tissue samples. CTSC mRNA expression showed a significant positive correlation with the expression of T-helper 2 cytokines, including IL-4 and IL-13. In vitro experiments demonstrated that CTSC expression was induced in 16HBE cells upon IL-4/IL-13 stimulation. siRNA-mediated knockdown of CTSC led to pronounced suppression of IL-4/IL-13-induced release of CCL26, IL-25, and TNF-α, underscoring its functional involvement in inflammatory responses.ConclusionThis study identifies CTSC as a novel regulator of type 2 inflammation like eNP. It is specifically upregulated in nasal epithelial goblet and basal cells, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for airway diseases.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is a common chronic inflammatory disease of the upper airways that significantly impairs the quality of life.1 Based on the extent of eosinophil infiltration in the pathological tissue, CRSwNP can be classified into eosinophilic CRSwNP (eNP) and non-eosinophilic CRSwNP (neNP),2 with the former typically exhibiting a stronger T-helper type 2 (Th2) inflammatory bias and associated poorer treatment outcomes.3–5 Biologics that precisely target these Th2-associated pathways (eg IL-4/ IL-5/ IL-13) have been introduced to improve clinical outcomes in eNP, but drug resistance remains relatively common.6

The nasal mucosal epithelium not only acts as a key structure that actively participates in immune regulation and barrier maintenance but also serves as a target of inflammation.7–9 Under a Th2 inflammatory microenvironment, the epithelial barrier function is impaired, accompanied by a reduction in ciliated cells and hyperplasia of goblet cells.8 Elucidating the underlying mechanisms is therefore essential to refine stratified therapy and identify novel actionable targets.

Cathepsin C (CTSC), also known as dipetidylpeptidase I, is a lysosomal cysteine protease that is essential for the catalytic activation of many serine proteases.10 Previous studies have primarily focused on its role in systemic and neutrophil-dominated inflammation through the activation of neutrophil serine proteases,11–14 and it has consequently been regarded as a potential therapeutic target for inflammatory or autoimmune diseases.12,13,15 A recent study identified CTSC as a causal risk gene linked to chronic rhinosinusitis, supporting its specific role in upper airway inflammation.16 However, the specific function and subcellular localization of CTSC within the nasal mucosal epithelium remains poorly understood.

This study aims to investigate the expression and function of CTSC in CRSwNP. To this end, we will delineate the expression profile of CTSC by analyzing a public single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset.17 Subsequently, using clinical samples, we will validate the expression pattern of CTSC and examine its correlation with key inflammatory indicators as well as its regulatory role in airway epithelial inflammatory response. Furthermore, 16HBE14o- human bronchial epithelial (16HBE) cell line model will be employed to investigate the regulatory function of CTSC in epithelial inflammatory response. Our study is expected to provide new experimental evidence to clarify CRSwNP and to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

We conducted a retrospective study covering the period from January 2024 to August 2025. Nasal polyps (NP) tissues were obtained from 66 patients with CRSwNP and control ethmoid sinus (CON-ES) mucosal tissues from 16 control subjects at department of otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery, Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Guangdong Academy of Medical Sciences), Southern Medical University. Patient inclusion was based on the EPOS 2020 diagnostic criteria for CRSwNP.1 Control mucosal tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgery for cerebrospinal rhinorrhea, optic nerve decompression, cysts, or benign tumors, who had no evidence of sinonasal inflammatory disease. We conducted detailed statistical analyses of the participants, including age, sex, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score, Computed Tomography (CT) score, endoscopic score, Sinonasal Outcome Test-22 (SNOT-22) score, presence of olfactory dysfunction, smoking status, comorbidities, recurrence status, blood eosinophil count and serum total IgE level for each group. CT scans were scored using the Lund-Mackay system; patient symptom severity was assessed by VAS, such as nasal congestion, facial pressure, and rhinorrhea.18 Subject demographics and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1, and the number of samples utilized for each experiment is detailed in Table 2. Subjects who had received immunosuppressants, antihistamines, or corticosteroids within one month prior to surgery were excluded from the study. scRNA-Seq Analysis of CRSwNP Tissue

We reanalyzed a previously published scRNA-seq dataset (accession number: HRA000772), which profiled cell subpopulations from control (n=5), eNP (n=6), and neNP (n=5) samples.17 The NP tissues originating from the ethmoid sinus of CRSwNP were collected during endoscopic sinus surgery. Control mucosa were collected from normal ethmoid or sphenoid sinuses of patients who underwent endoscopic transnasal surgeries of cholesterol granuloma of the petrous apex, benign tumor of the infratemporal fossa or nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas. Using the GRCh38 human reference genome, we processed raw sequencing data through the Cell Ranger pipeline (version 6.1.2, 10x Genomics) to generate the initial gene expression matrices for each sample.

We performed scRNA-seq data analysis using R software (v4.3.1) and Seurat package (v5.0.0) on filtered gene-cell UMI matrices. Quality control involved sequential filtering: first, genes detected in fewer than 3 cells were excluded; subsequently, low-quality cells failing any of these thresholds were removed: 1) <200 detected genes, 2) >8000 genes, or 3) mitochondrial gene content ≥15%.

Doublet detection was conducted using scDblFinder software (v1.19.1), which identified artificial multiplets within each sample. Following initial removal, residual doublets were further eliminated through additional screening based on 2 biological implausibility criteria: 1) cells containing >8000 genes, or 2) co-expression of marker genes from distinct lineages (eg, EPCAM [epithelial], PECAM1 [endothelial], and CD3 [T cells]). Cells meeting either criterion were excluded to minimize false negatives.

We performed data integration, preprocessing, and batch effect correction using Seurat and Harmony (v1.2.0). Integrated gene expression matrices were normalized (NormalizeData), followed by identification of 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs; FindVariableFeatures) and z-score transformation (ScaleData). Principal components were computed based on these HVGs to serve as input for subsequent integration and clustering. Harmony-generated batch-corrected matrices enabled cross-sample integration. Cell clustering employed FindNeighbors and FindClusters, while nonlinear dimensionality reduction (RunUMAP) embedded cells in Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) space, grouping transcriptionally similar populations. Differential gene expression analysis between clusters used JoinLayers and FindAllMarkers (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Based on canonical markers, we identified 10 cell types: epithelial (EPCAM, KRT8, TP63, KRT5), endothelial (PECAM1, CLDN5, VWF), fibroblasts (COL1A1, COL3A1, LUM, DCN), smooth muscle (MYH11, ACTA2), T cells (CD3E, CD3D, CD3G, TRAC), B cells (CD19, MS4A1, CD79A), plasma cells (CD79A, JCHAIN), myeloid cells (HLA-DRA, CD14, CD86, XCR1), mast cells (KIT, CPA3), and neutrophils (FCGR3B, CSF3R) (Seurat methodology: [https://satijalab.org/seurat/articles/get_started.html]).

Given our primary focus on epithelial cells, this population was isolated using Seurat’s subsetting function and subjected to the full analytical pipeline (normalization, integration, clustering, and dimensionality reduction). Epithelial subtypes were defined by canonical markers: basal (KRT5, TP63, SERPINB3), goblet (MUC5AC, TFF3), secretory (SCGB1A1, SCGB3A1), ciliated (FOXJ1, PIFO), ionocyte (FOXI1, FOXI2, CFTR), and glandular cells (LYZ, LTF). Unsupervised clustering resolved 8 epithelial subpopulations, including 3 basal subsets. Differential expression analysis between subclusters employed FindAllMarkers and FindMarkers (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; test.use = “wilcox”), with stringent thresholds requiring |log2FoldChange| ≥ 0.15 and adjusted *P*-value < 0.05 for gene retention.

Morphological Evaluation

A detailed histopathological analysis was performed on the samples, which included scanning electron microscopy, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and whole-slide scanning using an automated slide scanner, to evaluate the eosinophil percentage and identify different types of abnormal epithelial characteristics. The primary procedures were as follows: tissue samples, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp, China) and embedded in paraffin, were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Eosinophils were counted in 10 randomly selected high-power fields (HPF, 40x magnification). Based on established histological criteria,2,9 tissues were diagnosed as eNP if eosinophils constituted ≥10% of the total inflammatory cells, and as neNP if below this threshold. We performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining by using a commercial kit (HC301-02, Vazyme, China), the sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was then carried out via microwave irradiation for 15 minutes using Tris-EDTA buffer (C1038, Solarbio, China). Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with a rabbit polyclonal primary antibody against CTSC (ab314644, Abcam, UK; diluted 1:3000). On the following day, a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody was applied and incubated for 20 minutes, followed by color development using the accompanying diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate. The percentage of the CTSC-immunopositive area was semi-quantitatively analyzed using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), in accordance with the method described by Kim et al.19 The positively stained area was quantified as a percentage by applying a color deconvolution algorithm to distinguish DAB and hematoxylin signals. Analysis was performed on a minimum of 5 randomly selected fields per section.

RNA Extraction, Reverse Transcription and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Following collection, each surgical specimen was immediately immersed in RNA stabilization solution (M6101, NCM Biotech, China) and stored at −80°C. Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using total RNA isolation reagent (R411-02, Vazyme, China), while cellular RNA was isolated using a Rapid Total RNA Extraction Kit (400–100, GOONIE, China) specifically designed for cells. RNA purity was assessed by determining the absorbance ratio at 260 nm and 280 nm, with an acceptable ratio ranging from 1.8 to 2.0. For each reaction, 5 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a PCR thermal cycler, and the resulting cDNA was used as the template for qPCR.

All primers were designed and synthesized by Beijing Liuhe BGI Co., Ltd. CTSC primer sequences: forward primer 5′-CGATGTCAACTGCTCGGTTAT-3′, reverse primer 5′-AAGGCAAACCACTTGTAGTCATT-3′. IL-4 primer sequences: forward primer 5′-ATGGGTCTCACCTCCCAACT-3′, reverse primer 5′-GATGTCTGTTACGGTCAACTCG-3′. IL-13 primer sequences: forward primer 5′-AGGGAAAGAAGGTGCTCAGG-3′, reverse primer 5′-TCTGGTTCTGGGTGATGTTGA-3′. IL-6 primer sequences: forward primer 5′-GGCACTGGCAGAAAACAACC-3′, reverse primer 5′-GCAAGTCTCCTCATTGAATCC-3′. IL-8 primer sequences: forward primer 5′-TTTTGCCAAGGAGTGCTAAAGA-3′, reverse primer 5′-AACCCTCTGCACCCAGTTTTC-3′. IL-25 primer sequences: forward primer 5′-CCCCCTGGAGATATGAGTTGGA-3′, reverse primer 5′-CATGTGGGAGCCTGTCTGTA-3′. CCL26 primer sequences: forward primer 5′-TTGAGGCTGAGCCAAAGACC-3′, reverse primer 5′-TGGTAGTGAATATCACAGCCCG-3′. TNF-α primer sequences: forward primer 5′-CCTCTCTCTAATCAGCCCTCTG-3′, reverse primer 5′-GAGGACCTGGGAGTAGATGAG-3′. IL-1β primer sequences: forward primer 5′-CGAATCTCCGACCACCACTA-3′, reverse primer 5′-AGGGAAAGAAGGTGCTCAGG-3′. GAPDH primer sequences: forward primer 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, reverse primer 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′. Data were normalized to the expression of a house-keeping gene (GAPDH) and relative expression was calculated using the comparative 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western Blot Analysis (WB)

The protein level of CTSC was detected by WB. Briefly, total protein was extracted from tissues or cells using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China). The extracted proteins were separated by electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Following blocking, it was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, including an anti-rabbit CTSC polyclonal antibody (ab314644, Abcam, UK; 1:1000 dilution) and an anti-mouse GAPDH monoclonal antibody (GB15002-100, Servicebio, China; 1:2000 dilution). After washing, the membrane was incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (RM3001, RM3002, Beijing Ray Antibody Biotech, China; 1:500 dilution), as appropriate. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (E170-04, Genstar, China). The intensity of the target protein bands was quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA) and normalized to that of GAPDH.

Cell Culture and Stimulation

The 16HBE cell line was obtained from BLB Co., Ltd. After thawing, cells were expanded and cultured in 6-well plates using DMEM (C11995500BT, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; FSP500, ExCell Bio, China) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (ANTI-ANTI; 15140148, Gibco, USA). One day before stimulation, cells in good growth condition were trypsinized, counted, and seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well. Each well contained 500 μL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, and the cells were cultured for 24 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. To investigate the potential regulatory mechanisms of CTSC in eNP, cells were subjected to a panel of exogenous stimuli designed to model distinct inflammatory microenvironment. Specifically, IL-4 and IL-13 were employed to recapitulate a Th2-skewed microenvironment; IL-17A, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were used to mimic generalized inflammatory or infectious conditions; and LPS and poly (I:C) were applied to simulate bacterial and viral pathogen-associated molecular pattern exposure, respectively. Following cell adhesion and reaching appropriate confluence, stimulants were applied at the following concentration gradients: IL-4 (200-04-5, PeproTech, USA), IL-13 (200-13-10UG, PeproTech, USA), IL-17A (AF-200-17-5, PeproTech, USA), IFN-γ (AF-300-02-100, PeproTech, USA) and TNF-α (300-01A-10, PeproTech, USA) at 0, 10, and 20 ng/mL; LPS (L2880-10MG, Sigma, Germany) and poly (I:C) (tlrl-pic, Invivogen, France) at 0, 50, and 100 μg/mL. Following a 24-hour incubation period at 37°C, cells were harvested for total RNA extraction.

Design of Small Interfering RNA (siRNA) and Transfection

To knock down CTSC, 16HBE cells were transfected with CTSC-specific siRNA. An siRNA duplex targeting human CTSC (5′-GGAGAAAUGUUCAUGGUAUCAAUTT-3′) was synthesized by GenePharma, and a non-targeting negative-control siRNA was included. 24 hours before transfection, 16HBE cells were seeded into 12-well plates at 4 × 104 cells per well in 1 mL DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% ANTI-ANTI. Upon reaching 50–60% confluence, cells were transfected with either CTSC-specific or control siRNA using mate transfection reagent (cat. no. G04027, GenePharma, China).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test or Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. One-way ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis H-test were used for comparison. Data are presented as mean ± standard error or median with interquartile range. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Included Subjects

A total of 16 CON-ES subjects and 66 patients with CRSwNP (34 eNP and 32 neNP) were enrolled in this study. As shown in Table 1, the demographic characteristics, comorbidities, key scale scores, and hematologic indicators of the three groups are as follows: The median age (IQR) was 42.65 years (31.25–51.25) in the CON-ES group, 53.56 years (46.00–57.75) in the eNP group, and 43.38 years (27.75–56.25) in the neNP group. The male proportion was 62.50% (10/16), 64.71% (22/34), and 53.13% (17/32) in these three groups, respectively. Regarding comorbidities, the proportion of smokers in the three groups was 18.80%, 14.70%, and 15.60%, respectively. The prevalence of asthma was 17.60% in the eNP group and 3.10% in the neNP group, while no asthma cases were reported in the CON-ES group. The prevalence of allergic rhinitis was 12.50%, 14.70%, and 18.80% across the three groups. In the eNP and neNP groups, the median (IQR) scores of clinical variables were as follows: total VAS score 7.50 (7.00–8.00) and 7.00 (6.00–8.00), CT score 14.53 (10.00–18.50) and 14.15 (11.00–16.00), endoscopic score 4.61 (4.00–6.00) and 3.83 (3.00–4.25), SNOT-22 score 16.78 (10.25–23.25) and 14.69 (10.00–20.00), and olfactory dysfunction score 3.83 (3.25–5.00) and 3.08 (2.00–5.00). Additionally, the NP recurrence rate was 20.60% in the eNP group and 3.10% in the neNP group. The median (IQR) blood eosinophil counts in the three groups were 0.18 (0.04–0.16) ×109 cells/L 0.48 (0.34–0.53) ×109 cells/L, and 0.25 (0.14–0.36) ×109 cells/L, respectively, while the median (IQR) serum total IgE levels were 127.62 (46.29–164.60) IU/mL, 209.12 (97.84–327.84) IU/mL, and 210.81 (91.37–316.75) IU/mL, respectively. Patients in the eNP group demonstrated a significant increase in blood eosinophil count compared to the neNP group (P < 0.01), while no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age (P = 0.05), sex (P = 0.46), total VAS score (P = 0.22), CT score (P = 0.73), smoking (P = 0.92), or comorbidities like, asthma (P = 0.11) and allergic rhinitis (P = 0.66).

Moreover, CTSC expression was predominantly localized to goblet cells and basal cells (Figure 1C). To validate the scRNA-seq findings, total RNA was extracted and CTSC mRNA levels were quantified by qPCR. Compared with CON-ES and neNP, CTSC mRNA expression was significantly elevated in the eNP subset (Figure 1E; P < 0.05), in line with the scRNA-seq data. Subsequently, CTSC protein level was assessed by WB. The protein level of CTSC was markedly higher in eNP than those in both CON-ES and neNP (Figure 1F and G; P < 0.05), mirroring the mRNA trend. Scanning electron microscopy revealed disorganized cilia and increased secretions in CRSwNP patients relative to CON-ES (Figure 1H). The location of CTSC in nasal epithelium was next detected by IHC. CTSC immunoreactivity was observed predominantly in the goblet cells and basal cells of the nasal epithelium in the eNP group (Figure 1H). Discussion

Our study reveals that CTSC is specifically upregulated in the epithelium of patients with eNP, and its expression level positively correlates with the levels of the Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13. Functional experiments in vitro further demonstrated that knockdown of CTSC significantly alleviates the IL-4/IL-13-induced inflammatory response in 16HBE cells. These results suggest, for the first time, that CTSC may play a promoting role in regulating epithelial inflammation, providing new evidence for its potential as a therapeutic target in type 2 inflammatory diseases such as eNP. Moreover, CTSC expression was found to correlate positively with peripheral blood eosinophil count, VAS score and olfactory dysfunction score in NP patients.

The role of CTSC in neutrophil-ldominated inflammatory diseases has been relatively well established, and it has also been linked to classical Th2-type diseases.12,20,21 For instance, previous studies reported elevated CTSC in airway remodeling parameters in asthma and up-regulation of its expression in eosinophilic esophagitis.21,22 By connecting CTSC to eNP, this study further supports its potential involvement in diverse type 2 inflammatory processes. However, unlike observations in psoriasis where inhibition of CTSC exacerbated inflammation,23 our findings highlight a context-dependent function, which may reflect the complex nature of CTSC activity: it is not simply a “pro-inflammatory” or “anti-inflammatory” factor, but rather its functional output depends heavily on the specific immune microenvironment and cell type involved. In the Th1/Th17 microenvironment of psoriasis, CTSC might contribute to immune homeostasis by regulating unknown substrates, whereas in the Th2 biased setting of eNP, it may directly drive inflammation by activating specific protease cascades within epithelial cells.23 In summary, the role of CTSC varies across different diseases and cell types.

Previous studies have associated high eosinophil activation with olfactory loss and noted eosinophil infiltration in the nasal mucosa of patients with refractory chronic rhinosinusitis and severe olfactory dysfunction.24,25 Our work extends these observations by showing that CTSC expression is upregulated in eNP and correlates positively with eosinophilic inflammation, olfactory impairment, and the VAS score. It may therefore serve primarily as a biomarker for olfactory dysfunction and patient-perceived symptom severity in the context of eosinophilic inflammation. However, the lack of correlation between CTSC and other clinical metrics such as CT score, SNOT-22 score, endoscopic score, or serum total IgE level indicates that CTSC may not directly reflect overall disease severity or structural burden. Therefore, it suggests that greater clinical attention should be directed toward the eosinophilic phenotype accompanied by olfactory dysfunction in future research. CTSC is an established therapeutic target as a lysosomal protease. Its druggability is supported by the fact that CTSC inhibitors are already in clinical trials for other inflammatory conditions. For example, the non-covalent CTSC inhibitor AZD7986 (Brensocatib) was evaluated in multiple clinical trials for the treatment of COVID-19 and non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis.13,15 Future research should validate whether targeting CTSC can mitigate CRSwNP formation and inflammatory response in animal models, and explore its potential effects on goblet cell differentiation and epithelial barrier repair.

The 16HBE cell line is a human bronchial epithelial cell model. Given the current lack of well-established commercialized upper airway cell models and the high similarity in key functional characteristics between upper and lower airway epithelial cells, the 16HBE cell line was selected for this study to investigate the pathogenesis of CRSwNP.26 Compared to primary upper airway epithelial cells, which are characterized by high technical difficulty, extended culture periods, and significant batch-to-batch variation,27 the 16HBE cell line serves as a mature and standardized in vitro model that effectively overcomes these limitations.28,29 Furthermore, the reliability of the 16HBE cell line in upper airway-related research has been demonstrated in previous studies, supporting its application in the present investigation.30,31 Our results indicated that siCTSC attenuated the inflammation induced by Th2 cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13, thereby alleviating the epithelial inflammatory state.

However, it must be noted that this study has several limitations. First, all experiments were based on cell line and clinical tissue samples. Since animal validation remains pending, further in vivo studies will be important to fully elucidate the anti-inflammatory function of CTSC. Second, although sc-RNA seq data and IHC results both indicated high expression of CTSC in goblet cells and basal cells, conditional gene knockout was not performed in this specific cell type in the present study. Consequently, further validation with larger sample sizes is needed to enable correlation with a more comprehensive set of clinical variables.

Conclusion

In summary, the expression of CTSC in the nasal epithelial tissue of eNP patients was significantly upregulated and it played a promotional role in the inflammatory response. The results indicated that CTSC might participate in the pathogenesis of eNP by enhancing the epithelial immune inflammatory response. Given its key role in the regulation of inflammation, CTSC is expected to be a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for eNP, providing a new direction for clinical intervention.
