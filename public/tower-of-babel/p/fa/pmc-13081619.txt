Screening of microhaplotypes for paternity testing from low-recombination regions in the human genome
Authors: Jing Cang, Rican Cong, Zhihao Du, Riga Wu, Lagabaiyila Zha, Guoqing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microhaplotypes are playing an increasingly important role in forensic evidence analysis. However, the impact of meiotic recombination events on the efficacy of microhaplotypes in paternity testing remains poorly understood. Based on the data from the 1000 Genomes Project and a high-resolution recombination map, we identified microhaplotypes from low recombination-rate regions (recombination rate < 0.1 cM/Mb) and validated their efficacy for paternity testing by assessing their performance in 580 trio families and in simulated datasets. The results showed that: (1) The low-recombination microhaplotypes outperformed their high-recombination counterparts in paternity testing; (2) A panel of 30 microhaplotypes, selected from low-recombination, non-coding regions of the autosomes, achieved a cumulative power of discrimination (CPD) of 1-6.77 × 10− 37 and cumulative probability of exclusion (CPE) of 1-1.72 × 10− 13; (3) Validation using real-world trios demonstrated that low-recombination microhaplotypes had significantly higher cumulative paternity index (CPI) values (p < 2e-16), outperforming conventional STR markers by seven orders of magnitude; (4) Simulation analyses revealed that low-recombination markers could distinguish true parentage with 100% accuracy and had higher paternity testing accuracy compared to high-recombination counterparts; (5) An online microhaplotype database (http://bioinformatics.gqliulab.com/microhaplotype/) was established, enabling users to query information and calculate forensic parameters. An open-access microhaplotype screening tool, MHpatools, was developed and is freely available at http://bioinformatics.gqliulab.com/MHpatools/. Collectively, the established microhaplotype system, database, and analytical tools would be useful in forensic genetics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12723-w.

Introduction

With the increasingly deep application of forensic genetics in more complex scenarios, such as mixed DNA analysis, degraded DNA samples, and kinship identification, the development of novel genetic markers and new screening methods for genetic markers has now become a crucial frontier area in the discipline. In current forensic practice, short tandem repeats (STRs) serve as the primary genetic markers for individual identification and paternity testing. Composed of 2–6 bp core repeat units, STRs offer advantages such as high polymorphism and balanced allelic diversity [1, 2]. However, STRs are prone to mutations, leading to gains or losses of repeat units [3]. When mutations resulted in violation of Mendelian inheritance laws, the accuracy of STR-based identification might be affected [1]. Generally, although only one STR in a STR-based system composed of more than twenty STR markers is subjected to mutation in a single paternity test [4], one should bear in mind that, the resulting misjudgment even in a single case may lead to a disaster for the related people. Moreover, STRs are less powerful in testing degraded samples due to their large amplicon size. Additional limitations of STRs include artifacts such as stutter peaks (caused by polymerase slippage during PCR amplification) and allelic amplification imbalance [2], both of which reduce the reliability of genetic identification.

Recently, researchers have shifted focus to single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based markers. SNPs, the most abundant genetic variants in the human genome, with over 84 million identified to date, are characterized by their genome-wide distribution, low mutation rates, and ancestral information retention [5]. Furthermore, SNP markers have a critical advantage: their smaller amplicon size enables analysis of highly degraded DNA, whereas the long amplicons of STRs limit their applicability to such samples [6, 7]. Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have facilitated SNP analysis by offering cost-effectiveness, high throughput, sensitivity, and precision [8]. These attributes enhance the forensic utility of SNPs, particularly for degraded or mixed samples. However, the biallelic nature of SNP alleles [9, 10] limits their discriminatory power for individual identification and paternity testing. To address this, Kidd et al. [11] introduced the concept of microhaplotypes (MHs) —multiallelic loci composed of tightly linked SNPs [12]. Microhaplotypes are genomic regions spanning ≤ 200 bp that contain two or more tightly linked SNPs [11]. The multi-allelic nature of microhaplotypes results in high heterozygosity (high polymorphism) and discrimination power per locus, exceeding that of individual SNPs. Each microhaplotype locus behaves like a multi-allelic marker, providing rich information that is crucial for statistically separating and identifying the individual contributors within a DNA mixture [13]. Microhaplotypes have mutation rates much lower than STRs. In addition, microhaplotypes have short amplification fragments, making them particularly advantageous for analyzing mixed DNA samples and highly degraded or fragmented DNA samples (e.g., from ancient remains, mass disasters, touch DNA, or environmentally exposed evidence) [2, 14]. Due to the various advantages, microhaplotypes have the potential to be the most promising markers in the field of forensic applications in the near future [15, 16]. There are some studies supporting this trend. For example, Cheung et al. demonstrated that the combination of three marker types (biallelic SNPs, triallelic SNPs, and microhaplotypes) achieved optimal performance in population differentiation and ancestry inference, and microhaplotypes outperformed other markers [17]. Multiple studies have validated microhaplotypes for diverse forensic applications [18–22]. Zhu et al. [23] and Kureshi et al. [24] demonstrated high accuracy in paternity testing, with Kureshi et al. achieving superior discriminatory power. For complex evidence, Wu et al. [25] confirmed the superiority of MHs over STRs in mixed DNA analysis, while Wen et al. [26] and Sun et al. [27] validated their utility in population studies and kinship, with Sun et al. [27] notably showing a lower misjudgment risk in consanguineous cases compared to STRs.

SNPs within a microhaplotype tend to be inherited together as a block and experience less recombination over generations. However, fine-scale high recombination regions are abundant in the human genome according to recent studies [28], and their effects on microhaplotype-based paternity testing remain elusive. Meiotic recombination between homologous chromosomes during gametogenesis may compromise microhaplotype efficacy in paternity testing. As a critical molecular event in eukaryotic meiosis, recombination involves crossover between sister chromatids [29, 30], driving genome evolution through mutation induction, gene conversion, and natural selection enhancement [28, 31, 32]. Recombination disrupts SNP linkage, leading to gamete-parent chromosome mismatches and altered parent-child genotype inheritance. Thus, markers near recombination hotspots may significantly impair paternity testing accuracy—a critical yet overlooked consideration. The human genome averages ~ 1% recombination probability per megabase (1 cM/Mb), with recombination rates in 200–300 bp regions comparable to SNP mutation rates [11]. While the original microhaplotype definition emphasized avoiding recombination hotspots [11], most studies neglect this criterion. Despite extensive comparisons between microhaplotypes and STRs [33, 34] and ongoing refinement of screening methods, recombination effects remain poorly addressed. Although some studies incorporate recombination maps, their limited resolution [35] fails to account for hyperlocalized hotspots, which may compromise marker performance even in tightly linked SNPs.

This study leverages the 1000 Genomes Project (GRCh38) SNP data and the high-resolution recombination map [36] to identify microhaplotypes from ultra-low-recombination regions. By comparing these with markers from high-recombination regions and validating their efficacy on real-world trios and simulated datasets, we comprehensively evaluate their forensic utility.

Materials

SNP data were obtained from the 1000 Genomes Project (GRCh38), comprising whole-genome genotype information from 2,504 unrelated individuals and 580 trio families. The phased VCF files were downloaded from http://ftp.1000genomes.ebi.ac.uk/vol1/ftp/data_collections/1000G_2504_high_coverage/working/20201028_3202_phased/. Recombination rate data used in this study were taken from a sex-specific high-resolution human recombination map, which was generated by quantifying non-crossover (NCO) and crossover (CO) recombination events transmitted from parents to offspring using family-based whole-genome sequencing data [36]. This map includes 626,828 genetic markers, with an average inter-marker distance of 682 bp. The positional information for simple repeat sequences in the human genome was obtained from the RepeatMasker annotation files (https://www.repeatmasker.org/species/hg.html). Mutation rates were obtained from Smith et al. [37], who estimated mutation rates for central nucleotides in each 7-mer oligonucleotide across Asian, African, and European populations using a K-mer-based model. MNase-seq paired-end sequencing data (GSE36979) from seven lymphoblastoid cell lines, generated by Gaffney et al. [38], were analyzed to assess nucleosome occupancy at microhaplotype loci.

Methods

In this study, we combined the data of 1000 Genomes Project (GRCh38) with the recombination rate map to obtain the SNPs in the low recombination rate regions. Then, using the Plink v1.9 [39] and screening methods, top microhaplotypes in low recombination rate regions were obtained. The power of low-recombination microhaplotypes was validated, through comparing with high-recombination microhaplotypes, by using both real-world trios and simulated data. In addition, a web server was built for researchers to query and analyze microhaplotype markers. The flow chart of this study is shown in Fig. 1. Details and specific steps of methods are described below.

Data preprocessing

VCFtools v0.1.16 [40] was used to filter missing, insertions, and multi-allelic genotypes from the 1000 Genomes VCF files, resulting in 58,732,837 biallelic SNP loci. The dataset was partitioned into low-recombination regions (RecRate < 0.1 cM/Mb) and high-recombination regions (RecRate > 5 cM/Mb) based on the distribution of recombination rates in the high-resolution human recombination map by Halldorsson et al. [36]. These thresholds represent the extremes of the distribution, enabling evaluation of microhaplotype characteristics across distinct genomic backgrounds. SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.1 were excluded [24], yielding 3,637,706 SNPs in low-recombination regions and 257,671 SNPs in high-recombination regions. Microhaplotypes (≤ 200 bp, ≤ 5 SNPs per block) were identified using Plink v1.9 [39]. Microhaplotype frequencies were calculated via Plink v1.9 [39] (code available in MHpatools: http://bioinformatics.gqliulab.com/MHpatools/).

Forensic parameters

Forensic parameters for each microhaplotype were calculated using an in-house python script. The calculated parameters included: Power of discrimination (PD), Probability of exclusion (PE), Heterozygosity (Het), Effective allele number (Ae), and Polymorphism information content (PIC). After top microhaplotypes were selected (details are provided in Sect. 3.3 Microhaplotype Screening), the following parameters for the microhaplotypes were calculated: Cumulative power of discrimination (CPD), Cumulative probability of exclusion (CPE), Paternity index (PI), and Cumulative paternity index (CPI). All these parameters were calculated, assuming Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. The definitions for the parameters were provided in Supplementary Information.

Microhaplotype screening

Threshold-based filtering

To ensure independence (non-linkage) between microhaplotypes, two filtering criteria were applied: (1) the physical distance between any two microhaplotypes on the same chromosome must exceed 1 Mb [41], and (2) the genetic distance between any two microhaplotypes must exceed 20 cM [42]. When two microhaplotypes violated either criterion, the one with the lower product-based contribution score (defined in Sect. 3.3.2) was removed. The microhaplotypes were further filtered using positional information of repetitive sequences defined by RepeatMasker [43], ensuring that microhaplotypes in both low- and high-recombination regions did not overlap with simple repeat sequences, thereby avoiding the impact of high mutation rates in short tandem repeats on paternity testing results. Additionally, the liftOver tool (default parameters) [44] was used to convert the coordinates of hg19-based recombination hotspots [45] to the hg38 genome version. Microhaplotypes falling within these regions were further removed from the low-recombination dataset to further avoid the effect of recombination. Ultimately, 102 candidate microhaplotypes were obtained from high-recombination regions and 88 candidate microhaplotypes from low-recombination regions.

Contribution score definition

To obtain microhaplotypes with both high PD and high PE, we proposed two contribution score indices, average rank-based contribution and product-based contribution. Power of discrimination (PD) represents the probability of correctly identifying an individual from a population, while probability of exclusion (PE) represents the probability of excluding an unrelated individual from paternity. Both metrics range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater discriminatory power and better performance for paternity testing. Microhaplotypes with high values for both PD and PE are particularly valuable, as they provide robust performance across diverse forensic applications.

Microhaplotypes are independently ranked in descending order based on PD and PE values. In other words, microhaplotypes with high contribution scores have not only high PE but also high PD. Lower average ranks indicate higher contribution and priority (e.g., rank 1 for both PD and PE yields a score of 1.0). Higher product values indicate greater contribution. The contribution scores quantify the relative importance of each microhaplotype, in terms of PD and PE. From the microhaplotypes sorted by contribution scores, top microhaplotypes are our selection.

Paternity index calculation incorporating mutation

In paternity testing, the paternity index (PI) is defined as the likelihood ratio of two probabilities (X/Y) under two competing hypotheses [46]. The numerator (X) is the probability of observing the child’s genotype (or the genetic evidence, such as a shared haplotype), given the hypothesis that the Alleged Father is the biological father. The denominator (Y) is the probability of observing the child’s genotype, given the hypothesis that a random, unrelated man is the biological father. When mutations occur, resulting in incomplete haplotype matches between the alleged father and child, X decreases. When calculating PI for trios (alleged father, mother, and child), PI was calculated using mutation-adjusted formulas in case of mutation detected in a microhaplotype (see Supplementary Table S1).

Validation using trio family data

The 580 trio families from the 1000 Genomes Project were used to validate the low- and high-recombination microhaplotype marker sets. CPI values, which represent the combined strength of paternity evidence across all loci, were calculated for each trio and compared between the two marker sets. Higher CPI values indicate stronger evidence supporting paternity.

Simulated dataset validation

Simulated datasets were generated using 1000 Genomes Project data. True parentage dataset (Positive set): Male and female individuals were randomly selected from the 1000 Genomes Project as the biological parents, and offspring genotypes were simulated by combining one chromosome from each parent. This dataset represents true biological parent-child relationships and should yield high cumulative paternity index (CPI) values. A total of 10,000 such simulated trios were generated. Non-parentage dataset (Negative set): The same selection procedure was used to identify male and female individuals as the alleged parents, but the offspring genotype was derived from an unrelated third individual. This dataset represents hypothetical non-biological parent-child relationships and should yield low CPI values. An additional 10,000 simulated trios were generated. CPI values were computed for both sets using the microhaplotype systems from low and high recombination regions.

Recombination event detection

A method was developed to detect recombination events in 1000 Genomes trios.

Chromosomal origin determination

Child chromosomes were traced to parental haplotypes by assessing allele consistency at SNP loci. A difference vector was constructed, where 0 indicates allele concordance and 1 indicates discordance.

Sliding window analysis

CO signals were evaluated by applying a 40-SNP sliding window to the difference vector. Forward and backward lagging difference methods (implemented in MHpatools) was used to quantify, respectively, forward CO signals and backward CO signals, then the total CO signals were obtained by summing the forward CO signals and backward CO signals. In other words, this method quantifies the number of crossover recombination events occurring in alleles flanking the central position of each 40-SNP sliding window, with a maximum recombination signal value of 40 per window.

Peak calling

Finally, peaks in the recombination signals were detected using a peak calling algorithm with the following parameters: upstream supporting SNPs ≥ 1, downstream supporting SNPs ≥ 1, minimum peak height = 26, and minimum inter-peak distance = 20. The minimum inter-peak distance of 20 is necessary to avoid two overlapping peaks, as two closely located COs are biologically implausible. The peak calling algorithm is used to detect recombination event within a window encompassing 40 SNPs. In principle, the stronger the signal (as measured by minimum peak height), the higher the likelihood of recombination at the locus. A small cutoff value results in fewer SNPs supporting the CO recombination event, thereby increasing the risk of false positives. But an extreme high cutoff value would cause high false negative rate. In a previous study [47], < 100 SNPs support may correspond to false positives in case of sequencing depth of 6.3X for mouse genomes. But the sequencing depth of 1KG data used in this study is as high as 30X. This suggests that much smaller number of SNPs (e.g. 25) is required to support CO recombination. Therefore, we also tested different cutoffs for the minimum peak height, ranging from 25 to 28.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, this approach pinpoints recombination breakpoints by integrating two directional signals, enabling precise genomic localization of recombination events in child chromosomes.

Microhaplotype mutation rate calculation

The mutation rate for each SNP within a microhaplotype was calculated using the oligonucleotide-centered mutation rate data from Smith et al. [37]. Specifically, Smith et al. provided mutation rates for the central nucleotide of all possible 7-mer oligonucleotides across European, Asian, and African populations, covering 24,576 mutation types. For each SNP in the microhaplotype, we: (i) Extracted the flanking three nucleotides upstream and downstream to construct the 7-mer oligonucleotide; (ii) Retrieved the population-averaged mutation rate (mean of European, Asian, and African rates) for each of the three possible substitution types (e.g., A→T, A→C, A→G) from the dataset; (iii) Summed the three substitution-specific rates to obtain the total mutation rate for the SNP; (iv) The mutation rate for a microhaplotype was then defined as the mean mutation rate across all its constituent SNP loci. We used the mean rather than the sum of SNP mutation rates to ensure comparability across microhaplotypes of varying lengths. It is worth noting that while the total haplotype mutation rate is typically calculated as the sum of mutation rates across all single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci within the haplotype, we defined the mutation rate for each microhaplotype as the sum of all SNP mutation rates divided by the number of SNPs to avoid bias introduced by variation in microhaplotype length.

Nucleosome occupancy analysis

SRA-format sequencing data from GSE36979 (GEO database) were downloaded. Single-end and paired-end reads were aligned to the reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 [48]. For paired-end data, reads mapping to multiple genomic locations were discarded. SAMtools [49] was used for sorting, format conversion (SAM→BAM), and filtering (Q-score < 30, unmapped reads excluded). Nucleosome occupancy at microhaplotype loci was quantified using computeMatrix reference-point function of DeepTools [50]. Heatmaps were generated via plotHeatmap for visualization.

Results and discussion

Screening of microhaplotypes in low-recombination regions and their efficiency

To screen microhaplotype markers with superior performance in forensic analysis, the following factors need to be considered: high PD, high PE, high polymorphism, genetic independence between markers, low mutation rates, and low recombination rates. Microhaplotypes were identified in low-recombination regions and filtered using specific criteria (see Sect. 3.3 Microhaplotype Screening) to select 88 candidate microhaplotypes. While the initial screening identified 88 candidate microhaplotypes, this panel size is impractical for routine forensic applications. Previous studies indicated that approximately 30 microhaplotypes are sufficient to achieve reliable discrimination and exclusion powers in paternity testing applications [27, 51]. We sought to efficiently and rapidly screen for the optimal subset of microhaplotypes from a larger pool of candidates for forensic applications, particularly paternity testing.

In paternity testing, PE is the most critical metric, while PD is prioritized in individual identification. Although PD and PE show a strong positive correlation overall (R = 0.99507, Figure S1), the relationship is not strictly monotonic.As PE increases, some microhaplotypes exhibit slight decreases in PD, indicating trade-offs between the two metrics at the individual marker level. To identify microhaplotypes with optimal combined performance, we developed two contribution score-based methods: the average ranking method and the product contribution method (see 3.3.2 Contribution Score Definition), which simultaneously optimize both metrics rather than relying on a single metric. To evaluate the performance of the two contribution score methods, we compared their outcomes using log-scale transformed CPD and CPE values. Specifically, we applied -log10 transformation to both metrics (resulting in -log10(1-CPD) and -log10(1-CPE)), which better visualizes the range and distribution of CPD and CPE values across candidate microhaplotypes (Fig. 3A). As the number of microhaplotypes increases, both methods yield highly consistent CPD and CPE values, indicating high consistency in their ability to balance PD and PE optimization. However, the product contribution method was selected for final marker selection because it simultaneously maximizes both metrics rather than averaging their ranks, providing superior combined performance. Using the product contribution method, a total of 30 microhaplotype genetic markers were screened from low-recombination regions. (Table 1; detailed data in Supplementary File 2). The Table 1 summarizes the forensic statistical metrics (e.g., PD, PE, Ae, Het) of the 30 microhaplotype markers identified in low-recombination regions, providing critical data for evaluating their efficacy in paternity testing and individual identification (calculation methods see in Supplementary Information). These 30 markers achieved a combined PD (CPD) of 1-6.77 × 10− 37 and a combined PE (CPE) of 1-1.72 × 10− 13. Using the same approach, 30 microhaplotypes were also selected from high-recombination regions. Comparative analysis of the two marker systems (Fig. 3B) revealed that, when using an equal number of microhaplotypes, the CPD and CPE of the low-recombination group outperformed those of the high-recombination group. This demonstrates that the genetic markers selected from low-recombination regions are more powerful either in individual identification or in paternity testing.

Genomic annotation using ChIPseeker [52] revealed that microhaplotypes in low-recombination regions were predominantly located in non-coding regions (Fig. 3C and D). Among the 88 candidate microhaplotypes, 36.36% were located in distal intergenic regions and 29.43% in intronic regions, together comprising 65.79% of all candidates. In contrast, only 11.11% resided in promoter regions and 1.67% in 3’UTRs. The final 30 microhaplotypes showed similar enrichment: distal intergenic (30%) and intronic regions (56.67%) together comprised 86.67%, with minimal representation in promoter and regulatory regions (7.33%). Previous studies suggest that nucleosome positioning stability correlates with both mutation and recombination rates [53]. For example, regions with strong nucleosome positioning (particularly nucleosome boundary regions) exhibit mutation rates significantly higher than the genome-wide average, likely due to low-fidelity DNA replication, insufficient mismatch repair, and DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) [53]. In mammalian genomes, meiotic recombination-associated DSBs preferentially occur in narrow regions flanking H3K4me3-marked nucleosomes [54], yet overall nucleosome occupancy remains high around DSB sites [55]. Our findings suggest that PRDM9, a key recombination protein, may bind to nucleosome-occupied recombination hotspots, facilitating nucleosome remodeling (e.g., sliding) and subsequent recombination processes [56]. To investigate the nucleosome positioning characteristics of the selected microhaplotypes, we analyzed nucleosome occupancy at the loci of candidate microhaplotypes (88 from low-recombination and 102 from high-recombination regions) and 30 microhaplotypes in low- and high-recombination regions using lymphoblastoid cell line data. The analysis was centered on the microhaplotype center (defined as the midpoint coordinate of each microhaplotype region) using DeepTools, which generated nucleosome occupancy heatmaps revealing distribution patterns around the microhaplotype loci (Fig. 3E and F). The results revealed significantly higher nucleosome occupancy in high-recombination regions compared to low-recombination regions. Elevated nucleosome occupancy is associated with increased mutation rates and DSB susceptibility [53], whereas nucleosome-depleted regions exhibit lower mutation rates. Thus, our findings suggest that microhaplotypes in low-recombination regions are able to inherently serve as genetic markers for paternity testing at the nucleosome positioning level.

Performance of microhaplotypes in low-recombination regions

To evaluate the performance of the microhaplotype marker system selected from low-recombination regions, we compared the forensic parameters (e.g., PD, PE, Ae, and PIC) of the 30 microhaplotype genetic markers screened from low- and high-recombination regions (Fig. 4A). The results demonstrated that PD, PE, Ae, and PIC were significantly higher in low-recombination regions than in high-recombination regions. To mitigate potential sampling bias due to limited sample size, we further compared the forensic parameters of all the candidate microhaplotypes screened from low- (n = 88) and high-recombination regions (n = 102) (Supplementary Figure S2). Our results consistently confirmed that microhaplotypes from low-recombination regions display higher paternity testing efficiency.

Regions with high recombination rates are associated with elevated mutation rates [57, 58], which poses challenges for paternity testing. To validate this hypothesis, we compared the mutation rates of microhaplotypes in low- and high-recombination regions (see Methods). The average mutation rate of microhaplotypes was estimated using a genome-wide 7-mer-based mutation rate model [36]. Initial comparisons of mutation rates between all candidate microhaplotypes screened from low- (n = 88) and high-recombination regions (n = 102) and 30 microhaplotypes screened from low- and high-recombination regions (Supplementary Figure S3) revealed minimal differences, likely due to the limited sample size being insufficient to detect significant divergence. However, when analyzing all microhaplotypes generated by Plink v1.9 from these regions without applying any filtering parameters, we observed that microhaplotypes in high-recombination regions exhibited higher mutation rates than those in low-recombination regions (Fig. 4B). These findings confirm that microhaplotypes in low-recombination regions have significantly lower mutation rates (0.00798 vs. 0.008207, p < 0.001), which substantially reduces false alleles introduced through mutation to enhance the reliability of genotype assignment in paternity.

Validation results

Validation in 1000 genomes trio families

A direct validation of the ability of the selected marker set is to calculate its cumulative paternity index (CPI), which directly predicts whether a child is biologically related to the alleged father, representing the overall probability of a true parent-child relationship. We applied the 30 screened microhaplotypes from low- and high-recombination regions to 580 trio families to calculate CPI. Comparative analysis revealed that the CPI for low-recombination microhaplotypes was significantly higher than that for high-recombination microhaplotypes (Fig. 4C), supporting the superior reliability of low-recombination markers in determining true parentage.

To evaluate the impact of meiotic recombination on paternity testing, we quantified the number of recombination events in real trio families for microhaplotypes from both regions. High-recombination microhaplotypes exhibited recombination events in 202 trios, whereas low-recombination microhaplotypes showed recombination in 83 trios. Further analysis of PI values for recombined microhaplotypes in these families revealed that some high-recombination trios had PI values below 1 (Fig. 4D), indicating that recombination events reduce PI and CPI, thereby compromising the reliability of paternity testing. Similar trends were obtained using different cutoffs for the minimum peak height in recombination event detection method (Figure S4).

Simulated validation results

Using simulated datasets (10,000 positive and 10,000 negative family sets generated via the MHpatools pipeline), we applied the 30 low-recombination microhaplotypes to calculate CPI. The results demonstrated significantly higher CPI values in positive datasets compared to negative datasets (Fig. 4E), validating the rationality and effectiveness of the selected markers. Additionally, re-validation of microhaplotypes from both regions in simulated positive datasets (Fig. 4F) mirrored findings from real trios, with low-recombination markers consistently yielding higher CPI values, further confirming their reliability and superiority.

Microhaplotype database and tool development

Database overview

To facilitate the use of our screened microhaplotypes in paternity testing, we developed a publicly accessible Microhaplotype Database (http://bioinformatics.gqliulab.com/microhaplotype/). The database has two functional panels: Data Query and Online Calculation. In Data Query panel, users can filter microhaplotypes based on criteria such as name, genomic coordinates, forensic parameters (PD, PE), mutation rates, and recombination rates (Fig. 5, left panel). In Online Calculation panel, users can upload haplotype frequency files (.frq.hap generated by Plink v1.9 [37]) to compute forensic parameters for custom microhaplotype sets (Fig. 5, right panel). This resource not only provides curated genetic markers but also enables users to screen and validate microhaplotypes tailored to specific paternity testing needs.

Microhaplotype tool development

By consolidating the workflow described above, we developed a Python-based tool, MHpatools.py, to automate the microhaplotype screening and analysis pipeline. All source code, test files, and documentation are freely available on GitHub (https://github.com/gqliu1010/microhaplotype/) and the project database (http://bioinformatics.gqliulab.com/MHpatools/). Users can view the tool’s functionalities by running “python MHpatools.py –h” in a Linux terminal.

Conclusion

This study represents the first systematic exploration of the impact of meiotic recombination on microhaplotype selection and paternity testing efficacy using data from the 1000 Genomes Project and a high-resolution recombination map. We identified high-performance microhaplotypes resistant to meiotic recombination and mutation. Validation across real-world and simulated datasets confirmed that microhaplotypes screened from low-recombination regions outperform those from high-recombination regions in paternity testing. Notably, the 30 microhaplotypes selected from low-recombination regions achieved superior CPD and CPE values compared to other genetic marker systems. Additionally, we developed a microhaplotype database for forensic parameter computation and marker querying, alongside an open-source tool (MHpatools) encapsulating all analytical workflows from this study. We anticipate that these findings and resources will advance the selection of microhaplotypes and enhance the reliability of paternity testing.
