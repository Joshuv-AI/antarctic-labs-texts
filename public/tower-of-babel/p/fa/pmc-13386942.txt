Prevalence of transfusion-transmissible infections and associated sociodemographic risk factors among blood donors at a tertiary care hospital in Lahore, Pakistan
Authors: Mejerrah Aamir, Ghulam Mujtaba, Aatif Amin, Tariq Bashir, Aurooj Butt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood transfusion is an essential component of healthcare delivery but poses a risk of transfusion-transmissible infections (TTIs), particularly in low- and middle-income countries where donor screening systems may be inadequate. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of major TTIs and assess their association with sociodemographic characteristics among blood donors at a tertiary care hospital in Lahore, Pakistan.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using blood donor records of 550 individuals. All donations were routinely screened for hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), syphilis, and malaria. Prevalence rates were calculated using descriptive statistics. Associations between TTIs and donor characteristics were evaluated using chi-square tests. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of infection.ResultsThe overall prevalence of TTIs was 12.18%. HBV was the most prevalent infection (4.73%), followed by HCV (4.18%), syphilis (2.55%), and HIV (0.73%). No cases of malaria were detected. Significant associations were observed between selected TTIs and donor characteristics, particularly age and donor status. Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that these sociodemographic factors remained independently associated with infection status.ConclusionsA substantial burden of transfusion-transmissible infections persists among blood donors in this setting. Strengthening donor selection, improving screening strategies, and promoting voluntary non-remunerated blood donation are essential to enhance transfusion safety and reduce the risk of TTIs in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Transfusion-transmissible infections, Blood donors, Hepatitis B virus, Hepatitis C virus, Human immunodeficiency virus, Syphilis, Blood transfusion safety, Seroprevalence, Pakistan

Introduction

Blood transfusion is an indispensable medical treatment that supports the transfer of whole blood or blood parts from healthy donors to patients in order to replenish blood volume, enhance oxygen supply or rectify coagulation defects. It is important in cases of accidents, big surgeries, childbirth emergencies, blood-related diseases, and cancer treatment. Every year, about 118 million blood donations are made all over the world which emphasizes the importance of transfusion services as an integral part of the healthcare systems globally [1, 2].

Blood transfusion, though very beneficial, poses the risk of transmitting infections through transfusion i.e. transfusion-transmissible infections (TTIs) that are still a big concern especially in low and middle-income countries where the situation is worse. Poor donor selection, lack of efficient screening technologies, and weak regulatory control greatly increase the risks of infections through transfusion [3, 4]. Besides, these difficulties are more evident in poor countries that have not yet developed their blood transfusion infrastructure [5, 6].

TTIs are caused by the transfusion of blood that is infected with pathogens of different sorts - viruses, bacteria, or parasites - and may result in acute or chronic illness with severe health implications over a long period. Among the pathogens, the viral ones are the most worrying since the donors infected with them might not show any symptoms for a long time thus leading to transmission during the diagnostic window period even when routine screening is carried out [6, 7]. The hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV types I and II), syphilis, and malaria are classified as the most significant TTIs clinically and hence are mandated for screening under international recommendations [2, 7].

Nevertheless, the use of quick serological tests, particularly in places with limited resources, hinders the identification of cases in the initial period of infection [8]. Wealthy nations have effectively minimized the risk of transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs) to a great extent due to the establishment of centralized blood services, the application of rigorous donor selection criteria, and the use of highly sophisticated screening techniques such as nucleic acid testing. Conversely, the majority of the underdeveloped areas still depend on serological or rapid tests primarily, which leads to a higher residual risk of TTIs [6, 9].

Transfusion-transmitted viral infections are mainly caused by HBV, HCV, and HIV worldwide, with low- and middle-income countries carrying the highest burden [7]. Although syphilis and malaria are confined to specific areas, they still play a significant role in the transmission of infections through blood in the regions where they are endemic [10].

Pakistan is among the high-burden countries for TTIs, mainly HBV and HCV, where millions of patients are living with chronic infections, and many of them are still unnoticed and fit for blood donation [11, 12].

The prevalence of TTIs among blood donors in Pakistan varies greatly from place to place with the regional disease burden, the strategies for donor recruitment, and the screening practices being the major factors influencing the variation. HCV has always been reported as the most common TTI followed by HBV, syphilis, HIV, and malaria [4, 13].

Even though blood screening is obligatory, the safety of transfusions is undermined by the irregular implementation of blood screening, dependence on rapid testing, scattered transfusion services, and reliance on family replacement donors [14, 15]. The country’s blood transfusion system is decentralized and divided into distinct segments: public hospitals, private medical institutions, and independent blood banks. National directives are followed to varying degrees in each segment [16],

In Pakistan, blood banks usually use rapid diagnostic tests as the main screening method, while enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is used for confirmation when available [17]. Although ELISA has very high sensitivity and specificity, the cost involved and the technical know-how still make it hard for low-resource areas to adopt it widely [18].

The aim of this research was to determine the prevalence of major transfusion-transmissible infections, such as the hepatitis B virus, the hepatitis C virus, the human immunodeficiency virus, malaria, and syphilis, in blood donors in Lahore, Pakistan. Moreover, it includes the investigation of the relationship between some sociodemographic and donation-related traits (age, body weight, and donor status) and the prevalence of infections, and to interpret the results in the light of national and international evidence available.

Methodology

Study design and study population

This retrospective cross-sectional analytical study focused on blood donors at the Blood Bank of the Institute of Nuclear Medicine and Oncology (INMOL) in Lahore, Pakistan. Its objective was to find out how common the transfusion-transmissible infections (TTIs) were and how they related to demographic and donation factors that had been specifically selected, all done with routinely maintained blood bank records as secondary data. All persons that came to INMOL Blood Bank to give blood, and who also met institutional eligibility criteria during the study period, were included. The INMOL blood bank received its blood donations from two main sources which included voluntary donors and family replacement donors. The study does not include commercial donors because national regulations prohibit paid blood donation. The donor-type classification remained unavailable throughout the retrospective records and therefore could not be analysed separately.

Due to the characteristics of this being a record-based study, there wouldn’t be any interaction with the donors and extra biological samples weren’t collected. The blood screening for HCV, HBV, HIV, syphilis, and malaria was done following the guidelines of both the national and institutional blood transfusion regulations. The screening of Hepatitis B (HBsAg), Hepatitis C (anti-HCV antibodies), HIV (anti-HIV-1/2 antibodies) and syphilis was performed using chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) on the Roche Cobas e411 immunoassay analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The screening of Malaria was performed using rapid immunochromatographic antigen detection test following the standard blood bank protocols. Reactive samples were managed according to institutional blood bank protocol. NAT testing or any other confirmatory testing beyond the CLIA was not performed. The results of screening for each blood donor were recorded in the registers by the blood bank staff. Only the final screening results that were recorded in the blood bank registers were taken and analyzed. No laboratory testing was done especially for this study. Study setting

INMOL Hospital Blood Bank, which is located in Lahore at a government tertiary care hospital, was the context of this research. The hospital offers diagnosis and treatment including nuclear medicine and oncology care. The blood bank serves both voluntary and replacement donors, mainly from Lahore and neighboring areas, and adheres to national and institutional policies regarding the selection of donors and screening for TTI’s.

Study duration

Data were retrospectively collected from the blood bank records covering a one-year period from October 2024 to October 2025. A total of 630 donors presented during the study period, of which 550 records met the inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All blood donors whose presence at the INMOL Blood Bank coincided with the study period and who met the standard eligibility criteria were considered for this study. The donors were a minimum of 18 years of age, weighed 50 kg or more and passed the routine clinical and hematological fitness tests.

If the donors were disqualified due to low hemoglobin level of < 13.5 g/dL, low platelet count of < 150 × 10⁹/L, low body weight of < 50 kg, hypertension or hypotension with systolic blood pressure < 100 mmHg or > 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure < 70 or > 90 mmHg, very high or too low pulse rate of < 60 or > 100 beats per minute, fever or very high temperature of > 98.6 °F, acute illness or substance abuse, or use of prohibited drugs, they would be excluded from the study. Donors were automatically excluded if they had given whole blood within the past 12–16 weeks or had undergone platelet apheresis within the past 2–4 weeks, according to the institutional guidelines.

To increase the statistical power, to enable subgroup and regression analyses, and to cope with the variability encountered in local studies which include the higher prevalence rates reported at a Lahore university hospital [19] - a larger sample was taken. The final analysis was based on 550 eligible blood donor records which is more than the minimum requirement and hence it increased the precision and robustness of estimates.

Data collection and variables

Data were obtained from blood bank records through a structured data collection format. Only one record per donor was included in the final dataset. The variables included demographic features (age, weight, occupation), donation-related factors (donor status and blood group), and the results of screening tests for TTIs.

Age limit was in categories of 18–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60, and above 60 years. Weight was classified into three groups: 50–75 kg, 75–100 kg, and > 100 kg. Occupation was defined as student, employed, or unemployed. Donor status was classified as repeat or non-repeat donor, where repeat donors were defined as the individuals that have donated blood at least once previously. Blood groups were documented according to the ABO and Rh systems. The blood bank registers were missing consistent documentation of gender and marital status and educational level and HBV vaccination history, which resulted in their exclusion from the study analysis. Screening outcomes for HCV, HBV, HIV, syphilis, and malaria were recorded as positive or negative.

Data coding and management

A meticulous inspection was done after collecting data. The data was then manually cleaned, coded, and entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (MS Office Excel 2016). The data set was screened for completeness. The study excluded donor records which had essential study data missing to maintain consistent statistical assessment. The process was executed during data cleaning through a complete-case method. The final dataset included only those donors who had complete documentation of all analyzed variables. The data was then imported into SPSS software version 27 (IBM) for further analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS. Descriptive statistics were generated for donor demographics, donation characteristics, and screening results. Categorical variables were represented by frequencies and percentages.

The association between categorical independent variables and infection status was tested with the chi-square test. A p-value of < 0.05 was taken as significant statistically.

Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate crude odds ratios (COR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Initially, univariate logistic regression was performed for each independent variable. Variables with estimable odds ratios were subsequently entered into the multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of infection. Variables with categories containing zero outcome events produced non-estimable parameters (COR = 0.000; CI = 0.000) due to complete separation and were therefore excluded from the multivariate analysis. For categorical variables, one category was designated as the reference group against which other categories were compared in the logistic regression analysis. The Enter method of SPSS was used to conduct multivariate logistic regression analysis which required all chosen independent variables to be added to the model at once for the purpose of controlling potential confounding variables. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test served to evaluate model calibration and goodness-of-fit by comparing actual results with predicted outcomes from the logistic regression models.

In regression analyses, age and weight were split into two categories (≤ 40 versus > 40 years; ≤75 versus > 75 kg) to support the model’s stability and increase its statistical power. Due to the extremely small number of HIV-positive cases identified in the dataset (n = 4), chi square and logistic regression analysis was not performed for HIV infection. Instead, HIV results were summarized using descriptive statistics only, as regression analysis with such sparse data would produce unreliable estimates.

Ethical considerations

The study, which was a retrospective analysis of anonymized blood bank records, did not involve direct contact with the donors and did not require informed consent as the case was in that instance. Throughout the whole process of the study, confidentiality and privacy were constantly kept of the donor’s information.

Results

Sociodemographic and donation related characteristics of blood donors

The study included a total of 550 blood donors. The most prevalent blood group was B-positive (38.4%), then O-positive (24.9%), and A-positive (23.1%), whereas Rh-negative blood types were rare, with A-negative being the least common (0.5%). The majority of donors were within the age bracket of 18–30 years (58.0%), followed by 30–40 years (30.2%), with a lesser percentage being over 40 years. More than half of the donors were of 50–75 kg weight (54.7%), and only 4.9% were of 100 kg or more weight. Most of the donors were employees (76.7%), and the students and unemployed accounted for 10.9% and 12.4% respectively. The sample consisted mainly of repeat donors representing 83.3%, and the first-time (non-repeated) donors made up 16.7% (Table 1).

Prevalence of transfusion-transmissible infections (TTIs)

The prevalence of transfusion-transmissible infections (TTIs) among blood donors overall was found to be 12.18%. Of the infections, HBV was the most common one with 4.73% of the donors being infected, followed by HCV with 4.18%. The least number of donors infected with 0.73% were HIV positive while 2.55% had syphilis. No cases of malaria were detected during the study period (Table 2).

Discussion

The research conducted at the time revealed blood donors to have a total infection rate of 12.18%, where the hepatitis B virus (4.73%) and hepatitis C virus (4.18%) were indicated as the most frequent infections, followed by syphilis (2.55%) and HIV (0.73%), while no malaria cases were reported. This is a significant increase when compared to the eastern part of Pakistan where several large-scale regional studies have reported a much lower prevalence [20, 21]. For instance, a four-year retrospective study performed in Peshawar gave an overall TTI prevalence of 4.61% with substantially lower rates for HBV and HCV [10]. Likewise, an extensive review of over 300,000 blood donors conducted in northern Pakistan registered a total TTI prevalence of 3.33% [22], while another research project covering a large patient population reported an overall prevalence of 3.9% [23]. The study found that there were no malaria-positive cases among the donors who participated. Blood donor studies conducted in Pakistan report similar results because they found extremely low malaria prevalence among screened donors [15, 24]. The present cohort lacks malaria because strict donor screening procedures enable the hospital to serve an urban donor population which resides in an area with low malaria transmission. The practice of routine screening needs to continue because asymptomatic parasitemia can occur in patients which creates a risk for people who will receive transfusions.

On the other hand, hospital-based studies have generally indicated higher rates of infection. A study done at Jinnah Hospital, Lahore, showed prevalence rates of HCV and HBV that were nearly the same as the ones in the present study, but a TTI rate was not mentioned [19]. Besides, small hospital-based studies have reported higher rates of TTI, which points to the role of contextual and institutional factors in determining the infection burden among donors [25]. The differences in infection rates could be due to variations in the criteria for donor selection, the methods of screening, and the composition of donors. TTI prevalence has been linked to the practice of relying heavily on replacement or first-time donors since pre-donation screening is not so stringent and these donors also possess a higher baseline risk than voluntary repeat donors [26]. Additionally, geographic variation, health literacy levels, and population-specific risk behaviors may further contribute to differences in reported prevalence.

Global comparisons strengthen this variability, with the studies from sub-Saharan Africa reporting the donor TTI prevalence of more than 10%, while the developed areas show a huge decrease in the rates owing to the proper donor selection and the rigorous screening [27, 28]. On the other hand, the studies from India show that the TTI prevalence is as low as 1.46%, which is a clear indication of the effectiveness of blood safety measures [29]. Systematic global evaluations reveal considerable differences between high- and low-resource settings in a similar manner [30].

The relatively high prevalence found in this study reveals very clearly the public health problems that are still there, one of them being the incomplete vaccination coverage, the lack of sensitive screening technologies, the presence of sociocultural factors that discourage voluntary blood donation, etc. These results point towards the necessity of better donor education, screening procedures that are more efficient, and transfusion safety programs that have been fortified.

Age demonstrated a strong relationship with the positivity rate of HBV and syphilis, where the higher prevalence was noticed among the 40–50 years old group. This is consistent with the evidence from Pakistan and other places, where older age has always been linked to the increased TTI risk as a result of the prolonged exposure and lower vaccination coverage in aging populations [31–34]. Conversely, the present study found no relationship across the age groups with HCV infection. This is in contrast with the results from northern Pakistan, where higher TTI prevalence was found among the younger donors [22]. Guerrero-García et al. (2021) and Kumari (2020) mention that the differences could be due to different transmission patterns, donor eligibility criteria and sample characteristics that vary by geography [35, 36].

Among the infections, donor status was statistically correlated with HBV and syphilis, the first-time donors being those with the highest rates of infection. The research showed that repeat donors have lower infection rates because their earlier screening tests and transfusion safety education provided them with better knowledge about their risk factors. In contrast, first-time donors may include individuals with previously undiagnosed infections or limited awareness regarding transfusion-transmissible disease risks. These findings reinforce the importance of promoting regular voluntary repeat blood donation programs as an effective strategy for improving transfusion safety in Pakistan. This corroborates the evidence of various national and international sources which states that repeat donors are a lower-risk population because of the prior screening, the rising awareness and the self-deferral of high-risk individuals [10, 21, 26, 37]. Similar findings have been reported across different countries which resulted in the same conclusion that repeat donors are a low-risk population due to prior screening, self-deferral of high-risk individuals and increased awareness of transfusion safety [21, 32]. There was no significant association between the ABO/Rh blood groups and the transmission of infections, which is in line with most studies that state blood group is not an independent risk factor [15]. In the same way, body weight did not correlate with the infection status which shows that body weight is not of much importance in donor-based TTI studies [31]. The study could not conduct gender-based analysis because the research sample contained almost all male donors with only one female donor in the dataset. The observed pattern shows how blood donation in Pakistan mainly depends on male donors who are influenced by both sociocultural factors and logistical challenges. Overall, the findings are highly consistent with existing national and international literature, with observed variations attributable to differences in donor demographics, epidemiological patterns, and study design.

The univariate and multivariate logistic regression usage provided a much stronger measure of independent risk factors in comparison to the chi-square analysis that controlled for the potential confounders only [21].

For HBV, both age above 40 years and non-repeated donor status were significant predictors even after adjustment, thereby confirming their independent association with HBsAg positive. The findings indicate that HBV infection risk among blood donors shows different patterns across various donor groups with older and first-time donors representing the comparatively higher-risk populations. The observed association with older age may reflect cumulative lifetime exposure and lower vaccination uptake in earlier decades. The higher risk found in non-repeated donors demonstrates the importance of routine donor screening combined with educational programs for donors. These results agree with the previous studies done in Pakistan and other areas which pointed out cumulative exposure, the past gaps in vaccination coverage, and first-time donors being more at risk [26]. There are some studies that have highlighted the attenuation of the associations related to age after adjustment, pointing out that regional factors may determine the strength of this relationship [31].

In the case of HCV, non-repeated donor status was found to be the only independent predictor in multivariate analysis among others. This finding further supports the role of donor status as an important determinant of transfusion safety, emphasizing the comparatively lower-risk profile of repeat donors. The difference in findings from the chi-square and regression is most likely due to the fact that they are based on different methodologies since logistic regression takes confounding into consideration and provides the risk estimates [38]. Similar relationships have been documented both nationally and internationally [26, 35, 39]. The singular association noted with the A-negative blood group in the univariate analysis was not carried forward after adjustment and should be cautiously interpreted due to limited data.

In present study, only 4 HIV positive cases were reported. The study needed sufficient outcome events for statistical models because these events were needed to make accurate predictions, so regression analysis was not conducted for HIV infection. HIV findings were presented through descriptive summary methods. Blood donor surveillance studies face similar difficulties because they cannot execute valid statistical association tests when HIV prevalence remains extremely low [26].

The results of the syphilis analyses were consistent, as both the chi-square and regression analyses pointed to older age and non-repeated donor status as the significant factors even after adjustment. This reliability of the results highlights a strong independent relationship and goes along with the findings from Ethiopia and Pakistan [34], where syphilis was more common in older and first-time donors [32].

The combined use of chi-square and logistic regression analyses enabled allowed identification of donor characteristics that showed independent association to TTI positivity. The results showed that TTI risk distribution varied across different donor groups because older donors and non-repeated donors represented higher-risk groups. The research demonstrates that resource-limited countries like Pakistan need better blood transfusion safety through risk-based donor recruitment methods and improved donor education and ongoing blood donation programs and continuous disease pattern monitoring.

Limitations

The study faces multiple challenges. The research was conducted at a single tertiary care center which limits its ability to apply results to other locations. The study used retrospective design to assess blood bank records which did not provide complete access to all donor demographic information including gender distribution, vaccination history, rural or urban residence, donation frequency, marital and educational status. The lack of access to advanced molecular screening methods which included nucleic acid testing (NAT) may have resulted in failure to detect infections during the study period. Blood donor populations do not reflect the general population because donors must complete pre-donation screening which creates a possibility of selection bias. The study failed to include longitudinal follow-up data which made it impossible to study changes in infection rates over time.

Conclusion

The study carried out at a tertiary care hospital in Lahore revealed that blood donors had a significant occurrence of transfusion-transmitted infections, with HBV and HCV being the most common viruses detected. It was found that some sociodemographic factors, mainly age and donor status, were significantly related to infection positivity, and some were still independent predictors after adjustment. The results call for better donor screening, regular voluntary blood donation promotion, and continuous monitoring to make blood transfusion practices safer.
