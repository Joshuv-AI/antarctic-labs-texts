Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses reveal the regulatory network underlying NtGSTU10-mediated nicotine synthesis and transport in tobacco
Authors: Yudan Zhou, Yuanfei Lou, Min Xie, Kai Pi, Zejun Mo, Chenhao Shi, Junting Zhang, Renxiang Liu, Qiwei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNicotine is the major alkaloid in tobacco and plays a key role in determining tobacco quality. Previous studies have shown that overexpression of NtGSTU10 enhances nicotine accumulation in tobacco hybrids; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain largely unclear. In this study, we investigated the regulatory role of NtGSTU10 in nicotine synthesis and distribution using integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses in NtGSTU10-overexpressing tobacco plants. Nicotine content and distribution between roots and leaves were quantified, and global transcriptional and metabolic profiles were analyzed to reveal molecular changes associated with NtGSTU10 overexpression. The results showed that NtGSTU10 overexpression was associated with increased nicotine accumulation in leaves and reduced nicotine levels in roots, resulting in significantly higher nicotine transport coefficients compared with wild-type plants. Transcriptomic analysis revealed coordinated changes in genes involved in nicotine biosynthesis, transport, and secondary metabolism. Metabolomic profiling indicated significant alterations in amino acid metabolism, nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism, purine metabolism, sugar metabolism, and alkaloid biosynthesis pathways. Integrated transcriptome–metabolome analysis further identified coordinated regulation of glutathione metabolism, ABC transporters, and MATE transporter–encoding genes. Overall, these results reveal a regulatory network associated with NtGSTU10-mediated nicotine biosynthesis and transport, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying nicotine metabolism in tobacco.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45473-6.Keywords: Tobacco, Nicotine, Transport, Transcriptome, MetabolomeSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Nicotine is the predominant alkaloid in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.), accounting for more than 90% of the total alkaloid content in tobacco plants1. As the principal bioactive compound in tobacco, nicotine plays a decisive role in determining the commercial value and sensory quality of tobacco products. In addition to its economic importance, nicotine functions as a defensive secondary metabolite that protects tobacco plants against herbivorous insects and other environmental stresses2. In tobacco plants, nicotine is synthesized primarily in the roots and subsequently transported through the xylem to the aerial tissues, where it accumulates mainly in leaf vacuoles3. Therefore, both nicotine biosynthesis and long-distance transport are critical biological processes influencing nicotine accumulation in tobacco leaves.

The biosynthesis of nicotine is a complex metabolic process involving multiple enzymatic steps and regulatory genes. Structurally, nicotine consists of a pyridine ring and a pyrrolidine ring derived from different metabolic pathways. The pyrrolidine ring originates from ornithine or arginine, which are converted into putrescine through the activities of ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) and arginine decarboxylase (ADC), respectively4,5. Putrescine is then methylated by putrescine N-methyltransferase (PMT) to form N-methylputrescine6, which is subsequently oxidized by N-methylputrescine oxidase (MPO) to generate the N-methylpyrrolinium cation7. Meanwhile, the pyridine ring is derived from aspartic acid through the quinolinic acid pathway, where quinolinic acid synthase (QS) and quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase (QPT) play essential roles in producing nicotinic acid intermediates involved in nicotine biosynthesis8,9. Finally, the condensation of the pyridine and pyrrolidine moieties is catalyzed by enzymes such as A622 and berberine bridge enzyme-like proteins (BBLs), resulting in the formation of nicotine10–13.

In addition to biosynthesis, the transport and compartmentalization of nicotine are essential processes that determine its accumulation in tobacco leaves14. Several transport proteins have been identified as key regulators of nicotine movement within tobacco plants. For example, the nicotine uptake permease NtNUP1 mediates nicotine transport across the plasma membrane and contributes to nicotine homeostasis in root tissues15. Members of the multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) transporter family, including NtMATE1, NtMATE2, NtJAT1, and NtJAT2, play important roles in vacuolar sequestration and long-distance transport of nicotine16,17. These transport systems protect plant cells from the toxic effects of alkaloids by transporting nicotine into vacuoles for storage. In addition, ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters have also been implicated in the transport of secondary metabolites, including nicotine18. Despite the identification of multiple biosynthetic enzymes and transporter proteins, the regulatory networks coordinating nicotine biosynthesis and transport remain incompletely understood.

Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) constitute a large and diverse family of enzymes widely distributed in plants. GSTs are traditionally known for their roles in detoxification and stress responses, where they catalyze the conjugation of glutathione to various electrophilic compounds. However, increasing evidence suggests that certain GST family members participate in the transport and accumulation of plant secondary metabolites. For example, GST proteins such as VvGST1 and VvGST4 in grapevine have been shown to facilitate the intracellular transport and sequestration of anthocyanins19,20. Similarly, GSTs have been implicated in the trafficking of flavonoids and other specialized metabolites in several plant species21. These findings indicate that GST proteins may function as carrier or adaptor proteins involved in metabolite transport and compartmentalization.

In our previous study, integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses were conducted to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying nicotine heterosis in tobacco hybrids, leading to the identification of the gene NtGSTU10 as a potential regulator associated with nicotine accumulation. Functional analysis further demonstrated that overexpression of NtGSTU10 significantly enhanced nicotine content in tobacco hybrids22. Although these findings suggested a potential role of NtGSTU10 in nicotine metabolism, the molecular mechanisms by which NtGSTU10 influences nicotine biosynthesis and distribution remain largely unclear. In particular, it is not known whether NtGSTU10 directly participates in nicotine transport or indirectly regulates nicotine metabolism through broader transcriptional and metabolic networks.

Recent advances in high-throughput omics technologies have provided powerful tools for investigating complex metabolic processes in plants. Transcriptomics enables the global characterization of gene expression patterns, whereas metabolomics allows comprehensive profiling of metabolic changes associated with specific biological conditions. The integration of transcriptomic and metabolomic data has proven particularly effective for uncovering regulatory networks underlying plant secondary metabolism. By combining these complementary approaches, it is possible to identify coordinated transcriptional and metabolic responses associated with specific genetic modifications.

Therefore, the objective of the present study was to investigate the regulatory mechanisms associated with NtGSTU10-mediated nicotine metabolism in tobacco using integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. Specifically, we analyzed nicotine accumulation and distribution between roots and leaves in NtGSTU10-overexpressing tobacco plants and performed comprehensive transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of these tissues. Through integrated multi-omics analysis, we aimed to identify key metabolic pathways and regulatory genes associated with NtGSTU10 expression. The results of this study provide new insights into the transcriptional and metabolic networks underlying nicotine biosynthesis and transport and contribute to a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms regulating nicotine accumulation in tobacco.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growing conditions

The wild-type tobacco cultivar K326 and three NtGSTU10-overexpression tobacco lines derived from the GDH11 background were used as plant materials in this study. GDH11 is a previously characterized tobacco line that served as the genetic background for transformation. Field experiments were conducted using a randomized block design with three biological replicates, each consisting of three rows per plot and 15 plants per row. The experiment was conducted in 2024 at the Anshun Tobacco Research Base of Guizhou University, using floating seedling cultivation. Seeds were sown on February 18 and transplanted on April 23. When 50% of the central flowers bloomed, all tobacco plants were topped, leaving 20 leaves. Cultivation and management techniques were based on the local high-quality flue-cured tobacco cultivation and production guidelines. The tobacco seedlings used in this study were cultivated in our laboratory from seeds of Nicotiana tabacum cv. K326 and NtGSTU10-overexpression transgenic lines.

Sample collection and nicotine measurement

Three healthy, disease-free individual plants were randomly selected from each experimental area on the day of topping, 7 days after topping, and 14 days after topping. Three leaves were collected from each plant at the 9th–11th leaf positions. Fresh leaf samples were collected using the punch method, placed in low-temperature storage tubes, and temporarily stored in liquid nitrogen to ensure that the root systems remained intact. Young root and stem samples were thoroughly washed, placed in cryogenic tubes, and stored in liquid nitrogen. Once all samples have been collected, they were stored in a -80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer until use for determining the relative expression levels of the NtGSTU10 gene, as well as for transcriptomics sequencing and metabolomics analysis. The remaining tobacco leaves were killed at 105 °C for 30 min, dried at 75 °C, and then ground to determine the nicotine content. Nicotine extraction and content determination were based on the method described by. All nicotine content measurements and nicotine transport coefficient calculations were performed using three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Vector construction and genetic transformation

NtGSTU10 was amplified by PCR and inserted into the pBWA(V)HS plasmid driven by the CaMV35S promoter. The constructed overexpression plasmid pBWA(V)HS-35S:NtGSTU10 was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Genetic transformation of the tobacco variety, K326, was performed using the leaf disc transformation method. Specific primers were designed for the Hyg sequence and synthesized by Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). DNA was extracted from the leaves of the transformed plants and used for the detection of the transfected gene via PCR. The overexpressing strains were screened and identified using qRT-PCR, and the mutation sites were sequenced and analyzed. Primer sequences used for qRT-PCR analysis are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Expression measurement

Total RNA was extracted from the root, stem, and leaf samples using TIANGEN’s RNAprep Pure Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Plant RNA Mini Kit). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using TIANGEN’s FastKing gDNA Dispersing RT SuperMix First Chain Synthesis Premix, which was designed for the NtABCG6L gene using the NCBI primer design tool and a specificity comparison online software (Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The synthesized cDNA was used for PCR detection of the introduced gene. Finally, a fluorescent quantitative system was prepared using the SYBRPRIME qPCR Kit (Baoguang Bio), and fluorescent product amplification was performed using the Bio-RAD CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-RAD, USA). The relative expression levels of genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method23.

Yeast transports nicotine

Following yeast transport experimental method, the NtGSTU10 gene was cloned into the pYES2 plasmid. The constructed pYES2-ABC plasmid was introduced into YMM12 yeast competent cells, which were screened for positive clones and inoculated into 50 mL of SD-Ura liquid medium. This was incubated at 30 °C and 200 rpm until the OD600 reached 0.4–0.6. After collecting the cells, they were cultured in a sugar-free medium to deplete the glucose in the cells, and 2% galactose was added to the SD-Ura medium for induction. Culturing was done on a shaking incubator at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 16–18 h. After induction, the bacterial suspension was diluted 1:10, and 2 mM nicotine was added, followed by culturing for 6 h. After incubation, the cells were collected and washed twice with PBS to remove residual nicotine and culture medium, and nicotine was extracted from the cells using a solution of 50% ethanol, 49.5% methanol, and 0.05% acetic acid (by volume). Cell breakdown was caused by vigorous shaking and the disruption by glass beads. The extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane and analyzed for nicotine using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an Agilent 1260 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). An Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18-A column was used, with a mobile phase of methanol: 20 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) = 40:60, column temperature of 30 °C, detection wavelength of 254 nm, flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, and injection volume of 10 μL. Nicotine content in roots, stems, and leaves was quantified on a dry weight basis, and all samples were dried to constant weight prior to analysis to ensure consistency across tissues.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

For transcriptomic analysis, three independent biological replicates were collected for each tissue (roots and leaves) from wild-type and NtGSTU10-overexpressing plants. Each biological replicate consisted of pooled samples from three individual plants. First, RNA extraction was performed to ensure that the sample had sufficient transcript sequencing quality (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0). mRNA with polyA tails was enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads and randomly fragmented, and short RNA fragments were selected using magnetic beads as templates for cDNA synthesis. DNA purification magnetic beads were used for fragment size selection, and PCR enrichment was performed to obtain the final cDNA library. After passing the warehouse inspection, sequencing was performed using the Illumina platform. Clean reads were aligned to the tobacco genome, and the expression levels of genes and transcripts were quantified using the RSEM software24. The DESeq2 R software package was used to analyze differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between samples25, and a set of differentially expressed genes between the two biological conditions was obtained. Genes with a fold change ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 and FDR < 0.05 were defined as DEGs. Finally, GO functional annotation, KOG functional classification, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed on the DEGs26, with a corrected p-value (p_fdr) ≤ 0.05 as the significance criterion.

Metabolomics sequencing analysis

Metabolomic profiling was performed using three independent biological replicates per group, with each replicate derived from an independent plant sample. The samples were placed in a freeze dryer (Scientz-100F) for vacuum freeze-drying and then ground into a powder using a grinder (30 Hz, 1.5 min). Next, 50 mg of the powder was dissolved in 1000 μL of 70% methanol internal standard extraction solution. Then, 500 μL petroleum ether was added, the mixture was vortexed for 5 min, allowed to settle, separated, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE filter membrane into the glass liner of a brown sample vial, and stored at -20 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis. Qualitative analysis of substances was performed using secondary spectrum information based on the MWDB (Metware Database). During analysis, isotope signals, repetitive signals containing K + , Na + , and NH4 + ions, and repetitive signals from fragment ions that were themselves part of larger molecular weight substances were removed. After obtaining the metabolic mass spectrometry analysis data from different samples, the peak areas of all substances were integrated, and the mass spectrometry peaks of the same metabolite in different samples were corrected by integration.

Data analysis methods

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple range test were used for variance analysis and multiple comparisons (α = 0.05). The nicotine transport coefficient was calculated as the ratio of nicotine content (dry weight basis) in leaves to that in roots.

Results and analysis

Phenotypic and nicotine distribution changes in NtGSTU10-overexpressing tobacco lines.

We investigated the effect of the NtGSTU10 gene on nicotine content in tobacco. In this study, the tobacco line GDH11 was used as the genetic background, and Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation was performed to generate tobacco plants overexpressing NtGSTU10 (Fig. 1A).Quantitative real-time PCR analysis showed that the expression levels of NtGSTU10 in the overexpression lines (OE1, OE2, and OE3) were significantly higher than those in the wild-type (WT) plants (Fig. 1B).

(A) Related pictures of genetic transformation process. (B) Expression levels of NtGSTU10 in each test material. Difference analysis of (C) nicotine content in root, (D) stem, (E) leaf, and (F) of transfer coefficient.

The nicotine content in the roots of each NtGSTU10-overexpressing transgenic tobacco line was quantified on the day of topping, as well as 7 and 14 d after topping (Fig. 1C). The nicotine content in the stems was analyzed at the same time points (Fig. 1D). Similarly, nicotine accumulation in the leaves was determined on the day of topping, 7 d after topping, and 14 d after topping (Fig. 1E).In the roots, on the day of topping, the nicotine content showed line-dependent differences among the NtGSTU10-overexpressing lines, with OE3 exhibiting a higher level than the wild type (WT), while OE2 showed no significant difference (Fig. 1C).The nicotine level even decreased significantly across lines from 7 to 14 days after topping. In the stems, all the overexpression lines had significantly higher nicotine content than the wild type on the day of topping; however, according to the statistical analysis indicated by lowercase letters in Fig. 1D, these differences were no longer significant at 7 and 14 days after topping.In the leaves, the nicotine content in the three overexpression lines did not differ from that of the WT on the day of topping (Fig. 1E). However, at 7 and 14 d after topping, the nicotine accumulated significantly in the leaves of all overexpression lines relative to the WT level. Overall, NtGSTU10 overexpression was associated with a clear shift in nicotine distribution between roots and leaves. At 7 and 14 days after topping, leaf nicotine content in overexpression lines was increased by approximately 25–40% relative to the wild type, whereas root nicotine content was reduced by approximately 15–30% (mean ± SD, n = 3 biological replicates), indicating a substantial effect size in addition to statistical significance.

NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with altered nicotine transport coefficients.

The nicotine transport coefficient was defined as the ratio of nicotine content in leaves to that in roots and was used as a relative proxy to reflect nicotine distribution between the two organs rather than a direct measurement of transport flux. On the day of topping, the nicotine transport coefficient of OE1 was significantly higher than that of the other overexpression lines and the wild type, whereas no significant differences were observed among the remaining lines based on the statistical analysis shown in Fig. 1F. On days 7 and 14 after topping, the transport coefficients of the overexpression lines were significantly higher than those of the WT lines. These results indicate that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with an increased nicotine transport coefficient, suggesting a potential involvement of NtGSTU10 in processes affecting nicotine distribution between roots and leaves.

To further examine whether NtGSTU10 expression affects nicotine accumulation in a heterologous system, we expressed NtGSTU10 in yeast, cultured the transformants in liquid medium containing nicotine, and subsequently measured intracellular nicotine levels. However, this result does not distinguish between enhanced nicotine uptake, intracellular retention, or altered detoxification processes, and therefore does not provide direct evidence that NtGSTU10 functions as a membrane nicotine transporter. Quantitatively, the nicotine transport coefficient of NtGSTU10-overexpressing lines was increased by approximately 20–35% compared with the wild type at 7 and 14 days after topping (mean ± SD, n = 3), indicating not only statistical significance but also a biologically meaningful effect size (Fig. 2).

Transcriptome analysis

To further analyze the molecular basis of NtGSTU10 involvement in nicotine synthesis and transport, we selected the OE3 strain and wild-type K326. These two lines exhibited relatively high nicotine content and transport coefficients, particularly in the leaves. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were subsequently performed on both roots and leaves to comprehensively characterize the regulatory role of NtGSTU10 in nicotine synthesis and transport in tobacco. In this study, good repeatability was observed among biological replicates of both mutant and wild-type samples in roots and leaves (Fig. 3A, B).

(A) Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot of two comparison group samples. (B) Correlation diagram of samples. (C) Difference in gene statistics of the two comparison groups. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEG’s) in (D) leaves, and (E) root.

At a significant level of P ≤ 0.05 and fold change value ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67, there were 2,992 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) observed in the OE leaves (OEL) relative to wild-type leaves (WTL), among which 1,623 genes were upregulated and 1,369 genes were downregulated (Fig. 3C). A total of 11,598 DEGs were observed in the OE root system (OER) relative to wild-type root system (WTR), of which 6,086 genes were upregulated and 5,512 genes were downregulated. Nicotine is a secondary metabolite synthesized in the roots of tobacco plants and transported to the leaves via the xylem for accumulation. The overexpression mutant plants exhibited a larger number of DEGs in the root system than in the leaves, indicating that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with more extensive transcriptional reprogramming in roots. However, this observation alone does not establish a stronger regulatory role in roots, as the organ-specific expression levels of NtGSTU10 and the baseline transcriptional complexity may also contribute to this difference. However, the organ-specific expression patterns of NtGSTU10 in roots and leaves under overexpression conditions require further investigation to fully explain the contrasting nicotine levels between the two tissues.

To clarify the metabolic pathways regulating these differentially expressed genes, KEGG metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the DEGs observed in OEL vs WTL and OER vs WTR. These DEGs in the leaves were mainly enriched in metabolic pathways such as plant-pathogen interactions, porphyrin metabolism, carbon metabolism, cofactor biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, photosynthesis, glycerol ester metabolism, zeatin biosynthesis, glutathione, and ABC transporters (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with transcriptional changes in pathways related to signal transduction, carbon metabolism, and substance biosynthesis and transport, which may contribute to the altered nicotine accumulation observed in tobacco leaves. The DEGs in the roots were mainly enriched in plant-pathogen interactions; glutathione metabolism; biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites; toluene, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis; flavonoid biosynthesis; monoterpene compound biosynthesis; nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism; arginine and proline metabolism; ABC transporters; alanine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid metabolism; and the biosynthesis of various alkaloids (Fig. 3E). A fold change threshold of ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 was applied to identify differentially expressed genes, together with a statistical significance cutoff of P ≤ 0.05. This relatively moderate threshold was selected to balance sensitivity and specificity, given the large and complex tobacco genome and the expectation that NtGSTU10 overexpression may exert broad but moderate regulatory effects, particularly in roots where nicotine biosynthesis occurs. We acknowledge that this approach may increase the number of detected DEGs; therefore, pathway enrichment, co-expression patterns, and consistency with metabolomic data were used to reduce potential false-positive interpretations. These results suggest that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with large-scale transcriptional reprogramming, particularly in root tissues where nicotine biosynthesis occurs.

Metabolome analysis

Qualitative and quantitative detection of metabolites in WTR, WTL, OER, and OEL showed a good correlation between samples (Fig. 4A), with 1,800 metabolites in 13 categories detected and identified in 12 samples. A total of 419 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were observed between the OEL and WTL, with 120 upregulated and 299 downregulated metabolites, and 663 DAMs were observed between the OER and WTR, with 390 upregulated and 273 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 4B).

(A) Correlation diagram between samples. (B) Statistical figures of differential metabolites between the two comparison groups. (C) Statistical chart of leaf difference metabolites between two comparison groups. (D) Statistical chart of root difference metabolites between two comparison groups. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in (E) root, and (F) leaves.

To understand the functions of these differentially expressed metabolites, 419 and 663 DAMs were classified into metabolite categories (Fig. 4C, D). The DAMs identified in the roots and leaves were primarily classified as alkaloids, amino acids and their derivatives, flavonoids, lipids, nucleotides and their derivatives, organic acids, steroids, tannins, and terpenoids. Among these, the most abundant category in the leaves was alkaloids, followed by amino acids and their derivatives; whereas, the most abundant category in the roots was flavonoids, followed by alkaloids (Fig. 4C, D).

KEGG analysis was performed for OER vs. WTR and OEL vs. WTL (Fig. 4E, F). The DAMs in leaves were annotated to various metabolic pathways, including the synthesis of secondary metabolites, cyanogenic amino acid metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, pantothenic acid and CoA biosynthesis, vitamin B6 metabolism, glucoside biosynthesis, purine metabolism, nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism, and zeatin biosynthesis. These results indicate that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with altered metabolite profiles in pathways related to secondary metabolism, amino acid metabolism, nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism, purine metabolism, and sugar metabolism in tobacco leaves, which may be linked to the observed changes in nicotine accumulation.

In roots, the DAMs were primarily related to the biosynthesis of flavonoid compounds, the biosynthesis of tropane, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloids, ABC transporters, plant hormone signal transduction, the biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites, alanine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid metabolism, nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism, the biosynthesis of various alkaloids, the biosynthesis of isoquinoline alkaloids, glutathione metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism. These results indicate that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with metabolic changes in pathways related to flavonoid metabolism, alkaloid biosynthesis, substance transport, amino acid metabolism, and signal transduction in tobacco roots. These metabolic alterations may be related to changes in nicotine synthesis and distribution observed in the roots.

Molecular pathways associated with NtGSTU10 overexpression revealed by integrated transcriptome and metabolome analysis

To further investigate the mechanism by which NtGSTU10 regulates nicotine content, we performed a joint analysis of the OEL vs. WTL and OER vs. WTR transcriptomes and metabolomes and performed KEGG co-enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes and metabolites (Fig. 5A, B). In the leaves, 22 metabolic pathways were jointly enriched, including the biosynthesis of various alkaloids, flavonoids, and ABC transporters; 54 metabolic pathways, including plant hormone signal transduction; arginine and proline metabolism; ABC transport; glutathione metabolism; amino acid biosynthesis; nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism; alanine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid metabolism; toluene, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis; and the biosynthesis of various alkaloids.

(A) Leaf transcriptome and metabolome combined KEGG enrichment analysis. (B) Root transcriptome and metabolome combined KEGG enrichment analysis. (C) Mechanism map of the regulation of nicotine content by NtGSTU10 gene.

The NtGSTU10 gene belongs to the GST family. We focused on the glutathione metabolism pathway and found that 15 genes, including Nitab4.5_0001864g0060(NtGSTU10) and Nitab4.5_0000652g0040, were significantly upregulated in the overexpressing line OE3. In the transcriptome, the Nitab4.5_0000023g0320 gene encoding the MATE nicotine transporter in the roots was also significantly upregulated in the overexpressing line. Based on the coordinated upregulation of glutathione metabolism–related genes and known nicotine transporter–encoding genes, including a MATE family member, we propose that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with transcriptional changes in pathways related to nicotine transport. These changes may contribute to altered nicotine distribution between roots and leaves; however, the direct involvement of NtGSTU10 in nicotine transport requires further functional validation.

Nicotine is synthesized in the roots of tobacco plants and transported to the leaves for accumulation. Further analysis revealed that Nitab4.5_0002522g0070, which encodes a quinolinic acid synthase (QS), was significantly upregulated. This gene catalyzes the conversion of imidoyl succinate to quinolinic acid. Quinolinic acid is further converted to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) by quinolinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase (QPRT). The Nitab4.5_0000316g0090 gene encoding the berberine bridge-linking enzyme (BBL) was also significantly upregulated. This gene catalyzes the conversion of 3,6-dihydro nicotinic acid and N-methylpyrrole salts into nicotine. These results indicate that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with increased expression of key nicotine biosynthesis–related genes, including QS and BBL, in the roots (Fig. 5C). Taken together, the integrated transcriptome–metabolome analysis suggests that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with coordinated regulation of nicotine biosynthesis genes, glutathione metabolism pathways, and transporter systems. These results indicate that NtGSTU10 may participate in a broader regulatory network influencing nicotine synthesis and distribution in tobacco.

Discussion

NtGSTU10 belongs to the glutathione S-transferase (GST) gene family, members of which have been reported to participate in the intracellular transport and accumulation of diverse secondary metabolites in plants. For example, VvGST1, VvGST4, and GmGST26A have been implicated in the trafficking and sequestration of flavonoids and other metabolites19,20. These studies suggest that some GST family members function as auxiliary factors in metabolite transport processes rather than as primary membrane transporters. Our previous study primarily demonstrated that NtGSTU10 overexpression enhanced nicotine accumulation in tobacco hybrids22. In contrast, the present study focuses on elucidating the potential molecular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. This multi-omics approach provides a systems-level perspective on the regulatory networks associated with NtGSTU10-mediated nicotine metabolism.

In this study, overexpression of NtGSTU10 was associated with altered nicotine distribution between roots and leaves, characterized by reduced nicotine levels in roots and increased accumulation in leaves. Because nicotine is synthesized predominantly in tobacco roots and subsequently transported to leaves for accumulation27, these observations are consistent with a shift in nicotine allocation between organs. However, such changes do not directly demonstrate that NtGSTU10 itself functions as a nicotine transporter. The nicotine transport coefficient used in this study reflects relative nicotine distribution between roots and leaves and serves as a comparative indicator rather than a direct measurement of transport flux.

Previous studies have shown that GSTs can act as carrier or adaptor proteins, facilitating metabolite sequestration in cooperation with membrane-localized transporters such as ABC and MATE proteins19–21,28. In tobacco, NtWBC13, an ABCG transporter, has been identified as a nicotine transporter18, highlighting the importance of transporter systems in nicotine movement29. In this context, the yeast heterologous expression assay employed in the present study cannot distinguish between enhanced nicotine uptake and increased intracellular retention or sequestration. Therefore, the elevated nicotine levels observed in NtGSTU10-expressing yeast cells may reflect indirect effects, such as binding or detoxification-related processes, rather than direct membrane transport activity.

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed coordinated changes in pathways related to glutathione metabolism, ABC and MATE transporters, hormone signaling, and alkaloid biosynthesis in NtGSTU10-overexpressing plants. Multiple transporter-encoding genes and GST family genes were upregulated, suggesting that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with broader regulatory reprogramming of nicotine-related metabolic networks. In roots, the primary site of nicotine biosynthesis, enrichment of amino acid metabolism, nicotinic acid and nicotinamide metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and alkaloid biosynthesis pathways was observed, accompanied by increased expression of key nicotine biosynthetic genes and accumulation of related metabolites.

In summary, this study demonstrates that NtGSTU10 overexpression is associated with altered nicotine distribution and large-scale transcriptional and metabolic changes in tobacco. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed coordinated regulation of pathways related to nicotine biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, and transporter systems. These findings provide new insights into the regulatory networks underlying nicotine metabolism and highlight the potential role of NtGSTU10 in modulating nicotine synthesis and transport.
