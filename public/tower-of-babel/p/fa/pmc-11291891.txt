The effect of screw insertion configuration of Sinus Tarsi plate on biomechanical performance using finite element analysis
Authors: Prinda Santichatngam, Adisorn Chongmuenwai, Supakit Rooppakhun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSinus Tarsi plates are used as implants for minimally invasive surgery of calcaneus bone fractures. This study evaluated the screw fixation patterns of Sinus Tarsi plates for optimal biomechanical performance. Six three-dimensional (3D) finite element models with different positional screws were evaluated for calcaneus fracture stabilization using Sinus Tarsi plates with 5, 6, and 7 holes. Walking stance conditions as heel strike, midstance, and push-off phases were used to compare loading. Results indicated that the equivalent (EQV) stress exhibited in the implant was higher than in the surrounding bone, with the highest value during the push-off phase. The maximum EQV stress or risk of failure decreased when an insertion screw was placed in the anterior bone using a 7-hole plate, and the most stable strain result at the fracture bone site was recorded for a Sinus Tarsi plate with 7 holes (TT 7-1). The screw insertion pattern and configuration of the Sinus Tarsi plate impacted the biomechanical performance of the calcaneal fracture.Keywords: Sinus Tarsi plate, Calcaneus fracture, Finite element analysis, Screw pattern, Biomechanical performanceSubject terms: Engineering, Biomedical engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Calcaneus fractures are most common in the tarsal bone and impede patient movement. Calcaneus fractures often occur by falling from a height as a sprained ankle. Intra-articular fractures comprise 70–75% or 1–3% of all fractures1. Calcaneus fracture cases increased by 10.9% per annum at Maharat Nakhon Ratchasima Hospital (Nakhon Ratchasima Province, Thailand) from 2014 to 20182, as shown in Fig. 1.

The number of patients recorded with calcaneus fractures at Maharat Nakhon Ratchasima Hospital between 2014 and 2018.

Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is a widely used method for calcaneus fracture treatment3. One of the conventional surgical techniques involves using Sinus Tarsi (ST) locking plate screws to stabilize the bone fracture. The type of bone fracture, including the number and placement pattern of screws used, impacts the performance of bone fixation4. Post-operation, the patients must not put weight on the injured foot or walk for 9 to 12 weeks during the early stage of bone healing5. The time required for bone union at the fracture site depends on the performance of the fixation device and individual rehabilitation conditions. Perren's theory6 states that a small amount of motion or weight-bearing should be applied to bone fractures during the early stages of bone healing to stimulate the callus to fill the gap. This research used three walking stance phases to simulate the load that should be applied during the early stage of callus formation and bone healing.

Chen et al.7 used finite element analysis (FEA) to simulate the biomechanical behavior of the plantar fascia during the walking posture, with results demonstrating increased tension and peak pressure of the fascia during gait and midstance to pre-swing. Haowei Zhang et al.8 found that normal heel bones and heel fractures had higher stress intensity during standing and walking after minimally invasive fixation (MIF). Their results suggested that early weight-bearing rehabilitation after MIF should be avoided. Chen et al.9 concluded that ORIF with a locking screw (LS) or non-locking screw (NS) plate provided good rigidity and allowed light touch weight-bearing for patients with Sanders IIB tongue-type calcaneal fractures in the early postoperative period. Qiang et al.10 observed that when a lateral locking plate was used to fix calcaneal fractures, the sustentaculum screw plate had less effect on the biomechanical performance of the calcaneus. The ORIF technique has been shown to perform well with LS or NS plates but the number of screws, screw insertion pattern, and plate configuration have not been studied for biomechanical performance.

Therefore, this study evaluated the biomechanical performance of an ST locking plate for calcaneus fracture stabilization under the three phases of walking stance (heel strike, midstance, and push-off). The equivalent (EQV) stress and total strain of the fracture bone site were considered by varying the number of locking screws and the plate fixation configuration using FEA. The maximum EQV stress recorded in the implant and the surrounding bone was used to analyze the risk of failure. The total strain at the fracture bone site was calculated as the ratio between the final deformation and the initial condition. A high total strain results in low stability, while a low value indicates higher stability. A mechanical test was used to validate the precision of the calcaneus modeling simulation. This study hypothesized that variation in the ST locking plate configuration exhibited diverse calcaneus fracture stabilization performance. An increase in the hole plate configuration size exhibited higher stabilization performance, while screw numbers and patterns of insertion also impacted the biomechanical performance of the ST locking plates.

Materials and methods

Three-dimensional (3D) models

All the experiments were performed by following the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was approved by the Licensing Committee of the Maharat Nakhon Ratchasima Hospital Institutional Ethics Board (clearance number or IRB number 034/2024). Computed tomography (CT) data were obtained from a 36-year-old volunteer (body weight 70 kg) with no musculoskeletal disorders, pain, previous foot surgery, or osteoporosis using a 64-slice spiral computed tomography scanner (Optima CT660, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The CT protocol was performed as follows: 120 kVA with an automatic value of 50 to 320 mA, a dose reduction of 20%, and a slice thickness of 2.5 mm with a reconstruction of 0.625 mm in a scan length of 30 to 90 cm. Three-dimensional (3D) models of calcaneus bone were reconstructed using medical imaging processing software (MIMICSv21.0, Materialise, Belgium) and reverse engineering software (Geomagic DesignX, 3D System, Digigate Technology, Thailand). The segmented 3D CT model consisted of cortical and cancellous bone using MIMICS software, as shown in Fig. 2. The 3D calcaneus intact bone model was then virtually performed, creating a fracture similar to the tongue-type bone classification with a gap distance of 0.1 mm5.

Using reversed engineering techniques, ST locking plates consisting of 5, 6, and 7 insertion screws were created using Geomagic DesignX computational software. The calcaneus fracture bone models were virtually assembled using ST locking plate stabilization. Six 3D line models with different screw placement patterns were considered, as shown in Fig. 3I. The yellow color represents the position of the screw placement, as shown in Fig. 3II, while the white color represents a blank insertion screw.

FEA modeling and simulation

The 3D modeling and simulation were performed using the FEA simulation software package (Ansys Workbench v2021R2, Ansys Inc., Canonsburg, Pennsylvania). The plantar fascia ligament was created as a 2-node truss element, with other parts assigned a 6-node 3D tetrahedron element type. The ground was considered to be a rigid 8-node, integrated hexahedral element type body. The screws were inserted into the locking plate for the contact condition and there was no interaction between the bone and the plate. The surrounding region between the cortical and cancellous bone including the inserted screw was considered, with no sliding or separation. A mesh convergence test was performed based on the different percentages, with maximum EQV stress not exceeding 2%, as shown in Fig. 4. The FEA model had 193,709 to 353,451 elements and 316,282 to 564,388 nodes in Fig. 4.

The mesh convergence test according to the EQV stress.

Boundary and loading conditions

This study simulated the three phases of walking stance as heel strike, midstance, and push-off. The angles between the foot and the ground of the heel strike, midstance, and push-off were + 11°, 0°, and − 11°, respectively15 as shown in Fig. 5. In the heel strike and midstance phases the inferior part of the calcaneal tuberosity was fixed, including the plate stabilization, as shown in Fig. 5a,b, respectively. For the push-off phase (Fig. 5c), the inferior part was fixed, and the remote fixation condition was applied to the position in the anterior part of the foot. The ground reaction forces (GRFs) corresponded to 110%, 95%, and 115% of body weight applied to the facets of the subtalar joint for the three walking stances at 770 N, 665 N, and 805 N, respectively16,17. For the midstance phase, the GRFs were shared between the anterior and posterior sides; therefore, the calcaneus reaction force was applied with 40% of the total loading7. The applied forces on the Achilles tendon for the three phases of heel strike, midstance, and push-off were 480 N, 550 N, and 1100 N, respectively15,18,19.

Results

Equivalent stress distribution

Figure 6a,b display the typical FEA results of the equivalent (EQV) stress distribution in the implant and calcaneus bone, respectively. The stress distribution showed that the maximum value in the implant occurred adjacent to the curvature area of the locking plate component. Simultaneously, the maximum stress was exhibited in the cortical bone, as the load-bearing area between the screw and the surrounding bone.

Typical FEA result of equivalent (EQV) stress distribution (MPa): (a) implant, and (b) calcaneus bone.

Source: Based on computational software (Ansys Workbench v2021R2, Ansys Inc., Canonsburg, Pennsylvania).

Figure 7 shows the maximum EQV stress on the implant for calcaneus stabilization with different implant configurations in each walking stance phase. The maximum EQV stress was exhibited in the 7-hole plate configuration (TT 7-1, TT 7-2, and TT 7-3) at 87.4 MPa, 125.0 MPa, and 187.0 MPa for the heel strike, midstance, and push-off, respectively. The maximum EQV stress was higher in the push-off phase than in the other phases, especially for the 7-hole plate configuration but it did not exceed the yield strength of the material.

Maximum EVQ stress on implants during each walking stance phase.

Figure 8 displays the maximum EQV stress on the bone for calcaneus stabilization with different implant configurations in each walking stance phase. During the heel strike walking phase, the maximum stress was exhibited in the 6-hole plate configuration at 80.9 MPa. By contrast, the maximum stress in the midstance phase was 45.4 MPa, displayed in the 6-hole plate configuration. During the push-off phase, maximum stress was recorded in a 7-hole plate configuration (82.5 MPa). By comparison, the maximum stress occurring in the bone was highest during the push-off phase, especially for the 7-hole plate configuration.

Maximum EQV stress on calcaneus bone during each walking stance phase.

Total strain at the fracture site

Figure 9 illustrates the total strain at the fracture site for each configuration of calcaneus fracture stabilized during the three walking stance phases. A high value of total strain at the fracture site means low stability, while a low value represents high stability of the device. Results revealed that the lowest total strain at the fracture site was exhibited in a 7-hole plate configuration (TT 7-1) with five screw insertions. The heel strike phase of the walking stance gave the highest ST locking plate stabilization compared to the midstance and push-off phases.

Total strain at the fracture bone site during each walking stance phase.

Finite element analysis validation

For mechanical validation, a 3D model of the calcaneus bone and subtalar joint was constructed using SC Orange Surgical guide printing material with a layer thickness of 50 microns using a 3D rapid prototyping machine (NextDent® 5100 3D Printer, 3D Systems). A titanium ST mini-plate and locking screws were used to stabilize the calcaneus fracture bone. The jig was designed to support the facets of cuboid and low calcaneal tuberosity of the 3D calcaneus model, as shown in Fig. 10. A mechanical test was performed to validate the FEA results according to standing loading simulation using a specifically designed jig tested by a UTM machine. The test applied a force of 100 N in the vertical direction to the subtalar joint component. Strain gauges (KFGS-02-120-C1-11, Kyowa Electronic Instruments Co., Ltd., Japan) were attached at two positions on the calcaneus bone model adjacent to the upper position of the first hole plate and the lower position of the third hole plate. Strain gauges were also attached to the ST locking plate in the region below the third hole plate and between the fourth- and fifth-hole plate. Data from these four strain positions were collected under three replicate tests.

Validation of the FEA analysis: (a) mechanical test with the designed jig, and (b) the FEA model.

Figure 11 compares the results between the mechanical test and the FEA simulation under the same loading conditions. The strain values were higher where the strain gauges were installed on the plate than on the bone. The strain results at position 4 had the highest values, followed by position 3, consistent with the results obtained from the FEA simulation revealing a maximum error of 9.74% and indicating that the software simulation test met the set quality standards.

Discussion

Open reduction and internal fixation using a minimally invasive ST approach and stabilization with a locking plate is now a widely accepted treatment for calcaneal fractures3. The bone fracture pattern and implant configuration affect the performance of stabilization. In this study, we evaluated the biomechanical performance of calcaneus fracture stabilization under different ST locking plate configurations based on the three phases of walking stance (heel strike, midstance, and push-off).

Results showed that the maximum EQV stress occurred in the locking plate component rather than in the surrounding bone for all three phases of the walking stance. Generally, the push-off loading generates the highest implant stress with 115% of body weight applied16,17. High stress on heel strike loading occurs because the difference between heel strike and push-off loading is only 5%. The boundary condition of the heel strike is 100% loading but during push-off the frontal remote point causes loading transfer. Using crutches to transfer the weight and assist with walking is necessary during the early stages of bone healing from 9 to 12 weeks5.

The maximum EQV stress was monitored at the point of failure risk. The maximum EQV stress of the implant displayed the highest value in a 7-hole plate configuration during the push-off phase due to the high bearing load of the body weight. However, it did not exceed the yield strength of the material, which generally ranges between 800 MPa and 1100 MPa. The risk of implant failure during ST locking plate stabilization decreased using a screw fixation pattern at the anterior bone location, such as in TT 7-1, which had the highest stress on the implant, but lower strain compared with the 5, 6, and 7 hole plates because seven screws connected the plate and the bone and the 7-hole plate was the longest, giving improved force distribution. Therefore, the number of screws and the screw pattern of the ST locking plate impacted the risk of implant failure. The FEA results of critical EQV stress in this study exhibited a similar trend in the push-off stage of walking as the findings presented by Zhang et al.8.

The total strain at the fracture site determines the stability of the implant. Our results exhibited the highest strain (> 10%) at the fracture site in the midstance and push-off phases for TT 6, TT 7-3, and TT 7-4. High strain at the fracture site indicates low stability, whereas low strain demonstrates high stability. Strain distribution is generally recommended at less than 2% of fracture sites for effective bone remodeling connection20. The strain at the fracture site can be between 2 and 10% and fibrocartilaginous callus formation may take 4 to 6 weeks.

A comparison between the four strain gauge results and the FEA simulation showed data errors between 2.16 and 9.74 percentage points, verifying that the data from the simulation concurred with the mechanical tests. In this study, we considered only tongue-type calcaneus fractures, as most commonly reported by the Maharat Nakhon Ratchasima Hospital. Tongue-type fractures occurred more than the other types of fracture (Joint depression type) in 61% of patients over 4 years (Fig. 1). The fracture pattern was shown to impact the biomechanical performance of the implant stabilization. Suggestions for studying the effects of other fractures are presented as follows.

This study only conducted finite element analysis without clinical results to compare data. To support our findings, further clinical studies of the effects of calcaneus fracture fixation stabilized with a Sinus Tarsi plate should be conducted. Also, the finite element method considered the boundary condition on only the calcaneus bone model. Finite element analysis of a complete model of the foot including the surrounding bones and muscle forces should be considered. This study predicted calcaneus fixation under different gait periods and our results can be used as a guideline for further clinical studies.

Conclusions

ST plate screw fixation patterns significantly impacted the biomechanical performance of calcaneal fractures. This study evaluated the biomechanical performance of calcaneus fracture stabilization under different ST locking plate configurations based on the three phases of walking stance (heel strike, midstance, and push-off). During the push-off phase, the EQV stress was higher than during the midstance and heel strike phases. The screw fixation pattern at the anterior bone site reduced the probability of implant failure during Sinus Tarsi plate stabilization. Sinus Tarsi plates with 6 holes and 7 holes gave better calcaneus bone fracture stabilization than the 5-hole plate. The screw pattern on the Sinus Tarsi plates significantly impacted the performance of calcaneal fracture stabilization. The TT 7-1 model recorded the best result, with low strain on the implant and high stability, giving better load distribution than in the surrounding calcaneus bone. However, the choice of plate size and number of screws ultimately depends on the surgeon. This study provides guidance in choosing plate size and screw positions to deliver optimal calcaneus bone fracture fixation results.
