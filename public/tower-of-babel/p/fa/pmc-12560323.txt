The prognostic value of POD24 for multiple myeloma: a comprehensive analysis based on traditional statistics and machine learning
Authors: Quane Zhang, Yifan Wang, Qiuni Chen, Jingjing Ma, Chuanyang Lu, Yue Chen, Yuye Shi, Wenting Shi, Zhengmei He, Liang Yu, Chunling Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn multiple myeloma, progression within 24 months (POD24) is a strong adverse prognostic factor. However, its impact on overall survival (OS) remains underexplored through machine learning.MethodsWe retrospectively collected clinical data from 155 patients and divided them into POD24 and non-POD24 groups. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves, univariate Cox regression, and multivariate Cox regression. We further evaluated the effect of these variables on overall survival using ten machine-learning algorithms.ResultsKM and Cox regression analyses revealed significantly poorer OS in the POD24 group (p < 0.001). Among machine learning models, ANN achieved the best performance in the test set. PCA-based visualization showed clear class separation and prediction consistency with original feature distributions. SHAP analysis identified POD24 as the strongest model-derived predictor in this cohort of mortality. Force plots further demonstrated that non-POD24 substantially contributed to lower predicted death risk.ConclusionThis study suggests that POD24 may be associated to the survival outcomes in multiple myeloma using both traditional statistical and machine learning approaches. Our findings highlight the potential value of POD24 in mortality risk prediction and demonstrate the utilization of ANN-based SHAP interpretation in enhancing model transparency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15089-6.Keywords: POD24, Multiple myeloma, Overall survival, Machine learning, Artificial neural network, SHAP

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a clonal plasma cell disorder characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of malignant plasma cells within the bone marrow, representing the second most prevalent hematologic malignancy worldwide [1]. Despite significant improvements in survival outcomes due to novel therapeutic agents and autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT), disease relapse remains inevitable, and curative outcomes persist as an unmet clinical need [2–5].

A substantial body of evidence indicates that 30–40% of newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) patients experience early relapse post-treatment, a phenomenon strongly correlated with dismal survival outcomes [6–11]. While the operational definition of early relapse remains contentious—with studies adopting varying thresholds ranging from 12 months [8] to 18 months [11]—the 24-month cutoff (POD24) has emerged as the most validated predictor of adverse prognosis in contemporary cohorts [10].

Current prognostic models predominantly rely on conventional statistical approaches, which inherently assume linear relationships between individual clinical indicators and outcomes, potentially limiting their ability to capture complex, non-linear effects. Recent advances in machine learning (ML), particularly decision tree-based algorithms such as XGBoost and deep neural networks, have shown promising predictive performance in modeling complex, nonlinear clinical data patterns [12–14]. Given the complexity of clinical indicators, machine learning methods offer complementary approaches to traditional statistical models by flexibly capturing non-linear relationships without requiring explicit model specification. To better evaluate their impact on overall survival, we applied ten machine learning algorithms, ranging from simple (linear models) to complex (ensemble models and neural networks), to predict overall survival probability based on 14 clinical features, including POD24, serum calcium, and ISS stage. Model performance was assessed through cross-validation, and the best-performing model was selected for feature importance and interpretability analysis. The identified features indicate the model’s reliance on these variables, but this does not imply clinical causality or independent prognostic value.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

The sequential flow of patient enrollment and selection is detailed in Fig. 1. This retrospective observational study examined clinical data from 155 NDMM patients who underwent first-line therapy at Huai’an First People’s Hospital between October 2014 and December 2022. Patients with missing data for any of the variables included in the analysis were excluded, adopting a complete case analysis approach. Follow-up primarily involved consulting outpatient and inpatient medical records, supplemented by telephone calls. Longitudinal follow-up data were collected through March 2025, prior to data analysis and manuscript preparation, with median follow-up time of 52 months. All enrolled patients received at least two courses of induction treatment with novel agents including proteasome inhibitors (PIs) and/or immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), with at least minimal response. A total of 23 (14.8%) patients underwent ASCT as part of their induction regimen. This study had a minimum follow-up of 2 years. POD24 was defined as disease progression or death due to MM within the first 24 months after treatment initiation. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to progression, relapse, death due to any cause, or last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death or last follow-up.

The sequential flow of patient enrollment and selection

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association of multiple myeloma POD24 with overall survival. The data included demographic and clinical characteristics at the time of initial diagnosis, including sex, age, Durie-Salmon (DS) stage, International Staging System (ISS) stage, hemoglobin (Hb) levels, platelet (Plt) count, serum albumin (ALB) levels, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, serum creatinine (Cr) levels, calcium (Ca2+) levels, β2-microglobulin (β2-MG) levels, the proportion of bone marrow plasma cells (BMPCs), and ASCT. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Huai’an No.1 People’s Hospital, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Due to the retrospective nature of the data collection, all patient identities were anonymized, and informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Huai’an No.1 People’s Hospital.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Met the diagnostic criteria for MM. 2) Had sufficient medical information available for analysis. 3) Aged between 18 and 80 years inclusive. Exclusion criteria: 1) Patients with major vital organ damage who failed to receive ≥ 2 cycles of induction therapy or refused treatment. 2) Insufficient medical records.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between two groups using Pearson’s chi-square test (for categorical variables) or independent t-test (for continuous variables). Descriptive variables are presented as mean ± SD (for normally distributed data) or categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages). R software (version 4.3.3) was used for construction and evaluation of the prediction model. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression and Cox regression were used to investigate the survival relationship between POD24 and OS.

Furthermore, this study employed ten machine learning algorithms to predict overall survival and to explore key prognostic clinical variables in newly diagnosed multiple myeloma. These models were used to compare predictive performance and to identify variables contributing most to the model outputs, rather than to infer causal relationships. These algorithms include Logistic Regression (LR), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Decision Trees (DT), Extremely Randomized Trees (ET), Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), LightGBM, Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and XGBoost. These algorithms were used to study the relationship between clinical features and overall survival status, where mortality was considered an adverse outcome. The selection of clinical features was mainly based on established clinical knowledge and a review of previous literature on the prognosis of multiple myeloma [9, 11, 14–16]. These included sex, age, DS stage, ISS stage, Hb, Plt, ALB, LDH, Cr, Ca2+, β2-MG, BMPCs, and ASCT.

Multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed on 155 patients to identify independent prognostic factors for OS. Continuous and categorical variables included were POD24 status, DS stage, ISS stage, sex, age, HB, Plt, ALB, LDH, Cr, Ca²⁺, β2-MG, BMPCs, and ASCT status. Unadjusted analyses (Fig. 3A and B) were performed using standard Cox regression without checking the proportional hazards assumption. POD24 positivity (HR = 0.154, 95% CI: 0.089–0.27, P < 0.001) and elevated serum calcium (HR = 3.56, 95% CI: 1.536–8.25, P = 0.003) were significant adverse prognostic factors, while other covariates were not significant (P > 0.05). In the model with dichotomized parameters (HB < 100 g/L, Plt < 164 × 10⁹/L, ALB < 35 g/L, LDH ≥ 247 U/L, Cr ≥ 177 µmol/L, Ca²⁺ ≥2.75 mmol/L, β2-MG ≥ 5.5 mg/L, BMPCs ≥ 20%, age ≥ 65 years), POD24 positivity remained the most significant adverse factor (HR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.088–0.26, P < 0.001), and ISS stage III and hypoalbuminemia were also associated with poorer prognosis. Both models showed good discriminative ability, with C-index values of 0.83 and 0.81. Adjusted analyses (Fig. 3C and D) accounted for the proportional hazards assumption, which was assessed for all covariates using Schoenfeld residuals. POD24, ISS stage III, and serum calcium were found to slightly violate the assumption. To address this, POD24 was included as a stratification factor, while other variables satisfied the assumption. These adjusted models confirmed the results of the unadjusted analyses, ensuring that estimates for other variables were reliable despite minor violations of the proportional hazards assumption.

Hyperparameter optimization and overfitting control

Hyperparameter optimization

To ensure optimal performance for each machine learning model, we performed supervised hyperparameter tuning using grid search within a nested cross-validation framework. Specifically, for each model, a predefined grid of hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, tree depth, regularization strength, number of estimators) was evaluated using GridSearchCV with 3-fold inner cross-validation. The model with the best average AUC score across the inner folds was selected, and its performance was then assessed on the outer validation fold. This process was repeated across 5 outer folds, and the average AUC was reported as the model’s performance. The full set of hyperparameter grids and selected optimal values are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Overfitting control

To mitigate the risk of overfitting, especially given the moderate sample size and high dimensionality, we adopted several strategies: We employed a 5-fold outer/3-fold inner nested cross-validation scheme with AUC as the optimization criterion. Hyperparameters were selected exclusively within the inner loop via grid search, and performance was assessed once on the corresponding outer validation fold to avoid optimistic bias. To prevent information leakage, models requiring normalization (logistic regression, SVM, KNN, MLP) were wrapped in a pipeline with standard scaler, fitted only on the training portion of each fold. Stratified folds preserved class balance; we report mean ± standard deviation of outer-fold AUCs.

In our approach, we systematically tuned hyperparameters that directly constrain model complexity, applying regularization techniques and structural constraints across various machine learning models. For logistic regression, we focused on adjusting the L2 regularization strength, which serves to penalize large coefficients, thereby preventing overfitting and ensuring better generalization. In the case of SVMs, we fine-tuned both the regularization parameter and the kernel parameter for the radial basis function kernel, with the objective of balancing the model’s ability to fit the data and its complexity, thus managing the trade-off between bias and variance. For artifical neural network, we incorporated weight decay as a form of L2 regularization to penalize overly large weights, while also exploring variations in network width through adjustments to the number of hidden layers and units, which directly impacts model capacity. Additionally, we limited the number of training iterations to control the risk of excessive training, which can lead to overfitting and high variance. Similarly, for KNN, hyperparameters such as the number of neighbors, the weighting scheme, and the distance metric (p) were explored to balance the model’s sensitivity to local data variations and its ability to generalize.

For tree-based ensemble methods, we applied structural constraints to control model variance. These models, including decision trees, random forests, and extra trees, were subjected to hyperparameter searches over tree depth, minimum samples for splitting, and feature selection, alongside the number of estimators. These constraints are designed to limit the complexity of individual trees, thus preventing overfitting. For gradient boosting models, we employed similar controls with a focus on learning rate, shallow tree structures, and subsampling techniques to introduce stochasticity and reduce overfitting. Furthermore, for advanced tree-based models such as XGBoost and LightGBM, we optimized parameters such as learning rate, tree depth or number of leaves, subsampling ratio, feature subsampling, and regularization, all aimed at balancing model complexity with generalization capacity. The combination of these hyperparameters helps achieve a balanced bias-variance trade-off, discouraging overly complex fits that might otherwise lead to high variance and poor generalization.

Beyond cross-validation, we quantified metric stability via nonparametric bootstrap resampling on held-out data (i.e., the outer-fold test sets). For each resample, AUC was recomputed from fixed model predictions. The bootstrap distribution provides a sensitivity check of performance to sampling variability.

The visualization results in Fig. 3 and Table 2 demonstrated significant differences in the performance of various machine learning algorithms on both training and testing sets. Among them, ANN outperformed other models on the test set in most metrics such as AUC, accuracy, and C-index. While certain models, such as KNN and RF, performed well on the training set, their performance on the test set was significantly inferior, indicating overfitting (Fig. 4). Further analysis of the confusion matrix on the test set revealed that the sum of diagonal elements of ANN is highest, while the sum of off-diagonal elements was the lowest, indicating that ANN provided the most stable predictions (Fig. 5).

The comparative analysis based on both bootstrap-derived AUC distributions (Fig. 7) and the DeLong’s test (Fig. 8) consistently highlights the superiority of the ANN over conventional machine learning classifiers. Specifically, the ANN achieves the highest mean AUC of approximately 0.78 (95% CI: 0.76–0.80), which surpasses alternative models such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest, as well as classical approaches including logistic regression (AUC: ~0.75), SVM (~ 0.75), and decision trees (~ 0.70). Importantly, the ANN’s confidence interval exhibits limited overlap with weaker models, reinforcing its statistically significant advantage in discriminative performance. This observation is further corroborated by the DeLong test, where the ANN significantly outperforms nearly all competitors (p < 0.01 across most pairwise comparisons), thereby ruling out random chance as an explanation for its superior AUC. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the ANN is uniquely capable of leveraging high-dimensional feature spaces and capturing nonlinear relationships, offering a distinct advantage for predictive modeling tasks demanding robust generalization.

Higher blue probability gradients represented lower prediction probabilities (close to 0), higher red probability gradients indicated higher probabilities (close to 1), and intermediate shades denoted probability values in between. The results showed a distinct clustering pattern in both the training and test set projections, with one group of points concentrated on the right and another on the left. Moreover, the color gradient trend aligned with the feature distribution captured by PCA (Fig. 9). Additionally, the PCA-based visualization of high- and low-probability predictions offered qualitative insight into how the ANN model captured patterns within the original feature space, supporting interpretability but not serving as a measure of model validity.

To further elucidate the contribution of individual features to the performance, we applied SHapley Additive explanations (SHAP) for feature importance analysis [29]. The resulting SHAP summary plot ranked features according to their overall impact on model predictions and provided insight into the directionality of their effects (Fig. 10). Features were ordered along the vertical axis based on their mean absolute SHAP values, with those at the top exerting the greatest influence on the model’s output. The horizontal axis represented the SHAP values, indicating the magnitude and direction (positive or negative) of each feature’s contribution to the prediction for each instance. Notably, POD24 was encoded such that POD24 = 1 and non-POD24 = 0. The SHAP value distribution for this variable displayed a wide range and a distinct color gradient, signifying its strong effect on survival prediction. Specifically, data points with lower values of POD24 (shown in blue) were predominantly associated to positive SHAP values, indicating that the absence of early disease progression contributed positively to the model’s prediction of higher survival probability. In contrast, higher values of POD24 (shown in red) were largely located in the negative SHAP value region, suggesting that early progression contributed negatively to the model’s predicted survival probability.

Discussion

This study utilized a combination of traditional statistical modeling and advanced machine learning techniques to predict early disease progression (POD24) and long-term survival outcomes in patients with MM. The results indicated that POD24 was significantly associated with OS, and POD24 status was an important factor influencing long-term prognosis. This finding is consistent with previous studies [6, 10, 11], highlighting that early disease progression is a significant independent predictor of poor prognosis in MM patients.

Previous studies have consistently shown that early relapse or progression within 24 months of treatment initiation is a robust marker of adverse prognosis in MM. Our results corroborate these findings, with the POD24 group exhibiting significantly lower median OS and a narrower interquartile range compared to the non-POD24 group. The Kaplan-Meier analysis further reinforced this association, revealing a stark divergence in survival curves between the two groups. These results suggest that POD24 status could serve as a critical stratification tool for identifying high-risk patients. This dynamic prognostic feature highlights the potential value of early treatment response in informing future risk stratification approaches in MM. For POD24-positive patients, timely escalation to intensified regimens incorporating novel therapies, such as monoclonal antibodies, bispecific T-cell engagers, or CAR-T cell therapy, may improve survival outcomes.

Multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed POD24 status as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes, with a strong association to inferior OS in both continuous and dichotomized models. In multivariable-adjusted models, hypercalcemia, ISS stage III, and hypoalbuminemia emerged as independent predictors of OS deterioration, corroborating their pathophysiological centrality in myeloma progression as evidenced by prior cohort studies. Hypercalcemia in MM is mechanistically linked to osteolytic bone disease and renal dysfunction, both hallmarks of aggressive disease biology [30–32]. This aligns with recent genomic studies associating hypercalcemia with high-risk cytogenetic profiles, such as t(4;14) and del(17p) [33], suggesting that Ca2+ may serve as a biomarker for underlying molecular aberrations. Clinically, these findings advocate for closer monitoring of calcium levels and early intervention to mitigate skeletal complications. The ISS stage is a prognostic stratification tool based on serum β2-microglobulin and albumin levels [34], which has been widely used for prognostic assessment in patients with MM. Patients with ISS stage III typically have elevated serum β2-microglobulin levels, indicating a high tumor burden and impaired renal function, both of which are associated with a poor prognosis. Low albumin levels are associated with several adverse prognostic factors, including inflammatory response, malnutrition, and increased tumor burden. In our study, low albumin levels were significantly associated with shorter OS, highlighting their independent prognostic value in MM patients. Durie-Salmon staging, age, and hemoglobin levels lacked statistical significance in our cohort, likely reflecting the modifying effects of novel agents (proteasome inhibitor/immunomodulatory drug-based therapies) on traditional prognostic markers [2, 32]. For instance, lenalidomide maintenance has been shown to mitigate the adverse prognostic impact of advanced age [35], while daratumumab-based regimens significantly improve survival in patients with high-risk cytogenetics [36]. These evolving treatment paradigms necessitate a critical reappraisal of conventional prognostic frameworks.

Furthermore, this study employed ten machine learning algorithms to predict OS and to explore key prognostic clinical variables in MM. Among the machine learning models, ANN demonstrated the highest performance in most evaluation metric on the test set, with an AUC of 0.789. PCA-based visualization suggested potential clustering patterns, indicating that the ANN model may have captured aspects of the underlying feature distributions. SHAP analysis identified POD24 as an important variable, with non-POD24 status contributing to lower predicted mortality risk. This study suggests that POD24 may have prognostic relevance in multiple myeloma.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the study design may introduce potential biases in data collection and patient selection. This study used complete case analysis for handling missing data, which may introduce selection bias if data are not missing at random. Second, the relatively small sample size (n = 155) may limit the robustness of our statistical analyses and affect the ability to generalize the findings to broader populations. A small sample size may also lead to model overfitting, especially when using complex machine learning models. Third, while we included key clinical variables, our dataset lacked certain biomarkers such as cytogenetic abnormalities or minimal residual disease (MRD) status, which could have additional prognostic value and further refine our predictive model. Additionally, the inherent heterogeneity of the dataset may introduce variability in the results, affecting the model’s generalizability.

Conclusion

This study suggests that POD24 is associated with survival outcomes in multiple myeloma, based on both traditional statistical and machine learning approaches. Our findings indicate that POD24 may be one of the important predictors of mortality risk, and the use of ANN-based SHAP analysis provides complementary insights by enhancing model interpretability. These findings provide preliminary evidence that dynamic risk stratification systems could be useful in MM, potentially contributing to more individualized treatment strategies.
