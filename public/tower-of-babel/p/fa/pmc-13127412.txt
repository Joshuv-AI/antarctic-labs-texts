The Readability of Patient-Reported Measures in Early Psychosis
Authors: Maria Abou Farhat, Negin Zamani, Simran Bajaj, Nicole Pawliuk, Navdeep Kaur, Jai Shah, Manuela Ferrari, Srividya N Iyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient-reported measures are increasingly central to early intervention in psychosis. Many persons with psychosis have lower educational attainment than the general population and cognitive difficulties, making the readability of measures salient for this population. However, the readability of patient-reported measures in psychosis has never been investigated. This study assessed the readability levels of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) in early intervention in psychosis.Study DesignPROMs and PREMs were identified from a scoping review of measures used in early intervention services for psychosis (January 2000-January 2024). Of 111 measures identified, 104 (89 PROMs; 15 PREMs) were assessed using mean grade levels derived from 3 established readability formulas (Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level, Simple Measure of Gobbledygook index, FORCAST formula), applied to instructions, items, and total scales.Study ResultsOverall, 82.7% of patient-reported measures exceeded the recommended 6th-grade reading level. Instructions were harder to read than items (mean readability: 9.44 vs. 7.95; t(90) = 6.54, P < .001). PREMs demanded higher readability levels than PROMs. Readability scores were different across formulas, with Flesch–Kincaid consistently producing lower scores. Except for one, the strikingly few measures co-developed with patients (n = 14) also exceeded 6th-grade reading level.ConclusionsMost patient-reported measures in early psychosis exceed recommended readability levels, raising concerns about accessibility, validity, and whose experiences are represented in patient-reported data. This creates impetus for integrating readability assessments into measures development. More fundamentally, centering the participation of persons with lived experience can support readable, relevant, and patient-centered assessments reflecting the language, priorities, and realities of people with psychosis, and advance epistemic justice.Keywords: readability level, patient-reported outcome measures, patient-reported experience measures, self-reported measures, psychosis, early intervention

Introduction

Early intervention for psychosis (referred to as coordinated specialty care in the USA) has been increasingly implemented in various parts of the world, given evidence of their superior outcomes compared to traditional care.1 Its core principle is a focus on patients’ voices and engagement, and shared decision-making.2,3 Given the alignment of patient-reported measures with this focus, there has been an increasing desire to incorporate such measures in early intervention services for psychosis.4

Broadly, such measures fall into 2 categories: (1) patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) on which patients self-evaluate their health status, quality of life, psychological well-being, social functioning, and symptom severity,5,6 and (2) patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) on which patients self-evaluate their experience with the service(s) provided to them.6 Together, patient-reported measures can provide insights into patients’ perspectives about their experience with the illness, services offered to them, and needs,7 while also generating new epistemic understandings of what psychosis, recovery, and care can mean from lived, relational, and context-embedded viewpoints, potentially challenging clinically dominant definitions and enriching how knowledge in mental health is produced and used. Their use can also facilitate patient engagement and involvement in decision-making processes and improve treatment effectiveness in early intervention services.4,8,9 These measures can provide a space for patients to share their concerns and expectations with service providers, influencing the patient–clinician relationship.10 In contrast to observer- or clinician-rated outcome measures, patient-reported measures directly capture patients’ lived experiences, needs, and views, which may be even more important when it comes to domains such as goal attainment, quality of life, and social inclusion, areas that patients consider important and are themselves often best positioned to articulate.11

The psychometric field has historically been grounded in positivist research traditions, with well-established assumptions about epistemology (what counts as valid knowledge) and ontology (what is assumed to exist and be measurable), and with psychometric methods playing a central role in the construction and validation of measures.12More recent developments, however, increasingly recognize the value of subjectivity and lived experience in the creation of instruments, particularly for developing less stigmatizing language and for uncovering dimensions of experience and meaning that are often missed by traditional methods.13In this context, approaches such as co-design and user-centered design are gaining prominence as ways to advance epistemic justice, by formally recognizing lived experience knowledge as legitimate expertise and embedding it within both research processes and care practices.14

Patient-reported measures are crucial in learning health systems and measurement-based care,4,15 both of which are rapidly gaining momentum in early intervention for psychosis.16–19 Within these approaches, patient-reported measures can continuously inform individual-level treatment decision-making as well as program-level quality improvement by providing real-time information regarding symptom presentation, recovery progress, functioning, and treatment preferences and experiences, and allowing comparisons of patients and services within and across different programs and countries.20

Despite the recognized importance of using patient-reported measures, there remain concerns about the selection of reliable and valid measures that are suitable for assessing a given domain.17,21 McKenzie et al.,20 in collaboration with service users, service providers, and researchers, developed an international standard set of PROMs for psychotic disorders. The selection of measures was informed by several criteria, including psychometrics, accessibility (eg, cost-free, availability in at least 2 languages), service user inputs, and feasibility (eg, administration time, training requirements). While these criteria are essential, we posit that readability is critical in evaluating a measure’s validity because if a measure is difficult to understand, it can lead to measurement errors, hindering the accurate assessment of outcomes and experiences of patients.22,23 Although a 5th-6th-grade reading level has been recommended for patient-reported measures by the American Medical Association,24 the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality,25 and the International Society of Quality of Life Research,26 many measures used in mental health exceed this reading level.23,27,28

To our knowledge, the readability of patient-reported measures used in psychosis research has thus far not been examined. This is particularly pertinent for this patient population, given its persistent gap in educational attainment, with a reported 2.58 times higher chance of not graduating from high school compared to healthy individuals.29 A sizable proportion (up to 50%) of individuals with first-episode psychosis have not completed high school,30 with many having a highest education level equivalent to elementary or primary school.31

Lower educational attainment among people who experience psychosis is attributable not only to individual factors but also structural challenges. Contributing factors may include premorbid mental health, substance use and/or learning differences, as well as illness-related experiences such as cognitive changes, reduced motivation, medication effects, and the substantial time demands of treatment (eg, hospital admissions and outpatient appointments), all of which can disrupt educational continuity. These challenges are often compounded by stigma, reduced confidence, and family circumstances (eg, expectations, dynamics, and available support). Critically, structural and institutional conditions (including poverty, inflexible educational systems, lack of reasonable accommodations, insufficiently tailored educational services, and discrimination) shape educational trajectories by constraining access, participation, and re-engagement.29,32,33

Furthermore, individuals with psychotic illnesses often have considerable difficulties in cognitive functioning, in various domains including intellectual capacity, attention, learning, memory, language, and executive functions,34–36 which can be present even prior to the onset of the illness.37 While cognitive difficulties can be present in other mental illnesses, they are more severe and widespread in schizophrenia and related psychotic disorders.38,39 People with psychotic disorders can also experience challenges in reading skills, particularly in phonological processing, comprehension, and reading rate.40 Neurodevelopmental reading difficulties, such as dyslexia, are common among patients with schizophrenia.41 The educational gap and cognitive impairments further strengthen the case for carefully evaluating the readability of patient-reported measures in psychosis. If measures are written above recommended reading levels, they may result in patients leaving questionnaires incomplete or filling them out without adequately comprehending them.

The present study therefore aimed to investigate the readability levels of patient-reported measures used in early intervention in psychosis. Specifically, we assessed the extent to which patient-reported measures in psychosis met recommended reading standards (6th grade or lower). It is desirable, effective and equitable that measures do not require higher reading levels, given that measures should be designed not only to gather high-quality data, but also to be grounded in people’s actual ability to understand items and express their experiences and needs.

As additional analyses, we aimed to examine whether readability levels for the same measure(s) differed when using different readability formulas, as findings could inform methods for integrating readability assessments in developing and evaluating measures in the future. We posited that measures co-developed with persons with lived experience would be more accessible in terms of reading level (ie, have reading levels that were lower or close to the recommended 6th-grade level). To test this, we compared the readability of measures co-developed with persons with lived experience with those not co-developed. Finally, we compared the readability levels of PROMs and PREMs to see if assessment type influenced readability levels and draw more focused conclusions for clinicians, evaluators, and researchers.

Methods

Selection of Patient-Reported Measures

The patient-reported measures for this study were identified from our team’s published comprehensive scoping review of all measures used in early psychosis research, which involved a search of 5 databases (EMBASE, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library).4 We updated this search in January 2024. The original and updated searches resulted in the identification of 111 patient-reported measures (96 PROMs and 15 PREMs) used in early intervention services for psychosis. To access the complete versions of these measures, we first searched the original publications describing the development of the scales, which sometimes included the complete measure in an appendix or table. When complete versions were not available in the original publications, we conducted advanced searches using Google and Google Scholar. If the measure and its instructions were not accessible online or required authorization from the author, we reached out to the author via email to request a copy of the measure. Ultimately, 7 measures could not be located, resulting in 104 patient-reported measures whose readability was assessed (89 PROMs and 15 PREMs).

Readability Formulas

To assess the readability of the measures in our study, we applied 3 standard readability formulas: the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level,42 the Simple Measure of Gobbledygook index,43 and the FORCAST formula.44 These tools evaluate text complexity by analyzing factors such as sentence length and syllable count, providing a grade-level score indicative of the text’s readability.22,45 As they have been designed specifically for English texts and rely on syllable-based metrics, they do not yield accurate results for non-English content. The Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level Formula was selected due to its popularity in assessing the readability of measures and its frequent use in readability research.46,47 The Simple Measure of Gobbledygook index was included for its relevance to health-related questionnaires, while the FORCAST formula was chosen for its appropriateness in evaluating questionnaires and multiple-choice scales, as it does not rely on complete sentences.43–45 Further details about these formulas are provided in Table S1. All 3 formulas were used, as there is no consensus on which of these should be used to assess the readability of patient-reported measures.

We used the readability software “readable.com” to compute the readability of each measure using these 3 formulas.45 As noted by Zhou et al.,47 discrepancies in word or syllable counting methods within the software can lead to different outcomes for the same text, even when using the same readability formula. Therefore, to reduce potential biases, we consistently used this same readability software for all formulas, ensuring that any counting discrepancies would remain uniform across all formulas.

Readability Assessment

For each measure, we separately assessed the readability of the instructions, the items, and the total scale (comprising both instructions and items). This approach allowed us to explore how each component might influence the overall readability of the measures.27

Given the large sample of measures included in this study, which were available in different formats (eg, questionnaires, multiple-choice answers, Likert scales, tables, and text-only), we developed a standardized method for preparing the measures, converting them into a Word document, before inputting them into the readability software. In cases where measures contained sentences outside the instructions that provided additional guidance, such as in the SF-12 Health Survey “The following questions are about activities you might do during a typical day”,48 these were included with the instructions, and not as part of the items. This approach was adopted for 2 reasons. First, these guiding sentences function more as instructional aids rather than core measurement items, helping patients to better understand the questions and provide accurate answers. Co-Development

Recognizing the involvement of persons with lived experience as foundational for the development of patient-reported measures,11,49 we gathered information about whether each of the 104 measures had been co-developed with persons with lived experience of psychosis. To do so, we located and reviewed the paper linked to the associated citation(s) about the development and psychometrics of each measure, either in the original paper or through Google Scholar. Co-development was conceptualized to include involvement in construct development and/or item generation, reviewing or giving feedback on items, and cognitive interviewing while developing/refining scale/items. To be inclusive, we considered scales as co-developed when they mentioned using qualitative data (eg, narratives, focus groups) from patients to inform scale construction, even if they did not otherwise engage persons with lived experience in scale development. Involving patients in psychometric testing alone was not considered co-development.

Statistical Analysis

The analysis of readability levels was performed using SPSS version 24. For each measure, the mean grade level of the 3 formulas was calculated separately for the instructions, the items, and the total scale. We also examined whether readability levels for the same measure differed across the 3 readability formulas using Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (RMANOVA). Finally, we planned to compare the readability levels of measures that were co-developed with those that were not co-developed, as well as PROMs versus PREMs using independent-samples t-tests.

Results

Out of the 104 included patient-reported measures, instructions were available (and their readability level scored) for 91 measures. Of the remaining thirteen measures, 7 did not originally have instructions, and instructions could not be found for the other 6 measures. The readability level of items was scored for all 104 measures. Lastly, the readability level of the total scale (ie, instructions plus items) was scored for 98 measures (91 that had instructions and 7 that did not originally include instructions).

Readability of Measures Co-Developed with Persons with Lived Experience

Of the 104 measures, only 14 involved some level of co-development with persons with lived experience of psychosis or schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (see Table S2). Given this small sample of co-developed measures, we did not conduct a subgroup analysis comparing the readability of measures that were and were not co-developed. Only one measure of the 14 co-developed measures had a 6th-grade readability level with all others being above that. Generally, the associated publications had limited to no information about the sociodemographic characteristics (particularly, educational attainment) of the patients involved in co-development and the co-development process. This did not allow any inferences about whether patients involved in co-development have higher education levels than persons with psychosis more generally, thereby contributing to co-developed measures also having elevated reading levels.

Readability of PROMs vs PREMs

PREMs had higher mean readability levels for the total scale (8.86) compared to PROMs (7.99) [t(96) = −2.21, P < .05], as well as for instructions [10.71 for PREMs, 9.27 for PROMs; t(89) = −2.22, P < .05]. No significant differences were found in the mean readability levels for items (PREMS Mean: 8.53, PROMS Mean = 7.93). Only 4 of 15 PREMs (26.7%) had items that were written at or below a 6th-grade reading level. Similarly, out of the 89 PROMS, 26 (29.2%) had items that were written at or below this reading level.

Discussion

General Findings

This study assessed the readability levels of patient-reported measures used in early intervention services for psychosis. Most of the analyzed PROMs and PREMs (82.7%) had total scale readability levels above the recommended 6th-grade reading level. This is concerning given the lower levels of educational attainment and high school completion reported among individuals with psychotic disorders.29,30 This mismatch between the readability level of patient-reported measures and the typical levels of educational attainment among persons with psychosis, further exacerbated by cognitive difficulties that are prominent in psychosis, may result in measures being misunderstood or inaccurately completed, thereby compromising their validity.22 More deeply, it raises concerns about whose perspectives are systematically captured, amplified, or excluded within patient-reported data.

Higher readability levels may lead to difficulty in understanding a measure, leaving participants feeling ashamed, potentially reinforcing existing stigma or negative emotions about themselves or their illness. Over time, such experiences may dampen patients’ willingness to fill out measures, potentially contributing to the low completion rates of self-report measures and the under-representation of those with cognitive difficulties in research.50–52

Our findings are consistent with previous research on the readability of patient-reported measures in other psychiatric disorders. Nickel et al. reported that all 9 self-report measures used in bipolar disorder had either items and/or instructions written above recommended reading levels (means of 8.78 and 9.35, respectively). McHugh and Behar27 found that 93% of 105 self-report measures for anxiety and depression were written above recommended reading levels, with anxiety measures scoring higher than depression ones. Another study on alcohol misuse28 (20 measures) and binge eating23 (13 measures) reported similar findings, with most or all measures written above a 6th-grade level. Perhaps even more disconcertingly, clinical46 and quality of life53 self-report measures for children and adolescents have also been found to far exceed the readings levels for their targeted age groups. Even the widely used Strengths and Difficulties questionnaire in child and adolescent mental health was found to be not suitable for young people with a reading age below 13-14 using different reading formulas.54 These trends have also been observed in physical health domains,55,56 indicating that limited accessibility of self-report measures due to high readability levels may be a cross-cutting issue, with one exception (orthopedics) where the majority of measures were found to be below the 6th-grade reading level.57

As highlighted by Papadakos et al.56 for patient-reported measures in cancer and by Richards et al.23 for self-report measures in binge eating, one potential explanation for the high levels of readability required by measures may be the use of skewed validation samples (with higher readability levels) in their development and validation. We observed a similar pattern for some of the measures we assessed. One example is the “Psychosis Recovery Inventory58” that was validated using a small sample of 48 participants from an early intervention service. Notably, individuals with moderate to severe learning disabilities were excluded, and the sample had an average education level of 11.9 years. Scale development and evaluation processes should carefully consider the extent to which their sampling represents the broader population of individuals with early psychosis.30

In orthopedics, PROMs have a long tradition of being developed and validated largely in clinical and field-based (rather than research) settings.59 They also often assess more concrete, functional content (“Can you climb stairs?”) compared to more subjective and nuanced constructs such as in mental health.59,60 Orthopedics also uses tools from NIH’s Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) initiative57 and NIH explicitly recommends that tools be at appropriate reading levels.61

Readability of Instructions and by Measure Type

Consistent with previous studies in mental health,23,27,28,62 the mean readability grade level required by instructions was significantly higher than that of the items, with over three-fourths of the included measures having instructions with a mean grade level of 8 or higher. This is especially concerning because if the instructions are misunderstood, responses may be invalid, affecting the accuracy of the measure. It is possible that instructions might have been written at a higher readability level because they were intended to be delivered or clarified by staff rather than directly read by patients, with any confusion to be addressed during administration. In the real world, however, due to time or resource constraints, such staff-supported administration of self-report measures may not occur.63–65

Of note, PREMs were less readable (had higher readability scores) than PROMs. This risks undermining the accuracy of patient feedback regarding experiences in and satisfaction with care, thereby limiting the role of PREMs in supporting service development and quality improvement. This concern is particularly relevant in early intervention services for psychosis, which emphasize collaboration, shared decision-making, and a patient-centered approach to enhance young patients’ experiences with care.3,66 PREMs’ higher readability levels may be because they have often been designed to capture the experiences of not only patients but also family members, staff, and other stakeholders (eg, The Friends and Family Test,67 PCORI Engagement Activity Inventory,68 the Public and Patient Engagement Evaluation Tool,69 Virtual Client Experience Survey for Mental Health and Addictions Care70). This lack of specific tailoring for patients may result in a failure to consider their accessibility needs, which could differ from those of family members or other stakeholders.

Readability and Co-Development of Measures

Given the extremely small number of patient-reported measures that were co-developed with patients, we were unable to conduct subgroup analyses that would have allowed us to draw definitive inferences about whether co-developed measures were more accessible in terms of their readability ratings. We did not conduct such analyses to prevent misleading (underpowered) conclusions about the association between readability and co-development of patient-reported measures in psychosis. Only one of the 14 co-developed measures had a 6th-grade readability level. Furthermore, because the studies provided little description of the samples involved in co-development or the co-development process, we are unable to draw conclusions about how the educational levels and other characteristics of those involved in co-development impact the readability of measures.

Overall, our findings bolster the recently growing concern about the lack or inadequacy of the involvement of patients in co-designing patient-reported measures in mental health.71 This lack of meaningful collaboration likely contributes not only to higher readability levels but also to the inclusion of constructs and language that may feel disconnected with or alienating to the lived realities and experiences of persons with psychosis, raising questions about the epistemic validity of measures.11,72

Fundamentally, this points to a continued under-recognition of persons with lived experience as central knowledge holders. Addressing this gap requires a paradigm shift in how measures are constructed: beyond relying primarily on theoretical constructs derived from clinical observation and psychometric modeling, the development of measures should be grounded in the recognition of subjective and experiential knowledge. Such knowledge offers critical opportunities to surface new meanings of mental health experiences and identify dimensions of quality of life and care that remain poorly captured by existing instruments.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, our rigorous assessment is the first-ever study of the readability of PROMs and PREMs used in early intervention in psychosis, across multiple dimensions (instructions, items, and total scales). It highlights the elevated reading requirements of many currently used measures, providing insights into the challenges patients may face, as well as the support they may require in completing measures. This study examined a large sample of 104 PROMs and PREMs, which is more extensive than most previous readability examinations of patient-reported measures in mental health. We used a clear approach for preparing the measures to assess their readability and used 3 established readability formulas (rather than arbitrarily choosing one which has been seen as problematic in other health fields57). Given the complexity of readability assessments and the lack of a gold standard in methodology, this approach could be replicated by future researchers aiming to evaluate the readability of large samples of measures in mental health and other domains. Furthermore, we believe that our focus on readability provides a tangible (measurable and malleable) and much needed direction for the improvement of patient-reported measures in psychosis.

Our study has some limitations. First, the analysis was limited to English-language PROMs and PREMs because the readability formulas used were designed specifically for English texts. Second, the analysis relied solely on mathematical readability formulas, without incorporating patient feedback or service users’ perspectives. Future research examining the readability of these measures should include user feedback on their readability, clarity, and acceptability.

Moreover, readability formulas have inherent limitations, as extensively discussed by Zhou et al.47 First, varying methods result in non-identical scores. We therefore used 3 different readability formulas and averaged their results. This approach minimized bias from any single formula and provided a more robust grade-level estimate. Length of passages may also influence readability levels, but findings on this issue remain inconsistent. Zhou et al.47 noted that formulas such as the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level and the Simple Measure of Gobbledygook index tend to stabilize with text samples between 500 and 900 words. They recommended analyzing entire texts to reduce variability; however, some of the instructions analyzed were very brief, often consisting of single sentences. Similarly, newly developed measures, such as the “Friends and Family Test to Improve Patient Experience” (NHS),67 consisted of a single item, which may have impacted the validity of their readability levels. However, Perez et al.,57 in their study of orthopedic PROMs, found no correlation between a measure’s length and its readability level.

Future Directions

Future studies should consider incorporating additional readability formulas to ensure the inclusion of patient-reported measures developed in languages other than English. Currently, there are only a few validated formulas available for non-English texts. For instance, the Lix readability formula has been proven valid for French, German, and Greek.73 Additionally, a specific adaptation of the Flesch index has been developed for Spanish.74

Along with ascertaining their reliability and validity using standard methods (eg, calculating test–retest reliability, comparing scores against a gold standard or scores from a measure of a related domain), it is critical to incorporate readability assessments during the scale development process. A PROM/PREM must be understandable to service users to be used effectively. Readability influences validity, as the accuracy of the obtained results from measures depends on patients’ comprehension.22 It is also crucial to test the readability of measures on a representative sample of the target population, rather than to test with individuals with higher education levels or fewer cognitive impairments who may be easier to engage in scale development efforts.

Additionally, methods such as cognitive interviewing, ideally with diverse populations with psychosis, should be used when developing a measure to assess how respondents interpret questions and whether items are understood as intended.75 In other health domains, cognitive interviewing has been shown to improve the comprehensibility and readability of measures.57,76 Using simple writing guidelines to edit patient-reported measures has been shown to be effective in attaining recommended reading levels in other health domains,57 which may be an option in psychosis, particularly for currently widely used measures that score above recommended reading levels.

While readability assessments provide an initial indication of a measure’s accessibility, they cannot fully capture its overall usability or comprehensibility. Other factors influencing comprehension, such as formatting, layout, and navigation features (eg, skip patterns), should also be considered. A text may score as highly readable but still confuse responders due to poor formatting or unclear tables.23,47,77 Tools like the PEMAT for Printable Materials Understandability Scale,78 as used by Papadakos et al.,56 can address some of these gaps by evaluating factors such as layout and understandability. Readability should also be considered in relation to cultural equivalence, as measures that meet formal reading-level thresholds may still rely on culturally specific language or normative assumptions. Beyond surface-level translation followed by readability assessments of measures, culturally appropriate and safe assessment entails examining how people experience, make sense of and name phenomena across diverse contexts.79

Accessibility, relevance, and validity may be inseparable from who holds epistemic authority in measure development. While readability is a critical accessibility feature, the lack of a lived-experience-lens may be more fundamentally impeding measurement-based care, learning health systems, and research in psychosis. Having been developed with minimal or no patient involvement, PROMs and PREMs may be failing to capture what truly matters to service users. Our findings provide a strong foundation for prioritizing the use of and creating measures that are meaningfully co-developed and reflect the diversity of lived experiences, goals, and definitions of illness, treatment, recovery, and well-being. Many aspects of psychosis, such as visual phenomena and altered perceptual experiences, are frequently described by patients but remain absent from existing measures.80,81

Future studies should prioritize the full involvement of patients at all stages of measure development and evaluation, not solely during reliability or validity testing.71 Such engagement can enhance the readability, as well as the relevance and validity of measures.11,49

To fully represent the lived experiences of people with psychosis, inclusive, participatory approaches such as narrative inquiry and arts-based methods (eg, photovoice, digital storytelling, cellphilming) are needed alongside traditional self-report instruments.82,83

Centering the participation of persons with lived experience can support the creation of readable, relevant, and patient-centered assessments that reflect the language, priorities, and realities of people with psychosis. From an epistemic justice perspective, this positions individuals with lived experience as legitimate knowers and co-producers of knowledge, not only within dominant clinical and linguistic frameworks, but also across diverse cultural contexts where meanings, categories, and even the absence of equivalent terms offer critical insights into how mental health and recovery are understood and articulated.14,84 It also creates space to surface previously unrecognized subjective experiences of psychosis, and the unique ways people make sense of, cope with, and redefine recovery in their own terms—dimensions that are often obscured by standardized clinical vocabularies and measurement frameworks.

Conclusions

This study is the first to assess the readability of patient-reported measures used in early intervention in psychosis. It revealed that most of these measures exceed the recommended 6th-grade reading level. The inherent limitations of readability formulas notwithstanding, it is clear that many currently used patient-reported measures in early intervention in psychosis may not be accessible enough to be accurately completed by patients. Their validity may therefore be compromised, particularly for individuals with cognitive dysfunction and low literacy levels who are already likely to be underrepresented in research. Future patient-reported measures research in psychosis must integrate readability assessments, explore non-textual factors impacting accessibility, and crucially, position individuals with lived experience as central knowledge holders, rather than beneficiaries or peripheral contributors.22,85
