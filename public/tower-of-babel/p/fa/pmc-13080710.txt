An autonomous bronchoscopy robot controlled by artificial intelligence (BronchoBot) outperforms experienced bronchoscopists in a simulated setting
Authors: Kristoffer M. Cold, Zhuoqi Cheng, Lars Konge, Anne Orholm Nielsen, Christian Skjoldvang Andersen, Thiusius R. Savarimuthu, Bruno Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerformance in flexible bronchoscopy varies even among experienced bronchoscopists. This study aimed to compare an autonomous bronchoscopy robot (BronchoBot) to human bronchoscopists in a standardised simulation-based setting.MethodsWe compared 30 bronchoscopies performed by humans (10 novices, 10 intermediates and 10 experienced) to 10 bronchoscopies performed by BronchoBot on a realistic phantom. The primary outcomes were Anatomy Rating (0–12 points) and Dexterity Rating (0–6 points) assessed by two blinded expert raters. Secondary outcomes were Diagnostic Completeness (DC; 0–18 segments), Structured Progress (SP; 0–18 progressions), Procedure Time (PT; seconds) and Mean Intersegmental Time (MIT; seconds per segment), assessed by artificial intelligence (AI). Tertiary outcomes included five targeted navigations by BronchoBot to RB4→RB5→RB6 and LB8→LB9→LB10, following the bronchoscopy skills and task assessment tool (BSTAT), rated by AI.ResultsBronchoBot significantly outperformed all three human groups for all outcome measures. Anatomy Rating (novice, intermediate, experienced versus BronchoBot): 4.8, 10.3, 10.3 versus 11.8 points; p<0.001. Dexterity Rating: 2.0, 3.0, 4.5 versus 5.3 points; p<0.001. DC: 9, 15, 16 versus 18 segments; p<0.001, p=0.001, p=0.003. SP: 2, 7, 8 versus 18 progressions; p<0.001. PT: 281, 251, 190 versus 124 s; p<0.001. MIT: 32, 16, 13 versus 7 s per segment; p<0.001. BronchoBot successfully completed targeted navigation in all attempts for RB4→RB5→RB6: median±interquartile range 26±2 s, and LB8→LB9→LB10 30±2 s.ConclusionsBronchoBot outperformed experienced human bronchoscopists using validated assessment tools based on expert human and AI ratings. BronchoBot can serve a role as benchmarking expert performance in a simulated setting, but further development and evaluation are required for use in patients.

Introduction

Flexible bronchoscopy is essential for diagnosing and treating lung pathologies [1], yet its operator-dependent nature often leads to incomplete segmental inspections and reduced diagnostic yields [2–4]. Even experienced bronchoscopists can miss bronchial segments [5–11], highlighting the need for systematic approaches and training [12].

Much emphasis has been put on simulation-based training [13], as virtual reality (VR) simulator training makes novices outperform experienced bronchoscopists, when tested on a phantom [5], and VR simulator training is superior to traditional apprenticeship training in bronchoscopy and endobronchial ultrasound when tested in a clinical setting [11, 14]. Therefore, several systematic reviews have identified VR and phantom training to be effective when learning bronchoscopy but fail to address how to improve the performance of intermediate and experienced bronchoscopists [15, 16].

Artificial intelligence (AI) navigation systems that identify the bronchial segments and help guide the bronchoscopist through the bronchial tree represent the newest advancement in bronchoscopy training and certification [17–19]. These AI systems improve novice performance, equipping them to inspect more bronchial segments in a more structured order and more efficiently, even with the AI disabled [17, 20]. Intermediate and experienced bronchoscopists benefit the same way from the AI [21], with it being even superior to expert instructions [22]. A systematic review identified several AI applications to optimise bronchoscopy performance [23], with one study developing an AI copilot robot to increase the steering in peripheral bronchoscopy [24].

In central diagnostic bronchoscopy, the movements of the bronchoscope through the bronchial tree should be done in a standardised way [12] using relatively few and controlled movements [25]. After 25 years of focus on improving bronchoscopy training, the question now arises whether a robot could be taught to perform the procedure autonomously. And if an autonomous robot controlled by an AI navigational system for systematically inspecting the bronchial tree could perhaps outperform even experienced bronchoscopists.

The aim of study was to test an autonomous bronchoscopy robot against human bronchoscopists in a simulated environment.

Methods

Study design

The study used the performance of an autonomous bronchoscopy robot controlled by AI (BronchoBot) as a prospective trial compared with human bronchoscopists’ performance from a crossover randomised controlled trial [21]. Detailed development regarding software and hardware development specifics are not discussed in this article as the concept is seeking patent approval; however, a description is attached as supplementary material S1. Rather than the development, we would like to address BronchoBot's performance, as is common practice for AI in bronchoscopy [21, 23, 26].

BronchoBot

BronchoBot is a fully automated robot using a single-use scope (aScope™ 5 Broncho HD 5.0/2.2, Ambu®, Ballerup, Denmark) connected to a portable monitor (aView™ 2 Advance, Ambu®, Ballerup, Denmark). BronchoBot consists of the motors controlling the scope in combination with the airway navigation software (supplementary videos S1 and S2). The total hardware cost is less than 1000 USD. We used a realistic phantom mimicking the first 4–5 divisions of the bronchial tree (Bronchoscopy Training Model LM-092, Koken Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) to test BronchoBot's performance in conducting full and systematic bronchoscopy (figure 1 and supplementary videos S1 and S2). BronchoBot was designed to navigate up to the segmental bronchi level, covering 18 target branches of a normal human lung anatomy and consistent with requirements for central full bronchoscopy (supplementary material S2 emphasises the development of BronchoBot). BronchoBot can be instructed either to perform a full and systematic bronchoscopy that automatically covers all bronchial segments in a standardised sequence (carina→RB1→RB10→carina→LB1→LB10; supplementary video S2), or to directly navigate to any designated target segment or route (supplementary video S3). During navigation, the system adapts its motion to reach each defined destination sequentially, based on the real-time analysis of images obtained from the bronchoscope tip.

To test its reliability and compare it with human bronchoscopists, we performed 10 bronchoscopies with BronchoBot. Similar to other AI bronchoscopy navigation studies [17, 20, 21, 23], the navigation algorithm was locked and there was no learning effect during testing.

Human bronchoscopists

The bronchoscopy recordings of 10 novices (0 bronchoscopies), intermediates (1–249 bronchoscopies) and experienced (≥250 bronchoscopies) bronchoscopists were included from a previous study, as it used an identical setup to BronchoBot [21]. The bronchoscopists were from a crossover randomised controlled trial, where they first performed a bronchoscopy with AI navigation and then a standard bronchoscopy without AI navigation. We used their second bronchoscopy without AI navigation to be compared with BronchoBot. For the novice group, all 10 bronchoscopies were included. For the intermediate and experienced groups, we selected the 10 best bronchoscopies based on Structured Progress, ensuring the comparison reflected their highest level of performance. The groups were divided by procedure volume, as this is the most reproducible method for categorisation in bronchoscopy education research, even though certification is increasingly advocated to be competence-based rather than volume-based (see the Discussion). We chose to have BronchoBot perform 10 bronchoscopies, as there were only 10 novices in the original study to compare to [21]. The human bronchoscopists came from 19 different countries and their characteristics are presented in supplementary material S2.

Primary outcome measures: experienced, blinded human rating

All bronchoscopy recordings were rated by two expert raters (A.O. Nielsen and C.S. Andersen), utilising a validated assessment tool to assess and differentiate bronchoscopy performance based on experience [6] and utilised in a similar study with the same two expert, blinded raters [19]. The Anatomy Rating (0–12 points) captures how completely and structed the bronchoscopist inspects the bronchial tree, while the Dexterity Rating (0–6 points) evaluates the efficiency and smoothness of scope handling. A full description of the scoring system and its components is provided in supplementary material S3. These ratings represent structured, standardised, but still subjective expert assessments rather than automated measures. The tool has been validated and shown to achieve high inter-rater reliability in previous studies [6, 19]. The expert raters also classified the overall performance of each bronchoscopy as novice, intermediate, experienced or expert performance based on overall judgement rather than by predetermined cut-off values.

Secondary outcome measures: automated AI rating

All bronchoscopies were also automatically rated by an AI navigation system (Ambu BPS, prototype version Ambu BPS training GUIDE v.0.0.1, Ambu, Ballerup, Denmark) according to the following validated outcome measures to assess bronchoscopy performance [19]: Diagnostic Completeness: indicating inspected segments and ranging from 0 to 18 segments [9, 17, 20, 21].Structured Progress: indicating structured progressions, ranging from 0 to 18 progressions [9, 17, 20, 21].Procedure Time: from visualising the carina to extraction of the scope [9, 17, 20, 21].Mean Intersegmental Time: indicating procedure efficiency as seconds per segment inspected (Procedure Time/Diagnostic Completeness) [9, 17, 20, 21].The AI used for assessing the secondary outcome measures was the same AI that the human bronchoscopists had used to conduct one bronchoscopy, before performing their procedure without AI navigation included in this study [19]. However, it was totally independent from the AI used to control BronchoBot.

Tertiary outcome measures: angling of the phantom and navigation to designated segments

To test BronchoBot's level of automation and adaptability, we asked it to perform three bronchoscopies for each of the following angled positions: the phantom angled 90° clockwise (simulating a patient lying on his right side), 180° (prone position) and 270° clockwise (patient lying on his left side). It then compared all the nine angled bronchoscopies to the 10 bronchoscopies at 0° (supine position).

The bronchoscopy skills and task assessment tool (BSTAT) [8] requires the bronchoscopist to first perform a full and systematic bronchoscopy as assessed by the primary, secondary and tertiary outcome measures, but also requires targeted navigations to RB4→RB5→RB6 and LB8→LB9→LB10. We therefore asked BronchoBot to navigate five times from the main carina to RB4→RB5→RB6 and return to the carina, and then five times from the main carina to LB8→LB9→LB10 and back.

Statistical analyses

Primary and secondary outcome measures

The performances of each group of the human bronchoscopists (novice, intermediate and experienced) were compared with the performances of BronchoBot.

Tertiary outcome measures

The nine angled bronchoscopies were compared with the 10 non-angled bronchoscopies by BronchoBot using the AI rating.

As Anatomy Rating, Dexterity Rating, Diagnostic Completeness and Structured Progress have a ceiling effect, and ≥11 participants are needed in each group to assume a normal distribution [27], nonparametric testing was chosen to compare the groups. Consequently, the median and interquartile range were used to compare the outcome measures using a Mann–Whitney U-test. A Fisher's exact test was used to test for differences in distribution among the expert raters’ classification of each bronchoscopy.

Statistical testing was done using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) v.27 (PASW v.27.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and figures were done using R (R v.4.1.2, Vienna, Austria). To ensure transparency, all data are available as supplementary material S4.

Results

30 recorded human bronchoscopies (10 novice, 10 intermediate and 10 experienced) were compared with 10 BronchoBot bronchoscopies within an identical, controlled, simulation-based setting.

Primary outcome measures (expert and blinded human rating) are shown in table 1 and figure 2. The expert, blinded human ratings had a very high level of agreement, as the inter-rater reliability was very high for Anatomy Rating (Pearson's r=0.91; p<0.001) and acceptable for Dexterity Rating (r=0.62; p<0.001). The internal consistency reliability was also high for both Anatomy Rating (Cronbach's α=0.90) and Dexterity Rating (0.85).

Discussion

This is the first study to demonstrate that a fully autonomous robot (BronchoBot) can outperform even experienced human bronchoscopists in a simulated setting. This has considerable implications for setting benchmark performance in bronchoscopy training in a simulated setting.

Experience does not ensure competence [28, 29], but competence gradually rises with experience, captured in this study by the median Anatomy Rating (figure 2), Diagnostic Completeness and Structured Progress (figure 3). However, the variances in each experience group were high, resulting in overlapping values of the best novices and the worst experienced (figures 2 and 3). These findings align with previous studies, as performance on average increases with experience [6, 7, 9, 10, 21, 29], but that experience does not ensure competence [28, 29], highlighted in table 2, as two bronchoscopies performed by experienced bronchoscopists were classified as novice performance. However, a new benchmark in a simulated setting is set with BronchoBot outperforming all groups on all performance measures (figures 2 and 3). Our study thereby supports previous studies, as BronchoBot was able to outperform the experienced group, because they frequently miss segments during simulation-based and clinical bronchoscopies [5–11]. This was done, despite the experienced group having a previous attempt with the same AI guidance system that rated them, giving them an advantage on BronchoBot, which had not been exposed to the AI rating tool. The superior performance by BronchoBot is further indicated, with nine bronchoscopies being classified as expert performance by the expert human raters, with only one bronchoscopy by the experienced group being classified as expert performance (table 2). BronchoBot also had a higher Dexterity Rating than the human bronchoscopists (4.5 versus 5.3 points; p<0.001) while also conducting a more efficient procedure, indicated by a lower Mean Intersegmental Time (13 versus 7 s per segment; p<0.001). Bronchoscopists learn to master anatomy before dexterity [6, 7], and automated tools to measure dexterity can discriminate between experience levels [25] and even differentiate among experienced bronchoscopists [10]. Thereby, BronchoBot has proven superiority for the more advanced part of learning bronchoscopy.

While prior research has illustrated how AI guidance can augment human performance during bronchoscopy [17, 20, 21], our findings show that full automation can outperform experienced clinicians under standardised conditions. AI guidance gives standardised and automated feedback and has proven superior to expert instructions in flexible bronchoscopy training [22]. Another study showed that with a median of 137±56 min of training with the same AI guidance system, novices can reach a median Diagnostic Completeness of 18±1 segments and Structured Progress of 16.5±3 progressions with the AI disabled in an identical setting as this study [17]. However, their Mean Intersegmental Time was higher than the robot's (13±7 versus 7±1 s per segment; p<0.001) [17]. Mastery learning, where a trainee trains towards proficiency standards is recommended in bronchoscopy training [13, 15], as it ensures competency and limits variation in performance upon course completion [30]. This was proved by the first study utilising mastery learning in bronchoscopy, again with an identical setup as this study, with the novices lowering their Mean Intersegmental Time and interquartile range, actually outperforming the bronchoscopy robot (6±1 versus 7±1 s per segment; p<0.001), with the AI navigation disabled [20]. Novices can thereby be trained to be more efficient than BronchoBot on a phantom model, and it should be achieved through mastery learning before continuing supervised practice in a clinical setting [20, 31]. In summary, standardising simulation-based training through AI guidance has proven effective, and we are developing the bronchoscopy robot to function as a training supervisor, as modelling examples have proven useful in bronchoscopy training to ensure a high level of dexterity [32].

A recent systematic review on AI in bronchoscopy concluded that AI augments rather than replaces the bronchoscopist [23], highlighted by the mentioned studies [17, 19–22]. Another of the analysed studies in the systematic review introduced AI as a copilot, assisting one intermediate to perform as well as one experienced in peripheral navigation [24]. Our study marks a significant leap, demonstrating autonomous navigation to outperform experienced bronchoscopists. This milestone reflects a shift from “assistance” to “autonomy”: from AI as a guide to AI as an operator and training supervisor.

From a clinical perspective, BronchoBot could serve several roles. First, as a benchmark for training centres, enabling trainees to compare their performance against a robot's “gold standard” in a simulated setting. Second, as a procedural tool in low-resource settings where skilled bronchoscopists are unavailable; in intensive care unit settings, systematic suction is needed to increase the patient saturation, but comes with risk to the patient and personnel, as bronchoscopy is an aerosol-producing procedure. Those safety concerns (particularly prominent in bronchoscopy during the COVID-19 pandemic [33]) could potentially be mitigated by using BronchoBot when developed in a clinical setting and also mastering technical feasibilities such as suctioning.

In colonoscopy, performance benchmarks have been established based on the adenoma detection rate as it inversely correlates to post-colonoscopy colorectal cancer [34, 35]. Large variations in endoscopists’ performances have been minimised using AI [36, 37]. Such standards do not exist in bronchoscopy, and no AI improvement system has been tested in a clinical setting in bronchoscopy [23]. We are therefore developing BronchoBot to work on cadavers, then patients. The ethical and regulatory implications of future robot-performed procedures must be considered. Autonomy does not equal agency, and while BronchoBot performed better than humans in this controlled context, responsibility for clinical decisions remains with doctors, as bronchoscopy contains multiple elements, addressed in the limitations section.

Strengths and limitations

We used both subjective (expert raters) and objective (AI) rating tools, validated to differentiate bronchoscopy performance levels [6, 19], and included parts of the BSTAT rating [8], with Structured Progress incorporated as it was recommended by the most recent systematic review in flexible bronchoscopy training [15]. The human participants spanned several countries, thereby not being susceptible to one institution's standards, but included conference delegates from 19 countries from five continents, improving the generalisability of our study. Another strength is that BronchoBot is not restricted to Ambu hardware; as it is guided by AI navigation software, it could in principle integrate with any bronchoscope operated by lever and rotational control.

We also have some limitations of the study to address. The performance of the bronchoscopy robot was compared with data obtained from previous studies [17, 20, 21]; however, all studies had an identical setup, and all human participants had been familiarised with the AI rating tool. To allow for this comparison, BronchoBot was trained and tested solely on the same standardised phantom, which is common practice in AI bronchoscopy research [21, 23, 26]. To test for adaptability to different settings, a slight decrease in Diagnostic Completeness and Structured Progress was observed when angling the phantom compared with the standard lying on its back position (Diagnostic Completeness of 17 versus 18 segments; p=0.043; Structured Progress of 16 versus 18 progressions; p=0.035). To prove real-life adaptability with changes in anatomy, we aim to test and train the robot on human cadavers, to close in on real patient clinical application. Regulatory frameworks, such as those currently being developed under the European Union AI Act [38], will be critical in defining the boundaries of such automation in medicine.

This study was conducted in a simulation-based environment using a standardised phantom, and results may not directly extrapolate to clinical scenarios. Real patients present anatomical variability, dynamic airway collapse, secretions, coughing, pathologies and other unpredictable features that are not presented in phantoms. Therefore, clinical decision-making in patient selection and biopsy technique should still be left to the doctors to decide.

Conclusions

BronchoBot outperformed experienced human bronchoscopists in a simulated environment, setting quality benchmarks for simulation-based training. Future studies should test its use on human cadavers, before transitioning into real-life patients.
