Factors associated with the costs of treating privately insured adults with complicated urinary tract infections
Authors: Kenneth Thorpe, Peter Joski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveComplicated urinary tract infections (c-UTI) are expensive and frequently require hospitalization. This study estimated the impact of patient comorbidities, readmissions and treatment delay on the costs of complicated urinary tract infections in both inpatient and outpatient settings. A related aim is to provide the first estimates of the potential savings of treating patients currently treated as inpatients that have clinical characteristics similar to, though less complicated, to patients treated as outpatients.DesignUsing one of the largest national claims data sets (30 million lives per year) on privately insured adults and dependents between 2019 and 2022, with regression models we estimate the treatment costs of complicated urinary tract infection (c-UTI) patients identified and treated in the hospital, identified and treated in an outpatient setting and identified in an outpatient setting but subsequently admitted. We statistically estimate potential savings associated with treating inpatient cases in the outpatient setting.SettingOur study was a retrospective analysis of claims data among privately insured adults and dependents with claims from February 1, 2019, to May 31,2022. Our analysis relies on the Merative- MarketScan Commercial Claims and Encounter database which is among the largest commercially insured set of claims data covering adults and dependents in the U.S. that included de-identified person-level claims data including inpatient, outpatient, and prescription drugs use and spending. It also included health plan spending, patient demographics, diagnosis, procedure and national drug codes covering more than 30 million active employees, early retirees, COBRA extenders, and dependents with data contributed by more than 160 employers and 40 health plans nationally each year.ParticipantsData were obtained for 93,228 c-UTI patients treated in both inpatient and outpatient settings in the United States.ResultsTreatment costs are highly positively skewed with a mean cost per episode of those identified in hospital of $42,000 and median cost of $27,784. High costs were in part linked to long lengths of stay for roughly 10% of patients of 15 days or longer. At the other extreme we found that many low-acuity patients were hospitalized. Switching antibiotics also increased treatment costs. The results show that 27% of c-UTI patients admitted to the hospital were low-acuity patients that potentially could be treated in an outpatient setting for over $31,000 per case less.ConclusionsThe results highlight several areas of opportunity to reduce dramatically the costs of treating c-UTI including treating currently hospitalized low-acuity patients without complications in an outpatient setting, reducing readmissions, and length of stay.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13595-x.Keywords: Complicated urinary tract infection, Low-acuity patients, Time to treat, Hospitalizations, Readmissions

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTI) are among the most common infectious diseases diagnosed and treated in an outpatient setting [1]. Recent studies of US physician offices found that the prevalence of complicated UTI (c-UTI) infections were 1.5% of the population accounting for over 3 million cases and 600,000 hospitalizations per year [2]. Older estimates of the direct costs of UTI infections totaled over $1.6 billion per year [3]. Recent studies have focused on the prevalence and costs of treating more complicated cases of UTI. Most cases of complicated UTI (c-UTI) are caused by Enterobacteriaceae, with Escherichia coli being the responsible pathogen [4]. These cases often have a wider range of bacteria leading to a higher risk of treatment failure and clinical complications. The treatment of more complicated UTI may fail initial therapy and treatment, increasing treatment duration. This places a clinical premium on early and proper identification of the underlying infection [4, 5].

Previous work has identified a large and expensive number of c-UTI patients. Recent estimates from Carreno and colleagues found nearly 3 million adults, or approximately 1.1% of the population were c-UTI cases or from other estimates 1.8% of hospitalizations [5, 6]. The previous studies have also found a range of 9% of patients under age 65 to over 26% aged 65 and older were readmitted within 30 days of an index admission [7, 8]. They estimate the annual costs of these cases at over $6 billion per year, though the incremental costs of readmissions were not tabulated separately.

Lodise and colleagues expanded on work by Carreno by using a similar clinical methodology to identify c-UTI cases by focusing on the acuity of hospitalized patients meeting the c-UTI case definition [9, 10]. While the estimated costs of providing hospital care to c-UTI patients were substantial, Lodise and colleague found that large numbers of patients were low acuity patients that most likely could have been treated as outpatient rather than admitted to the hospital. These admitted c-UTI patients reported no sepsis or systemic symptoms and had low scores (numbers) of comorbid conditions upon admission. Overall, this study found that 20% of admitted patients were low acuity raising the point that substantial potential savings could occur if these patients were treated on an outpatient basis [9]. Indeed, other clinical research has shown that patients presenting in the hospital or emergency department (ED) without complications could be treated in an outpatient setting once stabilized [11, 12]. Others have arrived at similar conclusions finding a reduction in disease severity among c-UTI patients hospitalized who were relatively young with few comorbid conditions [8, 11, 12]. For outpatient treatment, this does place a premium on early diagnosis and assignment of oral antimicrobial medications that could reduce antibiotic resistance rates. These studies did not, however, estimate the potential savings had these low-acuity patients been treated in an outpatient setting instead of admitted.

Despite the increased focus on the affordability of health care, providing care in the most clinically appropriate site, and antibiotic stewardship, work to date has not provided estimates of the cost of treating a c-UTI episode of care. Instead previous work focused on in-hospital costs, costs over a thirty- day period, or costs per year. There are no published estimates of the episode-based costs of treating c-UTI which are clinically and economically more important. Moreover, while previous work has noted that many hospitalized c-UTI patients are low acuity and with well-defined site-of-care clinical pathways clinically could safely be treated in an outpatient setting once stabilized rather than being admitted, there are no published estimates of the potential savings that would occur [12]. Finally, while previous work has shown a high rate of hospital readmissions among c-UTI patients, there are no published estimates of the incremental costs associated with them [13].

Building on this previous body of work, the objectives, goals and focus of this study were three-fold. First, we built on an extended previous work identifying c-UTI cases using the same previously published clinical algorithms of Carreno and Lodise but estimated episode-based costs of treatment [14]. Second, we estimated the incremental costs associated with a day of hospital care overall, a day in the intensive care unit and readmission to the hospital within 30 days. Finally, our third objective used our regression modeling to estimate the potential reduction in spending associated with treating low acuity patients without sepsis and no comorbid conditions who were hospitalized and used their clinical information to predict spending in an outpatient setting.

Methods

Study design

We used the 2025 definition of c-UTI developed by the Infectious Disease Association of America (IDSA) to include infections that have spread beyond the bladder (i.e. anything other than cystitis but for example including catheter-associated UTI, febrile or bacteremic UTI and pyelonephritis) [15]. Since more complicated cases of UTI are difficult to identify using a single reimbursement code, recent work has focused on developing clinical algorithms through existing ICD-10 and current procedural terminology (CPT) codes to identify c-UTI cases using claims data [7–10]. We use these previously published clinical definitions to identify c-UTI cases.

We start with an identification of complicated UTI cases using claims data that are consistent with the IDSA definition of c-UTI outlined above. Next, we outline the approach for developing episode-based health plan spending associated with the costs of treating c-UTI in the outpatient and inpatient setting. Our study was a retrospective analysis of claims data among privately insured adults and dependents with claims days from February 1, 2019, to May 31,2022. Our analysis relies on the Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims and Encounter database which is among the largest commercially insured set of claims data covering adults and dependents (under age 65) in the U.S. that includes de-identified person-level claims data on use including inpatient, outpatient, prescription drugs, physician and outpatient services, diagnosis, treatment and national drug codes, health plan and out-of-pocket spending, patient demographics, diagnoses, procedure and national drug codes covering more than 30 million active employees, early retirees, and dependents with data contributed by more than 160 employers and 40 health plans nationally each year [16].The analysis focuses on enrollees aged 18 to 64 (since the data include commercially insured and not Medicare patients) who were continuously enrolled for more than 6 months before and greater 29 days post our case definition date. All dollars reported are in 2024 US dollars (USD).

Case and episode-cost definitions

Our case definition of patients with c-UTI followed previously published work of both Carreno and colleagues and Lodise and colleagues noted above[7,8.9]. Since there is not a single diagnosis code for c-UTI previous clinical work developed algorithms to identify these patients. These codes were outlined in the supplement. Patients were considered to have c-UTI infection if (1) they had an inpatient or outpatient diagnosis of c-UTI (group A in the supplement), or (2) a diagnosis from group B and one or more inpatient or outpatient claims with a diagnosis or procedure code from group C within 7 days of each other (as outlined in the supplement). Once a c-UTI patient was identified using the ICD-10 and procedure codes, we created three mutually exclusive cohorts of patients. The inpatient cohort included patients if their first observed c-UTI diagnosis (i.e., the c-UTI diagnosis on the episode start date) occurred in an inpatient setting. The outpatient cohort included those whose first c-UTI diagnosis occurred in an outpatient setting. The third cohort includes patients where the c-UTI diagnosis was made in an outpatient setting but the patient was subsequently hospitalized. Once the cases were identified we created episodes of care and identified health plan payments linked only to the c-UTI course of treatment (i.e. an antibiotic filled within a day of the physician visit that provided the ultimate c-UTI diagnosis or any ambulatory care or hospitalization linked to a c-UTI diagnosis). Our approach differs from earlier work that reported 30- day or 12-month or hospital only c-UTI related spending. In contrast, our approach created spending within episodes of care [8]. Episodes of care have two major components; what clinical services specific to the condition treated to include in the episode and second the time dimension that identified the starting and end date of the episode. Our episode started with the c-UTI index date and ended with a “clean period” of no c-UTI related claims (hospital, physician, outpatient, prescriptions) for 30 days. We include all clinically related services (office visits followed by antibiotic fills within a day, procedures, labs and tests, related outpatient visits and ambulatory care, hospitalizations that we map to the c-UTI diagnosis) used to treat a c-UTI patient and we exclude any health plan payments not specifically associated (i.e. another non c-UTI diagnosis linked to the patient) with the ICD-10 c-UTI case definitions outlined above. We relied on rules used by commercial episode groupers (e.g. Truven/IBM/MEG) regarding defining the episode and condition specific spending (using temporal proximity) within the episode [17].

These cohorts are outlined below.

Inpatient cohort: Includes all outpatient events and antibiotic prescriptions and payments with service dates from the index date (the date of the c-UTI diagnosis associated with the hospital admission) to 30 days post index. It includes all inpatient stays and payments that begin from the index date to 30 days post index and all hospital readmissions and c-UTI related payments that occur within 30 days of index hospital discharge date given the index hospital discharge date is within 30 days post index date. As noted below, we provide a separate estimate for the incremental costs associated with a readmission within 30 days of the original discharge.

Inpatient cohort: Includes all outpatient events and antibiotic prescriptions and payments with service dates from the index date (the date of the c-UTI diagnosis associated with the hospital admission) to 30 days post index. It includes all inpatient stays and payments that begin from the index date to 30 days post index and all hospital readmissions and c-UTI related payments that occur within 30 days of index hospital discharge date given the index hospital discharge date is within 30 days post index date. As noted below, we provide a separate estimate for the incremental costs associated with a readmission within 30 days of the original discharge.

Outpatient to inpatient admission cohort: Includes all outpatient events and antibiotic prescriptions linked to the treatment of the c-UTI and payments with service dates from the index (ICD-10 code identifying the c-UTI) to 30 days post index and all inpatient stays (associated with an admission linked to one of the c-UTI ICD-10 codes) and payments that begin during the 30-day post index period.

Outpatient to inpatient admission cohort: Includes all outpatient events and antibiotic prescriptions linked to the treatment of the c-UTI and payments with service dates from the index (ICD-10 code identifying the c-UTI) to 30 days post index and all inpatient stays (associated with an admission linked to one of the c-UTI ICD-10 codes) and payments that begin during the 30-day post index period.

Outpatient only cohort: Includes all outpatient events and antibiotic prescriptions linked to the treatment of the c-UTI (c-UTI codes linked to the outpatient event and antibiotic filled from the event) and payments with service dates from the index to 30 days post index.

Outpatient only cohort: Includes all outpatient events and antibiotic prescriptions linked to the treatment of the c-UTI (c-UTI codes linked to the outpatient event and antibiotic filled from the event) and payments with service dates from the index to 30 days post index.

The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as a supplementary file. It was not feasible to include the public in the design of our study.

Results

Baseline characteristics of complicated UTI patients

As displayed in Table 1, we identified 93, 228 c-UTI patients in our analysis, with 27% (25,177) identified in the hospital, just under 5% (4,304) in an outpatient setting that were eventually admitted and over 68% (63,747) identified and treated in the outpatient setting. The average health plan cost of treating an episode of c-UTI care when identified in the hospital was $42,000 with a large variation in costs as reflected by the over $55,000 [standard deviation]. Average treatment costs were higher, nearly $47,900, for patients identified originally in an outpatient setting, but subsequently admitted. Finally, costs of treating a c-UTI episode in an outpatient setting was just over $5,800. As noted, the underlying distribution of health plan costs for hospitalized patients is heavily positively skewed. The median plan cost of treating a c-UTI patient identified in the hospital was $27,784, with the 25th and 75th percentiles of the distribution of plan costs $17,459 and $46,964 respectively. Similarly, the median health plan costs for those patients identified in an outpatient setting then admitted was higher $34,251 with the 25th and 75th percentiles of this distribution of plan costs $21,732 and $55,349 respectively. The analysis also found that just under 17% of c-UTI patients were readmitted to the hospital within 30 days of their original discharge. This finding was similar (findings of about 20%) to earlier work estimating readmission rates within 30 days [13]. Our estimated treatment costs are higher than earlier estimates for three reasons, first we focus on commercially insured patients where reimbursement rates are substantially higher (2 to 3 times more) than Medicare and Medicaid rates. Second, unlike some earlier estimates we focus on patient-level costs per episode, not cost per discharge that did not track the patient [10]. Finally, the dollars reported are in 2024 dollars compared to earlier dollar denominated cost estimates 6 to 10 years ago [7–9].

Means and Standard Deviations (SD)

The distribution of hospital length of stay (LOS) was similarly highly skewed, with an average of just over 4 days for those identified originally in hospital compared to nearly 4.8 days for those identified in the outpatient setting then admitted. In the data, nearly a third of the hospitalized patients had a LOS of 5 or more days. Over 8% of c-UTI admissions had a LOS of 15 days or more and nearly 12% of patients identified in an outpatient setting by admitted had a LOS of 15 days or more. Also of interest, however, is that ICU LOS was slightly shorter (1.3 vs. 1.2 days) for patients identified in-hospital versus in an outpatient setting.

Over 45% of the c-UTI patients identified in the hospitals had sepsis and another 5% had severe septic shock. In contrast, only 5.6% of those patients admitted, but identified in an outpatient setting, had sepsis upon admission. Complicated UTI patients identified in the hospital had a moderate Charlson comorbidity index (calculated in the 6-month period preceding the c-UTI index date) of 1.15 slightly lower than the 1.28 among those identified as outpatients before hospital admission. Over three-quarters of the patients were discharge to home, with over 10% transferred to home health or hospice upon discharge.

Like the results from earlier work, over 67% of c-UTI diagnosed in the hospital were female. Nearly a third were aged 50 to 59, with nearly 22% were 60 to 64, 19% were aged 40 to 49 and 27% were aged 18 to 39. Over 70% of those diagnosed, regardless of setting, were employed full-time and only 8 to 9% were retired. Nearly half were enrolled in a point-of-service plan, over 13% in an HMO, and 22% in a consumer-directed or a high deductible health plan.

Table 2 includes common, but not all, broad-spectrum antibiotics used to manage c-UTI patients. Over 46% of patients identified and treated as outpatients were only prescribed a non-broad-spectrum antibiotic, compared to 27% of those identified as outpatients then admitted and just under 19% of those identified in the hospital. The initial use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, around a third, was similar when identified as outpatients or inpatients. Just under 15% of patients had an outpatient antibiotic changed between 2 and 22 days after the initial diagnosis. Among c-UTI patients diagnosed as inpatients a third had no outpatient antibiotics, but most likely were provide drugs like ceftriaxone, Zosyn or IV therapies.

Examples of broad-spectrum antibiotics

Broad-spectrum antibiotics like ofloxacin were prescribed in 32% of patients identified in the inpatient setting and 37% among those identified and treated as outpatients. Complicated UTI patients were prescribed another broad-spectrum antibiotic ciprofloxacin in 20% of patients identified in the hospital compared to over 30% of those diagnosed and treated as outpatients. Other broad-spectrum antibiotics prescribed included doxycycline in 3 to 6% of c-UTI patients depending on the treatment setting. More narrowly targeted antibiotics including trimethoprim were used in 13% of inpatient c-UTI patients and 21% of those diagnosed and treated as outpatients. Finally, more narrow-spectrum antibiotics like cephalexin were used in 12% of patients identified and treated as inpatients and 21% of patients identified and treated as outpatients.

Finally, only roughly 2 to 5% of c-UTI patients were associated with a urinary catheter, and less than 1% had UTI kidney stones.

Statistical analyses

We conducted all analyses using Stata, version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). As highlighted in Table 1, the outcome variable of interest, the cost of treating an episode of c-UTI is highly skewed and using the Cameron and Trivedi Information Matrix test results from Stata 18 the spending data for each of the three cohorts had a non-constant variance (heteroscedastic, p<.001). To address these issues, we followed previous work on regression models using health care spending data, by using a generalized linear model (GLM) with log-link function to address the distribution of spending [18]. The GLM models have consistently had the best of goodness of fit tests for skewed health care spending data compared to ordinary least squares or log models [18]. We estimated three separate regression models and reported 95% confidence intervals and p-values. The first model examined episode-based spending associated with c-UTI patients identified in the inpatient setting, the second model focused on patients identified in an outpatient setting but subsequently hospitalized, the third model examined those identified and treated in an outpatient setting. These models served as the basis for estimating the marginal impact of our covariates on episode-based spending. They also served as the basis for our second analysis which identified low-acuity patients diagnosed and admitted with a c-UTI and no other diagnosis. For our analysis we define low- acuity as a patient without sepsis upon admission, no other ICD-10 diagnosis other than the c-UTI, and no comorbid conditions over the three years of observation. We measure comorbid conditions for each patient using the Charlson Comorbidity Index [19]. For reference, the average Charlson score among patients identified and treated as outpatients was 0.66 significantly lower than those treated as inpatients (1.15 and 1.28). Our definition of hospitalized low- acuity patients included those with a Charlson score of 0, significantly less clinically complicated than the patient typically treated as an outpatient. These low acuity patients accounted for over 27% of patients identified in the hospital (the emergency room or outpatient clinic) and subsequently hospitalized. Based on this identification we followed a two-step process. First, we estimate a counterfactual of what spending would have been if these low- acuity patients (using all their individual controls in the model) been treated as outpatients using the regression results from our third, outpatient treated only model. Next, we estimated the spending of these low-acuity patients as they were treated as inpatients with the inpatient regression model. Finally, we compared the two estimates of the predicted spending for the low-acuity patients as outpatients compared to treated as an inpatient (again comparing the exact same set of patients). This counterfactual estimate provided an indication of potential spending savings associated with early diagnosis and effective antibiotic treatments in the outpatient setting as noted in the discussion section.

For the two inpatient models, we included controls for overall length of stay (LOS) and ICU LOS, whether the patient was readmitted within 30 days post discharge, the patient discharge destination (i.e. Home, home health), an indicator of sepsis upon admission, septic shock and severe sepsis. Also included is the Charlson Comorbidity Index of concurrent health conditions, age groups, region, employment status (i.e. full-time, part-time), health plan type (as defined by MarketScan), use of broad spectrum or more target antibiotics only, the number of days after diagnosis antibiotics were changed from broad spectrum to more targeted, narrow-spectrum antibiotics. For the outpatient diagnosed and treatment regression the same controls were used other than LOS, the readmission indicator and discharge destination.

Table 3 presents the results of the regression analysis. We start with patients originally identified and treated in the inpatient setting and focused on the most important clinical results. Starting with LOS, the analysis shows that each additional day of care added nearly $2,490 and an additional day in the ICU added $3,742 to the cost of treating a c-UTI episode. Nearly 17% of patients were readmitted within 30 days, and these readmissions were expensive. Health plans paid another $12,642 for each patient readmitted within 30 days of their original discharge. The incremental costs of sepsis in treating an episode of care were substantial. Though they accounted for less than 4% of cases, the costs of treating those with severe sepsis during the episode added $11,000 to the costs of treating a c-UTI episode. The costs of treating sepsis patients upon admission were $1,640 higher compared to patients without sepsis at admission.

Costs of treating c-UTI catheter-related infections were nearly $7,800 higher than those without catheter use. Treatment costs of those with c-UTI stones were also higher by nearly $9,600 per episode. Relative to the use of more targeted, non-broad-spectrum antibiotics, episode-based treatment costs were $2,023 lower when using broad-spectrum drugs only, were $4,069 lower when antibiotic treatment was switched in 3–6 days after diagnosis but were over $4,000 to $5,000 higher when antibiotic switching was delayed under over 15 days.

Turning to those identified originally in an outpatient setting but subsequently admitted to the hospital. The mean and median treatment costs of these patients are substantially higher than those identified originally in the inpatient setting. For these c-UTI patients, the average and median treatment costs were $48,974 and $34,251 respectively compared to $42,837 and $27,784 for those identified in the inpatient the impact of additional LOS on treatment costs were similar to those identified originally in the inpatient setting. The estimates show that the marginal impacts of an additional hospital day were similar ($4,155 per additional ICU and $2,314 per non-ICU day) to those identified in the inpatient setting. Other controls in the model were generally not significantly different from zero other than treatment costs of those not receiving an outpatient antibiotic were $4,606 lower.

One major difference, however, that accounted for the higher treatment costs for those admitted to the hospital after the c-UTI was initially identified in an outpatient setting was their substantially longer length of stay compared to those identified originally in the hospital (Table 4). The length of stay for 31.8% of patients initially identified in an inpatient setting were 5 or more days compared to 34.8% of patients initially identified in an outpatient setting. Of note, however, is 8% of those identified in an inpatient setting had stays of 15 days or longer compared to nearly 12% of patients initially identified in an outpatient setting which is a major contributor to the higher treatment costs in the outpatient originated patients. The estimates presented above show that each additional day in the hospital is adding over $2,300 to the cost of treating an outpatient identified compared to an inpatient identified c-UTI patient episode.

Finally, we turn to the results for those patients initially identified and treated in an outpatient setting. Like the distribution of inpatient treatment costs, outpatient treatment costs are also positively skewed with the average cost per episode $5,812. Overall, the treatment costs were $7,100 higher for those patients originally presenting with sepsis. Treatment delay and failure added significantly to the cost of treating c-UTI patients in the outpatient setting. Relative to the use of non-broad more targeted antibiotics only, treatment costs were $334 higher when only using broad-spectrum antibiotics and substantially higher depending on the time to switch medications. When switching antibiotics by the second day, treatment costs were $1,590 higher, and were $1,672 higher, $3,210 higher. $2,835 higher and $2,691 higher when switching between days 3–6, 7–14,15–21 and over 22 days respectively after the diagnosis was made.

Our final set of results focused on hospitalized low- acuity patients. Using the results from our regression models, we predicted inpatient spending for the group of low-acuity patients (the 27% of those hospitalized) and compared it to their actual spending which was not significantly different, p < = 0.001 (the predicted and actual spending differed by 1.5% as displayed in Table 5). We then predicted treatment costs for these same set of low-acuity patients using our outpatient identified and treated regression results. The differences in predicted treatment costs were exceptionally large. When treated in an inpatient setting using our regression results, treatment costs for these low acuity c-UTI patients were predicted to be $36,472 (compared to their actual spending of $35,927). In contrast, using our outpatient regression results, had the same patients been treated in an outpatient setting their estimated treatment costs would have been $5,309, over $31,160 per episode less. Overall, this change in treatment venue just for privately insured adults would have reduced health insurance payments nationally by over $210 million per year.

Discussion

The analysis expands previous work on c-UTI in several areas. [7–11]. The analysis highlights several future clinical opportunities for reducing the costs of treating c-UTI through increased treatment in outpatient rather than inpatient settings, reductions in readmissions and hospital ICU and overall length of stay and reducing the time to switch from broad spectrum to more targeted antimicrobial therapies. Innovations through anti-microbial stewardship that reduce patient exposure to antibiotics that are not effective could address each of these issues. Antibiotic treatment (both effective and ineffective) can lead to resistant bacteria. Effective early treatment in outpatient settings can reduce antibiotic use and spread of resistant bacteria among hospitalized patients including those admitted from outpatient settings where effective initial treatment could have avoided a hospital stay. The results show substantial opportunities for reducing the costs of treating c-UTI and highlight the future need for precise and faster diagnosis and management [20]. The results highlighted the clinical and financial potential of reducing test delays and developing more formal clinical criteria for hospitalization. Today, conventional culture methods take 2 to 4 days to complete antibiotic susceptibility testing to determine the optimal antibiotic treatment. New technologies are needed to enable direct sample testing and reduce turnaround times. Direct sample testing could determine minimal inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of antibiotics for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria for a broad range of antibiotics. This quick turnaround compared to conventional culture methods could improve bacterial infection management, enhance antibiotic stewardship, and combat antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and as our results showed have the potential to reduce substantially the costs of treating a c-UTI patient.

Our results point to several areas (treatment of low-acuity hospitalized patients as outpatients, reduced readmissions and lengths of stay and reduced outpatient to inpatient admissions) where the treatment costs of patients with c-UTI could potentially be reduced with future innovations in antibiotic stewardship in detecting active bacterial metabolism and resistance in the original sample quickly instead of culturing and biochemical assays that take 2 to 4 days or more to receive testing results [20]. The analysis highlighted the large incremental treatment cost associated with switching antibiotics where the underlying pathogen was not addressed using empiric treatment while waiting for test results. These next generation tests are under development and as our results highlight have the potential to reduce the costs of treating c-UTI. These include direct urine phenotypic antimicrobial sustainability testing that provides single-shift results identifying the pathogen in question within 6 h allowing faster more targeted therapies [21]. When available, this will allow clinicians examining patients in the outpatient setting to match patients with an oral antibiotic, rather than waiting days for test results, admitting patients for observation using empiric treatment. These innovations could reduce hospitalization rates of low-acuity c-UTI patients, reduce treatment costs, improve patient outcomes and promote effective antibiotic stewardship [21].

The results also highlight the need to develop new clinical guidelines and pathways for hospital admission. These could be modeled after existing tools such as the Pneumonia Severity Index and the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index. Developing these new tools along with innovations that reduce test delay have enormous clinical and financial potential.

The analysis does have limitations. In particular, the potential to treat low-acuity hospitalized patients in the outpatient setting may be overstated since the analysis relies on claims data. We do not have physician notes, or laboratory values for patients that could provide a better understanding of why low-acuity patients were admitted. In addition, identification of c-UTI patients relied on complicated clinical algorithms based on previously published work but given their complexity may over or under count the number of c-UTI patients. Finally, while comprehensive another limitation of the analysis is the sole focus on commercially insured and not uninsured and patients with public insurance.

Conclusions

The analysis provided the first published estimates of several key clinical aspects of c-UTI treatment costs. We provided the first estimates of the cost per episode of c-UTI, the cost of readmission, the marginal cost of additional length of stay overall and in the ICU. While earlier work has estimated the number of low-acuity hospitalized patients, our analysis provided the first estimates of the potential reduction in health care spending and improvement in quality of care associated with outpatient treatment. In each area outlined above, substantial reductions in treatment costs through more effective initial diagnosis and treatment could be provided to the extent the next generation of testing generates results faster (for instance testing for carbapenem resistance to find an effective antibiotic earlier) enabling more accurate antibiotic selection directly from clinical samples. Our analysis highlights the substantial opportunities for more effective patient treatment and reductions in the costs of treating c-UTI patients that could result from on-going efforts to innovate the testing, bacteria identification and prescribing process.
