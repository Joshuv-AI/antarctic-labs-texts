Assessing barriers to musculoskeletal trauma care in Ethiopia: a national providers’ survey
Authors: Samhita Kadiyala, Cameron Geoffrey Almeida, Mengistu G Mengesha, Ephrem G Adem, James Berwin, Alpha Seifu Ali, Samuel Hailu, Kiran J Agarwal-Harding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal trauma represents a major burden in Ethiopia, yet system level barriers to effective care remain poorly studied. This study aimed to identify evidence-based, expert-informed priorities for national orthopaedic capacity building in Ethiopia.MethodsA scoping literature review guided the development of essential goals and challenges to musculoskeletal trauma care delivery in Ethiopia. These domains were refined through in-depth interviews with orthopaedic surgeons at tertiary hospitals. A structured survey was then distributed to providers and public health workers nationally through professional networks. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize responses and regional comparisons were performed.ResultsA total of 128 providers responded, primarily orthopaedic surgeons (n = 110) representing 8 of 14 regions. Most respondents reported managing tibiofibular fracture (92%), femoral shaft fracture (87%), and chronic bone infections (67%) on a weekly basis. Inadequate diagnosis (77%), treatment (83%), and rehabilitation (90%) were widely reported, with no significant regional variation (p > 0.05). In the prehospital setting, inadequate first aid (99%) and delayed triage and referral (96%) were the leading barriers. At primary and secondary hospitals, > 70% reported inadequate triage and referral. Access to slings and plaster for immobilization was inadequate (79%) in primary hospitals; surgical implants/instruments (89%) and blood products (67%) were lacking at secondary hospitals. Though > 70% of respondents reported appropriate and safe operative care delivery at tertiary centers, inadequate implants/instruments (76%), fluoroscopy (69%), and resources for managing bone (67%) and soft tissue defects (51%) were commonly reported. Negative Pressure Wound Therapy was frequently unavailable at secondary (81%) and tertiary (78%) hospitals. Insufficient trauma training was reported across all levels, most notably at primary (91%), and secondary (78%) hospitals.ConclusionsOur study suggests significant gaps in musculoskeletal trauma care delivery in Ethiopia. Key priorities should include strengthening prehospital care and triage/referral systems, improving access to essential surgical resources, and expanding system-wide trauma training. These findings provide actionable evidence to guide policy priorities and national strategies in orthopaedic trauma systems development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15141-6.Keywords: Trauma systems, Orthopaedic surgery, Musculoskeletal health, Emergency care, Triage, Referral systems, Infrastructure, Ethiopia

Background

The burden of musculoskeletal (MSK) trauma is growing worldwide, disproportionately affecting low-income countries like Ethiopia [1–3]. Understanding and addressing disparities in the burden of and access to care for injuries is essential for achieving health equity. Despite the high incidence of musculoskeletal trauma in Ethiopia, there is limited nationwide data on trauma system capacity [4–7] and the challenges faced by trauma care providers [8–11]. Understanding these challenges would help inform health policies and interventions in Ethiopia to enhance the quality and accessibility of musculoskeletal trauma care [12].

In Ethiopia, there is a substantial burden of musculoskeletal trauma and the care is delivered at local health centers and primary hospitals in the rural setting and multiple general and tertiary hospitals in the urban centers [13]. On average, one-third of trauma patients in hospitals nationwide present with road injuries [4], and about one-quarter of all injuries in tertiary centers are fractures [14].

While previous research has described trauma epidemiology, hospital-based injury patterns, trauma registry findings and surgical capacity constraints, no prior study has comprehensively evaluated the challenges and priorities for national trauma care development from providers perspective across multiple levels of care. The aim of this study was to identify provider-perceived barriers to MSK trauma care delivery in Ethiopia and determine priority areas for trauma system strengthening across prehospital, primary, secondary and tertiary care settings.

Methods

Literature review

We first performed a scoping literature review to define the essential goals and current challenges to musculoskeletal trauma care delivery in Ethiopia [15]. The methods and search strategies were guided by the framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley and Levac [16, 17]. Our literature search used a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to musculoskeletal trauma care in Ethiopia (Appendix 1). Between December 2022 and February 2023, we queried PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Hinari. Searches were performed with no date or study design restrictions and included only articles in English. All search results underwent preliminary title and abstract screening, followed by full text screening and review. We included all articles pertaining to musculoskeletal trauma care in Ethiopia at any phase of care, including preventative measures, prehospital care, rural health centers, district hospitals, and central hospitals. An analysis of references of included articles was also conducted to identify additional articles for inclusion via the snowballing technique. In total, we included 80 peer-reviewed publications, from which information was abstracted and synthesized to create a preliminary list of essential goals and potential challenges to musculoskeletal trauma care in Ethiopia (Appendix 2).

Next, exploratory interviews were conducted with senior orthopaedic surgeons at five major trauma centers. These included Black Lion Hospital (BLH) in Addis Ababa, University of Gondar Hospital, Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital (TSGH), Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH), and Soddo Missionary Hospital. These hospitals were selected to represent a range of geographic regions and facility types, capturing both public and charity sector perspectives. Experts were purposely selected based on their clinical experience in musculoskeletal trauma care, leadership roles, familiarity with the healthcare system of Ethiopia, and based on guidance from the Ethiopian Ministry of Health (MoH) and the leadership of the Ethiopian Society of Orthopaedics and Traumatology (ESOT). A consistent set of questions were used to explore the state of musculoskeletal care, challenges faced, management of common orthopaedic conditions, support needs, and examples of effective care delivery (Appendix 3). The interviews allowed for in-depth validation and refinement of challenges identified in the literature review, as well as identified additional barriers.

The consolidated list of essential goals and potential challenges to orthopaedic trauma care were then organized into five domains: injury prevention, prehospital care, primary hospitals, secondary hospitals, and tertiary hospitals, adhering to methodology in previously published literature on technical priorities for trauma care development in Malawi [15]. Challenges related to secondary and tertiary hospitals were further classified as impacting initial management, definitive care, or rehabilitation and aftercare. These goals and challenges were used to develop a provider survey.

National provider survey

A nationwide electronic survey was conducted among musculoskeletal healthcare providers at academic teaching hospitals, government hospitals, and faith-based hospitals across the country (Appendix 12). Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Addis Ababa University and was deemed exempt from full review. As the research involved a voluntary survey of orthopaedic surgeons and did not utilize patient data, the requirement for formal written informed consent was waived by the IRB. Informed consent was considered obtained through the voluntary completion and submission of the survey by the participants. The survey, conducted via Google Forms, was disseminated primarily through ESOT, as well as by members of the Ethiopian MoH and through Ethiopian authors’ academic institutions and professional networks. A convenient sampling strategy was used and participation was voluntary. Content validity was established through the literature review and expert consultation. Formal pilot testing, questionnaire validation and reliability assessment were not performed.

The survey included a mix of multiple-choice questions, Likert scale questions, and open-ended questions. Multiple-choice questions gathered quantitative data on available resources, such as the number of orthopaedic surgeons and the types of equipment available. Likert scale questions assessed the perceived adequacy of resources and barriers to care. The adequacy of various trauma care services was evaluated at each level of the health system. A service was considered “inadequate” if a survey respondent reported that fewer than 50% of patients received that service. Open-ended questions captured qualitative insights into challenges faced by healthcare workers in managing musculoskeletal trauma.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize findings. Regional comparisons were performed using Chi- square tests or Fisher’s exact. Missing data were handled using available case analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Since the regional analysis was exploratory, multiple comparison adjustment was not performed.

Results

A total of 128 Ethiopian healthcare providers completed the survey, mostly orthopaedic surgeons and trainees practicing in referral/tertiary centers (86%). Most respondents were from Sidama and Addis Ababa (23% each) followed by Amhara (21%), and Oromia (13%) (Table 1).

Burden of musculoskeletal pathology

Considering respondents who reported treating at least 1 case per week, the most frequently reported acute musculoskeletal conditions were tibiofibular shaft fractures (92%), femoral shaft fractures (87%), and wrist fractures and/or dislocations (74%). The most frequently reported chronic musculoskeletal conditions were bone infections/osteomyelitis/chronic wound infections (67%), post-traumatic stiffness (52%), chronic dislocations, malunions, and/or nonunions (45%), and amputations (37%) (Table 2). Most patients were adults, with 22%, 16%, and 4% of survey respondents, respectively, reporting the majority (> 50%) of patients treated with acute upper extremity, lower extremity, and spine injuries were children. Similarly, 25% of respondents reported that children represented > 50% of patients with chronic musculoskeletal conditions. There were no significant variations between geographic regions (p > 0.05), with the exception of amputations, which were reported more frequently in Sidama than in any other region (53%, p = 0.05) (Appendix 4–5).

Prevention, pre-hospital, and primary trauma care

The most commonly reported mechanism of injuries were road traffic incidents (95%), followed by falls (48%), and interpersonal violence (42%) (Fig. 1). Additionally, 96% of respondents felt that traditional bone setting practices may increase the burden of musculoskeletal pathology and delay definitive treatment. In the prehospital setting, the most frequently reported barriers were inadequate first aid (99%), poor triage/hospital referral (96%), and inadequate or delayed transport following trauma (92%) (Table 3).

Most common causes of MSK injuries

At the primary hospital level (101 respondents), inadequate risk stratification and triage (82%) was the most commonly reported, followed by inadequate/delayed transfer to a higher-level facility (75%) and suboptimal injury diagnosis and patient stabilization (74%) (Table 4). The key barriers to initial management of musculoskeletal injuries (88 respondents) included lack of staff training (91%); limited availability of splints, slings, and casts (80%), inadequate staffing (74%), restricted access to x-rays (73%), and deficiencies in triage (71%) (Table 3). There was no significant regional variation in reporting on pre-hospital and primary hospital barriers (p > 0.05) (Appendix 8–9).

Secondary & tertiary hospitals

At secondary hospitals (92 respondents), inadequacies were reported in injury diagnosis and stabilization (68%), risk stratification and triage (74%), and referral to tertiary care centers (75%) (Table 3). Major barriers to initial injury management included insufficient trauma care training (78%), shortage of blood products (67%), shortage of emergency care providers (64%), ineffective triage and referral (60%), and inability to perform external fixation (59%). Inadequate delivery of definitive treatment for common musculoskeletal conditions (67%) and limb- and life-saving interventions (66%), was mainly attributed to shortage of surgical implants and instruments (89%), limited procedural experience (70%), and insufficient orthopaedic workforce (68%) (Table 4).

At tertiary centers, 19% of respondents reported inadequate injury diagnosis and stabilization, and 25% reported inadequate risk stratification and triage. Key barriers to initial management included shortage of blood products (71%), lack of standardized trauma protocols (63%), limited advanced imaging (58%), and inadequate trauma training (53%). Approximately 30% reported that operative care delivery and comprehensive management of complications were inadequate (Table 4). Limited access to implants (76%), fluoroscopy (69%), equipment for managing complex fractures (66%), and patient financial constraints (51%) were reported (Table 3).

Across secondary and tertiary centers, negative pressure wound therapy was the most commonly unavailable resource (80.5%, 78%, respectively), followed by fluoroscopy (80.2% at secondary; 64% at tertiary). At secondary hospitals, additional gaps included a lack of intramedullary nails (61%) and plates and screws (58%) (Table 5). Blood product shortages were also noted by 67% of respondents at secondary hospitals (Table 4) and 71% at tertiary hospitals.

Discussion

This national survey of orthopaedic providers in Ethiopia demonstrates substantial gaps in the diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation of musculoskeletal trauma across all levels of care in Ethiopia. It identifies the most commonly encountered acute and chronic conditions while highlighting critical deficiencies in the trauma system. These findings should guide priorities for strengthening trauma care delivery and targeted strategies to expand access to high-quality musculoskeletal trauma care.

Starting in 2015, the Ethiopian MoH launched the National Surgical Care Strategic Plan: Saving Lives through Safe Surgery (SaLTS) initiatives, pioneering strategies to improve access to safe surgical and anaesthetic care across the country through changes to leadership and governance, infrastructure, supply chains, human resources, partnerships, innovations, education, and rigorous evaluation [18, 19]. While these initiatives have helped to mobilize nationwide efforts on improving surgical access, significant gaps in surgical care persist nationwide [20]. Notably, limited data are available regarding musculoskeletal trauma care capacity. While the full details we gathered regarding the current state of musculoskeletal trauma care in Ethiopia are available in the appendices, we highlight three key areas for improvement below.

Pre-hospital care: EMS infrastructure and triage

Since 2006, multiple national efforts have sought to expand emergency medical services and improve the standard of emergency care within Ethiopia [21]. Recent studies highlight persistent gaps in training, infrastructure, coordination, and availability of equipment and ambulance services [22]. Our findings validate this systemic gap, with nearly all respondents reporting inadequate first aid (99.2%) and inadequate triage or hospital referral (96.1%) in the pre-hospital setting.

At Black Lion Hospital, where open fractures account for a quarter of all fractures [23], prospective studies have suggested that nearly 85% of open fractures did not receive antibiotics within the recommended 2 h after injury [24, 25]. At Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital, nearly 60% of patients with open tibial shaft fractures received antibiotics over six hours after injury [26]. Compounded by the persistence of traditional bone setting in Ethiopia, which contributes to delayed presentation [27] the burden of chronic bone and wound infections is substantial. At Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, the prevalence of chronic osteomyelitis in hospitalized pediatric patients was 86.3% [28]. At BLH, a prospective study of 442 patients with chronic osteomyelitis found that over half of patients sought the services of a traditional bonesetter, frequently due to hospital bed shortages at lower-level hospitals [29]. We similarly found that a staggering 67% of respondents treat a chonic bone or wound infection every week. Building on existing literature, our findings suggest that strengthening the pre-hospital frontier through expanded ambulance networks, standardized open fracture protocols, and improved training and communication with traditional bonesetters is essential to prevent these high-morbidity complications.

Primary and secondary hospitals: standardizing triage and developing robust referral systems

While district health centers and primary hospitals are frequently the health facilities where patients with traumatic injuries present [30], they often lack the diagnostic capacity and workforce to triage and manage these cases safely [31]. Though some tertiary hospitals like ALERT have implemented structured triage systems [14], their adoption at lower-level hospitals in Ethiopia is unknown. However, poor triage and referral systems likely contribute to low-severity injuries being referred to and overburdening tertiary centers: Mengesha et al. at Hawassa University found 17% of referred patients had a moderate trauma score and < 1% a severe trauma score [32]. We found 82.2% of respondents identifying inadequate risk stratification and triage as a major barrier for the management of musculoskeletal trauma at the primary level and 73.9% at the secondary level. Poor triage and delayed referral can exacerbate morbidity and further burden the health system. Especially for common pathologies such as open fractures or osteomyelitis, standardized triage and referral protocols including staff training and resource provision to ensure adequate implementation may improve patient outcomes and lessen the burden on tertiary hospitals.

Tertiary hospitals: supply chain of essential technology

For more complex injuries reaching secondary and tertiary centers [33], a key limiting factor becomes the lack of a reliable supply chain for essential orthopaedic surgical technology such as intraoperative fluoroscopy and negative pressure wound therapy. Prior research conducted at Surgical Implant Generation Network (SIGN) hospitals globally has highlighted the lack of computed tomography (CT) and C-arms for intraoperative fluoroscopy [34]. At Hawassa University, a tertiary center which serves a catchment area of 18 million people, the hospital’s singular C-arm was non-functional and could not be repaired, requiring international donor funding to replace in 2021 [35]. Our findings reveal that 80% of respondents felt that fluoroscopy is an essential but largely unavailable technology. At BLH, which manages the nation’s highest volume of acetabular fractures, surgeons with specialized training in pelvic and acetabular surgery have achieved remarkable outcomes even in the absence of a C-arm [36], though this requires considerable skill to be performed safely and should not be considered the standard of care. While the number of functional c-arms in orthopaedic departments across Ethiopia is not well-described in the literature, our survey suggests that most hospitals operate with very limited access to fluoroscopy, a key area for improvement. Increasing the number of C-arm machines and expanding training opportunities for technicians to repair these technologies as the need arises is essential for maintaining access to safe surgery.

Additionally, specialized tools for management of soft tissue injuries (e.g. negative pressure wound therapy, NPWT) and fractures (e.g. periarticular locking plates) are similarly difficult to access, with nearly 80% of respondents reporting NPWT technology unavailable at tertiary hospitals. The few papers which describe the usage of NPWT in orthopaedic departments in Ethiopia utilize pumps donated by international organizations [37]. Introducing low-cost NPWT that has been developed and tested with promising results in LMICs [38] to Ethiopia may help improve outcomes for the high-volume of complex wounds without relying on donations. At present, organizations such as SIGN Fracture Care fill a crucial gap by providing intramedullary nails at no cost [39], though continued dependence on externally donated implants is not locally sustainable and leaves essential trauma care vulnerable to supply chain interruptions. However, further research is needed to elucidate effective strategies to improve the availability and affordability of technologies such as NPWT and orthopaedic implants in the Ethiopian context to enable a sustainable supply chain and reduce donor dependency.

Ultimately, improving training, infrastructure, and surgical supply availability will require extensive multisectoral collaboration between the MoH, ESOT, funders, and innovators globally. Standardized trauma registries are critically lacking across Ethiopia, impeding epidemiological research and quality improvement efforts [9]. Applying a data-driven approach to the improvement of the musculoskeletal trauma systems in Ethiopia will help policymakers and clinicians target resources, time, and efforts to areas that most need it.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Participation was voluntary, introducing the potential for non-response and self-selection biases. Additionally, the uneven geographic representation, with some regions such as Central Ethiopia Regional State, South Ethiopia Regional State, Somali, and Harari (grouped as “Other” in geographic analyses) having few respondents, may reduce the generalizability of findings. Exploratory regional comparisons were conducted without adjustment for multiple testing. Additionally, while structured interviews with multiple Ethiopian orthopaedic surgeons were conducted to develop and refine the survey, formal questionnaire validation through construct validity testing, reliability assessment, and measurement of internal consistency was not conducted given the exploratory nature of the study. The present study’s method for survey dissemination was primarily through the ESOT annual conference, where orthopaedic surgeons and tertiary care providers were overrepresented, limiting representation of primary healthcare workers, emergency physicians, rehabilitation specialists, nurses and rural providers, which may not be a representative across the country. Finally, the lack of patient outcome data precludes an evaluation of the direct impact of these barriers on care quality and survival. Despite its limitations, however, this study was the first of its kind to utilize evidence-based, expert-driven insights to highlight and assess local priorities for building musculoskeletal trauma care capacity in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

This national survey identified major provider-perceived gaps in musculoskeletal trauma care at all levels of the health system. Deficiencies in diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation were widely reported, spanning the pre-hospital, hospital, and rehabilitation services. Key priorities include strengthening triage and referral systems, expanding surgical and rehabilitation capacity, and addressing workforce training gaps, particularly at primary and secondary facilities. Persistent shortages of essential resources including fluoroscopy, implants, and blood products further constrain safe surgery even at tertiary hospitals. These findings provide clear direction for targeted investment in trauma systems and capacity building nationwide.
