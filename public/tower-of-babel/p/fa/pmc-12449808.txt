Estimation of graphene oxide nanoparticle reinforcement on the mechanical properties of maxillofacial silicone
Authors: Vigneshvar Kandaswamy Srinivasan, Naveen Gopi Chander, Muthukumar Balasubramaniam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaxillofacial silicone is commonly used for facial prostheses but suffers from low tensile and tear strength and limited elasticity. The integration of graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles has been proposed to improve these mechanical shortcomings.AimThis in vitro study aimed to evaluate the effect of varying GO concentrations (0 %, 0.5 %, 1 %, 2 %, 3 %, and 5 %) on the tensile strength, tear strength, and Shore A hardness of maxillofacial silicone elastomers.Materials and methodsA total of 504 silicone samples were fabricated and divided into six groups based on GO concentration, with 28 samples per group for each test. Silicone and GO were homogenized and cured per standard protocols. Tensile and tear strength were measured using a universal testing machine; Shore A hardness was assessed with a durometer. Material characterization was conducted using FTIR and FESEM.ResultsThe highest tensile strength (2.253 MPa) was observed in the 3 % GO group (SG-3), indicating superior stress resistance. Tear strength peaked in the 0.5 % GO group (15.625 MPa), with SG-3 showing nearly comparable results (15.184 MPa). Shore A hardness increased slightly with GO content, with SG-3 offering an optimal flexibility-rigidity balance. FESEM confirmed uniform GO dispersion in SG-3, enhancing filler bonding. FTIR revealed increased peak intensities, suggesting improved molecular interactions and a denser polymer matrix.ConclusionGraphene oxide significantly enhanced the mechanical performance of maxillofacial silicone, with 3 % GO yielding the most favorable combination of strength, elasticity, and material integrity.

Introduction

Maxillofacial silicone elastomers play a crucial role in prosthodontics, particularly in the fabrication of extra oral prostheses for patients with congenital or acquired defects.1 These materials are widely preferred due to their excellent biocompatibility, ease of manipulation, and ability to replicate the natural appearance of soft tissues.2 However, despite these advantages, maxillofacial silicones exhibit inherent limitations such as inadequate tear strength, low resistance to mechanical stress, and susceptibility to environmental degradation, leading to compromised prosthesis longevity.3

Over the years, extensive research has been conducted to improve the mechanical properties of maxillofacial silicone. The enhancement of the mechanical properties of maxillofacial silicone elastomers remains a significant challenge in prosthodontics. Maxillofacial prosthetic materials must withstand various mechanical and environmental stresses while maintaining biocompatibility and esthetic integrity. However, the inherent limitations of conventional silicone, such as low tear resistance and susceptibility to degradation, necessitate the exploration of advanced reinforcement strategies.4 Various reinforcement strategies, including the incorporation of polymeric additives, surface treatments, and filler modifications, have been explored to enhance durability and performance.4 Among these approaches, nanotechnology has gained significant attention due to the superior reinforcing potential of nanomaterials, which can enhance the mechanical, thermal, and antimicrobial properties of polymeric matrices while maintaining their biocompatibility.5 Among the materials, graphene-based nanomaterials, particularly graphene oxide (GO), have emerged as promising material for reinforcing maxillofacial silicone elastomers.6

Graphene oxide possesses exceptional mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties, making it a widely studied material in biomedical applications.7 With a tensile strength approximately 200 times greater than steel, GO exhibits superior mechanical reinforcement potential, high surface area, and excellent dispersion within polymer matrices.8 Unlike pristine graphene, GO contains oxygenated functional groups that enhance its solubility and compatibility with polymeric systems, ensuring uniform distribution and effective interaction within the silicone elastomer network.9 Furthermore, GO has demonstrated significant antibacterial and antifungal properties through multiple mechanisms, including oxidative stress induction, membrane disruption, and electron transfer, which may contribute to improved prosthesis longevity in oral and facial environments.10

Despite these promising attributes, limited research has been conducted on the application of GO in maxillofacial silicone elastomers.11 Several studies have reported the reinforcement potential of GO in polymeric materials, showing improved tensile strength, elasticity, and thermal stability, yet its impact on maxillofacial silicone remains underexplored.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Given the challenges associated with the durability and functionality of silicone prostheses, an investigation into the effects of GO reinforcement is warranted.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 Understanding its impact on key mechanical properties, such as tensile strength, tear resistance, and Shore hardness, is critical to determining its suitability as a reinforcing agent for maxillofacial prosthetic applications.24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32

This study aims to evaluate the effect of varying concentrations of graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles on the mechanical properties of maxillofacial silicone elastomers. It investigates the influence of GO incorporation on tensile strength, tear resistance, and Shore hardness to determine its impact on the material's overall performance.

The null hypothesis states that the incorporation of GO nanoparticles into maxillofacial silicone elastomers does not significantly alter their mechanical properties. The study shall provide evidence on the feasibility of GO as a reinforcing agent for maxillofacial silicone by analyzing these parameters. The findings may contribute to the development of more durable and functionally superior prosthetic materials, ultimately improving the quality of life for patients requiring facial rehabilitation.

Material and methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (XXX XX XXX IRB no.XXXXX) Nano-graphene oxide (GO) particles (ULTRANANOTECH) used in the study had particle size ranging from 1 to 5 nm. Prior to incorporation, the GO particles were precisely weighed using a high-precision electronic balance (MSU224S-100-DU analytical balance) to ensure weight accuracy. The balanced nanoparticles were gradually incorporated into pre-measured quantities of room-temperature vulcanizing (RTV) silicone elastomer (M511 Platinum Silicone, Techno Vent) designated for various test groups.

The sample size was determined through a pilot study based on an effect size of 0.4, an α error probability of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95, with six study groups. The estimated sample size per group was 28, resulting in a total of 168 samples per parameter and an overall sample size of 504 for the study.

A total of 504 samples were fabricated for mechanical testing. The specimens were divided into six groups based on the weight percentage of GO incorporated into the maxillofacial silicone. The control group (SG-0) contained no GO, while the intervention groups included SG-0.5, SG-1, SG-2, SG-3, and SG-5, corresponding to GO weight percentages of 0.5 %, 1 %, 2 %, 3 %, and 5 %, respectively. Each group consisted of 28 specimens per mechanical property evaluation, totaling 168 specimens for each mechanical test to ensure statistical robustness and minimize variability. To maintain consistency, all specimens were fabricated by the same operator using standardized protocols.

The preparation of the GO-reinforced RTV silicone elastomer followed a structured mixing protocol. Nano-graphene oxide at predetermined concentrations (0 %, 0.5 %, 1 %, 2 %, 3 %, 5 % w/w) was introduced into the RTV silicone elastomer using the base and catalyst method. The mixing process was performed through mechanical stirring at 1500 rpm for 30 min at room temperature, accompanied by continuous cooling to prevent excessive heat buildup, agglomeration, or premature polymerization. This method ensured uniform nanoparticle dispersion within the maxillofacial silicone matrix. The homogenized mixture was then transferred into pre-fabricated molds conforming to ISO standard dimensions specified for each mechanical property assessment, including tensile strength, tear strength, and Shore A hardness. To eliminate air bubbles and enhance material uniformity, the filled molds were subjected to a vacuum degassing process for 5 min before curing. The specimens were allowed to cure undisturbed for 24 h at room temperature in a controlled environment to achieve optimal polymerization. After polymerization, the cured samples were carefully demolded and stored in a desiccator until further testing to prevent moisture absorption or environmental degradation.

The tensile strength of the GO-reinforced maxillofacial silicone was evaluated in accordance with ISO 527–1:2019 standards using a Universal Testing Machine (INSTRON 3400). The specimens were clamped and subjected to uniaxial tensile loading at a crosshead speed of 50 mm/min until failure. The maximum force required to fracture the specimen was recorded, and tensile strength was calculated using the equation Ts = F/A, where Ts represents tensile strength (MPa), F is the maximum force at rupture (N), and A denotes the cross-sectional area of the unstrained specimen (mm2). Each test was conducted in triplicate to ensure reliability and repeatability.

Tear strength was assessed following ISO 34–1:2015 guidelines. The specimens were notched to create a controlled defect and then subjected to a progressively applied force until rupture. The maximum force at failure was recorded, and tear strength was determined using the equation T = F/D, where T represents tear strength (N/mm), F is the maximum force at rupture (N), and D denotes the thickness of the specimen (mm). The tests were conducted in a controlled environment to eliminate external influences on material behavior.

The Shore A hardness of the specimens was measured using a calibrated digital durometer (Shore Digital Durometer) in accordance with ISO 7619–1:2010 guidelines. The durometer was positioned perpendicularly to the specimen surface, and a standardized force of 1 kg was applied. The hardness reading was recorded after 1 s of firm contact, following standard testing protocols. Each specimen underwent five independent indentation measurements at different locations, and the mean value was calculated to obtain the final Shore A hardness reading. To minimize operator-induced variability, all measurements were taken by the same examiner.

One representative sample from each group were subjected to characterization studies of Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) and Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis. FESEM imaging was conducted to assess the dispersion of GO particles within the silicone matrix, verify nanoparticle distribution, and evaluate the structural integrity of the composite material. High-resolution images were obtained at higher magnifications to capture both surface morphology and internal nanoparticle distribution. FTIR spectroscopy was employed to identify potential chemical interactions between the GO and RTV silicone by detecting shifts in characteristic absorption peaks, ensuring homogeneous integration without adverse material degradation. These analyses provided insight into the material's structural and chemical properties following nanoparticle incorporation.

The results of the mechanical property tests, including tensile strength, tear strength, and Shore A hardness, were statistically analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test to determine significant differences between the experimental groups. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted where necessary to determine specific group differences. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp.).

Discussion

The study findings demonstrated that GO incorporation significantly affected the mechanical properties of silicone and the effects were concentration-dependent. Hence the study rejected the null hypothesis. The improvements in mechanical performance at lower GO concentrations and the subsequent decline at higher concentrations indicate a complex interplay between nanoparticle dispersion, interfacial interactions, and matrix integrity.3,4,10

At lower concentrations (e.g., 0.5 wt% GO), the homogeneous distribution of GO within the silicone matrix facilitated efficient stress transfer, thereby enhancing tensile strength. This improvement can be attributed to the high aspect ratio and large surface area of GO, which enables effective load distribution within the polymer network. Additionally, strong interfacial adhesion between GO and the silicone chains potentially contributes to improved stress resistance. However, beyond an optimal threshold, GO nanoparticles tend to agglomerate, forming clusters that act as stress concentration points, thereby neutralizing or even reversing the reinforcement effect. This phenomenon is in agreement with prior studies, which have reported similar trends in other elastomeric nanocomposites.16,17 The clustering of GO beyond its percolation limit reduces its ability to interact uniformly with the polymer chains, thereby diminishing the mechanical benefits associated with its nanoscale dispersion.18,19

A similar effect was observed in the tear resistance of the silicone. At optimized GO concentrations, the nanoparticles enhanced interfacial bonding, leading to improved mechanical integrity and resistance to tearing forces.10 The role of GO in reinforcing silicone was supported by prior studies demonstrating that functionalized GO facilitates strong adhesion between reinforcing fillers and the silicone matrix.16, 17, 18 This enhancement is likely due to the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups on GO, which promote better stress distribution across the polymer network. However, excessive GO content resulted in nanoparticle agglomeration, disrupting uniform stress transfer and reducing tear strength.19 This suggests that precise control over GO loading is essential to maximize its reinforcing capabilities while preventing detrimental aggregation effects.

In contrast to tensile strength and tear resistance, Shore A hardness exhibited a gradual decline with increasing GO concentrations. This reduction in hardness is indicative of alterations in the polymer cross-linking density. At lower concentrations, GO restricts silicone chain mobility, contributing to increased rigidity. However, at higher concentrations, excessive filler incorporation disrupts the polymer network, leading to reduced cross-link density and, consequently, lower hardness values. This observation aligns with studies on graphene-based silicone composites, where controlled filler concentrations were shown to improve mechanical properties while excessive additions led to network disruption and diminished performance.20

Morphological and spectroscopic analyses provided further insight into the observed trends. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) confirmed that at optimal GO concentrations (e.g., 3 wt%), nanoparticles were well-dispersed, contributing to enhanced mechanical properties. However, at higher concentrations, the formation of GO agglomerates was evident, corroborating the mechanical test results that showed a decline in performance due to particle clustering. Furthermore, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy revealed characteristic absorption bands corresponding to oxygen-containing functional groups in GO, suggesting potential Si–O–C bond formation.17 This chemical interaction likely strengthens the interfacial adhesion between GO and the silicone matrix, reinforcing the composite structure. However, as the concentration of GO increases, the steric hindrance and phase separation effects become more pronounced, leading to reduced polymer matrix integrity.18, 19, 20

Despite the promising outcomes of this study, it had certain limitations. The investigation focused on specific GO concentrations, leaving intermediate values unexplored. Additionally, only one type of RTV silicone elastomer was tested, and variations in polymer formulations may influence the reinforcing effects of GO. Another limitation is the observed agglomeration at higher GO concentrations, highlighting the need for improved dispersion techniques. Future studies should explore surface modifications, functionalization strategies, or alternative processing methods such as high-energy sonication or in situ polymerization to enhance GO distribution and prevent clustering.

Further research should also focus on evaluating the long-term stability and degradation of GO-reinforced silicone elastomers under varying environmental conditions, including UV exposure, humidity, and thermal fluctuations. Additionally, as color changes were observed during the sample fabrication, further studies can evaluate these changes in relation to color stability and investigate the use of appropriate stains for effective masking. Moreover, biocompatibility assessments are crucial for the clinical translation of these materials, particularly for maxillofacial prosthetic applications. Investigating hybrid nanocomposites that incorporate GO in conjunction with other nanofillers, such as silica or carbon nanotubes, may provide synergistic enhancements in mechanical performance. Finally, in vivo studies are necessary to validate the clinical efficacy and safety of GO-modified silicone elastomers, ensuring their suitability for prosthodontic and biomedical applications.
