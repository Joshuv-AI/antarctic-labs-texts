Neonatal pulmonary hypertension in the NICU: a 10-year single-center experience
Authors: Oguzhan Ay, Sezgin Gunes, Erkan Erfidan, Ayse Simsek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackroundNeonatal pulmonary hypertension(PHT) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in newborns. Early diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary hypertension are critical for preventing long-term respiratory and cardiovascular complications. The aim of this study is to contribute to the literature by investigating the differences between neonates with PHT who receive medical treatment and those who do not, the relationship between echocardiographic measurements and the initiation of treatment, and the differences between preterm and term neonates.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 75 neonates diagnosed with pulmonary hypertension and treated in the neonatal intensive care unit were retrospectively analyzed. The diagnosis of PHT was established by echocardiography with estimation of pulmonary artery pressure via tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity measurement. Right Ventricular Systolic Pressure(RVSP)measurements were recorded as RVSP1 for the first two days, RVSP2 between days 3-7, and RVSP3 after 7 days.For statistical evaluation, patients were divided into two groups based on whether they received medical treatment for PHT and were compared. The group receiving PHT treatment was further divided into preterm (< 37 weeks) and term (≥ 37 weeks) neonates and compared.ResultsAmong the 7578 patients in the neonatal intensive care unit, 75 (1.07%) were diagnosed with pulmonary hypertension. 47 of these patients received PHT treatment. The treatment group had a greater gestational age and higher birth weight. There was no significant difference in laboratory parameters between the groups receiving and not receiving PHT treatment. However, in the group not receiving PHT, patients with low RVSP measurements had lower CRP values. Background

Neonatal pulmonary hypertension is a critical condition characterized by elevated pulmonary vascular resistance and low oxygen saturation in newborn neonates. While a mean pulmonary arterial pressure > 25 mmHg measured by cardiac catheterization after three months of age is diagnostic for PHT, a definitive measurement threshold in neonates remains unclear. Furthermore, echocardiography is more frequently employed for diagnosis due to the inherent risks associated with catheterization [1, 2].

PHT has a diverse array of etiologies. Prominent among these are pulmonary diseases such as meconium aspiration syndrome, respiratory distress syndrome, pneumonia, and sepsis. Structural abnormalities of the pulmonary vasculature can also contribute. Other potential causes include pulmonary hypoplasia, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, and congenital heart disease [3, 4].

The increased prevalence of high-risk pregnancy monitoring centers and neonatal intensive care units has likely led to a rise in PHT diagnoses. Simultaneously, advancements in neonatal care, including the use of prenatal corticosteroids, postnatal surfactant therapies, and improved respiratory equipment, have reduced mortality and complications in pulmonary hypertension arising from respiratory conditions (e.g., respiratory distress syndrome, transient tachypnea of the newborn, bronchopulmonary dysplasia) [5–9].

The incidence of neonatal PHT is reported as 1.9 per 1,000 live births. In severe cases, despite appropriate treatment, mortality ranges from 5 to 10%, and neurodevelopmental impairment can occur in up to 25% of cases [10, 11]. It is a significant cause of mortality, particularly in premature neonates, if not managed correctly [4].

In advanced stages, PHT can lead to right ventricular dysfunction and hemodynamic compromise. Elevated pulmonary vascular resistance increases right ventricular afterload, potentially causing early right ventricular dilatation and right heart failure. This can result in decreased blood flow from the systemic to the pulmonary circulation and reduced cardiac output [12–16]. If these progresses, inadequate pulmonary perfusion can lead to mortality. If left untreated, mortality rates in PHT complicated by right ventricular dysfunction and right heart failure are reported to be between 20–50% [3].

Management of neonatal PHT involves creating a low-stimulation, calm environment, including sedation if necessary. Respiratory support can range from supplemental oxygen in an incubator to intubation or high-frequency mechanical ventilation. Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) can be effective in reducing pulmonary vascular resistance, and surfactant replacement may also be necessary. For neonates unresponsive to iNO, sildenafil, epoprostenol, and iloprost may be considered. Combination therapies may be required, although side effects and long-term safety must be carefully evaluated. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation(ECMO) can be a life-saving intervention in cases where pharmacologic treatment is insufficient [17]. Systemic hypotension is managed with inotropic support and fluid resuscitation. The decision to close or maintain a patent ductus arteriosus is determined by the infant’s clinical condition [18–22].

Managing PHT in the NICU remains a significant challenge requiring a multifaceted approach [17]. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, treatment strategies, management, and survival outcomes of neonates with pulmonary hypertension treated at our institution between 2015 and 2024. The experiences of neonatal intensive care centers are crucial for advancing the care of this patient population.

Materials and methods

Study population

Between January 2015 and November 2024, neonates with a preliminary diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension in the neonatal intensive care unit were identified through a retrospective chart review. In our neonatal intensive care unit, over the past decade, 75 hypoxemic neonates have received an initial diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension based on clinical assessment by neonatologists. In our clinic, all neonates with a presumptive diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension, in whom pulmonary pathology has been excluded and who require FiO2 > 0.4, undergo echocardiography performed by a pediatric cardiologist within the first 24 h of life and subsequently. The study population consisted of patients who fulfilled the echocardiographic criteria for PHT, as outlined in the Laboratory and Echocardiography section. In our study, sildenafil, iNO (inhaled Nitric Oxide), and inhaled Iloprost were considered medical treatments for PHT. The 28 patients who did not received these medications comprised the PHT non-treatment group (Group 1), while the remaining 47 neonates who received treatment constituted the treatment group (Group 2).

Additionally, a secondary grouping was performed, and statistical analyses were conducted after stratifying the neonates receiving PHT treatment into preterm and term subgroup. Neonates born before 37 weeks of gestation were classified as preterm, and those born at or after 37 weeks were classified as term (Fig. 1).

Laboratory and echocardiography

Demographic data (mode of delivery, gestational age, birth weight), laboratory values ​​taken within the first 0–24 h of hospital admission (complete blood count, ALT, AST, uric acid, urea, creatinine, CRP), treatments received, and transthoracic echocardiography reports were retrospectively recorded. The echocardiographic examinations were conducted by a physician specializing in pediatric cardiology. The diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension was based on the following echocardiographic criteria in the setting of acute and persistent hypoxemia (FiO2 > 0.4): tricuspid regurgitation, a right ventricular pressure measured from the tricuspid valve exceeding 40 mmHg, right ventricular or right heart(ventricular and atrial) dysfunction or enlargement, or evidence of right-to-left shunting across the ductus arteriosus or patent foramen ovale [23, 24]. Neonates with clinical signs of hypoxemia (FiO2 > 0.4), right ventricular systolic pressure values > 40 mmHg within the first 7 days of life, and corroborating echocardiographic findings were included in this study.

Right ventricular systolic pressure(RVSP) values were obtained from the apical four-chamber view, in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines [25]. RVSP1 represents the initial measurement within the 48 h, RVSP2 represents measurements taken between days 3 and 7 of admission, and RVSP3 represents measurements taken after day 7. RVSP1-2 represents the difference between the second and first measurements, and RVSP2-3 represents the difference between the third and second measurements.

Diagnoses of concomitant conditions (e.g., respiratory distress syndrome, transient tachypnea of the newborn, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, congenital pneumonia, asphyxia, sepsis, hypoglycemia) were established using the guidelines of the Turkish Neonatal Society [26].

Ethics approval

Our study was approved by the Buca Seyfi Demirsoy Teaching and Research Hospital Ethics Committee with ethical decision number 2024/360 and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 25.0 software. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics for normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables are reported as median values. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies. Appropriate analyses were conducted to compare patient groups. For normally distributed continuous variables, mean ± standard deviation was used; for non-normally distributed continuous variables, the median was used; and for categorical variables, n was used. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to evaluate the significance of differences between median values, and Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the 75 neonates studied, 47 received PHT treatment based on clinical and echocardiographic evaluation. Twenty-eight neonates, despite echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension, did not receive treatment due to a lack of clinical correlation. Demographic characteristics and intensive care outcomes were compared between the two groups. Significant differences were observed between the PHT treatment and non-treatment groups regarding gestational age and birth weight. The non-treatment group exhibited lower GA and BW values, with differences of 1.2 weeks and 350 g, respectively (p = 0.035, p = 0.004).

A significant difference was also noted in uric acid levels, with higher mean values in the PHT treatment group (p = 0.047). No statistically significant differences were found between the groups for other laboratory parameters.

Analysis revealed that neonates in the non-treatment group with lower RVSP1(< 40mmHg) had significantly lower CRP levels (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.021).

Significant differences were observed in the duration of both invasive and non-invasive mechanical ventilation. The PHT treatment group required significantly longer durations of both invasive and non-invasive mechanical ventilation (Table 1).

No significant difference was found between the PHT treatment and non-treatment groups regarding the initial RVSP1 measurement (p = 0.935). However, RVSP2 (measured between days 3 and 7 of admission) and RVSP3 (measured after day 7) values were significantly higher in the treatment group compared to the non-treatment group (p = 0.002, p = 0.010). Despite receiving PHT treatment, repeat echocardiograms revealed persistent elevation of RVSP in these patients (Table 1).

When comparing RVSP for clinical progression, the difference between the first and second measurements (RVSP1-2) and the difference between the second and third measurements (RVSP2-3) were evaluated. The RVSP1-2 difference was statistically significant in the treatment group (p = 0.006). However, no significant difference was observed between the treatment and non-treatment groups regarding RVSP2-3 changes (p = 0.154) (Table 1).

Among the 47 patients receiving PHT treatment, 44 (93.6%) received sildenafil, 13 (27%) received iNO, and 9 (19.1%) received iloprost. Intravenous inotropic support was required in thirty patients (63.8%), with dopamine (34.6%), dobutamine (17.3%), norepinephrine (2.6%), epinephrine (8%), and milrinone (2.6%) being the agents administered. A combination of two or more inotropes was used in 14.6% of cases. Forty-five patients (95.7%) required invasive mechanical ventilation (minimum 1 day, maximum 137 days). During follow-up, 34 patients (72.3%) discontinued PHT treatment, 10 (21%) continued treatment, and 3 (6.4%) died.

No significant differences were observed between the preterm and term groups regarding the duration of invasive or non-invasive mechanical ventilation (p = 0.096 and p = 0.198). Echocardiographic assessment revealed significantly higher RVSP1 values in term neonates compared to preterm neonates on the first two day (p = 0.01). Discussion

In this study, the proportion of neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit who were diagnosed with pulmonary hypertension was 1.07%. Of the 75 neonates, 47 received a PHT diagnosis and treatment based on clinical and echocardiographic findings. The remaining 28 neonates, despite exhibiting echocardiographic evidence of PHT, did not receive treatment due to a clinical presentation inconsistent with PHT. This frequency aligns with literature reporting pulmonary hypertension in 0.43 to 6 per 1000 live births [26]. This range may be attributed to variations in study populations, diagnostic criteria, and diagnostic methods. Some studies rely solely on clinical findings, while others prioritize echocardiographic measurements. Therefore, it is crucial to consider the methods and criteria employed when evaluating the frequency of PHT. Furthermore, as noted by Sahin et al., PHT can have various etiological factors, which can influence its occurrence [26].

In our study, we examined the comparison between treated and untreated groups of patients diagnosed with pulmonary hypertension. We observed that the untreated group had significantly lower gestational age and birth weight. When treated patients were analyzed based on preterm and term status, we did not find a significant difference in treatment outcomes between the two groups. The challenges associated with prematurity and factors related to respiratory system development are considered major underlying causes of PHT in the literature [27–30]. Preterm infants with PHT represent a distinct subgroup. This finding in our study suggests a correlation with the more judicious application of specific PHT treatments in this unique group. The lack of difference in PHT treatment between preterm and term infants highlights the importance of carefully evaluating both echocardiographic findings and clinical status in the management of neonatal pulmonary hypertension. Further research is needed in this area.

A primary objective of this study was to compare PHT treatment and non-treatment groups to identify data informing PHT treatment decisions within the NICU. While no significant difference in CRP levels was observed between the two groups (p = 0.91), a key finding was that neonates with an RVSP1 measurement below 40 mmHg and a negative CRP did not require PHT treatment (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.021). In a study by Quarck et al., C-reactive protein levels were reported to be significantly higher in adult patients with pulmonary hypertension compared to a control group [31]. Furthermore, CRP levels have been correlated with PHT severity, and elevated CRP has been shown to predict adverse outcomes in PHT patients. Specifically, an increase in CRP levels during disease-specific treatment has been linked to increased mortality. These findings support the role of systemic and local inflammation in pulmonary hypertension [31–33]. As our study focused on neonates, we hypothesize a potential mechanism related to fetal development. Although neonates are more susceptible to sepsis and infection, the absence of elevated inflammatory markers should be considered when excluding PHT in patients with unclear clinical and echocardiographic findings. In our cohort, among patients with pulmonary hypertension in the non-treatment group exhibiting RVSP1 > 40 mmHg, elevated CRP levels were associated with the following etiological factors: congenital pneumonia (2 cases), sepsis (3 cases), transient tachypnea of the newborn (2 cases), and asphyxia (1 case). However, a similar number of patients with these etiological factors were observed in the group with lower CRP levels. Given these findings, a definitive etiological evaluation of CRP elevation was not feasible within the scope of this study. Due to the multifaceted nature of CRP and its susceptibility to various influencing factors in neonates, further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted.

Echocardiography serves as a valuable tool for confirming suspected pulmonary hypertension and assessing the need for cardiac catheterization. Pulmonary artery pressure can be estimated using the tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity [23]. While cardiac catheterization remains the gold standard, its feasibility in neonates can be challenging, thus highlighting the importance of non-invasive echocardiographic assessment. Moreover, echocardiography allows for the evaluation of other cardiac anomalies and conditions that may contribute to PHT [34]. In our study, RVSP1 values measured within the first two days did not differ significantly between the PHT treatment and non-treatment groups. However, RVSP2 (days 3–7) and RVSP3 (after day 7) values were significantly higher in the treatment group. We attribute this to the decline in pulmonary vascular resistance in non-PHT cases, allowing for more accurate pulmonary artery pressure measurements via echocardiography. Consequently, in clinically ambiguous cases where the decision to initiate PHT treatment is uncertain, delaying treatment may be considered if a decreasing trend in RVSP is observed after day 3, provided the clinical situation allows for observation.

Upon analyzing the data, we found no significant differences in laboratory results, echocardiographic measurements, or RVSP changes when we grouped PHT-treated patients as preterm and term. Our finding that the RVSP1 measured on the first two day was lower in preterms compared to term neonates suggests that there are no clear clinical and echocardiographic diagnostic thresholds for preterm neonates. A study by Seth et al. utilized echocardiography as a primary tool for diagnosing early-onset pulmonary hypertension, diagnosing it in 34.6% of 78 preterms using echocardiographic criteria [27]. Kim et al. found that the TR peak velocity was accurately measured in a small percentage (28.4%) of 67 preterms [29]. The subtle echocardiographic findings in preterms often make it challenging for clinicians to diagnose PHT. Similar to term newborns, repeated echocardiograms and clinical monitoring are crucial for diagnosing PAH in preterms. Further research is needed on this topic.

Our study found a correlation between PHT diagnosis and prolonged mechanical ventilation requirements. This aligns with the clinical presentation of PHT, which often necessitates long-term mechanical ventilation. Literature confirms a strong association between prematurity, additional etiological factors, and prolonged mechanical ventilation in both preterm and term neonates with PHT. Neonates with PHT experience greater difficulty weaning from mechanical ventilation due to significantly increased pulmonary vascular resistance. The management of neonatal PHT is complex, requiring a multifaceted approach encompassing pharmacotherapy and advanced respiratory support to minimize long-term respiratory sequelae and improve outcomes [21, 34–38].

Regarding intensive care approaches, we observed no significant differences between preterm and terms in the duration of invasive and non-invasive mechanical ventilation, the use of IV inotropic therapy, or the utilization of multiple PHT medications (Table 1; p = 0.096, p = 0.198; p = 0.708; p = 0.772, respectively). This suggests our treatment approach is consistent for both term and preterm. Existing research recommends early non-invasive mechanical ventilation for preterm neonates with pulmonary hypertension to support healthy lung development [33]. Our clinical practice also prioritizes non-invasive mechanical ventilation for preterm neonates diagnosed with PHT. Despite this, both term and preterm neonates receiving medical PHT treatment require similar amounts of inotropic and mechanical ventilation support during their intensive care stay.

Conclusion

Pulmonary hypertension in newborns is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. Our study indicates a strong correlation between persistent elevation of relative pulmonary artery pressure, measured by tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity after the third day, and the need for PHT treatment based on echocardiographic assessment. Neonates with in first two days RVSP below 40 mmHg and negative CRP did not require medical PHT treatment. In these cases, the decision to initiate PHT treatment should be guided by changes in clinical and echocardiographic findings during follow-up. Our findings demonstrate similar laboratory results, echocardiographic RVSP measurements, mechanical ventilation durations, medical PHT and inotropic treatment approaches, and overall intensive care management in both preterm and term neonates receiving PHT treatment.
