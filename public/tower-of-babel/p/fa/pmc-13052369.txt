Initial Experience With Single‐Port Robotic Donor Nephrectomy Using the da Vinci SP System: A Case Series Evaluating Safety, Feasibility, and Early Outcomes
Authors: Srinivasan Muthukrishnan, Lane T. Cavey, Ruchin Patel, Francheska Nieves‐Rivera, Roberto S. Kalil, Raphael P. H. Meier, Chandra S. Bhati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionMinimally invasive techniques for living donor nephrectomy have advanced considerably, with robotic‐assisted approaches offering improved visualization, reduced morbidity, and enhanced cosmesis. Single‐port robotic donor nephrectomy (SP‐RDN), utilizing the da Vinci SP platform, further minimizes the surgical footprint while aiming to maintain safety and efficacy. This case series presents our initial experience with SP‐RDN, focusing on perioperative outcomes and early postoperative renal function.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed six consecutive SP‐RDNs performed between December 2023 and April 2025. Demographic data, operative parameters, and renal function markers were analyzed. All procedures were performed via a periumbilical incision using the da Vinci SP system by two surgeons at our institution. Perioperative outcomes, including operative time, estimated blood loss (EBL), warm ischemia time (WIT), and hospital stay, were assessed. Renal function was monitored using serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) preoperatively, on postoperative day (POD) 0 and POD1, and at first follow‐up.ResultsMean donor age was 42 years (range 24–69), with an average BMI of 22.8 kg/m2. Three nephrectomies each were performed on the right and left sides; three patients had multiple renal arteries. Mean operative time was 211 min, EBL was 65 mL, and mean WIT was 7.6 min. No intraoperative or major postoperative complications occurred. eGFR declined from a preoperative mean of 108.6 to 70.8 mL/min/1.73m2 on POD1, partially recovering by the first postoperative visit.ConclusionSP‐RDN is a feasible and safe approach for living donor nephrectomy, with favorable early outcomes. Larger studies are needed to confirm its advantages over traditional techniques.

Introduction

For patients with end‐stage renal disease (ESRD), kidney transplantation remains the best option for both survival and quality of life [1]. For patients undergoing renal transplantation, a kidney from a living donor generally offers superior graft function and survival compared to kidneys from a deceased donor [2]. Though living donor renal transplantation could theoretically remove most patients on the waiting list for a transplant, the reality remains that most transplanted kidneys are from deceased donors [2] One method employed to improve access to living donation is paired donor exchange: such programs match incompatible donor‐recipient pairs with other pairs to enable compatible kidney transplants through donor swaps [3]. Though such programs exist, the reality remains that over 90 000 individuals are listed for kidney transplantation in the United States [4].

The first laparoscopic donor nephrectomies were performed by Ratner et al. in 1995 [5]. More recently, robotic‐assisted laparoscopic surgical approaches have refined donor nephrectomy and have become more widely utilized amongst transplant surgeons and urologists alike [6, 7]. Of particular interest for potential donors is the single‐port approach to robotic‐assisted surgery as the cosmetic benefits of a single incision may be superior to multiple port sites across the abdomen.

In this case series, we seek to describe the utility of single port robotic donor nephrectomy (SP‐RDN) and to characterize the perioperative and short‐term postoperative outcomes.

Methods

A retrospective chart review was conducted for all SP‐RDNs performed between December 2023 and April 2025. Institutional Review Board exemption was provided by the University of Maryland School of Medicine (HP‐00113745). Six consecutive living kidney donors undergoing SP‐RDN were identified. All procedures were performed using the da Vinci SP robotic surgical system (Intuitive Surgical, Mountain View, CA) via a periumbilical incision.

A 4 cm umbilical incision was made and SP access port with wound protector was introduced and SP robot was docked (Figure 1A,B ). Dissection was done using a combination of Fenestrated bipolar forceps, Maryland bipolar forceps, and Monopolar curved scissors. Stapler was introduced through assistant port of the access port (Figure 1C,D), and finally the kidney was removed through the same incision using an Endocatch bag. The surgical incision was closed in multiple layers with the skin closed in a running subcuticular fashion (Figure 1E).

(A) Access port in periumbilical incision; (B) da Vinci SP Robot docked; (C) Stapler across the renal vein; (D) Stapler across the renal artery; (E) Immediate postsurgical scar.

Patient demographics, surgical laterality, presence of multiple renal arteries, operative time, warm ischemia time (WIT), estimated blood loss (EBL), and hospital length of stay were recorded. Renal function was assessed using serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at baseline, POD 0, POD 1, and the first outpatient follow‐up visit (typically day 7–10). Intraoperative and postoperative complications were documented and classified by Clavien–Dindo criteria.

Means and standard deviations were calculated using Microsoft Excel. Descriptive statistics were used due to the small sample size.

Results

Six patients (four female, two male) underwent SP‐RDN during the study period. The average age was 42 ± 18.1 years (range 24–69), and mean BMI was 22.8 ± 4.7 kg/m2. Three left and three right nephrectomies were performed. Half of the patients had multiple renal arteries. All donated kidneys were successfully transplanted into the intended recipient, and each was noted to have immediate graft function. All donated kidneys required minimal backbench preparation for transplantation.

The results are summarized in Table 1. Mean operative time was 211 min and WIT averaged 7.6 min. EBL was minimal (<50 mL) in five cases; one patient with higher perinephric fat had an EBL of 200 mL. No conversions to multiport or open surgery occurred. Average hospital stay was 1.5 days; all patients were discharged within two days postoperatively. No Clavien–Dindo grade II or higher complications were reported. Pain was effectively managed for all patients with oral analgesics.

Renal function declined postoperatively as expected following unilateral nephrectomy. Mean preoperative eGFR was 108.6 mL/min/1.73m2. On POD 0, eGFR dropped by 12.1, reaching a nadir of 70.8 on POD 1, before improving to 77.7 at follow‐up. Creatinine increased from a preoperative mean of 0.7 mg/dL to 1.15 on POD 1 and stabilized thereafter at the first postoperative follow‐up visit (POD 12‐14). There were no incidences of incisional hernia after one year follow up in any of the patients.

Discussion

This case series supports the feasibility and safety of SP‐RDN as a novel approach for living donor nephrectomy. All six cases were completed successfully without conversions or significant complications. Donor nephrectomies using the SP platform were performed by two different surgeons at our institution. Operative parameters and short‐term renal outcomes were comparable to reported outcomes from conventional multiport robotic and laparoscopic approaches [6].

The average operative time of 211 min is consistent with early learning curves for new surgical platforms [6, 7]. Cases with multiple arteries or higher BMI tended to require longer operative times. These results align with previous reports suggesting that SP techniques become more efficient with experience [7]. SP‐RDN was associated with minimal blood loss and a low complication rate. The short length of stay and lack of readmissions underscore its safety and potential for enhanced recovery. Postoperative eGFR and creatinine trends mirrored expected physiologic changes following nephrectomy. Notably, renal function showed partial recovery by the first outpatient visit, suggesting early stabilization.

Chin et al. [7]. and Palese et al. [8]. have previously demonstrated comparable outcomes between SP‐RDN and multiport RDN. Our findings reaffirm that SP‐RDN provides similar safety and efficacy, with potential advantages in cosmesis and ergonomics. Garden et al. also reported encouraging results with SP‐RDN using the da Vinci SP system, including low complication rates and high patient satisfaction. Living donor nephrectomy involves operating on healthy individuals for altruistic reasons, mandating the safest possible approach. Introducing novel techniques like SP‐RDN in this context requires caution, institutional oversight, and informed consent. Our use of SP‐RDN was preceded by institutional review and performed by experienced robotic surgeons. Prior to the implementation of the SP platform for RDN, donor nephrectomies were primarily performed using multi‐port robotic and laparoscopic approaches, and these approaches are still relied upon for most donor operations. This study is limited by its small sample size, retrospective design, and short follow‐up duration. Because the focus of this study is on short term (<2 week) outcomes, long‐term renal function, and quality of life measures were not assessed. Additionally, cost analysis comparing SP and multiport robotic systems is warranted, though is not within the scope of this study. Prospective studies comparing SP‐RDN with multiport RDN and laparoscopic approaches are needed to further elucidate its benefits and limitations. Evaluation of long‐term outcomes, donor satisfaction, and cost‐effectiveness will be critical in defining its role in donor nephrectomy.

Conclusion

Single‐port robotic donor nephrectomy using the da Vinci SP system is a feasible and safe approach with promising early outcomes. Our initial experience demonstrates that SP‐RDN offers comparable perioperative metrics to conventional techniques [8, 9], with the benefit of requiring a single incision for donors. Further studies with larger cohorts and extended follow‐up are necessary to establish its definitive role in living donor kidney transplantation.
