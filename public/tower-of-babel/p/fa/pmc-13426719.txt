Relative handgrip strength, lifestyles and cancer risk in older adults: a multi-country cohort study
Authors: Yanxue Lian, Pincheng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of muscle strength in cancer prevention and the impact of modifiable factors on this association remain unclear.AimThis study investigated the association between relative handgrip strength and cancer risk while also examining the role of lifestyle factors on this association in adults aged 50+.DesignProspective cohort study using data from 16 countries across three surveys.MethodsRelative handgrip strength was the dominant-hand maximum grip divided by body mass index (BMI) and sex-standardized. Cancer cases were self-reported physician diagnoses. Cox proportional hazards models assessed the association between relative handgrip strength and cancer risk. Restricted cubic splines assessed potential nonlinear associations, with dose–response curves illustrating the relationship stratified by three lifestyle factors.ResultsOf 29,924 participants, 2614 developed cancer over 8.3 years of follow-up. Each 1-unit increase in relative handgrip strength was associated with a 7% lower cancer risk [HR (95% CI): 0.93 (0.89–0.97)]. Cancer risk declined progressively across strength quartiles, with Quartile 4 showing a 16% lower risk [HR (95% CI): 0.84 (0.74–0.95)] compared to Quartile 1, while Quartile 1 had a 20% higher risk [HR (95% CI): 1.20 (1.06–1.35)] than Quartile 4. Drinkers, smokers and physically inactive individuals consistently exhibited higher cancer risk than their healthier counterparts. A sex-specific tool was developed to estimate relative handgrip strength from BMI and absolute grip strength.ConclusionHigher relative handgrip strength was linearly associated with a lower cancer risk, with a stronger protective effect in individuals with healthy lifestyles.

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide.1 However, its burden extends beyond mortality, encompassing physical suffering from disease symptoms, substantial financial hardship due to diagnosis and treatment, and lasting psychosocial consequences for patients, their families and caregivers.1 To mitigate cancer risk, evidence-based guidelines and strategies have been developed, primarily emphasizing lifestyle and behavioral interventions.1 For instance, the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans (2018) recommend that adults engage in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, 75 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination per week.2 However, in terms of physical activity, these recommendations primarily emphasize moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, while muscle-strengthening activities, despite their recognized health benefits, lack sufficient evidence regarding their association with cancer incidence.1

Muscle-strengthening activities are well-established for enhancing overall muscle strength,3 and handgrip strength is commonly used in clinical settings as a marker of muscle strength due to its low cost and non-invasive nature.4 Relative handgrip strength, standardized for sex and body mass index (BMI), is emerging as a new indicator in addition to traditional absolute handgrip strength and has been linked to a range of adverse health outcomes, including muscle weakness and metabolic syndrome.5,6 However, research on relative handgrip strength and cancer has primarily focused on cancer-related mortality,7–9 with only a limited number of studies examining its link to cancer incidence.10–12 Crucially, no cross-regional studies have examined this relationship.

Moreover, identifying modifiable factors that may influence the potential relationship between relative handgrip strength and cancer risk holds significant public health importance. Beyond physical activity, lifestyle behaviors such as smoking and alcohol consumption are also known to affect both muscle strength and cancer risk,13,14 yet whether these factors modify the association between relative muscle strength and cancer incidence remains largely unexplored. To address these gaps, we conducted a multi-cohort study with two primary objectives: (i) to assess the association between relative handgrip strength and overall cancer risk and (ii) to evaluate whether physical activity, smoking and alcohol consumption modify this relationship.

Methods

Study design and participants

Following previous studies,15 we pooled data from 16 countries (Supplementary Table S1) derived from three harmonized cohort studies: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) and the Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe (SHARE).16 These surveys were developed using a shared methodological framework originally modeled on the US HRS. ELSA was explicitly designed to replicate the HRS methodology, while SHARE extended this approach to a pan-European context. Harmonization across the studies occurs both ex ante (through coordinated study design) and ex post (through the standardized public-use datasets).16 This process ensures consistency in variable construction, covering socioeconomic characteristics, physical and mental health indicators, retirement status, healthcare utilization and biomarkers, thereby enabling robust cross-regional analyses.

All three studies include detailed information on handgrip strength and cancer diagnoses among adults aged 50 years and older. To further enhance comparability in handgrip strength assessment and follow-up duration, we included participants who completed handgrip strength tests between 2004 and 2009 (waves 2–4 in ELSA, waves 7–9 in HRS and waves 1–2 in SHARE) and who had at least one follow-up. Follow-up data were used up to the final wave before the COVID-19 pandemic (wave 9 for ELSA, wave 14 for HRS and wave 7 for SHARE) (Supplementary Table S2). After excluding participants younger than 50 years, those diagnosed with cancer at baseline, those without follow-up data, and those missing information on handgrip strength test results, BMI, cancer status, or other covariates, the primary analysis included 3162 participants from ELSA, 127 from HRS and 26 635 from SHARE (Supplementary Figures S1–S3). Ethical approval was obtained from local research ethics committees, and written informed consent was provided by all participants. These surveys were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards set forth in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Relative handgrip strength test and cancer

Handgrip strength was measured using a dynamometer, which was adjusted to each participant’s hand size and reset to zero before testing. Participants received standardized instructions and were properly positioned prior to the assessment. Measurements were obtained two to three times for both the left and right hands, with the dominant hand’s performance recorded. Absolute handgrip strength was defined as the highest measurement of the dominant hand across the tests and subsequently divided by BMI to derive relative handgrip strength. This value was then standardized using sex-specific means and standard deviations from the entire sample.

Cancer was treated as a binary outcome variable, categorized as “yes” or “no”, based on participants’ self-reported physician-diagnosed cancer. For those who reported a cancer diagnosis, the diagnosis time was estimated as the midpoint between the year of the first reported cancer diagnosis and the last year in which they reported no cancer. Follow-up duration was calculated from this estimated diagnosis time to the baseline handgrip strength measurement. For participants without a cancer diagnosis, follow-up duration was defined as the time from their last participation year to the baseline handgrip strength measurement.

Covariates

Demographic factors including age (50–79, ≥80), marital status (married and partnered, unmarried and others), education level (primary school and below, secondary school, college and above), region (Northern America, Northern Europe, Western Europe, Southern Europe, Eastern Europe, West Asia) and household wealth quartiles stratified by region were included. Based on the participants’ responses to the questionnaire, their medical history and relevant medication use were extracted. Chronic conditions in this study are comprised of hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, chronic lung diseases and arthritis.

Furthermore, three lifestyle factors, physical activity, smoking and alcohol consumption, were evaluated through an in-person interview with study personnel using a questionnaire. Physical active was defined as engaging in vigorous or moderate exercise at least once per week. Smoking status was classified into two categories: smokers and non-smokers. Drinking status was categorized as either drinking or non-drinking. All of the above covariates were measured at baseline.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages (%). Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between relative handgrip strength and cancer incidence. Two models were applied: Model I was unadjusted (crude), and Model II was fully adjusted for all covariates, including age, marital status, education level, household wealth, region, smoking, drinking, physical activity and five chronic conditions. To explore potential nonlinear associations, Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) in Cox proportional hazards regression were applied. Model fit was evaluated by comparing linear and nonlinear models using a likelihood ratio test. Moreover, we analyzed the impact of different lifestyle factors on the relationship between relative handgrip strength and overall cancer risk and plotted the relevant dose–response curves. Stratified analyses were conducted by age, education level and marital status. Results were expressed as HRs with 95% CIs. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 18.0, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses

Listwise deletion was used to handle missing data in the main analyses. Four sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, to further ensure the robustness of the results, analyses were performed separately within each cohort (HRS, ELSA and SHARE), and the pooled estimates were then obtained using a fixed-effects meta-analysis. Second, to mitigate potential bias from short follow-up periods, individuals in the lowest 10% of follow-up duration were excluded, and the analysis was repeated. Third, to account for potential bias from extreme handgrip strength values, the analysis was re-run after excluding individuals with handgrip strength in the lowest 5% and highest 95%. Fourth, to examine the impact of missing data, multiple imputations using chained equations were performed, followed by repeated analyses.

Result

The studied population had an average age of 63.13 (9.29) years, with a higher proportion of females (52.96%) than males (47.04%). Among the 29 924 individuals, 2614 (8.74%) reported being diagnosed with cancer after an average follow-up of 8.29 years (SD: 3.59). While their absolute handgrip strength was similar, relative handgrip strength was lower in those who reported cancer than in those without cancer. Individuals with cancer were more likely to be male, older, alcohol consumers and to have multiple chronic conditions (Table 1).

Characteristics of participants by cancer status

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies (%).

As relative handgrip strength increased across quartiles, the number of individuals with cancer gradually decreased, while the number of those without cancer increased. Compared to participants in the lowest quartile of relative handgrip strength, those in the highest quartile were younger, more likely to have a higher education level, be married, have a lower BMI, greater household wealth, fewer chronic conditions, and engage in more physical activity (Supplementary Table S3).

A greater relative handgrip strength was associated with a lower overall cancer risk, as demonstrated in the stepwise adjustment models (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model (Model II), a 1-unit increase in relative handgrip strength was linked to a 7% reduction in cancer risk [HR (95% CI): 0.93 (0.89–0.97)]. Compared to individuals in the lowest quartile (Quartile 1), cancer risk decreased progressively across higher quartiles, with those in the highest quartile (Quartile 4) exhibiting a 16% lower risk [HR (95% CI): 0.84 (0.74–0.95)]. Conversely, lower relative handgrip strength was associated with a higher overall cancer risk. Using Quartile 4 as the reference group, cancer risk increased as handgrip strength declined across quartiles, with individuals in the lowest quartile (Quartile 1) facing a 20% higher risk [HR (95% CI): 1.20 (1.06–1.35)].

When analyses were conducted separately within each cohort (HRS, ELSA and SHARE) and combined using a random-effects meta-analysis, the pooled estimate [HR (95% CI): 0.92 (0.88–0.95)] remained stable and consistent with the main findings (Supplementary Figure S4). The other three sensitivity analyses (excluding individuals with a follow-up duration of <2.5 years, excluding participants with absolute handgrip strength > 55 or <16 kg, and using multiple imputations to account for missing data) also showed results consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Table S4–S6). Furthermore, there was a linear relationship between relative handgrip strength and cancer incidence (Figure 1), which was supported by the likelihood ratio test (Prob > χ2 = 0.89).

The significant inverse association between relative handgrip strength and cancer incidence persisted across lifestyle stratifications (Supplementary Table S7), with a more pronounced protective effect observed in individuals with healthier lifestyles (Figure 2). Dose–response curves for drinkers, smokers and physically inactive individuals were consistently above those of their healthier counterparts, indicating a higher overall cancer risk at any given relative handgrip strength level. Additionally, individuals in the highest quartile of relative handgrip strength (Quartile 4) consistently exhibited a lower cancer risk compared with those in the lowest quartile (Quartile 1), although this association was not statistically significant among participants aged 80 years and older, those living alone, or those with a primary school education or below (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

This study is the first cross-regional, prospective cohort analysis to examine the association between relative grip strength and overall cancer risk in individuals aged 50 years and older. Our findings indicated that higher relative grip strength was associated with a lower overall cancer risk, whereas lower relative grip strength was linked to an increased risk. These associations remained robust across sensitivity analyses and exhibited a linear relationship, being less affected by key lifestyle factors and demographic characteristics. However, unhealthy behaviors may attenuate the protective effects of muscular strength against cancer. Moreover, individuals with a higher BMI but lower maximum grip strength in the dominant hand may be a priority group for cancer prevention. These findings highlight the potential importance of increasing muscle strength and promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors to reduce cancer risk.

Consistent with previous single-center studies, relative handgrip strength appears to be a useful predictor of cancer incidence. A study using data from the UK Biobank reported that a 1-unit increase in relative handgrip strength (adjusted for BMI) was associated with an approximately 35% lower overall cancer risk.10 As a widely accepted proxy for overall muscle strength,17 handgrip strength is particularly relevant in aging populations. A large-scale systematic literature review demonstrated that both absolute and body size-normalized handgrip strength peak between ages 30 and 39, followed by a steady decline,18 with the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People recommending handgrip strength measurement as a primary diagnostic step for sarcopenia.19 Beyond its role in muscle health assessment, handgrip strength has been linked to the prevention and management of chronic conditions including cardiovascular disease,20 diabetes,21 stroke,22 chronic kidney disease,23 and fragility fractures.24 Lower handgrip strength has also been linked to increased hospitalization rates,25 reduced quality of life,26 and higher all-cause, cardiovascular-related and cancer-related mortality.27,28

This study also suggested that the association between higher relative handgrip strength and cancer risk may be further influenced by unhealthy behaviors, potentially reducing the benefit of muscle strength in cancer risk reduction. Both smoking and alcohol consumption can cause DNA damage, disrupt cell repair mechanisms, and promote abnormal cell growth and division.29,30 These behaviors have been linked to an increased overall cancer risk and the incidence of specific cancers, such as pancreatic and liver cancer.30,31 Notably, evidence indicates that even light smoking or drinking is significantly associated with cancer risk, with no safe threshold established.30,31

Regular physical activity may help reduce cancer risk by enhancing immune function, improving metabolic processes,32 regulating hormones involved in cancer development33 and promoting digestion to reduce exposure to potential carcinogens.34 To improve muscle strength, current guidelines for overall health recommend performing resistance exercises two to three non-consecutive days per week.35 For beginners, one set of 8–12 repetitions targeting all major muscle groups is advised, with a progressive increase in intensity over time.35 However, no specific recommendations exist for muscle strengthening in cancer prevention. Further research is needed to determine optimal resistance exercise dosing strategies for cancer risk reduction.

This study has several strengths. First, it utilized data from three national longitudinal cohorts spanning 16 countries, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Second, the results remained robust across sensitivity analyses, reinforcing their reliability. Third, by examining the effects of modifiable lifestyle factors on the association between handgrip strength and cancer risk, this study provided valuable translational insights. Fourth, this study provided valuable insights by presenting the distribution of relative handgrip strength categories across BMI and maximum dominant handgrip strength, offering a meaningful basis for health promotion. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. As an observational study, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, and causality cannot be established despite the use of complicated longitudinal analysis techniques. Additionally, cancer diagnosis and key covariates, such as smoking, alcohol consumption and chronic diseases, were self-reported, which may introduce recall or reporting bias. Lastly, while the inclusion of three national cohorts strengthens the study, data constraints and the relatively low number of site-specific cancer cases prevented a detailed analysis of the association between handgrip strength and specific cancer types.

Conclusion

This study provided evidence of a linear relationship between relative handgrip strength and overall cancer risk in adults aged 50 years and older, where higher relative handgrip strength was linked to lower risk and lower relative handgrip strength to higher risk. Maintaining a healthy lifestyle may enhance the protective effect of handgrip strength against cancer. Clinically, those with a greater BMI but a lower handgrip strength should be considered the priority groups for cancer prevention.
