Exploring Natural Sea Egg Capsules Decorated with Polyphenol-Inspired Polymers as a Remediating Solution toward Pharmaceutical Pollutants
Authors: Francesco De Mastro, Marilena Donnaloia, Gaurav Manik, Davide Blasi, Matteo Spagnuolo, Cesar Vicente-Garcia, Danilo Vona, Gennaro Brunetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biohybrid composites have emerged as highly effective and biocompatible materials of growing interest for applications in environmental remediation. In this work, the potential of porous collagen-/keratin-based capsules, which are naturally released by marine murex snails after their eggs hatch, was explored as an organic matrix for pollutant adsorption. In contrast to previous approaches, an all-in-water linker-mediated bis-condensation was performed directly on the egg capsules in order to synthesize a polyfunctional dendrimer version of polyphenol moieties, starting from gallic acid. The efficacy of this novel material to adsorb various organic pharmaceutical pollutants was evaluated by means of micro-Raman spectroscopy, micro-Fourier transform infrared (μFTIR) mapping, and ultraviolet (UV)–visible/fluorescence microscopy.

Introduction

Medical care and therapeutics are recognized to represent a major pathway for the introduction and dispersal of pharmaceutical active compounds (PhACs) in the environment. These compounds and their metabolites are categorized as emerging pollutants of increasing concern due to their detrimental environmental impacts, as they have the potential to persist in the environment over long time scales, causing negative effects on aquatic ecosystems, contributing to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms and affecting agricultural systems when irrigated with treated wastewater. The presence of PhACs in wastewater was first identified in the 1960s in the United States and Europe. Consequently, in 2007, the European Union (EU) expanded its list of priority pollutants to include several PhACs (such as diclofenac, carbamazepine, triclosan, and ibuprofen), mandating their removal within the following two decades to protect the ecological integrity of aquatic environments.

The development of effective and efficient treatment methods for PhAC removal from wastewater is thus crucial. Although several methods have been developed for this purpose, none have yielded satisfactory results, particularly in terms of sustainability. In particular, many conventional approaches require significant chemical inputs or high energy consumption, leading to the generation of waste and byproducts that need further disposal. Currently, the most effective removal in wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) is achieved by employing biological processes.

In recent years, the exploration of natural porous materials has emerged as a cost-effective and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional techniques for the removal of xenobiotics. Adsorption is considered an excellent process for treating wastewater containing low concentrations of contaminants, as it effectively removes various classes of pollutants, including organic compounds such as pesticides and PhACs, heavy metals, and excess nutrients such as phosphorus and nitrogen. Moreover, it is a versatile technology, as many adsorbents can be regenerated and reused, thereby reducing operational costs. A wide range of adsorbent materials have been investigated as alternatives to conventional options to remove PhACs, including natural materials, such as microalgae, , bacteria, upper terrestrial plants, or animal-derived extracts, bulk materials, oxidized cellulose, biofilms, activated sludge, aluminum, manganese and iron oxide, − organic materials (compost, humic acid, manure), iron nanoparticles, and activated charcoal. In particular, Pirozzi et al. employed magnetic chitosan for the removal of sulfamethoxazole from tertiary wastewater, reporting strong chemical interactions and demonstrating the potential for material reuse.

In this context, this study was focused on the potential adsorptive capacity for PhACs by the egg capsules deposited by sea snails during reproduction and the sustainability of the process. Several species of sea snails produce egg capsules of variable shapes and sizes to protect embryos until hatching. These capsules are secreted by specific glands and are subsequently hardened and molded at the base of the female’s foot through sclerotization processes. , The proteinaceous material of the egg capsules readily hydrates in water, maintaining adequate moisture in saline or dry conditions, thereby protecting the embryos from external agents, including desiccation, salt variations, and light exposure. The capsule wall is primary composed of keratin proteins (78%) with smaller amounts of biomineralized carbohydrates and lipids which confer high resistance to physical stress and chemical treatments.

On the basis of the above considerations, the aim of this study was to test the use of this readily available marine-derived matrix as a PhAC adsorbent after a rapid and environmentally friendly oxidative passivation process. This approach may represent a viable solution to address existing gaps in pharmaceutical wastewater remediation systems while avoiding chemically and thermally intensive pathways typically required for the production of conventional biobased adsorbents.

Materials and Methods

Bioorganic Functionalization and Characterization of Snail Egg Capsules

The reagents N-sulfohydroxysuccinimide (NHS, ≥98% purity), 1-ethyl-3-(3-(dimethylamino)­propyl)­carbodiimide (EDC, 97% purity), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, ≥99% purity), succinic acid (≥99% purity), gallic acid (≥98% purity), ethanol (≥99.5% purity), bidistilled water, hydrogen peroxide (30%, Suprapur), and hydrochloric acid (36% Suprapur) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany).

Raman spectra were collected using an Explora Plus Raman microscope (HORIBA Scientific, Lille, France) mounted with a charge-coupled device (CCD) (Sincerity), with a 532 nm excitation laser under ambient conditions. A low laser power (1% power intensity) was used to avoid heat-induced modification or degradation of the sample due to the focused laser light during spectral acquisition. The collection acquisition was set at 8–16 required accumulation steps and 2 s/measurement, and the excitation laser beam was focused through a 50X optical microscope. The spectral resolution was 1 cm–1.

A UV–vis Shimadzu 2600 PC spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to record the delivery of gallic acid from hydrolyzed GA-decorated samples.

The micro-FTIR measurements were recorded using a LUMOS I FTIR spectrophotometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA) interfaced with a white field microscope that was equipped with a video camera, a liquid-nitrogen-cooled mercury cadmium telluride (MCT/A) detector, and a computer-controlled mapping stage (OPUS software 2014). Individual spectra acquired in the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode were used for peak separation by measuring fields from representative spots with a high number of scans in order to increase the signal-to-noise ratio. Point-by-point spectral mapping of the films was carried out using a grid pattern, reading window, 4000–650 cm–1 at a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1.

Total organic carbon (TOC%) and nitrogen (N) concentrations were measured on 10–16 mg of powdered samples using a CN elemental analyzer Soli TOC Cube (Langenselbold, Germany).

An Axiomat, Zeiss microscope (Oberkochen, Germany) (all-based-cubes passband exploited) was used to record the fluorescence and surface topography of samples before and after chemical functionalization experiments.

Washing and Demineralization of Snail Egg Capsules

The sea snail egg capsules ( ess ) were collected along the Adriatic sea coasts around the city of Bari (Italy) and cut into pieces of 0.3 g mass. The washing steps for this material consisted of 3 × 50 mL of bidistilled water (bdH2O, 15 min, stirring) in order to remove the seawater salts. After the rinsing of the raw materials for 15 min in bdH2O, during which cuts easily restored the hydrated state as floating and submersed sponge-like composites, an overnight washing step was performed in 100 mL of bdH2O with 10 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) 30%V/V (decolouring/oxidizing external organic matrix) and 25 mL of hydrochloric acid (HCl) 0.1 M (for the removal of biomineralized calcium carbonate clays, CaCO3). After this treatment, the cuts were washed 3 times with bdH2O and dried in an oven (4 h, 55 °C).

All-in-Water Functionalization of Sea Egg Capsules with Gallic Acid: Synthetic Method

After the washing steps, the snail egg capsules, sized in cuts, were functionalized with gallic acid following the reported strategy. An amount of 0.39 mmol of succinic acid was dissolved and stirred in icy ethanol (5 mL) together with EDC (0.4 mmol, 1-ethyl-3-(3-(dimethylamino)­propyl)­carbodiimide), DMAP (0.04 mmol, 4-dimethylaminopyridine), and sulfo-NHS (0.41 mmol), and the reaction mix was kept for 30′ stirring. Moreover, this solution was added dropwise at room temperature in a phosphate buffer solution (PBS, pH 7.4, phosphate buffer 50 mM) together with 0.1 g of ess , and the mixture was stirred for 2 h. Finally, gallic acid (GA, 0.41 mmol) was added to this reaction vessel and the bioconjugation occurred for 2 h, with stirring. After several washing steps in ethanol/water and bdH2O, the final oxidative conversion of GA- ess in pGA- ess , bearing an organic polymerized surface, was obtained at r.t., for 4 h, with 0.5 mmol of free GA in solution and NaHCO3 0.1 M.

Point of Zero Charge (PZC) Measurement

The point of zero charge (PZC), namely, the pH above which the total surface of a sorbent is negatively charged, was measured by the pH drift method. In particular, 20 mL of a 0.01 M sodium chloride (NaCl) solution was placed in a Falcon vial, and N2 was bubbled through the solution to steady the pH by preventing the dissolution of CO2 in the solution. The pH was adjusted to selected initial values between 2 and 12 by dropping HCl 0.01 N or sodium hydroxide (NaOH) 0.01 N, and then 0.06 g of the sorbent was added to the solution. The final pH was reached after 4 h, measured with a pH meter (Hanna Instruments HI2002–02, Padova, Italy), and plotted against the initial pH. The pH at which the curve crosses the bisector pH­(final) = pH­(initial) yields the PZC of the given sorbent.

Biosorption Test of PhACs

In order to evaluate the behavior of the different ess matrices (bare and functionalized) as a function of pH, a set of biosorption experiments were conducted in batch mode in 5 mL Falcon vials under a constant stirring speed of 250 rpm. The experiments were carried out by varying the initial pH (5 vs 7.5 vs 9), using a constant quantity of 0.010 g of the biosorbent, an initial concentration of 100 ppb of PhACs, and a contact time of 24 h at 25 ± 1 °C. For pH adjustment, liquid sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 40 g/mol, ≥97%) and liquid hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.46 g/mol, 36.5–38.0% ACS) from Sigma-Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany) were used. The following ten analytical standard (purity >99%) PhACs, i.e., carbamazepine (CBZ), clarithromycin (CLR), climbazole (CLZ), fluconazole (FCZ), gemfibrozil (GFZ), ketoprofen (KET), metoprolol (MTP), naproxen (NPX), sulfamethoxazole (SMX), and trimethoprim (TMP), all provided by Lab Instruments (Castellana Grotte, Italy), were used. These PhAC samples were selected because they are commonly present in treated wastewater, as they are among the most commonly prescribed by doctors, and they feature a wide range of hydrophobicity (Ko w) and acidity (pK a) values (Table ). Wastewater was avoided here due to the intrinsic organic matter that could significantly enhance the adsorption of ad hoc spiking contaminants.

Nitrogen was used as the sheath and auxiliary gas. Data were acquired and processed by the Thermo Xcalibur 4.0.27.10, Chromeleon, and Trace Finder 3.3 methods. For each PhAC, 5 ppm of mass tolerance was used for the extracted ion chromatogram.

Adsorption Isotherms, Kinetics, and Recyclability Performed on the MTP Model

The adsorption of MTP, i.e., the PhAC that was most effectively removed, by pGA- ess was measured using a batch equilibrium method at RT (20 ± 2 °C). Briefly, aliquots of 10 mg of pGA- ess were weighed into 10 mL centrifuge tubes, and each was added with 5 mL of water solutions at pH 9 of MTP at concentrations of 0, 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 150, and 200 μg L–1. The mixtures were continuously mixed using an overhead shaker (Rotax 6.8, VELP Scientifica, Usmate, MB, Italy), and then the solid material was separated from the solution through a Whatman 0.45 μm glass fiber microfilter (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA). Finally, the residual MTP concentrations in the supernatants were determined by using the same method described in Section . To evaluate the adsorption mechanism of MTP on the surface of pGA- ess , the experimental data were fitted for linear, Langmuir, and Freundlich isotherm models (equations shown in Table S1).

Experimental assays were conducted to investigate the influence of the adsorbent mass and contact time on the adsorption performance. In the first set of experiments, 5 mL of an aqueous solution containing 100 μg L–1 MTP at pH 9 was brought in contact for 24 h with varying adsorbent masses (1, 2, 10, 30, and 50 mg). In the second set, 10 mg of pGA- ess was faced with the same solution volume and concentration as used previously, leaving all other experimental parameters at their optimized values. Equilibrium concentrations were monitored at 10 min intervals. Prior to quantitative determination, the samples were filtered through Whatman 0.45 μm glass fiber microfilters (Fisher Scientific). The kinetic data obtained were fitted to different kinetic models, including the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models, as reported in Table S1.

The adsorbent recyclability was assessed over consecutive adsorption cycles. After each 24 h cycle, the adsorbent was removed from the tube and transferred to a fresh tube containing 5 mL of a fresh aqueous solution of MTP (100 μg L–1) at pH 9. This process was repeated for five cycles, with the residual MTP concentration in the solution measured after each cycle (Figure ).

Statistical Validation

The results were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Bartlett’s test, both performed with R software (version 3.2.3). As the data met the assumptions of normal distribution and homoscedasticity, they were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA). Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test. The statistical significance was determined at p ≤0.05.

Results

Growth of Gallic Acid-Based Polymers on ess Materials

Inspiration for the use and optimization of new materials, envisaging supported chemistry and remediation, comes again from the sea snails Hexaplex trunculus (the banded dye-murex) and Bolinus brandaris. As is known, these snails release a proteinaceous, biomineralized porous material after eggs ( ess ) etching, which inevitably, and seasonably, becomes part of the coastal seabed. From a technological point of view, these egg capsules have been already proposed in literature for its strong capacity to adsorb the tetracycline pollutant from water samples, especially in combination with a specific surface decoration with the polyphenol gallic acid (GA). Therefore, here, GA surface chemistry has been once more chosen due to its natural abundance, ease of performing, and aims to increase the surface activity of ess . To do this, the bioorganic functionalization proposed in the previous paper has been hugely optimized here, consisting now in an all-in-water synthetic procedure (Figure A). In fact, the first activation of the short bicarboxylic linker succinic acid was performed in ethanol, with water-soluble sulfo-N-hydroxysuccinimide (sNHS). Thanks to the shortness of this organic moiety (with respect to the lone biacid suberate), the low hydrophobicity, and the presence of a sulfo-NHS, the following surface chemical decoration of the proteinic bulk of ess with gallic acid was finally possible all-in-water.

The addressed amino groups of protruding lysines could then act as chemical platforms for addressing gallic acid monomers and induce an oxidative polymerization of the triphenolic bulk using base/slightly oxidative conditions. The final product consisted of a sugar-free tannin-like material directly anchored and aged over the ess surface. Through a set of micrograph reports, shading from white to pale orange can be visible over the ess surfaces, together with a different translucency (Figure B,C).

An indirect method to obtain the quantity of the GA moiety on the egg matrix surface involved an incubation of GA-functionalized ess with a 0.5 N HCl aqueous solution at 40 °C, followed by analysis of the extraction eluate, using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Figure ). The acidic conditions induced ester hydrolysis, releasing the GA moiety, which was detectable until a stabilized plateau signal at 282 nm within 45 min, corresponding to a 35 ± 4% of surface-bound GA over the initial supplied polyphenol quantity. This value was calculated using an interpolation curve of a calibration curve.

Delivery of the gallic acid moiety from the egg matrix under stirring in an aqueous solution with HCl 0.5 N (UV–visible spectra recorded for the phenyl moiety, from bottom to top: 10, 30, 45, and 60 min).

The stability of the system with and without polyphenols was also verified by using thermogravimetric profilometry. Consistent with a literature study that thermally profiles keratin fibers, the material (both with and without a polyphenol coating) was stable, with a water loss from 30 to 100 °C, representing approximately 10% of the mass of the whole samples. The mass loss setting the TGA profile until 35–45% (at approximately 350 °C) is related to the thermoassisted oxidative decarboxylation of the protein bulk. This kinetics is minimally influenced by the added surface polyphenol. The further mass loss recorded between 380 and 460 °C (5–8%) is certainly attributable to the removal of the surface polyaromatic matrix, added during chemical functionalization (Figure S1).

Characterization of Functionalized Snail Egg Capsules

Raman spectroscopy is a technique used to analyze the vibrational modes of molecules, which are unambiguously related to chemical bonds and molecular symmetry. As a result, Raman spectroscopy creates a molecular fingerprint that helps to identify chemical species, examining bonding and intramolecular structures, and this technique is widely used in chemistry for structural identification of organic functional groups, especially in hybrid layered materials.

To confirm the surface chemistry characterization, Raman spectra (Figure ) were registered on sea snail eggs before, after functionalization with gallic acid (GA) and after oxidative dendrimerization of GA via ether O–C sp2 bonding. Spectra have been recorded and are presented in the range 800–1800 cm–1. Bands dominate the Raman spectrum of the control sample, merely bare unfunctionalized ess , ascribable to CH, CN, NH, CO, OH, and C–O bonds that always overcome the weaker CC bands. After functionalization with GA and especially due to the insertion of the dimer, trimer, and poly-GA coming from the oxidative transformation, the region from 1150 to 1265 cm–1 increases and becomes noisy, thanks to the vibrational bands of CC bonds which exhibit overtones and intensification. An increase in the additional bands at ∼1300 and 1665 cm–1 occurs, respectively, due to COH bending, OH deformation, and CC/CO stretching deformation from the polyphenol moiety. , Raman spectra recorded for the lone GA crystals have been reported in Figure S2.

Raman spectra comparison of (A) bare, (B) GA-functionalized, and (C) oxidized pGA- ess .

To confirm the functionalization with GA and oxidized pGA, total organic carbon % (TOC) values given by elemental analyses have been reported and compared in Table S2, underlining an increase passing from bare ess to molecular GA functionalization to obtain an oxidative surface polymerization.

The materials’ topographical emissions of bare samples and GA- and oxidatively polymerized GA- ess have been assessed via 2D reflectance fluorescence microscopy. An evidence of autophotoluminescence coming from the few collagen fibers dispersed in the keratin bulk of the bare material can be already proved (Figure ). This luminescent trait generally correlates with specific aromatic amino acids within protein pockets (including bare or oxidized tryptophan or tyrosine moieties). Through a complete analysis of the 3 main emission windows (fluorescein isothiocyanate, FITC filter; tetramethylrhodamine isothiocyanate, TRITC filter; 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, DAPI cube filter), the fluorescence emission color changed entirely, from the predecoration to the two postchemical functionalization states, likely due to shifts in the luminescence behavior of the amino acid composites in the solid form, affected by the polyphenolic-rich chemical environment. Furthermore, exploring the material under DAPI excitation/emission cubes, a greenish shift comes from the decoration, while the general response to FITC and TRITC filters is intensified with respect to the bare material after molecular GA functionalization. Conversely, the photoluminescence of aged GA- ess dramatically decreased, likely due to an increase in C–O linking dendrimers, causing both a conversion of dimers and trimers into an adsorbing, low-emitting polymer matrix and an aggregation behavior of the polymeric GA moieties, especially thanks to the π–π orthogonal interactions.

The microporous structures constituting the bulk of the egg capsules are part of a highly anisotropic material. This can cause issues with the homogeneous distribution of molecular moieties during and after surface chemical functionalization. For this reason, GA- ess subjected to oxidative polymerization at a weakly alkaline environment was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared microscopy (μFTIR), in mapping mode, ad hoc made to assess large image areas coming from the samples. As shown in Figure (also reporting the extended recomposed area), the map (in ATR mode, attenuated total reflectance) was built using the infrared spectrum at each sampling point, integrating the main bands belonging to the functionalized material (symmetric/asymmetric −C–H stretching band, 2990–2825 cm–1, aromatic overtone plane, 2150–1825 cm–1, amide plane with −CO stretching, and −NH bending, 1825–1525 cm–1), and related integration. The distribution of the bands was detected describing the homogeneity in an area of 8 × 4.5 mm (repeating intersampling for 16 fields). The analyses underlined a calculated homogeneity index of 89 ± 7% (see Figure caption).

Biosorption of PhACs

The proposed protein material, on which a polymer was grown by addressing GA as a small molecule, thus inducing oxidative high-density linking, was tested on various organic molecules of pharmaceutical interest. The overall interaction assessment, performed here as a preliminary discrimination test and expressed as a percentage of adsorption, was conducted first without considering pH fluctuations.

These ten PhACs were dissolved in probe solutions at a final concentration of 100 μg L–1. As shown, no differences in terms of the chemical interaction of CBZ, CLR, CLZ, FCZ, and TMP with pGA- ess were detected, when compared to the control (Table ; for the acronym of chemical structures, see Table ). Despite this, the remaining molecules exhibited significant adsorption behaviors once approaching the proposed bioinspired composite. Among GFZ, SMX, and MTP, the last one resulted in a particularly interesting case to study, as highlighted in a detailed discussion, since this pollutant was most effectively removed by the polyphenol-decorated material compared to the bare ess material (Δremoval %: 15.5).

Since the functionalization of snail egg capsules can enhance PhAC adsorption in some cases, further experiments were carried out to evaluate the material’s performance under different pH conditions. Considering that the pH of natural aquatic environments generally ranges between 5 and 9, tests were conducted at three pH values (5, 7.5, and 9) to investigate the influence of pH on the adsorption process (Figure ). The experiments revealed a dual behavior. Most PhACs were more efficiently adsorbed under alkaline conditions, while only NPX, KET, and GFZ showed higher adsorption at acidic pH, with statistically more significant differences across the three pH levels compared with those of the other group of molecules. These three molecules are specific as bearing carboxyl groups which remain undissociated at low pH, thus leading to the possibility of performing dimer:dimer bimolecular interactions with polyphenol-decorated ess . In the first case, the most adsorbed compound was CLZ (approximately 80%), followed by SMX (60%), whereas at pH 5, the adsorption range was between 35 and 45% for all PhACs.

Prior to evaluation of the potential reuse of the functionalized material, its stability was confirmed to ensure that the polymer maintained its integrity and performance. A simple degradation test has been performed (in triplicate) by embedding 10 mg of our materials coated with polyphenols in different assayed solutions. Embedding occurred in bidistilled water and 10, 20, and 30 psu salty model waters, and UV–visible radiation has been exploited for testing the delivery of gallic acid after 24 h. Each sample gave an outcome, in terms of delivery of the GA monomer, with respect to initial GA used, set below 1.1% m/m, revealing a good stability of the investigated and proposed polymer.

Regarding desorption, a reduction greater than 60% occurred (Figure A) already after 1 h of contact between the PhAC-saturated capsules and fresh water. This percentage hugely decreased for all PhACs after approximately 8 h (four wash cycles). Focusing on the first hour of contact, three out of the ten PhACs exhibited desorption rates close to 90%, whereas the others ranged between 70 and 80% (Figure B).

Adsorption Isotherms, Kinetic Models, and Recyclability Tests

Figure S3 shows the adsorption isotherm obtained by contacting the functionalized material with MTP solutions at increasing concentrations. The maximum experimental adsorption capacity (q max) was 10.39 mg g–1. The adsorption process occurred rapidly at lower concentrations and then gradually slowed along the experiment, likely due to the saturation of the active sites on the adsorbent surface.

To gain deeper insight into the interaction mechanism between MTP and the adsorbent material, the experimental isotherm data were fitted to different adsorption models, including the linear, Langmuir, and Freundlich models (Table S1).

For optimizing the adsorption removal process, a correlation coefficient close to 1.0 was essential to select a model that accurately describes the adsorption mechanism. The experimental isotherm data were best fitted by the Langmuir model based on the highest correlation coefficients (R 2) obtained (Table S3). The Linear and Freundlich models showed lower R 2 values compared to those of Langmuir.

Kinetic studies performed to evaluate the influence of contact time on the adsorptive removal of MTP revealed that the adsorption rate was initially rapid during the first 20 min and subsequently reached equilibrium after 30 min (Figure S4).

Analysis of the kinetic parameters reported in Table S4, specifically for MTP, indicated that the pseudo-second-order model best describes the experimental data, suggesting that the adsorption process is primarily controlled by chemisorption. Although the pseudo-first-order model showed a slightly higher R 2 value, it failed to accurately describe the entire adsorption process, particularly near equilibrium. In contrast, the pseudo-second-order model provided a more realistic representation of the adsorption mechanism, yielding equilibrium capacities consistent with experimental data. Nonetheless, a secondary contribution from physical adsorption cannot be excluded depending on the availability of active sites and the pharmaceutical concentration.

For MTP, further investigations, such as the residual MTP concentration (μg L–1) vs different masses of the biosorbent pGA- ess , and the recycle test, were performed and are reported in the Supporting Information (Figures S5, S6). Over 30 mg of the biosorbent, the maximal performance in terms of adsorption has been reached. The maximum biosorbent performance was achieved above 30 mg during the first three cycles.

Discussion

Natural sea sources provide us precious porous, biocompatible, and readily available materials that can be potentially exploited for a variety of applications, ranging from environmental remediation to biomedicine and precision medicine, until the possibility of producing devices that can be integrated to generate alternative energy. Many of these natural materials are suitable starting points for their applications. However, they often require chemical functionalization to enhance their activity. For example, it is possible to increase their surface activity toward a pollutant or to simply upsurge the density of functional groups on their usable surface. Among all of the small organic molecules and polymer coatings, useful bioorganic approaches can involve natural inspired antioxidants including polyphenols, which generally regard molecular models of acid phenols or flavonoid/nonflavonoid chemicals. Polyphenol coatings of different materials, including silica nanoparticles, liposomes, and ferromagnetic nanomaterials, have been reported in literature as systems useful for modulated drug delivery and to enhance diffuse antimicrobial activities. In the remediation field, some artificial examples have been investigated, including graphene and few biopolymers decorated with the triphenolic gallic acid moiety.

The ease of chemistry in simple phenolic acids becomes an envisaging story here. They exhibit organic acid properties, peculiar redox behavior to form reversible radicals/ketone species, and a certain chemical reactivity to easily generate complex esters as in tannic acid, in the simple state or in the sugar-conjugated version. In this paper, the ess protein-based naturally porous material has been addressed using an all-in-water reaction, exploiting cheap, biocompatible succinic acid as an organic linker and gallic acid as a polyphenol. Gallic acid is naturally sourced in plants and fungi, acting as an antioxidant both in organisms and tissues and with an industrial application in foods. GA could either be formed starting from phenylalanine or from an early shikimate intermediate, and this dual pathway strongly depends on the age of leaves. Despite the easy bioorganic approach exploited in this work, the most promising procedure consisted in the last oxidative polymerization (bimolecular oxygen-based), at weak alkaline conditions (sodium hydrogen carbonate), leading to incredible remediation properties toward different chemicals. The O–C sp2‑linked dendrimerization of triphenol moieties is the key factor mechanism which induces an easy but dramatic modification of the surface chemistry of ess . A general consideration stands on the molecular rearrangement cascade, induced by activators like oxygen, pH unbalance, and the presence of trace metals. These steps allow small phenol-based molecules to become medium- and high-molecular-weight polyphenols. Here, the ease of finding substrates (such as gallic acid), the short reaction times, the use of cheap and ubiquitous catalysts, and the generation of colors become intuitive advantages that feature the polyphenol synthesis reaction directly for an in situ property enhancement.

In this work, we simulated the in vitro generation of simple tannin moieties, composed exclusively of gallic acid (GA, 3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid) units, activated and oligo-polymerized via an induced radical oxidative pathway, directly on ess matrices after covalent bonding of molecular GA. Mechanistically, GA on the ess surface is first dissociated in a weakly alkaline environment and then activated under huge stirring in the presence of oxygen, found in the environment. Final decorated ess , colored in different shades and grades (from orange to dark green), was recovered and washed several times with ethanol/water solutions and finally with water. From a chemical point of view, GA is a weak poly­(protic acid) with four acidic releasable protons that can be transferred to an acceptor base (Figure ). So, GA easily converts into the gallate anion, and then a mixture of oxidized products, including GA O-radicals and intramolecular GA ketones, appears due to the spontaneous O2-based autoxidation at alkaline conditions. The cascade of radical oligomerization of GA starts with an anionic/polyradical functional group in the same molecule, produced by single-electron transfer (SET). Then, an increase of side O-linked radical species occurs during the reaction, thanks to a secondary process, known as hydrogen atom transfer (HAT). These gallate radicals can lead to further reactions, essentially comprising a serial, multistep oxidative polymerization in an alkaline environment contemplating an unusually high number of transferred electrons together with hydroxyl radical groups. This radical-induced polymerization pathway especially contains C–O repetitive dimers and a few C–C repetitive dimers (oxidative direct arylation).

Understanding the mechanistic basis of intramolecular GA rearrangements leads to a clearer and more verifiable understanding of how GA can interact with different analytes, including molecules to be removed from contaminated liquid samples. The most interesting aspect of this mechanical process is the occurrence of macroscopic sequential events, such as a color change, in a very short reaction time. The colors are the same as those observed in nature, where these galloyl derivatives and hexahydroxydiphenoyl moieties (dimers) continue to oxidize, dissociate, and polymerize to form natural polyphenols, catechins, and hydrolyzable tannins.

In terms of biosorption of PhACs, the starting, bare, and undecorated material already showed a good surface activity toward PhACs (CLZ, SMX). In some specific cases (GFZ, MTP, and SMX), a slight increase of the biosorption property was observed after polyphenol-based decoration. Consistently, CLZ, CLR, FCZ, and TMP did not exhibit increased adsorption on the functionalized adsorbent surface compared with the control. In other works, this behavior is fundamentally observed due to the low molecular weight of these molecules. In this case, a mere consideration under pH light should be done. For instance, in the case of NPX, KET, and GFZ, the decorated material showed higher adsorption at acidic pH. This is likely due to these molecules bearing free carboxyl groups, which remain undissociated at low pH, thus leading to performing dimer:dimer bimolecular interactions with the carboxyl groups of surface polyphenols. Conversely, at pH 9, the COOH group dissociates, and the resulting anionic form undergoes repulsive electrostatic interactions with the polyphenolic anions of the coating (Figure A).

Under a deep chemical point of view, among all of the PhACs, MTP can be employed here as a model compound. Polyphenol functionalization on ess resulted in the best efficiency when the MTP molecular bulk was removed from the aqueous solution. This should be consequent to the specific noncovalent interaction with many functional groups in principle exploitable in the remediation process. In Figure B, it is first shown that an interaction can occur once the aryl moiety core can π–π overlap with the polyphenol moiety belonging to protruding, available GA structures. Moreover, the free phenolic hydroxyl groups act as donors in hydrogen bonding with a large amount of functional groups, including free hydroxyl exhibited by the aliphatic tail of MTP. Finally, the drug molecule can exhibit proximal monoaminium functions, which can facilitate the interaction with protruding free carboxyl groups of gallic acid in pGA- ess , thus leading to an increase of the electrostatic interactions standing for the production of inner salty bridging. A similar bridging effect can likely be observed when the adsorbed considered drug is sulfamethoxazole (SMX), which is a monoamino-protruding chemical capable of protonating and directly interacting with free carboxyl groups of polyphenol moieties.

Strong interactions between the adsorbent and PhACs have been reported by Pedrosa et al., who employed spirulina-based activated carbon (ASBCM800) with a very high specific surface area to remove MTP and other PhACs from contaminated water. In this study, the tested biosorbent efficiently removed over 88% of pharmaceuticals spiked at 100 μg L–1 in the secondary effluent from an urban wastewater treatment plant within a short contact time.

Generally speaking, adsorption of PhACs is influenced not only by the properties of the adsorbent and adsorbate but also by the nature of the solution. Typically, the pH observed in aquatic environments ranges from 5 to 9. Such variations have a significant impact on the overall PhAC adsorption process due to electrostatic or other noncovalent interactions between the contaminants and the surface charges of the adsorbent. The differing behaviors of the drugs can be attributed to their pK a values. PhACs that are more extensively adsorbed at pH 9 and 5 are characterized by pK a values higher than the solution pH, and thus, the molecules are deprotonated. Even though the carboxyphenol moieties can become negatively charged, the PhACs can overcome intrinsic repulsion using different forces: dipolar interactions, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and transient acid dimerization.

Therefore, assessing the relationship between the charge of the contaminant and that of the adsorbent medium remains crucial for elucidating the adsorption mechanisms. Ragab et al. also reported that two PhACs, amoxicillin and diclofenac, were electrostatically attracted to the positively charged adsorption sites of a porous nanocomposite material composed of limestone, activated carbon, and sodium alginate, achieving maximum removal efficiencies (99.6% for AMX and 98.4% for DCF) under acidic pH conditions. In general, it can be concluded that electrostatic forces play a significant role in the adsorption process; however, depending on the specific nature of each drug, other interactions, such as π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces, may also occur.

pGA- ess also demonstrated an excellent regeneration capability within a short time. The release percentage of nearly all PhACs, ranging from 75 to 85% after 1 h of contact with distilled water, highlights the potential of this newly functionalized material. Even higher release efficiencies (up to 90%) were observed for FCZ, MTP, SMX, and TMP, indicating a strong correlation between the desorption dynamics of these compounds and their chemical properties. These compounds, characterized by low log Kow values, are highly hydrophilic, exhibit high aqueous solubility, and show low adsorption coefficients in soils/adsorbent materials. Recently, log Kow has emerged as a key parameter in research on the removal of organic pollutants and their environmental fate, and it is often considered when evaluating adsorption-based strategies for removing organic contaminants from wastewater. Finally, the near-complete release of the adsorbed PhACs from pGA- ess within less than 10 h (third or fourth wash cycle) makes the material cost-effective and enhances its commercial value, while enabling virtually unlimited reuse. In comparison with other materials, the requirement of only distilled water for regeneration renders pGA- ess more environmentally sustainable, thereby eliminating the need for chemical treatments or the use of organic solvents such as methanol, ethanol, and acetonitrile.

The adsorption behavior of MTP can be attributed to monolayer adsorption occurring without interactions between molecules on adjacent sites. This suggests that the available sites become saturated at a certain concentration. Our results are consistent with those reported by Waleng et al. In their study, the adsorption of two β-blockers (acebutolol and metoprolol) was evaluated using a chromium-based magnetic nanocomposite, MIL-101 (Fe3O4@MIL-101­(Cr)). Similarly, their experimental isotherm and kinetic data are better described by the Langmuir isotherm model.

Although no adsorption behavior specific to an entire pharmaceutical class can be universally described by a single isotherm model, previous studies , reported that drugs such as trimethoprim, ciprofloxacin, sulfamethazine, and amoxicillin (AMX) (antibiotics); diclofenac, ibuprofen, naproxen, and ketoprofen (NSAIDs); paracetamol (AP) (analgesic and antipyretic); carbamazepine (anticonvulsant); and metoprolol (β-blocker) were all well fitted by the Langmuir model.

The adsorption kinetics of MTP support the findings obtained from the adsorption isotherms, as the best fit with the pseudo-second-order model indicates that the adsorption of MTP onto the adsorbent surface is predominantly governed by a chemical process rather than a pure physical entrapment. The rapid initial adsorption can be attributed to the high surface area of the functionalized material and the abundance of active sites, which contributed to a shorter equilibrium time. Similarly, Belkharchouche et al. reported that MTP can be adsorbed within a very short time; in their study, using phosphoric acid-activated carbon, equilibrium was achieved within 5 min at slightly lower concentrations below 150 mg L–1.

A comprehensive evaluation of the potential application of bioadsorbents in wastewater remediation requires assessing their capacity for multiple reuses while retaining stability and high removal efficiency. In this study, recyclability experiments along with the evaluation of the optimal adsorbent dosage to maximize MTP removal further demonstrated that appropriately functionalized natural materials can effectively support water treatment processes.

In light of the results obtained, the novelty of this approach stems from the integration of a readily available and fully biodegradable marine-derived matrix with a rapid and environmentally benign bioorganic functionalization, enabling the development of a sustainable, recyclable, cost-effective, and multifunctional adsorbent for emerging contaminant removal.

The added value of this study lies in the fact that unlike other biologically derived adsorbents, the selected material can be directly sourced from coastal remediation activities. In contrast, previous studies on MTP removal have relied on adsorbents requiring extensive preparation, such as activated carbons obtained via chemical impregnation of bean husks with phosphoric or sulfuric acid, biochar produced from discarded cabbage leaves combined with kaolinite and anthocyanins, or chitosan hydrogel beads. Unlike sea egg capsules, these conventional adsorbents necessitate complex chemical or thermal treatments, such as chitin deacetylation, biorefinery and pulping processes, or pyrolysis, which entail high costs and notable environmental impacts. In contrast, the material proposed here offers a more sustainable and cost-effective alternative.

Finally, the functionalization strategy adopted in this study consists of an oxidative passivation process, whereby the oxygen-containing organic triphenol undergoes spontaneous transformation in the presence of commercial bases, resulting in the formation of a bioinspired organic coating.

Conclusions

A particularly relevant aspect concerns the multiple novelties of this matrix as a multifunctional platform with specific chemical and physical properties.

First, after polymerization, it functions as a protein-based matrix exhibiting different behaviors depending on the pH and, consequently, showing modulated interactions with PhACs, particularly according to their pK a values. Another distinctive feature of this matrix is its behavior as a chemical bulk material with enhanced physical and mechanical confinement capabilities, owing to the polymeric meshes formed on its surface and the reticular architecture typical of tannin-like amorphous matrices. A key outcome of our research is the combination of naturally occurring sea egg capsules, easily obtainable in coastal environments, with chemically modified, biologically inspired polyphenols to induce a range of multifactorial adsorption–desorption processes.

Furthermore, the novelty highlighted by the results of this study is also reflected in the promising future applications of this biomaterial. pGA- ess , similar to other materials previously investigated in the literature, such as modified clays, zeolites, and biochar, − can offer significant potential for integration into conventional wastewater treatment systems, either through direct application in treatment tanks or via incorporation into membrane-based technologies.

Finally, its applicability extends to the targeted remediation of high-impact effluents including hospital and industrial wastewaters. Further studies should evaluate process scalability, material stability under real operational conditions, and economic feasibility to support its deployment on a full scale.
