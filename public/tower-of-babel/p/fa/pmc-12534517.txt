Dysfunction of the rostral lateral septum GABAergic neurons induces mania-like behavior in male mice
Authors: Yu Zhou, Hongjun Liu, Ziyang Jiang, Xiangyu Pan, Junming Xia, Lili Feng, Jun-Li Cao, Wenxian Li, Yuan Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Manic episodes in bipolar disorder are characterized by hyperactivity, elevated mood, and sleep disturbances. The precise brain regions and neuronal populations underlying these behaviors remain elusive. We demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition or ablation of rostral lateral septum (LS) GABAergic neurons induced mania-like behaviors in mice, encompassing hyperactivity, reduced anxiety, anti-depressive-like behaviors, and shortened sleep duration. Chronic administration of lithium and valproic acid effectively attenuated hypermobility and normalized elevated mood in these mice. Regarding neural circuit mechanisms, we identified that mania-like behaviors induced by LS GABAergic neuronal dysfunction primarily involve the lateral hypothalamic circuit and, to a lesser extent, the diagonal band of Broca. These findings elucidate the neurobiological mechanisms of manic episodes and identify the LS GABAergic-lateral hypothalamic pathway as a potential therapeutic target for bipolar disorder.

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD), a chronic and severe psychiatric condition, is characterized by recurrent episodes of depression and mania, affecting 2.4% of individuals across their lifespan within the BD spectrum [1]. While both phases are clinically significant, manic episodes are especially disruptive and constitute the defining feature of BD, differentiating it from unipolar major depressive disorder [2]. Manic episodes have been attributed to genetic mutations and deletions [3–5], as well as structural and functional changes in the frontal-limbic system [6]. Despite extensive research [7–9], the exact molecular mechanisms and neural circuits underpinning manic behaviors remain unclear.

Recent research paradigms have evolved towards conceptualizing manic episodes as originating from dysfunctional information processing in specific brain circuits and networks [10, 11]. This approach has paved the way for novel investigations into the neural substrates of mania-like behaviors. Multiple clinical functional imaging studies have demonstrated that reductions in septum pellucidum volume and the formation of cavum septum pellucidum (CSP) in humans are strongly associated with schizophrenia [12–15], bipolar disorder [16, 17], major depressive disorder [18], and other mental disorders [19]. Neuromorphometric analysis of bipolar patients’ brains revealed significantly lower neuronal density in the lateral septal nucleus compared to control subjects [20]. Vascular and tumoral ablations of septal tissue or resection of central neurocytomas can induce neuropsychiatric disturbances mimicking schizophrenia [21], secondary organic bipolar disorder [22], or psychotic symptoms [23]. These studies suggest that disrupted neuronal plasticity underlies the neuropathology of various psychiatric disorders, including mania, and that lateral septum (LS) deficits may represent a critical pathogenic mechanism.

The LS integrates subcortical and cortical areas, relaying cognitive, emotional and motivational information to the hypothalamus [24, 25], a key brain region implicated in the pathophysiology of affective disorders [11, 26, 27]. The LS is known to be predominantly composed of GABAergic neurons [28]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that septal ablation, especially of the rostral part, induces hyperactivity, hyperemotionality, heightened defensive behaviors, and aggression in rodents, collectively termed septal rage [29, 30]. Notably, the behavioral manifestations of septal rage in rodents closely parallel the core symptoms of manic episodes in humans, including hyperactivity, elevated mood, impulsivity, and decreased sleep [31]. Recent evidence suggests that functional abnormalities in the LS contribute to various pathological processes, including mood disorders [24], defense [32], and aggression [33, 34]. Virtually all clinically effective antipsychotic drugs, both typical and atypical, enhance cFOS expression, a common marker of neuronal activation, in the lateral septal nucleus [35–38]. This correlation suggests that LS dysfunction may be a key contributor to mania-like behaviors, thus representing a promising target for pharmacotherapy. However, most existing studies are correlational, lacking direct causal evidence linking LS dysfunction to manic behaviors. Further investigation is necessary to determine whether the LS contributes to mania and to delineate the specific neural circuits through which it exerts its effects.

In this study, we investigated the various behavioral phenotypes induced by inhibiting or ablating LS GABAergic neurons, including hyperactivity, reduced depression and anxiety, loss of sleep rebound, and decreased sleep. GABAergic dysfunction in the lateral septum not only recapitulates key pathological features of manic syndrome but also exhibits responsiveness to commonly used antimanic pharmacological treatments. We also mapped the downstream connectivity of the LS and verified that the mania-like behaviors resulting from LS GABAergic neuronal dysfunction are mediated via the LSGABA-LH circuit. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that specific alterations in the LS and septo-hypothalamic circuits are integral to the symptoms observed in manic episodes. This study provided novel insights into the neurobiological basis of manic episodes and revealed potential new targets for therapeutic interventions in mania.

Material and methods

Animals

This study utilized adult male C57BL/6J wild-type mice, heterozygous VGluT-cre (VGluT2-IRES-Cre: Slc17a6tm2(cre)Lowl/J, The Jackson Laboratory, JAX stock 016963) and heterozygous VGat-ires-Cre transgenic mice (VGat-ires-Cre: Slc32a1tm2(cre)Lowl/J, The Jackson Laboratory, JAX stock 016962). heterozygous Hcrt-ires-Cre mice were kindly donated by Dr. Yiyan Dong (Zhejiang University). Mice used in all experiments were housed in custom-made polycarbonate cages under controlled conditions: temperature (22 ± 1 °C), humidity (30–50%), and a stable 12-h light/dark cycle starting at 7 AM. Food and water were available ad libitum. At the start of the experiments, all mice were 8 weeks old and weighed between 23 and 30 grams. Experimenters conducting the behavioral tests were blinded to group assignments. Unless otherwise specified, behavioral experiments were conducted during the dark phase. At the end of the experiments, mice were anesthetized deeply with 5% sevoflurane and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. All procedures complied with standard ethical guidelines, as per the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University.

Behavioral experiments

Prior to experimentation, all mice underwent a 3-day habituation period, involving 2-min daily handling sessions. For all experiments, male mice were randomized and placed into groups based on weight. Behavioral experiments, excluding EEG/EMG recordings requiring 24-h data sampling, were conducted 1–4 h post-lights-off (20:00–24:00) under dim red light illumination. To eliminate olfactory cues, all apparatus were sanitized with 75% ethanol before and after each test. Behavioral tests were conducted in ascending order of stress induction, with a minimum inter-test interval of 24 h: open field test, elevated plus maze, sucrose preference test, tail suspension test, and forced swim test. A minimum 7-day recovery period was implemented before initiating subsequent behavioral test series. We used the same cohort of mice for the behavioral tests described above, and detailed temporal parameters for each behavioral test are provided in the results section and corresponding figure legends. To mitigate potential confounding effects of EEG/EMG electrode implantation on behavioral outcomes, separate cohorts of mice were utilized for EEG/EMG recordings.

Assay for locomotor activity (open field test)

The open field test was performed following established protocols [11], with minor modifications. Individual mice were placed in a white, cubic arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm) constructed of opaque plastic. Ambient illumination at the center of the arena was maintained between 30–60 lux. Spontaneous locomotor activity was recorded using an overhead digital camera. Locomotor parameters, including total distance traveled and average velocity, were quantified over a 30-min period using automated video tracking software (SMART3.0, Panlab, Barcelona, Spain).

Assay for elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze [24] (EPM) apparatus comprised two opposing open arms (35 × 6 cm) and two closed arms (35 × 6 cm, with 15 cm high walls) extending from a central platform (6 × 6 cm). The maze was elevated 70 cm above the ground level. Each subject was placed on the central platform facing an open arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for a 5-min period. Behavioral parameters quantified included the time spent in the open arms. The apparatus was sanitized with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues.

Assay for Tail-suspension test (TST)

For the tail suspension test [39] (TST), mice were initially acclimated to the testing room. Subsequently, each mouse was suspended by its tail, secured with adhesive tape near the distal end, at a height of 50 cm from the ground. This elevation prevented any physical contact with surfaces or climbing behavior during the assessment. Behavioral responses were video-recorded for a 5-min duration. Immobility duration during the final 4 min of the test was quantified by a separate, blinded observer. Immobility was operationally defined as the absence of escape-oriented behaviors, including struggling or swinging motions.

Assay for forced swimming test (FST)

For the forced swim test [40] (FST), individual mice were placed in a transparent cylindrical apparatus (diameter: 25 cm, height: 40 cm) containing 15 cm of water maintained at 23–25 °C. The water depth precluded subjects from touching the bottom with their limbs or tail. Swimming behavior was video-recorded for a 6-min period. Immobility duration during the final 4 min was quantified manually by a blinded observer. Immobility was operationally defined as the period during which the subject remained floating passively or made only minor movements required to maintain its head above water.

Assay for sucrose preference test (SPT)

For the sucrose preference test [41] (SPT), mice were housed individually and provided access to two bottles: one containing water and the other a 1% sucrose solution for 48 h. The positions of the bottles were interchanged after 24 h. Following a 10-h water deprivation period, mice were presented with one bottle of 2% sucrose solution and one bottle of water for a 4-h test period. Bottle positions were interchanged midway through the test period. Sucrose preference was quantified as the ratio of sucrose consumption to total fluid intake (sucrose plus water).

Pharmacological treatment

For all pharmacological interventions, mice initially underwent behavioral testing following a vehicle injection. Subsequently, after a 7-day washout period, the same cohort was administered the drug and subjected to behavioral testing. Behavioral assessments were repeated on the same cohort one week post-drug discontinuation. In the case of the 3-week Li-H2O regimen, additional behavioral testing was conducted 14 days after treatment cessation. Baseline behavioral assessments were performed in the absence of pharmacological intervention during this period. Detailed timelines for behavioral testing are provided in the respective results sections.

Mice received intraperitoneal injections of either sodium valproate (ApexBio, USA; 200 mg/kg in saline), lithium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich; 100 mg/kg in saline), or saline vehicle. For acute lithium or valproate administration (multiple injections), mice initially underwent behavioral tests (locomotion and tail suspension) following a vehicle injection. After a 7-day interval, the same cohort was administered lithium or valproate. For the Li-H2O paradigm [11], mice were administered lithium chloride via drinking water (300 mg/L) over a 3-week period. Baseline behavioral assessments were conducted with mice on regular water, followed by a 3-week Li-H2O regimen for the same cohort. Valproate and lithium treatments adhered to established protocols [42, 43], involving two injections at 4-h intervals on the day preceding and the day of behavioral testing. Behavioral assessments were performed 1 h post-final injection on the test day.

Statistical analyses and quality control

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Prism 9; GraphPad Software, Inc.). Sample sizes was determined based on our previous experience with assay variance and experimental feasibility. Normality was tested by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Equal variances were assessed by F-test. For datasets that were normally distributed, parametric tests of the variance and group differences (paired, unpaired t-tests, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s, Bonferroni’s or Tukey’s post hoc tests.) were used. Otherwise, the non-parametric Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test, and Mann Whitney Rank Sum Test were used. For the analysis of correlations between the LS ablation size and mania-like behaviors, we used the Pearson correlation for normally distributed data. Data are represented as the mean ± s.e.m. Statistical significance was set at four levels (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). If histological results do not confirm significant expression of mCherry in the target brain region, or if the expression significantly extends beyond the target area, or if there is a malfunction in the EEG/EMG recording technique during the experiment, the mouse will be excluded from the analysis. All the experiments were performed and analyzed blind to treatment conditions.

Detailed information about the materials and methods is provided in the supplementary material.

To evaluate the emotional behaviors of LSrGABA-hM4Di mice, we employed the elevated plus maze, tail suspension test (TST), forced swim test (FST), and sucrose preference test (SPT). Initially, we utilized the elevated plus maze to evaluate anxiety-like behaviors in these mouse models. Both CNO-treated LSrGABA-hM4Di mice exhibited an increased time spent in the open arms of the maze (Fig. 1E), indicating reduced anxiety. In the SPT, CNO-injected LSrGABA-hM4Di mice exhibited increased sucrose consumption compared to control mice (Fig. 1F), suggesting an enhanced hedonic state. In the TST and FST, immobilization times for CNO-injected LSrGABA-hM4Di mice were significantly shorter than those for saline-injected LSrGABA-hM4Di mice (Fig. 1G, H). This suggests that reducing the output of rostral LS GABAergic neurons through chemical inhibition can alleviate depressive-like behaviors. Additionally, intraperitoneal CNO injection did not affect these behaviors in LSrGABA-mCherry mice, serving as a control (S Fig. 1C–F).

Chemogenetic inhibition of GABAergic neurons in the caudal lateral septal nucleus failed to elicit mania-like behaviors

LS is a complex and heterogeneous structure with various subregions that serves distinct functions. Previous studies have suggested that the rostral, but not the caudal, segment of the LS may be particularly important for the expression of mania-like behaviors [29, 30]. To further investigate this hypothesis, we used the same strategy to chemogenetically inhibited GABAergic neurons in the caudal segment of the LS (LScGABA-hM4Di mice) (Fig. 1B). Chemogenetic inhibition of GABAergic neurons in the caudal LS did not alter locomotor activity or distance traveled in LScGABA-hM4Di mice compared with saline-injected mice (Fig. 1D). Additionally, no differences were observed in the time spent in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Fig. 1I) or the immobility times during the TST and FST between the two groups (Fig. 1K–L). However, LScGABA-hM4Di mice ingested more sucrose compared to control mice (Fig. 1J). These data served as an internal negative control, suggesting that inhibition of GABA neurons in the caudal segment of the LS did not produce hyperactivity or elevated mood.

Chronic chemogenetic inhibition or ablation of rostral LS GABAergic neurons resulted in enhanced locomotor activity and elevated mood

For the chronic inhibition strategy [45], afterwards, we repeatedly inhibited GABAergic neurons in the rostral LS. After injecting the AAV-hM4Di-mCherry vectors for 21days, the CNO was administered daily (3 mg/kg, i.p.) for another 21 days (S Fig. 2A). Following the CNO treatment, we assessed behavior using the open field test (OFT), elevated plus maze, TST, and FST. The CNO-injected LSrGABA-hM4Di mice exhibited pronounced motor hyperactivity and spent more time in the open arms of the maze (S Fig. 2B, C). Similarly, the immobility time for CNO-injected LSrGABA-hM4Di mice was significantly shorter than that for saline-injected controls (S Fig. 2D, E). These results suggest that, similar to the acute chemical inhibition of the LS (Fig. 1), long-term inhibition also induces mania-like behaviors in mice.

For the chronic ablation strategy, as before, we ablated the rostral LS GABAergic neurons using the AAV-DIO-Casp3 virus vector (for LSrGABA-Casp3 mice). Subsequently, to assess the results of neuronal ablation in the LS, we used an antibody targeting the neuronal marker NeuN (Fig. 2A). As a result, Casp3 effectively eliminated most of the neurons in the LS (Fig. 2B), which is a region-rich in GABAergic neurons. Consistent with acute inhibition of GABAergic neurons in the caudal LS, LSrGABA-Casp3 mice showed more pronounced motor hyperactivity compared to LSrGABA-mCherry mice in the open field test (OFT) (Fig. 2C, D). Initially, we utilized the elevated plus maze to evaluate anxiety-like behaviors in these mouse models. LSrGABA-Casp3 mice exhibited an increased time spent in the open arms of the maze compared to LSrGABA-mCherry mice (Fig. 2E), indicating reduced anxiety. In the SPT, LSrGABA-Casp3 mice exhibited increased sucrose consumption compared to LSrGABA-mCherry mice (Fig. 2F), suggesting an enhanced hedonic state. In the TST and FST, immobilization times for LSrGABA-Casp3 mice were significantly shorter than those for LSrGABA-mCherry mice (Fig. 2G, H). Chemogenetic inhibition or Chronic ablation of LS GABAergic neurons disrupted normal sleep patterns and associated needs

Sleep disturbances are a prominent feature of bipolar disorder, particularly severe during the manic phase compared to the depressive phase [46]. Sleep loss has been postulated as a final common pathway in the genesis of mania [47, 48]. We initially chemogenetic inhibition of LS GABAergic neurons resulted in reduced sleep and heightened arousal (Fig. 3B–D). These findings are corroborated by the observation that the amount of 24-h sleep in LSrGABA-Casp3 mice (Fig. 3E). In comparison to LSrGABA-mCherry mice, LSrGABA-Casp3 mice exhibited a significant increase in arousal (Fig. 3F) and a notable decrease in sleep (Fig. 3G, H). The sleep fragmentation in LSrGABA-Casp3 mice mainly occurs during the daytime (resting and sleeping mainly occur in the daytime light period) (S Fig. 4). Through further quantitative analysis of the sleep-wake structure, LSrGABA-Casp3 mice showed a significant increase in the number of wake episodes and NREMS episodes, as well as a significant decrease in the number of episodes of REMS compared with LSrGABA-mCherry mice (S Fig. 5A–C). Furthermore, there was a significant increase in the number of transitions from NREMS to Wake, and from Wake to NREMS (Fig. 3I). Next, we calculated the delta power during the daily sleep to assess the average level of sleep demand. The results revealed that LSrGABA-Casp3 mice had increased EEG power during both NREMS and REMS compared to LSrGABA-mCherry mice (Fig. 3J, K). This finding suggests that although LSrGABA-Casp3 mice slept less each day, more efficient deep sleep may compensate for some of the effects of sleep loss. These data indicate that the mechanisms regulating delta power during SWS and sleep volume are separate, which is consistent with previous findings [49, 50]. Therefore, we next conducted a sleep deprivation (SD) experiment, where control mice showed a significant NREMS rebound after 6 h of sleep deprivation. In contrast, LSrGABA-Casp3 mice took longer to enter NREMS after 6 h of SD (S Fig. 5D), suggesting that LSrGABA-Casp3 mice had less sleep stress after SD. Overall, we found that LS GABAergic neurons play a crucial role in regulating daily sleep volume and homeostasis in vivo.

Chemical inhibition or chronic ablation of LS GABAergic neurons resulted in social hyperactivity and irritability

Increased social presence is observed during manic episodes in humans. In the three-chamber social test (S Fig. 6A), we recorded the activity trajectories of the mice over a 10-min period and presented representative total tracks of the control group, LSrGABA-hM4Di mice, and LSrGABA-Casp3 mice in the chambers (S Fig. 6B). The results showed that both LSrGABA-hM4Di and LSrGABA-Casp3 mice had a significantly higher sociability index (SI) compared to the control group (S Fig. 6C, D).

Irritable responses in manic patients are usually an over-amplification of ordinary stimuli, even to small external changes or threats. Therefore, we next investigated irritable behavior induced by tactile and physical stimuli by subjecting each mouse to two consecutive manual tactile and two physical stimuli using plastic sticks, with 1-min intervals of rest after 35 min of habituation in a home cage (S Fig. 6E). The scoring criteria for behavior have been described previously40 and consist of 5 parameters: freezing, standing, hyperventilation, aggression, and avoidance. Each parameter is scored from 0 to 2, and the total score reflects the degree of defensive response. Both response scores were significantly increased in LSrGABA-hM4Di mice and LSrGABA-Casp3 mice compared to control mice (S Fig. 6F, G), indicating that these mice exhibited irritable behavior.

Mania-like behaviors induced by ablation of LS GABAergic neurons can be alleviated with mood stabilizers

Lithium and valproic acid (VPA), both mood stabilizers, are among the first-line treatment options for manic episodes of bipolar disorder [31, 51]. Therefore, we assessed the efficacy of sodium valproate and lithium in treating mania-like behaviors in LSrGABA-Casp3 mice (Fig. 4A). Initially, we investigated the effects of treatment with sodium valproate (200 mg/kg) (Fig. 4B). The excessive locomotor activity of LSrGABA-Casp3 mice in the open field was reduced to control levels after treatment with sodium valproate (Fig. 4C). In the elevated plus maze test, LSrGABA-Casp3 mice treated with valproate spent less time in the open arms compared to their pre-treatment levels (Fig. 4D). Similarly, treatment with sodium valproate significantly increased the immobilization time in the TST (Fig. 4E). However, two weeks after discontinuing sodium valproate treatment, the abnormal hyperarousal reappeared in LSrGABA-Casp3 mice (Fig. 4C–E). We next examined the effects of lithium treatments. Acute injection of lithium (100 mg/kg) did not affect the locomotor activity or the time spent in the open arms of LSrGABA-Casp3 mice (S Fig. 7B, C), but it decreased the immobility time of LSrGABA-mCherry control mice during the TST (S Fig. 7D). Furthermore, acute lithium chloride injection did not affect motor activity, time spent in the open arms of the maze, or immobility time in the TST in LSrGABA-mCherry control mice (S Fig. 9E–G). Treatment of LSrGABA-Casp3 mice with chronic Li-H2O treatment (300 mg/L) reduced the hyperlocomotion and the time in the open arms (S Fig. 8A–C), and fully restored the immobility time during the TST (S Fig. 8D). These studies suggest that, in alignment with clinical treatment strategies [52], long-term maintenance therapy with lithium is essential for managing manic episodes and achieving mood stabilization. These pharmacological findings further validate the efficacy of mania-like behaviors induced by ablation of GABAergic neurons in the LS.

To investigate the input from LS GABAergic neurons to the LH, we used optogenetic activation of LS GABAergic neuron terminals in the LH and recorded the calcium signals of GABAergic, glutamatergic, and orexinergic neurons in the LH (Fig. 5F). Our results show that optogenetic activation of LS GABAergic terminals significantly inhibited the calcium activity of LH GABAergic neurons (Fig. 5G), suggesting a direct inhibitory effect of LS GABAergic input on LH GABAergic neurons. In contrast, there were no significant changes in the calcium signals of LH glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 5H). Interestingly, activation of LS GABAergic terminals resulted in a marked increase in the calcium activity of LH orexinergic neurons (Fig. 5I), indicating a strong excitatory modulation of orexinergic neurons. These findings suggest that LH GABAergic neurons may receive direct inhibitory projections from LS, which could play a crucial role in the manic-like behaviors induced by LS functional inhibition.

Discussion

Extensive research has been dedicated to elucidating the neurobiological mechanisms of mania [55]. Nevertheless, the precise neural circuitry underlying mania-like behaviors remains elusive. Our study identified a specific brain region that mediates the development of mania-like behavior. Specifically, ablation or acute inhibition of LS GABAergic neurons can produce phenotypes similar to mania, including hyperactivity, elevated mood, reduced sleep, increased socialization, and irritability. Notably, these mania-like effects are attenuated by frontline clinical mood stabilizers, suggesting a potential correlation between the observed behaviors in our mouse model and human manic episodes in bipolar disorder. However, it remains unclear whether the manic phenotype is collectively produced by the disinhibition of multiple downstream target brain regions following ablation to or acute inhibition of LS GABAergic neurons. We further traced the downstream brain regions of LS GABAergic neurons and found that inhibition of the LS-LH projection induced a manic effect (hyperactive, anxiolytic and antidepressant), while inhibition of the LS-HDB projection induced partial manic effects, including increased locomotion and anxiolysis. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that inhibition or loss of LS GABAergic neurons, particularly those projecting to the LH, plays a critical role in the manifestation of mania-like behaviors (S Fig. 13).

The LS has been identified as a nexus of emotion and motivation, harboring abundant GABAergic neurons that regulate locomotion, mood fluctuations, and sleep-wake behaviors [56]. Behavioral hyperactivity following LS ablation primarily results from the disruption of the LS and its associated circuits. Theta oscillations in the hippocampus and LS, coordinating their primary subcortical outputs, modulate motor coding. Both chemical and optogenetic inhibition of dorsal hippocampal-LS projections lead to increased running speed. Consistent with our observations, stimulation of theta rhythms in LS projections to the LH decreases running speed, highlighting the critical role of the LH in locomotion control [57]. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that stimulation or inhibition of neuronal activity in the LS can induce or alleviate anxiety, respectively [58, 59]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that acute stress activates GABAergic neurons in the LS, inducing anxiety-like behaviors [24]. Gray and McNaughton proposed that the LS plays not only an important role in emotion regulation but also a crucial role in the modulation of behavioral inhibition, impulse control, and social interaction behaviors [60]. Therefore, damage to the LS may lead to the loss of behavioral inhibition (disinhibition), manifested as impulsive and aggressive behaviors, which is consistent with the behaviors we observed in the model, such as hyperactivity, sociability, and irritability.

Moreover, a reduced sleep requirement is a fundamental hallmark of mania, and many factors associated with disease relapse may trigger manic episodes through significantly decreased sleep [61–63]. Our study revealed that chronic ablation of GABAergic neurons in the LS induced multiple forms of sleep disturbances in mice, including increased wakefulness after sleep onset, reduced sleep requirement, unstable sleep-wake cycles, and fragmented sleep (Fig. 3). These findings align with clinical observations in patients with bipolar disorder [64]. Interestingly, our study demonstrated that delta power increased following LS ablation, which is consistent with clinical observations of enhanced delta/alpha EEG activity in patients with bipolar disorder [65]. During manic episodes, patients often experience sleep deprivation, reduced sleep efficiency, or fragmented sleep. The observed increase in delta power may represent a compensatory rebound mechanism, in which the brain attempts to restore disrupted neural homeostasis by enhancing slow-wave sleep. In addition, since delta oscillations are generated by synchronized discharges between the cortex and thalamus [66], damage to the LS may impair the modulation of these thalamo-cortical circuits, leading to abnormal amplification or dysregulation of delta rhythms. Given the potential bidirectional relationship between sleep abnormalities and mood symptoms [67], the fragmented sleep observed after LS dysfunction may not merely be a concomitant of manic-like behaviors. Instead, it could actively contribute to emotional dysregulation through interactions between arousal systems and limbic circuits, thereby exacerbating mood instability.

Our findings also indicate that mood stabilizers, such as lithium and valproic acid, partially or significantly alleviate mania-like symptoms. Administration of mood stabilizers (lithium or valproate) to rats reversed these mania-like behaviors [68], aligning with our findings and further validating this model. Lithium is an effective treatment for acute mania and relapse prevention. It preferentially targets excitatory neurons and accumulates inside cells via voltage-gated channels and neurotransmitter-coupled channels. Within the cell, lithium ions replace sodium ions at a 1:1 ratio, leading to a decrease in intracellular sodium concentration, thereby reducing neuronal excitability [69]. When voltage-gated sodium channels open, valproate inhibits the activity of these channels, exerting selective effects on the activity-dependent firing of rapidly firing neurons [70]. Consequently, valproate primarily suppresses active neurons, whereas lithium accumulates throughout the neuron, affecting depolarization of the soma and firing in the axon. Our experiments show that acute single-dose administration can partially alleviate manic-like behavior, suggesting that these drugs may exert short-term effects by rapidly modulating neural circuit activity, such as the GABA/glutamate balance [35–38]. Long-term use, however, may lead to more sustained effects through mechanisms like gene expression modulation and synaptic remodeling [71]. Therefore, exploring novel pharmacological interventions that target activated LS neurons for the treatment of manic episodes in bipolar disorder represents a promising avenue for future research.

An important issue regarding the role of the LS is the functional differences among its various subnuclei. Distinct subregions of the rodent LS have been delineated based on cellular structure, neuroanatomical connections, and the expression of various neurotransmitters [72, 73]. However, a comprehensive understanding of the functional specialization of LS subregions remains elusive, both in rodents and humans. The phenomenon of septal rage induced by LS ablation typically necessitates damage to the rostral LS [29]. Corroborating this spatial specificity, we found that inhibition of caudal LS GABAergic neurons failed to elicit mania-like behaviors (Fig. 1). In the present study, inhibition of rostral LS GABAergic neurons induced excessive arousal and disrupted sleep homeostasis. Conversely, our previous research demonstrated that inhibition of dorsal-intermediate LS GABAergic neurons attenuates arousal via the midbrain dopamine circuit [74]. These findings suggest that there are functional differences among LS subnuclei in modulating sleep-wake phenotypes. The LSc expresses somatostatin, substance P receptor, and arginine vasopressin 1a receptor, while the LSr expresses neurotensin, enkephalin, Type 2 corticotrophin-release factor receptor (CRFR2), and dopamine 3 receptor [75]. Recent optogenetic studies have shown that activation of CRFR2-positive neurons in the LS produces anxiogenic effects in the light–dark box, open field, and NO tests, and this action depends on monosynaptic projections from LSCRFR2 neurons to GABAergic neurons in the anterior hypothalamic area [76]. In contrast, activation of somatostatin-positive interneurons in the LSc exerts anxiolytic effects in the EPM [77]. Thus, different neuronal subpopulations within the LS exhibit functional heterogeneity in anxiety regulation. Ongoing research into the emotional functions of the LS, particularly with emphasis on the functional roles of distinct LS subregions and different neuron types, will facilitate the development of a cohesive model of LS function and provide more precise targets for potential applications in the treatment of psychiatric disorders.

The LS provides extensive neural innervation to brain regions that regulate different behaviors. Employing viral tracing and chemogenetic neural control, we identified the downstream targets of LS GABAergic neurons that mediate mania-like behavior, notably the LH and the HDB. The LH comprises orexin/glutamate neurons that facilitate arousal, motivation, and energy expenditure [78–80], alongside GABAergic neurons whose activation elicits arousal and locomotion [81, 82]. We observed that activation of LS GABAergic terminal projections suppressed LH GABAergic neurons, suggesting that LH GABAergic neurons may receive direct inhibitory projections from the LS and play a key role in the manifestation of mania-like behavior. Corroborating our findings, a previous study demonstrated that electrical stimulation of the LH at 180 Hz successfully induced mania-like behavior in rats, characterized by hyperactivity and reduced rest time [27]. Interestingly, we observed that activation of LS GABAergic neurons led to the activation of LH orexinergic neurons, which may reflect a disinhibition mechanism triggered by the suppression of LH GABAergic neurons. These results suggest that LS GABAergic neurons exert differential modulation on LH neuronal populations, highlighting the heterogeneity of LH cell clusters. Future studies involving more detailed neural manipulation of LH cell clusters may provide valuable insights into the neural network mechanisms underlying mania.

Several limitations of the present study warrant discussion. First, this study was conducted exclusively on male mice. While male patients exhibit a higher incidence of hypomania or mania compared to females [83], it is crucial to address gender differences in the manifestation and treatment efficacy of bipolar disorder [84, 85]. Consequently, future studies should incorporate female mice to address this limitation. Second, a notable limitation is the absence of lithium concentration monitoring during pharmacological treatment. In clinical practice, monitoring lithium serum levels is crucial for the safe administration of this drug [86]. Nonetheless, the dosage and duration of lithium administration in this study were based on previous research to minimize toxicity risk [11], and no abnormal behaviors were observed in the mice during treatment. Third, although we have preliminarily demonstrated the role of GABAergic neurons in the rostral LS in mediating mania-like behaviors, we have not yet defined the specific contributions of finer neuronal subpopulations within this region (such as neurotensin-positive neurons) to the mania-like phenotype. This will be a key direction for future investigations. Finally, similar to other studies on mania-like behaviors [50, 68, 87, 88], our research does not fully capture the entire spectrum of mania-related symptoms observed in all patients, nor does it reflect the transition between manic and depressive states [10]. In addition, anxiety- or depression-related tests that rely on locomotor activity, such as the open field test, may be confounded by the elevated activity levels per se. However, these limitations are common to most existing models used in mania research and are not unique to the present study. Despite these constraints, our model demonstrates strong cross-species comparability and translational potential in terms of behavioral phenotypes, pharmacological responses, and underlying neural circuit mechanisms.

In summary, our study demonstrated that aberrations in neuronal plasticity constitute the neuropathological basis for the generation of mania-like behavior, specifically manifesting as defects in the LS and its associated hypothalamic circuitry. This enhanced comprehension of these neural circuits is expected to facilitate the development of novel therapeutic strategies that target the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of bipolar disorder.
