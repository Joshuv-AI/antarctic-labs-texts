Epidemiology and Seasonal Trends of Pediatric Burns in North India: A Retrospective Study from a Tertiary Care Center
Authors: Sheikh Sarfraz Ali, Mohammed Fahud Khurram, Fahad Uddin Ansari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Pediatric burns are a major cause of morbidity and mortality in low- and middle-income countries, with significant social and economic consequences. This study aimed to analyze the epidemiology, etiology, and seasonal trends of pediatric burns in a tertiary care center in North India.Materials and Methods:A retrospective observational study was conducted in the Burns and Plastic Surgery Department, Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College and Hospital, Aligarh, from January to December 2024. All children ≤13 years admitted with burn injuries were included. Data on age, gender, etiology, total body surface area (TBSA), time of presentation, and seasonal variation were analyzed.Results:A total of 191 pediatric burn cases were admitted, all accidental in nature. Male children (n = 126) were nearly twice as affected as females (n = 65). The most vulnerable age group was 1–5 years (52%), followed by 6–10 years (24%). Scalds were the predominant cause (63%), mainly from hot milk and water, followed by flame burns (18%) and electrical injuries (14%). Most of the patients (66%) sustained burns involving <15% TBSA, although critical areas such as hands, trunk, buttocks, and genitalia were frequently involved. Twenty-five children presented late, often after receiving initial care from untrained practitioners. Seasonal analysis showed the highest incidence during winter and early spring (January–March, 30%), with the lowest in July (4%).Conclusion:Pediatric burns are largely preventable domestic injuries, with scalds being most common in young children. Prevention strategies should focus on parental education, safe cooking practices, improved housing and electrical infrastructure, and timely medical access. Seasonal peaks necessitate targeted awareness and preparedness programs during high-risk months.

INTRODUCTION

Burn injuries in children are a significant public health concern worldwide, with the greatest burden observed in low- and middle-income countries.[12] Young children, particularly those under 5 years of age, are most susceptible due to their developmental curiosity, limited hazard awareness, and dependence on caregivers.[3] Scald burns, commonly caused by hot liquids in domestic settings, account for the majority of pediatric cases.[456] In India, unsafe cooking practices, overcrowded households, and poor safety awareness contribute substantially to the incidence of pediatric burns.[78] Seasonal variations, especially during winter months and festive seasons such as Diwali, further elevate the risk.[9]

The causes of pediatric burns are multifactorial, ranging from household behaviors to broader infrastructural and cultural influences.[2] Floor-level cooking, loose clothing, and storage of hot liquids within children’s reach are recurring hazards in many homes.[47] Lack of supervision, limited access to safety equipment, and reliance on traditional cooking methods further increase exposure.[3] Beyond the immediate physical injury, burns impose lasting consequences, including pain, disability, disfigurement, and psychological trauma.[10] Families face prolonged hospital stays, emotional stress, and financial hardship, making burns not only a medical but also a social and economic challenge.[12]

India reports an estimated 6–7 million burn cases annually, with children comprising over one-fourth of all injuries.[57] Although mortality rates in specialized burn units have improved,[6] burns remain the second leading cause of trauma-related deaths after road accidents.[1] Scalds dominate among younger children, while flame burns are more common in older age groups.[2] Electrical burns, though less frequent, often result in severe tissue destruction and long-term disability.[9]

Seasonal and cultural patterns also influence burn epidemiology. Higher rates are consistently recorded in colder months due to the use of heating devices, hot water, and open flames.[3] Alarmingly, India accounts for nearly one-fifth of global fire-related deaths,[1] underscoring the urgent need for prevention and improved infrastructure.[10]

Understanding the epidemiology and etiology of pediatric burns is therefore essential for developing targeted preventive strategies.[2] Comprehensive data can inform parental education, community-based awareness campaigns, and evidence-based policies to reduce incidence and improve outcomes.[10] This study aims to analyze the epidemiological trends, causes, and seasonal variations of pediatric burns in a tertiary care center. The ultimate goal of the study is to support grassroots-level prevention and enhancing pediatric burn care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective observational study conducted in the Burns and Plastic Surgery Department, Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College and Hospital, Aligarh Muslim University, Aligarh. All cases of burn injury in the pediatric population up to 13 years of age were included. Data were collected for all admissions between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024. Data were collected from the hospital admission registers and departmental records. All types of burn patients were included, whether presenting directly to outpatient department (OPD) or emergency, or referred from other Centers. Readmissions were not counted again in the data. Only minor first-degree burns presenting to the OPD and treated on an outpatient basis were not included in the study.

The study variables included age, gender, mode of burn, time of reporting after injury, and the total body surface area (TBSA) involved. Etiology was given particular emphasis to assess seasonal and temporal trends. TBSA was calculated using the Lund and Browder chart, which is considered the gold standard for pediatric burn assessment.[34] The patient and their parents’ identity was kept confidential in the study. The data included were fully anonymized. Institutional ethics committee approval was taken nonetheless.

Data analysis focused on the frequency distribution of burn types, demographic factors, and seasonal variations. As this was a demography-focused study, the management and complications encountered in burn victims were not included. Focus was mainly on etiology and demography.

RESULTS

A total of 191 pediatric patients under the age of 14 years were admitted during the study period (January 1–December 31, 2024). All cases were accidental burns; no suicidal or homicidal cases were reported, consistent with patterns described in similar studies.[47]

Male children were disproportionately affected, with nearly twice as many males (n = 126) as females (n = 65) [Table 1], a trend also observed in previous Indian and international reports.[35] The majority of cases occurred in children aged 1–5 years (n = 99), followed by 6–10 years (n = 46), aligning with known vulnerability in toddlers and preschool-aged children [Table 2 and Figure 1].[24]

Scalds were the most common cause, accounting for 63% (n = 120) of cases, primarily from hot milk, water, and oil [Table 3]. This predominance of scald injuries mirrors existing literature identifying hot liquids as the leading cause of pediatric burns in India and other low-resource settings.[56] Flame burns were noted in 18% (n = 35), while electrical injuries (n = 27, 14%) disproportionately affected older children, as reported elsewhere.[89] A smaller number of contact burns (n = 5) and firecracker-related burns (n = 4) were also observed, consistent with seasonal festival risks.[3]

Eight patients with TBSA of more than 40% died due to major complications such as sepsis, shock, and the presence of inhalational burn. Sepsis was seen as a major complication in 25 cases. Sepsis was seen more commonly in children reporting late to our institute with a history of being treated first by quacks. Fasciotomies were carried out in 12 patients with electric burns, with 3 requiring amputation of the limb during the course of treatment. Two patients with firecracker burns required amputation of the mangled fingers with stump closure.

Seasonal variation showed peak incidence between January and March (n = 58, 30%), followed by October–December (n = 52, 27%) [Figure 3 and Table 5]. The lowest incidence was in July (n = 8). These findings correspond with the increased winter use of hot liquids, open flames, and heating devices described in earlier research.[39]

DISCUSSION

Burn care and management remain challenging, particularly in pediatric populations, where injuries carry long-term physical and psychosocial consequences.[210] Effective management requires not only acute medical intervention but also rehabilitation and prevention strategies at household and community levels.[3]

Our findings reaffirm that children aged 1–5 years are at greatest risk, reflecting their increased mobility, curiosity, and lack of hazard awareness [Figure 4].[24] Similar trends have been reported across India and other low- and middle-income countries.[567] Scalds, particularly from hot milk and water, dominated in our series, consistent with prior studies that identify domestic hot liquids as the leading cause of pediatric burns [Figure 5].[456] This highlights the urgent need for parental education, safe kitchen practices, and restricted access of children to cooking areas.[7]

Most burns occurred in overcrowded or resource-limited households, where floor-level cooking and storage of hot liquids are common.[78] Comparable studies show that unsafe cooking environments and lack of supervision are recurrent hazards in rural and urban India.[56] Preventive strategies must therefore include infrastructural improvements, such as safer stoves, piped cooking gas, and improved housing design.[2]

Electrical burns (14%) were disproportionately high in older children, often due to contact with high-voltage power lines while playing on rooftops or flying kites. This pattern has also been documented in other Indian series.[89] While parental vigilance may reduce risks of low-voltage injuries, prevention of high-tension injuries requires municipal-level action, such as raising or insulating electrical lines.[2]

Seasonal variation showed a clear peak in winter and early spring (January–March), consistent with prior epidemiological data linking increased burn incidence to heating practices and use of boiling water during colder months.[39] Firecracker-related burns were observed during festival months, reflecting cultural influences on burn epidemiology.[9] These findings emphasize the need for seasonal preparedness in burn units and targeted community awareness campaigns during high-risk periods.

Late hospital presentation, often after treatment by untrained practitioners or “quacks,” was observed in 25 cases. Similar delays have been noted in other Indian studies, where reliance on traditional remedies contributes to worsened outcomes.[67] This underscores the importance of public education on early referral to specialized burn units.[2]

Although the majority of our patients had TBSA <15%, involvement of critical sites such as hands, trunk, buttocks, and genitalia posed significant challenges in terms of function and rehabilitation [Figure 6].[410] This pattern of injury reinforces the importance of specialized pediatric burn care, including splinting and long-term physiotherapy, to minimize contractures and disability.[3]

The limitations of our study include its short 1-year duration and focus on admitted patients only, excluding minor burns managed on an outpatient basis. We included a single year of data, as we did not feel that multi-year data were needed to validate seasonal variation. Nevertheless, our findings align closely with the existing literature and highlight urgent public health priorities for pediatric burn prevention and care in India.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the significant epidemiological burden of pediatric burns in a tertiary care center, with clear demographic and seasonal patterns. Children aged 1–5 years were most vulnerable, with scald burns from hot liquids emerging as the predominant cause, consistent with national and international data.[456] Male children were disproportionately affected, reflecting both behavioral and environmental exposures.[3]

Most burns occurred within the home environment, particularly in overcrowded or unsafe cooking settings, underscoring the urgent need for preventive interventions at the household level.[78] Seasonal peaks during winter and festive months further emphasize the importance of preparedness and targeted awareness programs.[39]

Although the majority of cases involved limited TBSA (<15%), the frequent involvement of critical sites such as hands and genitalia highlights the need for specialized pediatric burn care and rehabilitation services.[10] The high incidence of electrical burns among older children reflects infrastructural hazards that demand policy-level interventions, including safer power distribution and enforcement of municipal safety standards.[29]

Overall, pediatric burns remain largely preventable injuries. A multifaceted approach involving parental education, safe cooking practices, improved housing and electrical infrastructure, and early medical referral is essential to reduce the burden.[210] Strengthened healthcare systems and community-based preventive programs can substantially improve outcomes. Future research with longer follow-up periods should also address management outcomes, rehabilitation, and psychosocial impacts to provide a more holistic understanding of the burden of pediatric burns.[2]
