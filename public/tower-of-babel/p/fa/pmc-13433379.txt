Exploring factors that influence the implementation of presurgical smoking cessation interventions: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Eva Gavilan Castillo, Esteve Fernández, Jon Aritz Panera, Montse Ballbè, Laura Antón, Clara Mercader, Miquel Ferré, Helena Serrano, Cristina Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe aimed to evaluate the level of implementation of health care professionals in helping patients undergoing surgery quit smoking using the Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, and Arrange Follow-Up (5As) brief intervention model, and to identify the main barriers and facilitators affecting the implementation during preoperative visits at hospitals in Catalonia, Spain.MethodsIn a multicentre cross-sectional study, we administed an online survey to clinical health care professionals who perform preoperative visits (anesthesiologists, nurses, and psychologists). The main outcome variable was the implementation of 5As. We used the validated Knowledge, Attitudes, Behaviours, and Organization (KABO) questionnaire to assess barriers and facilitators. We performed univariate and bivariate analyses of the 5As and the KABO domains according to professional category and sociodemographic variables. We conducted multivariable analyses using generalized estimating equations controlling for covariates.ResultsThe survey was completed by 216 professionals of 633 invited to participate (34.1%). Engagement was high in the categories Ask (median [interquartile range] 0–10 Likert scale score, 10.0 [9.0–10.0]) and Advise (7.5 [5.0–10.0]), but very low in Assist (1.0 [0.0–3.0]) and Arrange Follow-Up (0.0 [0.0–2.0]). “Individual skills” and “organizational support/resources” had significantly lower scores compared with other domains (Holm-adjusted P < 0.05), whereas “Individual commitment” and “Beliefs about patients’ desire/readiness” had significantly higher scores.ConclusionsThe level of implementation of smoking cessation in preoperative visits was poor. The main barriers detected were lack of knowledge and lack of resources and organizational support. Hospitals should promote strategies that address the main barriers identified and should apply interventions to help people quit smoking as a routine practice in preoperative visits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12630-026-03158-8.

In all types of surgery, smoking is associated with an increased risk of mortality and general morbidity.1–4 Clinical trials have shown that presurgical interventions to help patients quit smoking increase their likelihood of giving up smoking in the long term and reduce surgical complications.5–8 The presurgical period is considered an ideal moment for promoting smoking cessation,9 which, as is well known, offers short- and long-term health benefits and helps reduce health care costs.10,11

Health care professionals responsible for preoperative visits have a crucial opportunity to intervene in the smoking behaviour of patients scheduled for surgery and thus improve their health.12,13 Nevertheless, previous research indicates that the assistance provided in these visits to encourage patients to stop smoking is insufficient.7,14,15 Most of the studies published date back more than 15 years and were carried out outside the European context, for example, in Japan,16 China,17 and the USA.14,18

Most treatment guidelines propose addressing smoking cessation using the Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, and Arrange Follow-Up (5As) brief intervention model, which consists of five steps.19 In the perioperative setting, the American Society of Anesthesiologists currently recommends a shorter three-step model called “Ask, Advise, and Connect,”20 which involves asking, advising, and referring (or connecting) smokers to expert professionals or services that promote smoking cessation, in this case before a surgical procedure.20,21

Outside the surgical domain, the barriers to promoting smoking cessation most frequently identified by professionals are 1) individual factors such as low motivation and confidence about proposing interventions, lack of time, and limited knowledge and training;14,16,17,22–25 2) cognitive factors such as preconceived ideas about the ineffectiveness of interventions;26 3) organizational barriers such as the lack of protocols;24,27 and 4) insufficient organizational support.28,29

Despite the recommendations and evidence available regarding the importance of intervening before surgery, little is known at present about professionals’ level of implementation in this area or about the factors that they perceive to be barriers or facilitators. The validated Knowledge, Attitudes, Behaviours, and Organization (KABO) questionnaire30 is a tool designed to assess the implementation level of smoking cessation interventions based on the 5As brief intervention and identify perceived barriers and facilitators among health care professionals for conducting these interventions. This questionnaire can be relevant for evaluating health care professionals’ interventions during presurgical visits. Although the briefer Ask, Advise, and Connect (3As) model is recommended in this context, it is inherently included and grounded in the broader 5As model.

Currently, the prevalence of smoking in the adult population (aged 15 yr or older) in Catalonia is 21.4%.31 A significant percentage of these individuals who smoke will be candidates for some type of surgery in their life course, presenting a unique opportunity for smoking cessation interventions. Nevertheless, the current level of implementation of smoking cessation interventions during presurgical visits in this region remains unknown to us. Thus, the aims of this study were 1) to evaluate the level of implementation of smoking cessation based on the 5As brief intervention model among health care professionals and 2) to identify the main barriers and facilitators for the implementation of this model in presurgical visits at the hospitals belonging to the Catalan Network of Smoke-Free Hospitals (CNSfH) (www.xchsf.cat).1

Methods

Design

In a multicentre cross-sectional study, we administered an online survey among professionals who carry out presurgical visits in Catalonia, Spain. The National Health System in Catalonia is a mixed health care model that integrates all available health care resources, whether publicly or privately (nonprofit organizations) owned, to guarantee high-quality public coverage services for the entire population (universal coverage). To achieve this, it contracts with various provider entities that directly deliver services to citizens. In this context, all patients scheduled for any surgical intervention indicated by surgeons from different specialties receive an optimization presurgical visit. This visit is primarily conducted face to face by a dedicated team of anesthesiologists, specialized nurses, and psychologists to ensure thorough patient optimization. This process is functionally and procedurally distinct from the surgeons’ specialist consultations, which focus exclusively on the indication and technical aspects of the procedure. The protocol of the study, including the prespecified statistical analysis plan, was approved by the Drug Research Ethics Committee of the Vall d’Hebron University Hospital in Barcelona, Spain (PR[AG]295/2022), and it complied with the recommendations of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist (STROBE)32 and the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS).33

Participants

We enrolled clinical health practitioners (anesthesiologists, nurses, or psychologists) who carry out presurgical visits at CNSfH member hospitals. The CNSfH members include almost all public, private, and nonprofit private hospitals (n = 89) (private institutions that offer public services funded by the Catalan Government). The CNSfH covers a region of slightly more than 8 million inhabitants.

All respondents gave informed consent prior to their participation.

Procedures

In January 2023, we sent a letter explaining the study by e-mail to the coordinators of the Smoke-Free Hospital Project of the 62 CNSfH member hospitals where surgery is performed, and their participation was requested. Specifically, we asked for permission to invite clinical health care professionals who conduct the presurgical visits to collaborate in the study. The decision to participate was taken by the Management and Ethics Committees of each of the health centres. Of the 62 hospitals, 44 agreed to participate, and 18 declined. Of those that agreed, 18 provided the corporate e-mail addresses of the professionals involved in the presurgical visits, and 26 decided to publicize the study internally and appointed a member of staff to recruit respondents. We invited 633 professionals to participate. The professionals were sent an e-mail in June 2023 with a brief description of the study’s objective and a link to provide informed consent and complete the questionnaire in Spanish language on the REDCap™ Research Electronic Data Capture platform (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA) (Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] eAppendix 1). To maximize the response rate, we sent two reinvitations: one in July and another in September 2023. Confidentiality and anonymity were guaranteed.

Variables

To evaluate health care professionals’ level of implementation in providing smoking cessation we used the brief intervention model of the 5As,19 in which each component or “A” was scored from 0 to 10 (0 = never to 10 = always). To assess the barriers and facilitators in the implementation of the intervention, the we we used the validated KABO questionnaire (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88).30 The KABO comprises seven domains with 26 items that are scored on a 0–10 Likert scale (0 = not at all to 10 = totally). The seven domains are 1) “individual skills” (maximum of 60 points), 2) “attitudes and beliefs” (maximum of 40 points), 3) “individual commitment” (maximum of 40 points), 4) “beliefs about the patient’s desire/readiness to quit smoking” (maximum of 30 points), 5) “positive support from the organization” (maximum of 30 points), 6) “organizational resources” (maximum of 40 points), and 7) “organizational endorsement” (maximum of 20 points). The score for each subscale is obtained from the sum of the corresponding items, with higher scores indicating a higher level of support and implementation of help (ESM eAppendices 1 and 2).

The questionnaire also includes questions related to 1) sociodemographic and individual characteristics of the practitioners, namely sex, age (recoded as < 45 or ≥ 45 yr), professional category (anesthesiologist, nurse, or other), years of professional experience (< 10, ≥ 10, < 20, or ≥ 20), smoker (yes/no), type of organization (public or nonprofit private hospital, private, or other), previous smoking cessation training (yes/no), level of education (bachelor’s degree, master’s or postgraduate, or other continuing education), and course delivery format (face-to-face or virtual) as well as 2) other factors related to the implementation of the smoking cessation intervention, such as technology level according to the Catalan Health Service Classification in basic general hospitals (cover the usual health care needs of the population), complementary basic hospitals (provide faster general and urgent care to the population, typically operating without inpatient services), regional reference hospitals (serve as referral centres for specific pathologies or groups of pathologies within a defined territorial area), high-tech hospitals (are at the top tier of the health system, offering supra-specialties as wells as advanced diagnostic and therapeutic technologies), and main work unit (outpatient clinic, operating room); work area (patient care only, patient care and others, or administration and others); and deployment of an Enhanced Recovery after Surgery (ERAS®) program (yes/no).34

We conducted a pilot test of the questionnaire and the delivery method with 10 health care professionals prior to launch to confirm the proper functioning of the survey link and evaluate platform accessibility.

Statistical analysis

The main variables of interest were the implementation of the 5As and the seven domains of the KABO instrument. We planned nonparametric tests for data that were not normally distributed. We performed univariate and bivariate analyses for the KABO instrument and for each of the seven domains, both according to professional category (anesthesiologists, nurses and others) and overall. We expressed the data as medians and interquartile ranges [IQRs] and applied the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test for their comparisons. We analyzed the level of implementation of the 5As according to the following variables: professional group, sociodemographic characteristics, individual professional characteristics, smoker/nonsmoker, organizational characteristics, and previous training. Similar to the KABO data, we computed medians [IQRs] and used the Kruskal–Wallis test to identify the differences between categories. In addition, we conducted multivariable analyses using generalized estimating equations (GEEs) clustered by hospital type and adjusted for sex, age group, and smoking status.

The main objective of these analyses was to determine which factors might influence the performance of the 5As components and the KABO domains. Univariate analyses described the distribution of all independent variables. Bivariate analyses (Wilcoxon and Kruskal–Wallis tests) explored which independent variables were associated with 5As scores and KABO domains. The standardized mean differences, together with the bivariate results, informed the selection of covariates for the multivariable models (ESM eTable 2). We performed the analyses with RStudio version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

We assessed data for normality using histograms and statistical tests. Given that most of the variables did not follow a normal distribution, we subsequently applied nonparametric tests.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Of the 633 professionals invited to participate, 216 (34.1%) completed the survey. Table 1 presents their sociodemographic characteristics according to professional group. The majority of the practitioners were anesthesiologists (73%), and the remaining 27% comprised 57 nurses and one psychologist. Of the respondents, 72% were female, around 50% had more than 20 years of work experience, and 85% were nonsmokers. Almost all were employed at public or nonprofit private hospitals (97%), and most of them were dedicated to patient care only (77%); an ERAS program was in place in 86% of these hospitals. Discussion

In our study, based on the 5As brief intervention model, we found a low overall level of implementation in promoting smoking cessation during presurgical visits in hospitals belonging to the CNSfH network in Catalonia, Spain. Although professionals at least asked and advised all patients about smoking, they did not assess their level of dependence, provide assistance, or refer their patients to other professionals or specialized services to quit smoking. An analysis of the domains that make up the KABO questionnaire indicated that the main barriers were the lack of individual skills, limited resources, and low level of support from organizations. Nevertheless, high levels of individual commitment were recorded, as well as high scores in beliefs about patients’ desire/readiness to quit smoking.

This study explored the level of performance of the 5As brief intervention model in the presurgical setting in a large European region. Our results are in line with those reported in previous studies carried out in other countries, in which surveys of professionals also indicated low levels of implementation in terms of promoting smoking cessation in presurgical visits.14,16,18 A study conducted in the USA14 found that 90% of anesthesiologists asked patients about their smoking habits during preoperative visits, but only 30% advised them to quit smoking. In Japan, 62% of anesthesiologists reported asking about tobacco consumption, but only 17% provided antismoking counselling.16 In two similar studies, 73% of anesthesiologists in China asked about smoking,17 as did 92% of anesthetic nurses in the USA,18 while 51% and 53%, respectively, proffered advice. Thus, health care professionals tend to ask patients about their tobacco consumption and offer advice, but hardly any of them provide assistance or arrange follow-up.

The present study identified the main barriers and facilitators affecting the implementation of the intervention in the specific context of presurgical visits in hospitals in Catalonia. Previous work in other countries has indicated that the main barriers to offering intervention reported by these professionals are the lack of training (47%,14 51%,16 73%,17 70%18) and the lack of time, reported as a barrier by around 65% in all studies.14,16–18 Nevertheless, the majority of health care professionals believe that promoting smoking cessation is part of their professional role, and more than 60% reported being motivated both to help their patients and to receive training. These results are also in consonance with the high scores obtained in the domains “individual commitment,” “attitudes and beliefs,” and “belief in the patient’s desire/readiness” assessed in our study.

The presurgical environment represents a unique opportunity to help smokers quit smoking,10 and the interventions have been shown to be beneficial from both a health perspective and an economic standpoint.11 For all these reasons, the “Ask, Advise, and Connect” strategy should be routinely incorporated into all presurgical visits in a protocolized manner.12 Nevertheless, as shown in our study, the levels of implementation are suboptimal and do not constitute a standard of care.

Currently, various programs and initiatives are in place to promote optimal results after surgical interventions. The most important at the international level are the ERAS programs.34 These accelerated recovery protocols incorporate multimodal perioperative strategies, including interventions to help patients quit smoking.35 The main objective is to reduce risk factors that may delay the patient’s recovery and lead to complications. Its implementation in Catalonia is widespread (in fact, 86.1% of health care professionals reported that it is applied at their centres). Nevertheless, it does not seem to have an impact on the overall application of the 5As, since the scores obtained by professionals at hospitals that implemented ERAS programs were similar to those that did not. The results show that the 5As are not fully applied, likely because professionals lack sufficient training or adequate resources and support from organizations. Among the gaps identified in a previous study are the limited availability of nicotine replacement therapy and the lack of protocols and records that facilitate the effective provision of smoking cessation support.36

Training broadens professionals’ familiarity with smoking cessation interventions and motivates them to increase their engagement and level of implementation.37,38 An earlier study in Catalonia found online training to have a positive impact, leading to an increase in assisting smokers and arranging follow-ups.22 Nevertheless, training alone is not enough, and resource-related organizational factors, such as protocols, records, and so on, also need to be improved.22,39

In this regard, the most recent report on tobacco use published by the World Health Organization promotes the implementation of policies that transform health care systems to better address tobacco use and nicotine dependence.40 Health systems must play their part by introducing organizational changes to ensure that tobacco addiction is systematically treated.41,42 A range of implementation strategies have been proposed in recent years. One example is the Ottawa Model,43 which has proven its effectiveness and has served as a framework for the introduction of smoking cessation programs at various hospitals and primary care centres based on the 5A or 3A approach.44,45 This model, which systematically identifies smokers and provides treatment and follow-up in a range of clinical settings, is based on making organizational changes before the implementation of the programs to ensure their viability in practice. These organizational changes include two key elements: an evidence-based action protocol and modifications of organizational practices, such as the training of professionals and the provision of resources such as documentation, guidelines, educational materials, and their integration into electronic clinical records. A recent study in Catalonia44 reported the effectiveness of the model in the hospital setting in terms of increasing the application of the 5As by professionals. Therefore, it seems appropriate and feasible to propose its use in the presurgical context, where professionals have already shown their predisposition and motivation; with adequate training and organizational support, this model may well prove successful.

Limitations

The main limitations of this study are those inherent in any survey administered to professionals, but it was the most feasible method for this study. The first is the low participation rate, as only 34% of the 633 possible participants responded, this response rate is in line with those reported in other studies based on surveys of health care professionals.45 The second limitation is the risk of selective participation bias, as the health care professionals who responded may be those with the highest level of motivation regarding tobacco control.

The main strength of this study is that health care professionals from 44 Catalan hospitals participated, all of them from the CNSfH, and from different levels of technology distributed throughout Catalonia. Therefore, we consider the results obtained to be representative of the reality in Catalonia. Another strong point is the use of a validated instrument, the KABO questionnaire, which has high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88) and has been successfully applied in previous studies with other professionals in the hospital context.30

Conclusions

Our results show that the levels of implementation of the 5As smoking cessation intervention model in presurgical visits at hospitals in the CNSfH did not reach the desired levels. Professionals viewed the intervention positively, but individual skills and organizational support are areas that need improvement if the systematic identification and treatment of smokers in this context are to be achieved. Therefore, it is necessary to formalize implementation strategies that address the main barriers identified, such as the lack of training, organizational resources, and support for interventions to promote smoking cessation as a routine practice in preoperative visits.
