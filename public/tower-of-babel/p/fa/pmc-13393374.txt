From Simplified Pretreatment to Sensitive Analysis: An Immobilization-Free Cathodic Photoelectrochemical Assay for Sulfonylurea Herbicide Residue and Its Accumulation
Authors: Kaidi Zhan, Yaxiling Wang, Jing Lin, Fengxu Chen, Xia Fan, Peiwen Hu, Jiangwen Wen, Fangduanyang Gong, Nan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sensitive and selective detection of agricultural chemicals in complex matrices necessitates streamlined workflows without performance loss. By combining the inherent high sensitivity of photoelectrochemical (PEC) analysis with the high selectivity of enzymatic recognition, an immobilization-free PEC assay for the sulfonylurea herbicide chlorsulfuron (Chs) was developed, with purification steps omitted and analytical procedure simplified. Specifically, BiOI nanoflakes (NFs)/NiO heterojunction was fabricated as a sensing platform, generating strong and stable cathodic photocurrent in the presence of dissolved O2. The catalytic reaction of acetolactate synthase (ALS) consumed O2, quenching the photocurrent response. Conversely, upon addition of the model target Chs, a representative sulfonylurea herbicide and effective inhibitor of ALS, the signal was recovered. By monitoring the photocurrent change before and after the enzymatic reaction, Chs was quantified over a linear range of 0.25–100 μg/L, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.16 μg/L. The assay avoids laborious probe-immobilization steps by directly adding the enzyme into the electrolyte, significantly simplifying the detection protocol. Furthermore, by omitting traditional purification procedures, the overall workflow was further optimized. The method was successfully applied to detect Chs residues in wheat powder and to evaluate its accumulation in wheat seedlings, with accuracy and reliability validated by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). This work achieves highly sensitive and immobilization-free PEC analysis of Chs and offers a feasible approach for sulfonylurea herbicide detection in selected agricultural samples, with streamlined pretreatment process and enhanced analytical efficiency.

Introduction

Sulfonylurea herbicides constitute a major class of herbicides globally, representing approximately 11% of the worldwide herbicide market due to their high herbicidal efficacy, excellent crop selectivity, and low application rates. Their mode of action involves systemic translocation within plants, where they inhibit ALS, a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of branched-chain amino acids such as valine and leucine. However, the high solubility and polarity of these compounds also confer significant environmental mobility, raising concerns over ecological and health risks. A representative example is Chs, the first commercially introduced sulfonylurea herbicide. Its pronounced solubility and chemical stability contribute to persistent environmental residues. Studies indicate that Chs can adversely affect nontarget organisms by inhibiting the growth of algae, fish, and crustaceans, causing irritation to mammalian skin and mucous membranes, and even inducing acute poisoning in humans. Moreover, its extended soil residual activity may impair germination and growth of subsequent crops, resulting in carryover injury within crop rotation systems. In light of these risks, regulatory measures have been implemented in various regions. For instance, China has prohibited the use of Chs and established a stringent maximum residue limit (MRL) of 0.01 mg/kg in food commodities (GB2763–2026). Such regulatory rigor highlights the urgent need for the development of facile, sensitive, and reliable analytical methods to monitor sulfonylurea herbicide residues in environmental and agricultural matrices.

Conventional analytical methods for detecting sulfonylurea herbicides include HPLC, capillary electrophoresis, inductively coupled plasma tandem mass spectrometry, liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and multicolloidal gold immunochromatography assay. While being widely used, these techniques often require expensive instrumentation, involve complex sample pretreatment, and may suffer from limited analytical sensitivity. PEC analysis has emerged as a dynamically evolving field that attracts considerable research interest due to its intrinsic advantages, such as high sensitivity, low background signal, cost-effectiveness, and ease of miniaturization. PEC sensing has demonstrated broad design flexibility and promising applications across various fields, including disease diagnosis, environmental pollutant monitoring, and food safety inspection. Several elegant PEC designs have also been reported for herbicide residue analysis. For instance, Swainsbury et al. developed a biohybrid PEC cell for triazides atrazine and terbutryn based on their inhibition of photosynthesis process of purple bacteria. Utilizing aptamer as both a recognition element and signal switcher, Zhang et al. realized the sensitive detection of atrazine with a detection limit of 0.1 pM. Our group previously constructed a PEC sensor for glyphosate by exploiting its inhibitory effect on the peroxidase-mimicking Fe3O4 NPs and three-dimensional polymer phenylethnylcopper/nitrogen-doped graphene aerogel electrode. Despite these advances, reports on PEC analysis targeting sulfonylurea herbicide residues remain scarce, indicating a significant gap and opportunity in this research area.

To enable effective recognition-dependent charge transfer, conventional PEC sensors generally rely on tedious and reagent-intensive immobilization of recognition probessuch as aptamers, peptides, enzymes, or molecularly imprinted polymersonto electrode surfaces. − These procedures not only reduce analytical throughput but may also impair detection accuracy due to nonspecific adsorption and interbatch variability. In response, immobilization-free PEC strategies have been developed to streamline the assay workflow. These approaches allow the recognition and signal amplification steps to take place directly in the electrolyte while maintaining high analytical sensitivity. In 2016, Hou et al. reported the first immobilization-free PEC platform for detecting acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and its inhibitor aldicarb, which was based on AChE-mediated in situ generation and electrostatic adsorption of photoactive CdS onto the electrode. Subsequently, the same group introduced a diffusion-dependent signaling method employing methylene blue for microRNA analysis. Our team has also developed an immobilization-free PEC protocol for kojic acid detection in food samples, leveraging tyrosinase-mediated sensitization of TiO2 nanoparticles. Despite these advances, the design space for facile and generalizable immobilization-free PEC assays applicable to diverse analytes remains largely unexplored, presenting both a challenge and an opportunity for future sensor development.

Pretreatment is essential for the accurate and sensitive detection of agrochemicals in complex, real-world matrices. Conventional analytical methods for pesticides and herbicides typically involve laborious extraction and purification steps, followed by chromatographic detection. Although magnetic solid-phase extraction can simplify this workflow by enabling straightforward adsorption, magnetic separation, and subsequent analyte desorption, , the design, fabrication, and selectivity of the corresponding materials remain challenging. In contrast, PEC analysis offers inherently high sensitivity and selectivity, making it possible to mitigate matrix interference through simple sample dilution. Together with specific enzymatic recognition, the reliable detection of trace analytes could be ensured. Consequently, time-consuming purification steps may be omitted without compromising the analytical performance.

Herein, we present an immobilization-free cathodic PEC assay for the detection of sulfonylurea herbicide Chs in wheat, with simplified sample pretreatment, as illustrated in Scheme . The sensing platform was constructed based on a BiOI NFs/NiO heterojunction, which exhibited a strong and stable cathodic photocurrent under light irradiation in the presence of dissolved O2. When the enzyme ALS was introduced into the electrolyte, it catalytically consumed dissolved O2, leading to a pronounced quenching of the photocurrent. Chs, a representative sulfonylurea herbicide, acted as an ALS inhibitor; its presence led to enzymatic activity loss, thereby recovering the photocurrent signal. By monitoring the photocurrent change of the BiOI NFs/NiO electrode before and after the enzymatic reaction, Chs can be quantified in a linear detection range of 0.25–100 μg/L with a LOD of 0.16 μg/L. This protocol was applied in the detection of Chs residue in wheat powder, as well as both residual and accumulation levels in wheat seedlings, demonstrating its potential for monitoring herbicide residues in food and assessing their accumulation in crops. Owing to the high intrinsic sensitivity of PEC detection and the selectivity afforded by the enzymatic recognition step, conventional purification procedures could be omitted from the workflow. The accuracy of the proposed method was confirmed through comparison with a standard HPLC protocol. This work achieved highly sensitive and immobilization-free PEC analysis of Chs, with significantly streamlined pretreatment process and enhanced overall analytical efficiency, and offers a feasible approach for sulfonylurea herbicide detection in real agricultural samples.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Instruments

FTO conductive glass was purchased from Zhuhai Kaiwei Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd. Nickel nitrate, hexamethylenetetramine, and ethanol were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Hydrochloric acid was sourced from Nanjing Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Bismuth iodide and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Sodium pyruvate (SP) was acquired from J&K. Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) was procured from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl2·6H2O) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were supplied by Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. ALS was obtained from Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were recorded using a Hitachi Regulus 8100 scanning electron microscope. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectrum was characterized by Rigaku Miniflex 600. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were recorded by a FEI TalosF200X G2 transmission electron microscope. The UV–vis diffuse-reflectance spectra were obtained on a Shimadzu UV-3600 UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on a PHI5000 VersaProbe spectrometer (ULVAC-PHI Co., Japan). PEC measurements were performed with a homemade PEC system: 5 W LED lamp with 410 nm light was used as an irradiation source. Photocurrent was measured on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation (China) with a three-electrode system: a prepared electrode with a geometric circular area (0.5 cm in diameter) as the working electrode, a Pt wire as the counter electrode, a saturated Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode. All PEC measurements were performed at 0 V and using PBS (pH 7.4) as the electrolyte. HPLC experiments were carried out on a Waters HPLC system (Waters Co., USA) equipped with a 2489 dual-wavelength UV detector, a 515 quantitative infusion pump, and a 7725i manual injector.

Electrode Preparation

Based on the method described in the reference with minor modifications (Lepleux et al., 2009), NiO material was synthesized on FTO conductive glass substrates via a hydrothermal method. Specifically, the purchased FTO conductive glass was cut into pieces measuring 3.3 cm × 0.7 cm. These pieces were then sequentially ultrasonicated in acetone, anhydrous ethanol containing 1 mol/L NaOH, ultrapure water, anhydrous ethanol, and ultrapure water for 20 min each. After cleaning, the substrates were dried at 60 °C and stored for subsequent use.

A green solution was prepared by dissolving 1.816 g of Ni­(NO3)2 and 0.8762 g of hexamethylenetetramine in 25 mL of purified water, resulting in a concentration of 0.25 mol/L for each reagent. The mixture was stirred for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution. Then, 12 mL of the resulting solution was transferred into a 100 mL polytetrafluoroethylene-lined autoclave. The cleaned FTO glass was placed into the solution, with the conductive side facing upward and leaning against the wall of the liner at an angle. The autoclave was placed in an oven and maintained at 90 °C for 15 min. The FTO electrode coated with a NiO thin film was then rinsed three times with purified water, air-dried, and annealed in a tube furnace at 300 °C for 30 min.

The BiOI NFs/NiO electrode was prepared based on a reported method with some modifications. Briefly, 0.4717 g of BiI3 was added to 10 mL of anhydrous ethanol containing 0.08 mol/L HCl and sonicated to obtain a clear red solution. Then, 600 μL of this solution was pipetted into a 2 mL centrifuge tube. The NiO electrode was immersed in the solution and kept at room temperature for 40 min. The resulting electrode was then directly transferred to an oven and dried at 80 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of purified water was added to a 100 mL beaker and heated until slightly boiling. The dried electrode was placed against the inner wall of the beaker and maintained in the boiling water for 10 min. After the electrode surface turned light reddish-brown, it was allowed to cool and dry naturally at ambient temperature.

Sample Detection

PEC Detection of ALS Activity

First, a substrate solution was prepared by sequentially dissolving 0.023 g TPP, 0.0015 g FAD, 0.002 g MgCl2, and 0.0242 g SP in 100 mL of PBS (pH = 7.4). From this solution, 7 mL was aliquoted to measure the baseline photocurrent of the BiOI NFs/NiO electrode, denoted as I0. The substrate solution was then transferred to a centrifuge tube, and 100 μL of ALS enzyme solutions at varying concentrations were added. The tube was sealed with a sealing film to isolate it from ambient air and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. After incubation, the photocurrent of the BiOI NFs/NiO electrode was immediately measured again in the resulting reaction solution, denoted as I.

PEC Detection of Chs Concentration

A stock solution of Chs at 100 mg/L was prepared in methanol and subsequently diluted with purified water to achieve lower concentrations. Then, 100 μL of Chs solutions at varying concentrations were mixed with 100 μL of the enzyme solution. The detection procedure was the same as described in Section .

In the aforementioned PEC detection process, each sample was measured three times with three independent BiOI NFs/NiO electrodes, and the average value was used as the final data.

HPLC Detection of Chs Concentration

The chromatographic conditions for sample analysis were as follows: mobile phase consisting of acetonitrile and water at a ratio of 35:65 (v/v) containing 0.04% phosphoric acid, flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, ID, 5 μm), detection wavelength of 240 nm, column temperature at room temperature, and injection volume of 20 μL. For the establishment of the Chs standard curve, standard solutions with concentrations of 0.05 mg/L, 0.1 mg/L, 0.5 mg/L, 1 mg/L, 5 mg/L, and 10 mg/L were prepared in chromatographic-grade acetonitrile. Under the above conditions, the correlation coefficient R 2 of the calibration curve was 0.9999.

Detection of Chs in Wheat Seedlings

Material Cultivation

Plump wheat seeds were selected and sterilized by immersion in a 5% NaClO solution for 30 min. They were then rinsed thoroughly with tap water, followed by purified water until no NaClO residue remained. The seeds were placed in a Petri dish lined with a moist filter paper and germinated in the dark at 25 °C for 24 h. Germinated seeds were sown in 500 mL polyethylene pots, each containing 100 g of soil. The plants were grown under a 14 h photoperiod with day/night temperatures of 25 °C/20 °C and a light intensity of 300 μmol·m–2·s–1. The soil was maintained at 70% water-holding capacity. After 14 days of cultivation, the seedlings were harvested for further analysis.

Results and Discussion

Structural Characterization of the BiOI NFs/NiO Electrode

First of all, the BiOI NFs/NiO heterojunction was constructed as the photoactive material, leveraging the high charge-carrier accessibility of NiO and the broad visible-light absorption of BiOI. As shown in Figure A, the as-synthesized NiO displayed a thin, rough nanoflake-like morphology. After being deposited on the electrode, it formed a highly interconnected three-dimensional framework composed of densely packed nanosheets (Figure B). This porous architecture facilitated the subsequent growth of BiOI. Following BiOI modification, the original NiO surface was covered, and the resulting heterojunction electrode exhibited randomly oriented BiOI NFs (Figure C). The TEM images of the heterojunction confirmed the coexistence of BiOI NFs and NiO, indicating the successful integration of the two components (Figure D). A clear interface between NiO (lighter region) and BiOI (darker region) NFs is visible in Figure E. The BiOI region shows well-defined lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.284 nm, corresponding to the (110) crystal plane of BiOI (Figure F).

(G) XRD patterns of the NiO and BiOI NFs/NiO electrodes. (H) XPS survey spectra of the NiO and BiOI NFs/NiO electrodes. (I) High-resolution XPS spectra of O 1s of the NiO and BiOI NFs/NiO electrodes.

The XRD pattern of the NiO electrode and BiOI NFs/NiO electrode is presented in Figure G. The diffraction peaks observed at 26.61°, 33.89°, 37.95°, 51.78°, 54.76°, 61.87°, 65.94°, 78.71°, and 81.14° were assigned to SnO2 (JCPDS no. 41-1445), originating from the FTO substrate. The characteristic peaks at 37.26°, 43.30°, and 62.89° belonged to the (111), (200), and (220) crystal planes of NiO (JCPDS no. 78-0643), confirming the successful synthesis of NiO on the electrode. After the deposition of BiOI NFs, additional peaks at 9.66°, 19.36°, 29.65°, 31.66°, 39.37°, 45.38°, and 55.15° were detected, corresponding to the (001), (002), (102), (110), (004), (200), and (212) crystal planes of tetragonal BiOI (JCPDS no. 10-0445). Notably, diffraction peaks attributable to NiO were no longer detected. This absence can be attributed to the limited penetration depth of X-rays, which were largely absorbed by the overlying BiOI layer, thereby attenuating the signal from the underlying NiO.

To gain further insight into the elemental composition of the electrode, XPS was conducted. The survey spectra of the NiO and BiOI NFs/NiO electrodes are shown in Figure H, with the corresponding atomic percentages summarized in Table S1. Prior to BiOI modification, the NiO electrode consisted primarily of Ni (37.45 at %) and O (41.91 at %). Following the deposition of BiOI, distinct signals for Bi and I appeared, with atomic concentrations of 18.51% and 21.69%, respectively, while the contents of Ni and O decreased significantly to 8.82% and 23.89%. The high-resolution XPS spectra of the O 1s region provided additional chemical-state information. As displayed in Figure I, the O 1s spectrum of the pristine NiO electrode could be deconvoluted into two components at binding energies of 529.0 and 530.7 eV, assigned to lattice oxygen (O–Ni bonds) and surface-adsorbed hydroxyl groups (adsorbed oxygen), respectively. After BiOI modification, the O 1s profile shifted and changed in shape. The spectrum was fitted with two peaks centered at 529.7 and 531.1 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen in BiOI (O–Bi bonds) and adsorbed oxygen species. The distinct difference in the O 1s spectra before and after BiOI deposition further verifies the formation of the BiOI NFs/NiO heterojunction.

Moreover, contact angle measurements were performed to evaluate the changes in surface wettability after each modification step. As shown in Figure S1, the water contact angles of the bare FTO, NiO, and BiOI NFs/NiO electrodes were 87.40°, 10.80°, and 34.56°, respectively. The moderate hydrophilicity of the BiOI NFs/NiO electrode would be beneficial for the penetration of the aqueous electrolyte, which in turn facilitated the following photocurrent generation.

PEC Characterization of the BiOI NFs/NiO Electrode

To assess the PEC performance of the BiOI NFs/NiO heterojunction, its optical absorption properties were first examined by UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS). As displayed in Figure A, the pure NiO electrode showes minimal absorption in the visible region (>400 nm), while the BiOI NFs/NiO composite electrode exhibits markedly enhanced light absorption across the visible range (400–600 nm). Band gap energies (E g) were further determined via Tauc plot analysis of UV–vis DRS data. , As shown in Figure B, the E g values of NiO and BiOI NFs/NiO were 3.60 and 1.97 eV, respectively. The narrower band gap of the latter confirmed the successful formation of the BiOI NFs/NiO heterojunction and indicated its potential for enhanced PEC response under visible light.

The PEC responses of the electrodes were subsequently evaluated under intermittent 410 nm illumination. As shown in Figure C, the bare FTO electrode did not demonstrate obvious photocurrent (curve a). The pristine NiO electrode generated a weak cathodic photocurrent of about 0.08 μA (curve b). After modification with BiOI NFs, the composite electrode displayed a pronounced increase in absolute value of photocurrent, reaching 1.21 μA (curve c), approximately 15-fold higher than that of pure NiO. This significant enhancement can be ascribed to the strong visible-light-harvesting ability of narrow-band-gap BiOI, along with its favorable energy-level alignment with NiO, which collectively promote the formation of a type-II heterojunction. Such a structure effectively facilitates charge separation, accelerates the transport of photogenerated electrons, and suppresses electron–hole recombination. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) Nyquist plots were utilized to investigate the charge-transfer resistance (R ct). As shown in Figure S2, R ct followed the order: NiO > BiOI NFs/NiO > bare FTO. The high R ct value of NiO/FTO indicates poor conductivity of NiO, while the decreased R ct after BiOI modification confirms that the BiOI NFs/NiO heterojunction facilitates interfacial charge transfer.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the cathodic photocurrent generation, XPS valence spectrum was employed to elucidate the band structure of BiOI NFs/NiO. As demonstrated in Figure D, the valence band (VB) of NiO was estimated to be 0.55 V; combined with the band gap of 3.60 eV, the conduction band (CB) position of NiO was derived as −3.05 V. Similarly, the VB and CB of BiOI NFs/NiO were determined as 0.80 V and −1.17 V, respectively, which were consistent with previous report. Furthermore, since the BiOI NFs/NiO conduction-band potential (−1.17 V) was more negative than the O2/O2 ·‑ redox potential (−0.33 V vs NHE), O2 molecules could act as electron acceptors (Figure S3). To further validate this hypothesis experimentally, a deoxygenation experiment was performed by purging the electrolyte with N2. As shown in Figure E, the photocurrent decreased progressively with an extended deoxygenation time. After 40 min, the cathodic photocurrent dropped from 1.21 μA to 0.17 μA, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 86.0% (photocurrent response demonstrated as curve d in Figure C). This observation confirms the essential role of dissolved O2 in sustaining the photocurrent. Under light irradiation, electrons in the heterojunction were photoexcited, separated from holes, and migrated to the electrode/electrolyte interface. Dissolved O2 acted as an efficient electron acceptor in the electrolyte, promoting charge transfer and thereby generating a strong and stable cathodic photocurrent (Figure F). In contrast, when dissolved O2 was removed, electron–hole recombination increases significantly, leading to a substantial decline in the photocurrent.

Detection of ALS and Chs Concentrations

ALS is a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of branched-chain amino acids (valine, leucine, and isoleucine) and serves as the primary target of sulfonylurea herbicides. Leveraging the specific inhibitory effect of these herbicides on ALS, taking Chs as a representative analyte, together with the O2-sensitive BiOI NFs/NiO photoelectrode, a detection protocol for sulfonylurea herbicide was developed. Briefly, the cathodic photocurrent of the BiOI NFs/NiO electrode was first measured in a SP substrate solution (denoted as I0). ALS was then introduced into the electrolyte, the cell was sealed, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 45 min before the photocurrent was recorded again (denoted as I). As shown in Figure A, after the enzymatic reaction, the photocurrent decreased from 1.2 μA to 0.24 μA (curve a to b), corresponding to a reduction of 80%. This quenching effect was attributed to the oxygenase activity of ALS. In contrast, when 1 mg/L Chs was present in the reaction system, the cathodic photocurrent after the enzymatic catalysis step only declined to 1.05 μA, representing a decrease of about 12.5% relative to I0. This attenuated response results from the inhibition effect of ALS by Chs, which partially occupies the substrate-binding pocket of the enzyme and thus significantly retards O2 consumption.

To further validate the origin of the signal changes, control experiments were carried out. Different components involved in the enzymatic reactionincluding SP, TPP, FAD, MgCl2, ALS, Chs, and their combinationswere separately introduced into the electrolyte, and the corresponding photocurrents were recorded (Figure B). A pronounced decrease in photocurrent was observed only when SP, cofactors (TPP, FAD, MgCl2), and ALS were all present (column k). In contrast, the addition of Chs significantly attenuated this photocurrent drop (column l). Moreover, when the reacted mixture was re-exposed to air to allow O2 redissolution, the photocurrent recovered to near its initial level in subsequent PEC measurements (column m). This result provided direct experimental support for the proposed mechanism that the photocurrent decrease stemmed from enzymatic O2 consumption. On this basis, Chs acted as an ALS inhibitor, reducing O2 depletion and thereby preserving the photocurrent signal.

To optimize the activity of ALS in the assay, the correlation between ALS activity and the rate of photocurrent change was examined (Figure C). As the ALS activity increased from 1.25 IU/L to 1250 IU/L, the absolute value of photocurrent response decreased from 1.20 μA to 0.24 μA. This decline was attributed to the O2-consuming oxygenase activity of ALS, which lowered the dissolved O2 level in the electrolyte. Above 1250 IU/L, however, the photocurrent remained nearly constant, indicating that 1250 IU/L of ALS was sufficient to deplete the available O2 under the experimental conditions. Therefore, an ALS activity of 1250 IU/L was selected for all subsequent experiments. Furthermore, a linear relationship was observed between the photocurrent change, (I – I 0)/I 0, and ALS activity over the range of 1.25–1250 IU/L (Figure D). The fitted regression equation was (I – I 0)/I 0 = −0.00047 CALS −0.16361 (R 2 = 0.997), confirming the potential of the proposed PEC assay for the quantitative evaluation of ALS activity.

Leveraging the inhibitory effect of sulfonylurea herbicide on ALS activity, a detection method for sulfonylurea herbicide was developed, taking Chs as a representative analyte. As shown in Figure E, the cathodic photocurrent increased progressively from 0.30 μA to 1.05 μA as the Chs concentration raised from 0.25 μg/L to 100 μg/L, while the corresponding photocurrent change rate (I – I 0)/I 0 decreased accordingly from −0.746 to −0.124 (Figure F). Above 100 μg/L, the photocurrent reached a plateau, indicating saturation of the inhibition effect. A linear relationship was observed between photocurrent change and the logarithm of Chs concentration over the range of 0.25–100 μg/L (Figure F), fitted by the equation (I – I 0)/I 0 = 0.23407 log CChs + 0.09395 (R 2 = 0.998). The LOD was determined by measuring the blank signal (without Chs) five times, calculating the mean (−0.80) and standard deviation (σ = 0.0018). The signal threshold for LOD was set as mean +3σ. This threshold value was then inserted into the calibration curve equation to back-calculate the corresponding concentration, which yielded an LOD of 0.16 μg/L. Moreover, the performance of this method with respect to the detection range and LOD was better than or comparable to that of several previously reported methods, as indicated in Table S2. Reproducibility of the assay was assessed by calculating the interassay relative standard deviation (RSD) for five independent electrodes using 100 μg/L Chs, which yielded an RSD of 1.4%.

To evaluate the specificity of the proposed method, PEC responses were recorded in the presence of various pesticides and herbicides (each at 1 mg/L). As shown in Figure S4, organophosphorus insecticides (chlorpyrifos and triazophos) and nonsulfonylurea herbicides (mesotrione and atrazine) produced negligible recovery of the photocurrent (signal changes of around −0.80). In contrast, sulfonylurea herbicides elicited pronounced signal recovery. For example, at 1 mg/L, metsulfuron-methyl, sulfosulfuron, and tribenuron-methyl gave signal changes of −0.455, −0.568, and −0.580, respectively. These responses were attributed to their shared mechanism of ALS inhibition, confirming the potential of this platform for detecting other sulfonylurea herbicides. Notably, Chs gave a signal change of −0.122, which restored the photocurrent to the greatest extent. This indicated that the sensor exhibited the highest sensitivity toward Chs among the tested sulfonylurea herbicides. The differential recovery may be ascribed to variations in the binding affinity of each sulfonylurea herbicide to ALS, maybe due to differences in their molecular structures that affect the interaction with the enzyme. Nonetheless, the clear response to all sulfonylureas underscores the method’s utility as a class-selective screening tool for ALS-inhibiting herbicides, with Chs serving as the most responsive model analyte.

Detection of Chs in Real Samples

Wheat fields are the primary sites for the application of sulfonylurea herbicides; therefore, wheat flour and wheat seedlings were selected as matrices to validate the feasibility of the proposed method for real sample analysis. HPLC was simultaneously used as a reference.

As for wheat flour, different concentrations of Chs were spiked into the flour to reach concentrations of 0.05 mg/kg, 0.1 mg/kg, and 1 mg/kg. The samples then underwent an extraction and purification process by the HPLC method and analyzed by HPLC; on the other hand, solutions after purification were diluted 100 times and then tested by PEC. In the HPLC method, no Chs was detected in the blank wheat flour sample. The recovery rates for the three spiked concentrations were 94.4%, 94.1%, and 94.4%, with RSDs of 3.06%, 5.07%, and 2.36%, respectively, validating the accuracy of the HPLC method. On the other hand, the purified extract was diluted 100-fold and analyzed using the PEC detection method established in this study, with the results summarized in Table . No Chs was detected in the blank sample. Owing to the low detection limit and anti-interference ability of the proposed PEC assay, direct determination of Chs accumulation in wheat seedlings was achieved using diluted unpurified extracts. Under Chs treatments of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 mg/kg, the accumulation levels detected by this method were 0.017, 0.038, 0.061, and 0.17 mg/kg. These findings demonstrated that as the Chs application rate increases, its accumulation in wheat plants also gradually increased, which was correlated to a typical bioaccumulation effect. The relatively high residue levels of Chs also suggested the potential bioaccumulation risks toward rotational crops and also indicated the necessity of Chs detection. In comparison, extracts were purified and then evaluated by HPLC, and the accumulations were determined as 0.018, 0.037, 0.063, and 0.16 mg/kg, respectively, which were consistent with the results measured by PEC (Figure E). The PEC method developed in this study exhibited excellent accuracy and reproducibility, making it suitable for the specific and quantitative detection of Chs in complex matrices.

Conclusions

In this work, a sensitive, immobilization-free PEC assay was developed for the detection of Chs, featuring simplified pretreatment and a straightforward operational workflow. The sensing platform was constructed using a BiOI NFs/NiO heterojunction, whose cathodic photocurrent was directly proportional to the concentration of dissolved O2. The enzyme ALS catalyzed an O2-consuming oxygenase reaction, which depleted dissolved O2 and consequently suppressed the PEC signal. When the sulfonylurea herbicide Chsa typical ALS inhibitorwas introduced, enzymatic O2 consumption was reduced, leading to signal recovery. By monitoring the photocurrent change before and after the enzymatic reaction, Chs could be quantified over a linear range of 0.25–100 μg/L, with a LOD of 0.16 μg/L. The assay eliminated laborious probe-immobilization steps by directly introducing the enzyme into the electrolyte, substantially simplifying the detection protocol. Moreover, by omitting conventional purification procedures, the overall workflow was further streamlined. The method was successfully applied to determine Chs residues in wheat flour and to assess both residual and accumulation levels in wheat seedlings, with the results validated by HPLC. This work demonstrated a highly sensitive, immobilization-free PEC strategy for Chs analysis and offers a feasible approach for sulfonylurea herbicide detection in selected agricultural samples.
