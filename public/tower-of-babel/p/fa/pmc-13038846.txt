Machine learning-guided rational engineering of ACE2-derived peptides for broad-spectrum neutralization of SARS-CoV-2 variants
Authors: Mubarak A. Alamri, Abdullah S. Alawam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The dynamic mutational landscape of SARS-CoV-2, especially within the spike glycoprotein's RBD, continues to undermine the effectiveness of available therapeutics. Although ACE2-derived peptides have previously been explored as spike inhibitors, their rational optimization has largely lacked systematic, data-driven selection strategies. This article describes an integrative computer-aided approach involving molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and machine learning-assisted peptide engineering to engineer potent peptide-based inhibitors targeting the RBD–ACE2 interface. Molecular dynamics simulations of wild-type and variant RBD–ACE2 complexes were first employed to identify energetically unfavorable interfacial residues, enabling the rational extraction and refinement of a 19-residue ACE2-derived peptide. A supervised regression model of the type XGBoost, trained using experimentally obtained peptide-protein affinity matrices (R2 = 0.6958), estimated binding potentials for 54 synthetically designed peptide variants. The best lead, M1 (HAHTFLETFNYEAQTLSYE), exhibited improved docking energies (–257.35 kcal/mol for Omicron and –234.24 kcal/mol for wild-type) and improved free-binding energies (–35.67 kcal/mol and –21.63 kcal/mol, respectively), relative to the native peptide, with binding energies comparable to or exceeding reported RBD–hACE2 interaction benchmarks (− 13 to − 18 kcal/mol). Molecular dynamics analyses further confirmed enhanced conformational stability, sustained hydrogen bonding, and favourable energetic landscapes for the M1–RBD complexes. Collectively, this study demonstrates that integrating energetic decomposition with ML-driven peptide optimization provides a scalable and mechanistically informed strategy for developing peptide-based inhibitors against rapidly evolving viral pathogens.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44446-026-00073-3.

Introduction

SARS coronaviruses are just one of many viruses that undergo natural and common evolution through genetic mutations (COVID-19). Due to evolutionary adaptations brought about by mutations, SARS-CoV-2 has persistently shown up as different variants. As of September 24, 2023, more than 500 million individuals have been infected, and over 6 million have died as a direct consequence of the variant found in the SARS-CoV-2 epidemic (Kannan et al. 2022). Vaccines and treatments have been greatly advanced by the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries in response to the ever-evolving changes in SARS-CoV-2 (Chakraborty et al. 2023; Dubey et al. 2021). However, the effectiveness of such treatments against emerging variants remains a critical concern, as some variants are demonstrating the ability to evade neutralizing antibodies generated by existing vaccines, rendering pandemic control more challenging (Jha et al. 2021; Rajpoot et al. 2022; Tareq et al. 2021). The evolving strain of the COVID-19 virus has presented unique and significant obstacles to public health interventions during the pandemic, of which the major variant of concern was the delta variant. This is due to the variant's ability to spread effectively, causing more severe illness (CDC 2020). As such, these variants have spread in many countries worldwide as they can easily be transmitted and can cause severe symptoms.

Due to the serious health complications observed from COVID-19 infections (Hossain et al. 2021; Kumar et al. 2021), researchers are increasingly focused on protein–protein interaction inhibitors for their properties that might be effective inhibitors of the SARS-CoV-2 major protease (Mpro) (Chowdhury et al. 2020). Inhibitors from traditional medicinal plants have shown antiviral activity against various pathogens (Tallei et al. 2021), such as compounds derived from Tinospora crispa and Avicennia officinalis, which have been examined for their capability to impede COVID-19 Mpro using molecular docking experiments (Mahmud et al. 2021; Rakib et al. 2020). Furthermore, COVID-19 poses a serious threat to persons who are immunocompromised, as infection can lead to damage to many organs simultaneously (Rabaan et al. 2021). As such, vaccine research and public health policy must begin with a thorough understanding of the mechanisms of SARS-CoV-2 reinfection (Nainu et al. 2020). This can be achieved by studying the virus's ability to mutate as well as the host's immunological response. The need for constant modifications to vaccine formulations due to the virus's fast pace of mutation is a contested topic (Cele et al. 2022; Planas et al. 2022). The World Health Organisation (WHO) identified five SARS-CoV-2 variations, which have been labelled as Variants of Concern (VOCs). These VOCs are as follows: Alpha (B.1.1.7), Beta (B.1.351), Gamma (P.1), and Delta (B.1.617.2) are the alphabetically preceding members of the most recent addition, Omicron (B.1.1.529) (Aleem et al. 2025). Mutations, such as D614G, which enhance the infectious rate of the virus, have been identified via studies on several SARS-CoV-2 variations (Korber et al. 2020; Yurkovetskiy et al. 2020). The variant of Omicron was classified as a VOC because of its elevated risk of reinfection, extensive mutational profile, and increased transmissibility (Saxena et al. 2022). The rapid global spread of the Omicron variant has prompted concerns about the potential impact on vaccinated individuals. Furthermore, the spike protein has three to five times as many mutations as in any of its predecessors (Mannar et al. 2022). Although mutations can appear anywhere on the viral genome, they are considered most critical in the spike protein, mainly in the RBD region extending from ARG319 to PHE541 (da Costa et al. 2022). Due to the RBD protein interaction with the human ACE-2 receptor, this region is vital for infectivity (Ortega et al. 2021a). The possible effects of the Omicron variant on viral infectiousness and host interactions can only be understood by understanding the molecular alterations involved.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have revealed insights into how environmental factors, such as heat flux, impact the structural dynamics and stability of the virus, which in turn uncovers potential vulnerabilities for therapeutic targeting (Xiao et al. 2024). Furthermore, computational approaches integrating MD simulations with molecular docking have expedited the discovery of promising drug candidates by screening extensive compound libraries for high binding affinities to viral targets (Ghahremanian et al. 2022). In addition to studying viruses directly, MD simulations have shown peptide interactions in biological systems, especially in nanochannels, showing promise for creating peptide-based treatments to fight new variants of SARS-CoV-2 (Shi et al. 2023). Also, previous MM/PBSA studies on SARS-CoV reported binding free energies in the range of − 12 to − 18 kcal/mol, with specific mutations enhancing ACE2 affinity. These findings motivate the exploration of ACE2-derived peptide inhibitors, as optimized peptides may competitively mimic or disrupt native RBD–ACE2 interactions (Y. Han et al. 2021a, b). Spike-RDB protein is an interesting therapeutic target as it is responsible for evading host cells when it binds to the human ACE2 receptors (Yang et al. 2020). RBD-ACE2 receptor binding is a central target for neutralization by antibodies and other inhibitors (Manček-Keber et al. 2021; Tai et al. 2020). Changes in the interaction site control the presentation of the antibodies created to neutralize the RDB when associated with the ACE2 on host cells (Gayvert et al. 2023).

This study aims to fill in some of the blanks in the current literature and provide insight into the nature of SARS-CoV-2 variants. It aims to lay the groundwork for developing vital therapeutic strategies to address the emergence of new coronavirus variants. This strategy improves the comprehension of viral dynamics and establishes the foundation for future experimental confirmations and clinical implementations to address the difficulties presented by SARS-CoV-2 variations.

Materials and methodology

Protein structure

This study focused on the spike protein's RBD region, which binds to the host organism's ACE2 receptor. 3D structures of the ACE2 and spike-RBD protein complex from all known variants were collected from the protein data bank (Burley et al. 2025) for the purpose of this investigation. Their PDB IDs were 6M0J (Lan et al. 2020), 7EKF (Han et al. 2021a, b), 7EKG (Han et al. 2021a, b), 7EKC (Han et al. 2021a, b), 7WBQ (Han et al. 2022), and 7WSK (Li et al. 2022a, b). Mutation analysis of the spike-RBD protein variants was conducted using the GISIAD database (Elbe and Buckland-Merrett 2017). Multiple acceptable sources were used for the collection of genomic data from the SARS coronavirus. Moreover, the amino acid sequence was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Selected sequences were analysed using CoVsurver, a tool that is accessible on GISAID. To identify and investigate the different mutations found in the RBD region of the spike protein, this method was used to analyse the SARS-CoV-2 genome. A thorough sequence analysis was performed utilizing the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool for Proteins (BLASTP) online platform to ascertain the degree of similarity between the first SARS-CoV-2 variant termed as wild type in this study, and the other variants reported (Johnson et al. 2008). The amino acid sequence of spike proteins from all variations was compared using BLASTP. The BLASTP algorithm finds statistically significant matches between protein sequences and databases of sequences. To deduce functional and evolutionary relationships between sequences, CLUSTAL omega was also employed (Sievers et al. 2011).

MD (Molecular Dynamic) simulation

Molecular dynamics simulation was used to examine the binding phenomena of the ACE2 receptor and spike protein for all variations. The complicated interaction of the viral spike protein with host ACE2 receptor emphasizes the significance of this enzyme in the pathogenesis of COVID-19. Further, it shows that ACE2 is a potential receptor that could be used in COVID-19 treatments and prevention strategies (Bian and Li 2021). To assess the binding stability of human ACE2-RBD complexes, MD simulation lasting 300 ns was performed for each of the six variations in the study. The study used Gromacs 2021.2 software (Abraham et al. 2015; Bauer et al. 2022), widely used in scientific circles for its simulation capabilities. CHARMM36 forcefield was used to generate the initial protein topology and to assign charges (Huang and MacKerell 2013). The Edward Particle Mesh algorithm is a known method which is used in this study to calculate the Electric field strength (Darden et al. 1993). Subsequently, the entire system was kept within a periodic boundary condition with box dimension of 13 × 13 × 13 nm and solvated using a water model TIP3P to simulate a natural biological environment (Izadi et al. 2014). To neutralize the system, sodium cations (Na +) and chloride anions (Cl-) were added. The steepest descent method that minimizes the function to achieve the minimum energy state was used to minimize the structure of the complex with solvent molecules. The system was heated to 310 K after all hydrogen bonds were restricted using the LINCS methods (Hess 2008). Two equilibration cycles for each complex followed the energy minimization process. An initial 1 ns equilibration was conducted under the NVT ensemble. Another 1 ns of equilibration time was then added under the NPT ensemble (Bussi et al. 2007). For stable simulation, temperature was controlled using the velocity rescaling thermostat under the NVT ensemble, while the pressure was regulated through the Parrinello-Rahman barostat under the NPT ensemble (Martonák et al. 2003). A molecular dynamics simulation lasting 300 ns was carried out on the already equilibrated system. Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) and root mean square deviation (RMSD) were calculated for post-simulation to assess the structural stability and flexibility of the system (Hollingsworth and Dror 2018). Molecular mechanics generalized Born surface area (MM/GBSA) was used to estimate binding free energies, and an analysis using the concept of a free energy landscape (FEL) was performed to study conformational variation and the energetic stability of the ACE2–RBDs among viral variants.

MM/GBSA and MM/PBSA

The binding free energy of the ligand to the target protein can be calculated by MM/PBSA or MM/GBSA methods, using snapshots extracted from the final 20 ns of the MD trajectories. Frames were sampled at 10 ps intervals, resulting in an ensemble of conformations used for averaging to ensure statistically reliable estimation of binding free energies. which are offered as advanced post-processing tools in the GROMACS suite (Chen et al. 2020; Kumari et al. 2014; Miller et al. 2012). Protein-peptide docking, MD simulation and MM/GBSA

HDOCK employs protein-peptide docking, a computational method for predicting a protein target's optimal peptide-protein binding mode (Yan et al. 2017). HDOCK (Li et al. 2022a, b) is a docking server that combines ab initio docking and template-based modelling techniques. It combines free docking and homology modelling using a fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. The ACE2 protein complex was used to obtain the three-dimensional structure of the receptor-binding protein. The continuous sequence of the peptide from the ACE2 binding site residues contributing to the per-residues decomposition analysis were extracted. The HDOCK server is configured with the input data. The spike-RBD structure was provided in the receptor option, while the peptide structure was provided in the ligand option in the HDOCK server. Semi-flexible docking was used, wherein the spike RBD receptor was treated as rigid, while the peptide was allowed full conformational flexibility during docking. Parameters are adjusted according to the specific requirements of the interaction study, such as the binding site if known or desired constraints to guide the docking process. To ensure the biologically relevant binding poses, interface residues involved in ACE2 recognition were defined as binding-site constraints based on prior structural analysis and per-residue energy decomposition results. These residues included key RBD interface amino acids contributing significantly to ACE2 binding. By restricting the docking search space to this region, the docking protocol was guided toward physiologically meaningful RBD–peptide interaction modes. HDOCK generates multiple possible complexes ranked by their docking scores. The binding between the protein and peptide was studied through LigPlot + version 2.2.9 (Laskowski and Swindells 2011). It simplifies the interpretation of complicated three-dimensional structures by producing schematic diagrams of the hydrophobic and hydrogen bond interactions among ligands and proteins. Later, to evaluate the dynamic interaction strength of the peptide and protein, a 100 ns simulation was performed and the corresponding ∆G of binding was calculated. The optimal variation peptide was docked, and MD simulated with the RBD spike proteins. The sequences of the variant peptide were used to model the 3D structure using PepFold3 (Lamiable et al. 2016).

Free energy landscape of simulation trajectory

The research used the free energy landscape (FEL) approach to examine the protein's energy profiles and structural dynamics (Maisuradze et al. 2010). The application of FEL analysis is essential to attain a more profound understanding of molecular fluctuation phenomena and biomolecule energetic variation mechanisms. The free energy value for a specific position is determined using the formula ΔG(X) = -kBT. ln P(X), Where ΔG stands for the variation in Gibbs free energy, kB for the Boltzmann constant, T for the system temperature, X for the reaction coordinate, and P(X) for the probability distribution of states. Application of this calculation enabled the identification of the protein-peptide complex's stable conformations and transient configurations, denoted on the FEL by the smallest and highest barriers, respectively. Utilizing this methodology resulted in a thorough comprehension of the energetic and molecular interactions that define the protein-peptide interactions being studied.

Variant peptide generation

Energy decomposition was used to identify the native peptide residues with an unfavourable binding free energy that was identified to surround the RBD spike protein residues. Shorter peptide fragments were not selected, as truncation of this region resulted in the loss of key interfacial residues critical for stabilizing RBD binding. These residues were further mutated to make it more soluble. This was done using an ML-based mutation model. The mutants were generated using the tool module in Python, and the resultant stretch was subjected to mutation using a user-defined function written in script (Python 2021). This mutation was made to increase its solubility. These mutants were further used for screening against the RBD spike proteins.

ML based screening

The machine learning model was trained using experimentally resolved protein–peptide complex data obtained from the PDBbind database (Wang et al. 2004), a curated resource containing binding affinity measurements derived from biochemical and biophysical experiments. Only protein–peptide complexes with clearly annotated peptide chains and experimentally reported binding affinities were retained for model development.

Dataset preparation included complexes involving small-molecule ligands, nucleic acids, or ambiguous binding annotations were excluded to ensure consistency with the peptide–protein interaction context of the study. Peptides included in the dataset spanned short to medium-length sequences, representing interface-binding peptides comparable in size to the ACE2-derived peptides considered here. Binding affinity values in the dataset covered a broad dynamic range, encompassing weak to high-affinity interactions, thereby enabling effective regression learning across diverse interaction strengths. Affinity values were standardized prior to training to minimize scale-dependent bias. Feature generation was performed using sequence- and structure-informed descriptors derived from peptide and protein sequences, capturing physicochemical properties, residue composition, and interaction-relevant characteristics. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training and validation subsets, and model performance was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R2) and correlation metrics. Although the exact number of retained complexes and peptide length distribution varied following quality filtering and preprocessing, the final dataset provided sufficient diversity and coverage to support supervised regression modeling for relative binding affinity prediction, as evidenced by consistent model performance across validation sets. The best model was used to screen the peptides, and the top peptides were used further for docking against RBD spike proteins.

Results and discussions

Spike RBD variants

The spike protein is crucial in initiating infection by binding to the host cell receptor. Through binding to the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, situated primarily on the cell membrane, viral infectivity is determined (Shirbhate et al. 2021). Spike protein is composed of two functional domains, S1 and S2, which make distinct contributions to the mechanism of infection. Receptor-binding domain (RBD) in the S1 subunit is directly involved in engaging the ACE2 receptor, thus facilitating viral attachment. Next, structural remodelling of the S2 subunit leads to membrane fusion, which does so by progressing through the formation of a six-helix bundle (Huang et al. 2020). Here, we examine structural and functional divergence among several SARS-CoV-2 variants, namely Omicron, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and the original wild type. The 3D conformations of spike proteins of these variants were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) under the identities Alpha (PDB ID: 6MOJ), Beta (7EKF), Gamma (7KEG), Delta (7WBQ), and Omicron (7WSK).

The structures under consideration demonstrate minimal deviation from one another, as indicated by the relatively low RMSD values. MM/PBSA

Using the Molecular Mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MM/PBSA) method, the thermodynamic profiles of the human ACE2 receptor interplaying with several spike-RBD SARS-CoV-2 variants were quantitatively assessed. Furthermore, by investigating its nonpolar solvation effects, the complex's solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), which affected its stability, was assessed. By integrating many energy components, the total binding free energies for the ACE2-RBD complex of every variety were calculated. The probability of complex formation among biomolecules can be evaluated quantitatively by comparing their binding affinity, usually expressed as the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG, in kcal/mol). ΔG indicates that complex formation is thermodynamically favourable and more likely to proceed under biological conditions, and it is therefore the key parameter in comprehending and predicting molecular recognition and binding processes. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), the binding affinity was highest for the wild-type spike-RBD toward human ACE2. Precisely, the wild-type form was revealed to have the highest binding affinity, as indicated by the ΔG value of −47.33 ± 13.03 kcal/mol (Fig. 3(f)), while Omicron was close second with a value of −30.41 ± 10.39 kcal/mol. For comparison, the Alpha was weaker, having an association value of −22.62 ± 9.69 kcal/mol (Fig. 3(b)). The Beta and Delta presented relatively equal affinities, having association values of −27.38 ± 10.70 kcal/mol and −27.44 ± 12.91 kcal/mol, respectively (Figs. 3(c) and (d)). Gamma had a relatively equal association value as well, which was −26.26 ± 9.66 kcal/mol (Fig. 3(e)). With these observations, Omicron and wild-type spike-RBDs possessing the most favourable association values were chosen for detailed structural and energetic investigations.

Molecular docking (Peptide)

Molecular docking can be performed between protein–protein and protein-peptide to understand the binding mechanism. This aided in the design of antiviral drugs (Jones et al. 1995). The docking scores obtained from the HDOCK server represent raw empirical scoring values derived from shape complementarity, electrostatics, and statistical potentials. The docking scores for the peptide and the wild-type spike-RBD in this investigation were −200.99 kcal/mol and −230.49 kcal/mol, respectively. A higher negative docking score suggests a more stable complex, reflecting a higher anticipated binding affinity between the two molecules. Variants of peptide

The peptide mutation strategy primarily guided by per-residue MM/PBSA energy decomposition analysis, with the objective of directly optimizing interaction energetics at the spike RBD binding interface. Residues exhibiting unfavorable energetic contributions were selectively targeted for mutation to improve binding affinity and solubility while preserving the overall peptide length and sequence context. Using energy decomposition analysis, residues in the native peptide with unfavorable binding free energy when interacting with the RBD spike protein were identified at positions 1, 3, 15, and 19. The 19-residue ACE2-derived peptide (Leu24–Gln42) was selected based on per-residue binding energy decomposition analysis of the ACE2–RBD complex which revealed that this continuous segment contributes most significantly and consistently to favorable binding free energy. Residues within this region form a dense interaction network with the spike RBD, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, while maintaining structural continuity at the interface. These residues were mutated using a machine learning-based mutation model to enhance the peptide's solubility. At positions 1 and 3, Leucine and Lysine were substituted with Serine (S), Tyrosine (Y), or Histidine (H). At position 15, Glutamic Acid (Glu) was changed to Serine (S), Tyrosine (Y), or Aspartic Acid (D). At position 19, Glutamine (Gln) was mutated to either Aspartic Acid (Asp) or Glutamic Acid (Glu). This approach generated 3 × 3 × 3 × 2 = 54 peptide variants to explore combinations that might improve solubility systematically. In this study, 54 peptide variants were evaluated, and the top-performing peptide was identified based on the predicted binding scores. Subsequently, molecular docking was conducted to determine the peptide with the most favorable binding affinity.

Additionally, python scripts were used to perform these mutations and create the peptide variants, which were then screened for binding against the RBD spikes of Omicron and SARS-CoV-2. To train the ML-based model, data on protein-peptide complexes and their binding scores were taken from the PDBind database. According to Table 3, the peptide screening was conducted using the model with the greatest R2 and correlation. The greatest performance was shown by XG Boost Regressor, which achieved an R2 of 0.6958 and a correlation of 0.834. Feature representation for machine learning was derived from sequence- and structure-informed descriptors of protein–peptide complexes, including amino acid composition, physicochemical properties (hydrophobicity, charge, polarity), and interaction-relevant features capturing residue-level contributions to binding. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (80%) and independent test (20%) sets to evaluate model generalizability. Model training was further validated using five-fold cross-validation on the training set to minimize overfitting and assess robustness. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) and Pearson correlation coefficient between predicted and experimental binding affinities. Among the evaluated models, the XGBoost regressor consistently demonstrated superior predictive performance and was therefore selected for peptide variant screening. Hyperparameters for the XGBoost model were optimized using grid search within the cross-validation framework. This model effectively screened the peptide variants to identify those with the best binding affinities as shown in Table 4. Four peptides (M1, M2, M3, and M4) exhibited the highest performance against both the Omicron variant and wild-type SARS-CoV-2. These peptides demonstrated comparable predicted binding scores of −17.671938 for SARS-CoV-2 and −13.715812 for the Omicron variant, indicating strong binding potential across both strains. To determine the most effective peptide among these top candidates, molecular docking analyses were subsequently performed. The top-performing peptides from this screening were further evaluated through docking studies to assess their interactions with the RBD spike proteins of Omicron and SARS-CoV-2, aiming to identify peptide variants with enhanced solubility and optimal binding properties. Molecular dynamics simulation (Variant Peptide)

To study the binding of the M1 peptide to the RBD of the spike proteins in both the wild-type and Omicron versions of SARS-CoV-2, 100 ns of MD simulations were performed. The RMSD of the wild-type and Omicron RBDs linked to the M1 peptide is shown in Fig. S3 (a). The wild-type (black) and Omicron (red) proteins maintained steady RMSD values throughout the 100 ns simulation, with variations of around 0.2 nm. The observed stability indicates that the protein conformations remain consistent throughout the simulation.

The mutant peptide's RMSD, when attached to both the Omicron and wild-type spike proteins, is shown in Fig. S3 (b). Understanding the M1 peptide's stability and binding conformation in relation to the RBD spike proteins may be greatly enhanced by calculating its RMSD. The RMSD values of the bound M1 peptide show more variation (0.3 nm to 0.9 nm) than those of the proteins when bound to either the wild-type or Omicron versions. Throughout most of the 100-ns simulation, the wild-type maintains an RMSD of M1 of 0.6 nm. In contrast, when M1 is attached to the Omicron variant, its RMSD starts at 0.6 nm and increases to a stable value of 0.9 nm. Despite these fluctuations, the peptide remains bound to the RBD, indicating ongoing interactions and structural adaptations.

The proteins' RMSF is shown in Fig. S3 (c). The wild type and Omicron variants show similar patterns of residue flexibility in the RMSF graphs, with certain places showing more variations than others. RMSF fluctuation peaks, which may represent protein loops or terminal segments, show areas of increased flexibility.

During the course of the 100 ns MD simulation, the M1 peptide exhibited stable and sustained interactions with the RBD of the wild-type and Omicron variants of SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins. RMSD analysis validated the general structural stability of the spike proteins but emphasized the dynamic binding ability of the M1 peptide. RMSF profiling additionally disclosed the areas of high flexibility in the protein structures, gaining more insight into the protein–peptide complex conformational flexibility. Collectively, these findings validate the potential of the M1 peptide as an excellent compound for targeting the SARS-CoV-2 variants’ RBD. Supplementary Fig. S4 shows the comparative binding affinity of the peptide to the two variants’ spike-RBDs. An MM/GBSA binding free energy calculation experiment indicated that the M1 peptide was more strongly bound to the Omicron variant, having a binding free energy of –35.67 kcal/mol (Fig. S4a) compared to –21.63 kcal/mol in the wild-type complex (Fig. S4b). The higher binding affinity in the Omicron complex mainly arises due to more favourable van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, reflecting a stronger and more stable association profile.

Discussions

According to Ortega et al., previous research on single residue mutants, RBD variants with multiple mutations may stabilize the viral spike-host ACE2 receptor interaction (Ortega et al. 2021b). In the Omicron variant of the viral spike, eight out of thirty mutations take place in the receptor-binding domain of the RBD (Hodcroft 2021). Among the numerous mutations displayed by these RBD variations are Lys417Asn, Ser477Asn, Thr478Lys, Glu484Ala, and Asn501Tyr (Hadfield et al. 2018; Wang and Cheng 2022). Evidence from studies by Lim et al. (2020) and others (Lim et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020) suggests that this variation may have a higher affinity for ACE2 and a higher risk of reinfection (Pulliam et al. 2022). Previous artificial intelligence models built utilizing extensive experimental data and verified by SARS-CoV-2 testing suggested that Omicron may be nearly ten times more infectious than the original virus or 2.8 times more virulent than the Delta variant (Chen et al. 2021). Hence, it is important to pursue the development of potent antiviral medications targeting Omicron to bolster defence against current and future viral outbreaks and mitigate the ongoing epidemic.

The experimental design used computational methods to analyse SARS-CoV-2 spike-RBD variants' ACE2 receptor binding. This method is justified by requiring rapid and precise insights into viral transmission and pathogenic molecular mechanisms. Computational modelling and MD simulations accurately predict binding affinities and stability, making therapeutic drug screening efficient. The continuing alterations in the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 greatly impede the development of vaccines and therapeutics. Focusing on their interactions with the human ACE2 receptor, this study used computational approaches to evaluate the spike proteins of several SARS-CoV-2 variants. A significantly lower binding free energy of −30.41 kcal/mol was observed in the Omicron variant compared to the wild-type form (−47.33 kcal/mol), indicating a higher binding affinity. The binding energy between the wild-type and Omicron spike-RBD proteins is critical for ACE2, which is regulated by the residues LEU24, THR27, PHE28, and TYR41. An evaluation was conducted to establish the binding ability of a 19-residue peptide (LAKTFLETFNYEAQELSYQ) to the spike-RBD of the original wild-type and Omicron variants. According to the molecular docking study, the wild-type protein had a confidence score of 0.73 and a docking score of −200.99 kcal/mol, but the Omicron variant protein had a higher confidence score of 0.83 and a lower docking score of −230.49 kcal/mol. The analysis conducted after the MM/GBSA showed that the binding free energy with the Omicron spike-RBD was −24.77 kcal/mol, better than the wild-type, which is −15.15 kcal/mol. The results indicate that the engineered peptide demonstrates a high level of affinity for binding, especially to the Omicron version, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, among other computational techniques, have efficiently and accurately revealed the viral transmission and pathogenic molecular mechanisms of SARS-CoV-2.

The M1 peptide (HAHTFLETFNYEAQTLSYE) has exhibited improved efficacy in various important measures compared to the original peptide. Compared to the native peptide, the docking scores of the RBD spike proteins were greater for the Omicron variation (−257.35 kcal/mol) and the wild type (−234.24 kcal/mol). Furthermore, compared to the original peptide, which had binding free energies of −24.77 kcal/mol and −15.15 kcal/mol, respectively, the M1 peptide had improved binding free energies, with ΔG values of −35.67 kcal/mol for Omicron and −21.63 kcal/mol for the wild type. Furthermore, the MD simulations demonstrated that the M1 peptide exhibited a consistently steady RMSD profile, indicating a higher level of structural stability during the whole simulation duration. The data show that the M1 peptide has better binding capabilities, is more stable, and may be a better therapy choice than the original peptide. In addition to radius of gyration and hydrogen bond analyses, RMSD convergence was carefully examined to assess the temporal stability of the ACE2–RBD complexes. The RMSD profiles indicate an initial equilibration phase followed by stable trajectories, suggesting that the systems reached conformational equilibrium during the simulation time. Moreover, the absence of abrupt RMSD deviations implies preservation of the overall secondary structural framework, supporting the structural stability of the complexes throughout the simulation.

According to previous research, virus infection and immune system evasion may be significantly impacted by mutations in the spike protein's receptor-binding domain (RBD) (Starr et al. 2020; Weisblum et al. 2020). Research has shown that mutations such as N501Y and E484K enhance spike-RBD's ACE2 receptor attachment capabilities, leading to enhanced viral transmission and likely resistance to antibodies that neutralize it (Garcia-Beltran et al. 2021). Moreover, a study has demonstrated that the Omicron variant's numerous mutations give it a greater ability to attach to ACE2, which is associated with a higher transmissibility level than previous variations. According to these studies, important residues and interactions provide Omicron spike-RBD a stronger binding affinity to ACE2. To produce effective vaccines and medicines, our results highlight the need of ongoing monitoring and adaptable methods. Previously reported cholesterol-modified ACE2-derived peptides are typically longer constructs that rely on lipid conjugation to enhance membrane association and in vivo retention. In contrast, the M1 peptide identified in this study is a shorter, minimally modified sequence that was optimized through structure-guided mutagenesis and energetically driven screening, enabling straightforward synthesis and flexible post-design functionalization. While cholesterol conjugation has been shown to increase local membrane concentration, the reduced length and unmodified backbone of M1 provide advantages in manufacturing simplicity and allow modular adaptation to different delivery strategies, including PEGylation or lipid attachment, if required for in vivo application. The physiological feasibility of peptide-based inhibitors is governed by factors such as proteolytic stability, bioavailability, and delivery efficiency. Although direct pharmacokinetic evaluation was beyond the scope of the present computational study, the optimized M1 peptide represents a suitable scaffold for established stabilization strategies. PEGylation may improve plasma half-life and reduce renal clearance, whereas lipid conjugation, including cholesterol or fatty-acid attachment, may enhance pulmonary retention and antiviral efficacy. The human ACE2-derived origin of M1 is expected to confer favourable biocompatibility, while its linear architecture permits incorporation of stabilizing modifications without disrupting the binding interface.

Importantly, this work advances beyond previously reported ACE2-derived peptide inhibitors by implementing a multi-level computational optimization framework. Rather than relying solely on empirical sequence truncation or chemical modification, the present study integrates per-residue energetic decomposition, systematic in silico mutagenesis, machine learning–based affinity prediction trained on experimentally derived datasets, and extensive molecular dynamics and MM/GBSA validation. This approach enabled identification of an optimized peptide with robust binding stability across both wild-type and Omicron spike RBDs, establishing a rational and generalizable strategy for peptide inhibitor design against evolving viral variants.

The current research highlights the significance of learning about spike-RBD mutations to create efficient therapies and vaccinations. Therapeutic approaches targeting present and emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants may be initiated using the discovered peptide. This fits in with the continuous endeavours to improve the effectiveness of current vaccinations and create new therapies to tackle the difficulties caused by the virus's ever-changing nature. Taken together, the results of this research provide light on the molecular pathways that cause the pathogenesis and transmission of SARS-CoV-2. To lessen the blow that existing and future SARS-CoV-2 variations deal to world health, this research identifies critical residues and interactions within the spike protein, paving the way for the quick creation of tailored treatments and vaccines.

Limitations

Despite the strengths and advantages of computational approaches, they maybe some-times coupled with natural limitations. Current methods rely on existing structural data and theoretical models, which may not capture the complexity of biological interactions in vivo. It is acknowledged that linear peptides may be susceptible to rapid proteolytic cleavage under physiological conditions, potentially limiting plasma half-life. Future work will incorporate stability-oriented optimization strategies, including backbone cyclization, terminal capping, incorporation of D-amino acids or non-natural residues, and PEGylation, as well as in silico protease susceptibility prediction and experimental serum stability assays, to enhance translational potential. Although more in vivo and in vitro studies are required to fully determine the discovered peptide's safety and effectiveness in clinical trials, the results are very promising and can provide the necessary foundation for further extensive research. Furthermore, the dynamic nature of viral evolution requires continuous surveillance and therapy strategy adaption to successfully address emerging variations.

Conclusion

The findings provide light on the binding interactions between the human ACE2 receptor and SARS-CoV-2 spike-RBD variants, which are a crucial aspect of the research. The spike-RBD has undergone targeted alterations that give the Omicron variant an advantage over the wild-type regarding binding affinity. A novel 19-residue peptide (LAKTFLETFNYEAQELSYQ) shows strong binding to both variants and potential as a therapeutic agent, as evidenced by molecular docking and MD simulations. Further, mutation using an ML-based model showed that the M1 mutant peptide (HAHTFLETFNYEAQTLSYE) has demonstrated superior performance to the native peptide across several key metrics. Interactions with it resulted in higher docking scores for the RBD spike proteins of the wild and Omicron variants than for the native peptide. Furthermore, the binding free energies of the M1 peptide were better than those of the original peptide, as evidenced by ΔG values for Omicron wild type. These results emphasize the significance of adaptive therapy approaches to tackle changing SARS-CoV-2 variations, demonstrating the peptide's therapeutic agent potential. A computationally designed and validated peptide with excellent binding affinity for wild-type and omicron spike-RBD is a unique component of our work. More experimental validation is necessary to establish the peptide's effectiveness and potentially lead to successful therapies against existing and future versions of SARS-CoV-2.
