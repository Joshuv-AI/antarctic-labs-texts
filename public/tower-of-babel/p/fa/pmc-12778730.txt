A Novel Case of Transverse Sinus Stenting and Ticagrelor Use During Pregnancy for Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension
Authors: Mayur S. Patel, Asad S. Akhter, Matthew T. Rocco, Abdussamad Akhter, Shahid M. Nimjee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is prevalent among the US population, with exacerbation of symptoms during pregnancy. Transverse sinus stenting is a new effective treatment for IIH. Stenting is avoided in pregnancy largely due to the requirement of dual antiplatelet therapy.MethodsWe present a case of a pregnant patient in her first trimester with signs and symptoms of fulminant IIH, including progressive visual loss, who underwent placement of a transverse sinus stent and administration of dual antiplatelet therapy with ticagrelor.ResultsTicagrelor was administered pre–operatively. The patient underwent venous sinus stenting for fulminant IIH. There were no complications. She had resolution of symptoms and underwent cesarean delivery without issues. Her child displayed no signs and symptoms of abnormalities.ConclusionVenous sinus stenting for IIH can be considered in pregnant patients presenting with new or worsening IIH with associated papilledema. The use of ticagrelor did not lead to any adverse outcomes for the patient or the fetus in our case.

Fulminant idiopathic intracranial hypertension (FIIH) is defined by acute symptoms and signs of idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) within 4 weeks of developing symptoms and severe vision loss with normal brain magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography venography. In such patients, prompt surgical treatment may be necessary to mitigate vision deterioration. 1

Management of FIIH includes cerebrospinal fluid shunting, optic nerve sheath fenestration, and venous sinus stenting (VSS). 1 Medical options include acetazolamide and furosemide; however, acetazolamide and intravenous methylprednisolone should be administered while awaiting surgery. 1 , 2 Although weight reduction is the cornerstone of IIH treatment, in the setting of FIIH and pregnancy, it is less feasible. 2 The use of medications is also complicated during pregnancy.

VSS has been found to improve papilledema, pulsatile tinnitus, and headache. Previous studies have shown it to be as effective as optic nerve sheath fenestration or shunting. 3 Furthermore, VSS is avoided during pregnancy due to the need of dual antiplatelet therapy, which is not well studied in pregnancy. Currently, there are no guidelines for treatment of FIIH during pregnancy and no studies that suggest the superiority of a surgical procedure. 2 , 4

Ticagrelor, a P2Y12 receptor antagonist, may be favorable for intracranial stenting or embolization as it has a rapid onset and reversable binding, allowing its discontinuation prior to any invasive procedure. 5 Its safety has not been proven in pregnancy and remains in Food and Drug Administration category C. To our knowledge, we present the first case where a first trimester pregnant patient newly diagnosed with FIIH undergoes VSS while using aspirin and ticagrelor.

Case Description

A 26‐year‐old woman, G2P1001, at 12 weeks of gestation presented with 3 weeks of intermittent headaches and worsening visual acuity. Computed tomography venography revealed bilateral stenosis of the transverse‐sigmoid sinus junctions. Ophthalmologic evaluation further demonstrated +4 papilledema and right cranial nerve VI palsy. Her opening pressure on a lumbar puncture was >55 mm Hg. She underwent digital subtraction angiography showing a pressure gradient of 67 mm Hg on the left and 17 mm Hg on the right.

The patient did not meet criteria for optic nerve sheath fenestration per our institution's guidelines as the opening pressure exceeded the threshold. She was offered a ventriculoperitoneal shunt but declined it. After discussions with the obstetricians, we recommended placement of a left‐sided venous sinus stent and prescription of ticagrelor and aspirin as they have a reversible binding profile for P2Y12, with a half‐life of ≈7 hours. Our patient had a high‐risk pregnancy, and, if she needed a cesarean section, stopping the drug for ≈24 hours would clear >95% of its active metabolites (Figure). VSS was chosen to target the structural abnormality causing her symptoms. The benefits of the procedure would outweigh the risks of radiation exposure due to the urgency of the case. She underwent left VSS using a 10 × 40‐mm Zilver stent (Cook Medical) under general anesthesia and received 11000 units of heparin intravenously during the 1‐hour procedure. Her pressure gradient improved significantly immediately after stent placement. The superior sagittal sinus was 27 mm Hg, 24 mm Hg in the left transverse sinus, 23 mm Hg in the left sigmoid sinus, and 22 mm Hg in the left internal jugular vein.

She was discharged without postoperative complications. Outpatient ophthalmologic evaluation demonstrated subjective improvement at 1‐month follow‐up. At 3‐month follow‐up, there were no signs of transient visual obscurations, diplopia, or papilledema on fundoscopic examination. She completed her 3‐month course of ticagrelor, per our institution's guidelines, and continued taking aspirin. She underwent planned cesarean delivery at 36 weeks of gestation due to pre‐eclampsia and her child was born without abnormalities. Her ocular examination remained stable post partum.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report on the use of ticagrelor for VSS for patients with FIIH in pregnancy. There is also a paucity of studies, with at least 2 reported cases of ticagrelor use during pregnancy for acute myocardial infarctions. 6 , 7 It has been demonstrated to be effective and safe in intracranial sinus thrombosis during pregnancy. 8

As no specific procedure is superior, the treatment of FIIH should be administered on a case‐by‐case basis with multidisciplinary discussions. 1 , 2 Despite the concern for radiation and dual antiplatelet therapies during pregnancy, VSS may not be detrimental to the mother and her fetus in urgent cases, but the risks and benefits of the procedure need to be compared.

In addition, treatment of IIH in pregnancy can be complicated by requiring medication, and our case demonstrates that dual antiplatelet therapy may be permissible to use during pregnancy. Ticagrelor has added benefit, as the drug binds reversibly to the P2Y12 receptor, has a rapid onset of action, and does not require metabolic activation, if the patient were to undergo urgent delivery. 5

Conclusion

VSS for FIIH can be considered in pregnant patients. Ticagrelor may be safe during pregnancy with no effect on the fetus. Further research needs to be performed to assess the safety profile of ticagrelor in pregnant patients with cerebrovascular disease.
