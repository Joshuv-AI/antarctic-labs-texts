GCNT3 and ST3GAL1 expression correlates with HER2 status and MUC1/β-catenin/Cyclin D1 axis in breast cancer
Authors: Rohitesh Gupta, Indresh K Gopalsamy, Aisha Shigna Nadukkandy, Ankit Singh, Charan Raju Tangella, Liza Esther Alexander, Rekha A Nair, Marco Cordani, Lekha Dinesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe status of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is a critical determinant of breast cancer progression and outcome, but the role of glycosylation in modulating HER2-related pathways remains poorly understood. Here, we examined the relationship between HER2 and the glycosyltransferases ST3GAL1 and GCNT3, combining data mining with experimental and clinicopathological validation. In silico analyses across public BC cohorts showed that higher expression of ST3GAL1, GCNT3, and HER2 was associated with reduced survival. Immunohistochemistry on invasive ductal carcinoma specimens (n = 25) demonstrated increased ST3GAL1 and GCNT3 in advanced stages/grades. Cross-platform correlation analyses revealed a positive association between ST3GAL1 and HER2, whereas GCNT3 showed an inverse association with HER2. Functional assays in HER2-negative cell lines (MCF7, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-435) and the HER2-positive line SKBR3 indicated that GCNT3 supports migratory capacity and clonogenicity, consistent with an oncogenic role independent of HER2 status. Gene set enrichment pointed to upregulation of MUC1 and β-catenin; tissue validation of MUC1, β-catenin, and Cyclin D1 confirmed their clinicopathological relevance, with HER2 expression inversely correlated with β-catenin and Cyclin D1. Collectively, these findings suggested a model in which GCNT3-driven O-glycosylation might remodel the MUC1/β-catenin/Cyclin D1 axis in BC, adding a glyco-regulatory layer to HER2-linked pathobiology. From a diagnostic perspective, ST3GAL1 (positive with HER2) and GCNT3 (inverse with HER2) could be considered as candidate biomarkers that might complement HER2 assessment for risk stratification. Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), Breast Cancer (BC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women, globally [1]. Given its intrinsic heterogeneity, there is a need for cost-effective identification of molecular subtypes and their biological drivers to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes. There are four major classifications of BC amongst which HER2-positive (+ ve) BC is regarded as one of the most aggressive subtypes [2, 3]. HER2-positive BC constitutes about 15–25% of all cases [4] occurring mainly in younger women and is marred by poor prognosis, higher relapse rate, and mortality if diagnosed late [5]. Although the role of HER2-driven signaling in BC has been extensively studied, its interface with glycosylation remains underexplored in spite of the mounting evidence that glycans influence tumor biology and may serve as biomarkers in diagnostic pathology.

N-glycosylation sites on HER2 have been predicted by the NetNglyc server and validated in HER2-positive tissues by mass spectrometry [6, 7]. However, the broader implications of glycosylation in relation to HER2 status have not been comprehensively addressed. Beyond N-glycans, O-linked glycans are critical modulators of cell–cell interactions, adhesion, and metastasis [8, 9]. Aberrantly expressed O-glycan structures such as Thomsen-Friedenreich (Tf) antigen (Galβ1-3GalNAcα1-O-Ser/Thr), Thomsen-nouveau (Tn) antigen (GalNAcα1-O-Ser/Thr), sialyl-Tf (Neu5Acα2-3Galβ1-3GalNAc-O-Ser/Thr), and sialyl-Tn (Neu5Acα2-6GalNAcα1-O-Ser/Thr) antigens are frequently implicated in BC progression [8]. Among these, addition of sialic acid residue on Core-1 or TF-antigen can either be catalyzed by a member of the ST3GAL family, most commonly ST3GAL1, resulting in a terminal a2,3 sialic acid (sialyl-TF) and no further extension, or catalyzed by GCNT1/3 resulting in branching of this structure into Core-2 or Core-4 glycans [10–12]. Linked to shorter sialylated core 1 side-chains in primary breast carcinoma, ST3GAL1 expression is significantly elevated in breast carcinomas compared to normal tissues, where it also correlates with tumor grade [13]. Extension of Core-2 trisaccharide by other glycogenes can lead to the formation of sLeA or sLeX type glycans [14]. These glycans participate in the metastasis of cancer cells by aiding the extravasation through the endothelium via E-selectin–mediated adhesion, a mechanism supported by studies showing that sialyl-Lewis X–dependent engagement of E-selectin promotes cell migration in breast cancer [15, 16].

To explore the relation between O-glycosylation and HER2, we selected these important glycosyltransferases, namely ST3GAL1 and GCNT3 for further experimentation. At the outset, we carried out in silico analysis and validated our findings using clinical specimens to quantify expressional variation of these glycogenes in BC. Using orthogonal techniques, we observed the correlation of ST3GAL1 and GCNT3 with HER2 status (Figs. 1A, 3A and C and 4D and F). Gupta et al., (2020) has previously shown the role of GCNT3 in pancreatic cancer progression and metastasis [17]. Thus, to further investigate the role of GCNT3 in BC, we carried out various in vitro assays using its inhibitor, talniflumate, to assess its oncogenic nature.

Moreover, global GSEA analysis on healthy and BC patients also identified an upregulation of certain non-glycogenes such as MUC1 and β-catenin, in tumor samples [18, 19]. Gupta et al. (2020), and others have earlier shown influence of mucins and β-catenin/Wnt signaling in pancreatic cancer [17]. Mucins have particularly been shown to be influenced by various O-glycosylation genes. Additionally, since MUC1 driven β-catenin/Wnt signaling is known to play an important role in BC progression, we evaluated their clinical significance, along with their target gene, Cyclin D1. Notably, the cytoplasmic tail of MUC1 has been reported to interact directly with β-catenin, facilitating its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation of Wnt target genes, including Cyclin D1 [20, 21]. The study also identified the differential expression of MUC1, β-catenin, and Cyclin D1 with respect to the HER2 status. Taken together, this study provides a preliminary evaluation of HER2-associated O-glycosylation, highlighting GCNT3 as a potential modulator of the MUC1/β-catenin/Cyclin D1 axis. These findings shed light on breast cancer biology and could help improve diagnostic stratification and the identification of glycosylation-related biomarkers with clinical relevance.

Materials and methods

In silico gene expression analysis

Using the GEO, GTex, TCGA, and TARGET databases for breast cancer patients, the online web function of KMPlot (TNMplot.com) was used for gene expression analysis of specific genes namely ST3GAL1, GCNT3, and ERBB2/HER2 [22]. A Violin plot was generated for these three genes to compare the expression levels between tumor and normal patients, with p ≤ 0.05 (Mann-Whitney test) being considered statistically significant for this analysis. Additionally, TCGA BC dataset was used from the GEPIA to perform differential gene expression for HER2, ST3GAL1, and GCNT3 and generate a box plot for HER2, ST3GAL1, and GCNT3, respectively.

Survival analysis

For survival analysis of ST3GAL1, GCNT3 and HER2; KMPlot was used [23]. This online tool permitted the generation of survival plots for genes from various cancer datasets, including breast cancer. Patient data was split according to trichotomization while other parameters were kept unchanged. The portal compared survival data between the high and low gene expressions and also provided information on the median survival. Herein, statistical significance was based on p ≤ 0.05 using LogRank test.

Correlational analysis from R2 and cbioportal

Breast Cancer Pan Atlas TCGA dataset was retrieved from the R2 database and analysed using “geneset” mode to assess for glycosylation and mucin pathways [24]. Subsequently, a heatmap was generated displaying the patient chart for glycogenes. To obtain the volcano plot, the KEGG gene pathway was chosen followed by specific genes, such as ERBB2. To perform data differentiation, profiling was carried out based on HER2 IHC score, and a specific pathway was selected. Consequently, a volcano plot was generated and edited to show correlation of ERBB2/HER2 with ST3GAL1. Additional analysis was conducted using the scatter plots from R2 and cBioPortal based on the Breast Cancer TCGA dataset, which includes 1084 samples [25]. Oncomine portal was searched for the ST3GAL1 glycogene from the TCGA Breast Cancer database [26]. The output plot was generated based on the HER2 immunohistochemistry values ranging from 0 to 3+. Bar plots were generated using these parameters and exported from the website for further analysis.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

From the NCBI based GEO database, file (accession # GSE29431) containing the probe ID, phenotype ID, sample ID, and data was exported. Using these files as input, data and phenotype files were generated for the BC patient samples in the format of the GSEA desktop toolkit [27]. We chose an appropriate chip file to collapse the data that resulted in the analysis of approximately 20,000 genes in these samples. After inputting files and running the GSEA toolkit, index.html file was obtained as an output data which was further used for the experiments. For Enrichment Map Visualization, Cytoscape 3.3 + and Enrichment Map plugins were installed locally and the GSEA result file was inputted in the Enrichment Map tool [28]. This enabled the visualization of the enrichment plots on the plugins.

Cell culture

Mycoplasma free cell lines were procured from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and were subcultured on reaching appropriate confluence. Breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 (ATCC Catalog HTB-26), MDA-MB-435 (ATCC Catalog HTB-129), and MCF-7 (ATCC Catalog HTB-22) were cultured along with a control cell line HEK-293 (ATCC Catalog CRL-1573) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). HER2 positive cell line SKBR3 (ATCC Catalog HTB-30) was cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium. All cell lines were grown in the presence of 10% FBS and 5% CO2 that are used for downstream experiments as desired.

Toxicity assay

Standard MTT assay was performed in order to find out the IC50 value of talniflumate (Sigma, SML1710) for various cell lines. Different BC cell lines were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS media (SkBr3 in McCoy’s 5 A medium) and seeded in 96-well plate at a concentration of 10,000 cells/well. After 24 h, varying concentrations (0–120 µM) of talniflumate were added to the wells and allowed to incubate. After 48 h, the media was removed and cells were incubated with 50 µl of serum free media and 10–20 µl of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) for 3–4 h (extreme care was taken for SKBR3 cells while aspiration since they are semi-adherent). Thereafter, 10–20 µl of DMSO (Himedia, TC433) was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. The plate was wrapped with foil and incubated for 15 min and absorbance was measured at 570 nm. Cell activity was calculated by comparing the absorbance of treated vs. untreated control. For all the cell lines, 4 technical and 3 biological replicates were used to measure toxicity. After standardization, a concentration of 100 μm talniflumate was used for further experiments.

Wound healing (Scratch) assay

Cell migration was assessed for different BC cell lines in the presence and absence of GCNT3 inhibitor, talniflumate [17]. The Olympus microscope for advanced live imaging applications was used to monitor the wound closure at different time points:12 h, 24 h and 48 h. The wound closure area was measured using the NIH-based ImageJ software. For each cell line, n = 3 technical and biological replicates were used.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded at a concentration of 500–1000 cells/ well in a six-well plate and allowed to grow. After 2–3 days of seeding cells, talniflumate (100 μm) was added to the wells with DMSO (0.05%) as control (DMSO was used to dissolve talniflumate). This procedure was repeated every 3 days and after 12–15 days, ice-cold methanol was added onto the wells for 15–20 min to fix the cells and stained with crystal violet solution (0.1% w/v in methanol). Colonies were counted using the NIH-based ImageJ software [29]. For each cell line, n = 3 technical and biological replicates were used.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR

Cells harvested post trypsinization were pelleted down and dissolved in the RNA lysis buffer. Thereafter, RNA was isolated using the Nucleospin RNA extraction kit (Macherey-Nagel company, 740955) following manufacturer’s instructions and estimated the yield. Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out after checking RNA quality on the agarose gel. As a first step, RNA was converted into cDNA using Prime Script 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen, 18080093). Then, reactions were carried in a 384-well plate using 1 µl of cDNA per sample and respective glycogene specific primers using Power SYBR Green master mix (Applied Biosystems, 4368702) and Ct values were recorded. This data was normalized with respect to the house-keeping gene, β-actin (ACTB) [additional housekeeping genes could be used for better comparison] and relative fold-change was calculated using the delta-delta Ct method by employing Ct values from HEK293 cells that was used as a control. For all cell lines, 3 technical and 3 biological replicates were used.

Patient sample collection

25 Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC) samples of different grades and stages were collected from women aged between 45 and 65 years from Regional Cancer Centre (RCC) for gene expression studies fulfilling inclusion [women aged > = 45 < = 65 years; naive sample without chemotherapy; confirmed IDC diagnosis and confirmed HER2 + ve cases] and exclusion criteria [women aged < 45 > 65 years; breast cancer other than IDC; all IDC cases that underwent chemotherapy, all cases than HER -ve cases] as suggested initially by Internal Review Board (IRB), RCC. Later, ethical approvals were taken from the committees of both Regional Cancer Centre (RCC), Thiruvananthapuram and Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology (CCMB), Hyderabad for further experimentation. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, after obtaining written informed consent from all patients. Upon pathological examination, samples were segregated into Grade 2 and Grade 3, and Stages I, II, III within each grade. They were also sub-classified based on their hormonal status of the tissues as ER +ve/-ve, PR +ve/-ve, HER2 + ve/-ve tissues; and determined tumor vs. adjacent normal status of the tissues as ascertained by pathologists at RCC. They were then used to carry out immunohistochemical analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The IHC protocol followed was as described in Kumar et al., [30]. Slides were washed with decreasing concentration of isopropanol (IPA) from 100% to 70% and thereafter incubated for 30 min in boiling antigen retrieval buffer (either Sodium Citrate or Tris-EDTA depending on the antibody). Thereafter, slides were allowed to cool, washed in TBST and then blocked for 2 h with 10% BSA. This was followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies, rabbit anti-ST3GalAL1 polyclonal antibody (Abcam, ab96129), rabbit anti-GCNT3 polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-24455), rabbit anti-MUC1 monoclonal antibody (Abcam, ab109185), rabbit anti-β-Catenin monoclonal antibody (Abcam, ab32572) and rabbit anti-Cyclin D1 monoclonal antibody (Abcam, ab16663). Subsequently, endogenous peroxidase blocking with H2O2 + Methanol was carried out for 20 min followed by TBS washes. Thereafter, anti-Rb secondary antibody conjugated with HRP (Abcam, 205718) incubation was carried out for 1.5 h followed by 3 washes with TBST. The staining procedure was carried out with DAB + chromogen (DAKO, DM827, Denmark A/S) followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Thereafter, slides were incubated with increasing concentrations of IPA from 70% to 100% and placed in xylene before mounting. NIH-based ImageJ software was used to quantify slides. In all cases, n = 3 technical and biological replicates were used.

Statistics

Data are shown as mean ± SEM (n = 3). Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism software. Wherever relevant, standard error was calculated and reported using the formulae Standard Deviation/Sqrt (No of samples). Student’s t-test was used to calculate the p-values in various experiments. p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant in all cases. Violin plots depict the data distribution, and statistical significance was determined using the Mann–Whitney U test (p < 0.05). For bioinformatics data, p-value calculations have been reported separately in the methods section. For survival analysis, statistical significance was based on p ≤ 0.05 using the LogRank test.

Results

In silico analysis revealed differentially expressed glycogenes in HER2 + ve BC that could promote tumorigenic pathways

Aberrant glycosylation is a distinct feature of cellular dysfunction and thus it’s imperative to study the genes involved in its process during cancer malignancy. To address this, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using glycosylation genesets on the HER2 + ve and -ve phenotypes of BC patients. Qualitative assessment from this analysis suggested a differential expression of several glycogenes namely B3GAT3, GCNT1, GALNT14, B3GALT4, GALNT7, FUT3, MGAT4A, C1GALT1, B3GNT9, MUC2, ST3GAL1, ST6GALNAC2, etc. on the HER2 + ve phenotype (Fig. S1 A – F, Fig. 1A). To this end, additional experimental evidence based on RT-PCR and immunoblot analysis is needed to assess their elevated expression. From the gene sets enriched in the HER2 + ve phenotype of GSEA analysis, we focused on glycosylation-related and KRAS-dependent genesets for further analysis. Glycosylation related genes were queried to Panther, which identified more than 20 signaling pathways [31]. Some of the key pathways obtained through this analysis were Integrin signaling pathway, mannose metabolism, N-acetylglucosamine metabolism, O-antigen biosynthesis, Wnt signaling, TGF-β signaling pathways etc. The associated biological processes obtained along with these included adhesion, regulation, cellular process, developmental process, locomotion, metabolic process, signaling etc. (Fig. 1B). Genes inputted from the “KRAS” dependent geneset revealed a list of 36 pathways that include angiogenesis, apoptosis signaling pathway, cell cycle, DNA replication, TGF-β signaling pathway, etc., suggesting a strong affinity towards cancer growth in HER2 + ve phenotype. Also, the biological processes as observed for the “KRAS” gene set consisted of adhesion, regulation, biomineralization, localization, reproduction, signaling, etc. (Fig. 1C).

These results suggested that dysregulated glycogene expression might contribute to tumorigenic pathways in HER2 positive breast cancer. Subsequently, based on their predicted roles, this study selected specific glycogenes namely ST3GAL1 (positive HER2 correlation from GSEA analysis) and GCNT3 (no HER2 correlation from GSEA analysis); and aimed to consolidate their correlation with the HER2 phenotype.

ST3GAL1 exhibits overexpression in BC and reveals an association with HER2 phenotype

As the GSEA analysis from the glycogenes on the HER2 background presented a positive correlation with ST3GAL1, it became imperative to validate any possible correlation between the two genes [32–37]. Using KM plotter, which mined data from the GEO and GDC datasets, Kaplan Mayer plots for HER2 (No. of samples: high-308 vs. low-635) and ST3GAL1 (No. of samples: high-671 vs. low-1361) predicted poor survival for higher expression of these genes in a statistically significant manner. Patients with low ST3GAL1 expression had a median survival of 38 months, while those with high expression had a median survival of 30 months (Fig. 2A).

Additionally, quantitative transcriptomic analysis of HER2 and ST3GAL1 further exhibited their overexpression in BC using two different datasets (Fig. 2C, E). The study utilized three different platforms namely R2, Oncomine and cBioPortal to ascertain the correlation between HER2 and ST3GAL1. In the R2 platform, the data for ST3GAL1 was visualized in the form of a Volcano plot, which depicted the negative correlation of genes towards the left half and positive correlation towards the right half. As HER2 was located on the right half of the volcano plot, it indicated a positive correlation with ST3GAL1 (Fig. S2A). The Oncomine analysis also showed an increased ST3GAL1 expression with an increasing IHC score of HER2 (score indicates protein expression levels graded from 0-negative to 4-very strong). (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the Scatterplot from R2 and cBioPortal identified a statistically significant correlation (p ≤ 0.05) between ST3GAL1 and HER2 (Fig. 2D, F).

Experimental validation of the correlation between ST3GAL1 and HER2 was carried out by RT-PCR and immunohistochemical analysis. The qRT-PCR results revealed a higher expression of ST3GAL1 gene in HER2 + ve SKBR3 cells (22-fold) as compared to HER2-ve MDAMB231 (1.8-fold), MCF7 (2.5-fold), and MDAMB435 (5-fold), respectively (Fig. 3A). All experiments were repeated as per MIQE guidelines and results were represented as relative quantification (fold-change) with significant p-value ≤ 0.05. To further validate this finding clinically, we performed immunohistochemistry analysis for ST3GAL1 in HER2 + ve and HER2 -ve BC tissues of different grades (Grade 2 & 3) and stages (Stages I, II & III) of IDC samples. Clinical significance of ST3GAL1 was observed in BC as its expression increased from Stage I to III in Grade 2 and Grade 3 BC tissues (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, the expression of ST3GAL1 in HER2 + ve patients was 2-fold higher in Stage II (Grade3) patients compared to similar stage and grade of HER2 negative IDC patients (Fig. 3B, D). These findings corroborate with the earlier qRT-PCR findings that there might be a positive correlation between HER2 with ST3GAL1.

In silico and in vitro analysis of GCNT3 suggests its overexpression in BC and predicts inverse relation with HER2

GCNT3 is an important glycosyltransferase gene belonging to the GCNT/C2GNT family, hence assessed for its role in breast cancer. The Kaplan-Meier plot for GCNT3 highlighted poor survival outcomes associated with its higher expression (No. of samples: high-1396 vs. low-483) with the median survival being 135.88 vs. 84 months for low vs. high expression, respectively (Fig. 4A). Quantitative transcriptomic analysis of GCNT3 exhibited its overexpression in BC using two different datasets (Fig. 4B, C). Quantitative RT-PCR data reported inverse correlation of GCNT3 with HER2 as found for HER2-ve MCF7 (11-fold) and MDAMB231 (5-fold) as compared to MDAMB435 (0.05-fold) and SKBR3 (3-fold) (Fig. 4D). Regarding its relationship with HER2, GSEA analysis from the glycogenes on the HER2 background presented no trend for GCNT3. Clinically, steady progress in the expression of GCNT3 was noticed from Stage I to III in Grade 2 and Grade 3 BC tissues (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E, F). However, the expression of GCNT3 in HER2 -ve patients was ~ 2-fold higher in Stage II (Grade 3) patients compared to similar stage and grade of HER2 + ve IDC patients (Fig. 4E, G). Taken together, these results indicated the inverse relation of GCNT3 with HER2 in BC.

Glycosyltransferase GCNT3 plays an oncogenic role in breast cancer independent of HER2 status

Several studies have earlier shown the oncogenic role of GCNT3 in different cancers using knockdown, knockout and talniflumate inhibitor experiments [38, 39]. Prior structural, transcriptional, and functional studies have established talniflumate as the most extensively characterized GCNT3 inhibitor, with demonstrated inhibitory effects at the mRNA and protein levels across multiple cancer models [17, 39–41]. Thus, to identify the functional significance of overexpressed GCNT3 in BC, we used talniflumate to inhibit its expression in HER2 + ve and -ve breast cancer cells. Talniflumate, was used at 100 µM in HER2 + ve SKBR3 cells as well as HER2 -ve MCF7, MDAMB231, and MDAMB435 cell lines after determining the IC50 value using MTT assay (Fig. S3A) [17]. Talniflumate at 100 µM inhibited GCNT3 mRNA levels in MDAMB231 (p = 0.04) and MCF7 (p = 0.024) whereas there was insignificant difference in MDAMB435 (p = 0.095) suggesting its inhibitory effect (Fig. S3B). The scratch assay of the treated cells showed a reduced area closure for wound healing at 48 h, 24 h and 24 h respectively in MCF7, MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-435 cells (Fig. 5A, C, E). This suggested an oncogenic role of GCNT3 in breast cancer pathogenesis. These results were reinstated by colony formation assays in which SKBR3 and MCF7 showed 2-fold reduction in the number of colonies compared to other cell lines (MDAMB231, MDAMB435) where it was 6-10-fold reduction after the inhibitor treatment indicating a functional role for GCNT3 in supporting clonogenic growth (Fig. 5B, D, F, H). Overall, these findings suggested an oncogenic role of GCNT3 in breast cancer pathogenesis, independent of the HER2 status.

Glycoprotein MUC1, β-Catenin and Cyclin D1 are differentially expressed in BC clinical specimens

Since glycosylation results in the synthesis of glycoproteins, we aimed to explore additional potential partners that might play an important role in BC. Thus, global GSEA was performed on the normal vs. tumor BC, using the same GEO dataset as earlier, that resulted in an enrichment of “immunity” along with “glycosylation” genesets. To this end, we found an over-expression of MUC1, MUC5Ac, GCNT1, GCNT3 etc., in tumor samples, suggesting their role in BC pathogenesis (Fig. S4A). We selected the glycoprotein MUC1 (which modulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling), β-catenin (the signaling’s key effector) and Cyclin D1 (a Wnt target gene) to assess their clinical relevance in breast cancer. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the expression of β-catenin increased from Stage I to III in both Grade 2 and 3 tissues. In Stage III Grade 3 tissue, we observed a translocation of β-catenin expression from cytoplasm to nucleus suggesting its role in tumorigenesis (Fig. 6A, D). However, MUC1 expressions in different grades and stages varied from being cytoplasmic to membranous localization. Its expression was found to be the lowest in Stage I Grade 2 tissue but remained similar through from Stage II Grade 2 tissues to Stage III Grade 3. This might suggest a role of MUC1 in breast cancer initiation and progression, but further experiments are required to validate the same (Fig. 6B, E). Unlike β-catenin and MUC1, the overall expression of Cyclin D1 was less in BC tissues in immunohistochemical analysis. However, the quantification based on ImageJ analysis revealed an increased expression of Cyclin D1 from Stage I to III in Grade 2 tissues, while higher expression in Grade 3 tissues remained consistent across Stage I to III (Fig. 6C, F). Additionally, we used HER2 + ve BC tissues to quantify the expression of MUC1, β-catenin and Cyclin D1. As reported in previous studies, we found no significant correlation pattern for MUC1, β-catenin expression with the HER2 status. However, an inverse correlation was observed for Cyclin D1 with HER2 (Fig. 7A, B, C). This analysis further showed the involvement of downstream genes of the Wnt signaling pathway, particularly along the MUC1/β-catenin/Cyclin D1 axis in BC progression and metastasis.

GCNT3 might interact with MUC1/β-catenin/Cyclin D1 axis in BC

Several studies showed regulation of mucins such as MUC1, MUC4, MUC5Ac etc. by GCNT3 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [17, 40]. Since MUC1 is one of the most prominent biomarkers in BC, and the study identified its relationship with GCNT3 and other non-glycogenes, we performed an in-silico correlation analysis to this end. Thus, we used the TCGA BC dataset and queried MUC1 on the R2 portal to obtain correlation plots with GCNT3, β-catenin, and Cyclin D1. We found a positive correlation of MUC1 with all these genes, suggesting they might interact in breast cancer pathogenesis (Fig. 7D). Inputting GCNT3 on STRING, we identified MUC1 as one of the top 20 interacting nodes (Fig. 7E). Taken together, we explore the role of glycogenes in the HER2-dependent manner on the BC cell lines. Moreover, inverse correlation of HER2 to GCNT3, β-catenin and Cyclin-D1 hints at a mechanistic link between GCNT3 and the β-catenin/Cyclin D1 axis in breast cancer in which MUC1 also plays a role (Fig. 7F), suggesting that GCNT3 may modulate this pathway through transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation.

Discussion

Breast cancer progression and metastasis are driven by multiple processes, including aberrant signaling and glycosylation [42], complicating clinical management. HER2 positive BC is a heterogeneous disease that can progress aggressively and contribute to increased mortality if not treated early. Anti-HER2 therapies have not seen maximal clinical benefit with the advancements in cancer research, in the recent past, due to the inherent complexity in BC [43]. There is a need to investigate other parameters along with HER2 to identify critical areas for therapeutic improvement. From a diagnostic pathology perspective, glycosylation markers may provide additional layers of information to stratify HER2 + and HER2 − breast cancer patients, potentially improving risk assessment and guiding therapeutic choices. To this end, evidence from our in vitro and in silico studies revealed an association between glycosylation and MUC1/β-catenin/cyclin D1 axis in the context of HER2 status of BC.

Earlier studies on BC have highlighted the role of ST3GAL1 in tumorigenesis. Hence, we have focused on this gene as one of the key glycosyltransferases. To this end, it was found out that ST3GAL1, along with other glycogenes, may play an important role in BC, potentially influenced by HER2 status. This is particularly noteworthy, as previous studies have investigated ST3GAL1 in the context of BC, but not specifically in relation to HER2 expression. Consequently, the overexpression of ST3GAL1 might contribute to HER2-dependent BC tumorigenesis. These findings suggest that evaluation of ST3GAL1 expression in routine histopathological samples could complement HER2 testing, opening avenues for refined prognostic panels. However, in contrast to our findings, Patil et al. (2014) reported an inverse relationship between sialyl-TF antigen expression and HER2 level in BC tissues [44]. Thus, there may be factors other than ST3GAL1 expression, driving the synthesis of sialyl-TF antigen in BC, warranting further investigations. The role of ST3GAL1 in generating the glycan structures that interact with Siglecs on innate immune cells, inhibiting NK and T cell function, and driving the generation of pathogenic macrophage phenotypes in distinct cancers might also contribute to a suppressed tumor immune landscape [45–47].

Along with ST3GAL1, we also looked at the expression of GCNT3, which forms Core-2 trisaccharide on O-glycans. While C2GNT1 also facilitates Core 2 formation, we prioritized the investigation of GCNT3 to highlight its relatively unexplored role in BC. There has been only one report before, which partially focused on GCNT3 in BC [38]. Thus, this study provides early knowledge into the role of GCNT3 expression in BC progression from Stage I to III in Grade 2 and 3 invasive ductal carcinoma tissues. Importantly, the progressive increase of GCNT3 expression across tumor stages highlights its future potential as a tissue-based biomarker accessible to pathology practice. Moreover, we also found its transcriptional expression to be highly elevated in HER2 -ve BC cells. GCNT3 is a glycosyltransferase which belongs to the GCNT/C2GnT family that adds N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) to N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) thereby synthesizing Core-2 trisaccharide on O-glycans [48–50]. Synthesis of this structure is critical as this is further elongated by polylactosamine structures to form sLeX or extended sLeX type glycans [51]. These glycans, when present on cancer cells, have crucial functions as they bind to P- and E-selectin on endothelium thereby promoting metastatic growth [17, 52].

In the presence of talniflumate, a GCNT3 inhibitor, we observed reduction in wound-healing and colony formation suggesting an oncogenic role for this gene in BC pathogenesis. This further adheres with the above explanation suggesting that reduction in sLeX due to the loss of GCNT3 may reduce BC onset. While genetic silencing (KD/KO) was not performed in the current study, a chemical-genetic phenocopy for GCNT3 inhibition versus talniflumate treatment has been established previously. Specifically, Sumardika et al. (2018) demonstrated that both siRNA-mediated GCNT3 silencing and pharmacologic inhibition with talniflumate produce identical anti-migratory phenotypes for melanoma cell lines. Furthermore, because talniflumate has been shown to suppress the increased motility driven by GCNT3 overexpression; this obviates the need for additional rescue experiments [53]. Since talniflumate suppresses malignant behaviors in a manner that mirrors genetic depletion in independent models, these published data provide robust on-target validation for our cellular experiment findings [17, 39–41, 53]. However, we did not observe any consistent pattern between the GCNT3 expression and the degree of oncogenicity in HER2- and HER2 + BC cell lines. This variability underscores the importance of validating GCNT3 in larger, clinically annotated cohorts to assess its diagnostic and prognostic utility.

Gupta et al. 2020, highlighted the importance of GCNT3 in pancreatic cancer and exhibited its role towards the β-catenin/MUC4 axis. The authors also reported here the differential regulation of other mucins by GCNT3 [17]. In the current study, our immunohistochemistry data suggested elevated expression of β-catenin in the Stage III BC samples as compared to Stage I. This corroborates with the earlier findings from Wang et al. [54]. Moreover, we found high expression of MUC1 from Stage II, Grade 2 onwards in BC tissues. This expression remained similar across other tissues suggesting MUC1’s role starting from the early stages of breast cancer. The MUC1 expression was present in cytoplasmic and membranous regions as earlier reported. As MUC1 cytoplasmic tail is known to interact with β-catenin to activate Wnt signaling, thus its expression might suggest a crucial role in driving BC progression and metastasis through Wnt signalling [20, 21]. The concordance between IHC findings (MUC1, β-catenin, Cyclin D1) and transcriptional analyses underscores the translational significance of our study, providing pathologists with molecularly anchored markers for tissue evaluation. Moreover, we also clinically validate the expression of Cyclin D1, a known target gene of the Wnt pathway. To this end, we found its elevated expression in Stage I to III, Grade 3 tissues. In Grade 2 BC tissues, the Cyclin D1 expression was lower than Grade 3 tissues, suggesting its role in the later stages of breast cancer.

The current results together hint at an interplay of GCNT3 and MUC1/β-catenin/Cyclin-D1 axis in BC. This is supported by the fact that GCNT3 recognizes MUC1 as one of the interacting partners in STRING. Additionally, querying MUC1 in the R2 dataset shows a positive correlation with GCNT3, β-catenin and Cyclin D1. Therefore, our findings suggest the role of ST3GAL1 in breast cancer progression and correlate the involvement of GCNT3 as a mechanistic driver in glycosylation-driven signaling axis of breast cancer pathogenesis.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings establish a connection between glycogene expression and HER2 status in breast cancer, highlighting the contribution of O-glycosylation genes such as ST3GAL1 and GCNT3 to disease progression. We further demonstrated that multiple oncogenic pathways are modulated in the context of elevated HER2 expression, underscoring the complexity of tumor biology. Importantly, we delineate a role for GCNT3 in remodeling the MUC1/β-catenin/Cyclin D1 axis, emphasizing the contribution of MUC1-driven Wnt signaling to breast cancer progression and metastasis. Taken together, these results suggest that glycosylation-related markers may complement established HER2 profiling in the pathology setting and hold potential for improving patient stratification and therapeutic decision-making. Additionally, we also report limitations in the study as the glycogene analysis data is shown only at the mRNA and protein levels (IHC analysis). Further studies at the transcriptomic and proteomic levels are required to support the glycosylation changes with respect to HER2 status of breast cancer pathogenesis.
