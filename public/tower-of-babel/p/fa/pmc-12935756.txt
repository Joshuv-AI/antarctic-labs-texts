A Comparative Study on the Effect of PRP in Healing Diabetic Foot Ulcers Compared to Standard Treatment in Diabetic Patients: A Randomized Clinical Trial Study
Authors: Hossein Parsa, Leila Haji Maghsoudi, Alireza Mohammadzadeh, Heydar Afshar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsEmerging therapies such as autologous platelet‐rich plasma (PRP), which delivers a supraphysiological concentration of growth factors including but not limited to PDGF, VEGF, and TGF‐β have shown promise in enhancing tissue regeneration and healing. This study compares two treatments for diabetic foot ulcers: the standard method (normal saline washing and routine debridement) and the PRP method (autologous platelet‐rich plasma). It evaluates PRP's effectiveness in accelerating healing compared to standard care in diabetic patients, specifically in terms of wound area reduction over a 6‐week period, the proportion of patients achieving complete healing, and the average time to healing.MethodsIn this double‐blind randomized clinical trial conducted on patients referred to Velayat Hospital, 50 eligible type I and II diabetic patients aged 40–70 were randomly allocated to two groups using color‐coded sealed envelopes, ensuring allocation concealment. One group received PRP and the other standard treatment. Double‐blinding was maintained by using identical syringes for both treatments, and independent assessors unaware of group assignments evaluated wound healing weekly.ResultsThere was no significant difference between the two groups regarding gender and HbA1c levels. However, a significant difference was observed in wound healing rates between the PRP method and the standard method across the two groups. Additionally, a meaningful relationship was found when comparing the average age of patients treated with both methods. There was no significant difference between the two groups regarding gender and HbA1c levels.ConclusionsUltimately, our research indicates that the PRP method is a more effective alternative to the standard method alone for the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers. Additionally, younger patients demonstrated a faster rate of diabetic foot ulcer healing. PRP's superior efficacy may justify its moderate upfront costs given potential long‐term savings from reduced complications.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent one of the most common and debilitating complications of diabetes mellitus, contributing significantly to healthcare costs and patient morbidity [1]. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the global prevalence of DFUs among diabetic individuals is approximately 6.3%, with lifetime incidence ranging between 19% and 34% [IDF, 2023] [2]. In the United States alone, over 100,000 lower‐extremity amputations are performed annually due to diabetic complications, with DFUs preceding approximately 85% of these cases [CDC, 2022] [3]. Moreover, DFUs are associated with a 5‐year mortality rate that approaches that of several cancers, underscoring the urgency of effective treatment strategies [4].

Although several therapeutic options exist, including offloading, wound debridement, infection control, and skin grafting, many ulcers remain non‐healing [5]. Among emerging adjunctive treatments, platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) has garnered attention for its potential to accelerate healing through the delivery of concentrated autologous growth factors [6]. While some studies have demonstrated improved healing rates with PRP in chronic wounds including DFUs the literature remains mixed, with discrepancies in preparation protocols, treatment regimens, and clinical endpoints [7, 8].

Blood sugar control is vital for wound healing. Managing infection, considering osteomyelitis, and using effective antibiotics are essential. Extensive debridement and proper footwear to relieve pressure aid healing [9]. PDGF and GMCSF ointments have shown limited success, and skin grafts, though costly, are effective. Preventive care and foot management are crucial in managing DFUs [10].

Chronic wounds in diabetic patients result from the inability to respond to normal regulatory signals, leading to reduced growth factor synthesis and increased factor degradation due to excessive protease activity [11]. Fibroblasts in chronic wounds show decreased proliferative activity, possibly due to aging or fewer growth factor receptors. Treating these wounds with homologous, autologous, or recombinant growth factors can accelerate healing and re‐epithelialization [12]. Autologous growth factors, obtained from the patient's platelets (PRP), have an unclear concentration and composition but are used in a physiological ratio for treatment [13].

With the growing understanding of platelet roles in wound healing, their use in treatment has increased. Studies show platelet use helps preserve limbs, reduces amputation rates, and lowers treatment costs in high‐risk diabetic patients [14]. Platelets initiate healing by forming clots, promoting hemostasis, and releasing active proteins. Hematomas naturally consist of 40% RBC, 9% platelets, and 1% WBC, while PRP contains 40% platelets, 9% RBC, and 1% WBC, along with clotting factors and secretory proteins [15].

Beyond clinical outcomes, evaluating the economic viability of PRP is critical for adoption in public health systems, where cost constraints often dictate treatment accessibility.

DFUs represent a major global healthcare burden, and exploring cost‐effective and efficacious interventions such as PRP is of critical importance. Our study was designed with this goal in mind to evaluate whether autologous PRP can serve as a viable adjunct to standard care in a randomized clinical setting.

Methods

This double‐blind, randomized clinical trial evaluated the clinical efficacy of PRP versus standard treatment for DFUs. The study, conducted at Velayat Hospital, enrolled eligible patients aged 40–70 after screening and the application of exclusion criteria, such as uncontrolled comorbidities and advanced frailty.

Fifty patients were randomly assigned to two treatment groups using a sealed envelope allocation method to ensure allocation concealment. Specifically, 50 identical opaque envelopes were prepared, each containing a color‐coded card labeled either PRP or Control (25 each). These were shuffled thoroughly and drawn in sequence by an independent third party not involved in treatment or assessment. Double‐blinding was ensured by masking the interventions for both patients and assessors. Patients received either PRP or normal saline in identical syringes, making the treatments visually indistinguishable. Additionally, independent assessors responsible for weekly wound measurements were kept unaware of group allocations throughout the study to prevent bias in outcome evaluation.

Each group consisted of 25 patients. Diabetic wound healing involves daily washing with N/S saline. In the PRP treatment group, wounds were washed with N/S saline as in the standard method but were also treated with weekly PRP injections. Patients were evaluated weekly by a surgeon, with the wound's length and width measured at the start of treatment and then weekly using a ruler. Results were recorded on separate sheets, and wound healing and area reduction were assessed by comparing the first and last weeks.

In this study, the patient's whole blood was centrifuged into layers: platelet‐poor plasma, RBCs, and platelets with WBCs.

Exclusion criteria for this study included infected wounds, women who are pregnant or breastfeeding, wounds causing Charcot deformity, bone involvement, and wounds smaller than 0.4 cm or larger than 34 cm.

The primary outcome of > 50% wound area reduction was selected based on the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) guidelines, which define this threshold as clinically meaningful for evaluating DFU interventions (IWGDF) [16]. Secondary outcomes (complete healing, time to healing) align with FDA benchmarks for chronic wound trials [FDA Guidance [17]. Sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds (e.g., ≥ 75% reduction).

Sampling Procedures

The sample size was calculated a priori using a standard formula for comparing two proportions in clinical trials. Assuming a significance level (α) of 0.05 and power (1–β) of 0.9 (Z₁ = 1.28), and expecting a difference in healing rates between groups based on prior literature (P₁ = 0.7 for PRP group vs. P₂ = 0.3 for control group), the calculated effect size (D = 0.4) indicated that 25 patients per group would be sufficient to detect a statistically significant difference. This estimation was also guided by data reported in the study by Knighton et al. [14], which investigated similar biological treatments for chronic wounds. We have now clarified this in the text for transparency and reproducibility.

Economic parameters (e.g., cost per application, hospitalization days) were recorded but not analyzed statistically due to sample size constraints.

Limitations include a lack of cooperation from patients in attending visits, a lack of cooperation from patients in filling out questionnaires, cases of death due to reasons other than DFUs, and the inability to access patients in such cases. To address these issues, efforts will be made to encourage and advise patients to attend visits while assuring them about confidentiality.

The analysis will be performed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistical methods, Chi‐square test, paired t‐test, and repeated measures ANOVA will be used.

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Qazvin University of Medical Sciences, Qazvin, Iran.

Results

Patient Demographics

A total of 50 patients with DFUs were enrolled and equally divided into two groups (n = 25 per group): one treated with the standard method and the other with PRP therapy.

There were no significant differences between the two groups regarding gender (p = 0.41) or mean age (P = 1.00) (Figure 1). Similarly, HbA1c levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (P = 1.00) (Figure 2).

Wound Healing Outcomes

After 9 weeks of treatment, 60% of PRP‐treated patients (15/25) achieved > 50% wound area reduction, compared to 30% (7/25) in the standard care group (p = 0.032).

Mean wound area decreased from 12.3 to 4.1 cm² in the PRP group and from 11.8 to 7.9 cm² in the standard group (p < 0.05).

Clinical Significance

According to IWGDF (International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot) standards, a > 50% wound reduction threshold represents significant clinical improvement. Using this benchmark:

PRP therapy achieved > 50% reduction in 60% of patients versus 30% in standard care (p = 0.032).

Complete healing (100% wound closure) occurred in 28% of PRP‐treated patients compared to 8% in the standard group (p = 0.04).

These findings underscore the superior efficacy of PRP therapy over standard treatment in promoting wound healing among diabetic foot ulcer patients.

Discussion

Diabetes mellitus is a widespread metabolic disorder that significantly impairs wound healing and increases the risk of infections, making DFUs a prevalent and challenging complication. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of autologous PRP with standard wound care (debridement and saline irrigation) for the management of DFUs. Our findings demonstrate the superiority of PRP in promoting wound healing.

The pathophysiological basis for DFUs involves reduced levels of essential growth factors and diminished biological stimuli due to ischemic and metabolic disturbances in diabetic patients, which collectively inhibit the natural healing process and facilitate infection [11]. PRP addresses this core deficit by delivering a concentrated source of growth factors and cytokines directly to the wound bed. The biological rationale for PRP is well‐established; for instance, Rodriguez demonstrated that thrombin‐activated platelets release a cascade of growth factors, initiating the natural repair process [13]. This release begins within minutes and activates critical repair pathways, with Knighton et al. further showing that these factors stimulate angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, and cellular proliferation [14]. The accelerated healing facilitated by PRP, often reaching its peak effect by the third week, provides a mechanistic explanation for our clinical results.

Our results are consistent with a substantial body of evidence supporting the use of PRP for DFUs. A large study by Villela, Santos, and Deleon et al. on 344 patients found that PRP initiated healing in the majority of cases, with rapid improvement observed in 300 diabetic wounds [15]. This is corroborated by meta‐analyses, such as the one by Carter et al., which confirmed autologous PRP gel as an effective treatment for severe diabetic wounds [16]. Furthermore, our findings align with several contemporary studies. Both our work and that of Malekpour Alamdari et al. [18], Pal Singh et al. [19], and Deng et al. [20] confirm that PRP accelerates DFU healing more effectively than standard care alone. We also extend the findings of Syafira et al. [21], who position PRP as a safe supplementary treatment, by demonstrating its potential as a primary therapeutic option. Similarly, while Peng et al. [22] and Ruiz‐Muñoz et al. [23] support PRP's effectiveness, our results show a pronounced positive impact on healing rates. Finally, our data support and strengthen the conclusions of Azam et al. [24], and del Pino‐Sedeño et al. specifically propose PRP as a potential treatment, a suggestion our findings robustly confirm by demonstrating its clear superiority [25].

An interesting observation from our study was the lack of age‐dependence in the PRP group's healing outcomes. This suggests that the exogenous application of potent growth factors in PRP may override age‐related deficits in endogenous repair mechanisms, upon which standard care relies. This characteristic makes PRP a particularly valuable option for a diverse patient population [26].

Beyond its clinical efficacy, PRP offers several practical advantages. As an autologous product, it carries no risk of transmissible diseases like hepatitis or HIV, and its preparation is straightforward and cost‐effective, ensuring its feasibility for continued use [12]. While the preparation involves moderate upfront costs, the economic profile of PRP is favorable [27]. Studies like Dougherty et al. suggest that faster healing reduces the need for frequent hospital visits, dressing changes, and management of complications [17]. In our cohort, PRP‐treated patients experienced faster recovery with fewer follow‐ups, and notably, no amputations occurred in the PRP group—a significant finding given the profound financial and personal cost of such procedures. Therefore, as an onsite‐prepared autologous therapy, PRP presents a promising and potentially cost‐effective cell‐based treatment for non‐healing DFUs, especially in public healthcare settings, though further studies are warranted to fully assess its cost‐effectiveness on a larger scale.

Limitations and Future Directions

Our study has several limitations. The single‐center design and relatively small sample size may affect the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, while we focused on short‐term healing outcomes, long‐term data on ulcer recurrence and patient‐reported quality of life were not collected. Future research should involve larger, multi‐center trials that include these patient‐centered outcomes and incorporate formal cost‐effectiveness analyses to provide robust evidence for healthcare policymakers.

Conclusion

These findings align with prior research supporting PRP's efficacy, and they suggest that PRP therapy may be a valuable adjunct to standard care. However, further large‐scale, blinded, randomized trials are needed to confirm these findings and assess long‐term outcomes, including recurrence rates and cost‐benefit ratios.
