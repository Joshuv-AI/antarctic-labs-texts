Lived experiences of individuals with femoroacetabular impingement syndrome: a qualitative investigation
Authors: Lindsay Sullivan, Harrison Vriese, Emily Williams, Ellie Ritter, Tyler Barker, Sonal Singh, Jennifer Carter, Jay Patel, Marcia Edwards, William K. Vasileff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the increasing rate of hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS), very little research has examined the lived experiences of individuals scheduled to undergo hip arthroscopy for FAIS. This study aimed to understand the experiences of individuals aged 18 and older with a planned hip arthroscopy for FAIS and to present the findings through their voices. Understanding the lived experiences of patients with FAIS may inform the development of preoperative preparation programs that meet the needs of this growing population.MethodsWe conducted 12 semi-structured telephone interviews with individuals aged 18 and older with FAIS (women = 9, men = 3, age = 39 ± 18.8 years). All participants were scheduled for a hip arthroscopy for FAIS within 30 days of the interview. Interviews were conducted between March 2023 and February 2024. Data were analyzed using the methods of open coding, axial coding, and selective coding.ResultsWe identified eight themes during data analysis, which we divided into three categories: impact of hip condition (chronic pain, emotional and mental well-being, activities of daily living), views toward surgery and the rehabilitation process (optimism, pessimism), and perceived facilitators to recovery following hip arthroscopy (information gathering, preparation for surgery, social support).ConclusionsOur results support the need for interventions that promote social, emotional, and mental well-being in individuals with a planned hip arthroscopy for FAIS. Preoperative preparation programs that seek to provide comprehensive emotional and cognitive preparation for hip arthroscopy for FAIS and recovery from surgery may be warranted.

Introduction

Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) is a condition characterized by contact between the femoral head–neck junction and the acetabular rim due to altered bone morphology [1, 2], potentially leading to hip pain and joint degeneration [3, 4]. FAIS is linked to factors such as younger age (i.e., adolescents and young adults), Caucasian ethnicity, and participation in high-intensity sports during adolescence [4–9]. Athletes involved in high-impact activities and individuals in physically demanding occupations exhibit a higher prevalence of FAIS compared to non-athletes [3, 4]. In recent years, the incidence of FAIS has increased significantly. Specifically, the incidence of FAIS has risen from 40.3 per 100,000 person-years between 2000 and 2004 to 72.6 per 100,000 person-years between 2010 and 2016, representing an approximate 80% increase over this period [10].

Accurate diagnosis of FAIS relies on comprehensive clinical evaluations, including specific physical examination or imaging techniques (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging) [3, 4]. Conservative treatments such as activity modification, rest, physical therapy, anti-inflammatory medications and injections are generally the initial approach for FAIS. However, surgical options including hip arthroscopy may be considered if these methods do not alleviate patient-reported symptoms [2, 3]. The utilization of hip arthroscopy for FAIS has risen dramatically over recent years due to advancements in clinician awareness, diagnostic imaging, and surgical techniques [4, 10]. Data show that hip arthroscopy utilization increased from 1 to 20% among patients diagnosed with FAIS between 2000 and 2016 [10], and overall implementation of hip arthroscopy increased by up to 600% from 2006 to 2010 [11]. Hip arthroscopy for FAIS has demonstrated high success rates, with significant improvements in pain relief and function [12–17], and low revision rates reported at mid- to long-term follow-ups [4–6, 18–20].

FAIS significantly impacts patients’ physical functioning [20–22] and quality of life [23, 24]. Severe hip pain, which is one of the most common symptoms of FAIS, can limit mobility and disrupt daily activities, including work, leisure, and parenting responsibilities, leading to considerable emotional distress [3, 25–27]. Additionally, FAIS increases the risk of developing musculoskeletal injuries such as hamstring tendon injury, labral damage, and advancing osteoarthritis [4]. Recent studies have reported a high prevalence of mental health issues among patients with FAIS, with anxiety or depression affecting over one-third of preoperative patients [28–32]. Moreover, pre-existing mental health disorders have been associated with inferior outcomes following hip arthroscopy for FAIS, suggesting that mental health plays a crucial role in recovery [19, 32–36]. These psychological factors underscore the importance of mental health in recovery from hip arthroscopy for FAIS and suggest that addressing preoperative mental health concerns may improve postoperative outcomes.

Despite these findings, few qualitative studies have explored the lived experiences of patients with FAIS, particularly regarding the interplay between physical symptoms and mental health. More qualitative research is needed to better our understanding of the holistic impact of FAIS on patients’ lives and to address existing knowledge gaps. Addressing existing gaps in the literature is essential for developing comprehensive, patient-centered treatment approaches that consider both physical and mental health aspects of FAIS [25, 30]. This qualitative study aimed to explore the experiences and expectations of individuals aged 18 and older with a planned hip arthroscopy for FAIS.

Methods

Study design and participants

This qualitative study was conducted among male and female patients (≥ 18 years of age) with a planned primary hip arthroscopy to treat FAIS at a single academic institution (The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, Columbus OH USA) within 30 days of consent. The diagnosis of FAIS with a Grade 0–1 Tönnis Osteoarthritis and no significant dysplasia was confirmed in the electronic medical records and with the treating orthopaedic surgeon. The Institutional Review Board at The Ohio State University approved this study and its consent procedure (Protocol ID: 2022B0376). Online written informed consent was obtained from participants through REDCap prior to the telephone interview. The study findings are reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research [37].

After obtaining informed consent, participants completed a brief online survey via REDCap. The survey consisted of closed-ended items regarding demographics (e.g., gender, race/ethnicity, marital status), fear of surgery, and anxiety. Fear of surgery was assessed using the Surgical Fear Questionnaire (SFQ) [38]. The SFQ includes eight items that are divided into two subdimensions: (1) fear of short-term consequences (e.g., “I am afraid of the operation”, “I am afraid of the anesthesia”) and (2) fear of long-term consequences (e.g., “I am afraid that the operation will fail”, “I am afraid of the long duration of the rehabilitation after the operation”) [38]. The items are rated on a scale from 0 (not at all afraid) to 10 (very afraid), with higher scores indicating a greater fear of surgery. Anxiety was assessed using the State Trait Anxiety Inventory, a validated 40-item self-reported measure of current anxiety symptoms and an individual’s tendency to be anxious; [39] the total score ranges from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety.

We then conducted an in-depth, semi-structured telephone interview lasting up to 30 min with each individual participant. All interviews were independently performed by the same researcher and conducted within 30 days prior to the patient’s planned hip arthroscopy for FAIS. The development of the semi-structured interview guide was informed by current literature relating to FAIS and recovery from hip arthroscopy for FAIS. The interview guide focused on the patient’s emotional and behavioral responses to their hip condition (e.g., “How has your hip condition affected you?”) and anticipated views towards surgery and the recovery process (e.g., “In your own words, what do you anticipate being the most challenging aspect of your recovery from surgery?”). All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed, ensuring the removal of any identifying information to maintain anonymity. The recruitment period for this study was between January 2, 2023, and January 2, 2024.

Data analysis

Demographic characteristics of the participants were described, and descriptive data considered. Data from the qualitative interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis [40]. First, three authors (L.S., E.W., E.R.) independently read the transcripts several times to familiarize themselves with the data, documenting preliminary notes on important details from the interviews. Second, the authors (L.S., E.W., E.R.) coded the transcripts line-by-line, generating initial codes with respect to the research question. Third, the authors grouped codes into themes—the core ideas, patterns, or concepts that were identified from the data- based on broader patterns identified in the data. The coding scheme was designed to identify patients’ experiences with FAIS and their views towards their upcoming surgery. Initial themes were then discussed and revised among the authors through an iterative approach to create the code book. The authors (L.S., E.W., E.R.) then coded the transcripts using the agreed-upon themes (i.e., the codebook). This process was repeated until no new themes were identified by the authors. The authors then identified quotations that exemplified the themes pertaining to the research question.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 12 patients were interviewed before surgery. Of the 12 participants, most were female (n = 9, 75%) and White (n = 12, 100%) (Table 1). Participants ranged in age from 20 to 78 years old (mean = 39.3, standard deviation = 17.7). In general, three-quarters of participants reported their health as good (n = 6, 50.0%) or very good (n = 3, 25.0%). Two-thirds of participants (n = 8, 66.7%) self-reported a prior diagnosis of a mental health disorder, such as Generalized Anxiety Disorder or Major Depressive Disorder. The mean score of the Surgical Fear Questionnaire was 33.3 ± 16.1 (possible range = 0–80), the mean score of the short-term consequences subdimension was 15.7 ± 8.6 (possible range = 0–40), and the mean score of the long-term consequences subdimension was 17.6 ± 9.1 (possible range = 0–40). There is no universally accepted clinical cutoff score for the Surgical Fear Questionnaire, although higher scores indicate greater fear of surgery. The mean state anxiety score was 39.1 ± 12.3 (range = 22–58), while the mean trait anxiety score was 42.0 ± 13.3 (range = 20–63). Eight participants were categorized as having low state anxiety and four were categorized as having moderate state anxiety. For trait anxiety, seven participants reported low levels of trait anxiety, three reported a moderate level of anxiety, and two reported a severe level of anxiety. Themes and subthemes

Impact of hip condition

We discovered that living with FAIS can have a profound impact on daily life, causing the presence of chronic pain and leading to reduced emotional and mental well-being, and a considerable decrease in activities of daily living (ADL).

Chronic pain

For the subtheme of chronic pain, many participants described how their hip condition led to the development of chronic pain, with pain persisting for months to years before they were able to get a definitive diagnosis. One participant explained, “Every morning I wake up and I know that I’m going to be in pain for the entire day, no matter what I do… It’s really an awful feeling, knowing that there’s no way to prevent it.” (P2) Some participants noted that their pain was difficult to manage, resulting in failed treatments and frustration. Other participants indicated how their hip pain hindered their ability to engage in extracurricular activities.

Emotional and mental well-being

For the subtheme of emotional and mental well-being, participants generally described a significant decline in their emotional and mental well-being after the onset of their hip symptoms. Several participants highlighted how it took years to get a diagnosis, which delayed the formulation of an effective treatment plan. Participants indicated how this delay in treatment took a toll on their emotional and mental well-being. Participant 5 said, “… It’s been very, very hard on my mental health. I’ve actually recently been diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder, extreme anxiety, and severe depression.” (P5) One participant remarked how their hip condition exacerbated their pre-existing mental health problems. They said, “I struggle with anxiety and depression like it’s been diagnosed and stuff, and when you’re in pain like that stuff just gets ten times worse.” (P1) Another participant highlighted the importance of self-care and staying present-focused, noting that these approaches helped them cope with their hip condition and the associated symptoms. They said, “I just am trying to maintain a level of self-care and just take it day by day… Just trying to take it one step at a time and just not look too far forward…” (P3).

Activities of daily living

Most participants discussed how their hip condition prevented them from performing many activities of daily living (ADLs). Many participants noted how the impact of their condition on ADLs was frustrating and affected their autonomy and quality of life. One participant acknowledged this sentiment by stating, “It’s a frustrating thing just not to be able to do what you want to do on a normal basis. For something that’s completely out of your control.” (P4) Another participant shared how they missed who they used to be prior to the onset of FAIS and the associated symptoms, highlighting the impact of FAIS on their identity. Other participants expressed how their hip condition impacted their ability to engage in activities with their friends and family, which negatively affected their emotional, physical, and social wellness.

Views towards surgery and the rehabilitation process

The second theme that we identified was views towards surgery and the rehabilitation process, which yielded two subthemes: (1) optimism and (2) pessimism.

Optimism

Some participants described how hopeful they were about surgery and recovery. The participants who had a more positive outlook towards surgery described their eagerness for surgery and relief that there was a potential solution to address their hip symptoms and the pain associated with their condition. One participant said, “I was relieved, it’s nice to know there’s a solution to a long-term problem.” Another participant said, “I wish that I could’ve scheduled it the next day… It’s been going on for a very long time and I’m just excited to deal with it.” (P2) Several participants also noted how they were hopeful that their hip surgery would return them to their prior level of functioning.

Pessimism

However, not all participants were optimistic about surgery and the rehabilitation process. Several participants expressed fear and anxiety about their upcoming surgery, encompassing the preoperative, perioperative, and/or postoperative periods. Some participants expressed preoperative anxiety, particularly around their to-do list prior to undergoing surgery. Other participants described fear and anxiety about the surgical procedure itself, especially about the pain they may experience immediately following surgery. One participant said, “I just know it’s really going to hurt initially… knowing you’re deliberately doing something where you’re going to be in pain for a little while is just unnerving for me.” (P4) Another remarked about their fear of regaining mobility after surgery, stating, “I’m really concerned that it will be very scary to be mobile after surgery…” (P5) Several other participants noted fear and anxiety about the surgery not being successful in reducing their pain or returning them to their prior level of functioning.

Perceptions of facilitators for optimal recovery

We identified diverse views relating to perceived facilitators of optimal recovery, which was conceptualized as minimal postoperative complications, regaining control and independence, and the ability to perform ADLs without difficulty or pain. Perceptions of facilitators for optimal recovery were categorized into three subthemes: (1) information gathering, (2) preparation for surgery, and (3) social support.

Information gathering

Many participants described the importance of gathering information about the surgery and recovery process. Participants highlighted the importance of asking questions to one’s healthcare team as well as from individuals who had a history of hip arthroscopy. They emphasized the value of learning what to expect during surgery and the recovery process—including the typical recovery trajectory for hip arthroscopy patients, such as when individuals can return to ADLs following surgery.

It was noted that individuals should thoroughly review the information provided to them by their healthcare team. Many participants wanted their healthcare team to be transparent and thorough about what surgery and the recovery process entails, including providing suggestions to help promote optimal recovery from surgery, and to be responsive to patient questions. Several participants described how asking questions about the information provided by the healthcare team enabled them to obtain necessary information about their upcoming surgery and increased the quality of information they received prior to surgery.

Preparation for surgery

Several participants discussed the importance of preparing their home for surgery. Participants described the need to make their home environment more accessible, safe, and comfortable for the post-operative period. Some participants noted the need to distribute household responsibilities prior to surgery. Participants also highlighted the importance of organizing activities for the initial days and weeks following surgery when mobility is limited. Several participants discussed how identifying activities for the post-operative period could distract them from the pain and boredom they may experience. One participant said, “I’m going to distract myself, I have a list of Netflix movies and TV shows to catch up on, so hopefully I can focus on that even if I can’t do anything else, and that’ll keep me from like ruminating and perseverating on how terrible of a situation it is that week.” (P6) Others highlighted the importance of psychologically preparing for surgery—including setting realistic expectations for recovery, planning for post-surgery care, focusing on positive thinking, and learning about the surgery—to help individuals manage anxiety, stress, and fear before and after surgery.

Social support

Social support was identified as another facilitator to optimal recovery from surgery. Patients described various types and sources of social support that they perceived to be essential to recovery. Participants discussed the perceived importance of emotional support, informational support, social support, and tangible support during the recovery process. Some participants noted the importance of identifying individuals who would listen to them and care for them during the recovery process as well as those who would provide advice, guidance, or feedback. Participant 12 stated, “I think that just having people who are going to take care of you and your recovery, you know that’s really important.”

Moreover, participants identified various sources of social support to help them throughout the recovery process. Many participants indicated that they planned to turn to their family and friends for support during recovery. Others noted that they would also turn to their wider social network or healthcare team for support during recovery from surgery. For example, some participants described how they would turn to their gym community, church, or physical therapist for support. Conversely, one individual spoke to how a lack of social support led to personal struggles coping with their hip condition: “I’ve not really had someone to turn to, which is why I think I’ve struggled the most…” (P5) Regardless of the type or source of social support described, participants articulated how social support is essential to promote optimal recovery outcomes.

Discussion

This qualitative study aimed to fill an important gap in the literature by exploring the experiences and expectations of patients with a planned hip arthroscopy for FAIS. We identified three overall themes: (1) impact of hip condition, (2) views towards surgery and the rehabilitation process, and (3) perceptions of facilitators for optimal recovery.

Impact of hip condition

Most participants described how FAIS and the associated symptoms—including pain, discomfort, or stiffness—had a profound negative impact on their physical and social functioning. More specifically, many participants described experiencing persistent pain and functional impairments that disrupted their ability to work, exercise, or engage in social activities with their family or friends, leading to frustration, emotional distress and, in some cases, symptoms of anxiety or depression. Notably, delays in diagnosis and treatment appeared to further exacerbate emotional distress, reinforcing the need for timely recognition and intervention. Our findings, in line with prior quantitative studies [28, 41, 42], highlight the complex interplay between chronic pain, emotional distress, and social limitations in patients with FAIS and underscore the importance of addressing these factors in preoperative care to optimize surgical outcomes. Additionally, our findings align with those of Bell et al. [43] who reported that individuals with FAIS may experience fear of exercise due to concerns about worsening hip damage. Healthcare professionals should consider incorporating psychosocial screening into FAIS management to identify at-risk patients and provide appropriate support and resources to optimize recovery.

Views towards surgery and the rehabilitation process

We found variation in patients’ views towards surgery and the rehabilitation process. Some participants expressed relief and hope for surgery while others expressed fear and anxiety about the potential pain, complications, and the uncertainty of post-surgical outcomes. Adopting a positive outlook on surgery can influence both the physical and psychological aspects of recovery—including improving adherence to postoperative care recommendations and rehabilitation protocols, reducing surgical complications, and enhancing overall satisfaction with surgery [44, 45]. Conversely, preoperative anxiety and pessimism have been associated with prolonged recovery times and poorer postoperative mental health [46–50]. It is important for healthcare providers to recognize that each patient’s perspective on surgery is influenced by a combination of personal, psychological, cultural, and situational factors. Understanding these factors can help healthcare providers offer more tailored and empathetic patient-centered care. Our findings highlight the need for targeted preoperative and potentially ongoing postoperative counseling to help patients manage expectations and foster a positive mindset for surgery. For example, healthcare providers should consider implementing preoperative educational sessions and practical strategies, such as involving patients in their treatment plan and building trust by showing empathy and providing reassurance, to help foster positive mindsets in patients leading up to surgery.

Patients’ expectations for surgery and conservative treatment for FAIS may differ significantly due to perceptions of treatment efficacy, recovery speed, and long-term outcomes. Surgical patients often anticipate immediate and substantial improvements in pain, function, and psychosocial well-being, viewing arthroscopy as a definitive solution. In contrast, patients undergoing conservative treatment may expect gradual progress through physical therapy and lifestyle modifications, which may be perceived as less invasive but one that produces slower results [43, 51]. For example, Bell et al. [43] found that patients were willing to use over the counter medication to reduce pain and facilitate continued participation in physical activity, despite concerns about exacerbating their condition. Others have shown that many patients with FAIS hold negative expectations about conservative treatment options, such as physical therapy, doubting their effectiveness in relieving symptoms or improving physical function [51]. Despite these differences, patients pursuing either surgical or conservative treatment for FAIS often share similar goals—including improving ADL quality, pain relief, and improved physical function [43, 51]. Notably, higher preoperative expectations have been shown to predict better psychosocial outcomes following hip arthroscopy, such as reduced anxiety or depressive symptoms, [41, 52] underscoring the importance of understanding and managing patient expectations across both surgical and conservative treatment pathways.

Perceptions of facilitators for optimal recovery

We identified several perceived facilitators to optimal recovery from surgery—including physical and psychological preparation for surgery and a strong support system to assist with daily tasks and provide emotional support during recovery. Gathering information from one’s healthcare provider as well as individuals who have experienced hip arthroscopy for FAIS may help patients set realistic expectations for surgery and recovery, reducing the potential for disappointment or frustration if the outcome differs from what they anticipate. Comprehensive preparation of the patient for surgery reduces the risk of complications and promotes faster recovery [53–55]. It is essential that healthcare professionals help patients prepare both physically and mentally for surgery by providing patient education that informs patients about the procedure, recovery, and expected outcomes and encouraging active participation in preoperative preparations.

Our findings also indicate that patients perceive social support as valuable in their recovery from surgery, highlighting that it can positively impact both physical and psychological recovery from surgery. This finding is supported by extensive literature documenting the benefits of social support in recovery from surgery [56–59]. Social support can help reduce anxiety and stress, assist with daily activities and medical management after surgery, and promote adherence to recovery plans and long-term recovery goals [56]. To enhance recovery, healthcare providers should proactively assess patients’ existing support systems, including family, friends, and caregivers preoperatively. If a patient lacks adequate support, clinicians should consider referring them to peer support programs, patient mentorship initiatives, or online communities where they can connect with others undergoing similar experiences. These resources may provide emotional encouragement, practical advice, and a sense of belonging, all of which can contribute to improved recovery.

Clinical implications

This study has a number of important implications for healthcare providers. Our findings highlight the multifaceted burden of FAIS on patients and emphasize the need for more comprehensive, patient-centered approaches to care. Given the complex interplay between physical impairments, pain perception, and psychological well-being [60] an integrated treatment approach may be the most effective way to optimize outcomes in FAIS patients. A holistic, interdisciplinary approach to FAIS management—one that integrates physical, psychological, and social factors—has the potential to improve patient experiences and outcomes. Such an approach is in line with efforts to extend the interactive biopsychosocial model of illness and pain to orthopaedics.

Addressing both physical symptoms and psychological well-being should be a key consideration in surgical preparation and recovery planning. Mental health screening, such as the GAD-7 for anxiety and PHQ-9 for depression, should be a routine component of FAIS management, particularly given the high prevalence of symptoms of anxiety and depression observed in FAIS patients and the documented impact of poor mental health on patient outcomes [31]. In cases where mental health concerns are detected, early referral to mental health professionals for supportive counseling, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), or stress management strategies may improve both surgical outcomes and long-term recovery trajectories.

Further, given the association between optimism and improved recovery, healthcare providers should consider integrating psychological preparation techniques, such as preoperative counseling, mindfulness techniques, guided imagery, or resilience training into preoperative care to foster resilience and a positive mindset about surgery. Optimizing preoperative education and expectation management is crucial for improving patient experiences and reducing post-surgical distress. Structured preoperative education programs—including in-person counseling, printed materials, and online resources—should be provided to ensure that patients fully understand the anticipated course of recovery. Shared decision-making models that empower patients to take an active role in their treatment choices may enhance confidence and reduce feelings of uncertainty before surgery.

Limitations

There are several limitations of the current study that warrant attention. First, our sample was small and comprised mostly of White female patients, which limits the generalizability of our findings to larger populations of hip arthroscopy patients and different contexts. Second, although the qualitative nature of the study captured in-depth insights of patients’ experiences with FAIS, results may be subjected to observer bias and the researcher’s own beliefs may have unintentionally shaped the results of this study. Third, participants were asked to recall past events and share their personal experiences. This qualitative approach may introduce recall bias, resulting in data that may not accurately reflect participants’ actual experiences. Another limitation of this study is that the interviews were conducted by telephone rather than in person, which may have reduced the richness of the data by hindering rapport-building, shortening interview duration, and introducing environmental or technical distractions. Further, participants may not have fully disclosed sensitive information due to fear of judgment, leading to incomplete data. Despite these limitations, this study provides an important contribution to the literature by providing a nuanced understanding of patients’ experiences living with FAIS, which, in turn can help improve patient-centered care, patient-reported outcomes, and overall quality of life and well-being.

Conclusion

Our findings deepen our understanding of the lived experiences of patients with FAIS and their expectations for hip arthroscopy. FAIS significantly impacts patients’ physical, emotional, and social well-being, with chronic pain and delayed diagnosis generally contributing to heightened psychological distress. Perspectives on surgery vary across patients, with some patients possessing an optimistic attitude towards surgery and others with a pessimistic attitude. It is important for healthcare professionals to integrate patient-centered and empowerment-oriented strategies, such as mental health screenings, preoperative education, and structured support systems, into FAIS treatment plans. Integrating these types of strategies into treatment plans have great potential to promote positive mindsets, mentally and emotionally prepare patients for surgery, and create supportive environments, which, in turn may optimize surgical outcomes and overall experiences in individuals with FAIS. Future studies should examine the role of preoperative psychological interventions, enhanced patient education strategies, and structured social support programs in optimizing recovery after hip arthroscopy for FAIS.
