Veillonella parvula promotes root caries development through interactions with Streptococcus mutans and Candida albicans
Authors: Guo Li, Yuqiu Liu, Mengdie Zhang, Jia Ning, Linrui Wu, Lixiang Jian, Hongkun Wu, Xingqun Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoot caries is a subtype of dental caries that predominantly impacts older adults. The occurrence and progression of root caries are associated with the homeostasis of dental plaque biofilm, and microbial synergistic and antagonistic interactions in the biofilm play a significant role in maintaining the oral microecological balance. The objective of the current study was to investigate the role of Veillonella parvula in the microbial interactions and the pathogenesis of root caries. The analysis of clinical samples from patients with/without root caries revealed that Veillonella and V. parvula were abundant in the saliva of patients with root caries. More importantly, a significantly increased colonization of V. parvula was observed in root carious lesions. Further in vitro biofilm and animal study showed that V. parvula colonization increased the abundance and virulence of Streptococcus mutans and Candida albicans, leading to the formation of a polymicrobial biofilm with enhanced anti‐stress capacity and cariogenicity, consequently exacerbating the severity of carious lesions. Our results indicate the critical role of V. parvula infection in the occurrence of root caries, providing a new insight for the etiological investigation and prevention of root caries.

INTRODUCTION

Root caries is one of the most common dental diseases usually found on the exposed root surfaces or the margin of the cementoenamel junction. The prevalence of root caries in older adults ranges from 25% to 100%, becoming the major cause of the peak in dental caries in adults around the age of 70 (Gao et al., 2018; Hayes et al., 2017). Root caries occurs and progresses very easily, resulting in the spreading of caries laterally and in the surrounding of the entire tooth root, increasing the risk of tooth loss and influencing the quality of life (Abou Neel et al., 2016; AlQranei et al., 2021). The failure rate in the root surface restoration is very high due to the difficulties associated with the position of the cavity, access to the restoration site, and moisture insulation (Hayes et al., 2016; Meyer‐Lueckel et al., 2019). Therefore, it is of the utmost importance to prevent root caries.

Oral microbial dysbiosis is a major etiological factor in root caries (Takahashi & Nyvad, 2016). The microenvironment changes when the oral homeostasis is disturbed, and these changes allow the proliferation of opportunistic and potentially pathogenic microorganisms, causing an imbalance between remineralization and demineralization, ultimately leading to the initiation/progression of root caries (Head et al., 2021). Multiple genera of cariogenic microbes, including Streptococci, Actinomyces, Lactobacilli, Bifidobacteria, and Candida are associated with root caries (Preza et al., 2008; Takahashi & Nyvad, 2016). Streptococcus mutans is considered the principal etiological agent of root caries due to its ability to generate acids (acidogenicity) and to survive in an acid environment (aciduricity) (Preza, Olsen, Willumsen, Boches, et al., 2009; Sansone et al., 1993). The ecological functional patterns of S. mutans related to sugar utilization, heterofermentative metabolism, cell‐wall biosynthesis, and acid tolerance stress are most intense on carious root surfaces (Santos et al., 2022). A recent study found significantly increased colonization of Candida albicans in root carious lesions of the elder population, and C. albicans promoted tooth decay by inducing oral microbial dysbiosis (Du et al., 2021). S. mutans secretes glucosyltransferase B (GtfB), which binds to the mannans located on the surface of C. albicans, promoting the formation of extracellular matrix and co‐existence of these two microbes, consequently increasing the cariogenicity of the mixed biofilm (Hwang et al., 2017). The arginine biosynthesis pathway of C. albicans regulates interactions with Actinomyces viscosus to promote root caries (Xiong et al., 2022). These studies suggest that microbial interactions play critical roles in the aetiology of root caries. Thus, the discovery of their underlying mechanisms in ecological modulation might provide new insights into the prevention and treatment of root caries.

Veillonella is a common gram‐negative anaerobic bacterium, which acts as a bridge genus in plaques, being involved in the attachment of microorganisms to the tooth surface (Periasamy & Kolenbrander, 2010). Previous studies have shown that Veillonella is a beneficial genus for dental health, as it converts lactic acid (pK a ≈ 3.8) into weaker acids such as acetate and propionate (pK a ≈ 4.8), resulting in a less acidic milieu (Bradshaw & Marsh, 1998). However, with the development of 16S rRNA sequencing, the amount of Veillonella is revealed significantly high in the dental plaque samples of root caries or early childhood caries (Preza et al., 2008; Preza, Olsen, Willumsen, Grinde, et al., 2009). Veillonella species in the salivary samples are associated with poor oral health (Yamashita & Takeshita, 2017). Studies revealed a significant positive correlation between high levels of S. mutans and Veillonella parvula/dispar on the same teeth of root caries (Abram et al., 2022). In vitro studies have shown that the presence of V. parvula in the mixed biofilm facilitates the proliferation of S. mutans and contributes to an increase in biofilm biomass (Abram et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020). Moreover, the degradation of the organic matrix is an important stage during the development of root caries (Takahashi & Nyvad, 2016). Damé‐Teixeira et al found that S. mutans and V. parvula could express substantial collagenases to promote dentine degradation (Dame‐Teixeira et al., 2018). All these studies suggest that V. parvula might participate in the pathogenesis of dental caries; thus, its role in the microbial interaction and in the development of root caries needs further investigations.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the effect of V. parvula on the pathogenic virulence of the representative root caries‐related microbes S. mutans and C. albicans, and its role in root caries development was also assessed. Clinical samples of saliva and dental plaque were collected to analyse the microbial composition. In vitro single, dual or three species‐biofilm models were used to investigate the microbial interaction between V. parvula and S. mutans/C. albicans. Furthermore, an animal model was built to confirm the role of V. parvula in the pathogenesis of root caries. The results revealed an increased colonization of V. parvula in root carious lesions, which promoted the increase in the abundance and virulence of S. mutans and C. albicans, enhancing the cariogenicity of the polymicrobial biofilm.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Clinical sample collection

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (WCHSIRB‐D‐2020‐274). All participants were recruited at the West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University and clinical samples were collected. Thirty volunteers aged 60–85 years (15 patients with root caries and 15 healthy individuals without caries) who were not subjected to any medication, antibiotic treatment, hormone treatment or oral treatment within 3 months prior to the study were enrolled (Du et al., 2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants enrolled in this study. Oral examinations were performed by two endodontists who had been trained and calibrated before and during the study in the field. The kappa values of the two examiners were measured to exceed 0.8. Based on a visual and tactile assessment using the ICDAS II caries lesion activity criteria, subjects were included in the root caries group if they had at least five active root carious lesions indicated by codes 1 or 2 on root surfaces, subjects were included in the caries free group if they had neither coronal caries nor root caries (Dikmen, 2015). Community periodontal index was used to obtain the information of periodontal including gingival bleeding, the presence of calculus and periodontal pocket depth (Beltran‐Aguilar et al., 2012).

The subjects were asked to refrain from eating, drinking, brushing, or flossing 2 h prior to sample collection. Saliva was collected by instructing each participant to spit saliva directly into a sterile collection tube. The saliva samples were centrifuged at 2600 × g for 10 min to spin down large debris and eukaryotic cells, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C for further use. The flow rate, pH and buffering capacity of saliva were determined as previously described (Moritsuka et al., 2006). Pooled dental plaque samples were collected from the supragingival root carious lesions, from sound root surfaces of teeth without carious lesions of the same patients with root caries, as well as from the sound root surfaces of all teeth of the individual without root caries. The clinical samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1 software with an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 80%. Preliminary experiments indicated that the rate of V. parvula in the dental plaque of individuals without root caries was 20%, while in patients with root caries, it was 80%. Following the above results and using the statistical software, the minimum number of individuals with root caries or caries‐free controls was 12; thus, 15 individuals per group were included to perform our study.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from saliva and dental plaque samples using a QIAamp DNA mini kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) with an additional lysozyme treatment (3 mg/mL, 1.5 h). The DNA quality was evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Half of the saliva DNA was used for 16S rRNA sequencing. The 16S rRNA amplicon (V3–V4 regions) sequencing was performed using Illumina MiSeq technology (Personalbio, Shanghai, China) with the following primers: F (5′‐ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA‐3′) and R (5′‐TCGGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT‐3′). A special tag for each DNA sample was used to allow many samples in one run. The results were obtained on a 3% agarose gel. The gel was purified, the products were quantified using a Pico‐Green kit, pooled in an equimolar ratio, and sequenced by Agilent BioAnalyzer 2100 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Sequences were trimmed by Trimmomatic 2 and merged into longer reads by FLASH 3. Too short sequences or some including indefinite parts were considered as unqualified sequences and were removed. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered using Usearch (version 7.1, http://drive5.com/uparse/) at a 97% similarity level. Based on the above results, the final OTUs were revealed. The raw data were uploaded into the public database Sequence Read Archive (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Traces/sra) with accession no. PRJNA932549.

The simplified OTU table standard was used in all samples to the minimum read number. The results were analysed using the online Personalbio Cloud Platform (www.genescloud.cn). The differences in the relative abundance of bacterial taxa between groups were compared by Kruskal–Wallis test at the genus level. The Chao, Shannon, and Simpson indexes using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test were used to assess the alpha diversity. Beta diversity was calculated in terms of principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) or non‐metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and was compared using the weighted‐unifrac distance and Adonis with 999 permutations. R software (version 3.3.1; https://www.r‐project.org/) “vegan” (version 2.4‐3) package was used to assess the similarity and construct heatmaps. The significant different taxa between the two groups were evaluated by LefSe using the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) with a threshold LDA score of 4.08.

Detection rate and abundance of microorganisms

The genomic DNA from samples of saliva and dental plaque was used to investigate the detection rates and abundance of S. mutans, C. albicans and V. parvula by PCR and qPCR, respectively. The correlation between V. parvula and root caries, as well as with the related microorganisms S. mutans and C. albicans, were further analysed. The specific primers are listed in Table S1.

In vitro biofilm model

Streptococcus mutans UA159, V. parvula ATCC 10790T, and C. albicans SC5314 were commercially obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The biofilms of single species (S. mutans and C. albicans), two species (V. parvula + S. mutans, V. parvula + C. albicans, and S. mutans + C. albicans), and three species (V. parvula + S. mutans + C. albicans) were grown in SHI medium and incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37°C for 24 h. The initial concentrations of the species were adjusted in a 2 mL SHI medium based on the ratio detected in clinical samples: S. mutans (2 × 106 CFU/mL), C. albicans (2 × 104 CFU/mL), and V. parvula (1 × 107 CFU/mL) (Du et al., 2021; Kara et al., 2006).

Biofilm biomass and structure analysis

Crystal violet staining was used to quantify the biomass of the biofilms (Cheng et al., 2017). Briefly, the biofilms (n = 10/group) were washed twice with PBS, fixed in methanol for 15 min and stained with 1% crystal violet for 5 min. They were washed again three times with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and biofilm images were captured using a stereo‐microscope (Nikon MODEL C‐DSS230, Japan). Then, they were quantitatively analysed by adding 1 mL of 95% ethanol for 15 min to de‐stain the wells, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer (BioMate 3S Spectrophotometer, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Confocal laser scanning microscopy was used to observe the structure of biofilms. The microbe cells and the extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) were labelled with SYTO 9 (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA) and Alexa Fluor 647‐labelled dextran conjugate (Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) respectively (Xiao et al., 2012). Biofilm images were captured and analysed using a Leica TCS SP2 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with Fluoview SV1000 imaging software (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The population of microbes and EPS were analysed based on the integrate optical density (IOD) using Image pro plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics). The architecture of biofilms was also examined by scanning electron microscopy. Biofilms were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (v/v) for 12 h at 4°C, then serially dehydrated in ethanol solutions, and sputter coated with gold. Specimens were examined at magnifications of 5000×, and 20,000× under scanning electron microscopy (Quanta 200, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA). The independent biofilm assays were repeated at least three times.

Microbial composition and polysaccharide analysis of the biofilms

The microbial composition of the biofilms was quantified by species‐specific qPCR using the method described by Cheng et al. (2017). The DNA of the biofilms was extracted, and qPCR amplification was performed using the Bio‐Rad CFX96 system (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The reaction mixture (25 μL) contained SYBR qPCR Mix (Takara, Dalian, China), forward and reverse primers (10 μM), and template DNA. The thermal cycling conditions were the following: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. The threshold cycle values (CT) were determined, and the lg CFU/mL was calculated based on the standard curve (lg CFU/mL vs. CT) generated using standard stains. The specific primers are listed in Table S1.

The amount of water‐insoluble EPS in the biofilms was measured using the anthrone‐sulfuric method (Chen et al., 2016). Briefly, the biofilms in each well were resuspended in PBS. The suspension was centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The pellet was washed three times and resuspended in PBS to ensure that all the water‐soluble EPS was removed. The water‐insoluble polysaccharides were extracted using 1.0 M NaOH under agitation for 2 h at 37°C, added into three volumes of anthrone‐sulfuric acid reagent and heated in a water bath at 95°C for 5 min. Each sample was then cooled to room temperature, and the absorbance was read at 625 nm in a 96‐microplate reader. All the assays were performed in triplicate.

Determination of stress resistance and virulence gene expression of the biofilms

The pH values of the spent media of the biofilms were measured using an Orion Dual Star pH/ISE electrode (Thermo Scientific, Schwerte, Germany). The stress resistance of biofilms was determined as previously described (Cheng et al., 2017). Briefly, acid tolerance was assessed by the incubation of the biofilms in glycine buffer at pH 2.8 (challenge pH) and the number of survivors was measured following anaerobic incubation for 30 min. The oxygen tolerance was assessed by placing the biofilms in glycine buffer at pH 7.0 containing 0.2% H2O2, and then the number of alive microbes after incubation for 30 min under anaerobic conditions was calculated. Chlorhexidine at a concentration of 0.2% was added to biofilms, incubated for 5 min and removed, and the microbial survival rate was calculated to test the biofilm drug resistance.

The expression of genes (gtfB, gtfC, gtfD, and ldh genes of S. mutans, catA gene of V. parvula) associated with biofilm virulence was measured by qRT‐PCR. Total RNA isolation from biofilm samples, purification, and reverse transcription to cDNA was performed as previously described (Chen et al., 2016). qRT‐PCR analysis was performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq (Takara), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The CT was determined, the data were analysed using the BIO‐RAD CFX MANAGER software (version 2.0) and the relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Each sample was examined in triplicate. The primers used are listed in Table S1.

In vivo root caries rat model

A root caries rat model was used to further investigate the role of V. parvula and other related microbes in the pathogenesis of root caries. The animal protocol was reviewed and approved by the animal research committee of West China School of Stomatology, Sichuan University (WCHSIRB‐D‐2022‐044). All animal studies were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The rat model was established as previously described with some modifications (Figure S3) (Firestone et al., 1988). Thirty‐five specific‐pathogen‐free Sprague–Dawley rats (aged 17 days and weighing 60 ± 5 g) were purchased from the West China Laboratory Animal Center, Sichuan University. The rats were housed in a laboratory facility under a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, a controlled temperature of 25 ± 1°C, relative humidity of 50%–70% and were fed with a cariogenic diet and 5% sucrose water. Rats (n = 5 per group) were randomly divided into seven groups and infected with a single species (S. mutans or C. albicans), two species (V. parvula + S. mutans, V. parvula + C. albicans, S. mutans + C. albicans), or three species (V. parvula + S. mutans + C. albicans). Rats without infection were used as controls. Random groups were obtained by tagging numbers on rats and using random number table. All groups were placed in the same environment and fed or given stimulation at the same time. The rat (aged 24 days) was infected for three consecutive days by the respective microbes with the same ratio as they were in the biofilm model stated above. Gingivectomy was performed in all rats on day 10 after the initial infection. The rat (aged 38 days) received the second inoculation of the respective microbes for three consecutive days. Six weeks after the initial infection, animals were sacrificed under phenobarbital anaesthesia. The saliva and dental plaque of the rat were collected for the quantification of microbes by qPCR, and the jaws were collected for root caries scoring using the Doff's system (Doff et al., 1977). The determination of the root caries score as well as the detection of the quantification of microbes was performed by one experimenter who was blinded to the group assignments. The sample size was estimated considering the variation and mean of the samples calculated from the preliminary experiment. The type I error was equal to 5%, and type II error was equal to 10%. Thus, the minimum required sample size in each group was 5 based on these assumptions and using the G*Power 3.1 software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of data other than 16S rRNA sequencing was performed using SPSS software (version 16.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Categorical variables were presented as natural frequencies, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. The clinical characteristics of the participants were computed using the Kruskal–Wallis (with Dunn–Bonferroni post‐hoc) or chi‐square tests. The microbial detection rates and the quantitative analysis of the clinical samples were performed by the chi‐squared test and Rank test. Linear correlation and regression analyses were also used to assess the relationship between the level of V. parvula and S. mutans or C. albicans in clinical samples. Other data obtained from two independent groups were compared using Student's t‐test, and multiple group comparison was performed by one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test or Dunnett t‐test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Veillonella parvula was enriched in saliva and root carious lesions of subjects with root caries

The demographic data, such as age, gender, number of teeth, systemic diseases, and community periodontal index, were not significantly different between the caries‐free group and root caries group (n = 15/group) (Table S2). No between‐group difference was found in the flow rate and pH of the saliva. However, the salivary buffering capacity of the root caries group was significantly lower compared with that of the control group (Table S2).

The 16S rRNA sequencing data of the saliva are listed in Figure S1. No statistically significant difference was observed in the alpha diversity analysis (Figure S1C). However, the beta diversity analysis revealed a significant difference between the groups, since it was possible to clearly distinguish the samples and clustered them into two groups (Figure S1D), indicating that the overall community structure of the two groups was different. Further analysis revealed genus differences between the caries free group and root caries group (Figure 1A,B, Figure S1E), specifically, the abundance of Veillonella, Prevotella, and Leptotrichia was statistically higher, while that of Neisseria and Porphyromonas was lower in the saliva of the root caries group compared with that in the caries free group (Figure 1C).

The detection rate of V. parvula in the saliva of the root caries group was significantly higher as compared with that in the caries‐free group (86.7% and 46.7% respectively, Figure 1D). The co‐detection rate of V. parvula and representative root caries‐related microbes S. mutans and C. albicans in the saliva of the root caries group was much higher than that in the caries‐free group (60% and 13.3% respectively, Figure 1E). The correlation test revealed that the amount of V. parvula in saliva from both groups was positively correlated with that of S. mutans and C. albicans (Figure 1F,G, Figure S2A–C). V. parvula colonized the root carious lesions more frequently than the sound root surfaces, both in the same patients with root caries and in caries‐free individuals (Figure 1H). In addition, the abundance of V. parvula was significantly higher on the root carious lesions than on the sound root surfaces in patients with root caries and caries‐free controls (Figure 1I). All these results suggested that V. parvula was enriched in the saliva and root carious lesions of root caries subjects.

Veillonella parvula increased the pathogenic virulence of the biofilm when co‐cultured with S. mutans and C. albicans

The in vitro biofilms became more compact in the presence of V. parvula compared with those without this bacterium (Figure 2A). The addition of V. parvula increased the biofilm biomass (Figure 2B). Moreover, V. parvula significantly promoted the growth of S. mutans and C. albicans in the dual‐ and three‐species biofilms (Figure 2C,D), and increased the thickness and biomass of the biofilms (Figure 2E–G). The addition of V. parvula increased the production of EPS in the biofilms in which S. mutans was present (Figure 2E,G). Water‐insoluble EPS was significantly increased when V. parvula and S. mutans were co‐cultured (Figure 2H).

Veillonella parvula increased the abundance of pathogens in saliva and dental plaque and promoted the development of root caries in rats

Rats in the negative control group, C. albicans infection group and V. parvula + C. albicans co‐infection group showed almost no root caries (Figure 4A,B). The addition of V. parvula to the S. mutans and S. mutans + C. albicans groups resulted in an exacerbated development of root caries as compared to the groups without V. parvula (Figure 4A,B). Furthermore, the colonization of V. parvula significantly increased the abundance of S. mutans and C. albicans in both saliva and dental plaque of the rats (Figure 4C–F). These results indicated that the colonization of V. parvula exerted a significant positive impact on the abundance of the pathogens related to root caries, leading to a polymicrobial biofilm with enhanced cariogenic capacity.

DISCUSSION

The disturbance of the oral microbial balance plays a critical role in the promotion of many oral diseases, including root caries (Kreth & Merritt, 2022). Root caries is a crucial oral health issue that mainly affects the older adults. Previous studies have demonstrated that microbes closely associated with root caries include S. mutans, C. albicans, and A. viscosus (AlQranei et al., 2021; Du et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2022). V. parvula is another species that cannot be ignored in the development of root caries, but its role in root caries remains to be further elucidated. Therefore, this study explored the role of V. parvula in the pathogenesis of root caries.

This study revealed that Veillonella genus, V. parvula, S. mutans, and C. albicans species were enriched in the saliva of patients with root caries (Figure 1A–E, Figure S2A–C). Previous studies have demonstrated that S. mutans and C. albicans are enriched in root carious lesions (Du et al., 2021; Preza et al., 2008). This study found that V. parvula was also abundant in the root carious lesions of root caries subjects (Figure 1H,I). These results suggested that V. parvula is closely associated with root caries.

Previous studies have suggested that V. parvula, as a bridging bacterium, is not able to form biofilm in vitro and has a very low cariogenicity (Abram et al., 2022; Bechon et al., 2020). Therefore, our attention was not focused on a single species‐biofilm structure and virulence of V. parvula, but rather on the effect of V. parvula on the pathogenic virulence of representative root caries‐related microbes S. mutans and C. albicans. Indeed, the presence of V. parvula increased the abundance of S. mutans and C. albicans, as well as the biomass and density of the biofilms (Figure 2). V. parvula promoted the production of EPS in the biofilm since it promoted the growth of S. mutans and the expression of genes related to EPS synthesis (gtfB and gtfC) in dual‐ and three‐species biofilms (Figure 3E). EPS production facilitates the colonization of microbes and promotes the formation of the biofilm (Karygianni et al., 2020; Moschioni et al., 2010).

The anti‐stress ability of the biofilms was determined since the oral cavity is a specific microbial environment with various factors influencing the balance of dental plaque. When co‐cultured with S. mutans, V. parvula in the biofilms significantly enhanced the survival rate of microbes under conditions of acid, oxidative, and chlorhexidine stress (Figure 3B–D). The increased synthesis of EPS in the biofilms likely accounts for this enhanced anti‐stress capacity. The denser and thicker biofilms not only bolstered the microbes' resilience to environmental stress but also decreased their susceptibility to antimicrobial agents, as it was also previously reported (Karygianni et al., 2020). Furthermore, the expression of catA in V. parvula, a catalase encoded gene involving bacterial ecological competence, was significantly upregulated when V. parvula was co‐cultured with S. mutans (Figure 3F). Therefore, the biofilms involving these two bacteria exhibited heightened resistance to oxidative stress. Consistently, Luppens et al. found that V. parvula produces catalase to enhance the growth of S. mutans under oxidative stress and increases its competitive ability against commensal bacteria such as Streptococcus sanguinis that produces hydrogen peroxide (Luppens et al., 2008).

The pH value within the plaque is an important factor facilitating the development of dental caries (Bradshaw & Marsh, 1998). The pH level of supernatants from biofilms was measured and no difference was found between groups (Figure 3A). Although V. parvula can metabolize the lactic acid produced by S. mutans (Bradshaw & Marsh, 1998), the expression of the ldh gene of S. mutans was upregulated in the presence of V. parvula; thus, the total pH was not affected. Similar results were reported in clinical studies by Lima as well as Tanner et al., who found no significant regulatory effect on the pH levels of the dental plaque associated with increased V. parvula amount (Lima et al., 2011). Lin et al. (2015) found that the metabolism of lactic acid by V. parvula increased the biomass of S. mutans in the biofilm. These findings suggest that V. parvula, while possessing the beneficial trait of metabolizing lactate and converting it into less cariogenic acids, may also exert adverse effects on dental plaque. Specifically, V. parvula has been shown to enhance the survival of S. mutans and stimulate the production of lactic acid by this bacterium. Despite no observed change in the total pH of the biofilm supernatant in the presence of V. parvula, the increased lactic acid production by S. mutans suggests that the acid may not readily diffuse due to the denser structure of the biofilm. To further elucidate the impact of V. parvula on the pH dynamics within biofilms, particularly at the interface between the tooth surface and dental plaque, additional studies are warranted.

In the root caries rat model, the C. albicans infection group and V. parvula + C. albicans co‐infection group exhibited a remarkably low root caries index (Figure 4A,B). This result was consistent with the finding of Megan et al. who demonstrated that while C. albicans alone exerts minimal cariogenicity, it significantly facilitates the progression of dental caries through its synergistic interactions with other oral bacteria (Falsetta et al., 2014). The inclusion of V. parvula in the experimental groups, both with S. mutans alone and with the combination of S. mutans and C. albicans, led to a significantly exacerbated progression of root caries compared to groups lacking V. parvula. This effect was predictable, given that the presence of V. parvula in dental biofilms has been shown to augment both the quantity and virulence of S. mutans and C. albicans within root carious lesions. Furthermore, V. parvula's colonization has been associated with an enhanced anti‐stress capacity and increased survival competitiveness of the biofilm, which may contribute to the observed exacerbation of root caries.

It should be noted that the experimental models involving only a few microorganisms are insufficient to mimic the complexity of oral biofilms. In vitro saliva‐ or carious lesion‐derived biofilm can be used to better mimic the ecological condition of dental plaque. Furthermore, new techniques and models should be developed to overcome the drawback. For instance, constructing a synthetic microbial community that comprises specific strains and retains the essential characteristics of dental plaque may be a potential novel direction to explore the ecological role of V. parvula in root caries (Grasso et al., 2024; McCarty & Ledesma‐Amaro, 2019). Additionally, it might be interesting to explore how sub‐genera of Veillonella are differentially related to the incidence of root caries due to the site‐specificity and metabolism of different Veillonella species in the oral cavity (Dallmeier, 2022; Giacomini et al., 2023).

In conclusion, this study found that the colonization of V. parvula increased the abundance and virulence of S. mutans and C. albicans, leading to the formation of a polymicrobial biofilm with enhanced cariogenicity.
