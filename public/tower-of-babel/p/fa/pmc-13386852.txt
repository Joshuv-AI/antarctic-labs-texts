STUB1-mediated ubiquitination regulates Fli-1 stability and CD4⁺T cell activation during inflammation
Authors: Pengfei Li, Liu Liu, Yan Wu, Meng Liu, Maria Trojanowska, Hongkuan Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFli-1, a member of the ETS transcription factor family, has been implicated in multiple inflammatory and immune-related disorders, including sepsis, lupus, Alzheimer’s disease, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Recent studies further suggest a critical role for Fli-1 in CD4⁺ T cells in graft-versus-host disease, systemic sclerosis, and PTSD; however, its precise function and regulation during inflammatory CD4⁺ T cell responses remain incompletely understood.MethodsWild-type and CD4⁺ T cell–specific Fli-1 knockout mice were administered LPS via intraperitoneal injection. Survival rates and splenic T cell activation were subsequently assessed. In vitro, cultured Jurkat and HEK cells were utilized to investigate the STUB1/Fli-1–related signaling pathway.ResultsHere, we demonstrate that Fli-1 expression is markedly upregulated in splenic CD4⁺ T cells in a murine model of endotoxemia. CD4⁺ T cell-specific deletion of Fli-1 significantly improves survival and attenuates splenic CD4⁺ T cell activation. Mechanistically, we identify STUB1 as a novel E3 ubiquitin ligase for Fli-1 that promotes K27-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation under basal conditions. Inflammatory stimulation disrupts the STUB1/HSP70 interaction, reducing STUB1-mediated ubiquitination and enhancing Fli-1 protein stability. Consistent with these findings, Fli-1 knockout reduces TNFα, IFNγ, and IL-10 expression, whereas STUB1 knockout enhances their expression in Jurkat cells. Gain- and loss-of-function studies further reveal that Fli-1 modulates NFκB signaling, a key pathway in T cell activation.ConclusionsCollectively, our results identify the STUB1/Fli-1 axis as a previously unrecognized regulator of CD4⁺ T cell function and a potential therapeutic target for inflammatory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-026-01494-5.Keywords: Fli-1, STUB1, CD4+ T cells, NFκB, Endotoxemia

Introduction

Friend leukemia virus integration 1 (Fli-1), a member of the ETS transcription factor family, has been implicated in a broad range of pathological conditions, including cancer, fibrosis, pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), systemic sclerosis (SSc), lupus nephritis (LN), sepsis, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Li et al. 2022, 2024; Wang and Zhang 2025; Zhang et al. 2024). Fli-1 is expressed in multiple immune and vascular cell types, including pericytes, endothelial cells, macrophages, B cells, and T cells.(Wang and Zhang 2025; Zhang et al. 2008; Schutt et al. 2022) Functionally, Fli-1 regulates immune responses by controlling the expression of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as TNFα and IFNγ (Wang and Zhang 2025; He et al. 2021), and it plays a pivotal role in the proliferation and activation of B cells and monocytes/macrophages (Wang and Zhang 2025).

Recent studies have highlighted an emerging role for Fli-1 in T cell biology. In lupus-prone Fli-1+/− MRL/lpr mice, splenic T cells produce lower IL-6 and IL-4, accompanied by reduced T cell activation (Sato et al. 2014; Richard et al. 2013). Fli-1 insufficiency also impairs T cell infiltration into inflamed renal tissue and reduces the frequency of CD4⁺ IL-17⁺ T cells in vivo (Sundararaj et al. 2015; Sato et al. 2020). Additionally, Fli-1 promotes inflammatory phenotypes of CD4⁺ T cells in graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) (Schutt et al. 2022). Consistent with its role in inflammatory disorders, we previously observed elevated Fli-1 expression in circulating CD4+ T cells from combat veterans diagnosed with PTSD (Li et al. 2024). Despite accumulating evidence linking Fli-1 to CD4+ T cell–mediated immune responses, the precise role of Fli-1 in CD4+ T cell activation under inflammatory conditions remains poorly defined.

Emerging evidence indicates that Fli-1 expression is regulated by TLR4–MyD88-dependent signaling pathway in pericytes during lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation (Li et al. 2025), whereas its transcriptional activity is modulated by transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)–induced post-transcriptional mechanisms involving PKCδ-dependent phosphorylation and acetylation (Asano and Trojanowska 2009). Notably, the E3 ubiquitin ligase tripartite motif-containing protein 8 (TRIM8) has been shown to ubiquitinate and promote degradation of the EWS/FLI fusion oncoprotein in Ewing sarcoma (Seong et al. 2021), suggesting ubiquitin-mediated regulation may be critical for Fli-1 protein stability. However, whether ubiquitination governs the stability of endogenous Fli-1 in immune cells remains unknown. Ubiquitination is a key post-translational mechanism regulating transcription factor turnover and T cell activation during inflammation. STUB1 (also known as CHIP) is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that integrates proteostasis and inflammatory signaling and has been implicated in T cell function and immune regulation. Yet, whether STUB1 governs Fli-1 stability and how this pathway shapes CD4⁺ T cell responses under inflammatory conditions remain unexplored.

Here, we investigate the role of Fli-1 in CD4+ T cells during endotoxemia and uncover a previously unrecognized STUB1-dependent ubiquitination mechanism that regulates Fli-1 protein stability and CD4⁺ T cell activation.

Results

CD4+ T cell-specific deletion of Fli-1 reduces mortality in endotoxemic mice

To investigate the role of Fli-1 in CD4+ T cells during endotoxin exposure, we generated CD4+ T cell-specific Fli-1 knockout mice (CD4 Fli-1−/−). Splenic CD4+ T cells were isolated from both WT and CD4 Fli-1−/− mice, and cell purity was confirmed by flow cytometry (93.8% purity; Figure S1). The gating strategies for CD4+ T cell identification are shown in Figure S2A-D. Real-time PCR and immunoblot analyses demonstrated a marked reduction in both Fli-1 mRNA and protein levels in CD4⁺ T cells from CD4 Fli-1−/− mice compared with WT controls (n = 5–8 per group, Fig. 1A; p < 0.05).

To determine the functional consequences of Fli-1 loss in CD4+ T cells, WT and CD4 Fli-1−/− mice were challenged intraperitoneally with LPS (25 mg/kg body weight), and survival was monitored over a 7-day period. Notably, CD4+ T cell-specific deletion of Fli-1 significantly improved survival, increasing survival rates by approximately 48.6% compared with LPS-treated WT littermates (n = 13–16 per group, Fig. 1B; p < 0.05). These results indicate that Fli-1 expression in CD4⁺ T cells contributes to the lethality during endotoxemia.

Loss of Fli-1 in CD4+ T cells attenuates splenic T cell activation during endotoxemia

To further delineate the immunological effects of Fli-1 deletion in CD4+ T cells, we analyzed splenic T cell subsets and activation status following LPS challenge. WT and CD4 Fli-1−/− mice were injected intraperitoneally with LPS (100 μg/kg body weight), and spleens were collected 24 h later for flow cytometric analysis. Under both basal and LPS-treated conditions, the frequencies or total numbers of splenic CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells were comparable between WT and CD4 Fli-1−/− mice (Fig. 2A–E), indicating that Fli-1 deletion does not alter overall T cell composition.

LPS administration significantly increased T cells activation in WT mice, as reflected by enhanced expression of the early activation marker CD69 expression on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2F–J; p < 0.05). In contrast, this LPS-induced upregulation of CD69 was significantly attenuated in CD4 Fli-1−/− mice (Fig. 2F-J; p < 0.05). These findings suggest that Fli-1 in CD4+ T cells plays a critical role in promoting CD4+ T cell activation during systemic inflammation and may also indirectly modulate CD8+ T cell activation. The gating strategies used to identify CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, CD69⁺CD4⁺ T cells, and CD69⁺CD8⁺ T cells are shown in Figure S2E–J.

Fli-1 protein is upregulated in CD4⁺ T cells during inflammation through post-translational regulation

To further determine how inflammation affects Fli-1 expression, we subsequently examined Fli-1 levels in CD4⁺ T cells following LPS stimulation in vivo. Splenic CD4⁺ T cells were isolated from WT mice treated with or without LPS (100 μg/kg body weight), and Fli-1 expression was analyzed at 24 h after LPS administration. Western blot results revealed a significant increase in Fli-1 protein levels in CD4⁺ T cells from LPS-treated mice compared with untreated controls (Fig. 3A; p < 0.05). Consistent with this finding, stimulation of Jurkat cells with TNFα (10 ng/ml) led to a time-dependent increase in Fli-1 protein levels, whereas DMSO-treated cells showed no significant changes. Similar results were obtained from three independent experiments (Fig. 3B). HEK-TLR4 cells are human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293)-derived cells stably expressing Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) along with its co-receptors MD2 and CD14, enabling functional responsiveness to LPS. Using this model, we found that LPS stimulation (100 ng/ml) induced a marked, time-dependent increase in Fli-1 protein levels (Figure S3A). Since ubiquitination is a major post-translational modification that regulate protein stability and degradation, we next investigated whether ubiquitin-mediated regulation contributes to Fli-1 expression and stabilization during inflammation. Using the UbiBrowser prediction platform (http://ubibrowser.ncpsb.org/ubibrowser/home/index) (Li et al. 2017), STUB1 (also known as CHIP) was identified as potential E3 ubiquitin ligase interacting with Fli-1 (Fig. 3C). To validate this interaction, we generated Jurkat cells stably expressing HA-tagged STUB1. HA pull-down assays demonstrated that HA-STUB1 efficiently co-precipitated endogenous Fli-1 compared with vector control (Fig. 3D). This interaction was further confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation assays in HEK293T cells expressing STUB1 and Fli-1 (Figure S3B). Together, these results indicate that inflammatory stimulation enhances Fli-1 protein stability through post-translational regulation, and identify STUB1 as a potential E3 ubiquitin ligase mediating this process.

STUB1 mediates K27-linked ubiquitination of Fli-1 under basal conditions, which is inhibited during inflammation

Given that STUB1 interacts with Fli-1, we next investigated whether STUB1 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase to mediate Fli-1 ubiquitination. Co-immunoprecipitation analysis revealed robust ubiquitination of Fli-1 in cells co-expressing STUB1, whereas minimal ubiquitination was detected in the absence of STUB1 (Fig. 4A). To determine the specific ubiquitin linkage responsible for Fli-1 modification, a panel of ubiquitin mutants with individual lysine residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48 and K63) mutated to arginine (R) was tested (Wang et al. 2017). Among these mutants, only the K27R mutation markedly reduced Fli-1 ubiquitination (Fig. 4B), indicating that STUB1 predominantly mediates K27-linked ubiquitination of Fli-1. To further confirm the requirement of STUB1’s enzymatic activity, we examined the effect of an E3 ligase–deficient mutant (H260Q) of STUB1. This mutant failed to promote Fli-1 ubiquitination (Fig. 4C), confirming that the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of STUB1 is essential for Fli-1 modification. Consistently, knockdown of STUB1 in HEK293T cells significantly decreased Fli-1 ubiquitination compared to control cells (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, in LPS (100 ng/ml)-stimulated HEK-TLR4 cells, STUB1-mediated Fli-1 ubiquitination was markedly reduced compared with unstimulated conditions (Fig. 4E), suggesting that inflammatory signaling inhibits STUB1-dependent Fli-1 ubiquitination, thereby stabilizing Fli-1 protein levels during inflammation. To explore the underlying mechanism, we first examined whether LPS stimulation (100 ng/ml) affects the physical interaction between STUB1 and Fli-1. Co-immunoprecipitation analysis showed that LPS treatment did not significantly alter their binding (Fig. 4F), indicating that the reduced ubiquitination is not due to disrupted Fli-1-STUB1 interaction.

Given that STUB1 requires HSP70 as a co-chaperone for substrate recognition and ubiquitination, we next examined whether HSP70 is involved in Fli-1 regulation. Notably, the STUB1 K30A mutant, which fails to interact with HSP70, was unable to promote Fli-1 ubiquitination (Fig. 4G). Moreover, co-immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that LPS stimulation (100 ng/ml) weakened the interaction between STUB1 and HSP70 (Fig. 4H). Together, these findings suggest that inflammatory conditions disrupt the STUB1-HSP70 complex, thereby blocking STUB1-mediated K27-linked ubiquitination of Fli-1 and leading to enhanced Fli-1 protein stability.

STUB1-mediated ubiquitination regulates Fli-1 protein stability

Given that STUB1 mediates the ubiquitination of Fli-1, we next investigated whether STUB1 affects the stability of Fli-1 protein. Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays were performed to assess Fli-1 protein turnover under various conditions. In HEK293T cells, co-expression of STUB1 markedly accelerated the degradation of Fli-1 compared with cells expressing Fli-1 alone (Fig. 5A), indicating that STUB1 promotes Fli-1 destabilization. Consistently, in Jurkat cells stably overexpressing STUB1, Fli-1 protein levels declined more rapidly upon CHX treatment compared with control cells (Fig. 5B), further confirming that STUB1 enhances Fli-1 degradation. Conversely, knockout of STUB1 in Jurkat cells resulted in significantly prolonged Fli-1 protein half-life relative to control cells (Fig. 5C), suggesting that endogenous STUB1 is required for Fli-1 turnover. Similar results were obtained in STUB1-deficient HEK293T cells, where Fli-1 exhibited greater stability than in wild-type controls (Figure S3C). In addition, STUB1 knockdown also increased Fli-1 protein levels in Jurkat cells with or without MG132 treatment (Figure S3D). Interestingly, when STUB1-overexpressing Jurkat cells were treated with TNFα (10 ng/ml), Fli-1 protein stability was markedly increased compared with untreated STUB1-overexpressing cells and even exceeded that of control cells (Fig. 5D). These results align with our earlier findings that inflammatory conditions inhibits STUB1-mediated ubiquitination of Fli-1, thereby preventing its degradation. Together, these findings demonstrate that STUB1-mediated ubiquitination plays a key role in regulating Fli-1 protein stability under basal conditions, while inflammatory signaling inhibits this process, resulting in Fli-1 accumulation.

Fli-1 modulates inflammatory cytokine expression in T Cells

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying Fli-1-mediated T cell activation, we examined the effects of Fli-1 on the expression of inflammatory mediators. Deletion of Fli-1 significantly reduced mRNA levels of key inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, IFNγ, and IL-10, in Jurkat T cells (Fig. 6A; p < 0.05). In contrast, knockout of STUB1 markedly increased the expression of these cytokines (Fig. 6B; p < 0.05), likely due to reduced Fli-1 degradation and consequent stabilization of Fli-1 protein levels (Figure S3E). Consistently, Fli-1 knockout led to a significant decrease in phosphorylation of NFκB, whereas Fli-1 overexpression increased NFκB phosphorylation (Fig. 6C-D). Collectively, these results indicate that STUB1-mediated regulation of Fli-1 stability modulates T cell activation, at least in part through the NFκB signaling pathway.

Discussion

In the present study, we report several key findings that advance the understanding of STUB1/Fli-1 regulation and function in CD4⁺ T cells under inflammatory conditions. First, we observed a significant upregulation of Fli-1 in splenic CD4⁺ T cells from mice with endotoxemia, as well as in cultured Jurkat T cells following TNFα stimulation. Second, we demonstrate that Fli-1 contributes to CD4⁺ T cell activation and exacerbates LPS-induced mortality in vivo. Third, STUB1 functions as a key post-translational regulator of Fli-1: under homeostatic conditions, it facilitates Fli-1 ubiquitination and degradation, whereas during inflammatory activation, it enhances Fli-1 stability due to the disruption of the STUB1-HSP70 complex (Fig. 7). Moreover, Fli-1 contributes to CD4⁺ T cell activation, at least in part, via modulation of NFκB signaling. Collectively, these findings highlight the STUB1/Fli-1 axis as a central regulator of CD4⁺ T cell–mediated immune responses and position this pathway as a promising therapeutic target for inflammatory and immune-related diseases.

Schematic model for STUB1/Fli-1 axis regulating CD4+ T cell activation. Under normal conditions (A), the E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1, in association with HSP70, promotes ubiquitination of Fli-1, targeting it for proteasomal degradation. Reduced Fli-1 levels limit downstream transcriptional activity and suppress T-cell activation. In contrast, upon inflammatory stimulation with TNFα or LPS (B), STUB1/HSP70–mediated ubiquitination of Fli-1 is impaired, leading to Fli-1 stabilization and accumulation. Stabilized Fli-1 translocates to the nucleus and enhances transcription of immune-related genes, including NFκB–dependent pathways and cytokines such as IFNγ and IL-10, thereby promoting CD4+ T cell activation

To our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence that elevated Fli-1 expression in splenic CD4⁺ T cells contributes to LPS-induced mortality in mice. Fli-1 is a pivotal regulator of inflammation across multiple cell types and plays a central role in the pathogenesis of inflammatory and immune-mediated diseases (Wang and Zhang 2025). We previously reported increased Fli-1 expression in pericytes during sepsis and Alzheimer’s disease, where it promoted inflammation in the lung and brain (Li et al. 2018, 2022, 2025). Fli-1 is also upregulated in macrophages following LPS stimulation, modulating TLR4-mediated innate immune responses (Gao et al. 2016). In humans, elevated Fli-1 has been observed in PBMCs from lupus patients(Georgiou et al. 1996; Nowling et al. 2008), and our prior work further identified increased Fli-1 expression in PBMCs—particularly in CD4⁺ T cells—from individuals with PTSD (Li et al. 2024). Notably, Fli-1 protein levels are higher in the spleens of lupus mice and contribute to disease progression (Zhang et al. 2004). Our current study extends these findings by revealing elevated Fli-1 levels in splenic CD4⁺ T cells in a mouse model of LPS-induced endotoxemia (Fig. 3). Importantly, reduced Fli-1 expression markedly prolongs survival in lupus mice, associated with decreased levels of inflammatory mediators in the kidney, serum, and splenic T cells (Wang and Zhang 2025; Zhang et al. 2004). Similarly, CD4⁺ T cell-specific knockout of Fli-1 significantly improved survival in endotoxemic mice (Fig. 1), likely due to attenuated systemic inflammation. Supporting this, in vitro knockdown of Fli-1 in TNFα-stimulated Jurkat cells significantly reduced the expression of proinflammatory mediators such as TNFα and INFγ (Fig. 6). Furthermore, suppression of Fli-1 expression substantially diminished NFκB signaling, a central pathway governing T cell activation and inflammatory processes (Gerondakis et al. 2014; Mao et al. 2025), whereas Fli-1 overexpression enhanced NFκB activity in cultured Jurkat cells (Fig. 6). Collectively, these findings provide the first evidence that elevated Fli-1 in CD4⁺ T cells contributes to inflammation and mortality in endotoxemia.

The molecular regulation of Fli-1 remains incompletely understood. In this study, we identify for the first time that STUB1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, serves as a key post-translational regulator of Fli-1 under inflammatory conditions through ubiquitination. While previous work demonstrated that TLR4-MyD88 signaling is critical for the upregulation of Fli-1 expression in brain pericytes during inflammation(Li et al. 2025), post-translational regulation of Fli-1 has been less explored. Several post-translational modifications have been implicated in modulating Fli-1 activity. Notably, mutation of a known acetylation site significantly enhances Fli-1-driven activation of the G-CSF promoter, indicating that acetylation at this residue negatively regulates Fli-1 function (Wang and Zhang 2025; Lennard Richard et al. 2016). PKCδ phosphorylates Fli-1 at threonine 312, altering its transcriptional activity (Wang and Zhang 2025). Additionally, mutation of a phosphorylation site of Fli-1 results in increased activation of the GM-CSF promoter (Wang et al. 2021). While ubiquitination has been shown to modulate related ETS family proteins such as the degradation of EWS/FLI by TRIM8 in Ewing sarcoma (Seong et al. 2021); however, the ubiquitination of endogenous Fli-1 remains largely unknown. Our data establish that STUB1 mediates the ubiquitination of Fli-1 via two critical residues, K27 and H260 (Fig. 4). Intriguingly, STUB1 facilitates Fli-1 degradation under basal conditions, but stabilizes Fli-1 in response to inflammatory stimuli (Fig. 5). These findings reveal a novel mechanism of Fli-1 regulation and highlight the importance of context-dependent post-translational modifications in modulating Fli-1 function. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the role of this regulatory axis in inflammatory and immune-related pathologies.

Importantly, LPS stimulation has been reported to enhance splenic CD4⁺ T cell activation (Tough et al. 1997). Consistent with this, we observed a marked increase in splenic CD4⁺ T cell activation following LPS administration, whereas CD4⁺ T cell–specific deletion of Fli-1 significantly attenuated this response (Fig. 2). Notably, the reduction in CD4⁺ T-cell activation was accompanied by diminished LPS-induced CD8⁺ T-cell activation in Fli-1 CD4+ T cell-knockout mice (Fig. 2), likely reflecting the requirement for CD4⁺ T cell help in CD8⁺ T cell expansion and effector differentiation (Provine et al. 2016; Sun et al. 2004). Excessive CD4⁺ T-cell activation has been shown to drive a hyper-inflammatory neutrophil phenotype, promoting bacterial clearance but exacerbating tissue injury and mortality in sepsis (Kasten et al. 2010). Consistently, inhibition of Fli-1-mediated CD4⁺ T cell activation was associated with improved survival in endotoxemia (Fig. 1). Together, these findings suggest that modulation of Fli-1 activity in CD4⁺ T cells may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for controlling inflammation and improving outcomes in infection-associated conditions.

This study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, the effects of SUTB1 gain- or loss-of-function in vivo during endotoxemia require further investigation. Second, the role of Fli-1 in other immune cell types, such as CD8+ T cells, during inflammatory responses remains to be explored. Third, the involvement of the STUB1/Fli-1 axis in infection models, including cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis, requires additional confirmation. Finally, the mechanisms by which Fli-1 regulates the NFκB signaling pathway in T cells are still unclear and warrant further investigation.

In summary, our study identifies a previously unrecognized STUB1-dependent ubiquitination mechanism that regulates Fli-1 protein stability and uncovers a critical role for the Fli-1/NFκB axis in CD4+ T cell activation during inflammation. These findings provide new insight into the molecular control of T cell-mediated immune responses and suggest novel therapeutic opportunities for inflammatory and immune-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Generation of CD4+ T cell-specific Fli-1 knockout mice

CD4+ T cell-specific Fli-1 knockout mice were generated by crossing B6(129X1)-Tg(Cd4-cre/ERT2)11Gnri/J(Stock No:022356, The Jackson Laboratory) with Fli-1floxp/floxp mice (provided by Dr. Maria Trojanowska, Boston University). The knockout phenotype was induced by intraperitoneal (IP) injection of tamoxifen (75 mg/kg, #T5648, Sigma). Mouse genotyping analysis was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using DreamTaq DNA Polymerase (#EP0705; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Endotoxemia mouse model

All animal procedures complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Institute of Laboratory Resources, National Academy of Sciences, MD) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Medical University of South Carolina. Endotoxemia was induced in WT C57BL/6 mice and CD4+ T cell-specific Fli-1 KO mice (male and female, 2–6 months old) by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 25 mg/kg body weight; E. coli O55:B5; #L2880, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in sterile saline. Mouse survival was monitored for up to seven days.

In a separate experiment, WT and CD4+ T cell-specific Fli-1 KO mice received an intraperitoneal injection of LPS (100 μg/kg body weight; E. coli O55:B5; #L2880, Sigma-Aldrich) in sterile saline. After 24 h, splenic CD4+ T cells were isolated for purity assessment by flow cytometry and for Fli-1 expression analysis by quantitative real-time PCR and Western blot. Additionally, spleen tissues were collected for flow cytometric analysis.

Isolation of splenic CD4+ T cells

Splenic CD4⁺ T cells were isolated using the CD4⁺ T Cell Isolation Kit (#130–104–454, Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, a single-cell suspension was prepared from mouse spleens, and red blood cells were lysed using Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (#130–094–183, Miltenyi Biotec). CD4⁺ T cells were first enriched by negative selection using a cocktail of biotin-conjugated antibodies that label non-CD4⁺ T cells, followed by magnetic labeling with anti-biotin microbeads. Labeled non-target cells were retained on a MACS Column placed in a magnetic field (MACS Separator), allowing the unlabeled CD4⁺ T cells to pass through. The enriched CD4⁺ T cells were then further purified by positive selection using CD4 (L3T4) microbeads (#130–117-043, Miltenyi Biotec). The purity of the isolated CD4⁺ T cells was assessed by flow cytometry (Supplemental Figure S1).

Cell culture

HEK293T cells were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, HEK-TLR4 cells were obtained from InvivoGen, Jurkat cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection. HEK293T and HEK-TLR4 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, whereas Jurkat cells were maintained in complete RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Human TNFα (#210-TA) was purchased from R&D systems and MG132 was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (#S2619).

Transfection, lentivirus production, and infection

Plasmid transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000001, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For lentivirus production, target constructs containing single guide RNA (sgRNA), shRNA or complementary DNA (cDNA) were cotransfected with packaging plasmids (pMD2G and pSPAX2) into HEK293T cells using polyethylenimine (#23,966–1, Polysciences). Culture medium was replaced 24 h after transfection. Virus containing supernatants were harvested 48 h post-transfection and filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone (PES) filter. Jurkat cells were infected with virus and selected with hygromycin (200 μg/ml) or puromycin (0.5 μg/ml) for 5 days to eliminate the uninfected cells.

Plasmids

pcDNA3.1 ± FLI1-C‐(K)‐DYK (#129,497) was obtained from Addgene, and pcDNA3.1-C-(k)DYK-STUB1(#OHu27849) was purchased form GenScript. HA-STUB1 and HA-Fli-1 were generated by cloning the corresponding cDNA into the pRK5-HA vector. Various His-tagged ubiquitin constructs were generated as previously described.(Wang et al. 2017; Gao et al. 2009) STUB1 mutations were generated using the Q5® Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (#E0554S, New England Biolabs). Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting Fli-1 and STUB1 were designed at https://www.synthego.com and were cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (#52,961, Addgene). Fli-1-sg1: 5′-GGCCCTGCAGGAGGCTCTGT-3′; Fli-1-sg2: 5′- CCTGCAGGAGGCTCTGTCGG-3′, STUB1-sg1: 5′-ACAGCCTGGCCAAGGAGCAG-3′; STUB1-sg2: 5′- AAGGAGCAGCGGCTGAACTT-3′.

Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation

Cells were rinsed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in EBC buffer [50 mM tris (pH 7.5), 120 mM NaCl, and 0.5% NP-40] or Triton buffer [40 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% Triton X-100] supplemented with protease inhibitor (#A32953, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and phosphatase inhibitors (Calbiochem). The cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration of lysates was determined by Bio-Rad protein assay. Equal amounts of whole-cell lysates were resolved by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and immunoblotted with indicated antibodies. For immunoprecipitation (IP), 2000 to 5000 μg of lysates were incubated with agarose-conjugated antibodies for 3 to 5 h at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitants were washed three times with NETN buffer [20 mM tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.5% NP-40] or Triton buffer before being resolved by SDS-PAGE. Anti-HA agarose beads (#A2095) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

Primary antibodies were diluted with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST). Anti-HA (#3724) and anti–Myc-tag (#2278) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Mouse anti-HA (#901,503) was purchased from Biolegend. Rabbit anti-Flag (#F7425), mouse anti-Flag (#F3165), peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (#A4416), and anti-rabbit secondary antibody (#A4914) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Anti-STUB1 (#55,430–1-AP) antibody was purchased from Proteintech, and anti-Fli-1 (#ab124791) antibody was purchased from Abcam. Total NFκB p65 antibody was purchased from Thermo fisher Scientific (#33–9900) and Phospho-NFκB p65 antibody was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (#3039). GAPDH antibody was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (#5174).

Flow cytometry

Murine spleens were collected 24 h after LPS administration. Spleens were minced with scissors in a petri dish containing PBS supplemented with 2% FBS, then filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. The suspension was centrifuged at 400 × g for 6 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed with PBS. Red blood cells were lysed using 1 ml of Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (#130–094–183, Miltenyi Biotec) for 5 min on ice. After lysis, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and centrifuged again at 400 × g for 6 min at 4 °C. The final cell pellet was resuspended in Flow Cytometry Staining Buffer (#00–4222-26, eBioscience).

Splenic cells and isolated spleen CD4⁺ T cells were stained with the following antibodies: anti-mouse CD3-Alexa Fluor 700 (#100,216, BioLegend), anti-mouse CD4-APC (#100,412, BioLegend), anti-mouse CD8b-APC-Cy7 (#126,620, BioLegend), and anti-mouse CD69-FITC (#104,506, BioLegend). Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). Gating strategies are shown in the Supplemental Figure S2.

Real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from isolated splenic CD4+ or Jurkat cells using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (#74,134, Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (#4,368,814, ThermoFisher Scientific). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Kit (#208,056, Qiagen) and CFX96 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad). Primers used in this study were purchased from Qiagen. Data were analyzed with 2−ΔΔCt value calculation using GAPDH for normalization.

Data analysis

Statistical significance was determined by analysis of variance (ANOVA), Student’s t-test or log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test using GraphPad Prism software. For ANOVA, "Tukey's Multiple Comparison Test" was used for post-hoc comparisons. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
