Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy as an adjunctive treatment for gouty arthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xia Zhou, Shulei Peng, Lang Hu, Song Wei, Zhihuang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy (MSRT) combined with conventional Western medicine (MSRTW) in the treatment of gouty arthritis (GA) and to provide scientific evidence for clinical practice.MethodsA comprehensive search of both Chinese and English literature databases was conducted to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that met the inclusion criteria. The study compared MSRTW to Western medicine treatment (WM) alone. The primary outcome was changes in serum uric acid levels (SUA), and secondary outcomes included Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Additionally, the safety and incidence of adverse events were analyzed.ResultsA total of 6 RCTs involving 376 participants were included. Meta-analysis demonstrated that the MSRTW group was significantly more effective than the WM group in reducing SUA levels (MD = -99.94, P < 0.001), relieving pain (VAS score: MD = -1.50, P < 0.001), and improving ESR and CRP levels. Additionally, fewer adverse events were reported in the MSRTW group, indicating good safety. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the results. Subgroup analyses suggested that treatment duration, gender, and the use of urate-lowering therapy or colchicine may be potential sources of heterogeneity.ConclusionMSRT, as an adjunct therapy, may offer clinical benefits in managing gouty arthritis by reducing urate deposition, relieving inflammation, and improving joint function with good safety. However, due to the limited sample size and variability among studies, further high-quality, multi-center trials are needed to confirm its long-term efficacy and underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06123-1.

Introduction

Gouty arthritis is a common inflammatory arthritis, with its primary symptoms including joint pain, redness, swelling, and functional impairment [1]. It is mainly caused by the deposition of urate crystals in the joints and surrounding tissues, leading to local inflammatory reactions and pain [2]. According to a 2024 report in The Lancet, the global prevalence of gout in 2020 was 55.8 million, an increase of 150.6% compared to 1990 [3]. Although long-term management of gout requires lowering serum uric acid levels or promoting uric acid metabolism to reduce the deposition of urate crystals [4], the application of urate-lowering therapy (ULT) remains insufficient in the management of gout [5]. Current clinical practice primarily relies on non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), colchicine, and corticosteroids to relieve symptoms during acute flares. However, these drugs have certain side effects, including impacts on liver and kidney function, as well as gastrointestinal reactions. Particularly, even when inflammation is alleviated during a gout flare, or SUA levels are controlled by medication, the urate crystals deposited in the joints are difficult to completely dissolve, leading to recurrent gout attacks.

Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy is a novel traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) technique and device developed based on the holistic concepts of TCM and the theory of meridians and sinews. It incorporates principles from endoscopy, TCM needle-knife techniques, and minimally invasive surgery to treat local joint pain without damaging the body’s overall structure. This therapy is an improved version of traditional acupuncture tools, primarily focusing on the use of needle-knife arthroscopy and the water-needle system (see Fig. 1). Over the past decade, this technique has been widely applied in the clinical management of patients with persistent joint swelling and pain [6–8]. With its minimally invasive, flexible, safe, and easy-to-operate nature, this technique integrates traditional acupuncture principles and accupotomy practices while incorporating visual guidance and negative pressure conditions. It effectively resolves pathological nodes, alleviates soft tissue compression, and clears local inflammatory factors and metabolic by-products. These actions reduce tophi formation, relieve pain, improve joint mobility, and inhibit joint destruction, making it a widely adopted approach for treating GA [9–14].Fig. 1Minimally invasive equipment for Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy (A Accupotomy-Endoscope Combined Technique; B Hydro-Tendon Needle System; C Meridian-Sinew Release Acupuncture)

In recent years, RCTs investigating the effectiveness of MSRT in treating gout have increased. However, due to significant heterogeneity in study designs, intervention methods, and outcome measures, the efficacy and safety of this therapy remain controversial. Therefore, conducting a systematic meta-analysis is crucial to integrate the existing research data, thoroughly investigate the clinical effects and safety of MSRT for gout, and provide more solid scientific evidence for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study registration

Adhering to PRISMA guidelines, this meta-analysis was registered on the Open Science Framework [15] (https://osf.io/p7kaq).

Literature search design

To comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of MSRT in the treatment of GA, this systematic review and meta-analysis conducted a cross-database literature search in both Chinese and English. The search was conducted up to December 10, 2024. A rigorous search strategy was employed to ensure the inclusion of global studies. The databases searched included Chinese databases such as China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), China Science and Technology Journal Database (CQVIP), and Wanfang Data; and English databases including The Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. The search strategy covered core terms and synonyms related to GA and MSRT to ensure comprehensive inclusion and scientific validity. The Chinese keywords included “痛风性关节炎”, “痛风”, “高尿酸血症”, “尿酸结晶”, “针刀镜”, “针镜”, “经筋微创”, “经筋松解”, “水筋针”, etc.; the English keywords included “Gouty Arthritis,” “Gout” “Hyperuricemia”, “Urate Crystals”, “Needle-Knife Arthroscopy”, “Accupotomy Arthroscopy”, “Needle Knife Mirror”, “Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy”, “Hydro-Tendon Needle”, “Accupotomy-Endoscope Combined Technique”, “Water Tendon Needle”, etc. The search terms were logically combined using “OR” for synonymous or related terms and “AND” to integrate distinct conceptual elements. The complete and database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1. To increase the comprehensiveness of the study, gray literature and unpublished studies were also included.

Inclusion criteria

Study types

Only RCTs that evaluated the efficacy and safety of MSRTW for treating GA were included.

Patient inclusion

Only studies that enrolled patients diagnosed with GA according to the 2015 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) gout management guidelines [16] were included. There were no restrictions on gender, age, race, or disease duration.

Interventions

The intervention group received MSRTW. The control group received conventional WM, including NSAIDs, colchicine, corticosteroids, uricosuric agents, or ULT. Any study that combined MSRT with other treatments was excluded from this meta-analysis.

Data extraction

First, we used Endnote X9 software to classify the initially retrieved studies and eliminate duplicates. Then, two authors (ZX and PSL) independently screened and reviewed the studies according to the inclusion criteria. For studies with missing information, attempts were made to contact the original authors to obtain the required data; if not available, the study was excluded. In case of disagreements between the authors, a third researcher (HL) was consulted for arbitration. After confirming the studies that met the inclusion criteria, data were extracted by the two authors based on a pre-designed data extraction form. The extracted data included both qualitative and quantitative information, as follows: 1) baseline study information: first author, publication year, sample size, age range, and disease duration; 2) interventions: specific methods, treatment frequency, and treatment duration of MSRTW versus WM alone; 3) outcome measures: VAS, SUA, ESR, and CRP. Quality assessment of literature

Bias risk in RCTs was assessed by two authors (ZX and PSL) using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. This tool evaluates five bias domains (randomization process, deviation from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results). The overall risk of bias for each outcome was classified as “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk” [17]. If disagreements occurred during the evaluation process, a third researcher (HL) was consulted for arbitration.

Data merging and statistical analysis

Efficacy measurement

For binary data, risk ratios (RR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used for analysis; for continuous data, mean differences (MD) were used. If measurement standards were inconsistent, standardized mean differences (SMD) were used for statistical analysis [18].

Heterogeneity assessment

Following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews, I2 statistics and χ2 tests (Q test) were used to assess heterogeneity between studies. When P > 0.10 or I2 < 50%, low heterogeneity was assumed; otherwise, high heterogeneity was assumed when P < 0.10 or I2 ≥ 50% [19]. If more than 10 studies are available, meta-regression is generally recommended to further explore sources of heterogeneity.

Data merging

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) with the meta package (version 8.0.1). When I2 < 50%, a fixed-effects model was used; when I2 ≥ 50%, a random-effects model was used. Binary outcomes were analyzed with the metabin function, and continuous outcomes with metacont. If the number of included RCTs was insufficient, a systematic review was used instead of a quantitative merge to summarize the characteristics and conclusions of the included studies [20].

Sensitivity and subgroup analysis

When the χ2 test P value was < 0.10 or I2 ≥ 50%, sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing one study at a time to identify sources of heterogeneity. Forest plots for sensitivity analysis were generated using the metainf and forest functions from the meta package. If sensitivity analysis did not resolve the heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was performed. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on factors such as patient gender, treatment duration or concurrent drug use. These factors were chosen to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the intervention's effects [21]. Subgroup analyses were implemented using the subgroup argument within the metacont function, and the summary plot of subgroup comparisons was created using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.0) based on these results.

Bias risk

If more than 10 studies were involved in an outcome, a funnel plot was drawn to assess publication bias, and Egger's method was used to test for potential publication bias. The trim-and-fill method was used to adjust the estimated values [22].

Result

Search results and baseline

A comprehensive search was conducted in 7 databases, yielding a total of 519 records. After removing duplicates, 388 articles remained. During the screening process, 345 studies were excluded due to reasons such as experimental research on animals and cells (n = 17), irrelevance to the research topic (n = 265), and review articles, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, and cases (n = 63). Subsequently, full-text screening excluded another 37 studies for reasons such as non-RCT design (n = 31), insufficient or missing data (n = 1), and interventions not meeting the inclusion criteria (n = 5). Ultimately, 6 studies were included in this meta-analysis, as outlined in Fig. 2. This meta-analysis incorporated data from 6 studies, involving a total of 376 participants (189 in the experimental group and 187 in the control group). The publication years ranged from 2014 to 2023. Sample sizes varied from 42 to 80 participants per study. All included trials were conducted at single-center facilities. Quality assessment of included studies

In our study, one trial [9] reported the use of random number tables, and one trial [13] employed the coin-tossing method for randomization, while the remaining trials did not specify the randomization method used. None of the studies mentioned allocation concealment methods; however, baseline characteristics (age, disease duration, gender) showed no statistically significant differences, indicating balanced groups. All studies exhibited significant differences in treatment protocols and did not specify whether blinding was implemented for patients and researchers. The substantial differences in interventions made blinding difficult to achieve, and some outcome measurements (such as VAS scores) might be influenced by the lack of blinding. Although objective indicators are reliable, subjective measures may be subject to bias due to assessors being aware of group assignments. All studies reported complete data and conducted statistical analyses, but none mentioned trial registration information or pre-specified outcome indicators, which may pose a risk of selective reporting. The overall risk of bias assessment for the six studies was rated as high (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Risk-of-bias domains across included RCTs assessed by RoB 2.0 tool

Meta-analyses of outcomes

Changes in serum uric acid levels

SUA was the primary outcome of this meta-analysis. Six trials [9–14], involving a total of 376 participants, reported SUA as an outcome. Statistical analysis suggested that SUA levels were significantly lower in the experimental group compared to the control group. Fixed-effects model analysis showed [MD = −102.13, 95% CI (−113.95, −90.32)], while random-effects model analysis indicated [MD = −99.94, 95% CI (−138.06, −61.82)] (Fig. 4A). However, significant heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 91.6%, P < 0.0001). Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results by omitting one study at a time (Fig. 4B). The random-effects model yielded MD values ranging from −91.02 to −116.86, all of which remained statistically significant (P < 0.0001). These findings confirmed the stability of the results. Subgroup analysis based on treatment duration (≤ 1 week vs. > 1 week), patient gender (male-only vs. mixed-gender), and ULT usage (using ULT vs. not using ULT) showed significant SUA reductions in the experimental group across all subgroups (Fig. 4C). The > 1 week subgroup showed the greatest reduction [MD = −122.04, 95% CI: −153.23 to −90.86], followed by the male-only subgroup [MD = −125.90, 95% CI: −141.93 to −109.86]. Subgroup analysis based on ULT usage revealed a greater reduction in the non-ULT subgroup [MD = −137.54, 95% CI: −154.42 to −120.66] compared to the ULT subgroup [MD = −83.08, 95% CI: −131.84 to −34.33]. Test for subgroup differences was statistically significant for the ULT subgroup (P = 0.0386), but not for gender or treatment duration subgroups (P > 0.05), suggesting that ULT usage may be a potential source of heterogeneity.Fig. 4Meta‑analysis of SUA changes. Pain assessment using the visual analogue scale

A total of 5 trials [9, 11–14], involving 316 participants, reported VAS as an outcome measure. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in VAS scores in the experimental group compared to the control group. The fixed-effects model yielded [MD = −1.57, 95% CI (−1.84, −1.29)], while the random-effects model produced [MD = −1.50, 95% CI (−2.30, −0.69)] (Fig. 5A). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 89.6%, P < 0.0001). Sensitivity analysis showed that the results remained significant across all scenarios, with MD values ranging from −1.07 to −1.63 (Fig. 5B). However, heterogeneity was markedly reduced (I2 = 0%) when omitting the study by LIU Y Y 2023, suggesting this study as a possible source of variation. Subgroup analyses of VAS scores were conducted based on treatment duration (≤ 1 week vs. > 1 week), colchicine use (yes vs. no), and patient gender (male-only vs. mixed-gender). All subgroups showed significant pain relief in the experimental group (Fig. 5C). Greater reductions in VAS scores were observed in the > 1 week subgroup (MD = −1.00, 95% CI [−1.36, −0.64]), colchicine-free subgroup (MD = −1.78, 95% CI [−3.05, −0.51]), and mixed-gender subgroup (MD = −1.78, 95% CI [−3.05, −0.51]). Reported adverse events

Among the included studies, only one trial [13] explicitly reported the absence of adverse effects and wound infections. Another trial [10] documented six cases of gastrointestinal reactions in the experimental group, compared to ten cases of gastrointestinal reactions and five cases of hepatic and renal dysfunction in the control group. The remaining studies did not provide any information regarding adverse effects.

Discussion

Summary of study results

This meta-analysis included 6 RCTs with a total of 376 participants, aimed at evaluating the efficacy and safety of MSRT combined with gout stone removal for the treatment of GA. The results showed that the experimental group was significantly superior to the control group in lowering SUA levels, alleviating pain (VAS scores), and reducing ESR and CRP levels. Specifically, the SUA levels in the experimental group were significantly lower than in the control group (fixed-effects model MD = −102.13, random-effects model MD = −99.94, P < 0.001), VAS scores were significantly reduced (fixed-effects model MD = −1.57, random-effects model MD = −1.50, P < 0.001), and ESR and CRP levels also showed significant improvement (P < 0.001). Moreover, the experimental group had a lower incidence of adverse effects, indicating that MSRT has good safety for treating GA.

However, significant heterogeneity was observed in some outcomes, particularly SUA (I2 = 91.6%) and VAS (I2 = 89.6%). Subgroup analysis based on treatment duration (≤ 1 week vs. > 1 week) and patient gender (male-only vs. mixed-gender) revealed that SUA and VAS improvements were observed across all subgroups, with the greatest reductions noted in the > 1 week and male-only groups, respectively. Interestingly, the reduction in SUA and VAS was more pronounced in participants not receiving ULT or colchicine, respectively. Although this may seem counterintuitive given the established pharmacological effects of these agents, several explanations are possible. These include differences in ULT initiation timing, dosages, and combined treatment regimens, as well as variations in baseline SUA or pain levels. Additionally, colchicine administration during acute attacks might interact differently with symptom resolution dynamics compared to surgical or non-pharmacological approaches. These findings suggest that non-drug interventions such as MSRTW may exert robust therapeutic effects, potentially overshadowing the incremental benefits of conventional drugs when used simultaneously.

Mechanism of Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy

Current research shows that the pathogenesis of gout is well understood. When serum uric acid levels exceed 7 mg/l (420 μmol/l), monosodium urate (MSU) crystals form and deposit [23]. MSU crystals activate the inflammasome, promoting the production of IL-1β, which triggers inflammation and gouty arthritis [24]. The release of IL-1β through inflammasome activation leads to significant inflammatory responses, causing vasodilation and rapid neutrophil aggregation at the crystal deposition site, leading to acute inflammation and pain [25]. Therefore, alleviating gout symptoms requires interventions targeting urate deposition and inflammatory pain.

Although traditional medications can lower SUA levels, ULT is ineffective in some patients, making surgical methods a necessary adjunct [26]. In recent years, minimally invasive gout stone removal techniques have made significant progress. This technique removes gout stones through small incisions or punctures, reducing trauma, shortening recovery time, and significantly alleviating postoperative pain. Compared with traditional open surgery, minimally invasive surgery causes less trauma, recovers faster, and effectively lowers serum uric acid levels, thus reducing the frequency and severity of gout attacks [27, 28]. Moreover, the technique uses visual guidance to precisely remove urate crystals from the joint, protecting tendons, ligaments, and cartilage, reducing postoperative complications, and improving the patient's quality of life [27, 29, 30].

In the treatment of acute GA, three systematic reviews [23, 31, 32] have shown that acupuncture (including electroacupuncture, fire needling, warm needling, etc.) is superior to conventional Western drug therapy in relieving pain, lowering serum uric acid levels, and improving quality of life. Specifically, the electroacupuncture treatment group had significantly better analgesic effects, with a VAS score difference of −2.26 (95% CI = −2.71 to −1.81, P < 0.00001), and the mean difference in serum uric acid reduction was −31.60 (95% CI = −44.24 to −18.96, P < 0.00001). Fire needling and warm needling treatments, either alone or in combination with other therapies, also showed significantly better outcomes than Western medicine, particularly in pain relief and uric acid control.

In conclusion, combining acupuncture with modern minimally invasive equipment (such as visualized minimally invasive techniques), MSRT, as an adjunct, may offer unique clinical advantages in the treatment of acute GA.

Limitations of the study

Despite providing valuable conclusions, this meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the sample size of the 6 included RCTs is small, and all studies are from China, which may limit the generalizability of the results and introduce geographical bias. Second, although most studies reported primary outcome measures, some studies were unclear in their randomization methods, allocation concealment, and blinding procedures, which increased the risk of bias. Furthermore, heterogeneity analysis showed high heterogeneity in some outcome measures, such as SUA and VAS scores (I2 > 90%), which may be attributed to differences in treatment duration, patient gender distribution, and pharmaceutical co-interventions such as the use of urate-lowering therapy (ULT) or colchicine. Other potential sources of heterogeneity may involve differences in baseline disease severity, MSRTW implementation protocols (e.g., procedural details, frequency, or technique variations), and inconsistencies in outcome assessment time points. Unfortunately, these variables were either not uniformly reported or lacked sufficient detail across the included studies, preventing comprehensive stratified analysis. In addition, due to the limited number of eligible studies, meta-regression analysis could not be performed to further explore the potential contributors to heterogeneity. Finally, the evaluation of long-term effects was insufficient, as only a few studies included follow-up assessments, which limits the ability to assess the sustained efficacy and safety of MSRT in the management of GA. Therefore, future research should include larger sample sizes, standardized reporting, and extended follow-up to provide more robust and generalizable evidence.

Suggestions for future research

Firstly, to enhance the reliability and generalizability of findings, future studies should conduct multi-center, large-sample RCTs covering diverse regions and populations, thereby reducing geographical biases.

Secondly, in terms of study design, strict adherence to RCT protocols is essential. Future research should explicitly describe randomization methods, allocation concealment, and blinding procedures to minimize bias risk. Moreover, pre-registration of studies and reporting of pre-specified outcomes are recommended to prevent selective reporting bias.

Given the diverse techniques encompassed by Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy, developing standardized operational protocols is critical. Additionally, studies should explicitly account for potential influencing factors, such as gender, intervention duration, baseline clinical characteristics, and co-interventions like ULT and colchicine, to facilitate meaningful comparisons and robust subgroup analyses.

Regarding outcome assessment, future studies are encouraged to incorporate more objective evaluation methods, such as joint imaging assessments and functional measurements, alongside traditional indicators (e.g., SUA, VAS, ESR, and CRP), to achieve a more comprehensive evaluation of therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, given the chronic nature of gouty arthritis, long-term follow-up studies are needed to assess the sustained effects of Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy on gout recurrence prevention and quality-of-life improvements.

Finally, further research is warranted to explore the mechanisms of action underlying Meridian-Sinew Release Therapy. Investigating its biological basis in reducing urate deposition, alleviating inflammation, and improving joint function will provide critical insights for optimizing treatment strategies and enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that MSRTW, might reduce SUA levels, alleviates pain, and improves inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) in patients with gouty arthritis, while showing a lower incidence of adverse effects compared to WM alone. This therapy, combining acupuncture with modern minimally invasive techniques, effectively relieves pain, reduces urate crystal deposition, and enhances joint function. However, due to the small sample size and potential bias, further large-scale, multi-center RCTs are needed to confirm its long-term effects and mechanisms. Overall, MSRT offers a promising adjunctive treatment for GA, with potential benefits in clinical outcomes and safety.
