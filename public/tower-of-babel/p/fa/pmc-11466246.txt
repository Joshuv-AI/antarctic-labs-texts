Does exogenous glucocorticoid administration during pregnancy precipitate the timing of labor? A scoping review
Authors: Theodoros Karampitsakos, Fotini Kanouta, Christos Chatzakis, Vassilios Bakoulas, Alexandros Gryparis, Petros Drakakis, Djuro Macut, George Mastorakos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo investigate whether synthetic (s) glucocorticoids (GCs) administered between the 24th and the 34th gestational weeks in pre-term labor might precipitate labor, studies on sGCs administration were reviewed. The physiology of endogenous glucocorticoid-related increase in fetal–maternal circulation and its association with labor, followed by a scoping review of studies on exogenous sGCs administered for fetal lung maturation and the timing of labor, were included.Materials and methodsThe methodology of systematic reviews was followed. MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar databases were searched until October 2023, for original studies investigating the administration of sGCs in pregnancies risking pre-term labor. Duplicates were removed, and 1867 abstracts were excluded as irrelevant. Six controlled and four non-controlled studies were included. The index group consisted of 6001 subjects and 7691 controls in the former, while in the latter, the index group consisted of 2069 subjects.ResultsIn three out of the six controlled studies, gestational age at labor was significantly lower in sGC-treated women than in controls, while in three studies, gestational age at labor was lower in sGC-treated women than in controls, with a trend toward statistical significance. In one study, gestational age at labor was significantly lower in controls than in sGC-treated women. In the non-controlled studies, the majority of women delivered less than 1 week from the day of sGC administration.Conclusions In this scoping review, studies lack homogeneity. However, in the controlled studies, a pattern of earlier labor emerges among sGC-treated pregnant women. The use of multiple courses of antenatal sGCs appears to be associated with precipitated labor. Their use should be carefully weighed. Carefully designed trials should examine this ongoing scientific query.

Introduction

Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), a 41-amino acid peptide, as well as its receptors are found in several intrauterine structures such as endometrial glands, decidualized endometrial stroma, placental trophoblast, syncytiotrophoblast, and decidua (1, 2). Placental (pl) CRH is released into both maternal and fetal circulations (20-fold lower in the latter than in the former) (3). Placental, endometrial, and hypothalamic CRH are identical in structure (2, 4). The secretion of placental CRH increases gradually during the third trimester of pregnancy, reaching levels of 500–3000 pg/mL (compared to 10–100 pg/mL in the non-pregnant state), leading to relative maternal hypercortisolism (5, 6, 7) (Fig. 1). A circulating binding protein (CRHbp) regulates the biologic activity of CRH in maternal plasma. The concentrations of CRHbp fall during the last 6 weeks of human pregnancy, resulting in an increase in bioavailable plCRH concentrations (7, 8, 9, 10, 11) (Fig. 1). The expression of the plCRH gene is increased by endogenous (maternal or fetal adrenal) or exogenous synthetic glucocorticoids (sGCs), in opposition to the inhibitory effect these molecules exert on hypothalamic CRH synthesis (3, 5, 12). Placental CRH stimulates in a non-circadian, pulsatile mode, maternal pituitary corticotropin (ACTH), which further stimulates the maternal adrenal cortex to release cortisol (1). Similarly, it stimulates the secretion of fetal pituitary ACTH. This bi-directional stimulation leads to the relative hypercortisolemic state of pregnancy. The only part of the HPA axis that has been shown in the past to be suppressed during pregnancy is hypothalamic CRH neuron secretion (1). Increased plCRH concentrations enhance myometrial contractility, directly stimulate prostaglandin synthesis, increase uterine blood flow, or modulate the synthesis of other placental peptides involved in labor (13). Also, plCRH stimulates the fetal pituitary to release ACTH, as well as fetal adrenals, leading thus to the production of cortisol and DHEAS (Fig. 2). In humans and primates, at term, the placenta does not express cytochrome P450c17, an enzyme necessary for estrogen synthesis from progesterone. In placental syncytiotrophoblast, fetal and maternal adrenal DHEAS, DHEA, and its hydroxylated product 16OH-DHEA are converted to estrogens by enzymes (sulfatase, 3β-hydroxy-steroid-dehydrogenase (3β-HSD) and aromatase), thus bypassing the step catalyzed by P450c17 (14). Of note, GCs increase aromatase expression in the human placenta (15) (Fig. 2). Estrogens, among other substances, regulate the increase in the number of myometrial smooth muscle cell gap junctions prior to labor, while they activate oxytocin receptors, leading to myometrial contraction (13, 16) (Fig. 2). Labor (parturition; delivery) is initiated via a series of clinically observed physiological events. Glucocorticoid-induced plCRH secretion has been associated with triggering term (normally towards the end of the third trimester) or pre-term (it occurs earlier, often related to maternal or fetal stress) labor (17, 18, 19, 20). In fetal lambs, exogenous administration of CRH or GCs accelerates the timing of labor (21). Vaginal labor is associated with increased maternal circulating CRH concentrations, which, in women who deliver pre-term, increase faster between the 26th and 31st gestational week (gwk) while, at 31 gwk, they are greater compared with women who deliver at term(5, 17, 22, 23) (Fig. 3). Circulating maternal cortisol concentrations at 15 gwk were greater in women who delivered pre-term (with a similar trend for cortisol concentrations at 19 gwk) compared to women delivering at term, while they predicted the surge in plCRH at 31 weeks, thus suggesting that maternal cortisolemia might influence the timing of the onset of labor early in pregnancy via stimulation of plCRH secretion (3, 17) (Fig. 3). Role of exogenously administered sGCs on the timing of labor

Prophylactic administration of sGCs should be given antenatally: (i) to women at risk for iatrogenic or spontaneous pre-term labor between 24 + 0 and 33 + 6 gwks; (ii) to women with planned elective cesarean section prior to 36 + 6 gwks; (iii) to women at risk of pre-term labor between 23 + 0 and 23 + 6 gwks (24). The decision to administrate sGCs before the 24 + 0 gwks should be made at a senior level, taking all clinical aspects into consideration (24). Antenatal administration of sGCs accelerates fetal lung maturation and contributes to a decreased prevalence of neonatal death, RDS, and intraventricular hemorrhage, while being considered to be safe for the mother (25). Regarding protocols of administration, clinicians are advised to offer a single course of sGCs, a therapeutic approach which is not associated with any significant short-term maternal/fetal adverse effects (25). A single course of sGCs consists of two doses of 12 mg betamethasone given intramuscularly 24 h apart or in four doses of 6 mg dexamethasone given intramuscularly 12 h apart. Evidence for other dosing regimens, such as two doses of 12 mg betamethasone given 12 h apart, is sparse (25).

There are no human studies in the literature where the primary outcome is the timing of labor following exogenous sGCs administration during pregnancy. The studies included in the present scoping review were designed to study other primary outcomes. However, they provide useful conclusions to be drawn regarding the putative association of antenatally administered sGCs with precipitation of the timing of labor, given that GCs of endogenous or exogenous origin were associated in the past with the triggering of labor (12, 16).

Objective

A scoping review has been conducted regarding studies in which exogenous sGCs have been administered antenatally for fetal lung maturation in the face of suspected pre-term labor and their putative association with the precipitation of the timing of labor.

Materials and methods

Eligibility criteria

This scoping review includes in vivo studies regarding the role of antenatal administration of exogenous sGCs on the timing of labor, following the guidelines of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA; Supplementary Table 1, see section on supplementary materials given at the end of this article) (26). These studies have been selected based on the following eligibility criteria. Inclusion criteria: original studies investigating the administration of exogenous sGCs to pregnant women during the second or third trimester of pregnancy in situations at high risk for pre-term labor, such as fetal growth restriction or premature rupture of membranes, compared to the administration of placebo or no treatment to control groups of pregnant women not at risk for the above-mentioned pathologic entities. Exclusion criteria: editorials were excluded, while reviews were consulted only for the introduction and discussion sections (26) (Fig. 4). Study selection

Studies were identified by searching the MEDLINE (PubMed and PubMed Central), the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar databases up to October 2023 using combinations of the appropriate keywords (glucocorticoids AND pre-term labor/parturition; glucocorticoids AND labor/parturition; betamethasone AND pre-term labor/parturiton; dexamethasone AND pre-term labor/parturition), as well as by scanning references from already included articles and relevant reviews manually (26). The search was performed by title, abstract, keywords, and full text. Additionally, automatic alerts were created for the keywords in order to include the latest studies published. All eligible studies were included, independently of the year of publication. After duplicate results from the examined databases were removed, the abstracts of the remaining 1877 studies were read, and 1843 studies were excluded as irrelevant. Twenty-four articles related to animals were excluded. Thus, ten eligible studies finally emerged from this selection procedure as depicted in a flow diagram (26) (Fig. 4).

Assessment of risk of bias

For the qualitative assessment of the risk of bias in the two randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies, the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) was employed by two authors (TK and CC) (https://methods.cochrane.org/bias/resources/rob-2-revised-cochrane-risk-bias-tool-randomized-trials). Upon disagreement, a third author was consulted (FK). Selection, performance, detection, attrition, and other biases of these RCT studies were assessed through five different domains with 28 questions, and the estimations were classified as low, high, or some concerns (Supplementary Table 2).

For the assessment of risk of bias in non-randomized studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used. This scale is developed to assess the quality of non-randomized studies, including cohort studies. Each study is judged on eight items, categorized into three broad groups: the selection of the study groups, the comparability of the groups, and the ascertainment of either the exposure or the outcome of interest (Supplementary Table 3). The studies with no control group received the minimum in the sections of ‘selection of non-exposed cohort’ and ‘comparability’ (Supplementary Table 4) (https://www.ohri.ca/programs/clinical_epidemiology/oxford.asp).

Many confounders that can influence labor timing, including the use of tocolytic agents, are not well reported in the selected studies. Thus, conducting a meta-analysis out of the selected studies was not feasible.

Data synthesis

The extracted data were put together by the same author (TK). Whenever the synthesis of data needed a more elaborate presentation, a senior author was consulted (GM).

Statistics

Statistical comparisons were applied where data from the reviewed studies were available. Specifically, the t-test or Wilcoxon test was applied in quantitative data, whereas χ2 was applied to categorical data. All analyses were performed in IBM SPSS v.28, and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

All women studied in the included research were treated with steroids only for suspected spontaneous pre-term labor. None of them was treated with steroids before a planned induced delivery for maternal or fetal indications (e.g. pre-eclampsia, non-reassuring fetal heart tones). The included studies were either prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or retrospective cohort-controlled studies (Table 1) and retrospective cohort non-controlled studies (Table 2), published between 1995 and 2021. Characteristics and design of controlled studies (prospective RCTs)

Amin et al., in a retrospective controlled study, examined pre-term infants exposed prenatally to betamethasone. The authors compared 79 (index groups 1, 2, 3) betamethasone-treated pregnant women to 51 untreated pregnant women not at risk for pre-term labor (control group). Women in index groups 1 (n = 50), 2 (n = 12), and 3 (n = 17) received one, two, or more courses of betamethasone for one, two, or more consecutive days, respectively. Information regarding the gestational age at the administration of betamethasone is missing (27) (Table 1).

Pattanittum et al., in a retrospective controlled study, studied 104 (index group) compared to 148 (control group) pregnant women to assess the use of antenatal dexamethasone in women who gave birth in less than 34 gwks in nine hospitals across four Southeast Asian countries within the Southeast Asia Optimising Reproductive Health in Developing Countries (SEA-ORCHID) Project (28). Women in the index group received one or more courses of dexamethasone (98 and 6 women, respectively) between 24 and 34 gwks, while women in the control group did not receive anything. The last injection in the index group was administered at ≥24–27, 28–31, and 32–<34 gwks in 19% (n = 20), 40% (n = 42), and 40% (n = 42) of women, respectively (28).

Murphy et al., in a prospective RCT (Multiple Courses of Antenatal Corticosteroids for Preterm Birth Study), studied 935 (index group) compared to 918 (control group) pregnant women (22). The aim of the study was to estimate the effect of multiple courses of antenatal sGCs (betamethasone or dexamethasone) on neonatal size. Women in the index group received one to five courses of betamethasone or dexamethasone. Eight hundred and ten (87%) and 125 (13%) women received betamethasone or dexamethasone, respectively (5, 385, 305, 150, and 90 women received 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 courses, respectively), while 786 (86%) and 131 (14%) women in the control group received placebo for betamethasone or dexamethasone, respectively (5, 365, 273, 169, and 104 women received 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 courses, respectively). Betamethasone and dexamethasone were administered between ≥25 and <32 gwks (29).

Bastek et al., in a retrospective controlled study, studied 148 (index group) compared to 552 (control group) pregnant women to assess the effect of antenatally administered betamethasone or dexamethasone on late pre-term adverse neonatal respiratory outcomes. Women in the index group received one course of either betamethasone or dexamethasone, while women in the control group, who were not at risk for pre-term labor, did not receive any treatment. The last injection in the index group was administrated at <34 gwks (30).

Miyazaki et al., in a retrospective controlled study, examined pregnant women who delivered very low birthweight infants (<1500 g) and suffered from histologically proven chorioamnionitis. The aim was to evaluate the effect of non-specified antenatal sGCs therapy administered to 3271 (index group) pregnant women compared to 4625 (control group) pregnant women who did not receive sGCs, with regard to very low birth weight infants. Information regarding the gestational age at which sGCs were administered is missing (22).

Gyamfi-Bannerman et al., in a multi-center prospective RCT, studied the risks of neonatal morbidities in women who received betamethasone antenatally due to the risk of late pre-term labor. Pregnant women in the index group (n = 1428) received either half (n = 568) or one (n = 860) course of betamethasone, while those in the control group (n = 1397) received either half (n = 571) or one (n = 826) course of placebo. Betamethasone was administered at ≤34 gwk 6 days, at 35 gwk 0 days to 35 gwk 6 days, and at ≥36 gwk 0 days to 369 (25.8%), 571 (40.0%), and 471 (34.2%) pregnant women, respectively, while placebo was administered at ≤34 gwk 6 days, at 35 gwk 0 days to 35 gwk 6 days, and at ≥36 gwk 0 days to 399 (28.5%), 532 (37.9%), and 489 (33.6%) pregnant women, respectively (23).

Characteristics and design of retrospective cohort non-controlled studies

Elliottet al. studied 15 pregnant women who carried triplets and quadruplets to determine if sGCs administration causes increased contractions or labor in high-order multiple gestations. Pregnant women received at least one course of betamethasone. Information regarding the gestational age at the administration of sGCs is missing (31) (Table 2).

Skoll et al. studied 334 pregnant women to predict which patients presenting with threatened pre-term labor would deliver during the window of the benefit of antenatal betamethasone therapy. The pregnant women received at least one course of betamethasone (130 and 204 women received one and more than one course, respectively). The mean gestational age at betamethasone administration was 31.5 gwks (32).

Boesvelt et al. studied 1375 pregnant women to evaluate the risk for pre-term labor with respect to the prescription of antenatal betamethasone and referral to a tertiary center. One hundred and fifteen, 1182, and 78 women received an incomplete betamethasone course, one or two betamethasone courses, respectively, between 24 and 32 gwk (8).

Adams et al. studied 345 pregnant women to determine the practice patterns of antenatal sGCs administration in women at risk for pre-term labor. Thirty-one, 285, and 29 women received an incomplete course of betamethasone, one or two betamethasone courses, respectively, between 24 and 34 gwks (68 and 277 women at 30.7 ± 2.9 (mean ± s.e.) and 29.1 ± 3.1 gwks, respectively) (9).

Risk of bias of included studies

In the two RCT studies, the risk of bias was assessed, with a low risk of bias in all five domains assessed (https://methods.cochrane.org/bias/resources/rob-2-revised-cochrane-risk-bias-tool-randomized-trials) (Supplementary Table 2).

The methodological quality of non-randomized studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) (https://www.ohri.ca/programs/clinical_epidemiology/oxford.asp). The ratings of the included studies are shown in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. All studies had adequate outcome assessment methods, ascertainment of exposure, and incidence of disease. Four studies had no control group and received the minimum in the sections of ‘selection of non-exposed cohort’ and ‘comparability’ (https://www.ohri.ca/programs/clinical_epidemiology/oxford.asp).

Synthesis of outcomes of controlled studies (prospective RCTs)

Amin et al. found that the gestational age at labor for women in index groups 1, 2, and 3 was 30.2 ± 1.2, 30.4 ± 1.5, and 30.3 ± 0.99 gwks, respectively, while for women in the control group it was 30.6 ± 1.2 gwks. Statistics performed on the reported data revealed that gestational age at labor was significantly lower in group 1 than in the control group (t-test, P = 0.029), but not between either group 2 or 3 and the control group (t-test, P = 0.274 and P = 0.100, respectively) (Table 1). All women delivered between 28 and 32 gwks. Women in index group 1 delivered less than 1 week from the first course; women in index group 2 delivered less than 1 week from the second course, while women in index group 3 delivered less than 1 week after having received more than two courses of betamethasone. All women delivered less than 1 week after the last administered course of betamethasone (27).

Pattanittum et al. found that the gestational age at labor for the index and control groups was 30.4 ± 2.6 and 29.9 ± 2.7 gwks, respectively (statistics performed on the reported data: t-test, P < 0.001) (Table 1). The time duration between the administration of dexamethasone and labor is not reported (28).

Murphy et al. found that the gestational age at labor was <28 wks, 28–32 gwks, 33–36 gwks, and ≥37 gwks for 39 (4%), 280 (30%), 338 (36%), and 278 (30%) women of the index group, respectively, as well as for 27 (3%), 254 (28%), 319 (35%), and 318 (35%) women of the control group, respectively. Statistics performed on the reported data revealed an indicative difference between the two groups (χ 2, P = 0.088) (Table 1). The time duration between the administration of sGCs and labor was <48 h, 48 h–<7 days and ≥7 days for 92 (10%), 153 (16%), and 685 (73%) women in the index group, respectively, as well as for 91 (10%), 131 (14%), and 689 (75%) women in the control group, respectively. Statistics performed on the provided data revealed no difference between the two groups (χ2 , P = 0.465) (29).

Bastek et al. found that the gestational age at labor was <34 gwks, 34–35 gwks, and 35–36 gwks for 65, 40, and 43 women of the index group, respectively, as well as for 117, 181, and 254 women of the control group, respectively (χ2 , P = 0.001) (Table 1). The time duration between the administration of sGCs and labor is not reported for either the index or the control group (30).

Miyazaki et al. found that the gestational age at labor was 27.6 ± 2.6 (mean ± s.d.) and 27.8 ± 3.0 gwks for women in the index and the control groups, respectively (t-test, P < 0.001). The time duration between the administration of sGCs and labor is not reported for either the index or the control group (22).

Gyamfi-Bannerman et al. found that the gestational age at labor was <34 gwks + 6 days, 35–35 gwks + 6 days, 36–36 + 6 days, 37–38 gwks + 6 days, and >39 weeks for 193 (13.5%), 394 (27.6%), 609 (42.7%), 202 (14.2%), and 29 (2%) women in the index group, respectively, as well as for 213 (15.2%), 386 (27.6%), 568 (40.6%), 185 (13.2%), and 48 (3.4%) women in the control group, respectively (χ2 , P = 0.10). The time duration between the administration of sGCs or placebo and labor, respectively, was 33.0 h (median) (15.2–111.6, min–max) and 30.6 h (median) (14.6–111.0, min–max) for the index and control groups, respectively (Wilcoxon test, P = 0.57) (23).

Synthesis of outcomes of retrospective cohort non-controlled studies

Elliott et al. do not report the gestational age at labor. The time duration between the administration of the first course of sCS and labor was <48 h for 4 out of the 15 pregnant women studied (31) (Table 2).

Skoll et al. found that the gestational age at labor was <34 and >34 gwks for 103 and 149 women, respectively. The time duration between the administration (mean gwk = 31.5) of sCS and labor was <1 week for 82 pregnant women (32).

Boesvelt et al. found that the gestational age at labor was 29 gwks + 6 days (mean) and 35 gwks + 0 day (mean) for 467 (delivered <1 wk from sCS administration) and 908 (delivered >1 wk from sCS administration) pregnant women, respectively. The time duration between the administration of sCS and labor was <1 and >1 week for the same 467 and 908 women, respectively (8).

Adams et al. found that the gestational age at labor was 31.1 gwks ± 2.9 (mean ± s.d.) and 36.8 gwks ± 2.7 for 68 (delivered <1 wk from CS administration) and 277 (delivered >1 wk from administration of sCS) pregnant women, respectively. Of the pregnant women who delivered >1 wk after receiving sCS, 36 patients (13%) and 166 (60%) delivered at <34 and ≥37 weeks, respectively. The time duration between the administration of sCSs and labor was <1 and >1 week for the same 68 and 277 pregnant women, respectively (9).

Discussion

Human fetuses may be exposed to increased endogenous or exogenous sGCs in late gestation (24). Stress-associated maternal and/or fetal cortisol secretion, as well as exogenously administered sGCs, may lead to increased plCRH secretion (3, 33). The latter has been associated with the initiation of term and pre-term labor in humans (34, 35). On the other hand, in Europe and North America, pregnant women with fetuses at risk of pre-term labor are treated with sGCs to promote lung maturation, thus avoiding RDS in the first 48 h following labor (36). The cost of their administration may be the loss of brain cells, increased neurodevelopmental disability, fetal growth restriction, and an increased risk for postnatal hypertension, as well as increased postnatal activity of the HPA axis (37, 38). The paradigm of exogenously administered sGCs given to promote fetal lung maturation could reveal indirect hints about the role of sGCs in the length of human gestation.

In the present scoping review, six controlled studies (RCTs and retrospective cohort-controlled studies) and four retrospective cohort non-controlled studies were examined. All studies investigated pregnant women who received exogenous sGCs when at risk for pre-term labor and were compared to control pregnant women or not, respectively. The possibility of performing a meta-analysis was investigated, but this was impossible due to the substantial inhomogeneity of these studies (Tables 1 and 2). However, interesting observations emerged regarding the possible role of exogenously administered sGCs upon the timing of labor as well as the duration between the administration of the last course of sGCs and the timing of labor. Among the selected controlled studies, regarding the timing of labor, in three studies, we found that gestational age at labor was significantly lower in sGC-treated women than in controls (27, 30, 22). In another three studies, we found that gestational age at labor was lower in sGC-treated women than in controls with a trend toward statistical significance (27, 29, 23). However, in one study, we found that gestational age at labor was significantly lower in controls than in sGC-treated women (28). Among the selected controlled studies, regarding the duration between the administration of the last course of sGCs and the timing of labor, in one study, sGCs-treated women delivered less than 1 week after the last administered course of betamethasone (27), while in two studies, there was no significant difference in this duration between sGCs-treated women and controls (29, 23). Among the selected four non-controlled studies, a large part of sGC-treated women delivered less than 1 week after the administration of sGCs (8, 9, 31, 32).

Regarding experimental animals, in 1968, Liggins found that continuous and prolonged infusion of ACTH to single fetal lambs at 88–129 gestational days was followed by pre-term labor on the 4th–7th day of the infusion (21). At birth, fetal adrenal glands weighed at least as much as those of normal lambs at term. Infusions of cortisol into the lamb fetus at a rate of 50 mg/24 h were followed by pre-term labor within 5 days. These observations indicate that the pituitary–adrenal system of the fetus may play an active part in the initiation of labor in the ewe (40). No comparison with previously published human studies regarding the suggested hypothesis can be presented because, to our knowledge, relevant and specific studies are missing in the literature. Only Mati et al., in 1973, induced labor in six postdate women by administrating 20 mg of betamethasone into the amniotic fluid. The mean time to onset of labor in the sGCs-injected group was 67 ± 24.3 h compared to 312 ± 142 h in the placebo-injected patients (39). Of note, administration of sGCs in the amniotic fluid appeared to be more effective in provoking labor than systemic administration, reinforcing the concept that sGCs may act locally in the intrauterine tissues to induce labor. Interestingly, administration of intramuscular sGCs to pregnant women in the pre-term period to induce fetal lung maturation is associated with a transient increase in myometrial contractility, while the frequency of activity increases with advancing gestation as well as in multiple pregnancies (16).

During the past decades, there is enough knowledge gathered in pregnancy physiology, suggesting that the exogenous sGCs administration or an increase of endogenous glucocorticoids is implicated in the initiation of labor. The association of this knowledge with the prophylactic administration of sGCs is provocative. In our view, this hypothesis is a strength of this study. However, no studies directly examining this hypothesis are found in the literature, and therefore, the studies included in this scoping review were designed to examine the effect of sGCs on fetal lung maturation when administered in case of threatened premature labor. The absence of studies directly examining directly the suggested hypothesis becomes the major limitation of this scoping review. A side effect of this limitation is that, in the selected controlled studies, the controls are women who were not at risk for pre-term labor and thus were not given exogenous sGCs. As a result, the resulting pieces of information from the present review could only be indicative of this possible association and should be followed up by carefully designed RCTs, provided that one could bypass ethical issues that might emerge.

In summary, in the studies selected for this scoping review, it appears that in the controlled studies, a pattern of earlier labor emerges among sGC-treated women compared to controls. In three out of the six controlled studies, labor was observed significantly earlier in sGC-treated women than in controls, while in another three of these studies, labor occurred earlier in sGC-treated women with a trend toward statistical significance (27, 30, 22, 29, 23). Only one controlled study found that controls delivered earlier than sGC-treated women (28). Regarding the duration between the administration of sGCs and labor, the reported information in controlled studies is either inconclusive or missing. In two of these studies, the applied statistics did not show any difference between the sGC-treated women and controls (23, 29). Regarding the selected non-controlled studies, a large part of sGC-treated women delivered less than 1 week after the administration of sGCs. The presented data cannot justify a definite conclusion to the suggested hypothesis. However, based on the collected data, the hypothesis cannot be rejected either. Use of multiple courses of antenatal sGCs does not seem to have a delaying effect concerning the timing of labor. Thus, their use should be carefully weighed by the physician. Until then, the use of sGCs in suspected pre-term labor should be applied with caution.

This still open physiopathologic query warrants cohort or RCT trials for extracting solid information regarding the effect of antenatal administration of sGCs on the timing of labor in the various sub-groups of pregnancies at high risk of pre-term labor, such as multiple pregnancies, premature rupture of membranes, fetal growth restriction, etc. If this hypothesis is verified in the future, the administration of sGCs for lung maturation during pregnancy will be further restrained to strictly defined therapeutic demands.
