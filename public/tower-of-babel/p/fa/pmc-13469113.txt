Octahedral-motif-guided design of optoelectronic semiconductors via interpretable machine learning
Authors: Xiaoyu Yang, Wenyue Zhao, Xin He, Weidong Fei, Yuanhui Sun, Yu Zhao, Lijun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Halide perovskites’ remarkable optoelectronic properties stem from their metal halide octahedra. This connection underscores the promise of systematically extracting the physical rules encoded in octahedral motifs to steer the discovery of optoelectronic materials. Here, we develop an octahedral motif-centric, data-driven framework that couples interpretable machine learning with high-throughput first-principles calculations to accelerate the discovery of optoelectronic semiconductors. We construct motif-based descriptors to train a gradient boosting regression tree model for thermodynamic stability evaluation, achieving a low mean absolute error of 83 meV per atom on datasets comprising ~104 materials. Leveraging the model to accelerate materials discovery, we identify 19 unexplored thermodynamically stable semiconductors with favorable optoelectronic properties. Among them Ca2GaCoO5 was successfully synthesized and experimentally verified to exhibit a strong visible light photoresponse. These results support the effectiveness of the machine learning framework for octahedra-containing semiconductors and suggest its potential for extension to other motif-based materials families.

Introduction

Optoelectronic semiconductors enable solar energy conversion, photodetection, and light emission. A large and important subset of these materials is built from octahedrally coordinated units, most notably perovskite-type compounds dominated by BX6 motifs1–6. In halide perovskites, soft corner-sharing octahedral motifs produce strongly antibonding band edges, yielding direct gaps in the visible range, strong absorption, and small carrier effective masses6–8. Oxide-based octahedral frameworks, in contrast, typically exhibit more ionic bonding and stiffer lattices, which often widen band gaps but improve chemical and structural robustness9,10. More broadly, across octahedra-containing crystal families, changes in octahedral chemistry, distortion, and connectivity systematically reshape electronic structure and transport11–14. These trends suggest a general principle: local octahedral motifs provide a common structural handle linking optoelectronic behavior with materials stability. However, first-principles understanding of octahedral stability remains largely perovskite-centric, where systematic trends are typically derived within a few closely related prototypes. Extending such prototype-bound insights to the full landscape of octahedra-containing crystals is nontrivial because the same coordination motif can host widely different bonding and connectivity environments. This limitation is acute for heterogeneous spaces spanning oxides, halides, chalcogenides15,16, spinels17, and related frameworks. Hence, a quantitative and transferable approach is needed that captures stability trends directly from octahedral chemistry and geometry rather than relying on prototype-specific heuristics.

Data-driven machine learning (ML)18,19 has emerged as a powerful complement to first-principles calculations for materials discovery, especially when trained on density functional theory (DFT)20,21 datasets of crystal structures and formation energies22–24. Most existing ML models for crystalline materials rely primarily on elemental composition, global lattice descriptors, or generic local-environment representations25. For example, Voronoi-based crystal descriptors incorporate local coordination and packing information, while Smooth Overlap of Atomic Positions (SOAP)-type descriptors encode local atomic environments in a symmetry-preserving but high-dimensional form26,27. While highly effective, these approaches generally connect elemental and whole-crystal features directly to crystal-level properties, making it difficult to extract chemically explicit motif-level factors. Recently physics-informed descriptors that encode local bonding environments have shown improved interpretability, such as physically interpretable tolerance factors28 and octahedral distortion descriptors29. Bartel et al.28 proposed a Sure Independence Screening and Sparsifying Operator (SISSO)-derived analytical descriptor, the new octahedral factor, which provides probabilistic guidance for the stability of diverse perovskite oxides and halides. Here, we extend this perspective by treating the octahedral motif itself as the central, chemically resolved representation unit for understanding stability trends in octahedra-containing materials beyond a single perovskite-prototype family. At the same time, deep learning methods such as graph neural networks learn latent structural representations directly from crystal graphs and have shown strong predictive performance, but typically rely on much larger datasets and offer less direct chemical interpretability in the small, heterogeneous data regime considered here30.

Motivated by these challenges, we seek a framework that elevates octahedral motifs from descriptive structural units to quantitative and interpretable predictors of thermodynamic stability across chemically diverse crystal families. Rather than treating stability as a black box outcome of composition or lattice statistics, our approach explicitly encodes local octahedral chemistry and coordination contrasts in a motif-based representation designed to remain transferable beyond any single structural prototype. Specifically, we construct physically motivated, motif-based descriptors in which octahedral chemistry, local coordination contrast, and short-range structural heterogeneity are encoded at the level of the motif rather than only at the level of the full crystal or a generic local environment. These descriptors are used to train a gradient boosting regression tree (GBRT) model based on the dataset (detailed in Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1–4) for thermodynamic stability evaluation across chemically diverse crystal families, followed by symbolic regression (SR) to extract compact analytical expressions that expose the dominant stability factors (Fig. 1). Formation energy is chosen as the primary ML target because it provides the most general and physically consistent entry point for motif-level stability learning, whereas convex hull stability and optoelectronic properties are evaluated subsequently within the screening workflow. Beyond predictive accuracy, this framework differs from generic local environment encodings by yielding chemically interpretable motif-level stability rules, and differs from latent graph-based models by explicitly retaining a human readable connection to octahedral chemistry. Results

Development of octahedral-motif-centric, data-driven framework with interpretable ML

To construct an interpretable ML model to evaluate how octahedral motifs affect crystal stability, we built a formation energy prediction model using octahedral-motif-specific feature engineering (Fig. 2a). In feature construction (Supplementary Note 2), we adopt a hierarchical representation that links elemental chemistry, local BX6 octahedral motifs, and global crystal packings. This hierarchy makes the motif a physically meaningful bridge between elements and crystal frame, enabling interpretable rules that remain transferable across diverse prototypes. At the elemental level, we include standard atomic attributes (e.g., radius, electronegativity, electron affinity)31–33. At the motif level, we explicitly resolve the octahedral environment using 23 physics motivated descriptors spanning Goldschmidt tolerance factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{f}$$\end{document}Tf31,34,35, octahedral factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${O}_{f}$$\end{document}Of31,36, octahedral distortion measures37,38, local chemical contrast descriptors between the B-site and coordinating anions26, and indicators of octahedral connectivity. At the crystal level, we add global descriptors such as density, packing fraction, space group and Coulomb interactions39. This procedure generates an initial pool of 664 candidate features.Fig. 2Feature engineering and octahedra-specific descriptors.a Overview of the feature engineering workflow, comprising feature construction, iterative feature selection, and algebraic feature combination. We then refine the feature space through a 3-staged GBRT31,33,40,41 feature engineering workflow that iteratively couples feature selection with feature combination (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5). In stage 1, GBRT-based selection reduces the raw feature pool through five feature selection steps. In stages 2 and 3, octahedral motif anchors are combined by algebraic operators into level-1 and level-2 descriptors, followed by additional feature selection. This process reduces the descriptor number while enriching chemically interpretable descriptor families and lowering the model error. Subsequent recursive feature elimination (RFE)42,43 compacts the motif-informed pool to a 38-feature error plateau (Supplementary Fig. 6), followed by a final RFE to create a representative 34-feature compact set used for our GBRT training and SR (Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. 7). Complete workflow and the feature list are provided in methods section and Supplementary Table 1.

Using these 34 motif-informed descriptors, the GBRT model exhibits reliable predictive accuracy for DFT formation energy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{f}^{{DFT}}$$\end{document}EfDFT). Based on the constructed dataset of 3362 octahedra-containing structures, a 10-fold cross validation yields a mean absolute error (MAE) of 83 meV/atom and R2 = 0.97 (Fig. 3a). Additional learning-curve and diagnostic analyses show no strong signatures of overfitting and only weak inter-feature correlations (Supplementary Figs. 8–11). A complementary error analysis on one test set (Supplementary Note 5) shows that the model remains accurate for most samples (54.8% samples have absolute error below 50 meV/atom), while the largest errors are concentrated in a few chemically atypical outliers (Supplementary Fig. 12). To place this error in context, descriptor-based ML models for crystal formation energies commonly operate in the 100 meV/atom regime, while graph network models reach much lower errors only when trained on datasets orders of magnitude larger than the present one26,44–46. Identification of key motif-level factors and compact analytical expressions governing material stability

Analysis of GBRT feature importance reveals that octahedral-motif-derived descriptors dominate the prediction of formation energy, providing clear physicochemical insight into stability trends. Among the 34 retained features, those explicitly encoding local octahedral environments account for a disproportionate share of model importance (Supplementary Table 1), indicating that variations in octahedral chemistry rather than global lattice attributes primarily govern \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{f}$$\end{document}Ef. Within this motif-focused subset, two classes of descriptors emerge as the principal stability determinants (Fig. 2b). Here, electronegativity is scaled by the covalent radius to reflect bond-forming capability, and the factor of six explicitly encodes octahedral coordination. This descriptor quantifies the balance between the electron-attracting capability of the central \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}BOcta cation and that of its six coordinating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}XOcta ligands. Rather than serving as a purely statistical variable, it can be interpreted as a motif-level measure of how the octahedral coordination shell balances bond ionicity, covalency, and local bond competition. Consistent with periodic trends in electronegativity and covalent radius, it cleanly separates materials according to anion chemistry and exhibits a monotonic correlation with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{f}$$\end{document}Ef (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 13). Physically, this indicates that the thermodynamic stability of octahedral frameworks is governed by how the electron attraction of the central cation, measured on a covalent length scale, is balanced by its coordinating ligands.

In contrast, the low ranking of conventional perovskite descriptors highlights the limitations of classical rules when applied beyond fixed structural prototypes. Similarly, simple geometric metrics such as bond angles, connectivity counts, and space group identifiers show limited discriminative power and are eliminated during feature selection (Supplementary Note 6). These results demonstrate that prototype-specific heuristics fail once chemical diversity dominates, whereas motif-based descriptors remain transferable and physically interpretable.

This result does not contradict the established role of octahedral geometry in perovskite electronic structure. In halide perovskites, metal-halide-metal bond angles and octahedral tilting strongly affect the optical band gap by modulating metal-halide orbital overlap48, while bonding analyses have shown that metal-halide orbital interactions and antibonding band-edge states are central to their optoelectronic behavior49. These studies establish that octahedral geometry, covalency, and metal-ligand hybridization are tightly coupled in prototype perovskites50. Our results are consistent with this picture because the leading descriptors identified here emphasize electronegativity balance, covalent length scales, and local \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}BOcta-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}XOcta chemical contrast. However, in the present chemically heterogeneous octahedral dataset, these effects are distributed across motif-resolved chemical and local-difference descriptors rather than being captured primarily by a single perovskite-style geometric descriptor such as a bond angle, distortion, or octahedral factor.

To provide more direct evidence, we examine whether the retained motif-resolved descriptors miss important octahedral distortion information, we augmented the final 34-feature pool with six additional features regarding octahedral distortions or coordination environments and retrained the GBRT model (Supplementary Note 7 and Supplementary Fig. 14a)51. The predictive performance changes only marginally, with the 10-fold cross-validation MAE varying from 83 to 85 meV/atom, and all added descriptors remaining in the low-importance tail of the feature ranking (Supplementary Fig. 14b, c). A surrogate analysis further indicates that the original 34-feature set already captures a substantial fraction of the information carried by these geometry-related descriptors (Supplementary Fig. 14d). These results suggest that the retained motif-resolved descriptors encode distortion- and coordination-related effects in a distributed manner, even though they are not explicit one-to-one geometric measures.

The GBRT model is used as the primary predictive model to capture nonlinear interactions among motif-resolved descriptors across a chemically heterogeneous space. To further distill these insights, we employ SR52,53 to compress the GBRT-learned feature space into a compact analytical stability relation. This complementary use of GBRT and SR combines the flexibility of nonlinear prediction with the transparency of symbolic representation, enabling both stability-aware screening and chemically interpretable extraction of motif-level stability relations from the same descriptor space. To illustrate how these transport and absorption behaviors arise from the underlying octahedral frameworks, we examine four representative SOMs in more detail (Fig. 5d). CoSbO features a 3D network of strongly distorted CoO3Sb3 motifs connected by both corner and edge sharing. It has a direct band gap of 0.91 eV, with both VBM and CBM dominated by Co states and band-edge charge concentrated on Co at the junctions of the octahedra (Supplementary Fig. 20). The strong spatial overlap of band-edge states on this Co-centered framework is consistent with its exceptionally large absorption coefficient. Pt2NaS3 adopts a layered arrangement of edge sharing PtS6 and PtS4Na2 octahedra and is a near direct semiconductor with a band gap 1.46 eV. Both VBM and CBM are derived from Pt and S states, and the band-edge charge is localized mainly on S within the octahedra, so that an extended Pt-S network supports strong optical transitions and overall absorption is higher than that of MAPbI3. Ca2GaCoO5 represents a more regular 3D framework in which corner sharing CoO6 octahedra are filled by GaO3 tetrahedra and Ca-centered polyhedra. Its direct 2.01 eV gap at the ΓΓ-point arises from spin-polarized Co-d states localized at the connections between CoO6 octahedra, and the band dispersion combines dispersive and more localized components along the ΓΓ-X-S-Y-ΓΓ path. Consistent with this mixed dispersive character, the absorption coefficient is comparable to MAPbI3 at the edge but growing more moderately at higher photon energies. Zn4Ga2O7 highlights a different type of octahedral environment. Its GaO6 octahedral layers are supported by ZnO3 tetrahedra and GaO4 units. The reduced effective mass μμ = 0.16 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${m}_{0}$$\end{document}m0 is the smallest among all candidates, driven by a very light electron mass, and the VBM and CBM involve hybridized Zn, Ga, and O states with band-edge charge distributed over ZnO3, GaO4, and GaO6 polyhedra. Despite this highly dispersive conduction band, its overall absorption is more moderate, with α (E)α(E) increasing in steps and exhibiting several pronounced peaks rather than a single monotonically rising profile. These cases collectively demonstrate that strongly connected and chemically active octahedral frameworks, such as the CoO6 and PtS6 networks, tend to combine light band-edge quasiparticles with intense, broadband absorption, whereas more complex distributions of band-edge states over multiple motifs, as in Zn4Ga2O7, can decouple very light effective masses from the strong absorption responses.

To understand the nature of the chemical bonding in representative SOMs, we have performed the crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (COHP) analysis (Supplementary Note 12 and Supplementary Fig. 21). Consistent with the band structures, projected density of states, and band-edge charge densities (Supplementary Fig. 20), the chemically active interactions are concentrated on motif-resolved \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}BOcta-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}XOcta bonds rather than being distributed uniformly across the crystal. For example, in Ca2GaCoO5, although Ga-O provides the strongest overall framework bonding, the dominant near-edge antibonding contribution is localized on Co-O, consistent with the Co-derived band edges and the central role of CoO6 octahedra in the photoresponse. In Pt2NaS3, Pt-S overwhelmingly dominates both the total bonding strength and the near-edge antibonding contribution, while Pt-Na is negligible, confirming that the optoelectronic functionality is controlled by the Pt-S octahedral network rather than by all structural components equally. These COHP results connect the ML-identified descriptors to the established bonding picture of octahedral semiconductors48–50. Therefore, descriptors based on electronegativity contrast, covalency, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}BOcta-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}XOcta local differences act as physically interpretable proxies for the bonding competition and hybridization pathways that link octahedral chemistry to stability and band-edge activity.

High-performing SOMs correspond to octahedral frameworks with strong and well-defined \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}BOcta-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}XOcta chemical contrast, where chemically active bonds and band-edge states are concentrated on extended octahedral networks, giving rise to both light effective masses and intense optical transitions. In more motif-mixed environments, band-edge charge is distributed across multiple polyhedra, and the relevant bonding channels are correspondingly more delocalized, which decouples carrier transport from strong absorption. Octahedral-motif-based descriptors therefore provide a unified, physics-informed set of design handles that connect local chemistry, thermodynamic stability, and optoelectronic functionality, enabling the targeted discovery of new SOMs.

To further assess dynamical stability, we performed phonon calculations for all 19 SOMs identified in this work (Supplementary Note 13 and Supplementary Fig. 22). Six compounds, including Ca2GaCoO5, Ag2Rb4SbBr9, CoSbO, O2InNa3, Ge3Tl2ZrO9, and ZnRb2Bi2S5, exhibit no imaginary phonon modes within the harmonic approximation. We therefore regard the phonon stable subset as a group of candidate SOMs, while the remaining SOMs may still warrant further investigation under finite temperature or synthesis-dependent conditions. However, phonon instability within the harmonic approximation does not necessarily mean that a material cannot be synthesized. In practice, successful synthesis still faces nontrivial barriers and depends on multiple additional factors, such as reaction pathways, nucleation, diffusion, temperature effects, and kinetic trapping.

Experimental synthesis and characterization of newly discovered SOM Ca2GaCoO5

To test the practical realizability of the motif-guided screening strategy, we selected Ca2GaCoO5 for experimental verification as a proof-of-principle case. This compound exhibits a direct band gap, large absorption coefficients comparable to that of MAPbI365, phonon dynamical stability, and a practically accessible synthesis route66,67. This experimental validation supports the feasibility of the proposed workflow, while a broader demonstration of its general applicability will require synthesis and characterization of additional predicted candidates in future work.

Its synthesis strategy can be adapted from the isostructural compound Ca2GaFeO5 by replacing the Fe precursor with a Co precursor68. As a result, Ca2GaCoO5 was successfully synthesized using four different cobalt precursors (Supplementary Fig. 23), demonstrating its facile synthesizability. Experimental details are provided in the methods section. Figure 6a compares the measured X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern with the simulated pattern, confirming that Ca2GaCoO5 crystallizes in the orthorhombic Pnma space group. Refinement of stress-free powder yields lattice parameter (a = 5.333 Å, b = 14.734 Å, c = 5.547 Å) in excellent agreement with DFT optimized values (a = 5.335 Å, b = 14.356 Å, c = 5.615 Å), with deviation below 2%. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) further corroborated the Pnma crystal structure of Ca2GaCoO5. Lattice fringes corresponding to the (120) and (001) planes are clearly resolved, and the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern matches the calculated crystallographic model (Fig. 6b). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) in the lower panel of Fig. 6b reveals a densely sintered morphology with well-defined grain boundaries, no detectable secondary phases, and an average grain size of approximately 2.09 μm. Furthermore, complementary energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping shows a homogeneous distribution of Ca, Ga, Co, and O elements (Supplementary Fig. 24). Discussion

In summary, we establish a motif-centric and physically interpretable machine learning framework for the rational discovery of optoelectronic semiconductors dominated by octahedral coordination. By constructing descriptors that explicitly encode local octahedral chemistry and coordination contrasts, the model connects empirical chemical intuition with data-driven prediction and enables the extraction of transferable stability rules. The learned stability trends indicate that thermodynamic stability across octahedra-containing materials is primarily governed by motif-level chemical factors, especially electronegativity balance on a covalent length scale and local coordination shell contrast. Guided by these motif-level stability principles, we systematically explored a previously unexplored octahedra-containing chemical space and identified a diverse set of thermodynamically stable compounds with promising optoelectronic properties. Phonon calculations were further carried out for the representative SOMs and six compounds exhibit dynamical stability. Experimental synthesis and optoelectronic validation of Ca2GaCoO5 further demonstrate the practical reach of the approach.

From a conceptual perspective, the novelty of the present motif-centric framework introduces an intermediate structural-unit layer between elemental and whole-crystal features, thereby explicitly encoding chemically and geometrically meaningful mesoscale structural patterns. In chemically diverse yet motif-constrained materials spaces, such a representation facilitates the extraction of transferable stability rules with direct chemical meaning. At the same time, because the descriptor construction is defined on local center-coordination motifs rather than on a single crystal prototype, the same strategy may in principle be extended to other motif-based materials families beyond the octahedral systems studied here. While the resulting predictions are well grounded within the space of octahedra-containing materials, the framework has not explicitly captured the full complexity of structure formation, including phase competition, kinetic effects, or the emergence of alternative coordination motifs. Overcoming these limitations will require approaches that move beyond fixed motif spaces and allow the generation of new structural candidates, rather than selection from predefined configurations. In this regard, integrating interpretable motif-based principles with structure generation models offers a promising path forward, because such models could expand the search beyond parent-derived substitutions while preserving chemically meaningful motif constraints. We therefore view the present workflow as a complementary component for future generative materials design, where physically grounded octahedral stability rules can guide exploration of broader structural landscapes.

On the experimental side, beyond the validated Ca2GaCoO5, the framework identifies a broader set of octahedra-containing candidates that appear chemically and synthetically accessible. Several ternary compounds, such as CoSbO, NaPt2S3, and Zn4Ga2O7, are closely related to previously reported materials and can be understood as arising from straightforward elemental substitutions within known structural motifs, suggesting accessibility via conventional solid state or chalcogenide synthesis routes. More compositionally complex quaternary compounds, including ZnRb2Bi2S5 and Ag2Rb4SbBr9, extend beyond well-studied families but remain structurally analogous to established chalcogenide or pnictide frameworks. Their identification illustrates the practical reach of the motif-guided strategy and highlights clear opportunities for future experimental exploration. In this sense, the motif-based framework functions not only as a discovery tool but also as a chemically interpretable map that can help organize and prioritize experimental efforts across diverse octahedra-containing materials.

Methods

Dataset construction

The octahedral motif dataset was curated by exhaustively screening the ICSD (Data Release 2022.2) using the following criteria: presence of an octahedral motif, high crystal symmetry, inclusion of chalcogen or halogen elements, and a primitive cell with fewer than 50 atoms. As shown in the left panel of Fig. 1a, 3362 crystals featuring octahedral motifs were selected to construct the dataset. The methodology of identifying octahedral motifs and screening process are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

This dataset showcases extensively diverse structures that are distinct from traditional perovskite materials. These structures are distributed across 110 different space groups, which represent nearly half of all recognized space group types. In terms of composition, binary to quinary compounds make up the vast majority of the dataset (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The unit-cell sizes range from 2 to 48 atoms, with a median of 24 atoms (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The number of symmetry operations ranges from 6 to 192, with a median of 16, indicating that the dataset is not restricted only to highly symmetric structures (Supplementary Fig. 2c). In addition, dimensionality analysis shows that the dataset is dominated by 3D compounds but also contains a subset of low-dimensional octahedral materials, including 2D, 1D, and 0D systems (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Therefore, the dataset is designed to provide reliable evaluation performance for compositionally diverse and commonly encountered octahedra-containing materials. However, caution is needed when applying the present model to very low-symmetry structures, large-unit-cell compounds, or more complex multi-motif systems that are underrepresented in the current dataset.

Octahedral connectivity in the dataset is similarly varied. In addition to the common corner-sharing connections, octahedral motifs can also engage in edge-sharing, face-sharing, or a combination of multiple sharing modes when linked with themselves or other polyhedral motifs (Fig. 1b). For example, TiNi3S6 features both corner-sharing and face-sharing. Statistics in Supplementary Fig. 2e show that the dataset’s crystals featuring octahedral motifs predominantly exhibit corner-sharing (35.0%), edge-sharing (16.7%), and face-sharing (5.7%), with isolated configurations making up 13.5%. Mixed connectivity types are also common, including corner+edge (21.9%), corner+face (2.7%), edge+face (0.1%), and corner+edge+face (4.4%).

Density functional theory calculation

The high-throughput DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)21 and Jilin Artificial-Intelligence Aided Materials-Design Integrated Package (JAMIP)69. The electron−ion interactions were carried out using plane-wave pseudopotential methods21. The calculations were performed using a generalized gradient approximation70 within the framework of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)20 exchange-correlation functional. The kinetic energy cutoff for the plane-wave expansion of electron wavefunctions was set to 1.3 times of the maximum default energy cutoff, and a grid spacing of 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\pi }}}$$\end{document}π × 0.03 Å−1 was used for Brillouin zone sampling. The crystal structures were fully optimized with the energy convergence criteria of 1 × 10−6 eV and force convergence criteria of 0.01 eV Å−1. We employed the DFT + U method to accurately describe the strong correlation effects arising from the d-orbital electrons of the transition metal elements. The DFT + U method is implemented in JAMIP, which automatically identifies transition metal elements in the components and sets the U values71–73. For crystals that do not contain transition metals, we utilized the Heyd-Scuseria-Ernzerhof (HSE06)74 hybrid functional to correct the insufficiently accurate band gaps based on the PBE level. The obtained \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{g}^{{HSE}}$$\end{document}EgHSE values were utilized as a scissor to correct the band structure and optical absorption with respect to its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{g}^{{PBE}}$$\end{document}EgPBE. A similar methodology has been employed in previous studies75,76. The transport effective mass was obtained by employing BoltzTraP77. For crystal Ca2GaCoO5, the structural relaxations and electronic structure calculations of were carried out within the spin-polarized formalism to properly account for possible magnetic effects. Its band structure was obtained by considering strong correlation effects of Co d-orbital electrons under HSE06 hybrid functional.

The phonon calculations were performed with a supercell (ensuring lattice parameters larger than 10 Å) of the primitive cell using the finite-displacement method78. In the phonon calculations, the total energy convergence threshold was fixed at 1 × 10−8 eV. We also fully optimized the crystal structures with higher force and energy convergence criteria of 0.0001 eV/Å and 1 × 10−8 eV before we systematically performed our phonon calculations. Chemical bonding was analyzed using the crystal orbital Hamilton population method as implemented in LOBSTER79.

Feature engineering

We constructed an initial feature pool containing 409 elemental features, 23 local motif features, and 232 global crystal features. Elemental and statistical descriptors include maximum, minimum, mean, range, mode, average deviation, and standard deviation of elemental properties, while motif descriptors explicitly resolve the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}BOcta-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{Octa}}$$\end{document}XOcta coordination environment (More details are provided in Supplementary Note 2). Feature selection was performed using GBRT feature importance. At each selection step, a GBRT model was trained on the current feature pool, features were ranked by averaged importance, and weakly contributing features were removed. This procedure was used as a greedy refinement strategy to obtain a compact and informative descriptor subset, rather than to guarantee a globally optimal subset. Feature combination was introduced iteratively after feature selection. The allowed algebraic operators are addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. To keep the generated descriptors physically interpretable, feature combinations were constrained by descriptor family, physical compatibility, and feature complexity. Addition and subtraction were applied only to compatible feature families, division was used for physically meaningful normalization, and multiplication was mainly used in motif-template expressions such as octahedral coordination-weighted terms. The complete workflow and quantitative evaluation are provided in Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6.

Ensemble learning method

We employed the interpretable ensemble learning model GBRT in this work, which is one of the decision tree-based ML algorithms and adopts the gradient boosting method. It sequentially trains decision trees to fit the residuals from previous iterations. GBRT has been widely adopted for ML tasks in the field of materials science, such as predicting formation energy25,31, convex hull energy80, band gap25,31,40 of perovskites, and the infrared, piezoelectric, and dielectric responses of materials81. The model was constructed using the Scikit-learn package82. 10-fold cross-validation was conducted to ensure the robustness of the model’s performance and its insensitivity to specific data subsets utilized for training and testing. The metrics for regression models include mean absolute error (MAE), mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and coefficient of determination (R2). The lower the MAE, MSE, and RMSE values of a model, the better the model’s performance. Conversely, the closer the R2 value of a model is to 1, the better its performance. The hyperparameter search method, results, and the selected hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Note 14, Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Table 5, respectively.

Experimental details

Ca2GaCoO5 was synthesized by solid-state reaction. In addition to CaCO3 and Ga2O3, four cobalt precursors were adopted: CoO, Co3O4, Co2O3 (Rhawn Company), and Co2O3 (Seedior Company). Stoichiometric mixtures were wet-milled in ethanol for 12 h, dried at 80 °C, and then pre-sintered at 800 °C for 12 h in a muffle furnace. The powders obtained were re-milled for 12 h, pressed into 10 mm diameter pellets, and finally sintered at 1100 °C for 3 h with a heating rate of 5 °C/min.

The crystalline structure analysis was examined by X-ray diffraction (PANalytical Empyrean, Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA with a step size of 0.0065°. Crystal structure was further verified through HRTEM (FEI Talos F200X) with SAED pattern analysis. Grain morphology was characterized using dual-beam FIB-SEM (TESCAN AMBER), size distribution was obtained by statistical distribution analysis. Optical absorption spectra (200–2500 nm) were recorded on a Shimadzu UV-3600i Plus spectrophotometer, with the optical band gap determined by Tauc plot extrapolation. Photoresponse properties were evaluated under 630 nm (0.436 W/cm2) and 520 nm (0.296 W/cm2) illumination using a Keysight B1500A semiconductor parameter analyzer.
