Real-time estimation of the optimal coil placement in transcranial magnetic stimulation using multi-task deep learning
Authors: Philipp Moser, Gernot Reishofer, Robert Prückl, Stefan Schaffelhofer, Sascha Freigang, Stefan Thumfart, Kariem Mahdy Ali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has emerged as a promising neuromodulation technique with both therapeutic and diagnostic applications. As accurate coil placement is known to be essential for focal stimulation, computational models have been established to help find the optimal coil positioning by maximizing electric fields at the cortical target. While these numerical simulations provide realistic and subject-specific field distributions, they are computationally demanding, precluding their use in real-time applications. In this paper, we developed a novel multi-task deep neural network which simultaneously predicts the optimal coil placement for a given cortical target as well as the associated TMS-induced electric field. Trained on large amounts of preceding numerical optimizations, the Attention U-Net-based neural surrogate provided accurate coil optimizations in only 35 ms, a fraction of time compared to the state-of-the-art numerical framework. The mean errors on the position estimates were below 2 mm, i.e., smaller than previously reported manual coil positioning errors. The predicted electric fields were also highly correlated (r> 0.97) with their numerical references. In addition to healthy subjects, we validated our approach also in glioblastoma patients. We first statistically underlined the importance of using realistic heterogeneous tumor conductivities instead of simply adopting values from the surrounding healthy tissue. Second, applying the trained neural surrogate to tumor patients yielded similar accurate positioning and electric field estimates as in healthy subjects. Our findings provide a promising framework for future real-time electric field-optimized TMS applications.Keywords: Coil placement, Deep learning, Electric field optimization, Multi-task learning, Transcranial magnetic stimulationSubject terms: Biomedical engineering, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Computational neuroscience

Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), a non-invasive neuromodulation technique, has attracted significant attention in clinical and research domains with both diagnostic and therapeutic applications1–4. In clinical research, TMS is used for the treatment of depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder, but also for the preoperative functional mapping in tumor patients, which aims to identify specific brain regions to minimize the risk of post-surgical deficits5.

During TMS, a coil placed near the scalp induces intracortical electric currents which can either enhance or inhibit neuronal firing patterns. Accurate coil placement is of paramount importance as it directly influences the effectiveness, reliability and repeatability of the procedure6–8. Positioning and orienting the coil to maximize the electric field in the targeted cortical region has been described as beneficial for efficient and focal stimulation9–11. Since landmark-based positioning strategies are prone to misalignments between the coil placement and the cortical target9, computational toolkits have been developed to model the TMS-induced electric fields9,12–16. Today, finite-element-based frameworks with subject-specific brain models, e.g., SimNIBS12, are the gold standard regarding accuracy, but they are computationally demanding. Since SimNIBS and its built-in tissue segmentation do not yet have native support for cancerous tissue, previous studies involving malignancies have manually integrated tumor masks to SimNIBS’ head models17,18. However, many reports simply assumed the cancerous tissue to have the same conductivity as the surrounding healthy tissue19,20, which does not adequately reflect the intratumoral heterogeneity21–23.

Besides modeling the induced electric fields for a specific coil placement, SimNIBS can also be used to find the optimal coil placement for a given cortical stimulation target. These electric-field-based optimizations, however, come at a high computational cost as numerous “forward” simulation iterations are required until the optimal positioning is found out of a large number of possible options. To our knowledge, no report has investigated such coil optimizations in tumor patients, including the effects of using realistic tumor conductivities compared to just adopting values of the surrounding healthy tissue.

In recent years, deep learning has been successfully applied to modeling electric fields in TMS24–27. Trained on large amounts of numerical simulation data, these deep neural surrogates have shown both fast predictions during inference and promising levels of accuracy compared to numerical simulations. Consequently, previous reports have suggested the feasibility of real-time electric field calculations in navigated TMS either using neuronavigation or augmented-reality-based guidance systems28. While automated coil optimization approaches have already been proposed 29, to our knowledge, deep neural surrogates have only been employed for “forward” simulations, i.e., for modeling the electric field for a given coil placement. In a tight clinical workflow, directly obtaining the optimized coil placement without the need to perform an extensive grid search seems not only timesaving but could potentially also contribute to standardizing TMS procedures.

Within deep learning, multi-task learning is an approach in which a neural network is trained to perform multiple tasks simultaneously30, e.g., the classification and segmentation of medical images31,32. While several approaches have been described33, the most popular one leverages the different (yet related) task-specific information to obtain an effective shared feature representation in the hidden layers, while keeping multiple task-specific output layers. This hard parameter sharing can reduce model overfitting and improve generalizability of all the tasks34,35.

In this paper, we propose the first deep neural network to predict both the optimal coil placement and the associated TMS-induced electric field for a given cortical stimulation target. The neural network is implemented in a multi-task learning setting as suggested by the interdependence between coil placement and associated induced electric field. We evaluate the trained model in two subject cohorts, i.e., in healthy subjects and glioblastoma patients. Our approach allows to combine the speed of inference of neural networks with the high accuracy of numerical models and provides an important basis for real-time electric field-optimized TMS applications.

Methods

Data selection

All imaging data used in this study is open and publicly available: the 3T MRI data of 128 randomly selected subjects of the WU-Minn Human Connectome Project (HCP)36 was selected. In addition, 15 subjects from the University of Pennsylvania de novo glioblastoma (UPennGBM) cohort37 with tumors located in the left hemisphere were selected, see Supplementary Fig. S1. Imaging data was resampled to 2 mm isotropic resolution.

Data generation: electric field-based TMS optimization

TMS simulations and optimizations were performed using SimNIBS v4.01, a popular toolkit for finite element-based simulations of non-invasive brain stimulation. For the selected 128 HCP and 15 UPennGBM subjects, a huge number of numerical coil optimizations at different cortical targets were conducted which were subsequently used to train and test the neural surrogate.

First, individualized head models for each subject were created from subject-specific T1w- and T2w MR images using SimNIBS’s preprocessing pipeline charm38, which automatically performs whole-head multi-tissue segmentations, assigns conductivities to various tissues types and creates tetrahedral computational meshes, see Fig. 1a,b.

Example of a head model created by charm: (a) tissue segmentation assigned with realistic conductivity values, (b) computational (gray matter) brain mesh. The cortical stimulation targets in the (expert-segmented) motor cortex are visualized as red points. (c) Example patient from the UPennGBM dataset. T1-w MR image including the provided expert-based tumor segmentation map: peritumoral edematous/infiltrated tissue (ED) in orange, enhancing tumor (ET) in red, and the necrotic tumor core (NCR) in green.

SimNIBS’s TMSoptimize functionality allowed to find the optimal TMS coil position and orientation for a given cortical target point by shifting and rotating the coil in a grid around the target point39. The optimal coil position/orientation was defined as the configuration that maximized the magnitude of the electric field at the cortical target point. We used the auxiliary dipole method9 with the default scalar-valued tissue conductivities and optimization parameters (distance from coil to scalp of 4 mm, coil current dIdt=1Aμs), except for: the radius of the target area was 2 mm, the angular search resolution was 2.5∘, and the spatial search resolution was 1.5 mm. These settings resulted in around 74,500 possible coil placements per cortical target out of which SimNIBS’s optimization routine searched the optimum. The MagVenture-MCF-B65 Butterfly coil40 was selected because of its availability at our medical partners at Medical University of Graz.

For all subjects in this study (128 HCP subjects and 15 UPennGBM patients), the left primary motor cortex was manually segmented by a trained expert on the cortical gray matter mesh, and 174 points randomly distributed along the gyrus ridge were chosen as targets (Fig. 1b). In addition, for 23 HCP subjects we selected 32 cortical target points within the left Broca’s area. SimNIBS’s TMSoptimize and subsequent mesh-to-nifti conversion routines were employed for all target points.

SimNIBS in glioblastoma patients

Charm’s default segmentation routine included in SimNIBS 4.01 did not consider tumor tissue. Hence, we used the expert-refined tumor segmentation maps provided within the UPennGBM repository to add the necrotic tumor core (NCR), the enhancing tumor (ET), and the peritumoral edematous/infiltrated tissue (ED) to the tissue segmentation. An example patient is shown in Fig. 1c. We used tumor conductivities in the range of previously reported values: 0.8 S/m (NCR), 0.4 S/m (ET), 0.2 S/m (ED)21–23.

Neural network: design and training

Our proposed multi-task neural network was based on a 3D Attention U-Net41, an extension of the widely used convolutional encoder-decoder U-Net42,43. Additional attention gates filtered the features propagated through the skip connections and helped the network learn to focus on relevant structures. The network had three input channels, each with spatial dimensions of 112 × 96 × 80 voxels. The first two input channels were the subject-specific T1-w MR image and the tissue conductivity map. The third channel was the cortical target point. Instead of representing the target as a simple binary point, we chose to convert the target point into an inverse distance field to provide some directional information to the network. The U-Net had four depth layers with 32 initial filters. We extended the original 3D Attention U-Net by adding spatial dropout layers44 for model regularization during training (dropout was disabled during inference leading to deterministic model predictions). GELU activation functions45 were used to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and to improve training convergence.

The U-Net’s central bottleneck acted as the branch point of the multi-task network. The conventional U-Net decoder path (output 1) generated a single-channel output representing the magnitude of the TMS-induced electric field generated by the optimal coil positioning. As usual with U-Nets, the decoder output had the same spatial resolution as the input.

In addition, the U-Net’s central bottleneck was attached to a global max pooling layer (thereby reducing the feature map dimension) before being connected to two dense layers (three output neurons each). These yielded the spatial coordinates (output 2) and the orientation (output 3) of the optimal coil placement. The spatial coordinates were given in the RAS system (the x-axis increased from left to right, the y-axis increased from posterior to anterior, and the z-axis increased from inferior to superior) defined by the T1w image, with the origin at the center of the head. The coil orientations were represented as Euler angles in extrinsic z-x-y (ψ-φ-θ) convention with respect to the coil axes (as defined by SimNIBS). A schematic of the proposed network and the positional definitions are shown in Fig. 2.

Schematic of our proposed multi-task network architecture based on a 3D Attention U-Net. The decoder part of the U-Net generated the TMS-induced electric field (magnitude). In addition, the U-Net’s central bottleneck was also fed (via a global max pooling layer) into two dense layers which generated a set of three float values each: the positional coordinates and the orientation (in Euler angle representation) of the optimal TMS coil placement.

The total number of trainable parameters was 6,022,087. The training lasted 250 epochs, used Adam optimizers (initial learning rate = 5e−5) and a batch size of four. During training, the learning rate was reduced by 15% when the total validation loss stopped improving for ten consecutive epochs.

The total multi-task loss function was computed as weighted sum of the three individual loss terms: mean squared error (MSE) for the electric field (ω1 = 1) and mean absolute error (MAE) for the positioning coordinates (ω2 = 0.002). For each predicted rotation angles α∈{ψ,θ,φ}, an angle-aware loss (ω3 = 0.035) accounting for the periodicity of angles was employed:

The 128 HCP subjects were split into a training set and a (held-out) test set: the neural network was trained on 105 HCP subjects (each with 174 cortical targets in the left motor cortex totaling 18,270 samples) using five-fold cross-validation and included only motor cortex samples. The 23 HCP subjects with additionally segmented Broca’s area as well as the 15 UPennGBM patients were placed in the test set. The final predictions used the ensemble of all five trained folds by averaging the individual predictions. The training was performed in Tensorflow v2.1246 on a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090. One training fold took around 45 h on our workstation.

Evaluation strategy

The neural network was evaluated twofold: an internal evaluation using the 23 HCP test subjects and an external evaluation using the 15 UPennGBM glioblastoma patients. First, the internal evaluation with the healthy HCP subjects was performed: the primary internal evaluation focused on the motor cortex, i.e., the region for which the deep neural network had been trained. The secondary internal evaluation in the Broca’s area allowed to investigate the generalizability of the trained network to a brain region that was not part of the training.

Following the internal validation, the neural network was externally evaluated in the motor cortex of the UPennGBM patients which should highlight the network’s ability to handle other sources of imaging data and pathologies.

Statistical analysis

The evaluation metrics included mean absolute error (MAE), third quartile (Q3), root mean squared error (RMSE), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), and Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The predicted coil orientations were further assessed using the geometric error 47:

Rpred is the rotation matrix calculated from the network’s predicted Euler angles and Rtrue is the ground truth rotation matrix. T-statistics with a significance level of 0.05 was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Multi-task network training

The neural network successfully handled the multi-task training achieving decreasing/converging loss curves for all three output tasks, see Fig. 3. Once trained, the network’s total inference time was around 35 ms. To validate the chosen multi-task approach over training two individual neural networks, we compared the validation loss curves in Fig. 3 against the loss curves obtained when training two separate networks, i.e., one for the electric field distribution and one for the coil position/orientation. We found that the minimal validation losses were about 1.75% lower in the multi-task learning setting compared to the individual model approach.

Internal evaluation on HCP subjects: Broca’s area

The results for stimulation targets in the Broca’s area, which the neural network has not seen during training, were notably inferior to the motor cortex results in the previous section. For the electric fields, the MAE of 0.038 V/m (at dIdt=1Aμs) was about twice as high as in the motor cortex with a substantial drop in PSNR from around 34 dB to below 28 dB, see Table 1. The coil placement estimates were also worse in the Broca’s area: the three spatial positioning MAEs were 1.36 mm, 3.31 mm, and 2.63 mm, which is up to 87% higher than in the motor cortex evaluation. The rotational MAE were 2.38∘, 5.46∘, and 21.45∘ with a geodesic error of 20.82∘, which is mainly caused by the low accuracy in the predicted z-Euler angles. Exemplary electric field distributions are shown in the second row of Figs. 4 and 5.

External evaluation on UPennGBM patients: motor cortex

Before applying the neural network to the UPennGBM patients, we compared SimNIBS’s numerical optimization results obtained with integrated realistic tumor tissue conductivities versus simply assigning the conductivity values of the surrounding health tissue to the tumor volume. Paired t-tests showed statistically significant differences in the optimized coil positions and orientations. The mean absolute differences were 0.77 mm, 1.25 mm, 0.89 mm for the three spatial coordinates, while the values were 0.88∘, 0.76∘, 5.03∘ for the coil orientations, see Fig. 6. We also found significant differences regarding the TMS-induced electric fields: In tumor tissue, the electric fields were significantly lower correlated (mean correlation of 0.97, mean PSNR of 45) between the two SimNIBS optimizations (with and without integrating tumor tissue conductivities) compared to the left hemisphere gray matter (mean correlation of 0.99, mean PSNR of 54).

Discussion

While deep learning-based approaches have already been introduced to TMS24–26,28, previous reports focused primarily on forward simulations, i.e., learning the induced electric field for a given coil position. In this paper, we presented the first deep neural network to simultaneously estimate both the optimal coil position/orientation and the associated TMS-induced electric field for a given cortical target. Our approach could enable the medical practitioner to select a cortical target during a TMS session and rapidly obtain the optimal coil placement as well as the TMS-induced electric field for display via neuronavigation or augmented reality-based guidance systems. Also, the simulated electric fields provide valuable options to perform post-hoc analyses of cortical stimulation exposure versus observed effects or target versus off-target stimulation (Table 2).

To handle these different output types, we designed the network in a multi-task learning fashion with multiple outputs: the electric field distribution was predicted via the U-Net’s decoder path, while the optimal positional information was estimated via dense layers connected to the U-Net’s central latent space. We found slightly improved model generalizability using a multi-task approach compared to training multiple individual networks. This is in line with the reported benefits of multi-task learning35.

Once trained, the neural network’s inference time was around 35 ms per target, which is significantly lower than other numerical approaches: On our hardware, SimNIBS’s direct coil optimization required about 3.3 hours (MKL Pardiso solver) or 6.9 days (default CG+AMG solver) using 74,488 candidate coil configurations per target. The Auxiliary Dipole Method (ADM) required around 2 min per target (ADM’s runtime is roughly constant and starts increasing only beyond 100 thousand candidate configurations). A recent study on boundary element fast multipole methods reported runtimes for modeling TMS fields of 3 s 15, which would result in 2.6 days of optimization runtime with 74,488 candidate configurations.

We focused the network training and internal evaluation on healthy subjects and the (left) motor cortex, a frequently studied TMS region. However, we also evaluated the trained network network in the (left) Broca’s area, another area of interest in TMS. Overall, in the motor cortex the electric field predictions were highly correlated and in good agreement with their numerical references with error metrics being in the range of previous “forward neural surrogates”25. The obtained positioning accuracies were far below the reported manual placement errors48 and comparable to the accuracy of robotic positioning systems6,49. The rotations around the z-axis showed generally higher errors since the extrinsic z-x-y Euler convention used a broader angle distribution around the z-axis to represent the orientations in this study than for the other two axes. The high accuracy of the neural network’s positioning estimates was further confirmed by rerunning electric field simulations using the predicted optimized coil placements.

The evaluation in Broca’s area showed that the network successfully transferred some structural information to an unseen region. Nevertheless, the evaluation metrics (especially the angular errors) were significantly inferior to the motor cortex, which could be addressed in future studies by fine-tuning a pre-trained network to specific brain areas of interest. Future work will extend our approach to several TMS-relevant brain regions by including cortical targets from many brain regions in the training phase.

Besides neuroscience research in healthy subjects, TMS is an important (research) tool for both clinical and simulation studies addressing multiple diseases1. In tumor patients, for example, computational approaches have optimized the preoperative motor cortex mapping18 or improved tumor treating fields therapy17,22. While some reports employed realistic tumor conductivities17,18, others just adopted the values of the surrounding healthy tissue19,20. To our knowledge, no analysis regarding the effects of using realistic values on electric-field-based coil optimizations has been published so far. Our work fills this gap and shows that these changes in tissue properties modify the induced electric fields thereby significantly influencing the optimal coil positionings.

In an external evaluation we applied the trained neural network to stimulation targets in the motor cortex of glioblastoma patients. We included only patients with tumors located in the same hemisphere as the TMS targets since we anticipated that contralateral tumors would have little influence on the electric-field-based coil optimization due to the rapidly decaying intracortical electric fields. The accuracy of the network’s predictions in glioblastoma patients was comparable to the results in the internal motor cortex evaluation with healthy HCP subjects. This suggested that the network learned sufficiently well how electric fields propagate in different conductivity environments.

Limitations

In our study we optimized the coil placement based on maximized electric fields at the desired cortical target. Alternative optimization objectives could involve maximizing field components along specific anatomical or neuronal structures, rather than focusing on field magnitudes. These approaches might benefit from incorporating diffusion-weighted MR images and/or tractography data into the simulation. Our deep neural network architecture is designed to be flexible and can easily be extended to include any additional data inputs.

We used a multi-task loss function comprised of the weighted sum of the three individual loss terms. The weights were kept constant during training and were chosen to level the scales of the three validation losses at the end of the training. Although all three tasks were successfully trained, future work might investigate more sophisticated loss weighting strategies 50.

While our coil optimization relied on subject-specific anatomical head models coupled with realistic tissue properties, the suggested coil placements may not necessarily induce the strongest observable stimulation effect. Especially when dealing with diseased patients, cognitive networks may be altered and functional brain areas may be shifted in the presence of pathologies 51,52. Also, since various coil optimization approaches have been described in literature, e.g., considering scalp heuristics 53 or functional networks 29, we encourage future work that compares coil placements obtained with these different methods both in simulation and prospective subject studies.

Our deep neural network requires a tissue conductivity map as input, i.e., a head segmentation with realistic conductivity values assigned to the individual tissue types. We chose to use the segmentation maps provided by SimNIBS, but other high-quality routines may also be used 54. Regarding tumor patients, we used the tumor masks provided within the UPennGBM dataset, but tumor masks could also be obtained by manual segmentation (often available in presurgical functional mapping of brain tumor patients) or with automated/hybrid methods 55.

The tumor conductivities used in this study were chosen in the range of previous reports 21–23. These reported lower conductivity values in perinecrotic areas of tumors compared to the solid tumor compartments and highlighted significant (intratumoral) heterogeneity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a multi-task deep neural network was developed to simultaneously estimate the optimal coil placement and the associated TMS-induced electric field for a given cortical target. The neural network was broadly validated in healthy subjects as well as in patients with glioblastoma using realistic tumor tissue conductivity values. The proposed neural surrogate would allow the medical practitioner to select a cortical point during a TMS session and rapidly obtain information on how to optimally position the coil to induce the maximum electric field at the desired target. Our approach, hence, provides a fast and accurate framework for future real-time electric-field-optimized TMS applications.
