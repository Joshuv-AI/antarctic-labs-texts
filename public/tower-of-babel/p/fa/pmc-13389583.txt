Phytochemical characterization and assessment of anti-leishmanial activity of Toona ciliata M. Roem leaf extract in Leishmania donovani infection
Authors: Vishal Kumar Singh, Prasoon Madhukar, Rajneesh, Rahul Tiwari, Awnish Kumar, Shashi Bhushan Chauhan, Vibhav Gautam, Shyam Sundar, Rajiv Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leishmaniasis, a neglected tropical disease, affects millions of people globally. Various chemotherapeutic interventions have been employed for its treatment; however, the emergence of resistance in parasites and the adverse side effects associated with these medications have necessitated the screening of traditionally significant medicinal plants. We have investigated the phytochemistry screening, characterization, and in vitro anti-leishmanial activity of Toona ciliata M. Roem leaf extract. Leaf metabolites were extracted using a Soxhlet system with ethyl acetate as an extraction solvent. The total phenolic and total flavonoid content were quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu (FC) and aluminium chloride (AlCl3) methods, respectively. HPTLC, HRLC-MS, GC-MS, and FTIR analyses were conducted to ascertain the essential chemical composition and functional groups associated with phytoconstituents present in the extract. Leishmania inhibition assays were performed against both promastigote and amastigote forms, yielding IC50 values of 44.40 ± 5.53 µg ml−1 and 32.34 ± 1.89 µg ml−1 & 21.68 ± 0.59 µg ml−1 in J774A.1 and RAW264.7 cells respectively. Furthermore, to determine the extract's safety profile, a cytotoxicity assay conducted on cell lines such as J774A.1, RAW264.7 and THP-1, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and erythrocytes indicated no toxicity. These findings established a foundation for further comprehensive research on Toona ciliata M. Roem, aiming to identify novel bioactive compounds exhibiting anti-leishmanial activity within the crude extract.

Introduction

Visceral leishmaniasis (VL), or kala azar, cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL), are all clinical manifestations of leishmaniasis, a neglected tropical vector-borne disease caused by different species of Leishmania parasite.1 Post-Kala Azar Dermal Leishmaniasis (PKDL) can also result after a Leishmania infection and usually develops after apparent curing of VL; however, it may also develop in individuals without any prior history of symptomatic VL.2 Currently, over 1 billion people live in areas endemic for leishmaniasis and are at risk of infection. An estimated 30 000 new cases of VL and more than 1 million new cases of CL occur annually. Globally, 12 849 VL cases, were reported to the WHO in the year 2024.3 In general, Leishmania outbreaks are frequently confined to specific geographic regions with suitable climate and socioeconomic conditions that favour the breeding of vector flies. The emergence of drug resistance among parasites and vectors is also compromising the efficacy of currently available drugs for treatment. Therefore, it is imperative to adopt innovative approaches to ensure the identification of novel anti-leishmanial compounds for long-term control and management of this disease.4

Natural products have long been regarded as valuable sources of therapeutics, and advances in technology continue to expand their potential for drug discovery with fewer adverse effects, as demonstrated by the landmark discovery of the antimalarial drug artemisinin from Artemisia annua by Tu Youyou.5,6Toona ciliata M. Roem, a plant of Meliaceae family has been shown to have different pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, antiulcer, analgesic, antifungal, antimicrobial and anticancer activity.7 It has also been traditionally used to treat fever, fungal skin infections, menstrual irregularities, open sores, severe diarrhoea, chronic bacterial infections, and phlegm-related cough. Additionally, it is believed to provide strength and vitality, possess antiparasitic properties, and enhance sexual desire. Several compounds have been identified from different parts of Toona ciliata M. Roem, including 12-deacetoxytoonacilin and 6α-acetoxy-14α,15α-epoxyazadirone, from seeds.8 Additionally, 12α-hydroxystigmast-4-en-3-one, along with C-methyl coumarins, was isolated from the petroleum ether extract of Toona ciliata M. Roem. In addition, both nor-limonoids and limonoids have also been identified from leaves and stems of the plant.9 Other compounds, including toonaciliatone, methyl-3α-acetoxy-1-oxomelic-14 (15)-enate, perforin A, and cholest-14-ene-3,7,24,25-tetrol-21,23-epoxy-21-methoxy-4,4,8-trimethyl-3-(3-methyl-2-butenoate), were also reported in the leaves.10 The different biological activities associated with Toona ciliata M. Roem are attributed to the presence of diversity of phytoconstituents which collectively confers its pharmacological properties. In our study, we have reported several prominent molecules such as Naringenin, Lovastatin, Clindamycin, Berberine, Disogenin etc., which may individually or together confer anti-leishmanial property to the crude extract (Fig. 1). Recently, the antiplasmodial activity of aqueous methanolic leaf extract of the Toona ciliata MJ Roem has been reported by Nalini et al.11 As a result, in this study we evaluated the phytochemical contents and anti-leishmanial characteristics of an ethyl acetate extract obtained from the leaves of Toona ciliata M. Roem.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Chemicals like sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, MW:105.99 g mol−1), TCA (trichloroacetic acid; C2HCl3O2, MW:163.38 g mol−1), gallic acid (C7H6O5, MW:170.12 g mol−1), quercetin (C15H10O7, MW:302.236 g mol−1), and chloroform (CHCl3, MW:119.37 g mol−1) have been procured from SRL, ethyl alcohol (C2H6O, MW:46.069 g mol−1), MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (C18H16BrN5S, MW:414.3 g mol−1; VWR, Life Sciences), Miltefosine (C21H46NO4P, MW:407.576 g mol−1), Lab-Tek chamber slide, Media-M199 and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) were bought from Gibco, Annexin-V/PI Apoptosis Detection Kits (eBioscience™), JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Dye (eBioscience™), carboxy-fluorescein succinimidyl ester (C25H15NO9, MW:473.393 g mol−1; CFSE) (Thermo-Fisher, USA).

Sample collection and sterilization

Toona ciliata M. Roem leaf samples were collected from the Botanical Garden of the Institute of Agricultural Sciences, Banaras Hindu University. Leaf samples were validated and deposited to the plant repository museum of the Department of Dravyaguna, Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University, with accession number DG/22-23/508. Samples were collected in a sterile polyethylene bag and processed within 60 minutes of collection. To achieve full removal of surface contaminants, leaves were washed with running deionised water, followed by 70% ethanol, 0.1% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO, MW:74.442 g mol−1) and finally double distilled water (DDW).

Extraction of crude metabolites

Freshly harvested and sterilized leaves were air-dried under aseptic conditions and subsequently grinded into a fine powder using an electric grinder. The powdered material was extracted using Soxhlet at 60 °C for 72 hours with ethyl acetate (C4H8O2; EA, MW:88.106 g mol−1) as the solvent. The resulting extract was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 55 °C with a rotational speed of 120 rpm. The semi-solid leaf extract was stored at 4 °C until further use. The ethyl acetate extract of Toona ciliata M. Roem was designated as TCL-EA.

Phytochemical screening

To determine the various components of TCL-EA, quantitative phytochemical screening was carried out.

Evaluation of total phenolic content (TPC)

TCL-EA crude extract was diluted in methanol (CH3OH) to prepare a working concentration. TPC was determined using a colorimetric assay with minimal modification in the Folin–Ciocalteu (FC) method.12 The crude concentration of 1 mg ml−1 was mixed with 100 µl of 0.5N FC reagents diluted in water (1 : 1, v/v) and incubated for 15 minutes in the dark. Thereafter, 2.5 ml of sodium carbonate (20%) was added and further incubated for 35 minutes at room temperature. After incubation, the reaction cocktail was vortexed and absorbance was measured at 765 nm using UV-visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu). Methanol was used as the blank. Gallic acid was prepared in different concentrations (5–320 µg ml−1) to obtain a standard calibration plot (Fig. S1). Results were expressed as µg of gallic acid equivalent per mg of EA extract.13

Evaluation of total flavonoid content (TFC)

Total flavonoid content was determined using aluminium chloride method with minor modifications. Briefly, 500 µl (1 mg ml−1) of TCL-EA extract in methanol was mixed with 1.5 ml of methanol, 100 µl of aluminium chloride (AlCl3, MW:133.341 g mol−1, 10%), 100 µl of 1 M sodium acetate (CH3COONa, MW:88.2034 g mol−1) followed by 2.8 ml of water and mixed thoroughly. The reaction mixture was allowed to stand for 30 minutes at room temperature. Post-incubation, absorbance was taken at 430 nm using UV-visible spectrophotometer. A standard calibration plot with quercetin ranging from 25–200 µg ml−1 was used (Fig. S1). Methanol was used as a blank and results were expressed as µg of quercetin equivalent per mg of EA extract.13,14

High-performance thin layer chromatography (HPTLC) fingerprinting

HPTLC of the TCL-EA extract was performed using the CAMAG HPTLC instrument (ANCHROM, Switzerland). HPTLC finger printing was conducted on a 10 × 10 cm pre-coated silica gel 60 F254 TLC plate (Merck, Germany). TCL-EA extract (20 µl, 1 mg ml−1) was applied using a Linomat-4 autosampler equipped with a Hamilton syringe (100 µl) on the TLC plate with a band length of 8 mm. After this, the TLC plate was placed in HPTLC Twin trough chamber with a mobile phase of toluene : chloroform : ethyl alcohol (C7H8 : CHCl3 : C2H5OH; 4 : 4 : 1, v/v/v) for 20 minutes as saturation time. The resulting plate with development distance of 8 cm was allowed to dry at room temperature for 5 minutes and the image was captured by visualizer in short UV range of 254 nm and long UV range of 366 nm. TLC plate was scanned by TLC scanner 4 at UV λmax 254 nm (D2 light source) and 366 nm (Hg lamp, fluorescence mode), and area percentage and value of retention factor (Rf) for the separated components was analysed by winCATS software (version 1.4.10.0001). Anisaldehyde sulphuric acid reagent (C8H10O6S, MW:136.15 g mol−1, ASR) was used for post-chromatographic derivatization.13

Characterization

High-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HRLC-MS)

The crude extract of TCL-EA underwent high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HR-LCMS) analysis using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Dionex UltiMate 3000 RS UHPLC system. The analysis was conducted at the SATHI Foundation-BHU facility, Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi, India. An Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid Mass Spectrometer was employed for the HRLC-MS analysis, operating in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes for a duration of 30 minutes with mass accuracy of ±5 ppm. The sample was introduced using a direct syringe pump at a flow rate of 0.3 ml min−1. Three mobile phases were used for the solvent system: mobile phase A consisted of 100% water with 0.1% formic acid (HCOOH); mobile phase B was composed of 80% acetonitrile (C2H3N, MW:41.053 g mol−1) with 0.1% formic acid (HCOOH, MW:46.025 g mol−1); and mobile phase C contained 100% methanol (CH3OH MW:32.042 g mol−1) with 0.1% formic acid (HCOOH). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Hypersil GOLD™ C18 Selectivity HPLC column (particle size: 1.9 µm; diameter: 2.1 mm; length: 100 mm). All data were processed using the default parameters of “Compound Discoverer 3.3.2.31” and analysed with the assistance of web databases.15,16

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

The GC-MS of crude extract from leaves of Toona ciliata M. Roem was conducted using a Shimadzu QP-2012 Plus gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) apparatus equipped with a TD20 thermal desorption system. The analysis utilized a 30 m long RTx-5Sil MS column, with an internal diameter of 0.25 mm and a thickness of 0.25 µm. The high purity helium gas served as a carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.23 ml min−1. Ten microlitre of filtered (0.22 µm) ethyl acetate dissolved crude extract were injected in the split mode at a ratio of 1 : 100. The phytoconstituents' percentage was determined by GC peak area. The same column was used for mass determination and mass spectrometer was operated in the electron impact mode at 70 eV. The temperature of the ion source and transfer line was maintained at 250 °C. The mass spectra were determined on the scan of mass range 40 to 1000 amu. The spectra were identified based on the comparison of their retention time (RT) and mass spectra with WILEY8 and NIST08 library data from the GC-MS system.17

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The presence of functional groups in ethyl acetate leaf extract was assessed by FTIR (PerkinElmer Spectrum 100). Lyophilised extract was mixed up with dried KBr pellet (MW:119.002 g mol−1), utilized for background recording. The mixture of KBr salt and TCL-EA was compressed into the form of a thin salt disc using hydraulic press. The salt disc was placed on metallic holder of FTIR spectroscope. Hereby, further spectral measurement was scanned in the range of 500 to 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.18

Toxicity studies

Cell cytotoxicity assay

For cell cytotoxicity assay, the cell lines J774A.1 (passage number-15), RAW264.7 (passage number-12) and THP-1 (passage number-22) were procured from National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India, and maintained under an optimum condition in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 condition. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) in a T-25 flask and passaged using a cell scrapper upon reaching 80–90% confluency.

For the assay, 5 × 104 cells per well were seeded in a 96 well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. The cells were then treated with TCL-EA extract at concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 400 µg ml−1 and incubated for 48 hours in a CO2 incubator. Untreated cells serve as negative control. After incubation, 5 mg ml−1 of MTT reagent (VWR, Life Sciences, USA) was added to each well, and the plate was further incubated for 4 hours at 37 °C. Cell viability was directly correlated with the amount of formazan crystals formed. These crystals were dissolved in 150 µL of DMSO and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5–10 minutes. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a reference at 630 nm using a Multiskan microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA).19

Haemolytic assay

To examine the effect of TCL-EA on erythrocytes, a haemolysis assay was performed following the protocol suggested by Sæbø.et al. with slight modifications.20 All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of “Helsinki Declaration” and experiments were approved by the ethics committee of Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University (No. IMS/IEC/NOVEMBER/2025/48 dated 15.11.2025). Written informed consent was obtained from the participant of this study. Blood was collected from healthy volunteer in a heparinized tube and immediately centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 minutes. After centrifugation, supernatant was carefully removed and erythrocytes were gently washed thrice with 1x PBS (pH-7.0) until a clear supernatant was obtained. The erythrocytes were then suspended in 1x PBS to obtain a 3% haematocrit solution. A total of 1 × 105 erythrocytes/well were seeded in a 96-well polypropylene (flat bottom) plate, and cells were treated with several concentration of TCL-EA, 1x triton-X (0.4%) was used as a positive control and 1x PBS served as a negative control. Following treatment, the erythrocytes were incubated for 4 hours at room temperature. The plate was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes followed by adding 100 µL of the supernatant to a fresh transparent, flat-bottom 96-well plate. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader. The percentage (%) of haemolysis was calculated using the formula:% Haemolysis = OD (test) − OD (negative)/OD (positive) − OD (negative) × 100

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from the fresh blood of a healthy volunteer using previously reported protocol with little modifications.21 For assay, 1 × 105 cells per well were treated with increasing concentrations of TCL-EA ranging from 6.25–400 µg ml−1 and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 hours in CO2 incubator. After-incubation, 5 mg ml−1 of MTT reagent (VWR, Life Sciences, USA) was added into each well and incubated for 4 hours at 37 °C. The viability of cells is directly related to the amount of formazan crystals formed. These crystals were dissolved in 150 µL of DMSO and incubated in the dark for 5–10 minutes at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm using a Multiskan microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA).22

Microbial bioassays

The TCL-EA extract was evaluated for its anti-leishmanial activity against Leishmania donovani (LEM-138).

Promastigote inhibition assay

To evaluate the anti-leishmanial potential of secondary metabolites extracted from the leaf of Toona ciliata M. Roem, an antipromastigote assay was performed.23 A total of 5 × 104 parasites per well were seeded in 96-well polystyrene plate. The effects of increasing concentrations of TCL-EA (6.25–400 µg ml−1) on the stationary phase of L. donovani promastigotes were quantified after 24 h and 48 h of incubation at 26 ± 2 °C. Following incubation, parasite susceptibility was assessed using the colorimetric MTT assay. The amount of formazan crystals formed was directly proportional to cell viability. These crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO and incubated in dark at room temperature for 5–10 minutes. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm using a Multiskan microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA) and percent viability was calculated using the following formula.% Viability = (absorbance of treated/absorbance of control) × 100

Amastigote inhibition assay and selectivity indices (SI)

Murine macrophages J774A.1 and RAW 264.7 (5 × 104 cells) were seeded into 8-well Lab-Tek tissue culture slides supplemented with DMEM media with 10% FBS, which was incubated overnight in a CO2 incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. The next day, L. donovani promastigotes (LEM-138) in the metacyclic stage, cultured at 26 ± 2 °C in M199 medium (20% FBS) in a BOD incubator, were used to infect the macrophages. During infection, a parasite-to-host cell ratio of 10 : 1 was used, followed by overnight incubation. Thereafter, the free promastigotes were removed by washing three times using serum-free DMEM media. The infected macrophages were incubated overnight for amastigote transformation. Next day, infected macrophages were treated with increasing concentrations of TCL-EA (10–100 µg ml−1) extract for 48 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Post-incubation the cells were washed with PBS and stained using the Diff-Quick Staining Kit (Real-Gene Labs, Ghaziabad, India). After staining, the slides were observed under a microscope at 10× magnification (Olympus CKX53 Japan, Zeiss microscope, Germany).19 Selectivity index (SI) of TCL-EA was calculated by given formula.Selectivity index (SI) = CC50/IC50

Intracellular ROS/RNS detection using H2DCFDA reagent

For the determination of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS). RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 in two flat-bottom 96 well culture plate representing two cell groups, uninfected (cells only) and infected group (cells + parasites) respectively, and allowed to grow overnight in DMEM media. In infected group, a parasite to cell ratio of 10 : 1 was used, followed by overnight incubation. Thereafter, the free promastigotes were removed by washing two times using serum-free DMEM media. Following overnight incubation, these cells in both groups were treated with, varying concentrations of TCL-EA (6.25–400 µg ml−1) and incubated for 48 hours at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, MW: 34.014 g mol−1) at a concentration of 15 mM was used as a positive control. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with 1× PBS and incubated with H2DCFDA (C24H16Cl2O7, MW: 487.29 g mol−1) at 15 mM for 30 minutes in the dark at 37 °C. Following incubation, the fluorescence intensity was measured using multi-colour plate reader (Epoch) at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and emission wavelength of 535 nm. Cells treated with H2O2 were considered as 100% control in both groups and used as the reference point for comparing intracellular ROS/RNS generation in all extract-treated groups.24

Molecular validation of amastigote inhibition by real-time PCR

DNA was isolated from intracellular L. donovani amastigote cultures using the QIAamp blood DNA mini kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA equivalent to 106 parasites/reaction serially diluted to 10 parasites/reaction was used for plotting the standard curve. A SYBR Green-based, Leishmania genus-specific qPCR was used for the detection and quantification of parasite load as previously reported. Real-time PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 5 (Applied Biosystems) system. The reaction mixture contained 10 µl of SYBR Green master mix (Applied biosystems), 0.5 µl of 10 µM forward primer (5′-CTTTTCTGGTCCTCCGGGTAGG-3′), 0.5 µl of 10 µM reverse primer (5′-CCACCCGGCCCTATTTTACACCAA-3′), 4 µl nuclease free water and 5 µl template DNA making the total volume of 20 µl. All the reactions were performed in duplicate. To quantify the parasites, a standard curve (amplification efficiency-107.5%) was generated from DNA concentrations corresponding to 106–10 parasites/reaction through successive 10-fold dilutions. The threshold cycle (Ct) value for each sample was defined as the point where fluorescence surpassed the set threshold. The parasite load was presented as DNA per µl of parasite.25

Studying the nature of cell death: apoptosis/necrosis

To determine the mode of cell death in promastigotes, apoptosis/necrosis analysis was carried out using Annexin-V/PI dual staining (Dead Cell Apoptosis Kits with Annexin V, Thermo-Fisher, USA). The promastigotes were treated with three concentrations of TCL-EA (IC25, IC50 & IC75) and incubated for 48 hours in a BOD incubator. Miltefosine, a well-documented standard drug was used as a positive control at its IC50 concentration. While untreated cells served as a negative control. Following treatment, the cells were washed twice with 1× PBS, stained with Annexin V and PI following the manufacturer's guidelines, and analysed via BD LSRFortessa™ Cell Analyzer. Apoptotic and necrotic cells were detected using Alexa Flour 488 and 7-AAD respectively. A total of 50 000 events were recorded per sample and analysis was performed using FlowJo™ Software (version 10.10.1).26

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential by using JC-1 dye

The change in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed using a polychromatic 5,50,6,60-tetrachloro 1,10,3,30-tetraethylbenzimidoazolyl-carbocyanio iodide (C25H27Cl4IN4, MW: 652.23 g mol−1, JC-1) assay kit (eBioscience™, USA). Promastigotes (1 × 106/ml) per well were cultured in 6-well plates and treated with IC25, IC50, and IC75 concentrations of TCL-EA for 48 hours, with miltefosine (IC50) as the positive control. Untreated cells served as a negative control. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with 1x PBS and stained with JC-1dye containing media (2.5 µg ml−1) for 10 minutes at 37 °C, and were acquired using a BD LSRFortessa™ Cell Analyzer and data were analysed using FlowJo™ Software (version 10.10.1).27

In silico analysis

Molecular docking study

To execute in silico docking analysis the preparation of ligands and their target molecules play a crucial role. Out of 108 compounds obtained in HRLC-MS, only 75 compounds' 3D structures were available in the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) PubChem database. The crystallographic structures of six important enzymatic targets from different pathways of the genus Leishmania were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database. These includes acetyl-coenzyme a synthetase (8U2R), Trypanothione Reductase (6ER5), 2-desoxiribosyltransferase (6QAI), Triosephosphate Isomerase (1QDS), Pteridine reductase (1E92) and Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (6DWG). The cleaning and preparation were performed to remove unrequired entities like co-factors and water molecules, addition of polar hydrogen, charge (Gasteiger) and determination of active site residues in bonding pocket of targets using Discovery Studio 2024 Client and AutoDockTools-1.5.7. All the ligands were prepared and merged into a single file using chimeraX (version-1.10rc202505210000). The energy minimization of all the ligands was performed and converted into the pdbqt file using PyRx. The dimensions and locations of the grid boxes for all six targets were set according to active site information. The docking was performed in PyRx that uses AutoDock Vina, with an exhaustiveness value of 8. Based on the low binding free energy (kcal mol−1), the top lead compounds were considered for 3D & 2D binding interactions, which were visualized using Discovery Studio 2024 Client.26,28

Data analysis

All experiments evaluating cell cytotoxicity, parasite inhibition, nitric oxide production, and mechanisms of cell death were performed in triplicate (n = 3). The data are shown in the graphs as mean values ± standard error mean (SEM). We used GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 to perform a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and then Tukey's post hoc multiple comparison test to determine, if the results were statistically significant. Comparisons were made between each treatment group and the control group to determine statistically significant differences.

Results and discussion

Phytochemical screening

Evaluation of total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC)

The presence of phenols and flavonoids in the ethyl acetate (EA) leaf extract imparts significant pharmacological properties to the plant. Qualitative phytochemical screening of the TCL-EA confirmed the presence of phenolic compounds using the FC method, with a content of 327.22 µg of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per mg of crude extract. Gallic acid was used as the standard reference for the calibration curve. A linear regression relationship between gallic acid concentration and absorbance was established, yielding an R2 value of 0.9895 (Fig. S1). The higher phenolic content in the EA leaf extract demonstrated potential in controlling excessive inflammation caused by oxidative stress in various chronic diseases.29 Likewise, the flavonoid content in the TCL-EA was determined using AlCl3·H2O method. The estimated flavonoid content was found to be 202.28 µg of QE per mg of crude extract. Quercetin served as the standard reference for creating a calibration curve. A strong linear relationship between quercetin concentration and absorbance was obtained, with an R2 value of 0.9959 (Fig. S1). The flavonoid content in the leaf extract was lower than the phenolic content as shown in Table 1. Flavonoids in the leaf extract of plants are associated with several biological properties such as antioxidant activity, antimicrobial activity, antiparasitic activity, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and neurodegenerative disorders.30,31

High-performance thin layer chromatography fingerprinting (HPTLC)

The presence of different compounds in the extract was visualised from the chromatogram. When scanned at 254 nm, the TLC plate exhibited distinct bands at retention factor (Rf) values of 0.53, 0.85, 0.93, and 0.98, with area percentages of 24.00%, 11.48%, 10.46%, and 19.38%, respectively. Similarly, scanning at 366 nm revealed several bands, of which four were prominent: one unique band at Rf 0.42 (3.96%) and three others at Rf 0.53, 0.84, and 0.95, with area percentages of 13.85%, 20.99%, and 19.57%, respectively. Post-chromatography derivatization (PCD) using anisaldehyde-sulfuric acid reagent (ASR), followed by scanning under white light, revealed additional bands at Rf values of 0.25, 0.44, 0.68, and 0.77, with area percentages of 32.55%, 23.19%, 12.85%, and 19.08%, respectively (Fig. 2). PCD is carried out to identify different bioactive compounds such as phenols, terpenes, carbohydrates/polyols, and steroids by generating bands of violet and, blue/red colour of various intensities. Terpenoid, steroids, and phenols produce shades of blue-violet, red-violet, purple, and red colours after derivatization with ASR. The TCL-EA extract exhibited prominent bands of blue/red, purple, and violet colours, indicating the presence of terpenoids, phenols, and steroids.13,32

The analysis identified 45 compounds in negative-ion mode and 63 compounds in positive-ion mode. Toxicity studies

Cell cytotoxicity assay

The TCL-EA crude extract was evaluated for its cytotoxic effects on J774A.1, THP-1, and RAW 264.7 monocyte/macrophage cell lines. The results showed minimal impact on cell viability in J774A.1 and THP-1 cells at the tested concentrations. However, slight toxicity was observed in RAW 264.7 cells, with a half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of 399.4 ± 0.06 µg ml−1 after 48 hours of incubation. No CC50 values were detected for J774A.1 and THP-1 cells within the range of 0–400 µg ml−1 (Fig. 6).

Cytotoxic activity of TCL-EA was tested against three cell lines THP-1, J774A.1 and RAW 264.7 cells. (i) Positive control, (ii) THP (24 h of incubation), (iii) THP (48 h of incubation), (iv) J774A.1 (24 h of incubation), (v) J774A.1 (48 h of incubation), (vi) RAW 264.7 (24 h of incubation) and (vii) RAW 264.7 (48 h of incubation). All experiments were performed in triplicate and technically repeated twice. The P-value was calculated by comparing means ±SEM of percentage of cell viability of monocyte/macrophage cells, using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey to determine statistical significance. Statistical significance is as follows; ***, P ≤ 0.001; **, P ≤ 0.002; *, P ≤ 0.033.

Haemolytic assay

To assess the haemolytic potential of TCL-EA on erythrocytes, cells were exposed to a concentration range of 6.125 µg ml−1 to 400 µg ml−1. Minimal haemolysis was observed at lower concentrations, such as 0.4% at 6.125 µg ml−1. Haemolysis exhibited a progressive concentration-dependent increase, ranging from 2.7% at 200 µg ml−1 to 17.6% at 400 µg ml−1 (shown in Fig. S4). Importantly, this haemolysis level at 400 µg ml−1 concentration was considerably higher than the concentrations required to achieve anti-leishmanial efficacy observed in vitro. At lower relevant concentrations, the extract exhibited minimal haemolytic activity, indicating a reasonable preliminary safety margin. These findings suggest lower preferable concentrations for biological activity. Further, this sudden increase in haemolysis could be due to presence of membrane active components like Saquinavir, present in the extract which triggers cell death in human erythrocytes.64 For reference, 0.4% of Triton-X, a known surfactant, was used as a positive control and was considered to cause 100% haemolysis due to complete membrane disruption. The haemolytic activity of TCL-EA extract was calculated as a percentage of haemolysis, with results reported as the mean ± the standard error mean (SEM) from all replicates (Fig. 7). Together with the cytotoxicity data, these findings suggest a favourable safety profile of TCL-EA, supporting its potential application as leishmaniasis therapeutics. Also, it indicates that the extract holds promise for further development, particularly through purification approaches aimed at enriching the bioactive constituents while minimizing haemolytic activity.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)

The cytotoxicity of TCL-EA was evaluated using the MTT assay. The percentage viability of the PBMCs after incubation with various concentrations of TCL-EA was used as an indicator of cytotoxic potential. After 48 hours of incubation, the viability of TCL-EA treated PBMCs showed 83% viable cells at a concentration of 6.125 µg ml−1. Cell viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner, with a CC50 value of 330.14 ± 6.87 µg ml−1. The lowest viability was observed at the highest tested concentration (400 µg ml−1), where only 47.8% of PBMCs remained viable. These findings indicate that the crude leaf extract of Toona ciliata M. Roem exerts cytotoxic effects on lymphocytes only at higher concentrations (Fig. 8).

Microbial bioassays

Promastigote inhibition assay

The TCL-EA has shown significant inhibition activity against the promastigote stage of L. donovani in a concentration dependent manner. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for TCL-EA treated cells was found to be 44.40 ± 5.53 µg ml−1 with respect to a positive control, miltefosine which showed an IC50 value of 21.31 ± 0.332 µg ml−1. The graphical representation of these results is shown in Fig. 9. The assay principle involves the conversion of the water-soluble yellow tetrazolium dye into insoluble purple formazan crystals by mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase activity.65 The amount of formazan produced is directly proportional to the number of viable cells. The crystals were then dissolved in DMSO, and the OD was measured at 570 nm to determine cell viability. Based on OD values and IC50 calculations, TCL-EA was found to effectively suppress the growth of L. donovani promastigotes. After 24 hours of incubation, cell viability was reduced to approximately 66%, which further declined to ∼21% after 48 hours, suggesting that metabolites present in the leaf extract exert a relatively slow but effective inhibitory action.

Amastigote inhibition assay

The amastigote stage of the Leishmania parasite is regarded as the detrimental stage of the parasite due to its ability to induce pathological symptoms while residing intracellularly in the host. In this stage, the parasite reproduces, and spreads to other cells and tissues, ultimately resulting in the destruction of host cells and the infiltration of new macrophages.66 In this study an antiamastigote assay was performed to determine the inhibition of intracellular parasitic infection in macrophages. The IC50 value of TCL-EA extract on amastigote parasites after 48 hours of incubation in J774A.1 and RAW264.7 cells was found to be 32.34 ± 1.89 µg ml−1 and 21.68 ± 0.59 µg ml−1 respectively. Miltefosine, used as a positive control, exhibited an IC50 of 14.63 ± 1.2 µg ml−1 in J774A.1 cells, as shown in Fig. 10. Notably, parasite inhibition was more pronounced in the intracellular amastigote stage within RAW 264.7 cells compared to J774A.1 cells (Fig. S5). The SI in our study is the ratio of the compound's cytotoxic half-maximal concentration (CC50) of cell lines to its effective IC50 concentration against Leishmania parasites. This index indicates the safety and specificity of TCL-EA, with higher SI values reflecting better selectivity for parasites over host cells. The SI of TCL-EA towards the promastigote stage of L. donovani was found to be greater than 10 for THP-1, J774A.1 cells and 8.99 for RAW264.7 cells. Meanwhile, the SI value towards the amastigote stage was greater than 10 (SI > 10) in both J774A.1 and RAW264.7 cells, signifying the safety aspect of TCL-EA extract.

All experiments were performed in duplicate. Intracellular ROS/RNS generation detection using DCFHDA dye

The production of intracellular ROS/RNS in the uninfected and Leishmania infected RAW 264.7 cell line was assessed following treatment with increasing concentrations of TCL-EA using the DCFHDA dye. In untreated cells, baseline ROS/RNS generation was observed to be 41.42% and 79.65% in uninfected and infected cells, serving as a reference. Upon treatment with TCL-EA extract, there was a noticeable dose-dependent increase in intracellular ROS/RNS production in both groups. At the lowest concentration (6.125 µg ml−1), cells showed a modest increase in ROS/RNS generation to 48.48% and 82.65% in uninfected and infected groups respectively. With increasing concentrations, ROS/RNS production rose progressively, reaching 151.46% and 244.89% in uninfected and infected cell groups at 400 µg ml−1, representing a 3.65-fold increase compared to untreated cells, while in the infected group there was 3.07-fold increase in ROS/RNS generation. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at a concentration of 15 mM was used as a positive control in both groups, representing 100% ROS/RNS production (Fig. 11). These findings indicate that TCL-EA extract significantly elevates intracellular ROS/RNS levels in a concentration-dependent manner, which may contribute to the clearance of intracellular amastigotes in RAW 264.7 cells.

Studying the nature of cell death: apoptosis/necrosis

Annexin V/PI staining assay was used to examine the nature of cell death occurring in Leishmania promastigote cells under different treatment conditions. In the untreated control group, most cells (78.77%) remained viable, while smaller fractions were undergoing necrosis (7.51%), early apoptosis (3.04%), or late apoptosis (10.68%). When treated with an IC50 dose of miltefosine, there was a noticeable shift in the cell population, with a marked increase in apoptotic cells; 21.18% in early apoptosis and 17.66% in late apoptosis, indicating that miltefosine induces programmed cell death, particularly apoptosis, at its effective concentration. On the other hand, when promastigotes treated with TCL-EA at IC25, IC50, and IC75 doses, exhibited a strong tendency toward necrosis rather than apoptosis. At the IC25 dose, 63.82% of cells underwent necrosis, which further increased to 84.39% and 89.67% at IC50 and IC75, respectively (Fig. 13). This dose-dependent shift towards necrosis highlights the nature of TCL-EA's anti-leishmanial activity. Overall, the study suggests that while miltefosine promotes apoptosis in promastigote cells as already documented,67 TCL-EA primarily induces necrosis, with a stronger necrotic effect at higher doses, providing valuable insight into the mechanisms by which the extract eliminates Leishmania promastigotes.

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential by using JC-1 dye

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1 dye (eBioscience™), a lipophilic fluorescent cation that accumulates in mitochondria based on membrane potential. Healthy cells show red fluorescence due to JC-1 aggregation, while loss of potential during necrosis results in a shift to green fluorescence. This rapid red to green shift helps distinguish necrotic from apoptotic cells.68 Majority of cells in the untreated sample showed red fluorescence but when treated with an IC50 dose of miltefosine shifted the cell population towards green fluorescence usually due to segregation of dye into monomers. Similarly, treatment with the TCL-EA extract with IC25, IC50 and IC75 concentration showed dose dependent segregation of JC-1 aggregates (Alexa flour 488-A/Red) into JC-1 monomers (PE-A/Green) respectively. The segregation of JC-1 dye demonstrates hyperpolarized and disrupted mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) leading to cell death27 as shown in Fig. 14.

In silico studies

In the current study, molecular docking analyses demonstrated that various phytochemical constituents from the TCL-EA extract possess robust and selective binding affinities for six critical enzymatic targets from different pathways in Leishmania, highlighting their potential as promising anti-leishmanial lead candidates. Among the screened molecules, denticulaflavonol (PubChem CID: 10030667) was the most promising compound because it consistently showed high binding affinities to several key metabolic enzymes. Denticulaflavonol had very good binding energies of −11.4 kcal mol−1 against acetyl-coenzyme a synthetase and pteridine reductase 1, and −8.0 kcal mol−1 against 2-deoxyribosyltransferase. This means that protein-ligand interactions are stable and energetically favourable across multiple targets. The compound 2-(4-chloro-3,5-dimethylphenoxy)-N-{4-[4-(2-furoyl)-1-piperazinyl] phenyl} acetamide (PubChem CID: 1255242) also had a strong binding affinity for trypanothione reductase, with a docking score of −10.9 kcal mol−1. This means that it is very likely to effectively inhibit this redox-regulating enzyme that is vital for the survival of parasites. Similarly, Luteolin (PubChem CID: 5280445) showed the strongest interaction with triosephosphate isomerase, with a binding energy of −6.8 kcal mol−1, which demonstrates that it can strongly interfere with the glycolytic pathways of parasites. Compounds like Limonin (PubChem CID: 179651) exhibited a robust binding affinity (−10.7 kcal mol−1) upon docking with dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, an enzyme crucial for pyrimidine synthesis in Leishmania spp. These results collectively indicate that the bioactive compounds in TCL-EA can engage with multiple critical leishmanial targets exhibiting high binding stability, implying a potential multi-target mechanism of action and thus necessitating further experimental investigation. Conclusions

In this study, crude metabolites were extracted from the leaves of Toona ciliata M. Roem using ethyl acetate as the extraction solvent. Phytochemical analysis of the extract through TFC, TPC, and HPTLC fingerprinting revealed the presence of a wide range of bioactive flavonoid and phenolic compounds. TCL-EA demonstrated significant inhibitory activity against both the promastigote and intracellular amastigote stages of Leishmania donovani (LEM-138). The extract was non-toxic to J774A.1 and THP-1 cell lines, with only slight toxicity observed in RAW 264.7 cells. Moreover, it exhibited no haemolytic activity against erythrocytes within the tested concentration range. Treatment of RAW 264.7 cells with the extract led to a dose-dependent increase in intracellular ROS/RNS generation. These bioactivities may be attributed to the presence of biologically important molecules, as revealed by the characterization of the crude extract using HPTLC, HRLC-MS, GC-MS, and FTIR. Identified compounds included coumarin, naringenin, 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol, erucic acid, β-sitosterol, palmitic acid, myristic acid, and abietic acid, all associated with several functional groups potentially responsible for the observed biological activities. The mode of cell death induced by TCL-EA primarily involved necrosis, likely due to rapid changes in mitochondrial membrane potential.

Compounds like vanillin, lauric acid (dodecanoic acid and 1-(hydroxymethyl)-1,2-ethanediyl ester), and oleanolic acid, compounds present in the TCL-EA are reported to possess antiparasitic properties. These bioactive substances may have either individually or synergistically contributed to the anti-leishmanial efficacy of the Toona ciliata M. Roem leaf extract.69–71 Coumarins and their structurally related derivatives like 7-hydroxycoumarin, demonstrate enhanced leishmanicidal efficacy, which has been attributed to their capacity to interfere with parasite surface metalloproteases, modulate intracellular redox homeostasis, and trigger apoptosis-like programmed cell death.72,73 Importantly, these compounds often display selective toxicity towards the parasite with minimal cytotoxic effects on host macrophages, supporting their therapeutic relevance. Curcumin, was also observed in trace amounts in our metabolomics study, has also been reported to emerge as a potent anti-leishmanial molecule.74 Its activity is mediated through multiple mechanisms, including excessive generation of ROS/RNS, alteration in calcium signalling pathways, and activation of programmed cell death cascades in Leishmania species.75

Additionally, several other compounds in the TCL-EA extract are reported to induce oxidative stress, disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, or trigger necrotic cell death. For example, caffeic acid,76,77 genistein,78,79 azidoquinone, plazomicin, hydroxyritonavir, saquinavir, paclitaxel,80 azanidazole, and N-acetylsphingosine, 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol81 have all been reported to promote ROS/RNS generation, impair mitochondrial function, or activate necrosis-associated pathways. Many of these compounds act through redox cycling (e.g., azidoquinone), mitochondrial ROS/RNS generation (e.g., paclitaxel, genistein),82,83 lipid-mediated mitochondrial permeabilization or direct oxidative damage to proteins and membranes.

Our future research will focus on isolation and characterization of the individual compounds responsible for the strongest anti-leishmanial activity observed in the extract. Purified fractions will be subjected to detailed in vitro evaluation to determine their potency, selectivity, and mechanisms of action, including effects on parasite viability, oxidative balance, and mitochondrial function. The identified lead compounds will also be analysed using in silico approaches and pharmacological studies. Ultimately, the most promising compounds will be validated in appropriate in vivo models of leishmaniasis to assess therapeutic efficacy, bioavailability, and safety, thereby establishing their potential as novel anti-leishmanial drug candidates.
