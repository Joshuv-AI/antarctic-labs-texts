Assessment of the therapeutic efficacy of Holothuria polii extract during the muscular phase of Trichinella spiralis infection in albino mice
Authors: Salwa A El-Saidy, Asmaa Sabry, Gihan M El-Khodary, Amal A A Hassan, Dina I Elgendy, Yasmeen M Gawaan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine invertebrates are an important source of bioactive natural products with potential therapeutic applications, owing to their chemically diverse defense mechanisms. Trichinellosis is a widespread food-borne zoonosis caused by Trichinella spiralis (T. spiralis). Albendazole (ABZ) is the main treatment, but its efficacy against encysted and newborn larvae is limited. This limitation, together with emerging resistance, has prompted the search for safer alternative antiparasitic agents, particularly natural compounds. Holothuria polii (H. polii) is a bioactive-rich sea cucumber with promising therapeutic applications. The current study was designed to assess the anti-T. spiralis effectiveness of H. polii extract (HPE), for the first time, against the muscular phase of experimental trichinellosis, either alone or in combination with ABZ, and to explore its prospective application as an adjunct to ABZ therapy to enhance treatment outcomes and tissue recovery. To fulfill this aim, mice were distributed at random into five experimental groups, as follows: a control group and four T. spiralis-infected groups: untreated, ABZ-treated, HPE-treated, and combined therapy-treated. The treatment with HPE, alone or combined with ABZ, reduced larval burden and improved host muscular tissue health by reducing oxidative stress, enhancing antioxidant defenses, and restoring serum liver and muscular enzyme activities to their normal values. Histopathological examination of the treated groups revealed preservation of the skeletal and diaphragmatic muscular architecture, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and degeneration of larval capsules, particularly with combined therapy. These findings were accompanied by downregulation of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) immune expressions in both skeletal and diaphragmatic muscles compared with the untreated and ABZ-treated groups, indicating reduced inflammation and angiogenesis. The present study concluded that HPE exhibits diverse and efficacious activity against trichinellosis and may be regarded as a promising adjuvant treatment alongside ABZ for T. spiralis infection. However, further studies are required to validate its potential efficacy in clinical applications. Introduction

Marine invertebrates are an important source of bioactive natural products with biomedical potential1. Among them, sea cucumbers have gained attention for their broad-spectrum bioactivities, particularly antiparasitic properties2. These activities are mainly attributed to saponins, the principal secondary metabolites in sea cucumber tissues, which contribute to their therapeutic potential against parasitic diseases through their membranolytic properties3,4.

Trichinellosis is a globally distributed foodborne zoonotic disease caused by Trichinella spiralis (T. spiralis), transmitted to humans mainly through feeding on raw or undercooked pork containing encysted larvae5. After ingestion, larvae metamorphose into adults in the small intestine and subsequently release newborn larvae that migrate to striated muscles, initiating the muscular phase of infection, characterized by myalgia, periorbital edema, inflammation, and muscle damage6,7. Larval migration and excretory-secretory products induce inflammatory and allergic reactions, accompanied by lymphocyte and eosinophil infiltration and increased leakage of muscular enzymes into the circulation8. In the striated muscular tissues, larvae transform infected myocytes into nurse cells that support long-term parasite survival through encapsulation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion9. Encystment occurs preferentially in highly active muscles, where larvae become infective and may persist for years10. Hypoxia-induced vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression promotes angiogenesis around nurse cells, ensuring nutrient supply for larval maintenance11,12. Moreover, excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during infection contributes to lipid peroxidation and muscle injury13,14. Histopathological alterations include myofiber degeneration, inflammatory infiltration, edema, and collagen capsule formation around nurse cell–larva complexes, which may progress to fibrosis and calcification during chronic infection15,16.

Albendazole (ABZ) is a broad-spectrum benzimidazole anthelmintic drug widely used to treat T. spiralis infections because of its proven efficacy, oral administration, low cost, and favorable therapeutic index6,17. It is particularly effective during the early stage of infection, reducing adult worm burden and intestinal larval production17,18. Its antiparasitic activity is primarily attributed to impeding microtubule polymerization through selective binding to parasite β-tubulin and impairment of glucose uptake, leading to parasite starvation19. However, its efficacy against encysted and newborn larvae is limited18. Furthermore, ABZ suffers from poor solubility, low bioavailability, reduced intestinal absorption, and the emergence of drug resistance20,21. Its use may also be associated with gastrointestinal, hepatic, hematological, and neurological adverse effects at high doses22. Therefore, its use during pregnancy is generally avoided, particularly in the first trimester, due to potential teratogenic and fetotoxic risks. In addition, its use in young children, especially those under two years of age, is restricted because of the risk of adverse effects23. These limitations have driven interest in safer and more effective natural alternatives, particularly from marine invertebrates that have shown promising antiparasitic activities2.

Holothuria polii(H. polii)24 is a dominant holothurian species along the Egyptian Mediterranean coast, characterized by a dark-brown, cylindrical body with a thick integument containing calcareous ossicles25,26. Its biological activities are mainly attributed to bioactive metabolites present in its crude extract26,27. Previous studies have reported antiparasitic, antimicrobial, antitumor, and anti-inflammatory properties of H. polii extract (HPE), largely due to triterpene glycoside saponins such as echinosides A and B28–31. The antitumor activity of its aqueous fraction has been linked to inhibition of cell proliferation, suppression of epithelial–mesenchymal transition markers, and modulation of inflammatory mediators, resulting in reduced tumor progression and metastasis31. In addition, ethanolic HPE demonstrated significant activity against adult worms of T. spiralis26 and Schistosoma mansoni32. Accordingly, the current study was designed to evaluate the therapeutic effectiveness of HPE against the muscular phase of experimental trichinellosis in male Swiss albino mice and to assess its potential as an adjunctive therapy to ABZ. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate HPE in this context, aiming to enhance treatment outcomes and tissue recovery.

Materials and methods

Sampling and identification of H. polii

H. polii specimens were collected from Abu Qir, Alexandria, Egypt (31°18′45.96″N, 30°03′38.16″E, Fig. 1), following local regulations. Species identification was based on morphological traits and ossicle shape, as previously described25,26. In the laboratory, samples were washed and eviscerated, and the body wall was chopped and stored at − 20 °C until extraction.

A map of the H. polii sampling site, Abu Qir (red dot), on the Egyptian Mediterranean Sea coast, Alexandria. The map was created using ArcGIS Desktop version 10.8 (Esri, Redlands, CA, USA; https://desktop.arcgis.com/).

Preparation of H. polii ethanolic extract

The ethanolic HPE was prepared from the body wall following Mona et al.32. The tegument homogenate was prepared using 95% ethanol at a 1:4 weight-to-volume ratio and incubated in the dark at ambient temperature for three days with gentle agitation. The homogenate was then filtered, concentrated under reduced pressure, and lyophilized to yield 0.6% of the initial weight. Finally, the dried extract was preserved at − 80 °C until use.

Preparation of the inoculum of T. spiralis larvae

The isolate of T. spiralis (ISS6158) was initially recovered from infected pork meat at the Cairo abattoir and preserved in the laboratory via serial passages in mice. Five weeks post-infection, the mouse tissues were digested with artificial gastric juice (1% pepsin and 1% concentrated hydrochloric acid in 200 ml of warm distilled water) at 37 °C for 2 h with continuous stirring and then filtered. Larvae were recovered by sedimentation, extensively rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and finally enumerated microscopically using a McMaster counting chamber33,34.

In vivo experimental design and dosage regimen

Forty-five male Swiss albino mice, 6–8 weeks old and 25–30 g in weight, were acquired from the Theodore Bilharz Research Institute, Giza, Egypt. Mice were maintained in polypropylene cages in the Zoology Department’s animal house, Faculty of Science, Damanhour University, under standard laboratory conditions (five mice per cage, 25 °C, and a 12 h light/dark cycle) with adequate ventilation and unrestricted access to standard food and potable water throughout the experimental period. Mice were acclimatized for seven days before the initiation of the experiment. Mice were randomly assigned to five experimental groups as detailed below:

Control group (GI). Five mice were neither infected nor treated.

Infected and untreated group (GII). Ten mice were infected orally with 200 T. spiralis larvae in 0.25 ml of distilled water/mouse, based on El-Hamed et al.’s study35, and administered no treatment.

Infected and ABZ-treated group (GIII). Ten T. spiralis-infected mice were administered an oral dose of 50 mg ABZ/kg body weight (b.w.)/day, following Salama et al.7. ABZ [Alzental® suspension with a concentration of 20 mg/ml; Egyptian International Pharmaceutical Industries Co. (EIPICO)] was diluted with sterile distilled water to obtain the required dose.

Infected and HPE-treated group (GIV). Ten T. spiralis-infected mice were administered an oral dose of 5.4 mg HPE/kg b.w./day, a dosage selected based on the anti-schistosomal study by Mona et al.32.

Infected and combined therapy-treated group (GV). Ten T. spiralis-infected mice were orally administered both ABZ and HPE at the identical dosages as the preceding groups.

Treatment was administered on the 31st day post-infection (dpi) and continued for 14 days36. The animals were monitored daily throughout the experimental period for clinical signs, including changes in activity, behavior, fur condition, and posture, as well as mortality. Following overnight fasting, mice were deeply anesthetized intraperitoneally with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg b.w.), euthanized by cervical dislocation, and dissected to assess the treatment efficacy against T. spiralis muscle larvae.

Ethics approval

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and institutional regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals, which were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Science at Damanhour University in Egypt (Ethical Approval Code: DMU-SCI-CSRE-260302).

Evaluation of the treatment’s efficiency during the muscular phase

Isolation and counting of totalT. spiralis encysted larvae

The muscle larval count of infected mice was determined in the same way as that described in the muscle larval preparation method for infection34.

Biochemical analyses

Measurement of lipid peroxidation and antioxidant markers

A skeletal muscular tissue sample was excised, washed with ice-cold saline, finely chopped, and weighed. It was then homogenized in 0.1 M Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 7.4; 1:10 w/v). The homogenate was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was preserved at − 80 °C for further analyses. The lipid peroxidation end product and antioxidant biomarkers were measured using Biodiagnostic Company kits: malondialdehyde (MDA; catalog number MD 25 29) and antioxidant biomarkers [reduced glutathione (GSH; catalog number GR 25 11) & catalase (CAT; catalog number CA 25 17)]. MDA level was assessed by the thiobarbituric acid colorimetric assay37 (nmol/g tissue); GSH level was determined using the 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) colorimetric methodology of Beutler et al.38 (mg/g tissue); and CAT activity was measured according to Aebi’s method39 based on residual hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) detection (U/g tissue).

Determination of enzyme activities in blood serum

Blood samples were obtained via heart puncture into sterile tubes, allowed to clot at ambient temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The obtained sera were retained at − 20 °C until biochemical analyses. Serum activities of alanine aminotransferase (ALT; catalog number 265 001) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST; catalog number 291 000) were determined using Spectrum Diagnostics Company kits (Cairo, Egypt) according to the kinetic method of Young40. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP; catalog number AP 10 20) was determined using a Biodiagnostic Company kit (Giza, Egypt) following Belfield and Goldberg41, while the activities of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; catalog number 13201) and creatine kinase (CK; catalog number 11302) were assayed using Vitro Scient Company kits (Industrial area, Basatin Al Ismailia, Egypt) according to the methods of Moss et al.42 and Young40, respectively. All enzyme activities were expressed as U/L.

Histological study

Samples of thigh and diaphragm muscles (1–2 cm) were preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin overnight, then dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded in molten paraffin wax at 58 °C. Serial 4 μm sections were prepared, mounted, deparaffinized, and eventually stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Slides were examined using a light microscope (Olympus CX31, Tokyo, Japan) and then photographed to evaluate histological changes43.

Immunohistochemical study

Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and VEGF expressions in thigh and diaphragm muscle tissues were assessed using the methodology of Hsu et al.44. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 5 min. Sections were treated overnight at 37 °C with mouse anti-COX-2 monoclonal antibody (1:50; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., USA; catalog number sc-19999) and/or rabbit anti-VEGF polyclonal antibody (1:50; Boster Biological Technology, Valley Ave, Pleasanton, CA; catalog number PA1080). After PBS washing, sections were treated with a biotinylated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody for 30 min, followed by incubation with streptavidin-conjugated peroxidase for 30 min at 37 °C. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a 3,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride solution for 5 min and counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin; positively expressed cells showed brown cytoplasmic staining. Expression levels were quantified in random non-overlapping fields using ImageJ Version 1.47 software (RRID: SCR_003070)45.

Data analysis

For each quantitative analysis, data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent biological replicates per group. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (Tukey’s HSD) test for multiple comparisons using the statistical package for the social sciences (IBM-SPSS v20) software, with statistical significance established at p ≤ 0.0546,47.

Results

Clinical observations

During the experimental period, no overt clinical signs or mortalities were observed in mice across all groups, especially in the infected, untreated group, despite the successful establishment of the infection with 200 T. spiralis larvae per mouse. All the experimental animals appeared to have normal activity with normal intake of food and water.

Parasitological findings

Count of T. spiralis larvae in the muscles of the different infected mice groups

All treated groups showed highly significant reductions (p < 0.001) in muscle larval count relative to the infected, untreated group. The combined therapy achieved the highest reduction percentage (83.33%), followed by HPE treatment (68.47%), whereas ABZ treatment showed the lowest reduction percentage (54.04%) (Fig. 2 and Table S1).

Biochemical findings

Oxidative stress and antioxidant markers in the skeletal muscular tissues of the different mice groups

Oxidative stress markers were significantly altered in the infected, untreated group compared with the control group, as evidenced by a highly significant elevation (p < 0.001) in MDA level and highly significant decreases (p ˂ 0.001) in GSH level and CAT activity relative to the control group. Treatment with either ABZ or HPE significantly ameliorated these alterations (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively), although significant differences (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively) from the control group remained. In contrast, combined therapy restored these parameters to near-normal values, showing a highly significant improvement (p < 0.001) compared with the infected, untreated group and no significant difference (p > 0.05) from the control group (Fig. 3 and Table S2).

The data are presented as the mean ± SD of three independent biological replicates per group. Different letters (a, b, c, and d) demonstrate significant differences (p ≤ 0.05, one-way ANOVA).

Enzyme activities in the blood serum of the different mice groups

Serum enzyme activities were markedly altered following infection. The infected, untreated group showed highly significant elevations (p < 0.001) in ALT, AST, ALP, LDH, and CK activities relative to the control group. Treatment with ABZ, HPE, or their combination significantly ameliorated these alterations, resulting in highly significant reductions (p ≤ 0.001) in all measured enzyme activities relative to the infected, untreated group. Among the treated groups, combined therapy produced the greatest improvement with highly significant decreases (p ˂ 0.001) in all studied enzymes relative to the infected, untreated group, restoring their activities to levels that did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) from those of the control group. In contrast, ABZ- and HPE-treated groups still exhibited significant increases (p ˂ 0.01 and p ˂ 0.001) in some enzyme activities compared with the control group (Fig. 4 and Table S3).

Histological findings

Skeletal muscle observations of the different mice groups

Histological examination of the skeletal muscles revealed normal muscular architecture in the control group. In contrast, the infected, untreated group showed markedly degenerated skeletal muscle fibers with hyaline degeneration in some sections and numerous encysted T. spiralis larvae associated with severe inflammatory cell infiltrations around the collagen capsules and between the necrotic muscle fibers. Treatment with ABZ or HPE, alone or in combination, ameliorated these alterations to varying degrees. Treatment with ABZ reduced the parasite burden; however, a number of larval capsules and inflammatory changes persisted. HPE treatment improved muscle architecture and reduced inflammation. The combined therapy produced the most pronounced histopathological improvement, resulting in a nearly normal muscle architecture with a minimal level of inflammation between the muscle fibers, in addition to degeneration of larval capsules (Fig. 5).

Immunohistochemical findings

Cyclooxygenase-2 immune expressions in the skeletal and diaphragm muscle sections

COX-2 immunohistochemical expression was highly significantly elevated (p < 0.001) in both skeletal and diaphragm muscles of the infected, untreated group compared with the control group. Treatment with ABZ reduced COX-2 expression in both muscle types; however, levels remained significantly higher than those in the control group (p ≤ 0.001) and did not differ significantly from those in the infected, untreated group (p > 0.05). HPE treatment significantly decreased COX-2 expression compared with the infected, untreated group (p < 0.01), with expression levels approaching those of the control group, with a significant increase (p < 0.05) in the skeletal muscles and a non-significant increase (p > 0.05) in the diaphragmatic muscles. The combined therapy produced the greatest reduction in COX-2 expression in both muscle types, showing highly significant decreases (p < 0.001) compared with the infected, untreated group and no significant differences (p > 0.05) from the control group (Figs. 7 and 8 and Table S4).

Discussion

Adverse effects and emerging drug resistance remain major limitations of current anti-Trichinella therapies48, emphasizing the need for alternative treatment approaches. Previous studies have reported anti-Trichinella larval activity of various plant-derived compounds7,49,50; however, the potential effects of marine invertebrates on encysted larvae have not yet been explored. Therefore, this study evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of H. polii ethanol extract against the muscular phase of T. spiralis infection and its potential adjunctive effect with ABZ. Male mice were used to minimize sex-related hormonal and immunological variability that could influence infection progression and treatment response. This approach is supported by Hasby et al.51, who demonstrated that hormonal modulation significantly affected parasite burden and host immune responses in experimental T. spiralis infection.

The parasitological findings confirmed an adequate infection level, consistent with Albogami52, allowing a reliable evaluation of therapeutic efficacy. All treated groups showed significant reductions in muscle larval counts compared with the infected, untreated group. The combined therapy achieved the highest larval reduction (83.33%), followed by HPE (68.47%) and ABZ (54.04%). The lower efficacy of ABZ against encysted larvae agrees with Hamed et al.’s results53 (56.49%) and is attributed to its poor water solubility and low bioavailability54. Notably, the combined therapy demonstrated strong larvicidal activity (83.33%), close to that reported by El-Saidy et al.26 against adult worms (96.76%), indicating effectiveness against both adult and larval stages of T. spiralis. The antiparasitic effect of HPE is likely related to its rich content of bioactive compounds, particularly triterpene glycosides and other constituents previously reported to possess anthelmintic activity26,28,32,55.

During T. spiralis infection, excessive ROS and free radical production induce lipid peroxidation, reflected by elevated MDA levels and depletion of antioxidant defenses such as GSH level and CAT activity, which normally scavenge free radicals and prevent membrane damage13,56,57. In the current work, infected, untreated mice exhibited significantly increased MDA levels and decreased GSH levels and CAT activities in skeletal muscles compared with control mice, indicating oxidative imbalance consistent with previous findings13,58, likely due to antioxidant exhaustion during ROS scavenging59. Treatment with either ABZ or HPE alone, and more markedly their combination, significantly improved antioxidant status and reduced lipid peroxidation versus infected, untreated mice; however, only combined therapy restored values close to control levels, indicating stronger attenuation of oxidative stress. This improvement is attributed to reduced larval burden and restoration of redox balance, as reported by Albogami52, in addition to the antioxidant phytochemicals in HPE (saponins, phenolics, alkaloids, and flavonoids) and their synergism with ABZ26. These phytochemicals exert free radical scavenging activity and inhibit lipid oxidation60–62.

T. spiralis infection induces liver damage as a secondary effect of the host’s immune response to migrating larvae and infection-related oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, leading to hepatic lesions and dysfunction63. Muscle damage mainly results from direct larval invasion and encystment within muscle fibers8. Serum liver and muscle enzymes are widely used to evaluate infection severity and treatment efficacy during the muscular phase64. ALT is a specific marker of hepatocellular injury, whereas AST reflects both hepatic and muscular damage due to its high activity in these tissues65,66. ALP indicates plasma membrane integrity, and its altered activity level reflects membrane damage67. Additionally, elevated serum LDH and CK activities are indicators of muscle injury, with CK being closely associated with skeletal muscle destruction caused by Trichinella larval invasion68,69.

The present study demonstrated highly significant elevations in serum ALT, AST, ALP, LDH, and CK activities in infected, untreated mice relative to control mice, indicating hepatic and muscular damage induced by T. spiralis infection. These findings align with previous studies reporting elevated ALT, AST, LDH, and CK activity levels following T. spiralis infection64,66,70, which were associated with larval burden and parasite-induced tissue injury, as enzyme elevations were directly proportional to the number of inoculated larvae71. Treatment with either ABZ or HPE, alone or in combination, significantly reduced the activities of all investigated serum enzymes relative to infected, untreated mice, with the combined therapy showing the strongest normalization effect. The hepatoprotective and myoprotective effects of HPE may be linked to its antioxidant saponins and polyphenols. This is supported by studies showing improvements in liver function markers, ALT, AST, and ALP, following treatment with saponins extracted from Panax japonica and Astragalus72,73, while polyphenols may account for decreases in serum LDH and CK activities74. Additionally, Shalaby et al.75 reported reduced serum activity levels of AST and CK following treatment with Balanites aegyptiaca extract, likely through antioxidant-mediated protection against T. spiralis-induced tissue damage.

Histopathological examination of skeletal and diaphragmatic muscles in infected, untreated mice revealed severe architectural disruption, with many T. spiralis encysted larvae, accompanied by marked inflammatory infiltrations around the capsules and between degenerated muscle fibers, consistent with previous reports of Elmehy et al.14 and El-Wakil et al.49. Larval-induced ROS production and collagen capsule formation contribute to persistent inflammation and myocyte damage76,77. ABZ treatment reduced larval burden, as evidenced by fewer encysted larvae in both muscle tissues than those observed in the infected, untreated mice; however, partially degenerated capsules with inflammation and muscle fiber separation persisted, likely due to poor solubility and limited bioavailability of ABZ78. In contrast, HPE improved muscle architecture with mild inflammation, while combined therapy showed the most pronounced effect, preserving muscular tissue architecture with degenerated larval capsules and minimal inflammatory infiltrates, attributed to the anti-inflammatory capacity of HPE31, linked to its anti-inflammatory constituents, including saponins, phenolics, alkaloids, and flavonoids79–82.

COX-2 is an inducible pro-inflammatory enzyme upregulated by cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-1, and interleukin-17, resulting in increased prostaglandin E₂ synthesis and inflammation83–85. T. spiralis larvae induce angiogenesis around nurse cells, which is primarily associated with upregulation of VEGF, facilitating nutrient delivery and waste removal needed for larval survival9. In the current study, infected, untreated mice showed a significant increase in COX-2 and VEGF cytoplasmic expressions in both skeletal and diaphragmatic muscles, mainly within inflammatory cells between muscle fibers and around nurse cells, compared with control mice. These findings are consistent with the results of El-Aswad et al.86, who reported a strong positive expression of COX-2 in the skeletal muscles of T. spiralis-infected mice at the 35th dpi, and Elgendy et al.58, who reported a strong positive expression of VEGF in the T. spiralis-infected group. On the other hand, treatment with ABZ resulted in moderate positive COX-2 expression levels, while HPE treatment showed moderate to mild positive COX-2 and VEGF expression levels in sections of skeletal and diaphragmatic muscle fibers, respectively. Notably, combined therapy markedly reduced COX-2 and VEGF expression levels to near-control expression levels, with weak positive expression levels in sections of both skeletal and diaphragmatic muscles, showing no significant differences from control mice. The reduction in COX-2 expression indicates effective suppression of muscle inflammation, consistent with Lu et al.87, who reported that COX-2 upregulation is associated with inflammation, whereas its reduction reflects attenuation of inflammatory processes within the muscles. The observed reductions in VEGF expression levels in treated groups suggest inhibition of angiogenesis, which may compromise larval survival by limiting access to nutrients and impairing waste disposal34. Anti-angiogenic action is likely owing to the high saponin content of HPE, as saponins are known to suppress angiogenesis88. Although the present study demonstrated significant modulation of inflammatory and angiogenic markers by HPE, the underlying molecular pathways responsible for these effects were not fully elucidated and warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

HPE exhibited an anti-trichinellosis effect by reducing T. spiralis larval burden and ameliorating oxidative stress in infected tissues. It improved antioxidant status and normalized serum activity levels of liver and muscle enzymes. Histopathological and immunohistochemical findings revealed reduced tissue damage and downregulation of inflammatory and angiogenic marker expressions. The combined treatment achieved the highest efficacy, supporting HPE as a potential complementary treatment to ABZ against trichinellosis. However, further studies are recommended to purify the bioactive constituents of HPE and to elucidate their molecular mechanisms and their potential efficacy in clinical applications against trichinellosis.
