A Deep Learning Model Detects Glaucoma Based on an OCT Report, but Where Should the Clinician Look to Identify Glaucomatous Damage?
Authors: Donald C. Hood, Wai Tak Lau, Arin L. Stowman, Tayna Gebhardt, Grace Mao, Sol La Bruna, Ari Leshno, Emmanouil Tsamis, Kaveri Thakoor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess if the heat maps from a deep learning model (DLM) can guide clinicians to relevant locations on an optical coherence tomography (OCT) glaucoma report.MethodsGrad-CAM class activation (“heat”) maps were produced by a DLM, with an OCT glaucoma report as input. The reports were generated for 183 eyes/individuals with optic neuropathy consistent with glaucoma, as indicated by arcuate defects ranging from subtle to advanced. The number of eyes in which a particular element of the report had the hottest region and the region that reached a criterion level were tabulated. A theoretical framework was employed to assess if these locations were clinically relevant. This framework employs two prototypical arcuate patterns of damage, one for each hemiretina.ResultsIn 86.3% of the 183 eyes, the hottest spot appeared in one of the four thickness or deviation maps that have been used for published OCT grading criteria. These same maps accounted for 81.2% of all the hot spots reaching a criterion level. While the hot spots typically overlapped the predicted prototype regions, they were often misleading. For example, 57% of the 183 eyes had the hottest spot, or one or more hot spots, located largely outside the prototypical regions.ConclusionsAlthough the DLM heat maps are flagging clinically meaningful elements of the report, in their existing form, they are not optimal for guiding the clinician to relevant locations of damage.Translational RelevanceThe DLM can tag eyes with glaucomatous damage, and the prototypical patterns overlaid on the reports might aid the clinician in identifying and understanding this damage.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been proposed as a potential screening tool for glaucomatous damage.1–8 In the companion article by Lau et al.,9 we developed a deep learning model (DLM) that uses an optical coherence tomography (OCT) glaucoma report as input that demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity and outperformed conventional OCT metrics. While the DLM shows promise as a screening tool, it is unclear how best to utilize it in a clinical setting, where the goal is to aid clinicians diagnosing glaucoma.

To illustrate the problem, Figure 1A presents the OCT report for an eye that our DLM rated 100, the highest score on a scale of 100 (glaucoma [G]) to 0 (healthy [H]). While the score alone may be sufficient for screening purposes and/or referring the patient for further testing, the treating clinician requires additional information to determine the degree to which there is damage consistent with glaucoma. A common clinical method for this purpose looks primarily at the quadrant and clock hour charts, which are the enlarged insets at the bottom of Figure 1A. For this eye, these charts are all green, indicating that the OCT thickness measures are all greater than the fifth percentile for healthy controls of the same age as the patient. As previously demonstrated,10,11 relying solely on these charts would overlook the arcuate damage present in this eye. In this study, we address the following question: can the DLM provide guidance to the clinician by highlighting the abnormal regions in the eye?

The heat maps are on a white to black scale, with black being the “hottest.” (C) An OCT report for one of the 183 “pattern ON-G” with a subtle arcuate defect. (D) An OCT report for one of the 183 “pattern ON-G” with severe glaucomatous damage affecting both hemispheres and the central macular region.

One potential approach is the so-called heat map. Figure 1B shows a gradient-weighted class activation map (Grad-CAM),12 referred to as a “heat map” here, superimposed over the report in panel A. The heat map shows the most heavily weighted regions of an image for a particular label of a DLM. In Figure 1B, the clouds show these regions on a white-to-black scale, where black indicates the “hottest,” or most heavily weighted location, for a decision of glaucoma.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the utility of heat maps in guiding clinicians to relevant locations on an OCT glaucoma report.

Methods

DLM

As detailed in the companion article by Lau et al.,9 we fine-tuned an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 model9 to classify H versus G eyes based on widefield OCT reports (Fig. 1A). In particular, the DLM was trained on OCT reports (Fig. 1A) from 4932 healthy reports and 207 reports from eyes with clear damage consistent with glaucoma.9 The training used only the OCT reports.9 As a clinical diagnosis was not available, these eyes were labeled ON-G for optic neuropathy consistent with glaucoma using a reading center approach.13 All 5139 (4932 H + 207 G) eyes from 5139 individuals were scanned as part of refractive and screening care at seven optometry practices.13

The Pattern ON-G Cohort of 183 Eyes

The DLM was tested with three independent sets, including a cohort, called “pattern ON-G,” which is the focus of this study. This cohort included 183 eyes/individuals 60 years of age and older with glaucoma-like arcuate defects that ranged from very subtle defects likely to be missed on clinical exam and 24-2 visual fields (Fig. 1C) to damage affecting both hemispheres and the central macula (Fig. 1D).11 Using commercial software, OCT reports14,15 (aka Hood reports) (Fig. 1A) were generated for 183 eyes/individuals. These reports were generated from widefield (12 × 9 mm) OCT scans of the Maestro 2 instrument (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan), and they served as the input to the DLM. As indicated in Lau et al.,9 the DLM grade for each of the 183 eyes is the average of the outputs of five versions of the DLM, trained on different subsets of the data available for training.

Grad-CAM Maps

For each of the 183 eyes, Grad-CAM class activation (“heat”) maps (Fig. 1B) were produced by the DLM. Dark black is the “hottest” region (i.e., a region of highest weight for the DLM's decision). To assess the elements of the report most heavily weighted by the DLM, two measures were obtained: the number of the 183 eyes in which the element had a cloud and the number of eyes in which the element had the hottest spot.

Figure 2A shows the heat map for the eye in Figure 1. A customized R-program16 marked the hottest spot on the heat map, as shown by the red circle in panel A. This location is shown as the black circle on the report in panel B. The “hottest spot” is easy to determine and interpret, as it is the location with the highest weight. On the other hand, the location of the border of each cloud depends on the criterion value used to define the separation of gray and white. Setting the optimal criterion involves a trade-off between having relatively local clouds for identifying the location of the damage, on one hand, and the number of clouds, on the other. Supplementary Fig. S1 shows this trade-off for the seven eyes in Figures 2 to 6. The border for the chosen criterion level of gray is shown in red in Figure 2A and superimposed on the report in Figure 2B, as the black borders. Changing the criterion level did not affect the general conclusions.

The Prototypical Patterns of Glaucomatous Damage

As previously mentioned, our objective was to assess the efficacy of heat maps in guiding clinicians to relevant locations on an OCT glaucoma report. Consequently, we require a methodology for defining “relevant locations.” To this end, we employed two prototypical patterns, one for superior and the other for inferior, retinal glaucomatous damage, as depicted in Figure 4.

(A) The arcuate prototype for the inferior retina is superimposed on the report for eye B. The black and white arrows point to regions within the red borders of the prototype arcuate consistent with a glaucomatous arcuate defect. The gray arrows point to the same physical locations associated with the IVZ of the cpRNFL thickness. (B) The arcuate prototype for the superior retina is superimposed on the report for eye C.

The theoretical and empirical foundations of these prototypical patterns can be found in Hood17 and Hood et al.18 Briefly, the patterns in Figure 4 were established based on the following three principles: one, glaucomatous damage at or in the optic disc causes arcuate-like thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), and the associated regions of the ganglion cell plus inner plexiform layer (GCL+). The arcuate damaged regions depicted in Figure 4 were obtained from retinal nerve fiber bundle tracings, as previously described.17 They are shown with a red border on the RNFL thickness and on the probability/deviation maps (p-maps). For the p-maps, they are rotated around the horizontal meridian to be in field view (i.e., the upper hemifield is now the inferior retina). Two, the same physical locations on the retina should exhibit agreement. For example, in Figure 4A, the region labeled IVZ (inferior vulnerability zone) on the circumpapillary RNFL (cpRNFL) thickness curve is the same physical location as the region shown by the gray arc (gray arrow) on the insets for the RNFL p-map (upper right panel) and RNFL thickness map (lower panel). Likewise, in Figure 4B, the same is true for the region labeled SVZ (superior vulnerability zone) on the cpRNFL thickness curve. Three, and most importantly, there should be topographical agreement between corresponding locations on the GCL+ and RNFL thickness and p-maps. The most compelling evidence of ON-G is an arcuate confirmed on both RNFL and GCL+ maps.10,17 To make it easier to appreciate this agreement between RNFL and GCL+ maps, a schematic of the six sectors of the thick portion of the macular region (±10°) is superimposed upon the GCL+ and RNFL maps in Figure 4 as the light gray circle with six sectors.18

Notice the agreement with the prototype in both panels of Figure 4. There is thinning in the predicted regions within the red borders on the RNFL and GCL+ thickness maps, as well as abnormal (red) areas in the predicted regions within the red borders of the RNFL and GCL+ p-maps.

Results

Elements of the OCT Report and the Heat Maps

The Grad-CAM class activation (“heat”) maps provide an indication of the weighting of a particular report element for the DLM's G (glaucoma) classification. Figure 1B shows the heat maps superimposed on the OCT report for one individual. First, the hottest (blackest) regions fall within elements of the report—in this case, within the GCL+ thickness map, the GCL+ p-map, and the RNFL p-map. Second, the gray-black clouds also fall largely within the elements of the report. Third, and most importantly, these clouds do not provide a useful guide to where the clinician should focus their attention, except to emphasize the relative importance in this case of three of the eight elements. However, for these three elements, the clouds cover nearly the entire region of these maps. To compare the heat maps to the prototype patterns, we need more local measures of the DLM's “attention” (i.e., weighting). In the Methods, we described two measures: the location of the hottest spot and the location of a cloud of a criterion gray value.

For each of the eight elements of the report in Figure 3, the number of the 183 reports that had the hottest spot in that element is shown in the first column of data in the Table. For 71 (38.8%) of the 183 reports, the GCL+ thickness map had the “hottest” spot, while for 41 (22.4%) and 37 (20.2%) of the reports, the RNFL thickness and p-maps had the hottest spot. Interestingly, at least one of the two RNFL maps (Fig. 3, elements c and e) or one of the two GCL+ maps (Fig. 3, elements g and h) had the hottest spot in 86.3% of the eyes. On the other hand, the en face map (Fig. 3, element d) accounted for only 6% of the hottest spot, and the circumpapillary B-scan (Fig. 3, element a) and pie charts (Fig. 3, element f) were not the hottest location for any eye.

A similar pattern emerges if, for each element of the report, we take the total number of the 183 reports with a cloud containing a region above the criterion level (second column of data in the Table). The GCL thickness map again had the most total eyes meeting the criterion level. Further, the four RNFL and GCL thickness and p-maps account for over 81.2% of the total clouds reaching the criterion. On the other hand, the B-scan reached the criterion level in only one eye, while the pie charts did not reach the criterion in any of the eyes. Lowering or increasing the criterion level changed the number of clouds reaching the criterion, but it did not change the relative order of element weighting.

A Comparison to Prototypical Arcuate Patterns of Damage

Although the heat maps suggest that the DLM is most heavily weighting the clinically important elements of the report, it does not mean that these heat maps can assist clinicians in identifying features of glaucomatous damage beyond the general statement to look at these elements. To determine if the hot spots and clouds in the heat maps indicate “locations of clinical interest,” we developed the prototypical arcuate patterns of superior and inferior glaucomatous damage, described in the Methods and Figure 4.

To understand the weaknesses of the heat maps, we first need to consider how the clinician should use the prototypes. The clinician should be looking for evidence of arcuate damage within the red borders of six of the eight elements, elements a, b, c, e, g, and h in Figure 3. The black and white arrows in Figure 4A point to regions within the red borders of the prototype arcuate consistent with a glaucomatous arcuate defect. The cpRNFL and B-scan show thinned regions (arrows 1 and 2), the RNFL thickness map (arrow 3) and RNFL p-map (arrow 4) show arcuate-like changes, and the GCL+ thickness map (arrow 5) and GCL+ p-map (arrow 6) show macular GCL+ changes consistent with these arcuate-like changes. Overall, the agreement with the prototype is consistent with damage originating at or in the disc in the IVZ. This is a summary of what a clinician should attend to. A similar analysis is shown for the SVZ prototype in Figure 4B. Now consider what is available with the heat map.

Figure 5 contains the same reports for the two examples in Figure 4, but with the hottest spot (black circle) and cloud borders (black contours) superimposed. In Figure 5A (left panel), there are three clouds (gray arrows), and they, along with the hottest spot (black arrow), overlap or fall within the red borders of the prototype, as shown in the right panel. In this case, the heat map is consistent with the prototype model, although it is less informative in two ways. First, not all regions of interest have clouds, and for the ones that do have clouds, these clouds often cover areas within and outside the regions of interest. The same is true for the example in Figure 5B, where there are five clouds that overlap or fall within the regions of interest on the right panel.

All 183 eyes had one or more clouds overlapping a region of interest, although in many cases, the black spot and much of the associated cloud fell outside the region of the arcuate, as is the case in Figure 5B (gray arrow with asterisk). In general, while the clouds typically overlapped the predicted regions of arcuate damage, they were often imprecise and/or misleading. For example, 105 (57%) of the 183 eyes had either the hottest spot and/or one or more of the clouds located largely outside the locations of interest of the prototypes. Discussion

Our purpose in this study was to assess whether the heat maps from a DLM can be used to guide clinicians to relevant locations on an OCT glaucoma report. Before summarizing our findings, it is important to acknowledge that the DLM was trained to distinguish between OCT reports with and without changes consistent with glaucoma.9 It was not trained to identify the same patterns or employ the same method a human expert would use. In any case, there are two key aspects of our findings.

First, despite the caveat above, there are some noteworthy similarities between the elements of the OCT report most involved in the DLM labels of glaucoma versus healthy and the elements used by OCT experts reading these reports. The GCL+ and RNFL p-map, as well as the RNFL and GCL+ thickness maps, had the hottest spot on the report in 86% of the 183 ON-G reports (Table). These four maps are of fundamental importance in two reading center methods used to identify glaucoma with OCT.10,18,19 Further, there is agreement with a recent logistic regression model (LRM).11 This LRM has six metrics, of which three involve local regions of the GCL+ thickness map, while a fourth is a combination of the global GCL+ thickness plus the cpRNFL thickness. Thus, the LRM puts disproportional weight on the GCL+ thickness measures. Likewise, the DLM hotspots put disproportional weight on the GCL+ thickness map. The hottest spot was most commonly seen on the GCL+ thickness map (Table), and the two GCL+ maps (i.e., thickness and p-maps) had 44% of the hottest spots. On the other hand, the pie charts (element f in Fig. 3), which are ignored by both the LRM and the reading center approach, did not have a single cloud on the heat maps.

In any case, our second key finding suggests the heat maps associated with our DLM will not be optimal as an aid for the clinician. First, in most of the 183 reports, either the hottest spot and/or one or more of the clouds was largely located outside of the locations of interest of the prototypes. In some other cases, the cloud covered normal and abnormal regions and was thus ambiguous. Thus, it is difficult to see how the clinician could use these particular heat maps to distinguish glaucomatous patterns from artifacts.

As an alternative to, or in conjunction with, these heat maps, we propose using the prototypes to assist the clinician in confirming and understanding the glaucomatous damage in this eye. For this purpose, the prototype for glaucomatous damage in each hemiretina is superimposed on the report, as seen in Figures 4 to 6. The clinician is instructed to look for thinning in the regions within the red borders on the RNFL thickness and p-maps, as well as for corresponding changes within the red sectors of the GCL+ thickness and p-maps. Further, for quantitative evidence, we could even add the information from the six parameters in our LRM index.4

Future and parallel efforts to align DLM heat maps with expert regions of importance by leveraging ophthalmologist eye movements on OCT reports are ongoing and show the potential to enhance the utility of resulting DLM attention/heat maps and overall DLM glaucoma detection accuracy.20,21 Efforts to generate text-based summaries side-by-side with heat maps using large language models and expert dictations may further enhance the assistive value of DLM heat maps.22,23

Limitations and Caveats

There are two general limitations. First, we need to be clear that we are not rejecting the possibility of using heat maps to aid the clinician. Our DLM was not designed to mimic a human expert. However, we intend to explore the development of a DLM that produces heat map hotspots more aligned with the prototypes. Second, our prototype method needs to be tested in a real-world clinical setting.

Conclusions

Although the DLM heat map clouds are associated with clinically meaningful elements of the report, the current heat maps are not optimal for guiding the clinician to the relevant locations. However, overlaying the prototypical schematics of arcuate defects on reports identified as “glaucoma” by the DLM should be of help to clinicians.
