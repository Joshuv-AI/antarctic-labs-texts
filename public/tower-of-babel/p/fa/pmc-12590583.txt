Association between sleep duration, sleep apnea risk, and osteoporosis in older adults: a cross-sectional study in elder care institutions in Taiwan
Authors: Hong-Jhe Chen, Hsun Ou, Ru-Yih Chen, Yu-En Kuo, Bo-Yu Wong, Terry BJ Kuo, Cheryl CH Yang, Tien-Yu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the relationship between sleep disturbance, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and osteoporosis in older adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 326 older adults (mean age: 83.46 years) in elder care institutions in Taiwan. The presence of osteoporosis was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, while sleep quality and OSA risk were evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and STOP-Bang questionnaire, respectively. Heart rate variability (HRV) was measured to assess autonomic function. Logistic regression models, adjusted for age, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities, were used to examine the associations among sleep duration, OSA risk, and osteoporosis.ResultsOsteoporosis was more prevalent in females and those with lower BMI and neck circumference, and less common in individuals with knee osteoarthritis. Intermediate-to-high OSA risk was associated with the male sex, higher BMI, higher neck circumference, and cardiovascular comorbidities. Short sleep duration (< 4 h) was initially correlated with a higher osteoporosis risk, but this association disappeared after adjusting for covariates. However, after adjusting for covariates, intermediate-to-high OSA risk was significantly associated with osteoporosis. HRV showed no significant difference in assessing osteoporosis or OSA risk in this study.ConclusionShort sleep duration (< 4 h) and intermediate-to-high OSA risk were associated with a higher prevalence of osteoporosis among older adult participants, compared to those with 6–7 h of sleep or low OSA risk. Further research is required to explore the relationship between sleep and osteoporosis in older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06527-y.Keywords: Sleep duration, Sleep apnea, Osteoporosis, Older adults, Heart rate variability

Background

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal pathology characterized by reduced bone density, deteriorated bone quality, and compromised bone strength. Patients with osteoporosis are typically asymptomatic until fractures occur [1]. Recent analysis of nationwide data in Taiwan shows that osteoporosis prevalence rose from approximately 17.4% in 2001 to 25.0% in 2011 [2]. This notable increase underscores the growing osteoporosis burden amidst Taiwan’s aging population. However, the prevalence among institutionalized older adults or residents of long-term care facilities likely exceeds this national average, due to higher rates of comorbidities, malnutrition, and reduced mobility. Highlighting the need for targeted investigations in elder care institutions.

The standard approach for evaluating osteoporosis involves measuring bone mineral density (BMD) using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Various predictive factors have been reported for osteoporosis, including advanced age, female sex, previous fragility fractures, prolonged use of glucocorticoids, low body mass index (BMI), family history of hip fractures, cigarette smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption [1].

Complex and inconsistent results have been reported regarding the association between sleep duration and osteoporosis. Some studies have suggested that prolonged sleep may increase the risk of osteoporosis [3, 4], whereas others have proposed that insufficient sleep may pose the same risk [5, 6]. Further investigation into the relationship between sleep duration and osteoporosis, potentially incorporating objective physiological indicators such as heart rate variability (HRV), is warranted [7].

Assessment of sleep quality considers not only sleep duration but also obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), a significant and often underestimated sleep disorder [8]. According to our previous research, among participants in Taiwan aged 55 years on average, nearly 50% were found to have OSA based on home sleep apnea test results [9]. OSA is not only associated with daytime sleepiness and cognitive decline, but also contributes to systemic inflammation and cardiometabolic risk. A recent meta-analysis has further linked OSA with osteoporosis, highlighting its importance in geriatric health assessments [10]. Nevertheless, in elder care institutions, OSA screening is rarely implemented.

This study specifically focuses on older adults in elder care institutions in Taiwan, a demographic characterized by unique physiological and behavioral changes that may influence the associations between sleep and bone health. Advanced age is commonly accompanied by reduced sleep efficiency, greater susceptibility to OSA, and alterations in bone metabolism, all of which may interact in complex ways to affect osteoporosis risk. Furthermore, older individuals often present with distinct comorbidity profiles and lifestyle patterns that may confound relationships commonly observed in younger populations. By investigating this population, our findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of sleep–bone interactions in late life. These insights may ultimately inform more personalized clinical recommendations and preventive strategies tailored to the specific needs of the older population.

Methods

Data source and eligibility criteria

This cross-sectional study included older adults who received services from elder care institutions and were screened for the presence of osteoporosis. The inclusion criteria were individuals aged 50 years or older, associated with elder care institutions in Taiwan (including community-based elderly care centers and long-term care facilities such as veterans’ homes), who were conscious, able to communicate, and capable of providing informed consent. Although a very small number of participants aged 50–64 years were recruited, they were excluded from the final analytic sample because of their limited representation and the study’s primary focus on geriatric populations. Therefore, the final analysis was restricted to participants aged ≥ 65 years. The exclusion criteria included participation in other clinical interventional studies, a life expectancy of less than one year as determined by a physician, inability to undergo assessment as evaluated by the coordinator, and refusal to participate in the study. Older adults were examined using DXA equipment in a mobile smart vehicle between March and September 2020. Additionally, a questionnaire was used to obtain sleep, mood, and resting HRV data. Data collection commenced after obtaining informed consent, and all participants’ names and personal identifiers were excluded to adhere with ethical research standards. Initially, 357 participants and their original data were included. After excluding 31 participants with missing data, 326 were included in the final analysis. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (KSVGH20-CT3–01).

Clinical assessment

A structured self-administered questionnaire was used to collect participant data, including demographics, lifestyle practices, and personal and family medical histories. The full questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary File. The basic demographic data included sex, age, height, weight, and BMI. Personal medical history included thyroid diseases, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, stroke, peptic ulcer disease, gout, knee osteoarthritis, psychotic disorders, and other diseases. This study integrated collected data with BMD assessments to predict the risk of major osteoporotic and hip fractures over 10 years. This prediction was made using the Fracture Risk Assessment Tool [11]. Additionally, staff and volunteers assisted participants from these elder care institutions in completing relevant questionnaires. Most residents of these elder care institutions had retained their cognitive abilities.

DXA (Horizon; Hologic, Marlborough, MA, USA) equipment mounted in an intelligent vehicle was employed to measure BMD at the hip and lumbar spine. Osteopenia and osteoporosis were diagnosed based on T-scores. T-scores between − 1 and − 2.4 indicated osteopenia, while T-scores of − 2.5 or less indicated osteoporosis. This diagnostic approach was in accordance with the World Health Organization guidelines [12]. All scanning procedures were performed by a certified bone densitometrist.

Sleep condition was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) for sleep quality and the STOP-Bang questionnaire for OSA. The PSQI is a self-report questionnaire that evaluates sleep quality and disturbances within 1 month [13], comprising 19 items divided into seven key components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Total scores were obtained by summing the scores of these seven components. Generally, 5 points were used as the cutoff for sleep quality [14].

The STOP-Bang comprises eight dichotomous (yes/no) questions pertaining to the clinical characteristics associated with OSA, including snoring, tiredness, observed apnea, blood pressure, BMI, age, neck circumference, and sex. The scores on the scale range from 0 to 8, with scores of 0–2, 3–4, and 5–8 representing low-, intermediate-, and high-risk OSA, respectively. The STOP-Bang questionnaire is highly sensitive for detecting moderate-to-severe OSA (score ≥ 3) and serves as a quick and reliable screening tool for OSA [15].

HRV was assessed using a handheld ECG device (WG-101; Wegene, New Taipei, Taiwan) during a 5-minute resting period. Key metrics included R–R intervals, power spectral density for frequency bands as low frequency (LF) (0.04–0.15 Hz), high frequency (HF) (0.15–0.4 Hz), and the LF: HF ratio to evaluate autonomic balance. Time-domain measures and total power (TP) were also analyzed [16].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (v22.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Initially, a normality test was conducted to determine data distribution. In univariate analysis, the χ2 test and independent t-test were used for determining differences in various parameters related to osteoporosis. Subsequently, to explore the correlation between osteoporosis and sleep duration, multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to construct regression models adjusted for relevant parameters such as age, sex, and BMI, and odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Prior to regression analysis, multicollinearity among key predictors was assessed using standard collinearity diagnostics (Variance Inflation Factor [VIF], Tolerance, and Condition Index). All VIF values were below 2, and no variables demonstrated concerning collinearity based on Condition Index and variance proportion thresholds, confirming the stability and validity of the regression model [17]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relationship between sleep and osteoporosis in older adult participants with an average age of > 80 years, starting from a community-based perspective. We found that among patients diagnosed with osteoporosis, women, those with a lower BMI and neck circumference, and fewer individuals with comorbid knee osteoarthritis were more prevalent. Individuals at intermediate-to-high OSA risk were more likely to be male, have a higher BMI and neck circumference, and have comorbid conditions such as cardiovascular problems, diabetes, peptic ulcer disease, gout, and knee osteoarthritis. Furthermore, participants with short sleep durations (< 4 h) were more likely to have osteoporosis than those who slept for 6–7 h; however, this association was attenuated after adjusting for covariates such as age, BMI, and comorbidities. Similarly, the intermediate-to-high OSA risk group showed a higher prevalence of osteoporosis compared to the low-risk group after covariate adjustment.

This study has several noteworthy findings. First, we enrolled participants with an average age of >80 years, and the characteristics of the osteoporosis group were consistent with the findings of previous studies. Women are at higher risk of osteoporosis due to a lower peak bone mass compared to men and the rapid postmenopausal bone loss that occurs owing to decreased estrogen levels. Additionally, thinner individuals experience less mechanical stress on their bones, which leads to lower bone density and a higher risk of developing osteoporosis [18]. Some studies suggest an inverse correlation between osteoarthritis and osteoporosis. Osteoarthritis is associated with subchondral bone thickening and increased bone mineral density, which may protect against the development of osteoporosis [19].

Second, the demographic characteristics of the intermediate-to-high OSA risk group also fit the findings of previous literature. OSA is more prevalent in men than in women, likely because of differences in upper airway anatomy, fat distribution, and hormonal influences. Excess body weight, particularly central obesity, significantly contributes to OSA. Increased fat deposits around the neck and upper airways lead to airway narrowing, whereas visceral fat reduces lung volume and exacerbates airway collapsibility [8]. OSA causes intermittent hypoxia and sympathetic overactivity, which promotes hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular and other systemic comorbidities [8].

Third, our analysis indicated that older adults who reported sleep < 4 h per night were more likely to have osteoporosis compared to those who sleep 6–7 h per night. However, this association was not observed after adjusting for covariates such as age, BMI, and comorbidities. The association between sleep duration and osteoporosis remains unclear. Some studies, contrary to our findings, have suggested that prolonged sleep duration increases the risk of osteoporosis [3, 4]. However, we examined the patient populations in these studies and found that most recruited only middle-aged individuals or both middle-aged and older adults. Another meta-analysis suggested a U-shaped relationship, indicating that both excessively long and short sleep durations may lead to the development of osteoporosis [20]. The relationship between sleep duration and osteoporosis requires further research for validation.

Fourth, after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and comorbidities, the intermediate-to-high OSA risk group was found to have a higher prevalence of osteoporosis compared to the low OSA risk group. A population-based cohort study in Taiwan reported that individuals with OSA had a 2.74-fold higher risk of developing osteoporosis than those without OSA, after adjusting for factors such as age, sex, and comorbidities. Notably, older age and female sex further increased the risk [21]. However, a previous meta-analysis suggested that OSA is not significantly associated with an increased risk of osteoporosis. At that time, the reasoning was that OSA is often comorbid with obesity, and obesity itself is considered a protective factor against osteoporosis [22]. Nevertheless, a recent large-scale meta-analysis that reevaluated studies published in recent years concluded that OSA itself may contribute to an increased risk of osteoporosis [10]. Possible underlying mechanisms include chronic intermittent hypoxia in OSA, which may disrupt bone remodeling by promoting osteoclast activity and increasing bone resorption [23]. Additionally, OSA-induced hypoxia can trigger systemic inflammation by releasing cytokines that negatively affect the balance between bone formation and resorption [24]. Furthermore, OSA may alter hormone levels of cortisol and growth hormone, both of which are crucial for bone metabolism [25].

Fifth, HRV assessment did not reveal significant differences in evaluating osteoporosis or OSA risk in this study. Adults with obstructive sleep apnea may show reduced vagal tone and increased sympathetic responsiveness [26]. Additionally, some studies have used HRV to predict sleep quality in patients [7]. However, the absence of significant HRV findings in geriatric studies is likely multifactorial, influenced by intrinsic age-related autonomic decline, cardiovascular risk factors, and variability in physical activity during HRV assessment [27].

Given Taiwan’s rapidly aging population [28], early identification of modifiable risk factors for osteoporosis is crucial for reducing fracture risk and preserving quality of life in older adults. Our findings suggest that short sleep duration and intermediate-to-high risk of OSA are associated with a higher prevalence of osteoporosis among older adults in institutional care settings. Incorporating simple tools like the STOP-Bang questionnaire into routine geriatric assessments can facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals. In addition, non-invasive interventions, such as sleep hygiene education and minimizing nocturnal disturbances, have been shown to improve sleep quality in institutionalized older adults [29]. These approaches, combined with basic bone health strategies (e.g., fall prevention, vitamin D supplementation), may offer practical, low-cost solutions to enhance care [30]. As Taiwan’s older population continues to grow, incorporating sleep and bone health into national long-term care policies, particularly those emphasizing functional independence and preventive care, will become increasingly important.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, a cross-sectional observational design was adopted to assess sleep duration, OSA risk and osteoporosis at a single time point, hindering the ability of establish causal relationships. Second, sleep duration and OSA risk were measured using self-report questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias. However, given the advanced age of the participants, conducting overnight physiological monitoring studies is challenging. Third, several significant, unmeasured confounding factors may have affected the results. Variables that influence sleep quality, such as recent psychosocial stressors and lifestyle, were not considered in our analysis. Despite certain limitations in data collection, the methods used in this study may be unavoidable in older participants. Nevertheless, our findings in this older population can serve as valuable insights and inspire future research.

Conclusions

We found that among older adults, short sleep duration (< 4 h) and intermediate-to-high OSA risk were associated with a higher prevalence of osteoporosis compared to 6–7 h of sleep or low OSA risk. Further research is required to explore the relationship between sleep and osteoporosis in older adults.
