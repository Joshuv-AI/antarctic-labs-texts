Flow-Dependent Transient Asystole During Rapid Pneumoperitoneum Establishment Via 8-mm Trocar
Authors: Emma C Amjad, Ryan W Vandenbord, Hadi F Shaaban, Cathy V Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransient bradycardia and asystole are recognized complications of insufflation during laparoscopic surgery due to acute peritoneal stretch triggering vagal mediated reflexes. This reaction has been attributed primarily to elevated intraabdominal pressure (IAP) with less attention given to the potential role of carbon dioxide (CO2) flow rate, access modality, and port diameter. Here, we report the case of a 34-year-old male undergoing a robotic cholecystectomy due to gallstone hepatitis. After initial abdominal access was obtained using a Veress needle, pneumoperitoneum was established at a target pressure of 15 mmHg through an 8-mm trocar at a flow rate of 40 L/minute. Upon transition to trocar insufflation, the patient developed transient asystole at the time of insufflation lasting approximately 2–3 seconds, followed by sinus bradycardia. Insufflation was immediately discontinued, and glycopyrrolate and ephedrine were administered in response. The patient’s heart rate returned to his baseline shortly after deflation with the entire episode lasting less than 1 minute. Pneumoperitoneum was then re-established at a lower flow rate and pressure without recurrence of bradyarrhythmia. Although the target pressure was within standard operative guidelines, the high initial flow rate and large initial trocar size may have distended the peritoneum to a greater extent quicker, thus inducing a larger vagal response. The immediate onset, rapid reversibility with deflation, and absence of recurrence following slower insufflation support a rate-dependent stretch mechanism rather than the effect of absolute IAP alone.Keywords: Bradycardia, Insufflation, Laparoscopy, Minimally invasive surgery, Transient Asystole

INTRODUCTION

Laparoscopic surgery has become the standard approach for many common operative procedures due to its minimally invasive nature and favorable recovery, thus understanding the unique complications of these surgeries are imperative. One step unique to laparoscopy is insufflation of the abdomen. Bradycardia is a well-described complication during the insufflation phase of laparoscopic surgery.1–4 The 2018 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society (ACC/AHA/HRS) Guideline on the Evaluation and Management of Patients with Bradycardia identifies insufflation as a “critical period” for experiencing bradycardia.3 This cardiovascular response is attributed to activation of the peritoneal stretch receptors due to distension and their subsequent activation of vagally mediated reflexes.5 This response resembles the Bezold-Jarisch reflex, which is an inhibitory cardiovascular reflex in which mechanical or visceral stimulation increases parasympathetic activity and suppresses sympathetic tone, producing bradycardia, vasodilation, and hypotension.6 Most episodes are transient and self-limited, but cardiac arrest has been reported.4 In a retrospective review on 14 cases of laparoscopic cardiac arrest, 12 were associated temporally with insufflation with bradycardia preceding 9 of the 12 cases.4 Guidelines recommend maintaining intraabdominal pressure (IAP) between 10–15 mmHg while emphasizing use of the lowest effective pressures, thus minimizing changes to intrathoracic pressure and hemodynamics.7,8

Current insufflation guideline recommendations focus on target IAP values rather than the kinetics of pressure establishment or diameter of trocar access. Experimental models examining intestinal distension demonstrate that rapid (phasic) distension produces greater afferent firing compared to gradual (ramp) distension, and that neural discharge declines rapidly when the rate of stretch is reduced despite constant wall stress.9,10 These findings suggest rate of distension matters; thus, flow rate and diameter of the trocar may modulate autonomic reflex responses in addition to the target intra-abdominal pressures.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 34-year-old male with no past surgical or medical history presented for a robotic cholecystectomy following a diagnosis of gallstone hepatitis. The patient had baseline sinus bradycardia averaging in the midfifties with all other vitals within normal limits.

General anesthesia was induced with propofol, rocuronium, fentanyl, and lidocaine. Anesthesia was maintained with total intravenous anesthesia using propofol and dexmedetomidine. Hemodynamic parameters remained stable following induction and prior to pneumoperitoneum establishment. After sterile preparation, pneumoperitoneum was initially established using a Veress needle. Following this, an 8-mm trocar was inserted for continued insufflation. Upon transitioning to insufflation through the trocar at a target IAP of 15 mmHg and a flow rate of 40 L/minute, the patient’s heart rate rapidly dropped progressing to transient asystole lasting 2–3 seconds with subsequent stabilization of his heart rate in the midthirties. Notably, no hemodynamic instability occurred during initial insufflation via the Veress needle.

The abdomen was quickly deflated, and the patient was administered glycopyrrolate and ephedrine. Continuous electrocardiogram monitoring confirmed spontaneous return baseline of approximately 55 beats per minute with the entire episode lasting less than 1 minute. No chest compressions or advanced cardiac life support measures were required.

After hemodynamic stabilization, pneumoperitoneum was re-established gradually with target IAP dropped to 12 mmHg with an initial flow rate of 5 L/minute. The target IAP was elevated back to the original value of 15 mmHg and flow rate increased gradually as tolerated. The patient had no recurrence of bradyarrhythmia. The remainder of the procedure was completed without further hemodynamic instability. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on postoperative day one.

DISCUSSION

Bezold-Jarisch reflex is a well-described cardioinhibitory reflex demonstrating that abrupt mechanical or visceral stimulation can provoke increased parasympathetic and decreased sympathetic drive resulting in profound but reversible bradycardia.6 Peritoneal stretch fibers within the parietal peritoneum project to part of the spinal cord associated with mechanosensory transmission and trigger similar afferent activation with distension of the abdominal wall.5

Absolute IAP has been thoroughly discussed as a cause of bradycardia and asystole due to insufflation.7 However, pressure-mediated reductions in venous return typically develop after sustained elevation in intra-abdominal pressure rather than immediately. Elevated IAP can impair venous return by compressing the inferior vena cava and increasing intrathoracic pressure, leading to reduced preload and subsequent hypotension. This mechanism is typically progressive and dependent on sustained pressure rather than occurring instantaneously. While such hemodynamic changes are important contributors to cardiovascular instability during laparoscopy, their delayed onset contrasts with the immediate bradycardia observed in this case, further supporting a rapid, neurally mediated reflex as the primary mechanism. The immediate onset of bradycardia in this case supports a neutrally mediated vagal reflex triggered by rapid peritoneal stretch.

This case provides an internal comparison between 2 modes of insufflation within the same patient. Initial insufflation via Veress needle did not produce hemodynamic disturbance due to its inherent flow restriction. In contrast, transition to an 8 mm trocar permitted delivery of near-maximal insufflator flow, resulting in rapid peritoneal distension and triggering of vagally mediated response. While insufflators may be set to high flow rates, the actual delivered flow depends on the access device. Veress needles significantly restrict flow despite higher settings, whereas larger trocars allow delivery of flow rates approaching the programmed value. In this case, the use of an 8-mm trocar allowed the full insufflator flow rate of 40 L/minute, exceeding the commonly recommended initial rates of 10–15 L/minute.7

Experimental studies with rat jejunum models suggest the rate of mechanical distension may be equally as important. One study found that rapid stretch produces greater peak afferent firing than gradual distension, even with final wall stress being constant.10 Another study found that when the rate of stretch is reduced, despite maintained distension, neural discharge declines.9 These 2 studies combined demonstrate neurogenic adaptation and the idea that rate of distension determines the strength of afferent signal. Although derived from rat intestinal models, these findings suggest that the kinetics of stretch, not pressure alone, modulate afferent signaling intensity and provide a physiologic framework for understanding the clinical findings in this case.

Other causes of bradycardia during insufflation must also be considered like gas embolism and vascular injury. Gas embolism may present with sudden cardiovascular collapse, decreased end-tidal carbon dioxide (CO2), and hypoxia.11,12 Cardiac bradycardia or arrythmia occurs in majority of cases with cardiovascular collapse or cyanosis as the first manifestation in some patients.11 Transesophageal echocardiography is highly sensitive for detecting gas embolism, visualizing air bubbles in cardiac chambers and great vessels in real time.12 Management would be release of pneumoperitoneum, administration of 100% oxygen and positioning.12 Vascular injury during laparoscopy presents differently than gas embolism. Trocar and Veress needle injuries occur in approximately 0.18% of laparoscopic procedures, only one-third of these attributed to vessel injury.13 The presentation typically includes progressive hypotension and hemorrhage rather than immediate cardiovascular collapse.13 The mortality rate for unrecognized vascular injuries is significant, emphasizing the importance of intraoperative recognition.13 In this case, the rapid resolution of symptoms with cessation of insufflation, absence of hypoxia, and lack of ongoing instability make these diagnoses less likely and support a vagally mediated mechanism.14

Current guidelines emphasize maintaining IAP between 10–15 mmHg and using the lowest effective pressure to minimize the physiologic impact.7 However, these recommendations focus on target pressure rather than the rate of pressure establishment. Rapid increases in IAP can acutely alter preload, afterload, and autonomic tone, producing transient but significant cardiovascular perturbations.2 Experimental models of pneumoperitoneum establishment demonstrate that flow parameters and characteristics of the trocar influence intraabdominal gas dynamics.15,16 Although CO2 insufflation does not strictly conform to ideal laminar liquid flow assumptions, principles such as Poiseuille’s law illustrate how increases in port diameter reduce resistance and facilitate rapid gas delivery.

Anesthetic factors must be considered; for instance, propofol and dexmedetomidine can increase vagal tone and predispose the patient to bradycardia. However, heart rate remained stable following induction and prior to insufflation, and the arrhythmia occurred temporally with insufflation. In addition, no recurrence occurred despite continued anesthetic exposure when insufflation was performed gradually. These observations argue against a primary pharmacologic etiology and support a mechanically mediated trigger.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The generalizability of the animal mechanosensory models to human physiology is limited, and further research is needed to better define this relationship. Prospective studies evaluating controlled insufflation rates, access modalities, and patient susceptibility would provide clinically relevant insight into the best practices for pneumoperitoneum establishment.

CONCLUSION

Transient asystole and bradycardia can occur during pneumoperitoneum establishment even when target intra-abdominal pressures fall within commonly accepted ranges. In this case, the temporal relationship to high-flow insufflation through a larger-diameter trocar and the absence of recurrence during gradual re-establishment of pneumoperitoneum suggests the rate of abdominal distension plays a critical role in vagal response. These findings support that insufflation kinetics, including flow rate, access modality, and trocar diameter, in addition to pressure, may contribute to the autonomic instability seen in certain laparoscopic surgeries.
