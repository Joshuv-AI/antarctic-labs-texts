Single-Oligomer Characterization of Tau Phosphorylation and Mechanical State
Authors: Visakh V. S. Pillai, Tom Smeding, Jonathan X. Meng, David Klenerman, Francesco Simone Ruggeri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Post-translational modifications have emerged as a key biomolecular process in the onset of neurodegenerative disorders, such as the hyperphosphorylation of tau protein in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). High levels of phosphorylation are related to tau malfunction, self-assembly into amyloids forming neurofibrillary tangles in the brain, and cellular toxicity. These molecular processes are also reflected in human biofluids, allowing us to use tau phosphorylation as a biomarker of the disease onset and progression. However, it is not yet clear what structural changes the tau protein undergoes upon phosphorylation and what the early self-assembly steps are that lead to the formation of the final amyloid species. This knowledge gap is related in large part to the experimental challenge in achieving a multiparametric physical–chemical characterization of nanoscale size and heterogeneous amyloid at the single-molecule level. Here, we employ high-resolution and advanced atomic force microscopy methods to study the effect of phosphorylation on the tau pathway of self-assembly and on the physical–chemical properties of the heterogeneous amyloid species formed, down to the single-oligomer level. We report the correlative analysis of single-oligomer structural and chemical properties and achieve, for the first time, the quantitative determination of their phosphorylation state. Our findings reveal that hyperphosphorylation results in the formation of smaller, stiffer, and more adhesive oligomers, which might be critical for their pathological role in AD. This nanoresolved information might be, in turn, useful to understand the early molecular mechanisms of disease, as well as to improve the detection of pathological tau species in biofluids as diagnostic biomarkers.

Introduction

Abnormal phosphorylation of tau protein has emerged as a key pathological biomolecular process in the progression of several neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), , Pick’s disease, progressive supranuclear palsy, and corticobasal degeneration. These diseases are also known as tauopathies.

Under physiological conditions, tau plays a crucial role in stabilizing microtubules and maintaining neuronal integrity. However, in disease states, tau undergoes post-translational modification (PTM) and hyperphosphorylation at various sites, leading to conformational changes, its detachment from microtubules, and subsequent self-assembly into neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). Accordingly, in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood, it has been shown that elevated levels of phosphorylated tau (P-Tau) aggregates are considered reliable biomarkers for the early diagnosis and monitoring of the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. −

However, tau aggregates exist in multiple polymorphic forms, including soluble oligomers and insoluble fibrils, each with distinct biochemical properties and pathogenic potential. Among these, transient and soluble tau oligomers formed during early aggregation stages are considered particularly neurotoxic due to their small size and exposed hydrophobic regions. Recent studies have shown that phosphorylated tau can self-assemble into small, amorphous-like aggregates, whereas nonphosphorylated wild-type tau forms fibrils only in the presence of heparin. Functional assays have further demonstrated that phosphorylated tau exhibits higher cell toxicity; thus, these post-translational modified species may be implicated in the molecular mechanisms of disease and may serve as early biomarkers of neurodegenerative disease progression. ,

A wide range of advanced analytical techniques has been employed to explore both the structural organization and phosphorylation of tau aggregates, which are central to tauopathies. Structural characterization has been achieved via high-resolution methods, such as cryo-electron microscopy, which reveal the complex architecture of tau fibrils. However, cryo-EM only allows studying the core structure of twisted fibrils, prevents us from investigating their fuzzy coat, as well as is limited in investigating rodlike fibrils and smaller oligomeric species. Furthermore, cryo-EM does not allow the determination of the chemical state, such as PTMs and phosphorylation, of the aggregates. Chemical detection and quantification of phosphorylation events critical for understanding tau’s pathological misfolding and aggregation have been performed using immunoassays coupled with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). ,− Mass spectrometry approaches have enabled precise identification of tau isoforms and mapping of phosphorylation sites within aggregated forms, offering valuable insights into disease mechanisms. Yet, traditional bulk techniques often lack the resolution and sensitivity required to capture the heterogeneity of these early-stage species at the single-molecule level, highlighting the need for more refined, high-resolution approaches. , Overall, state-of-the-art microscopy and bulk methods used separately thus hamper the correlative characterization of the structural organization and chemical properties of single amyloidogenic oligomers.

Here, we report the first correlative detection and analysis of the morphology, stiffness, secondary structure, and phosphorylation state of single tau amyloid species, with focus on oligomers (Figure ). We studied recombinant wild-type tau (WT-Tau) isoform 0N4R versus its phosphorylated (P-Tau) counterpart (Figure a), aggregated over 120 h at 2 μM, which corresponds to the phase where both samples form first detectable aggregates, thus ensuring the comparison of similar oligomeric species. We employ bulk infrared (IR) spectroscopy (Figure b) together with atomic force microscopy (AFM) based methods (Figure c). High-resolution and phase-controlled AFM mapping allowed us to study the heterogeneity and quantify the three-dimensional (3D) morphological properties of different polymorphic amyloid species present in solution. Our recently developed acoustical-mechanically suppressed infrared nanospectroscopy (AFM-IR) approach further empowers the analysis of the phosphorylation state of amyloid species down to the single-oligomer level. Finally, peak-force quantitative nanomechanical mapping (PF-QNM) allows correlating the chemical differences to the Young’s modulus and adhesion properties of the single oligomers. Our findings reveal that phosphorylation correlates with the formation of smaller, stiffer, and more adhesive oligomers that might be related to their pathological role in AD.

(b) Bulk FTIR characterization to unravel the secondary structure and chemical state of the aggregates. (c) Single-molecule chemical characterization using AFM-based methods to unravel morphological, chemical, phosphorylation state and mechanical properties of single oligomers.

Experimental Section and Methods

Preparation of Wild-Type and Phosphorylated Tau Aggregates

Full-length recombinant tau protein (isoform 0N4R; InVivo Biotech, Germany) was phosphorylated following established protocols, where phosphorylation was carried out using the catalytic subunit of cAMP-dependent protein kinase A (PKA; BioVision, now part of Abcam), followed by using the second kinase SAPK4 (Abcam). To prepare aggregates, both wild-type and phosphorylated tau monomers were incubated at a concentration of 2 μM in a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 8 mM Na2HPO4, 1.5 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.3), supplemented with 0.01% sodium azide (NaN3). Heparin (Fisher Scientific) was added to the wild-type tau samples, while phosphorylated tau samples were incubated with NaN3 alone. Both samples were incubated at 37 °C for 5 days.

pFTAA Aggregation Kinetics and Analysis

WT-Tau and P-Tau aggregation kinetics in the absence of heparin were measured as previously described. Prior to imaging, borosilicate glass coverslips (Ø50 mm, VWR International) were cleaned using an argon plasma cleaner (PDC-002, Harrick Plasma) for 1 h to remove any autofluorescent residue. Multiwell slide chambers (CultureWell chambered cover glass 50 well, Grace Bio-Laboratories) were affixed onto the cleaned coverslips. To stain the aggregates for diffraction-limited imaging, the samples were diluted in PBS buffer containing 30 nM pFTAA (gift from Michel Goedert) to a final monomer equivalent concentration of 67 nM tau. Then, 15 μL of each sample was applied to each well on the coverslip for 15 min before imaging. Images were recorded for 50 frames, each with an exposure time of 50 ms. The image stacks were averaged by using ImageJ (NIH) software for further analysis.

For each sample, 9 fields of view were typically recorded. Individual image data were averaged overall the framed by ImageJ (NIH) software and then analyzed with a custom-written MATLAB script as previously described75 (R2018b, MathWorks) to count the total number of fibrils and aggregates, the size, and fluorescence intensity. Since both the concentrations of protein and pFTAA were controlled in each experiment, the number and intensity of fibrils and aggregates can be directly compared across different experiments. For particle identification, images were band-pass filtered to remove the modulated background and camera noise. To identify particle boundaries, the foreground was then blurred using a 2D Gaussian filter with a threshold applied based on the pixel intensity, with a criterion of 2% intensity above the background (median value of the whole image). The length of the aggregates was measured by thinning the boundaries of individual particles and then calculated with an image pixel size of 107.2 nm for our TIRF setup. To eliminate the background effect in our intensity calculation, signal-to-background ratio (SBR) was introduced to correct the intensity of pixels, where the SBR is defined as SBR=intensity above the backgroundbackground . For a given particle, its corrected intensity is the sum of each pixel’s SBR values within its boundary.

FTIR Measurements, Data Treatments, and Analysis

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer equipped with a Platinum ATR accessory featuring a diamond crystal. The spectra were acquired using OPUS software (version 7.8, Bruker, USA). For each measurement, 3 μL of a WT-Tau or P-Tau protein solution (2 μM) was deposited onto the ATR crystal. The spectra were recorded in the range of 3500–500 cm–1 with a resolution of 4 cm–1, an aperture setting of 5 mm, and a controlled temperature of 20 °C. For each sample, three independent measurements with three technical replicates each (256 co-average) were collected. The spectra were processed using OriginPro (version 2022b, OriginLab, USA). The spectra were truncated to the 1800–1150 cm–1 region. Smoothing was performed using adjacent averaging (3 pts) and a Savitzky–Golay filter (second order, 7pts), followed by baseline correction (zero-order polynomial) and normalization to [0,1]. The second derivative spectra were calculated in the amide I region (1600–1700 cm–1) to deconvolve overlapping peaks related to the secondary structure. The resulting spectra were then smoothed using a Savitzky–Golay filter (second order, 7pts), and the processed data were used to quantify the content of the secondary structure. Phosphorylation levels were determined separately by analyzing the absorption spectra in the 1200–1300 cm–1 range.

AFM and AFM-IR Sample Preparation

The sample preparation for AFM-based nanoscale morphology, nanomechanics, and AFM-IR analysis was performed on two different substrates: negatively charged mica and positively charged gold surfaces (Platypus Technologies, USA). For mica-based deposition, 10 μL of each sample at a concentration of 0.5 μM was applied to freshly cleaved mica and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The substrate was then rinsed three times with 1 mL of Milli-Q water and dried by using a gentle stream of nitrogen gas. For gold substrates, 10 μL of each sample at 2 μM was deposited onto freshly stripped gold and incubated for 5 min. The surface was subsequently rinsed twice with 1 mL of Milli-Q water and dried under a gentle nitrogen stream. All sample preparations were carried out at room temperature and under a laminar flow.

Nanoscale Imaging and Quantitative Analysis

High-resolution, phase-controlled AFM maps were acquired using a Multimode VIII system (Bruker, USA) under ambient conditions, operating in the tapping mode. For the measurements, we employed PPP-FMR-20 cantilevers (Nano-Sensors) with a silicon tip having a nominal radius of curvature of approximately 7 nm, a resonance frequency of 75 kHz, and a force constant of 2.8 N/m. Statistical mapping was done on both samples at different locations, with map sizes of 2 and 4 μm2, and a spatial resolution ranging from 2 to 4 nm per pixel. Image flattening and cross-sectional dimension analysis of oligomers were performed with the SPIP (Image Metrology, Denmark) and MountainsSPIP (Digital Surf, France) software. Each AFM image was initially flattened by using a first-order polynomial fit to correct for global surface shape variation. This was followed by a line-wise correction using a zero-order least-mean-square (LMS) fit. To further enhance image quality, additional line-wise flattening was applied using first- and second-order LMS fits, with the area of interest masked to preserve relevant features. Quantitative size and shape analyses of single oligomers of both samples were performed through particle analysis, applying filters based on diameter (threshold set at three times the pixel size) and maximum height (set at four times the substrate roughness). Deconvoluted particle diameters were extracted following a previously reported methodology. Briefly, the deconvoluted diameter was calculated using the measured width and AFM tip radius, based on the geometric relation, which corrects for tip-induced broadening: r=wl216Rt , where w I is the convoluted width, R T is the tip radius, and r is the deconvoluted radius. Data were analyzed and plots were created by means of OriginPro software. Single particle analysis was conducted by filtering features with a cross-sectional height exceeding four times the substrate roughness (< 0.1 nm for mica) and a diameter greater than three times the pixel size.

Nanochemical Measurements and Analysis

Nanochemical analysis via AFM-IR was conducted using a nanoIR3 system (Bruker, USA) on atomically flat gold substrates (Platypus Technologies, USA). The morphology of tau samples was imaged by using the nanoIR3 microscopy system operating in contact mode, with a line scan rate ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 Hz. All AFM maps were acquired at a spatial resolution of 2–5 pixels per nanometer. A silicon cantilever with a gold coating (PR-EX-nIR2, Anasys, USA), featuring a nominal tip radius of approximately 30 nm and an elastic constant of ∼0.2 N/m, was employed for the measurements. The AFM images were treated and analyzed using SPIP (earlier Image Metrology, Denmark, now Digital Surf, France) and Mountains SPIP (Digital Surf, France) software. The height images were first-order flattened. The infrared spectra were acquired by positioning the AFM tip directly on top of the oligomers under investigation. A tunable laser was used with a wavelength sampling interval of 1–2 cm–1 and a spectral resolution of 0.5 cm–1. Measurements were conducted within the 1200–1800 cm–1 range. All acquisitions were performed under off-resonance conditions using short laser pulses at low power. To ensure statistical relevance, multiple spectra were collected from different oligomers. Specifically, 14 spectra from 3 different oligomers were obtained for WT-Tau and 20 spectra from 4 different oligomers were acquired for P-Tau; then, the spectra were smoothened, normalized, and averaged by subtracting the signal from the gold substrate (Figure S5). Spectral analysis was carried out using the built-in Analysis Studio software (Anasys Instruments, USA) and OriginPro. The second derivative of the IR absorption in the amide I region was used to deconvolute the main contributions from different secondary structure conformations. Similarly, second derivative analysis in the phosphorylation region enabled quantification of phosphorylation states at the single-oligomer level.

Nanomechanical Measurements and Analysis

Nanomechanical characterization of oligomers was performed using Peak Force Quantitative Nanomechanical Mapping (PF-QNM) on mica substrates under ambient conditions, using a Multimode VIII system (Bruker, USA). PF-QNM enables high-speed, high-resolution mapping of mechanical properties. For the measurements, we used RTESPA-300 cantilevers (Nanosensors) with a tip radius of 8 nm, a nominal resonance frequency of approximately 300 kHz, and a nominal force constant of 40 N/m. Cantilever calibration was carried out using the relative method with a polystyrene reference sample (modulus ∼2 GPa). Data acquisition was conducted at scan rates ranging from 0.3 to 0.5 Hz, with a spatial resolution of 2 nm/pixel. Peak force parameters included a frequency of 4 kHz, an amplitude of 100 nm, and a set point force between 4 and 9 nN. To ensure controlled tip–sample interaction and eliminate surface effects, deformation was maintained <20% of the oligomer height. Analysis of the Derjaguin–Mueller–Toporov (DMT) modulus and adhesion force was performed on the acquired maps by using the SPIP software, and the resulting data were visualized by using OriginPro.

Results and Discussions

3D Morphological Analysis Using High-Resolution, Phase-Controlled AFM

To investigate the 3D shape of the heterogeneous aggregates formed by WT-Tau and P-Tau (Figure ), we used high-resolution and phase-controlled AFM with subangstrom noise level (Figure a).

Nanoscale heterogeneity of WT-Tau versus P-Tau. (a) Schematic illustration of high-resolution, phase-controlled AFM with subangstrom noise level (∼20 pm). To ensure consistent height comparison across different samples, measurements were conducted under a controlled phase shift within Δ20°. (b) AFM topographic maps of WT-Tau aggregates and (c) zoomed-in maps showing oligomers and fibrils in the WT-Tau sample. (d) AFM topographic maps of P-Tau aggregates and (e) zoomed-in AFM maps showing only oligomeric species. For both WT- and P-Tau, we scanned a total n = 35 maps, where n = 5 of 10 um2, n = 15 of 4 um2 and n = 15 of 2 um2; for a total area of 800 um2.

On both WT-Tau and P-Tau, statistical mapping was performed at randomly chosen locations on a mica substrate for a total area of 800 um2 to capture the heterogeneity of the aggregates present (Figure S2). In WT-Tau, the maps showed the coexistence of amyloid fibrils with a cross-sectional height of ∼5–10 nm, protofibrils with a cross-sectional height of ∼2–5 nm, and spherical and toroidal oligomers with a cross-sectional height of ∼2–10 nm (Figure b,c). In P-Tau, the maps showed only the presence of spherical and toroidal oligomeric species with heights of ∼2–5 nm (Figure d,e). In P-Tau, fibrillar aggregates were not observed on two different substrates with different states of charge, such as negative mica and hydrophobic HOPG, excluding surface effects.

While we analyzed the properties of both the fibrillar and oligomeric species, we focused this work on the comparison of the oligomers present in both samples, since soluble tau oligomeric species have shown significant toxicity and are challenging to study compared to other species. ,, However, WT-Tau does not aggregate alone and requires the addition of heparin; instead, hyperphosphorylated tau aggregates spontaneously. Since WT-Tau does not aggregate without an inducer (Figure S1), direct comparison under identical conditions is not possible, and we proceeded to study P-Tau in its most relevant biological state without the addition of heparin. It is also noteworthy to observe the presence of toroidal oligomers, which have also been found previously in the brain biopsies of AD patients and have shown toxic effects. −

We further proceeded with a single-molecule statistical analysis of the oligomer shape and size (Figure ). Although their cross-sectional height can be measured with angstrom-level precision, determining their cross-sectional diameter is limited by the size of the AFM tip and the resulting convolution artifacts. To address in part this limit, we applied well-established deconvolution methods to estimate the diameter of the aggregates. Figure a,b presents representative 3D topographic maps of single WT-Tau and P-Tau oligomers, respectively; and their corresponding cross-sectional profiles are shown in Figure c,d. A total of n = 1939 for WT-Tau and n = 3311 for P-Tau oligomers were measured and displayed in 2D kernel density plots, illustrating the relationship between cross-sectional height and deconvoluted diameter (Figure e,f). The analysis revealed that WT-Tau oligomers exhibit a significantly larger population than P-Tau oligomers; additionally, the analysis of the roundness of the oligomers showed that WT-Tau oligomers are more symmetrical than P-Tau oligomers (Figure S3).

Single-molecule 3D shape and size analysis of tau oligomers. AFM topographic images of (a) WT-Tau and (b) P-Tau oligomers, (c, d) accompanied by the representative cross-sectional profile. Kernel density plots illustrating the relationship between height and deconvoluted diameter for (e) WT-Tau (n = 1939) and (f) P-Tau oligomers (n = 3640); the plot uses a color gradient to show density, where darker regions indicate stronger clustering of data points and lighter regions reflect a lower number of data points.

Overall, the 3D analysis by AFM showed that phosphorylated P-Tau stabilizes smaller and less symmetrical oligomers, while WT-Tau forms larger oligomers and has a higher propensity for aggregation into amyloid fibrils than P-Tau oligomers (Figure e,f). This suggests that phosphorylation may act as a regulatory mechanism, limiting the progression of P-Tau toward more ordered, fibrillar aggregates. While amyloid oligomers are typically considered toxic, , the more structurally constrained nature of P-Tau may result in different mechanisms of toxicity. Indeed, this difference in the population of aggregates has implications for different neuroinflammatory and toxicity mechanisms in brain cells. However, AFM imaging alone is not sufficient to identify which chemical–structural properties of these aggregates may be related to different amyloidogenic pathways and toxicity.

Bulk Molecular and Structural Characterization of Tau Aggregates

We were further interested in studying whether the observed different molecular pathways of formation and morphologies between WT-Tau and P-Tau oligomers stem from underlying chemical–structural differences in the aggregates. Thus, we investigated and compared the bulk chemical properties of WT-Tau and P-Tau aggregates using the molecular recognition power of attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) (Figure ).

Bulk chemical–structural analysis of tau aggregates. (a) FTIR spectra highlighting the amide I region and phosphorylation-associated region for both WT-Tau (dark blue, independent experiments) and P-Tau (dark blue) (for both samples, N = 3 independent experiments were performed, n = 3 technical replicates, for a total of 9 spectra per sample, each spectrum was co-averaged 256 times; error bars represent SE). (b) Detailed view of the amide I region, showing a spectral shift at lower wavenumbers, indicating intermolecular β-sheet structures. (c) Schematic illustration depicting the carbonyl stretching vibration responsible for the amide I band in protein. (d, e) Second derivative analysis of the amide I band (left panel) and the quantification of native vs intermolecular β-sheet secondary structure (error bar represents SD). (f) Schematic representation of the PO stretching vibration associated with phosphorylation in protein. (g, h) Zoomed-in images of the IR spectra showing higher absorption in the PO region associated with phosphorylation and its quantification (error bar represents SD).

We acquired the spectra in the protein fingerprint region between 1800 and 1150 cm–1 (Figure a) and observed significant differences in the region associated with the IR absorption of PO groups, as well as small but significant differences in the amide I band (Figure b).

It is well established that the amide I band, primarily arising from the CO stretching vibrations, is highly sensitive to changes in the protein secondary structure (Figure c). We observed that the amide band I of WT-Tau had a small but significant shift at lower wavenumbers, consistent with the formation of an intermolecular cross-β sheet of amyloidogenic fibrils. To quantify these structural differences, we performed a second derivative analysis on the amide I region (1600–1700 cm–1), which is useful to determine the protein secondary structure (Figure d). Indeed, second derivative analysis allowed us to resolve the overlapping structural components within the amide I band. The second derivatives revealed a clear signature of both parallel and antiparallel intermolecular β-sheet in WT-Tau, at ∼1630 and ∼1690 cm–1, while P-tau aggregates had a predominantly α-helical signature at ∼1665 cm–1 with a minor content of intermolecular interactions. We further integrated the area of each component in the second derivatives to quantify the average secondary structure of the aggregates (Figure e). Overall, WT-Tau displayed approximately twice the intermolecular β-sheet content compared to P-Tau. Additionally, P-Tau showed a greater proportion of native-like structures, including α-helix (∼1654 cm–1), random coil (∼1640 cm–1), and β-turn (∼1678 cm–1), suggesting a less aggregated state relative to WT-Tau.

Similarly, we investigated the spectroscopic difference between WT-Tau and P-Tau in the PO stretching region. Phosphorylation, as a post-translational modification, exhibits a characteristic infrared absorption in the 1200–1300 cm–1 range, where PO stretching vibrations typically occur (Figure f). The IR absorption for unmodified WT-Tau in this region was related to the typical signature of protein in the amide III band. The additional presence of a characteristic double peak centered around ∼1230 cm–1 in P-Tau could be associated with the symmetric and antisymmetric vibration of PO groups instead. In Figure g, a zoomed-in image of the absolute IR absorbance in the PO region with normalization to the protein content (amide I) is presented, which enables direct comparison of phosphorylation levels via the Beer–Lambert law. In addition to the characteristic double peak, P-Tau exhibited approximately 2-fold higher IR absorption in the PO region compared to WT, indicating a higher phosphorylation state. This difference was further quantified and visualized in the histogram presented in Figure h.

Overall, the FTIR analysis confirmed that WT-Tau has a cross-β sheet signature, typically associated with the formation of amyloid fibrils. This cross-β signature is significantly lower in P-Tau, in agreement with the AFM analysis showing the formation of amyloid fibrils only in WT-Tau and the presence of only oligomers in P-Tau. However, bulk FTIR retrieves only bulk information, not allowing us to associate the average spectroscopic signature of each sample with the chemical–structural properties of the single populations of aggregates present, such as oligomers and fibrils. WT-Tau at 120 h has indeed both oligomer and fibrillar species, whose individual biophysical properties will be averaged through this ensemble assay, thereby preventing us from directly comparing WT-Tau oligomers and P-Tau oligomers. Thus, FTIR prevents the differentiation of the secondary structure of single oligomeric species and comparison of their structural and phosphorylation state.

Single Oligomeric Phosphorylation Characterization and Quantification Using AFM-IR

While bulk FTIR provided valuable insights into the average secondary structure and phosphorylation levels of tau aggregates, we aimed to delve deeper by examining phosphorylation state at the level of individual amyloid species and to explore how their chemical signature correlates with their morphological features. To achieve this objective, we utilized the unique capabilities of AFM-IR, combining nanoscale imaging with vibrational spectroscopy and enabling chemical characterization down to the single-oligomer level. ,

AFM-based photothermal infrared nanospectroscopy has been proven to be able to detect the chemical and structural signature of single molecules. , Yet, for protein, its sensitivity has remained limited by signal-to-noise constraints to the detection and quantification of secondary structure to molecules with molecular weight MW > 400 kDa. Enhancing the signal, such as through gold probe/surface nanogap configurations, is often necessary to approach single-molecule resolution. Employing short laser pulses in off-resonance mode (ORS-nanoIR), together with the recently developed acoustical–mechanical isolated AFM-IR for single polymer chain analysis, we achieved high signal-to-noise ratio to investigate the chemical–structural properties of single oligomers with a high signal-to-noise ratio; by using composite foams to effectively suppress mechanical and acoustic noise transmission from cables, vibrating components inside and outside the AFM-IR system, as well as from ground vibrations. Tau aggregates were deposited on a template-stripped gold substrate to leverage field enhancement at the nanogap between a gold-coated probe and the substrate (Figure a). Representative AFM maps of WT-Tau and P-Tau oligomers are shown in Figure b, from which then nanolocalized IR spectra were acquired at the nanoscale for each oligomer (colored crosses on the map).

As in conventional FTIR, the secondary structure of single oligomers was assessed by analyzing the amide I band (1600–1700 cm–1) (Figure c), while phosphorylation was evaluated by monitoring the PO stretching bands in the 1200–1300 cm–1 region (Figure d).

The average second derivatives of the IR spectra in the amide I between 1700 and 1600 cm–1 were used to quantify the secondary structure of WT-Tau vs P-Tau oligomers (Figure e). Both oligomers exhibited comparable levels of intermolecular β-sheet content and native structural elements, although P-tau oligomers exhibited a slightly higher content of intermolecular β-sheet, which was instead shifted significantly at lower wavenumbers, indicating the formation of a more extended β-sheet structure (Figure f). While statistically significant differences in secondary structure were not observed between the oligomers, this limitation was not solely due to statistical constraints. The challenge also stems from the low signal-to-noise ratio inherent in nanoscale spectral acquisition of oligomers as small as a few nanometers and with an estimated molecular weight of only MW ∼300–600 kDa, which is near the state-of-the-art sensitivity limit of detection of AFM-IR. Although structure determination is possible now for oligomers smaller than 400 kDa, detecting subtle structural differences between aggregates of similar size may further require higher AFM-IR sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio. Thus, as a control, we performed the nanochemical analysis on the larger and easier-to-measure fibrils comparable to both WT-Tau fibrils (Figure S4). The fibrils showed a higher content of intermolecular β-sheet, and a phosphorylation level comparable to WT oligomers, significantly lower than P-Tau oligomers. Finally, although we could not fully exclude the influence of heparin on the structural properties of WT fibrils and oligomers, the post-translational states of single tau oligomers may only be minimally affected by residual heparin molecules (mean MW ∼15 kDa), as even the contribution of several molecules together to the spectrum of a single oligomer is at the limit of AFM-IR detection (>200 kDa).

Figure d displays the IR absorption spectra within the 1200–1400 cm–1 range, where the PO stretching associated with phosphorylation occurs, showing that WT-Tau and P-Tau spectra were significantly different. However, the PO signal peaked at around 1230 cm–1 and was convoluted with the typical signal of the AFM probe at 1260 cm–1. Thus, as shown in Figure h, the spectra were normalized to the probe absorption, and a second derivative analysis was used to deconvolve the peaks. The analysis revealed a characteristic double peak related to symmetric and antisymmetric PO stretching bands at around ∼1230 cm–1 in P-Tau against a weaker single band at a lower wavenumber for WT-Tau (Figure g), consistent with FTIR analysis. The quantification of the area of the relative bands proved to have a significantly higher phosphorylation level in P-Tau oligomers than for WT ones (Figure h). These findings on the phosphorylation state are consistent with the previous work, which showed through high-resolution native mass spectrometry that WT-Tau was not phosphorylated, while P-Tau generated using PKA and SAPK4 kinases was hyperphosphorylated with ∼19 phosphate groups per molecule. The levels of phosphorylation are compatible with the native state of tau with 2–3 phosphate groups and with the hyperphosphorylation state of pathological tau species in vivo with up to 9 phosphate groups. Moreover, our hyperphosphorylated tau is modified at AD-specific epitopes, including AT8, AT180, AT100, and PHF-1. Thus, the level and epitope position of phosphorylation highlight the pathological relevance of the Tau model used in our study and validate its use as a representative model for studying tau pathology.

Overall, the AFM-IR analysis demonstrated for the first time the quantitative comparison of the secondary structure and phosphorylation state of pathology-relevant amyloid oligomers at the single-molecule level. The correlation between distinct size and post-translational phosphorylation state, together with minor differences in their secondary structure, reinforces the idea that phosphorylation influences and reshapes the physical–chemical properties of Tau oligomers. Thus, it suggests that their adhesive and mechanical properties may also differ further, as we will investigate in the next paragraph.

Single-Oligomer Nanomechanical Analysis Using PF-QNM

Building on our previous observations, we aimed to determine whether the morphological and chemical differences between WT-Tau and P-Tau oligomers also reflect variations in their nanomechanical properties (Figure ), which are increasingly recognized as critical determinants of their cytotoxicity through different mechanisms, including membrane permeabilization and proinflammatory signaling in cells.

To evaluate the nanomechanical properties of single tau oligomers, we employed peak-force quantitative nanomechanical mapping. As illustrated in Figure a–c, PF-QNM operates in a dynamic AFM mode where each probe–oligomer interaction generates a force–distance curve, allowing simultaneous mapping and quantification of topography and mechanical properties, making it ideal for characterizing soft biological samples like tau oligomers. For our analysis, we applied the Derjaguin–Muller–Toporov (DMT) model, which incorporates adhesion forces, to extract both the elastic modulus and adhesion values from the force curves.

Figure d presents representative maps of morphology, modulus, and adhesion for WT-Tau (left) and P-Tau (right panel) oligomers. To quantify the information in the maps to avoid artifacts due to edge and surface effects, we first focused our analysis on the central regions of individual oligomers, excluding edges to minimize artifacts (Figure S6). Furthermore, to ensure a fair comparison between different WT- and P-tau samples, we selected oligomers of comparable size between 3 and 8 nm and kept constant deformation smaller than 20% of the cross-sectional height of each oligomer to avoid substrate effects that may affect the measured stiffness (Figure S7). We analyzed n = 20 oligomers for both WT- and P-tau, and the results of the analysis demonstrate that P-Tau oligomers exhibit small but significant higher stiffness (E WT = 0.29 ± 0.07 GPa, E P = 0.4 ± 0.1 GPa; p < 0.001) and adhesion (F WT = 3.0 ± 0.9 nN, F P = 4.0 ± 1.2 nN; p < 0.001) than WT-Tau oligomers. Although WT oligomers are larger in size compared to P-Tau oligomers, they exhibit a lower Young’s modulus, confirming that size does not influence the measured stiffness. Moreover, to prove that measured stiffness is not dependent on size or substrate effects, we further show that the stiffness of P-Tau oligomers is independent of their size across the selected range for analysis (Figure S9). The measured moduli values ranged between 0.2 and 0.5 GPa, in agreement with the relatively low content of intermolecular β-sheet structures observed in both bulk and single-molecule IR spectroscopic analysis and typical values of stiffness of amyloidogenic oligomers. Since oligomers have a typical height between 2 and 5 nm, the reliability of our measurements was further validated by comparing the oligomers to the WT-Tau fibrils, which exhibited higher stiffness and adhesion than oligomeric species, consistent as reported in the literature ,, (Figure S8). However, we cannot exclude the influence of heparin on the structural properties of the WT fibrils and oligomers. Thus, the observed structural difference in our 3D morphology measurement and mechanical measurements is likely due to a combination of heparin presence, phosphorylation state, and their effects on aggregation kinetics and thermodynamics.

Overall, the nanomechanical measurements demonstrate that different phosphorylation states influence adhesive and mechanical properties of the oligomers, with P-Tau oligomers having higher stiffness and adhesion. These mechanical differences may contribute to cytotoxic pathological mechanisms, as stiffer aggregates are more likely to disrupt cellular membranes and interfere with intracellular processes.

Conclusion

Conventional bulk and microscopy techniques have significantly advanced our understanding of protein misfolding and aggregation in neurodegeneration. Yet, the multidimensional characterization of single oligomeric species, particularly those modified by key post-translational events, such as phosphorylation, remains a formidable challenge. Their transient nature, chemical–structural heterogeneity, and low abundance often place them beyond the reach of traditional methods, which typically lack the resolution or sensitivity to capture these early-stage biologically relevant intermediates.

In this study, we overcome these limitations by employing a suite of nanoanalytical techniques, enabling, for the first time, the direct detection and quantification of the phosphorylation state of single tau oligomers, implicated in the onset of tauopathies as AD. Our tau model system has similar phosphorylated states as in vivo WT (∼2–3 PO groups) and hyperphosphorylated tau (∼9 PO groups); moreover, the phosphorylation sites occur at AD-relevant specific epitopes, such as AT8, AT180, AT100, and PHF-1. Thus, our model oligomeric aggregates of tau are relevant to studying the early molecular features of AD pathology. We then provide evidence of how phosphorylation modulates the tau pathway of aggregation and the properties of the oligomeric species formed, from morphology and secondary structure to chemical composition and mechanical behavior. In the time scales of our experiment, we prove that hyperphosphorylation prevents tau protein from forming amyloid fibrillar aggregates and stabilizes a population of oligomers that is smaller, more adhesive, and stiffer and has a slightly increased cross-β sheet content than wild-type oligomers. These structural differences may be critically related to different mechanisms of toxicity; indeed, it is widely accepted that increased stiffness, adhesion, and intermolecular β-sheet content are critical properties to drive inflammation and membrane permeabilization.

Our results not only deepen our understanding of the effect of phosphorylation on tau self-assembly but also provide useful insights into the early molecular mechanisms of pathology. The approach developed here also opens new avenues to study the phosphorylation state of the protein in more complex human biopsies, as well as provides single-molecule information for therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting aggregate species or post-translational modifications in a wide range of associated neurodegenerative disorders.
