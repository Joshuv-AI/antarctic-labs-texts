Microbiological investigation of conjunctivitis in cats and dogs from Kars, Türkiye (2024–2025): culture- and MALDI-TOF MS-based identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing of bacterial isolates
Authors: Serdal Tarhane, Fatih Büyük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConjunctivitis is a common clinical disorder in cats and dogs, frequently associated with bacterial and fungal infections. Accurate identification of pathogens and evaluation of their antimicrobial resistance profiles are essential for effective treatment.MethodsBacterial and fungal agents were analyzed using cultural and MALDI-TOF MS methods in conjunctival swab samples taken from 59 domestic animals (53 cats and 6 dogs) diagnosed with conjunctivitis and presented to veterinary clinics in Çankırı, Türkiye, during the period from 2024 to 2025.ResultsAmong the 59 conjunctival swab samples collected from cats and dogs with conjunctivitis, 20 (33.9%) were positive for bacterial culture. Of these, 17 positive cultures were detected in 53 cat samples (32.08%), whereas 3 were detected in 6 dog samples (50.0%). No growth was observed in cultures for anaerobic and Mycoplasma agents, and no evidence of fungal agents was found. Multiple bacterial isolates were isolated in six (10.17%) animals [5 cats (9.43%), 1 dog (16.67%)]. Species identification by MALDI-TOF MS revealed a total of 16 different bacterial species with high confidence scores (≥2.0) across both animal species. The most frequently isolated strains were Staphylococcus epidermidis (n= 7), Corynebacterium mucifaciens (n= 5), Psychrobacter spp. (n= 5), and Streptococcus lutetiensis (n= 4). Rare strains such as Brachybacterium muris (n= 1), Micrococcus luteus (n= 1), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n= 1) were also detected. In cats, S. epidermidis, Corynebacterium spp., Psychrobacter spp., and Streptococcus hyovaginalis were the most frequently isolated strains. In dogs, Streptococcus canis, Enterococcus durans, Corynebacterium mucifaciens, Micrococcus luteus, and Staphylococcus hominis ssp. hominis were detected. Antibiotic susceptibility of the isolates was evaluated according to EUCAST and CLSI criteria and interpreted based on the inhibition zone diameter. Of the 37 bacterial isolates recovered, 32 (86.49%) exhibited multidrug-resistant (MDR) profiles. Particularly noteworthy were the extensive MDR patterns observed in Enterococcus durans, Enterococcus faecium, Psychrobacter sanguinis, Streptococcus hyovaginalis, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates, which showed resistance to 10-12 antimicrobial agents from multiple classes, including penicillins, tetracyclines, and cephalosporins.ConclusionAlthough no fungal agents were recovered, the study demonstrates that the bacterial species isolated from cats and dogs with conjunctivitis are highly diverse and that multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates are common. The antimicrobial susceptibility results provide valuable information on the resistance profiles of microorganisms associated with conjunctivitis cases in the study population. In addition, the detection of certain microorganisms with zoonotic potential underscores the importance of ongoing surveillance from a veterinary public health perspective.Keywords: Antimicrobial susceptibility, Cat, Conjunctivitis, Culture, Dog, MALDI-TOF MS

Introduction

Conjunctivitis is an inflammatory disorder of the conjunctival tissue and one of the most common ocular diseases in companion animal practice. It may be caused by infectious, allergic, traumatic, environmental, or immunological factors and can affect animals of all ages and breeds [1]. Among infectious agents, bacterial pathogens constitute a major cause of conjunctivitis in dogs and cats, while viral and fungal agents may also contribute to ocular infections [2, 3].

Common bacterial species associated with conjunctivitis in companion animals include Staphylococcus pseudintermedius, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Streptococcus spp. In cats, Chlamydia felis and feline herpesvirus type 1 (FHV-1) are also important pathogens, often predisposing to secondary bacterial infections [1, 4, 5]. Although less frequently reported, fungal agents may also be involved in ocular infections [6, 7]. Conjunctivitis may develop as a primary condition or secondary to systemic infections and impaired local immunity [8].

Accurate identification of the causative agent is essential for effective treatment. While conventional culture remains the standard diagnostic method [9], it is time-consuming and may fail to detect fastidious microorganisms [10]. Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) enables rapid and reliable species-level identification in veterinary diagnostic laboratories [11–14].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an increasing concern in both human and veterinary medicine. Variability in susceptibility patterns among ocular bacterial isolates highlights the need for culture-based antimicrobial testing prior to therapy [15, 16]. In particular, multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria may reduce treatment success and facilitate the spread of resistant strains [17].

Despite its clinical importance, data on bacterial conjunctivitis in companion animals remain limited in many regions. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify bacterial species isolated from conjunctival swabs of dogs and cats using MALDI-TOF MS and to evaluate their antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, with emphasis on multidrug resistance patterns.

Materials and methods

Study materials

The study material consisted of 59 conjunctival swab samples collected using sterile swabs from 53 cats and 6 dogs diagnosed with conjunctivitis and presented to veterinary clinics in Çankırı, Türkiye, during the period from 2024 to 2025. All animals were privately owned and were brought by their owners to different veterinary clinics for routine diagnosis and treatment. Of the cats sampled in the study, 34 were female (mean age 3.97 months) and 19 were male (mean age 12.68 months), while of the dogs, 3 were female (mean age 7.67 months) and 3 were male (mean age 47.67 months). A history and physical examination were performed on each cat and dog. The cats and dogs had not received any antibiotics, anti-inflammatory drugs, immunosuppressive medications, or vaccinations in the three months prior to sample collection. Conjunctival swab samples were collected by gently swabbing the inferior fornix (lower conjunctival sac) of each eye while avoiding contact with the eyelid margins and eyelashes. Swabs were placed in microcentrifuge tubes containing 0.5 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), transported to the laboratory under cold-chain conditions, and processed for microbiological culture within 24 h of collection. Following culture analysis, aliquots of the remaining sample material were stored at -80 °C to ensure long-term preservation of microbial material and to allow potential future molecular analyses and repeat testing under optimal storage conditions.

Isolation and phenotypic identification of bacterial agents

For the isolation of bacterial agents, 100 µL of PBS containing conjunctival swabs was taken and transferred to 7% Sheep Blood Agar (SBA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) in triplicate and inoculated using the spread plate method. The sown SBAs were incubated at 37 °C in three different atmospheric environments (aerobic, anaerobic, and microaerobic) and their growth status was checked daily for 1 week [18].

Bacterial agents grown on the media were re-passaged onto SBA to obtain pure colonies and incubated under appropriate conditions. Preliminary identification of the bacterial agents grown at the end of this period was performed using morphological, cultural, and phenotypic methods [19].

For Mycoplasma spp. isolation, samples were pre-enriched by incubating them in 3 mL of Mycoplasma broth supplemented with 10% fresh yeast extract (Oxoid, UK), 20% heat-inactivated horse serum (Sigma, USA), 0.025% thallium acetate (Fluka, Belgium), and 100 IU of penicillin G (IE, Istanbul) for 3 days at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. Then, 100 µL of the pre-enriched content was plated on Mycoplasma agar (Oxoid, England) enriched with 10% fresh yeast extract, 20% heat-inactivated horse serum, 0.025% thallium acetate, and 100 U of penicillin G. The agar plates were further incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The growth of Mycoplasma spp. was monitored for 21 days [20].

Isolation and phenotypic identification of fungal agents

For the isolation of fungal agents, 100 µL of PBS containing conjunctival swabs was taken and inoculated onto fungal Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) using the spread plate method. Considering the presence of slow-growing fungal agents (such as dermatophytes, Histoplasma spp., and Blastomyces spp.), the samples were incubated aerobically at 25 °C for 2 weeks [21].

Identification of isolates by MALDI TOF-MS

Identification of bacterial and fungal isolates purified on SDA and SBA was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) with the MALDI Biotyper® system (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Spectra were analyzed using MBT Compass software and compared with the MBT Compass Reference Library (version 2026) for species-level identification. MALDI TOF-MS analyses were performed through service contracts from the Molecular Biology Laboratory of the General Directorate of Public Health, Ministry of Health of the Republic of Turkey. A fresh colony was taken with a loop to place on the MALDI TOF plate. Then, 1 µL of α-cyano4-hydroxynamic acid (α-CHCA) matrix solution was added to the well and dried. Identification was done by leaving the plate in the MALDI-TOF MS device for analysis. The MALDI Biotyper produces log score values ranging from 0.000 to 3.000. A score ≥ 2.0 indicates probable identification to the species level; scores between 1.700 and 1.999 indicate probable genus-level identification, and scores < 1.700 are not considered reliable for identification [22].

Antimicrobial susceptibility analysis of the isolates

The antimicrobial susceptibilities of identified bacterial strains were determined using the disk diffusion method. Colonies from the isolates were inoculated on SBA and incubated at 37 °C under appropriate atmospheric conditions for 18–24 h. Inocula were prepared according to the McFarland 0.5 turbidity standard. One hundred µL of these inocula was inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton Agar and was spread by a L-shaped spreader. After drying the inoculated media for 10 min at room temperature, 4–5 antibiotic disks were placed on the inoculated media and incubated at 37 °C under appropriate atmospheric conditions for 18–24 h. In this context, MY: lincomycin (15 µg), BA: bacitracin (10 IU), OT: oxytetracycline (30 µg), IMI: imipenem (10 µg), AMP: ampicillin (10 µg), SXT: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (25 µg), NA: nalidixic acid (30 µg), TE: tetracycline (30 µg), C: kanamycin (30 µg), C: chloramphenicol (30 µg), CFP: cefoperazone (75 µg), CN: gentamicin (30 µg), AMC: amoxicillin-clavulonic acid (30 µg), ENR: enrofloxacin (5 µg), ETP: ertapenem (10 µg), FUR: ceftiofur (30 µg), P: penicillin G (1 U), VA: vancomycin (30 µg) antibiotic disks (Liofilchem, Italy) were used. The inhibition zone diameters formed after incubation were measured in millimeters using a ruler. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Evaluations were made according to the CLSI [23] and the EUCAST [24] standards. In addition, test results for gentamicin and kanamycin were interpreted using EUCAST epidemiological cut-off values (ECV) [25].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. Associations between culture-positivity and variables such as animal species and gender were evaluated using the Pearson chi-square test and Fisher’s Exact test. To compare age parameters between culture-positive and culture-negative groups, the distribution of each group was first assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Variables showing normal distribution were analyzed with the Independent t-test, while those not showing normal distribution were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to small subgroup sizes and missing data, the analysis of culture-positivity and breed variables was considered exploratory. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Only MDR-related analyses were performed, as standardized breakpoints were unavailable for several species and most data consisted of inhibition zone diameters. The relationships between MDR status and animal demographic characteristics were evaluated using categorical statistical methods and expressed as frequencies and percentages. Associations between MDR status and gender, breed, and age group were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

Results

Isolation and identification results

Conjunctival swab samples from 59 animals, including 53 cats and 6 dogs, were analyzed by cultural methods. Bacterial growth was detected in 20 animals (33.9%), including 17 cats (32.08%) and 3 dogs (50%). A total of 37 bacterial isolates were obtained from these culture-positive samples. No growth was observed under anaerobic conditions on SBA, and all samples were negative for Mycoplasma spp. In addition, no fungal agents were isolated on SDA. Multiple bacterial isolates were identified in 6 animals (10.17%), including 5 cats (9.43%) and 1 dog (16.67%).

Species identification was first performed using morphological and phenotypic characteristics and was subsequently confirmed by MALDI-TOF MS. Reliable species-level identification (score ≥ 2.0) was achieved for all isolates, and 16 different bacterial species were identified. The most frequently isolated species were Staphylococcus epidermidis (n = 7), Corynebacterium mucifaciens (n = 5), and Streptococcus lutetiensis (n = 4). Other isolates included Brachybacterium muris, Corynebacterium simulans, Corynebacterium freneyi, Micrococcus luteus, Staphylococcus hominis, Enterococcus durans, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Antimicrobial susceptibility test results

Since breakpoints are not available in the manuals for B. muris 160419_7 BRB, M. luteus DSM 14,234, and Psychrobacter spp., results are reported as inhibition zone diameters. B. muris was resistant to ampicillin but showed relatively large zones for carbapenems, quinolones, and chloramphenicol. M. luteus displayed variable susceptibility, with moderate inhibition zones (20–23 mm) for commonly active agents and a quintet MDR profile (BA_OT_P_K_VA). Among the Psychrobacter isolates, all were resistant to bacitracin, while resistance patterns varied among strains; the widest inhibition zones were observed for ertapenem and gentamicin. No MDR was detected in P. lutiphocae, whereas P. sanguinis isolates exhibited MDR profiles with resistance to 6, 10, and 14 antimicrobial agents (Table 2).

Only tetracycline and vancomycin results for the Corynebacterium isolates could be interpreted according to EUCAST breakpoints, with resistance rates of 28.57% (2/7) and 14.29% (1/7), respectively. For the remaining antimicrobials without established breakpoints, results are reported as inhibition zone diameters. Most Corynebacterium isolates (85.71%) showed resistance to the tested agents, particularly vancomycin, tetracycline, ampicillin, and bacitracin, while generally larger inhibition zones (20–30 mm) were observed for lincomycin, ertapenem, imipenem, kanamycin, and chloramphenicol. MDR analysis revealed no resistance in C. freneyi 14103038_4 MVD, whereas C. mucifaciens isolates exhibited resistance to 4–6 antimicrobial agents, and one C. simulans isolate showed resistance to 4 antimicrobial agents (Table 2).

The reactions of the Enterococcus isolates to different antimicrobials were interpreted according to EUCAST and CLSI guidelines, depending on the antibiotic. Of the isolates, 33.33% (1/3) were susceptible to tetracycline and 66.67% (2/3) showed intermediate susceptibility to chloramphenicol, while all isolates were resistant to amoxicillin, ampicillin, kanamycin, nalidixic acid, vancomycin, and penicillin. For agents without established breakpoints, results were expressed as inhibition zone diameters, with carbapenems (ertapenem and imipenem) and gentamicin showing the largest zones. For gentamicin in E. faecium, results were additionally interpreted using the ECV. MDR analysis revealed resistance to 10 antimicrobial agents in E. durans, and resistance to 11 and 12 antimicrobial agents in the E. faecium isolates (Table 2).

Most the E. coli isolates were evaluated according to EUCAST, CLSI, or both guidelines. For antibiotics without established breakpoints (lincomycin, bacitracin, oxytetracycline, ceftiofur, enrofloxacin, and vancomycin), results are reported as inhibition zone diameters, with mean zones of 18–22 mm observed for all agents except bacitracin. Susceptibility to imipenem, ampicillin, and gentamicin was consistent across both manuals. However, discrepancies were noted for ertapenem, where one isolate was classified as resistant and the other as intermediate according to EUCAST, while both showed different interpretations under CLSI. Notably, resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and penicillin was prominent. MDR analysis revealed resistance to 10 antimicrobial agents in one isolate and resistance to 6 antimicrobial agents in the other (Table 2).

For Pseudomonas aeruginosa, only imipenem had established breakpoints in both EUCAST and CLSI manuals, with the isolate classified as resistant according to EUCAST and susceptible according to CLSI. The isolate showed resistance to most tested antibiotics; among the agents evaluated, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid showed the highest inhibition zone, while others ranged between 7 and 15 mm. This isolate also exhibited resistance to 12 antimicrobial agents (Table 2).

The reactions of the Staphylococcus isolates to selected antibiotics were evaluated using CLSI, and in some cases both CLSI and EUCAST criteria. High susceptibility (75-87.5%) was observed for trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, gentamicin, and chloramphenicol, whereas complete (100%) resistance to penicillin was noted. Tetracycline results differed between manuals (62.5% EUCAST vs. 87.5% CLSI). For kanamycin, where no EUCAST breakpoints were available for S. epidermidis and S. hominis, results were interpreted using inhibition zone diameters and the ECV. In general, isolates showed resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, ceftiofur, and nalidixic acid, while larger inhibition zones (15–27 mm) were observed for lincomycin, ertapenem, cefoperazone, and enrofloxacin. MDR analysis revealed variable resistance profiles in S. epidermidis (ranging from resistance to 4 to 9 antimicrobial agents), while the S. hominis isolate exhibited resistance to 4 antimicrobial agents (Table 2).

The reactions of the Streptococcus isolates to selected antibiotics (penicillin, ampicillin, and chloramphenicol) were evaluated according to CLSI, while tetracycline and vancomycin were assessed using both CLSI and EUCAST criteria. All isolates were resistant to penicillin and ampicillin, whereas susceptibility rates were 33.33% for tetracycline and 22.22% for vancomycin. For other antimicrobials without established breakpoints, results are reported as inhibition zone diameters. Overall, isolates showed resistance to several agents, including bacitracin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, and nalidixic acid, while larger inhibition zones (15–30 mm) were observed for carbapenems and cefoperazone. MDR analysis revealed variable resistance patterns, including resistance to 6 antimicrobial agents in S. canis and resistance ranging from 7 to 12 antimicrobial agents in the S. hyovaginalis isolates (Table 2).

Statistical analysis results

The association between culture-positivity and animal species was evaluated using the Fisher’s Exact Test. Culture-positivity was observed in 17 of 53 cats (32.08%) and 3 of 6 dogs (50%), with no statistically significant difference between species (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The relationship between culture-positivity and animal species and gender was evaluated using the Fisher’s Exact Test or the Chi-Square Test. Among male animals, 9 of 22 (40.91%) were culture-positive, while 11 of 37 females (29.73%) were positive. Although culture positivity appeared higher in males, no statistically significant difference was observed (p > 0.05). In cats, culture positivity was detected in 8 of 19 males (42.11%) and 9 of 34 females (26.47%), with no significant difference between genders (p > 0.05). In dogs, culture positivity was detected in 1 of 3 males (33.33%) and 2 of 3 females (66.67%), with no significant difference between genders (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The relationship between culture-positivity and age was evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U Test, and no statistically significant association was found between age and culture-positivity (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Culture-positivity was also evaluated according to breed using the Chi-Square Test. Culture-positivity was higher in the Siamese cat (40.91%). However, no statistically significant association was observed between breed and culture-positivity (p > 0.05). In dogs, culture positivity was detected in 3 of 4 crossbred dogs (75.0%), which was significantly higher compared with other groups (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Among the 32 bacterial isolates obtained from cats, 27 (84.4%) were classified as MDR. No significant associations were found between MDR status and host demographic variables, including gender, breed, and age group (p > 0.05). In dogs, all isolates (n = 5, 100%) were MDR-positive, therefore, statistical comparisons were not performed (Table 2).

Discussion

Conjunctivitis is a common inflammatory ocular disorder in companion animals with a multifactorial etiology, including infectious and non-infectious causes [26, 27]. The causative agents vary between species, with viral pathogens such as FHV-1, C. felis, calicivirus, and Mycoplasma spp. being more prominent in cats, whereas bacterial agents, including Staphylococcus spp., E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, are frequently reported in dogs [15, 28, 29]. Host immunity, environmental conditions, and management conditions further influence pathogen distribution [1, 8].

In this study, culture positivity was detected in 32.08% (17/53) of cat samples and 50% (3/6) of dogs, with no statistically significant difference between species (p > 0.05). Multiple bacterial isolates in a subset of animals, 9.43% of cats and 16.67% of dogs, suggesting that polymicrobial involvement may contribute to conjunctivitis pathogenesis. However, the limited canine sample size may have influenced statistical power. Similar variability in bacterial isolation rates has been reported in previous studies, reflecting differences in geographic region, diagnostic methods, prior antimicrobial exposure, and the presence of concurrent ocular or systemic diseases [1, 8, 30]. No associations were found between culture positivity and host sex and age (p > 0.05). Although a higher age was observed in male animals, this likely reflects population structure rather than a biological relationship. Interestingly, higher culture positivity in crossbreed dogs (p < 0.05) may be related to environmental exposure, management differences, or genetic diversity, however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the small number of dogs included.

All samples were negative for Mycoplasma spp., anaerobic bacteria, and fungal agents. These findings are consistent with the relatively low frequency of these organisms in conjunctival infections and may also reflect the fastidious nature of certain pathogens and limitations of routine culture methods [28, 29, 31]. Therefore, the absence of these organisms in culture does not necessarily exclude their involvement in conjunctival disease.

In the present study, MALDI TOF-MS enabled rapid and reliable species-level identification of bacterial isolates with a high-confidence score (≥ 2.0), resulting in the identification of 16 bacterial species. Previous studies have demonstrated that MALDI-TOF MS is a rapid, accurate, and cost-effective tool for microbial identification, providing reliable species-level discrimination in both human and veterinary microbiology laboratories [29, 32–35]. The present findings further support its utility in veterinary ophthalmology.

The most frequently isolated species from feline and canine conjunctivitis cases were S. epidermidis, C. mucifaciens, and S. lutetiensis; which have been described as members of the conjunctival microflora or opportunistic pathogens [29, 32–35]. Furthermore, the isolation of species with zoonotic potential, including P. aeruginosa, S. canis, and E. faecium, from both cats and dogs highlights the importance of evaluating conjunctival infections within a One Health framework. Companion animals can act as reservoirs of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria and may contribute to bidirectional transmission between animals and humans. Consequently, surveillance of ocular pathogens and their antimicrobial resistance profiles is relevant not only for veterinary medicine but also for public health [36, 37].

Several bacterial species identified in the present study, including B. muris, P. sanguinis, P. lutiphocae, and S. hyovaginalis, are rarely reported in ocular infections [38, 39]. Their identification reflects the improved species-level resolution provided by MALDI-TOF MS. Although these organisms are generally regarded as environmental, commensal, or opportunistic bacteria, the presence of MDR phenotypes in several isolates suggest that they may act as opportunistic pathogens under favorable host or environmental conditions. Further molecular and epidemiological studies are needed to clarify their clinical relevance.

The antimicrobial susceptibility profiles obtained in the present study revealed a high prevalence of MDR phenotypes among conjunctival bacterial isolates of cats and dogs. Compared with previous reports of companion animal ocular infections, the overall MDR rate and resistance to commonly used antimicrobial classes such as β-lactams, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides were consistent with the increasing trend of antimicrobial resistance reported in veterinary ophthalmology [40–43]. These findings reinforce the importance of routine culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing before empirical treatment.

Species-specific comparisons with the literature revealed both similarities and differences. Staphylococcus spp. demonstrated complete resistance to penicillin, consistent with previous reports describing widespread β-lactam resistance among ocular staphylococci in dogs and cats [43]. However, relatively higher susceptibility to gentamicin, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole compared to earlier reports [29, 43] may reflect regional or population-related differences in antimicrobial use and selection pressure.

Streptococcus spp. also exhibited complete resistance to penicillin and ampicillin, contrasting with previous reports of variable susceptibility in feline and canine conjunctivitis [41, 43]. The MDR profile of S. hyovaginalis, with resistance to 7–12 antimicrobial agents, suggests the presence of highly resistant strains ocular strains.

For E. coli, resistance to penicillins and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was consistent with previous reports in companion animal [44], whereas carbapenems retained in vitro activity, supporting their role as reserve agents in severe infections [45, 46]. In contrast, P. aeruginosa demonstrated extensive resistance to most tested antimicrobials, consistent with previous reports describing this species as an opportunistic ocular pathogen with high levels of antimicrobial resistance [40, 41, 43, 47].

Less commonly reported organisms such as B. muris, M. luteus, and Psychrobacter spp. also exhibited variable resistance patterns, including MDR phenotypes. The limited conjunctivitis-specific susceptibility data available for these species highlights the novelty of the present findings and warrants further investigation.

Enterococcus spp. demonstrated higher resistance rates than those previously reported in companion animal conjunctivitis [29], particularly against β-lactams, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, and nalidixic acid. MDR profiles involving resistance to 10–12 antimicrobial agents further suggest their potential role as emerging multidrug-resistant ocular pathogens.

Corynebacterium spp. exhibited resistance patterns that differed from those previously reported in canine ocular infections [30, 40]. Although resistance to tetracycline and vancomycin remained relatively low, MDR profiles involving 4–6 antimicrobial agents, particularly among the C. mucifaciens isolates, suggest that these bacteria may act as reservoirs of antimicrobial resistance in conjunctival infections.

Overall, the observed discrepancies between the present findings and previous reports may reflect differences in geographic region, antimicrobial use, host population, sampling methods, and laboratory protocols. In addition, the lack of standardized interpretive breakpoints for several species limited direct comparison of susceptibility data across studies. The inconsistency between EUCAST and CLSI interpretive criteria for certain antibiotics further highlights the need for harmonized veterinary antimicrobial susceptibility standards [25, 48]. Nevertheless, the high frequency of MDR isolates underscores the importance of evidence-based antimicrobial selection and continued surveillance in veterinary ocular infections.

The high prevalence of MDR detected in the present study is consistent with growing concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance in companion animals worldwide and with previous reports describing MDR phenotypes in bacterial isolates from canine and feline ocular infections [29, 41, 43]. In the present study, MDR was detected in 84.4% of feline isolates, and all canine isolates were MDR-positive; however, no significant associations were observed between MDR status and host demographic variables in cats (gender, breed, age group; p > 0.05), while statistical evaluation was not possible in dogs due to universal MDR positivity. Resistance to 4 to 14 antimicrobial agents highlights the therapeutic challenges posed by conjunctival bacterial infections and underscores the importance of culture-based diagnosis, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, continued surveillance, and harmonization of veterinary antimicrobial susceptibility standards.

Conclusion

This study suggests that differences may exist in the distribution of conjunctival bacterial species between cats and dogs; however, given the limited number of dogs included (n = 6), with only three culture-positive cases, any interpretation regarding host-specific pathogenic differences should be made with caution. Nevertheless, the presence of species such as P. aeruginosa, S. canis, and E. faecium in particular warrants careful evaluation from both a veterinary and public health perspectives due to their zoonotic potential. Furthermore, the simultaneous isolation of multiple bacterial agents highlights the complex nature of conjunctival infections. Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that many isolates carried MDR profiles, suggesting that routine treatment approaches should be reconsidered accordingly. The MALDI TOF-MS method used in this study provided significant advantages, particularly in the rapid and reliable identification of rare and unexpected bacterial species. However, differences in interpretation between different standards, such as EUCAST and CLSI, as well as the lack of established breakpoints for some species, represent limitations in data interpretation. This highlights the need to standardize the testing methods and evaluation criteria used in veterinary diagnostic laboratories. In conclusion, more comprehensive, multicenter, and standardized studies on conjunctival bacterial pathogens and their antimicrobial susceptibility are needed to improve clinical treatment outcomes and to contribute to veterinary public health within the framework of the One Health framework.

Study limitations

The present study was limited by the relatively small number of canine samples, which restricted species-specific statistical analyses. In addition, although MALDI-TOF MS yielded high-confidence identifications, bacterial isolates were not confirmed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Furthermore, direct PCR screening for viral and fastidious bacterial pathogens associated with conjunctivitis was not performed, potentially leading to an underestimation of their role in culture-negative cases. Future studies should combine larger sample sizes with molecular diagnostic approaches to better characterize the etiology of conjunctivitis in dogs and cats.
