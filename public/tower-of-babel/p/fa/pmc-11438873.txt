A multi-epitope protein vaccine encapsulated in alginate nanoparticles as a candidate vaccine against Shigella sonnei
Authors: Parisa Hashemi, Mahmoud Osanloo, Akbar Farjadfar, Mahdi Nasiri-Ghiri, Elham Zarenezhad, Shirin Mahmoodi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractShigellosis, caused by the Gram-negative bacterium Shigella, is a major global health challenge. Despite extensive research over the past two decades, no commercial vaccine is available to prevent Shigella infection. Developing multi-epitope vaccines offers a promising and innovative approach to tackling infectious diseases. In this study, we produced a multi-epitope vaccine candidate using E. coli BL21 (DE3) plysS bacteria and purified the vaccine protein with Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. We then prepared alginate nanoparticles containing the vaccine protein, with a particle size of 122 ± 6 nm, PDI 0.17, SPAN 0.83, and zeta potential of -27 ± 2 mV. Successful protein loading was confirmed through nanodrop and ATR-FTIR analyses. To evaluate the immunogenicity of the encapsulated vaccine, mice were orally vaccinated, and their serum was analyzed for IgG, IL-4, and IFN-γ levels cytokines. The results showed a significant increase in IgG level in the vaccinated group compared to controls. Additionally, the vaccinated group exhibited a notable increase in IL-4 and IFN-γ cytokines, indicating a robust Th-cell-mediated immune response essential for combating Shigella. Our nano-vaccine demonstrated high efficacy in activating both humoral and cellular immunity, effectively protecting against the bacteria. The alginate-based oral vaccine candidate thus emerges as a promising strategy for developing a multi-epitope vaccine candidate against Shigella.Keywords: Shigellosis, Vaccine, Multi-epitope, Alginate nanoparticles, Oral vaccinationSubject terms: Biotechnology, Immunology

Introduction

Shigella is a Gram-negative bacterium from the Enterobacteriaceae family that can contaminate the gastrointestinal tract (GI) of humans and primates, which causes digestive-related problems1. Shigellosis is a disease that leads to significant public health concerns, mainly in low- and middle-income countries. According to the Global Burden of Diseases research conducted in 2016, shigellosis caused 164.7 million cases of diarrhea that year, with 69% of those cases occurring in children under the age of 5 years. Additionally, shigellosis was responsible for 212,438 deaths in 2016, with 63,713 of those deaths occurring in children under the age of 5 years2. More than 50 serotypes of Shigella have been identified, which are categorized into four groups based on their O antigen, containing Shigella flexneri, Shigella dysenteriae, Shigella sonnei, and Shigella boydii3,4. Shigella relies on various types of proteins to survive, reproduce, and attack host cells. Shigella could not colonize the gut without these proteins, invade host cells, and evade the immune system5. These proteins are classified into three categories: the proteins that contribute to bacterial pathogenicity, known as virulence factors6. The structural proteins that form the framework of the bacteria, and the regulatory proteins that control the expression of genes and cellular processes. Developing interventions to prevent and treat the infection can be achieved by understanding these proteins’ functions. One of the essential virulence factors is the Type III Secretion System (T3SS), composed of different protein components7. These components form a needle-like structure that allows the bacteria to inject effector proteins directly into the host cell8. Among the effective proteins, the invasion plasmid antigens (Ipas) have a significant role in the attachment and attack of bacteria to the host cells7,9. Shigella can easily be transmitted orally to healthy people through water, food, or surfaces in contact with feces8. Lack of proper sanitation, clean water, and hygiene practices, particularly among children, are the leading causes of the high prevalence of Shigella infection in impoverished areas10,11. Shigella bacteria enter the body through the mouth, then travel down to the small intestine, attach to the intestine cells, and invade them12. When the cells are filled with bacteria, the membrane breaks, and bacteria spread from cell to cell, causing inflammation and damage to the intestinal lining. The damaged intestine starts leaking water and electrolytes into the stool, leading to diarrhea, the main sign of shigellosis. In addition to diarrhea, Shigella can also cause fever, abdominal cramps, and nausea13. The probability of bacteria entering the blood is very low, but if Shigella toxin enters the bloodstream, it can cause hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS)14.

Antibiotics can kill pathogenic bacteria, shorten the time of the disease, and decrease the risk of transmitting the disease to others. However, serious concerns exist about the extreme usage of antibiotics and the spread of antibiotic-resistant species, especially in recent years15. Shigella strains have become resistant to increasing antibiotics, containing ciprofloxacin, azithromycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and ampicillin16. Based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the prevalence of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Shigella strains in the USA was 5% in 2019 compared to 0% in 2015. It is thus necessary to consider appropriate actions to stop the misuse of antibiotics and direct research towards developing new antibiotics and vaccines17. Vaccination can protect individuals from shigellosis, significantly decrease the rate of infection spread and the occurrence of antibiotic-resistant strains, improve public health and decrease the disease burden on the healthcare system.

Different vaccine platforms have been studied against Shigella spp. over the years, such as live attenuated, inactivated, and conjugated vaccines. Despite these efforts, a licensed vaccine for widespread use is still unavailable. For example, live attenuated Shigella sonnei vaccine candidate WRSS1 showed promise in early-phase clinical trials but faced challenges related to immunogenicity and efficacy18. In another study, inactivated Shigella flexneri 2a vaccine candidate developed by Subhra Chakraborty et al. showed this candidate is safe, and immunogen in the clinical trial phase 1, but its efficacy against wild species of Shigella should be determined. It is also unclear whether the vaccine protects 4 different species of Shigella19. More recently, innovative approaches such as multi-epitope vaccines have gained attention for their potential to elicit robust and broad-spectrum immune responses20.

Since ingestion is the natural route of infection with Shigella strains, and it causes disease mostly in children, trying to make oral vaccines can be a good idea. So far, there have been successful experiences in using oral vaccines against intestinal pathogens such as rotavirus and cholera vaccine21–23. Oral vaccination can stimulate effective immune system response without patients having pain or difficulty receiving multiple doses. In addition, oral injection can stimulate local and systemic immunity and produce significant levels of IgA24–26. However, there are challenges regarding the stability and absorption of oral vaccines in the GI tract. Therefore, efficient technologies are necessary to reduce this method’s challenges27. Using nanocarriers is one of the ways to overcome these challenges.

Previous studies have explored various nanoparticle-based approaches for Shigella vaccine delivery. For instance, poly(dl-lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) nanoparticles have been used for their biocompatibility and controlled release properties28. In another study, chitosan nanoparticles showed promise in improving Shigella vaccine stability and immunogenicity29. Also, a study by AI Camacho et al. demonstrated that using poly(anhydride) nanoparticles for Shigella antigen delivery enhances the vaccine’s protective capacity30.

Alginate is a biocompatible and biodegradable polymer derived from seaweed31. Alginate nanoparticles have distinctive properties that make them a suitable platform for the oral delivery of protein vaccines. Gentle gelation conditions (typically involving aqueous conditions at room temperature and neutral pH, using metal ions such as Ca2+) minimize the risk of protein denaturation32,33. Alginate can be an adjuvant, enhancing the immune response to the encapsulated antigen. Previous studies showed that alginate can activate innate immune cells such as macrophages34,35, and macrophages play a crucial role in defense against Shigella. Besides, alginate has mucoadhesive properties, which can enhance the retention time of the vaccine at mucosal sites36,37. This prolonged exposure allows for a more sustained interaction between the antigen and the mucosal immune cells, potentially leading to a stronger and more durable immune response. Although alginate has been used extensively in many studies for drug delivery, and its role as a drug carrier is well-documented, limited studies have used this delivery system to develop Shigella vaccines38–40.

The development of bioinformatics as a new tool made it possible to design novel multi-epitope vaccines. Multi-epitope vaccines comprise T CD 4 + and T CD8 + epitopes, and adjuvants can target multiple pathogen parts. They have several advantages: (1) the vaccine does not become ineffective during mutations in the pathogen. (2) the level of immunity it creates is incredible and broader because the immune system creates specific antibodies against each type of epitope, and more white blood cells are involved. (3) conserved epitopes of different strains can be used in the design of these vaccines, and they protect people against a wide range of pathogens41,42.

Several different multi-epitope vaccines against Shigella have been developed. Sara Aiman et al. employed a reverse vaccinology approach to design a multi-epitope vaccine based on the outer membrane proteins of Shigella Sonnei. Their study showed that the proposed vaccine construct is expected to be safe and able to trigger strong immune responses against S. sonnei infections43. In another study, Girmay Desalegn et al. developed a multi-epitope vaccine comprising conserved regions of outer membrane proteins (Omps), invasion plasmid antigens (Ipas), and VirG protein. Immunization with this construct elicited significant humoral immune responses in animal models44.

This research aims to produce and encapsulate the multi-epitope vaccine candidate in alginate nanoparticles designed against Shigella spp. by bioinformatics tool in our previous research20. The designed construct is a cross-protective candidate vaccine for Shigella spp, which composed of epitopes from Shigella spp (Shigella flexneri, Shigella dysenteriae, Shigella sonnei, and Shigella boydii) antigens such as Ipa B (7 epitopes), Ipa D (6 epitopes), Omp A (3 epitopes) including the D0 and D1 flagellin domains of Salmonella enterica as the toll-like receptor 5 (TLR5) agonist and a mucosal adjuvant (Fig. 1). The encapsulated vaccine candidate will be used orally in mice, and then the immunogenicity of the vaccine will be tested.

Designed vaccine construct including adjuvant (D0 and D1 domains of flagellin in N and C terminal), selected Helper T cell Lymphocytes (HTL) epitopes from Ipa B, Omp A, and Ipa D antigens which connected by suitable amino acid linkers20.

Materials and methods

Transformation, expression, and purification of the vaccine

The plasmid pET-14b containing the gene of vaccine construct was synthesized in our previous research45 and transferred to the bacterial host E. coli BL21(DE3) plysS using the CaCl2 method46. The expression of the desired protein was performed using the following method: a colony of transformed E. coli BL21(DE3) plysS was inoculated in 50 mL Super Optimal Broth (SOB) culture medium containing ampicillin (100 µg/mL) and was incubated overnight at 37 °C with 180 rpm shaking, then 100 ml of fresh SOB culture medium containing ampicillin was prepared and 1000 µL of overnight culture medium was added and incubated at 37 °C, in 180 rpm. In the optical density of 0.6, at 600 nm wavelength, protein expression was induced using the concentration of 1 mM IPTG for 4, 6, and 8 h. Afterward, the bacterial culture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 7 min, and the bacterial pellet was collected to evaluate the protein expression by 12% SDS-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) technic47. In the designed vaccine construct, a histidine residue (6His-tag) was incorporated; therefore, a Ni-NTA chromatography column (Qiagen Ni-NTA Spin Kit) was utilized to purify the recombinant vaccine candidate. The bacterial cell lysate was prepared using lysis buffer (100 mM NaH2PO4, 7 M Urea, 10 mM Tris-Hcl, pH 8.0) and sonication. The lysate was centrifuged at 3900 rpm for 20 min. Afterward, the supernatant was passed through the chromatography column and washed using washing buffers, including (100 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris-HCl, and 8 M Urea, pH 6.3). The purified protein was collected in the elution solution using elution buffer (100 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris-HCl, and 8 M Urea, pH 4.5) and was evaluated using a 12% SDS-PAGE gel. The Bradford assay method determined the expressed and purified protein concentrations48.

Preparation and characterization of alginate nanoparticles containing vaccine candidate

Alginate nanoparticles were prepared using the ionic gelation technique through electrostatic interaction between hydroxyl groups of alginate and calcium ions of calcium chloride (CaCl2)49. Alginate powder (0.5 w/v%) was first dissolved in the vaccine candidate solution (0.6 mg/ml) containing the target protein on a stirrer (2000 rpm) at room temperature. Different concentrations of CaCl2 (0.1–0.01 w/v%) were then added to the solutions and stirred for 40 min at room temperature to complete the gelation process. The volume ratio of alginate and calcium chloride solution was 1:1; the final protein concentration in the nanoparticles was thus 0.3 mg/ml.

The size and zeta potential of the obtained particles were calculated (in triplicate) by applying a DLS-type apparatus (SZ-100, Horiba, Japan). Nanoparticles with a mean particle size of < 200 nm, PDI < 0.7, and SPAN < 1 are considered optimum size characteristics50. Only one sample with 0.5% alginate and 0.03% CaCl2 showed the optimum size characteristics. Free alginate nanoparticles were also prepared like the optimum sample but without protein.

Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) was used to confirm vaccine candidate loading in alginate nanoparticles qualitatively. Spectra of vaccine candidate, free alginate nanoparticles, and alginate nanoparticles containing vaccine candidate were recorded at 400–3900 cm−1 (Bruker Co., model Tensor II, and Germany). Besides, the encapsulation efficacy of alginate nanoparticles was investigated using the indirect method51. The prepared alginate nanoparticles containing the vaccine candidate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min to precipitate the nanoparticles. The remaining protein in the supernatant was measured with nanodrop, and encapsulation efficacy was calculated using the following equation.

Encapsulation efficacy (%) = (vaccine candidate added – vaccine candidate in supernatant) / vaccine candidate added × 100.

Immunization of mice

Animal experiments were approved using the ethics committee of Fasa University of Medical Sciences (ethic code: IR.FUMS.REC.1400.152). All experiments followed standard animal guidelines and regulations, and the authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under ketamine-xylazine anesthesia.

Overall, 24 six to eight-week-old female BALB/C mice were prepared from the Comparative Medicine Center, Shiraz University of Medical Sciences.

Mice were divided into four groups randomly. The first group received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The second group received the vaccine candidate, and the third group received alginate nanoparticles. The fourth group received alginate nanoparticles containing vaccine candidate. The vaccination protocol consisted of oral administration of three doses, each dose containing 200 µl with the concentration of 0.3 mg/ml. We held the mice’s head in place by gently extending the head back; then, the gavage tube was placed in the diatom of the mouth. The tube gently advanced along the upper palate until the esophagus was reached.

These doses were administered at two-week intervals on days 0, 14, and 28. Before each vaccination, mice suffered a 12-hour fasting period during which they had access to water only. On the 56th day, each group was randomly divided into two subgroups; one group was investigated to measure humoral and cellular immunity, and another was used for the challenge study.

IgG serum detection

On the 56th day, blood samples were taken from the hearts of all four mice groups to assess the humoral immune response and were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The serum was separated to measure the antibody titer. Total IgG was calculated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)52.

Cytokine secretion

To assess cellular immune response, the spleen was separated from mice under aseptic conditions and was mechanically crushed into small pieces and then homogenized to create mononuclear cell suspension. The cell suspension was centrifuged with Ficoll; splenocytes were carefully collected and washed after centrifugation. The isolated splenocytes were cultured in a 96-well plate containing RPMI medium with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% concentration of penicillin and streptomycin antibiotics for stimulating of cells, 10 µl of purified vaccine candidate was added to the culture medium. The plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 incubator for 72 h. The ELISA method calculated the IFN-γ and IL-4 cytokines titer52.

Challenge assay

A challenge assay was performed to evaluate the protective efficacy of nano-vaccine. Mice were orally exposed to Shigella sonnei strain ATCC-9290 (1 × 107 CFU/mouse) in a total volume of 50 µL and checked for 30 days53.

Statistical analysis

A one-way ANOVA was used for the statistical analysis of the data. For comparisons, a P value < 0.05 was measured as significant for all tests. The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 9 Software (Version 5.04, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was applied for graphical presentations.

Results

Expression and purification of the recombinant vaccine

The pET-14b plasmid containing gene vaccine construct was successfully transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) plysS strain. The optimum protein expression was achieved at 8 h after inducing with the concentration of 1 mM IPTG. Protein purification was performed using a Ni-NTA chromatography column and was validated by 12% SDS-PAGE gel. The band of the vaccine candidate was about 42.5 kDa, following the molecular weight of the designed vaccine construct as calculated in silico20 (Fig. 2). Also, the expressed and purified protein concentrations were calculated to be 4.2 mg/ml and 0.6 mg/ml respectively by the Bradford assay method.

Particle size analyses of the protein-free state of this sample (free alginate nanoparticles) were obtained as particle size 10 ± 2 nm, PDI 0.9, and SPAN 0.23. ATR-FTIR of A: pure Alginate, B: free alginate nanoparticles, and C: alginate nanoparticles containing vaccine candidate.

The ATR-FTIR spectra of free alginate nanoparticles (Fig. 5B) displayed a broad peak between 3300 and 3713 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups in water and alginate due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding. The peaks at 2900 cm−1 can be connected to the stretching vibration of CH. The characteristic band at 1622 cm−1 is connected to C = O. The stretching vibration at 1595 and 1463 cm−1 related to the symmetric and asymmetric carbonyl groups due to monovalent ions (sodium) displacing the proton. These peaks confirmed the increase in hydrogen bonding. The vibration at 1156 and 1073 cm−1 can be attributed to the presence of C-O groups.

The ATR-FTIR spectra of alginate nanoparticles containing vaccine candidate (Fig. 5C) revealed the broadband at about 3400–3700 cm−1, attributed to OH groups, due to hydrogen bonding in vaccine candidate, alginate, and water. The bands at 3205 and 3355 cm−1 can be related to the stretching vibration of –NH groups. The stretching vibration at 2985 cm−1 corresponded to C-H and the vibration at 1622 cm−1 related to the carbonyl group in vaccine candidate. The spectra at 1593 and 1459 cm−1 are allocated to symmetric and asymmetric C = O groups. The strong characteristic band at 1071 cm−1 is related to the reaction between calcium ion and carboxyl (CO-Ca-CO group structure), which increased C-O vibration. This characteristic band displayed ionic crosslinking56,57.

The spectra show a minor variation in the frequency and width of the peaks between alginate nanoparticles containing vaccine candidate and free alginate nanoparticles. When the spectra were examined, it was discovered that there had been a slight change in the peaks of the amino, carboxyl, and amide groups. This suggested that the vaccine candidate amino groups and the alginate carbonyl groups interacted ionically58. A similar study by Mitrevej et al. (2001) observed that carboxylate groups’ peaks are present at the wavenumber area of 1000–1400 cm−1, as in the current study59.

Discussion

Shigellosis is an infectious disease caused by different strains of Shigella bacteria. This bacteria mainly affects children under 5 years, although it can cause disease at any age; because of the low infectious dose of the bacteria, the rate of its spread is high. The best way to prevent this disease is to observe hygiene, but accessing health facilities, water, and healthy food in deprived areas is impossible. Unfortunately, many people get infected with this disease and die every year60,61.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), many Shigella infections are highly resistant to antibiotics. In 2022, 5% of reported Shigella cases were extensively drug-resistant (XDR), compared to none in 2015. XDR Shigella strains can also spread their antibiotic resistance to other bacteria. To prevent this problem and its consequences, vaccination is essential. One of the strategies that recently received attention in the vaccine development field is multi-epitope vaccines62. Multi-epitope vaccine candidates are composed of epitopes of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, T helper, and B lymphocytes, which have been used against pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus63, Helicobacter pylori64, Vibrio cholera65, and Salmonella66. In 2018, Farhani et al. designed a new multi-epitope vaccine candidate for Shigella bacteria using highly conserved antigens among Shigella spp. including Ipa B, Ipa D, and Omp A, as well as an adjuvant using bioinformatics tools; therefore, the designed construct is a cross-protective candidate vaccine against Shigella spp.

The immunological and physicochemical properties of the vaccine were calculated, and the results showed that the designed vaccine is a potent immunogen and non-allergenic20. In the current research, the immunogenicity of the designed vaccine was assessed in vivo.

In the structure of the current vaccine, flagellin domains as a mucosal adjuvant were used, so the usual method of administering it can be through the mucosal route (nasally or orally). Mucosal vaccines have shown great potential to fight infectious diseases because they can target pathogens at the point of entry into the body and prevent them from colonizing67. Several studies in the past have evaluated the immunogenicity of the Shigella vaccine through intranasal mucosa52,68. The results showed that this route stimulated the immune system well, and protection against Shigella was established52,68. In previous research, our designed vaccine was also produced and evaluated via intranasal and subcutaneous routes. Based on the results, both humoral and cellular immune responses were stimulated in mice45. Since Shigella infection occurs naturally through the mouth, a vaccine candidate was administered orally to mice to measure the induced immune response in the current study. It was acknowledged that administering vaccines orally poses a challenge due to the severe conditions of the digestive tract69,70. To address these issues, we utilized the potential of nanotechnology. Encapsulating soluble antigens with nanocarriers can significantly enhance their stability, bioavailability, and immunogenicity71–73. Therefore, an effective vaccine carrier is required for successful oral administration. Studies have shown that alginate has properties such as adjuvanticity and mucoadhesive, making it an ideal carrier35,36,74–76. This study used alginate, a natural polymer, for vaccine delivery. Similarly, in Chuang Xu and colleagues’ research, alginate was used to deliver the oral Grass carp reovirus (GCRV) vaccine. Other groups used another polymer particles as vaccine carrier such as silica nanoparticles77, alginate-chitosan combination78, chitosan and its derivatives29,79,80, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose phthalate (HPMCP)81, etc.

The carrier size directly impacts how well the vaccine is absorbed and triggers an immune response. Smaller particles boast a larger surface area compared to their volume. This increased surface area allows more significant interaction with the gut lining (intestinal epithelium), facilitating absorption and transport across the intestinal wall82. As a result, this improves the bioavailability and delivery of the vaccine to immune cells49,82. Particles smaller than 200 nm are effectively absorbed and transported in intestinal cells83. The current study used the ionic gelation method to make alginate nanoparticles containing protein with a particle size of 122 ± 6 nm, PDI 0.17, and SPAN 0.83. In nanoparticle synthesis or preparation, achieving a narrow size distribution is critical for many applications. Two key metrics used to assess the size distribution of nanoparticles are PDI and SPAN. Suitable values for these metrics PDI below 0.7 and SPAN below 1 are crucial to achieving a narrow size distribution. This uniformity enhances nanoparticles’ consistency, functionality, performance, reliability, and reproducibility in various applications84,85.

The zeta potential of the nanoparticles was − 27 mV, which means that the necessary repulsion between the nanoparticles was achieved to prevent aggregation86. Another critical parameter in the nanoencapsulation process is the encapsulation efficiency, which determines how much of the desired protein has been successfully encapsulated inside the nanoparticles. In the present study, encapsulation efficiency achieved 69%, indicating desirable loading. This is consistent with Gilvand et al. chitosan-MxiH recombinant vaccine candidate29 and Wei et al. Hepatitis E vaccine87.

In vaccine development, the aim is to activate the same immune pathway that the body naturally uses to fight and eradicate pathogens. A successful Shigella vaccine must induce cell-mediated and humoral immunity88,89. To combat Shigella, cooperation between cell-mediated and humoral immunity is vital; however, previous research has identified that specific cells have a crucial role in the fight against disease, including T helper cells, macrophages, and NK cells90–93. The ELISA test results, which measure the total amount of IgG antibodies, indicate a noteworthy increase in antibody titer in the test group that received the nano-coated vaccine compared to the control groups. This proposes that the humoral immunity is effectively activated. Furthermore, the findings demonstrate that the antibody titer in the mice immunized with nanoparticles containing the vaccine was significantly higher than the group that received pure vaccine protein.

Cell-mediated immune response was also assessed by measuring IL-4 (Th2 representative cytokines) and interferon-gamma (Th1 representative cytokines) levels. Previous studies have demonstrated that CD4 + T cells and their secreted cytokines, including IL-4 and IFN-γ, are essential in combating and eradicating Shigella91,94,95.

Additionally, cytokines evaluation results revealed that groups immunized with a nano-formulated vaccine produced considerably higher levels of IFN-γ and IL-4 than the control groups. Our findings align with existing literature indicating that higher levels of IgG antibodies, INF-γ, and IL-4 cytokines are associated with protection against Shigella infection post-vaccination29,52,95–97. Ensuring that the cytokines respond specifically to the vaccine antigen is crucial. We measured the production of cytokines in contact with the antigen (vaccine candidate) and the culture medium alone (as a control). The results revealed a noteworthy difference between the amounts of secreted cytokines after cell stimulation with the antigen and uninduced cells (control) (Fig. 7). This directly indicates the effect of the vaccine on the production of considered cytokines.

Moreover, alginate has some unique properties that make it an outstanding platform for the delivery of proteins. Due to mild gelation, the risk of protein degradation is reduced. Furthermore, alginate can assist as protection from degradation till protein release32. The significant increase in the level of IL-4, IFN-γ, the IgG titer, and observation of symptoms of infection in the test group mice compared to control groups confirmed that the protein was intact in vivo upon release.

Encapsulating the protein vaccine candidate resulted in a significant increase in IFN-γ compared to IL-4. This phenomenon might be attributed to several factors. One potential mechanism is the enhanced uptake of antigens by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) due to encapsulation. Macrophages, a type of APC, are known producers of IFN-γ. Therefore, increased antigen uptake by macrophages could lead to a subsequent rise in IFN-γ production98.

Additionally, nano-vaccines increase the cross-presentation of APCs to activate T CD8+ Cell responses, in which T CD8+ cells secrete IFN-γ99. Also, the characteristics of alginate nanoparticles, including adjuvanticity, controlled release of the antigen, and prolonged exposure to the antigen, further promote Th1-type response, which causes IFN-γ production71. Th1 responses are crucial for combating intracellular pathogens such as Shigella100. The production of cytokines such as IFN-γ characterizes these responses. Our results confirmed that encapsulating the vaccine switched the response towards Th1.

This study used Shigella sonnei ATCC-9290 (1 × 107 CFU/mouse) for challenge assay via oral administration. Our choice of the oral challenge route is particularly relevant for studying enteric pathogens like Shigella, as it more closely mimics the natural route of infection in humans.

The control groups exhibited the main symptoms of shigellosis, including diarrhea, weight loss, weakness, and reduction in mobility, while immunized mice with nano-vaccine remained active and maintained normal health. This indicates that the nano protein candidate vaccine protected Shigella symptoms. Some factors, such as the strain and dose used and the administration route, can make our results different from those of previous studies101,102. However, mortality was not observed in control groups, but diarrhea is a hallmark symptom of shigellosis. Control groups developed diarrhea; in contrast, vaccinated mice in the test group showed no diarrhea (Fig. 9), demonstrating the vaccine’s effectiveness in preventing gastrointestinal pathology. Additionally, the graph of weight loss of mice after exposure to Shigella sonnei is shown in Fig. 8; mice in the control groups exhibited significant weight loss following the oral challenge, whereas vaccinated mice maintained their weight, indicating better overall health and resistance to infection. Our data showed significant differences in symptom severity between the control and vaccinated groups, indicating the vaccine’s protective effect.

Conclusion

This study explored a novel oral multi-epitope vaccine candidate against Shigella delivered via alginate nanoparticles. The encapsulated vaccine effectively activated both humoral and cellular immunity in mice. Vaccinated mice exhibited a significant rise in IgG level compared to controls, indicating a robust antibody response. Elevated levels of IL-4 and IFN-γ, key Th1 and Th2 cytokines, suggest a well-balanced immune response crucial for fighting Shigella. These findings demonstrate the potential of alginate nanoparticle-based oral multi-epitope vaccines as a promising strategy for developing a Shigella vaccine.
