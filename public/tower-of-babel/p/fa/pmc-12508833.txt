The Clinical Relevance of Immunopsychiatric Treatment: Lessons From a Transdiagnostic Case Series on Young People
Authors: Luca Sforzini, Carmine M. Pariante
Published: 2025

Immunopsychiatry, building on decades of evidence that has linked immune dysregulation to mental illness, is emerging as a key avenue toward precision medicine in psychiatry (1). In particular, targeting immune mechanisms in individuals with well-defined clinical profiles and biological markers suggestive of immune involvement may offer substantial therapeutic benefits.

In their extended case series published in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science, Scott et al. (2) provide valuable preliminary data toward operationalizing this approach. They adopt a pragmatic and phenotypically informed diagnostic framework that combines clinical presentation with biological markers. With the limitation of a case series, their findings offer persuasive evidence that immune-targeting treatments can produce substantial benefit in a subset of adolescents and young adults with suspected neuroimmune-mediated major mood or psychotic syndromes.

The field must now move beyond broad correlational findings toward identifying and treating more discrete, clinically and biologically defined immune-related phenotypes.

A first strength of the article is the focus on a youth cohort (ages 12–30 years), encompassing adolescence through early adulthood. This developmental window is critical because it represents the peak period for the onset of major psychiatric disorders. Intervening during this stage offers an opportunity not only to alleviate current symptoms but also to alter long-term trajectories of illness, functioning, and treatment burden, as well as to better understand the biological changes associated with specific clinical presentations. The immune system is among the multiple biological systems that undergo significant plasticity during this period and plays a crucial role in the maturation of neural circuits (3).

These considerations are particularly relevant when we consider biological data, and youth-focused immunopsychiatry has the potential to uncover specific mechanisms underlying the onset and development of psychopathology. While some evidence exists in youth populations, these populations remain underexplored and underreported compared with adult samples, as highlighted in a recent scoping review from our group focused on 11- to 18-year-old adolescents (4). As a result, immune-related markers have primarily been studied in adults and have only secondarily been translated into adolescent mental health research. This likely represents a missed opportunity to investigate how immune mechanisms influence brain development and behavior during a critical period for both the etiopathogenesis and pathophysiology of psychiatric illness.

A second strength is the multimodal diagnostic approach, which combines objective immune markers with immune-related clinical phenotypes. It is widely accepted that, despite the high prevalence of immune dysregulations in multiple psychiatric populations, these are not observable in all individuals. Rather, such abnormalities appear to characterize a specific subgroup of patients with shared clinical and biological features. In fact, the article provides clinical operational criteria to identify a transdiagnostic immune subtype of illness that spans mood and psychotic disorders and is grounded in immune-related clinical indicators.

In particular, the authors pragmatically defined clinically positive neuroimmune-mediated neuropsychiatric syndromes (NIMNPS) by the presence of core clinical features (marked psychomotor disturbance, major neurological signs, cognitive dysfunction, and profound sleep disruption), in combination with specific illness course patterns (such as treatment nonresponse), all of which are suggestive of immune involvement. This structured definition enables the identification of immune-driven pathology that would otherwise evade traditional diagnostic pathways.

Importantly, the authors take a further step by complementing their clinical criteria with in-depth objective immune-related biological examinations, including blood-based markers, auto-antibodies, cerebrospinal fluid, electroencephalography, and neuroimaging. The integration of such biological data into psychiatric assessment is increasingly being recognized as essential for advancing precision psychiatry and moving beyond traditional symptom-based classifications toward a biology-informed framework (5). Notably, while these markers were not directly included in the selection process, 91% of individuals had elevated nonspecific immune markers, and 93% had central nervous system abnormalities. The high prevalence of immune abnormalities in this clinically defined cohort further supports the idea that immune-mediated pathophysiology is underrecognized in psychiatric populations and demonstrates the need for biological investigations aligned with clinical profiles.

This approach is essential to advance immunopsychiatric research. Although there is clear causal evidence supporting the role of immune processes in disrupting specific neurocircuits and associated behaviors, many immunopsychiatric trials have failed to demonstrate the efficacy of immune-targeting treatments, also because they failed to select participants with demonstrable immune alterations (6). Some evidence suggests that these treatments are most effective in individuals with higher levels of inflammation, measured for example with C-reactive protein (7,8). However, the role of the immune system in mental health is complex, involving multiple interacting pathways with predisposing, protective, and compensatory mechanisms (9). This complexity makes it difficult to use single markers and calls for more advanced and complex stratification strategies. Additionally, most trials have failed to consider and assess immune-related behavioral symptoms, an issue that the authors address in their article. Another common limitation is the lack of confirmation of target engagement, which weakens the ability to establish causal associations between clinical outcomes and underlying biological mechanisms.

Perhaps the most impactful strength of this study lies in its real-world clinical implications. The authors report notable improvements in symptoms and functioning, which is encouraging given that most participants had a severe illness that did not respond to conventional treatments. This clearly represents a clinically relevant population for whom new and alternative treatment strategies are urgently needed.

Among the 45 participants, 43 received personalized immune-targeted treatments, although not in the narrow sense of matching specific drugs to specific molecular signatures. Treatment decisions were based on clinical evaluation and tailored to a stratified subgroup with immune-related illness, an important and pragmatic first step. This resulted in an 88% clinical response rate (Clinical Global Impression-Improvement), as well as reductions in psychiatric hospitalizations (from 93% to 7%) and polypharmacy (from 84% to 9%). Functional measures also improved, with Social and Occupational Functioning Assessment Scale scores increasing by over 25 points within 6 to 12 months and “not in employment, education, or training” status decreasing from 81% to 7%.

These findings support the validity of immune targets in psychiatry and, although preliminary, provide encouraging real-world evidence that immune-targeted therapy may be effective in a well-defined subgroup, underscoring the need for more tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

There were no significant differences between participants with autoimmune encephalitis (40%) and patients with atypical mood or psychotic syndromes (60%) in clinical features or illness course, suggesting shared immunopathology and similar therapeutic effects despite different diagnoses. The strongest outcome predictor was shorter time from presentation to immunotherapy initiation, highlighting the importance of early intervention, especially in youth. The 72% relapse rate after stopping treatment suggests that prolonged immune modulation may be needed, similar to treatment strategies in other immune-related diseases.

The insights of Scott et al.’s (2) case series should be weighed against some limitations. The focus on a highly selected population with NIMNPS limits generalizability to broader psychiatric populations. The lack of randomization and absence of a comparison group that received standard care necessarily restrict conclusions about treatment specificity. The predominance of female patients (93%) likely reflects sex differences in immune-related disorders and their psychiatric manifestations but limits applicability to male youth. Future studies should confirm and expand these findings using more granular clinical outcome measures. Lastly, the authors acknowledge that the immunopathology underlying these syndromes remains controversial, emphasizing the need for further research to identify and validate reliable, clinically actionable biomarkers linked to specific scientifically based presentations.

Even with these caveats, this study lays important groundwork for symptom- and mechanism-based stratification strategies in immunopsychiatry. The authors’ clinical criteria offer a useful foundation for identifying individuals who may benefit from targeted treatment. While these criteria may not apply universally, future research should seek to replicate and refine them across diverse populations, integrating them with biological evidence. The overall strategy presents a promising framework that could pave the way for more tailored use of anti-inflammatories in psychiatric conditions. This advanced stratification approach forms the basis of a recently funded project that our group is conducting, focusing on the efficacy of anti-inflammatory treatments in people with depression stratified by clinical, biological, and neuroimaging markers (10). Notably, severe depression was present in 80% of the case series sample, highlighting the potential relevance of immune-targeting strategies in this population.

Immunopsychiatric treatments may be beneficial where there is immune dysregulation. Although future research may not confirm a direct causal link between inflammation and the efficacy (and real-world effectiveness) of immune-targeting treatments, the emerging evidence base justifies cautious optimism. Nevertheless, only well-designed controlled trials can confirm whether immune dysfunction can be reliably targeted in psychiatric care.
