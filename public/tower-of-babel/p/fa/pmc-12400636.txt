Impact of stopping Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate at various times on elevated postpartum ALT levels and breastfeeding in pregnant women with chronic hepatitis B: a prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Siying Li, Yi Jiang, Jinfeng Shi, Xiaoxian Jiang, Yinghua Li, Zhiyuan Ma, Jie Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the impact of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) withdrawal timing on elevated postpartum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels and breastfeeding in pregnant women who received TDF therapy to prevent mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection.MethodsAll enrolled women began treatment with TDF during weeks 24–32 of pregnancy and were divided into three groups that stopped TDF at delivery (Group A), at 4 weeks postpartum (Group B), or at 12 weeks postpartum (Group C). The biochemical and virological markers of hepatitis B were regularly measured and compared. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to explore the risk factors of elevated ALT levels.ResultsElevated postpartum ALT levels were observed in 91 of the 160 patients, including 38 out of 59 (64.41%) in Group A, 27 out of 51 (52.94%) in Group B, and 26 out of 50 (54%) in Group C. The incidence of elevated ALT levels did not differ significantly among the three groups (P = 0.338). Moreover, there was no significant difference in the severity of elevated ALT levels among the three groups (P = 0.558). Binary logistic regression analysis showed that ALT at delivery was an independent risk factor for elevated ALT levels (odds ratio = 1.098, 95% confidence interval, 1.039–1.160, P = 0.001). ROC analysis identified ALT ≥ 23 U/L at delivery as the optimal cutoff (AUC = 0.703, 95% CI: 0.622–0.785) for predicting elevated postpartum ALT levels. Breastfeeding rates were significantly higher in Group A (79.66%) compared to Group B (25.49%) and Group C (42%), both P values = 0.000.ConclusionsWhen TDF is used to prevent MTCT of HBV, withdrawal timing did not significantly affect overall ALT elevation patterns. However, early TDF withdrawal at delivery was associated with higher breastfeeding rates, and mothers with ALT levels ≥ 23 U/L at delivery may benefit from extended postpartum antiviral therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08078-x.Keywords: Antiviral therapy, Chronic hepatitis B, Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, Mother-to-child transmission, Elevated ALT, Breastfeed

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a global public health problem, affecting approximately 240 million people worldwide [1]. Mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) is the major route of transmission. In China, approximately 5.5% (4.9–11.8%) of women of childbearing age (20–49 years) are positive for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) [2–5]. Immunoprophylaxis protects > 90% of newborns from HBV infection [6]; however, 5–7% still develop chronic hepatitis B (CHB) from highly viremic mothers [7]. Antiviral treatment for mothers with high HBV DNA levels during pregnancy is an effective and economic strategy to avoid MTCT. Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) is recommended as the first-line antiviral therapy (AVT) due to its efficacy, low resistance rate, and high safety profile during pregnancy and breastfeeding [8–10].

To prevent MTCT, TDF treatment should be initiated during the second or third trimester of pregnancy [5]. While global guidelines agree on discontinuing antiviral prophylaxis in the early postpartum period, their specific timing recommendations differ [8, 10–13]. For example, the European Association for the Study of the Liver and the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases recommend discontinuing TDF either at delivery or within 12 weeks postpartum [8, 10]. In contrast, in Taiwan, it is advised to stop TDF 1 month after delivery [11], while in Chinese mainland it is recommended to stop TDF either at delivery or within 1–3 months postpartum [13]. The World Health Organization merely indicates that AVT to block MTCT can be stopped at delivery or later [12]. This clinical flexibility reflects the need for individualized decision-making based on patient-specific factors.

A previous study showed that postpartum elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels are more common in patients with than without HBV infection [14]. Several studies compared the incidence of postpartum hepatitis in women with CHB who were and were not treated with antiviral drugs. In these studies, the different time points at which the drug was stopped after delivery may have influenced the likelihood of development of postpartum ALT flare [15–17]. However, no studies to date have systematically compared the three main drug withdrawal timings recommended by the guidelines. In addition, HBV-infected mothers’ breastfeeding needs are often overlooked. In earlier studies conducted before widespread adoption of antiviral use during pregnancy, women receiving antivirals were often advised not to breastfeed due to limited safety data [18–20]. While current guidelines support breastfeeding during TDF based on its established safety profile [5, 8, 10, 12, 13], the practical implementation and acceptance of this recommendation in real-world clinical settings remain uncertain.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective study with the following objectives: (1) to evaluate the impact of TDF withdrawal timing on elevated postpartum ALT levels in women receiving TDF for HBV MTCT prophylaxis, and (2) to evaluate the association between TDF withdrawal timing and breastfeeding outcomes in this population. We examined three guideline-recommended withdrawal timings: at delivery, at 4 weeks postpartum and 12 weeks postpartum, comparing the characteristics of elevated ALT levels and breastfeeding rates among the three groups. To capture potential late elevated ALT levels, follow-up was extended to 48 weeks postpartum.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted in Hangzhou First People’s Hospital. Pregnant women with CHB were enrolled from July 2017 to December 2020. This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou First People’s Hospital (2017 Research Medical Review No. 116-01), and all participants provided written informed consent before their enrollment.

The eligibility criteria were an age of 20–40 years, more than 6 months positivity for HBsAg and hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), an HBV DNA level of > 200,000 IU/mL during weeks 24–32 of pregnancy, an ALT level below the upper limit of normal (ULN) (40 U/L), and receipt of TDF for MTCT blockade during pregnancy. The exclusion criteria were receipt of any anti-HBV treatment prior to or during pregnancy (except for antivirals administered to prevent MTCT during a previous pregnancy), coinfection with other viruses (e.g., human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis C virus, hepatitis E virus), presence of other liver diseases (e.g., fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis), evidence of hepatocellular carcinoma or fibrosis/cirrhosis, long-term medication use during pregnancy and postpartum, fetal malformation identified by three-dimensional ultrasound and a history of congenital malformation or congenital genetic disease in a previous pregnancy.

All eligible women initiated treatment with a single 300-mg tablet of TDF (Viread; Gilead Sciences, Foster City, CA, USA) once daily between 24 and 32 weeks of gestation. They received standardized counseling from specialists, confirming that breastfeeding is permitted during TDF therapy according to current guidelines, and were actively encouraged to breastfeed. They were divided into three groups based on their willingness to stop TDF at delivery (Group A), at 4 weeks postpartum (Group B), or at 12 weeks postpartum (Group C).

Biochemical and virological markers of hepatitis B, including the ALT, total bilirubin, creatinine, and HBV DNA levels, were monitored at various time points: at enrollment, 4 weeks after enrollment, at delivery, and 6 ± 2, 12, 18 ± 2, 24, 36, and 48 weeks postpartum. Anti-HBc IgM was not tested in this study, and postpartum hepatitis B exacerbation were assessed based on repeated ALT measurements (retested within 2–4 weeks if ALT > 40 U/L). Infants were administered a 100-IU dose of intramuscular hepatitis B immunoglobulin and an initial 10-µg dose of HBV vaccine within 6 h of birth, with subsequent doses given at 1 and 6 months postpartum. The HBsAg, anti-HBs, and/or HBV DNA levels were assessed at 1 month after full immunization, which typically occurred at approximately 7 months of age but could extend to 12 months of age in cases of delayed vaccination.

Elevated ALT levels was defined as an ALT level exceeding the upper limit of normal (ULN), which is 40 U/L. Other diseases leading to elevated ALT levels were excluded. The degree of elevated ALT levels was divided into three categories [14, 21, 22]: mild elevated ALT levels, 1 × ULN < ALT ≤ 5 × ULN; ALT flare, 5 × ULN < ALT ≤ 10 × ULN; and ALT exacerbation, ALT > 10 × ULN.

Biochemical examination

All biochemical indices were tested by the central laboratory of Hangzhou First People’s Hospital. Liver and renal function were assessed with an AU5800 automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), for which the ULN of ALT in women was 40 U/L. HBV serological markers were detected by an Abbott ARCHITECT i2000SR microparticle chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA), which had a lower detection limit of 0.05 IU/mL for HBsAg. The serum HBV DNA level was detected by fluorescent quantitative polymerase chain reaction detection reagent (Daan Gene Co., Ltd. of Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China), the detection limit of which was l × 102 IU/mL.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as proportions, and non-normally distributed quantitative data were presented as median (M) with interquartile range (P25, P75). Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to analyze categorical variables, while the non-parametric statistics were used to analyze non-normally distributed quantitative data. Risk factors were assessed through binary logistic regression analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Of 236 HBsAg-positive pregnant women screened, 187 were enrolled (details are shown in Fig. 1). Groups A–C comprised 65, 60, and 62 women, respectively. Overall, 27 (14.44%) women were lost to follow-up, primarily due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, 160 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of these women, nine delivered at 35–36 weeks of gestation, while the others delivered at 37–41 weeks of gestation. These women gave birth to 161 infants (including one set of twins), comprising 86 boys and 75 girls. There were two cases of fetal malformation, one case of syndactyly, and one case of preauricular fistula. Six infants were of low birth weight. All these 161 infants (including those with malformations or low birth weight) were included in the analysis, and all tested negative for HBsAg and positive for anti-HBs, except one who tested negative for anti-HBs after completing the vaccination course (Table S1).

Elevated postpartum ALT levels

Incidence and severity of elevated ALT levels

During the first 48 weeks postpartum, 91 (56.88%) of 160 mothers developed elevated ALT levels (> 1 × ULN), and 5 (3.13%) of 160 mothers exhibited ALT exacerbation (> 10 × ULN), with no significant differences among groups (P = 0.338 and P = 0.103, respectively) (Table 2). These findings suggest that TDF therapy duration had limited impact on long-term ALT levels. Within 12 weeks postpartum, the incidence of elevated ALT levels was 57.63% in Group A, 49.02% in Group B, and 40.00% in Group C, with no significant difference among the three groups (P = 0.186) (Table 2). A cross-threshold analysis revealed consistently higher elevated ALT level rates in Group A compared to the combined Groups B and C, though statistically non-significant: >1 × ULN (64.41% vs. 52.48%, P = 0.141), > 5 × ULN (11.86% vs. 4.95%, P = 0.197), and > 10 × ULN (6.78% vs. 0.99%, P = 0.062) (Table S2). All five ALT exacerbations occurred within 12 weeks postpartum, identifying this period as the critical monitoring window.

Among 91 mothers with elevated postpartum ALT levels, further analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in the severity of elevated ALT levels among the three groups (P = 0.558) (Table 2) or between Group A and the combined Groups B and C (P = 0.254) (Table S2). Mild elevated ALT levels was observed in 86.81% (79/91) mothers: 81.58% in Group A, 92.59% in Group B, and 88.46% in Group C. ALT flare was observed in 7.69% (7/91) mothers: 7.89% in Group A, 7.41% in Group B, and 7.69% in Group C. ALT exacerbation was observed in 5.50% (5/91) mothers: 10.53% in Group A, 0.00% in Group B, and 3.85% in Group C (Table 2). While no significant intergroup differences in peak ALT levels were observed (P = 0.401) (Table 2; Fig. 2a), it was noteworthy that all three cases with extreme elevated ALT levels (510–968 U/L) occurred in Group A, suggesting potential association between early TDF withdrawal and severe hepatic flares. No significant bilirubin elevation (> 2 × ULN) was observed in mothers with elevated ALT levels during follow-up, and no acute viral infections were detected concurrent with ALT abnormalities.

Onset times of elevated ALT levels

The onset times of elevated ALT levels are shown in Table 3. The median onset time was 8 weeks postpartum in all groups, with no significant difference (P = 0.798). Among the 91 mothers with elevated ALT levels, 63 (69.23%) exhibited the initial elevated ALT levels at approximately 6 weeks postpartum (follow-up time point of 6 ± 2 weeks postpartum), 16 (17.58%) at 12 weeks postpartum, 5 (5.49%) at 18 ± 2 weeks postpartum, 4 (4.40%) at 24 weeks postpartum, 3 (3.30%) at 36 weeks postpartum, and none at 48 weeks postpartum. There were no differences in the elevated ALT levels proportions at each follow-up time point among groups (P = 0.561) (Table 3).

Comparison of HBV DNA levels at onset of elevated ALT levels

The median HBV DNA levels at the onset of elevated ALT levels in Groups A–C were 8.03 (7.50–8.21), 5.61 (4.11–7.95), and 3.35 (2.00–4.94) lg IU/mL, respectively (H = 36.98, P = 0.000). The HBV DNA level was significantly lower in Group B than Group A, and in Group C than in Groups A and B (Group A vs. Group B: P = 0.012, Group A vs. Group C: P = 0.000, Group B vs. Group C: P = 0.010, all P values had been adjusted for multiple comparisons) (Fig. 2b).

Risk factors for elevated ALT levels

All mothers were divided into normal ALT group and elevated ALT group according to whether they had elevated postpartum ALT levels (Fig. 1). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in age, mode of delivery, breastfeeding, baseline HBsAg, HBeAg, HBV DNA level, elevated ALT during pregnancy, HBV DNA level at delivery, or TDF withdrawal timing (P > 0.05 for all) (Table 4). The elevated ALT group demonstrated significantly higher ALT levels at delivery than the normal ALT group (24 vs. 18 U/L, Z = − 4.386, P = 0.000) (Table 4). Binary logistic regression analysis showed that ALT level at delivery was independently correlated with postpartum ALT elevation (odds ratio = 1.098, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.039–1.160, P = 0.001). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that delivery ALT level had modest diagnostic utility for predicting postpartum ALT elevation, with an area under the curve of 0.703 (95% CI, 0.622–0.785) (Fig. 3), and the cut-off value was ≥ 23 U/L for the Youden index. An ALT level of ≥ 23 U/L showed a positive predictive value of 81.97% for the occurrence of elevated postpartum ALT levels, and the sensitivity and specificity were 54.95% and 84.06%, respectively (Table 5).

Among 61 mothers with ALT levels ≥ 23 U/L at delivery in the three groups, elevated ALT levels were observed in 50 (81.97%) mothers: 22/23 (95.65%) in group A, 14/17 (82.35%) in group B, and 14/21 (66.67%) in group C. A statistically significant difference in the incidence of elevated ALT levels was observed between group A and group C (P = 0.048), while no significant differences were observed between Group A vs. Group B (P = 0.591) or Group B vs. Group C (P = 0.711), all P values had been adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Re-AVT or long-term AVT postpartum

Among the 91 mothers with elevated postpartum ALT levels, 13 (14.29%) received re-AVT or long-term AVT postpartum (8 in Group A, 3 in Group B, and 2 in Group C) (Table S3), with no statistically significant differences among groups (P = 0.179). All developed an elevated ALT level within 12 weeks postpartum, with peak ALT levels (181–968 U/L) occurring within this period in 76.92% (10/13) of cases. 10 mothers (7 in Group A, 2 in Group B, and 1 in Group C) developed ALT > 5 × ULN (HBV DNA 7.13–8.93 lg IU/mL) after TDF withdrawal and hence recommenced AVT. Notably, 2 mothers with postpartum ALT levels < 200 U/L, 1 in Group A (peak ALT 197 U/L, HBV DNA 8.58 lg IU/mL) and the other in Group B (peak ALT 181 U/L, HBV DNA 8.23 lg IU/mL), also received re-AVT due to absent remission. 1 mother in Group C developed ALT exacerbation (peak ALT 463 U/L at 8 weeks postpartum, HBV DNA 3.72 lg IU/mL) while taking TDF and received long-term AVT.

Breastfeeding rates

A total of 47 of 59 mothers in Group A, 13 of 51 in Group B, and 21 of 50 in Group C chose to breastfeed. The breastfeeding rate was significantly higher in Group A (79.66%) than in Group B (25.49%) and Group C (42.00%), both P values were 0.000. There was no significant difference in breastfeeding rate between Groups B and C (P = 0.079).

Discussion

This prospective study compares three guideline-recommended TDF withdrawal strategies in CHB mothers. While withdrawal timing did not significantly impact overall ALT elevation patterns, two key findings emerged: (1) early TDF withdrawal at delivery was associated with higher breastfeeding rates, and (2) extending TDF therapy to 12 weeks postpartum significantly reduced the incidence of elevated ALT levels in mothers with delivery ALT levels ≥ 23 U/L. These insights advance personalized postpartum management by balancing liver safety and breastfeeding.

The overall incidence rates of elevated ALT levels (ALT > 1 × ULN) and ALT exacerbation (ALT > 10 × ULN) were 56.88% and 3.13%, respectively, among 160 chronic HBV carriers in this study. Xiao et al. [23] reported that approximately 50% of patients experienced elevated ALT levels, which aligns with our results. Most elevated ALT levels observed in the present study were of mild-to-moderate severity, with the peak incidence occurring at approximately 6 weeks postpartum, and ALT exacerbation typically occurred within 12 weeks postpartum. This temporal pattern likely reflected postpartum immune reconstitution dynamics: Natural killer cell populations increased beginning at 6 weeks and plateau during 6–24 weeks postpartum, potentially inducing liver inflammation through non-antigen-specific cytotoxicity [24, 25]. In HBV-infected women, this recovering immune function may additionally trigger HBV-specific responses, leading to the characteristic ALT elevation peak at 6–12 weeks postpartum [24]. These findings highlight 6 and 12 weeks postpartum as critical monitoring windows.

The HBV DNA levels at the onset of elevated ALT levels varied among the three groups, with Group C (TDF withdrawal at 12 weeks postpartum) showing significantly lower levels than Groups A and B (P < 0.05). Notably, despite continuous antiviral treatment and low viral loads through 12 weeks postpartum, 40% of Group C still developed elevated ALT levels during this period (including one case of ALT exacerbation at 8 weeks postpartum). This finding further corroborates the “high ALT with low virus” phenomenon, indicating that among women receiving TDF for HBV MTCT prophylaxis, elevated ALT levels may occur independently of postpartum AVT or HBV DNA level. This aligns with prior research by Liu et al. [15], who found that 34.4% of mothers developed elevated ALT levels while undergoing telbivudine treatment during 12 weeks postpartum. Potential mechanisms involve postpartum cortisol decline, which impairs hepatic anti-inflammatory regulation, and HBV DNA/cytokine-induced enhancement of HBV-specific T cell response, thereby disrupting immune tolerance and promoting liver inflammation [26, 27].

In a previous study, Li et al. [16] reported that 92.3% of postpartum hepatitis cases occurred within 12 weeks postpartum in mothers who discontinued treatment at delivery, compared to 77.7% in those who discontinued at 6 weeks postpartum. Consistent with their hypothesis that extended antiviral therapy may delay elevated postpartum ALT levels in some individuals, our study showed that 89.48% and 92.59% of elevated ALT levels in Group A (TDF withdrawal at delivery) and Group B (TDF withdrawal at 4 weeks postpartum) occurred within 12 weeks postpartum, with a lower rate of 76.92% in Group C (TDF withdrawal at 12 weeks postpartum). To further explore the underlying factors, our subgroup analysis revealed a significant clinical insight: among mothers with ALT levels ≥ 23 U/L at delivery, the incidence of elevated postpartum ALT levels was significantly lower in Group C compared to Group A (66.67% vs. 95.65%, P < 0.05). This finding suggests that extending TDF therapy to 12 weeks postpartum significantly reduces the incidence of elevated ALT levels. Clinicians can leverage the ALT level at delivery as a biomarker to identify patients who may benefit from extended antiviral therapy, thus tailoring treatment to individual liver function status. Furthermore, while the overall comparison between immediate discontinuation (Group A) and later withdrawal groups (Groups B and C) did not reach statistical significance, the trend toward higher ALT exacerbation rates in Group A (P = 0.062) highlights potential risks associated with early discontinuation, and prolonged TDF therapy may provide increasing protection against severe liver inflammation. These observations provide a plausible explanation for the higher incidence of postpartum ALT exacerbation and Re-AVT observed in Group A, highlighting the potential impact of early discontinuation on hepatic inflammation. In addition, these findings underscore the need for large-scale, longitudinal studies to validate our observations and establish evidence-based guidelines.

The independent predictors of elevated postpartum ALT levels remain uncertain. Yi et al. [14] found that an ALT level of > 40 U/L at delivery was an independent risk factor for postpartum ALT flare (ALT ≥ 5 × ULN). Liu et al. [15] found that elevated ALT levels during pregnancy were associated with postpartum ALT flares after telbivudine withdrawal at 12 weeks postpartum, though these findings remain controversial. In a large retrospective cohort study, Zhou et al. [28] found that an ALT cutoff of 19 U/L at delivery more sensitively predicted the risk of postpartum ALT flare (ALT ≥ 5 × ULN) within 28 weeks postpartum in women with CHB. Our study also established a notable correlation between the ALT level at delivery (ALT cutoff of 23 U/L) and elevated postpartum ALT levels (> 40 U/L), further aiding the identification of independent predictors.

In China, where breastfeeding is actively promoted, 94.5% of infants under six months receive breastfeeding, with 29.5% being exclusively breastfed, showing regional variations in feeding patterns [29]. Our current findings demonstrate a possible association between TDF discontinuation timing and breastfeeding behavior. Specifically, 79.7% of mothers who discontinued TDF at delivery chose to breastfeed, compared to only 25.49% and 42.00% of those continuing postpartum therapy for 4 or 12 weeks (both P < 0.001). This substantial disparity likely reflects persistent maternal concerns about drug exposure through breastmilk, despite robust evidence demonstrating the safety of TDF during lactation [5, 13, 30]. These findings underscore the critical need for targeted patient education to mitigate misconceptions. Notably, breastfeeding rates did not differ between mothers with and without elevated ALT levels (51.7% vs. 49.3%, P = 0.766), suggesting no significant association between feeding practices and elevated ALT levels. We must underscore that the observed association between TDF discontinuation and breastfeeding rates does not imply causation, given the observational study design. Although we adjusted for maternal age and HBV DNA load, unmeasured confounders (e.g., education, social support, cultural factors) could potentially influence feeding practices.

This study had four main limitations. First, patient-determined TDF discontinuation timing may introduce selection bias. Specifically, early discontinuation decisions influenced by medication safety concerns, economic factors, or personal beliefs could independently affect both postpartum ALT levels and breastfeeding practices. Although we adjusted for known confounders, residual confounding remains and may overestimate safety outcomes—an inherent limitation of observational studies. Limited by small sample sizes without a priori power calculation, we may have missed clinically meaningful differences in elevated postpartum ALT levels. Second, the findings are strictly applicable to HBeAg-positive immunotolerant Asian CHB patients (ALT < 40 U/L, high HBV DNA), and the predictive value of our identified ALT threshold (23 U/L) requires validation in other populations and disease phases. Third, although the loss to follow-up (14.44%) was balanced across groups and likely non-differential due to the COVID-19 pandemic, this may affect outcome generalizability. Fourth, while the “high ALT with low virus” mechanism requires further study, this study also lacked anti-HBc IgM testing (which could help differentiate exacerbation types) and long-term follow-up of re-AVT efficacy in CHB mothers after MTCT blockade. These limitations highlight the need for randomized trials with standardized protocols, sensitivity analysis and comprehensive longitudinal data collection to confirm these preliminary findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, individualized management is essential for postpartum TDF withdrawal in CHB mothers. Early TDF withdrawal at delivery was associated with higher breastfeeding rates, and the potential risk of elevated ALT requires attention. However, mothers with ALT levels ≥ 23 U/L at delivery may benefit from extended postpartum antiviral therapy by reducing hepatic inflammation. These findings, limited by the study’s observational design, underscore the need for individualized postpartum management and further randomized trials to confirm causality.
