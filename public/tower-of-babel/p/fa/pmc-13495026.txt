Characterization of a Heme-Binding Radical SAM Enzyme HemW from Moraxella catarrhalis
Authors: Caitlin M. Padgett, Melissa M. Bollmeyer, Kaleb Boswinkle, Anthony T. Iavarone, Valérie de Crécy-Lagard, R. David Britt, Wen Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Moraxella catarrhalis is an emerging human respiratory pathogen responsible for various infectious diseases. Recent transposon sequencing analysis identified yggW (renamed hemW) as essential for M. catarrhalis growth under iron-limiting conditions, mimicking host-imposed nutritional immunity. HemW is annotated as a putative radical S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) enzyme and belongs to the HemN-like subfamily, but its biochemical properties remain unclear. Here, we report on the first experimental characterization of M. catarrhalis HemW (McHemW). Our bioinformatic analysis confirmed its evolutionary relationship with the putative heme-binding radical SAM enzyme HemW in Escherichia coli. We experimentally demonstrated that McHemW contains a catalytically active [4Fe-4S] cluster and binds heme through biochemical assays and spectroscopy. Using hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, we demonstrated, for the first time, that the heme-binding site resides within the C-terminal auxiliary domain unique to HemW. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the molecular basis of heme recognition in McHemW and establish a foundation for understanding its role in the pathogenicity of M. catarrhalis under iron-limited conditions.

Introduction

Moraxella catarrhalis is a bacterial pathogen associated with infections of the human upper and lower respiratory tract. , Despite its clinical significance, our understanding of its metabolic vulnerabilities remains limited. , This knowledge gap is particularly pressing given the rising prevalence of antibiotic resistance and the urgent need to identify novel targets for antimicrobial development. − Using genomic array footprinting, along with targeted gene deletion and complementation, recent studies identified a previously uncharacterized gene, yggW (renamed hemW), as essential for the growth of pathogenic M. catarrhalis BBH18 under iron-starvation conditions. These conditions are thought to reflect the physiological stresses that pathogens encounter within the human host. − The conservation of hemW across multiple M. catarrhalis isolates suggests that hemW may play a critical role in their pathogenicity.

The protein encoded by M. catarrhalis hemW is annotated as a putative radical S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) enzyme, HemW. With over 800,000 members, the radical SAM superfamily has emerged as one of the largest and most functionally diverse superfamilies, carrying out essential biological functions across all kingdoms of life. − Most members of this superfamily share a conserved CX3CX2C motif in the α6β6 partial TIM barrel used to coordinate a [4Fe-4S] cluster. − This [4Fe-4S] cluster can potentially mediate the reductive cleavage of SAM, initiating a cascade of radical-based reactions that enable a wide range of distinct transformations of a second substrate, typically with high regio- and stereoselectivity. − Chemical versatility and broad substrate scope of these enzymes arise from auxiliary domains appended to the conserved partial-TIM barrel core (Figure a). ,− Functional annotation of new radical SAM enzymes is often hindered by the limited knowledge of their auxiliary domains. − An example is the oxygen-independent coproporphyrinogen III oxidase-like enzyme subfamily, sometimes referred to as HemN-like radical SAM enzymes. − Members of this subfamily have been proposed to use two equivalents of SAM to catalyze C–C bond-forming or cleaving reactions on diverse substrates. − Among the few experimentally characterized members, HemN and HemZ function in heme biosynthesis (Figure b). ,− The only X-ray crystal structure available for this subfamily, HemN, reveals two distinct SAM-binding sites. This structure supports a proposed mechanism in which one SAM molecule, coordinated to the [4Fe-4S] cluster, generates the 5′-deoxyadenosyl (5′-dAdo) radical, while a second SAM generates a methylene radical to initiate oxidative decarboxylation of coproporphyrinogen III to produce protoporphyrinogen IX (Figure b). Other HemN-like enzymes, such as ChuW and HutW, are proposed to share the same radical-initiation strategy while catalyzing heme degradation (Figure b). − To date, the auxiliary domains of these enzymes remain functionally unannotated, and no porphyrin-binding site has been experimentally identified. Although computational modeling has proposed a potential binding site within the catalytic cleft, direct experimental evidence is still lacking.

In this study, we heterologously expressed and purified M. catarrhalis HemW (McHemW). Using a combination of bioinformatics, biochemical, and spectroscopic methods, along with hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) analysis, we show that McHemW contains a catalytically active [4Fe-4S] cluster that exhibits enhanced SAM cleavage activity in the presence of heme. Our HDX-MS analysis, for the first time, identifies the C-terminal auxiliary domain as the primary region involved in heme binding, providing experimental insight into heme recognition in HemW homologues.

Results

Sequence Similarity Network (SSN) Identifies McHemW Clustered with EcHemW

The sequence of McHemW contains a characteristic CX3CX2C motif, indicative of a putative radical SAM enzyme. Sequence similarity analysis using RadicalSAM.org revealed that McHemW belongs to the oxygen-independent coproporphyrinogen III oxidase-like enzymes, which are found across bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes (Figure S1). When we systematically increased sequence alignment stringency until previously characterized enzymes segregated into distinct functional clusters, at the 110 alignment score threshold, McHemW grouped with the same cluster as Escherichia coli HemW (EcHemW), a putative heme chaperone (Figures a and S2). The phylogenetic analysis with other radical SAM enzymes within the same subfamily revealed a clear separation between heme biosynthesis, heme degradation, and HemW (Figure b and Table S1). Although RSAD1, the putative HemW homologue in the animal and plant kingdoms, is more closely related to HemW than other HemN-like radical SAM enzymes, it remains a distinct branch with McHemW (Figure S1). − Within the HemW clade, Lactococcus lactis HemW, LlHemW, forms a distinct branch, while McHemW clusters closely with EcHemW, sharing 43% sequence identity. This is consistent with our SSN analysis, which shows that McHemW shares high sequence similarity with EcHemW (Figure S2).

To better define the sequence features that distinguish McHemW from neighboring subclusters, we performed a multiple sequence alignment of HemW with other representative radical SAM enzymes involved in heme biosynthesis and degradation pathways (Figure S3). It demonstrated that McHemW contains C22–C26–C29 and G71-G72-G73-T74-P75 motifs, which are required for [4Fe-4S] cluster and SAM binding (Figure c). A major distinction between HemW and other HemN-like enzymes lies in the residues associated with the proposed second SAM-binding site. In HemW homologues, these residues are replaced by a characteristic CXHNX2YW motif, along with a C-terminal auxiliary domain containing multiple conserved residues distributed throughout the domain (Figure d). Mapping these regions onto the AlphaFold-predicted McHemW structural model indicates that canonical radical SAM features are likely retained in HemW, while the CXHNX2YW motif and the auxiliary domain appear to be unique to HemW homologues (Figure e).

McHemW Contains an Iron–Sulfur Cluster

We expressed and purified His-tagged McHemW in E. coli BL21-Gold (DE3) under anaerobic conditions. Intact protein mass spectrometry confirmed a mass of 46 137 Da, consistent with the expected sequence (Figure S4). The purified protein is brown in color and migrates as a single band on SDS-PAGE (Figure a). The iron and sulfur content of the as-purified sample was 3.1 ± 0.7 and 3.4 ± 0.5 per monomer, respectively. Chemical reconstitution gave 6.1 ± 0.6 Fe and 5.9 ± 0.9 S per monomer, indicating the presence of at least one iron–sulfur cluster and likely binding of adventitious iron and sulfur. In the UV–visible spectra, we observed the broad absorption feature at 410 nm of McHemW, and the addition of sodium dithionite (DTH) abolished this feature (Figure b). To test if the iron–sulfur cluster is coordinated by the conserved CX3CX2C motif within the partial TIM barrel, we generated a double-cysteine knockout variant, C22A/C29A, to disrupt the putative radical SAM (RS) cluster-binding site. This variant, designated ΔRS, was purified to homogeneity at a level comparable to that of the wild-type (WT) protein but lacked the characteristic brown color (Figure a), showed no absorbance at 410 nm and contained no detectable iron (Figure b). We conclude that McHemW coordinates an iron–sulfur cluster via the C22-C26–C29 motif.

Native-PAGE analysis revealed that chemically reconstituted McHemW exists in both monomeric and dimeric forms in solution (Figure S5). To further characterize its oligomeric state, we performed analytical size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) under anaerobic conditions (Figure c). Two distinct peaks corresponding to ∼46 kDa and ∼100 kDa confirmed the presence of both monomer and dimer species. Increasing protein concentration does not significantly shift the relative abundance of the dimer (Figure S6). In contrast, the ΔRS variant, which lacks the iron–sulfur cluster, predominantly existed as a monomer at the same concentration (Figure c). These findings are consistent with previous observations for EcHemW, in which the intact iron–sulfur cluster binding site promotes dimerization.

Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) Characterization of [4Fe-4S] Cluster Coordination

The continuous-wave (CW) X-band EPR spectrum of chemically reconstituted McHemW contained a signal at g = 4.3 attributed to adventitious Fe (III) and a sharp signal at g = 2.01 that we assign to a [3Fe-4S]+ cluster, which could be the result of incomplete cofactor incorporation (Figure a). Upon the addition of DTH, a new S = 1/2 signal appeared with a g-tensor of [1.99, 1.92, 1.82] (Table ). The g = 2.01 feature remained after the addition of DTH, indicating that the [3Fe-4S]+ cluster was not fully reduced. Both signals were absent in the spectra of the ΔRS variant and can, therefore, be attributed to the RS cluster. A simulation of the reduced McHemW spectrum indicates that the signal with g max = 1.99 is the dominant species, accounting for approximately 97% of the total spins detected (Figure b). The low g max value of 1.99 is more consistent with a [2Fe-2S]+ cluster, whereas [4Fe-4S]+ clusters typically have g max values greater than g e. − However, increasing the temperature completely diminished this signal, leaving only the signal corresponding to the [3Fe-4S]+ cluster. This fast-relaxing behavior is more consistent with [4Fe-4S]+ clusters, while [2Fe-2S]+ clusters tend to relax more slowly and retain signal even at 60 K. The low value of g max and fast relaxing behavior of this signal make the assignment of the cluster as either a [2Fe-2S]+ or [4Fe-4S]+ species ambiguous.

To establish whether the reduced iron–sulfur cluster signal could be assigned to a [4Fe-4S]+ RS cluster, we used 13C-labeled cyanide as a probe. Cyanide can bind to the unique Fe of an RS cluster, but cannot bind to a cluster fully coordinated by cysteine residues in a [2Fe-2S] cluster. When reduced McHemW was incubated with 100 equiv of K13CN, a new species with g = [2.07, 1.95, 1.91] appeared (Figure b). This new rhombic g-tensor is similar to those of other 13CN-bound RS clusters, including PqqE, IspH, HydG, and Pyrococcus furiosus ferredoxin. ,− While this signal is consistent with a 13CN-bound [4Fe-4S]+ RS species, some of the unbound [4Fe-4S]+ cluster remained, indicating that there was incomplete binding. Further characterization of 13CN-treated McHemW using X-band hyperfine sublevel correlation (HYSCORE) spectroscopy acquired at g = 1.94 revealed one set of cross-peaks centered at the 13C Larmor frequency (Figure c). These cross-peaks were well simulated using a pseudoaxial hyperfine coupling tensor A 13C = [−5.0, −4.5, 1.9] MHz and Euler angle = [0, 20, 0]° (Figure d). This hyperfine tensor is similar to those observed for other 13CN-ligated [4Fe-4S]+ clusters and provides additional support for the presence of a typical [4Fe-4S]-loaded RS cluster. ,−

Heme Binds to McHemW Noncovalently

A previous study of EcHemW showed that the protein covalently binds heme in a 1:1 stoichiometry in vitro. To explore the nature of heme binding in McHemW, we reconstituted McHemW with heme at a 1:1 molar ratio and analyzed the complex by UV–vis spectroscopy to assess its heme-binding capability (Figure a). Heme in aqueous solution exhibited a broad absorption band at 350–385 nm, with a weak absorption peak at 600 nm. The heme-reconstituted McHemW displayed Soret band at 411 nm, suggesting that heme is bound in protein at an oxidized Fe (III) state within an electronic environment distinct from that of free heme. In contrast, the UV–vis spectrum of the ΔRS variant showed only weak, red-shifted features at 360–390 nm, with no discernible Soret band signals. This feature was also absent in the sample in which HemW was replaced with a well-characterized radical SAM enzyme, PqqE, which contains a distinct auxiliary domain from HemW. ,, The addition of DTH to WT McHemW results in a red shift of the Soret band to 420 nm, accompanied by an increase in intensity, and the appearance of the distinct Q-bands at 529 and 562 nm (Figure b). The appearance of the 562 nm Q-band likely reflects ligand-induced changes, possibly due to the reduction to Fe­(II) or stabilization of a defined spin state. When heme was titrated into a fixed concentration of McHemW, both the Soret and Q-band signals increased (Figure c). These features were not present in the spectra in the absence of protein, indicating that the elevated absorbance at 411 nm arises from the formation of the McHemW-heme complex rather than from the elevated background signal of free heme (Figure S7). The calculated dissociation constant (K d) of McHemW for heme is 27 ± 7 μM, which is higher than the value previously observed for HemW homologues in Campylobacter jejuni CgdH2 (4.9 μM) and L. lactis HemW (8 μM), respectively. ,

We next investigated whether both the dimer and monomer of McHemW can bind heme. To this end, we performed native-PAGE on heme-reconstituted WT and the ΔRS variant, followed by dual staining with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMBZ) for heme detection and Coomassie Blue for total protein analysis (Figure S5). We observed both the monomer and the dimer of the WT enzyme in the native-PAGE gel, consistent with our SEC analysis. When the same gel was treated with TMBZ, both dimeric and monomeric forms of WT McHemW stained with TMBZ at high protein concentration, indicating that both forms are competent for heme binding. In contrast, the ΔRS variant contains a monomeric form on native-PAGE when stained with Coomassie Blue. TMBZ staining of the ΔRS variant also shows a much weaker band than the same concentration in the WT sample at the size corresponding to the dimeric form of the ΔRS variant on native-PAGE. When we repeated the dual staining experiment on SDS-PAGE gels using heme-reconstituted WT and the ΔRS variant, no heme staining was observed in the ΔRS variant, despite the protein band being readily visible on the Coomassie stain (Figure S5). We observed weak heme binding only at high protein concentrations (50 μM) for WT, consistent with similar interactions previously reported for EcHemW. We suspected that the heme staining observed at high protein concentrations arises from incomplete denaturation in an overloaded SDS-PAGE. To confirm this hypothesis, we performed intact protein mass spectrometry on heme-reconstituted WT McHemW. Upon incubation with heme, no shift in protein mass was detected, and only free heme was detected at the expected mass of 616 Da, confirming that heme does not form a covalent adduct with McHemW (Figure S4). As the ΔRS variant disrupts [4Fe-4S] cluster coordination and impairs dimerization, the difference between WT and ΔRS suggests that the heme-binding favors the enzyme containing an intact [4Fe-4S] cluster or its binding motif, likely due to the influence on the local conformation of the heme coordination site.

HDX-MS Identifies the Auxiliary Domain as the Heme-Binding Site

To identify the heme-binding site in McHemW, we performed anaerobic HDX-MS experiments. HDX-MS has been widely used to map ligand-binding interactions and probe conformational dynamics in enzymes and proteins, although implementing the technique under strictly anaerobic conditions remains technically challenging. ,− We optimized the deuterium-labeling and proteolysis workflows under anaerobic conditions to maximize peptide coverage and spatial resolution of the resulting peptide library (see HDX Data Summary).

Using these optimized conditions, we carried out deuterium-labeling experiments at multiple labeling time points (t = 0, 120, 600, 1800, 3600, and 10800 s) in triplicate to compare deuteron uptake patterns between those of McHemW alone and heme-bound McHemW. Across all conditions, we selected 24 nonoverlapping McHemW-derived peptides, ranging in length from 8 to 32 amino acid residues, to achieve optimal coverage and resolution. All selected peptides exhibited EX2 exchange behavior, indicating that conformational opening and closing occur in rapid equilibrium prior to the rate-limiting chemical exchange of backbone amide hydrogens with deuterium.

In the McHemW-alone sample, the majority of peptides displayed a clear time-dependent deuteron uptake pattern (Figures S8 and S9), consistent with progressive solvent exchange over the labeling period. Mapping the exchange profiles onto the AlphaFold-predicted structure revealed a persistently low-exchange region at residues 235–267, located within the protein core across all time points, indicating limited solvent accessibility under the experimental conditions. This region contains the highly conserved CXHNX2YW motif, a characteristic sequence feature of HemW homologues that distinguishes them from other radical SAM enzymes (Figure S2).

To quantitatively compare the H/D exchange of McHemW in the presence and absence of heme (Figure S9), we calculated the differences in deuteron uptake for each McHemW-derived peptide between the McHemW alone and heme-bound states. Based on the measured K d values described above, the estimated heme-bound fraction of McHemW under these conditions was 43%. Differences in deuteron uptake between McHemW alone and heme-bound McHemW were quantified as Δ%D at matched labeling time points. Negative Δ%D values indicate protection from exchange upon heme binding, consistent with reduced solvent accessibility and/or increased local structural stabilization, whereas positive Δ%D values reflect increased exchange, suggesting enhanced solvent exposure in the presence of heme. Statistical significance was evaluated using a t-test, with a threshold of p < 0.01. Because the number of exchangeable backbone amides varies among peptides of different lengths, we further applied a magnitude cutoff of |Δ%D| > 10% to focus on biologically meaningful changes. For most peptides in the data set, this threshold corresponds approximately to a difference of one incorporated deuteron at any given time point (Supporting Notes and Figure S10). Peptides satisfying both criteria (p < 0.01 and |Δ%D| > 10%) were subsequently mapped onto the AlphaFold model of McHemW to identify potential heme-induced H/D exchange perturbations.

Our HDX results show that in the presence of heme, several peptides exhibited significantly reduced H/D exchange at multiple time points (Figure a), indicating localized protection upon heme binding. At the earliest labeling time point (t 1 = 120 s), decreased deuterium incorporation was detected in three C-terminal peptides, p349–365, p366–385, and p381–391, with Δ%D values of −10 ± 1, −10 ± 1, and −20 ± 2, respectively (Figure b). Mapping these peptides onto the AlphaFold-predicted structure revealed that the protected regions cluster within the helix-rich C-terminal auxiliary domain, suggesting direct or proximal interaction with heme (Figure c). At a later labeling time point (t 3 = 1800 s), an additional peptide p341–349 exhibited a Δ%D value of −12 ± 2 (Figure b). This peptide is positioned adjacent to the helices identified at earlier time points, whereas most other McHemW-derived peptides showed little to no protection upon heme binding. At longer labeling times, the differences between heme-bound and McHemW alone gradually diminished, likely reflecting heme dissociation and continued deuterium incorporation, which progressively attenuate the exchange differences established at early time points. Similar behavior has been reported for weakly bound ligand-protein complexes. Overall, our analysis supports the C-terminal auxiliary domain as the primary heme-binding region in McHemW.

EPR Characterization of Heme Binding

When heme was added to McHemW, the EPR spectrum of the oxidized sample featured a new low-spin heme signal in addition to the [3Fe-4S]+ signal near g = 2 (Figure ). This low-spin heme signal is distinct from the axial S = 5/2 signal of heme and is indicative of heme binding to the enzyme. , The spectrum of heme-bound McHemW was best fitted with two S = 1/2 species with g = [2.45, 2.28, 1.90] and g = [2.52, 2.27, 1.87] in a 2:1 ratio. Differences in heme conformation or axial ligation could account for the two distinct sets of g values. The observed heterogeneity likely reflects the mixture of dimeric and monomeric protein populations. In the heme-reconstituted ΔRS variant, we observed two closely related species, with the second heme species exhibiting slightly increased rhombicity, characterized by g values of [2.53, 2.28, 1.86]. Since we observe a minor dimeric form in the ΔRS variant by SEC and native-PAGE, the more rhombic species (g = [2.53, 2.28, 1.86]) in the ΔRS variant of McHemW likely represent heme bound to the monomeric form, which has an altered binding conformation. This indicates that binding of the [4Fe-4S] cluster is important for the overall fold of the enzyme and its interaction with heme.

Catalytic Competence of the McHemW

Our sequence alignment and spectroscopic analyses suggest that McHemW uses the conserved CX3CX2C motif to coordinate a [4Fe-4S] cluster with an open Fe site for SAM binding, suggesting potential catalytic competence. When McHemW was incubated with SAM in the presence of DTH, HPLC analysis revealed new peaks eluting at the exact retention times of the commercial standards for 5′-dAdo and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) (Figure a). LC-MS supports the identities of these products, which have m/z values of 252.1 and 385.1, respectively (Figure b). Omission of DTH, SAM, or McHemW abolished the formation of both products. A time-dependent activity assay showed that 5′-dAdo and SAH were formed with an initial rate of 0.26 ± 0.09 and 0.14 ± 0.05 μM/h, respectively (Figure c). Intriguingly, when the activity assays were repeated using heme-reconstituted McHemW, the same product peaks were observed, but their intensities increased, resulting in 4.6-fold increases in the initial rates of 5′-dAdo and SAH formation (Figure c). The McHemW sample without chemical reconstitution showed a similar trend in our time-course measurement, with a slightly slower initial rate (Figure S11). Given that SAM and DTH are present in large excess, the low initial rate and low product conversion suggest that the reaction likely proceeds predominantly via single-turnover. The amount of SAH formed is substoichiometric relative to 5′-dAdo, likely reflecting a shunt pathway operating during the reaction. , These results demonstrate that the RS [4Fe-4S] cluster of McHemW is catalytically competent for reductive cleavage of SAM, albeit much less efficiently than other radical SAM enzymes, such as BsQueE. The presence of heme enhances SAM cleavage activity at the radical SAM site of McHemW, further implying crosstalk between the radical SAM active site and the heme-binding site in the C-terminal auxiliary domain.

Discussion

Our bioinformatic analysis shows that McHemW is clearly separated from the previously characterized HemN homologues and constitutes a distinct subclass. In M. catarrhalis, the McHemW operon contains two additional genes, annotated as ATP-dependent RNA helicase RhIB and a hypothetical protein that does not appear to contribute directly to the iron-deficiency phenotype (Figure S12). , Similarly, the EcHemW operon contains predicted regulatory genes hypothesized to participate in stress-response pathways that remodel gene expression under adverse conditions (Figure S12). Further comparative genomics analyses reveal two types of gene patterns of neighborhood conservation surrounding hemW (Figure S12). In α- and γ-proteobacteria, hemW frequently co-occurs with transcriptional activator rdgB and genes involved in nucleic acid-related metabolism, including rph (ribonuclease PH) (Figure S12). , The functional implications of this association remain unclear, but it may hint at an unrecognized link between HemW and pathways that sense or respond to nucleic acids. A second, highly conserved pattern appears across many Gram-positive lineages and a subset of Gram-negative organisms, in which hemW is located adjacent to hrcA and canonical heat-shock responsive genes, such as grpE, dnaK, and dnaJ. − In Bacillus subtilis, this linkage is experimentally validated, with hemW residing within a single operon that includes lepA, hrcA, grpE, and dnaK. The conservation of these genetic contexts suggests that HemW may interface with cellular stress pathways more broadly than previously appreciated. In particular, coregulation with heat-shock genes could reflect a requirement for HemW function during proteotoxic or oxidative stress. ,

We have shown that McHemW harbors an RS [4Fe-4S] cluster that can be readily reduced by DTH. This behavior contrasts with previous reports on EcHemW, in which the cluster could not be reduced by multiple electron donors, including sodium dithionite and titanium­(III) citrate, thereby precluding successful EPR characterization, although the reduction potential determined by cyclic voltammetry matches that of a typical RS [4Fe-4S] cluster. The reported EPR study of HemN employed photochemical reduction mediated by excess sodium oxalate and 5-deazaflavin, a reductant system stronger than DTH. In comparison, EPR analyses of HutW, a heme-degrading enzyme, show that it can be efficiently reduced using DTH. , We have also demonstrated that the oligomerization state of McHemW depends on the integrity of the [4Fe-4S] cluster-binding site. Loss of the [4Fe-4S] cluster shifted the oligomerization state toward monomers. This observation is consistent with findings for EcHemW, in which the incorporation of the iron–sulfur cluster likewise promotes dimer formation.

In contrast to EcHemW, which has been reported to bind heme covalently, our study demonstrated that McHemW binds heme noncovalently. Given that intracellular labile heme concentrations are maintained at extremely low levels, the micromolar binding affinity argues against direct sequestration of heme from a freely diffusible intracellular pool. Our HDX-MS analysis of McHemW for the first time reveals a localized structural response to heme binding, with significant protection from H/D exchange concentrated within the C-terminal auxiliary domain that is conserved among HemW homologues. The magnitude and rapid onset of protection, together with the EX2 exchange behavior, support a direct role for this region in heme coordination and/or formation of the heme-binding interface. This interpretation is consistent with previous HDX-MS studies of other metalloenzymes, including MshA, SurE, and PqqE. ,, Previously, the conserved CXHNX2YW motif, unique to HemW, was proposed as a putative heme-binding region. , For comparison, cytochrome c family members employ a CX2CH motif in which the two cysteines form covalent, thioether bonds with the heme vinyl groups, anchoring the cofactor to the protein, while the histidine often serves as an axial ligand to the heme iron. Intriguingly, the conserved CXHNX2YW motif in McHemW does not show significant protection from H/D exchange upon heme binding, and the deuteron uptake kinetics only differ slightly in the presence and absence of heme (see Supporting Notes and Figure S9). In contrast, the C-terminal auxiliary domain contains several highly conserved residues, including T346, T376, N384, and F390 (Figure d), that may participate in heme recognition. In the absence of an experimentally determined McHemW structure, AlphaFold provides high-confidence predictions (pLDDT > 90%) for the CXHNX2YW motif and auxiliary domain. If the heme-binding site is indeed located more than 35 Å from the radical SAM site, as suggested by the structural model (Figure c), this spatial separation may explain why HemW does not behave similarly to HemN, whose heme-binding site is postulated to be located near the catalytic cleft of the enzyme.

Although we observed low SAM cleavage activity in HemW in vitro, which can be enhanced by heme binding, its biological role remains to be further explored. ChuW, HutW, and HmuW are classified as class C radical SAM methyltransferases that function in the anaerobic heme degradation pathway. ,,− These enzymes are proposed to use two equivalents of SAM, one to generate a 5′-dAdo radical, and the other to undergo homolytic C–S bond cleavage, producing anaerobilin and related analogs as end products. ,,,, In these systems, 5′-dAdo and SAH are generated in the absence or the presence of heme. , Other known members of the radical SAM enzyme superfamily that utilize two equivalents of SAM are those that catalyze one-carbon transfer reactions to install methyl groups or cyclopropane moieties in a variety of natural products. ,,, In our SSN analysis, HemN, ChuW, HutW, and HmuW consistently formed a tight cluster but did not group with McHemW, indicating a more distant evolutionary relationship (Figure S2). This divergence may explain the weak SAM cleavage activity we observed for McHemW compared to bona fide porphyrin-modifying enzymes. The low residual enzyme activity observed for McHemW was enhanced in the presence of heme and was not biased toward either 5′-dAdo or SAH production, suggesting that heme may play a role in steps preceding the divergence into 5′-dAdo or SAH production. It has been proposed that the heme-Fe center could facilitate electron transfer in HutW. A similar mechanism may explain the increased activity in heme-reconstituted McHemW. Since the CXHNX2YW motif is located between the RS cavity and the heme-binding pocket, raising the possibility that aromatic residues within this region may facilitate communication between the two sites (Figure e). Although this idea remains to be tested, the influence of heme on the activity at the RS site suggests a plausible indirect interaction between heme and the [4Fe-4S] cluster in McHemW.

Conclusion

Using complementary bioinformatic, biochemical, spectroscopic, and HDX-MS approaches, we demonstrate that McHemW is a heme-binding radical SAM enzyme containing a catalytically active [4Fe-4S] cluster. Heme binding enhances SAM cleavage activity, and HDX-MS analysis localizes the heme-binding region to the C-terminal auxiliary domain, providing the first experimental evidence for a putative porphyrin-binding site in a HemW homologue. These findings establish a mechanistic foundation for future investigation of HemW-family enzymes and their biological function in M. catarrhalis.

Materials and Methods

General Information

All chemicals and reagents were purchased from Merck Sigma-Aldrich and Fisher Scientific unless otherwise noted. Plasmid DNA was synthesized by GenScript (Piscataway, NJ). DNA sequencing was performed at the Biological Core Facility at Florida State University. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis for protein and peptide samples was performed at the QB3 Mass Spectrometry Facility, University of California, Berkeley (Berkeley, CA). Amino acid analysis was performed at the Molecular Structure Facility at the University of California, Davis. Primers used for site-directed mutagenesis were obtained from Eurofins (Louisville, KY). Anaerobic experiments were conducted in a mBraun Unilab Pro SP anaerobic chamber equipped with an O2 sensor (Stratham, NH).

Bioinformatic Analysis

Sequence similarity analysis was performed using the RadicalSAM.org Sequence Similarity Network (SSN), a curated database of networks of radical S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) enzymes based on sequence homology. McHemW was queried to determine its cluster membership and relatedness to other radical SAM enzymes. The SSN was examined across a range of alignment score thresholds to evaluate how McHemW grouped with related sequences at varying levels of sequence similarity. Additional sequences listed in Table S1 were similarly queried using their respective UniProt accession numbers to identify their cluster locations and subcluster assignments within the SSN. Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MAFFT, and the resulting alignments were visualized in Jalview to assess the conservation of functionally relevant motifs. To analyze the gene neighborhood of hemW in bacteria, we first searched for HemW homologues in diverse bacterial phyla via BLAST. Homologues were confirmed based on the CXHNX2YW and motif CX3CX2C. Sequences containing the CX3CX2CX2C motif found in HemN were excluded. Gene neighborhoods from the diverse bacteria were initially analyzed using fast.genomics and conserved gene neighborhoods were then visualized using Gene Graphics.

Construct Design and Protein Expression

The gene encoding the M. catarrhalis HemW protein was codon-optimized for E. coli expression, synthesized, and inserted into the pET28b vector between the NdeI and BamHI restriction sites, resulting in the N-terminal His-tagged McHemW plasmid (Table S2). All the McHemW variants were generated using the QuikChange method with the primers listed in Table S2. DNA sequences of these mutations were confirmed by Sanger DNA sequencing using T7 forward and reverse primers. The plasmid encoding the McHemW gene was transformed into E. coli TOP10 competent cells for plasmid amplification and BL21-Gold (DE3) competent cells for protein expression. The expression condition was adapted from the previously published protocol with modifications. Briefly, the transformed McHemW-BL21-Gold (DE3) cells were grown overnight in LB broth containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin at 37 °C. Ten mL of the overnight culture was used to inoculate 1 L of LB broth containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin. The cells were cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached approximately 0.6. The incubation temperature was then reduced to 18 °C with shaking at 90 rpm for 1 h. Protein expression was induced after the 1-h cooling period by the addition of isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (0.3 mM), cysteine (1.5 mM), magnesium sulfate (0.75 mM), and ammonium iron­(III) citrate (0.15 mM). The culture grew at 18 °C with shaking at 90 rpm for 18 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting cell pellet was frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen until purification.

Protein Purification

Protein purification was conducted in the mBruan anaerobic chamber with O2 level maintained below 1 ppm. The purification method was adapted from the previous work with modifications. , The frozen cell pellet was resuspended in Buffer A (50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, pH 7.5), with the addition of 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1× BugBusterⓇ, 100 μM ammonium Fe­(III) citrate, 10 U/mL benzonase, and 0.5 mg/mL lysozyme with continuous stirring for 1 h. The cell lysate was transferred to sealed centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 45 min at 4 °C outside of the anaerobic chamber. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to the anaerobic chamber and loaded onto a Ni-NTA column pre-equilibrated with Buffer A. The column was washed with at least five column volumes of Buffer A containing 1 mM DTT. McHemW was eluted with Buffer B (50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT, pH 7.5). The colored elution was collected and concentrated using an Amicon Ultra 0.5 mL 30K spin filter, with each concentration step performed for 12 min at 14,000 × g. The concentrated protein was loaded onto a PD-10 column equilibrated with Buffer C (50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) to remove imidazole. When chemical reconstitution was performed, ammonium Fe­(III) citrate and sodium sulfide were added to the protein sample after the initial PD-10 desalting step and incubated at room temperature while mixing for 30 min. The sample was then passed through a second PD-10 column equilibrated with Buffer C to remove excess iron and sulfur. Protein samples were further concentrated before being flash-frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen immediately after removal from the anaerobic chamber. Protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay with bovine serum albumin as the standard. A calibration curve was generated by measuring absorbance at 595 nm after incubation with Bradford reagent (Thermo Scientific Chemicals Bradford Dye Reagent, Ready-to-Use soln.). Quantitative amino acid analysis of McHemW was performed at the Molecular Structure Facility at the University of California, Davis, as previously published. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay, yielding 25.5 μg/μL. The same sample was subsequently analyzed by amino acid analysis, which gave a concentration of 16.6 μg/μL. A correction factor of 0.65 was calculated and applied to all measured McHemW protein concentrations.

Iron Content Determination

The iron content assay was performed under aerobic conditions using a ferrozine-based method, as previously reported. The assay required the preparation of the following solutions: 22% (v/v) HNO3, 7.5% (w/v) ammonium acetate, freshly prepared 12.5% (w/v) ascorbic acid, and 10 mM ferrozine. A standard curve was generated using iron standards diluted with water at concentrations ranging from 0 μM to 1000 μM. For each standard or sample, 100 μL was incubated in triplicate with 100 μL of 22% HNO3 at 90 °C for 30 min. After incubation, samples and standards were cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 min to pellet any precipitate. 600 μL of 7.5% ammonium acetate was added to neutralize the solution. Subsequently, 100 μL of 12.5% ascorbic acid and 100 μL of 10 mM ferrozine were added to each tube. The mixtures were centrifuged again at 15,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatants were transferred to cuvettes. Absorbance at 562 nm was measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. A linear standard curve was plotted to determine the iron content in the samples.

Sulfur Content Determination

The sulfur content of McHemW was determined by following previously reported methods under anaerobic conditions. , Sodium sulfide standards ranging from 0 to 1000 μM were prepared in water and used for each assay to generate a calibration curve for calculating the sulfide content per monomer of McHemW. A 200 μL aliquot of each sodium sulfide standard and a triplicate of McHemW samples were incubated for 15 min at room temperature with 600 μL of 1% (w/v) zinc acetate (freshly prepared) and 50 μL of 12% (v/v) NaOH. The samples were then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 1 min before adding 150 μL of 0.1% N,N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (freshly prepared in 5 M HCl) and 150 μL of 10 mM FeCl3 (freshly prepared in 1 M HCl). The solutions were then mixed by inversion and incubated for 30 min before being removed from the anaerobic chamber and transferred to cuvettes, and the absorbance at 670 nm was monitored using an Agilent Cary 60 UV–vis spectrophotometer. McHemW sulfide content per monomer was calculated using the linear regression equation obtained from the sulfide-standard calibration curve.

UV–Vis Absorption Spectroscopy

Freshly purified protein samples were diluted to 10 μM in the anaerobic chamber, and the resulting solutions were transferred to 1 mL quartz cuvettes sealed with screw caps before being removed from the chamber. UV–vis spectra were recorded using an Agilent Cary 60 spectrometer. For DTH-treated samples, 250 μM DTH was added to the protein solution in the anaerobic chamber, followed by a 10 min incubation before being transferred to the cuvette for measurement.

Anaerobic Size Exclusion Chromatography

The oligomerization state of McHemW was determined using fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) with a Cytiva Superdex 200 Increase size-exclusion chromatography column. McHemW samples at various concentrations (10, 30, 40, 50, 100, and 200 μM) were injected into the column, which had been equilibrated with degassed Buffer C. Elution was performed using the same buffer at a flow rate of 0.22 mL/min, and the absorbance at 280 nm was monitored. This was repeated with 50 μM of the McHemW ΔRS variant. Sigma Protein Standard Mix (15–600 kDa) was analyzed under identical conditions to generate a standard curve. The molecular weight of McHemW was estimated based on its elution time relative to the standard curve.

Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) Spectroscopy Sample Preparation

All EPR samples were prepared in an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratories, 98% N2, 2% H2). Glycerol was added to a final concentration of 15% in the 200–300 μM McHemW sample in Buffer C for all EPR measurements. For dithionite (DTH)-reduced samples, 10 equiv of DTH were added to the McHemW sample, which was then transferred to an X-band EPR quartz tube and incubated for 10 min in the anaerobic chamber before being flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen inside the chamber. For the samples containing K13CN, ∼100 equiv of K13CN was mixed with the reduced McHemW for 2 min before flash frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Continuous Wave (CW) EPR Experiments

CW EPR measurements were carried out at X-band (∼9.4 GHz) using a Bruker EleXsys E500 spectrometer equipped with a superhigh Q resonator (ER4122SHQE). Cryogenic temperatures were maintained using an Oxford Instruments ESR900 continuous-flow liquid helium cryostat with a temperature controller (Oxford Instruments ITC503). Spectra were collected at 10 K unless otherwise noted. Spectrometer parameters were as follows: 60 ms conversion time, 0.2 mW power, 5 G modulation amplitude, and 100 kHz modulation frequency. Spectral simulations were performed using EasySpin 6.0.4 in MATLAB.

Pulsed EPR Experiments

Hyperfine sublevel correlation (HYSCORE) spectra were obtained at X-band and 10 K on a Bruker Biospin EleXsys E-580 spectrometer using a MS5 split-ring resonator. The standard four-pulsed sequence for HYSCORE π/2−τ–π/2–t1−π–t2−π/2−τ–echo with 8-step phase cycling was programmed in PulseSPEL via the XEPR interface. A pulse-length of 12 ns was used for the π/2 pulse (t π/2) and 24 ns for the inversion pulse (t π). Time domain spectra were baseline-corrected (third-order polynomial), apodized with a hamming window, zero-filled to 8-fold points, and fast Fourier-transformed to yield the frequency domain spectra. Spectrometer settings were: microwave frequency of 9.195 GHz, magnetic field of 338.6 mT, and τ of 140 ns for g = 1.94; the time increment in both dimensions was 24 ns with 180 steps.

Heme Binding Assays

Heme reconstitution was performed under anaerobic conditions. A heme stock solution was prepared by dissolving 4 mg of hemin (ferriprotoporphyrin IX chloride, Sigma-Aldrich) in 400 μL of 50 mM NaOH. The solution was mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min to ensure complete dissolution. Following incubation, 400 μL of 1 M Tris-Cl was added to the solution, and the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 12 min. The concentration of the resulting supernatant was determined to be 2 mM using an extinction coefficient of 58.4 mM–1cm–1 at 385 nm. 10 μM of heme solution was added to 10 μM McHemW and the mixture was incubated overnight at 25 °C for 16 h. Binding controls include 10 μM BSA or 10 μM PqqE (prepared as described previously) with and without 10 μM of heme. For the UV–vis scan under reduced conditions, 250 μM DTH was incubated with 10 μM protein for 10 min in the presence or absence of 10 μM heme. Heme titration experiments were performed in the same manner with 0, 0.5, 1, 3.5, 5, 10, and 15 μM heme added to 5 μM McHemW. The control titration experiment was performed in the absence of McHemW. All samples were transferred to sealed cuvettes and analyzed using a UV–vis spectrometer. To determine the dissociation constant (K d), we converted the UV–vis measurements to plate-reader format to increase throughput. Five μM protein or buffer control was incubated anaerobically with increasing heme concentrations (0, 5, 10, 12.5, 15, 17.5, 20, 22.5, 25, 30, and 32.5 μM) for 30 min before being transferred to a 384-well plate in duplicate. The plate was sealed with a clear film, and absorbance spectra were collected from 220 to 650 nm using the BMG Labstar VANTAstar microplate reader. Binding was monitored at 411 nm, and the resulting data were fitted with a four-parameter variable-slope model to determine the K d.

Heme Staining PAGE Assays

To assess heme binding by SDS-PAGE and native-PAGE, we analyzed WT McHemW, heme-reconstituted WT McHemW, ΔRS samples, and appropriate controls, including cytochrome c, hemoglobin, and free heme. An equal molar amount of heme solution was added to McHemW and incubated overnight at 25 °C for 16 h prior to measurement. The denatured protein marker (Precision Plus Protein Dual Color Standard) was used for SDS-PAGE and native-PAGE. All samples were run on Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels under standard electrophoresis conditions. The heme staining solution containing 3,3′,5,5′ tetramethylbenzidine (TMBZ) and sodium acetate was freshly prepared in the dark. 6.3 mM TMBZ dissolved in methanol and mixed with 0.25 M sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.0, in a 7:3 ratio. Following electrophoresis, the gel was incubated in the staining solution in the dark for 1.5 h with occasional mixing. Incubation was followed by the addition of 30% H2O2 to a final concentration of 0.3% (v/v), and heme staining became visible within approximately 30 min. After the TMBZ reaction, the same gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to visualize the protein.

HemW-Derived Peptide Identification Using LC-MS/MS

McHemW-derived peptide libraries were generated anaerobically using a proteolysis procedure optimized to maximize peptide coverage across McHemW. Aliquots of stock solutions and buffers stored outside the anaerobic chamber were degassed and equilibrated under anaerobic conditions for at least 30 min, with purging prior to setting up the reactions. A stock of McHemW was diluted into a H2O Buffer (50 mM EPPS, 10 mM TCEP, 30 mM KCl, pH 8.0) to obtain a final concentration of 50 μM McHemW. 40 μL of H2O Buffer was added to 10 μL of 50 μM McHemW. After a 30 min incubation, 15 μL Quenching Buffer (2 M citric acid, 3 M guanidine-HCl, 3 mM TCEP, pH 1.0) was added to the protein sample at room temperature, followed by the addition of 4 μL of 2 mg/mL pepsin and 1 μL of 2 mg/mL protease from Aspergillus saitoi Type XIII. The resulting samples were immediately placed within a −4 °C cooler for 12 min before being flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. These samples were analyzed using an LTQ-Orbitrap-XL mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source and connected in line with a 1200 Series LC system (Agilent) with a C8 column. Peptide fragments derived from McHemW and pepsin were separated and analyzed as described before. ,,

HDX-MS Sample Preparation

All HDX labeling experiments were performed at room temperature under anaerobic conditions. Aliquots of stock solutions and buffers stored outside the anaerobic chamber were degassed and equilibrated under anaerobic conditions for at least 30 min, with purging prior to setting up the reactions. For the sample containing McHemW alone, a 10 μL aliquot of 50 μM of McHemW stock was incubated at room temperature for 15 min before mixing with 40 μL of D2O Buffer (50 mM EPPS, 10 mM TCEP, 30 mM KCl, pD 8.0), yielding a final McHemW concentration of 10 μM during labeling. For the heme-bound samples, 25 μM heme was included during labeling. The percentage of deuterated water during labeling is 79% under all conditions. The labeling reactions were quenched by mixing 15 μL of Quenching Buffer (2 M citric acid, 3 M guanidine-HCl, 3 mM TCEP, pH 1.0) at 120, 600, 1800, 3600, and 10800 s at −4 °C for 2 min in the anaerobic chamber before proteolysis was initiated by adding 4 μL of 2 mg/mL pepsin and 1 μL 2 mg/mL protease from A. saitoi Type XIII for 12 min. 60 μL of the resulting solution was flash frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen before being injected into the high-resolution LC-MS. A 0 min time point was also prepared using the same procedure as the 2 min time point, except that H2O Buffer (50 mM EPPS, 10 mM TCEP, 30 mM KCl, pH 8.0) was used instead of the D2O Buffer. All experiments were performed in triplicate using protein samples obtained from separate expression and purification batches. The LC-MS method was adopted from previous publications. ,,

HDX-MS Data Analysis

The raw MS data were analyzed using HDX WorkBench. A set of high-quality nonoverlapping peptides was selected and analyzed to provide the best coverage of the entire protein (see HDX Data Summary). Each analyzed peptide was curated manually after automatic peak detection to ensure consistency and accuracy. , The data reported here were not corrected for back exchange, as only the difference plot between the two conditions was considered during analysis. An unpaired Walsh’s t-test was performed on the triplicate % exchange data for each set of peptides under different conditions to compare conditions and determine the difference in percent deuterium uptake (Δ%D) at each time point. Volcano plots were generated by comparing the difference between two sets of percentage deuterium uptake, and statistically significant peptides were identified as P < 0.01. The Δ%D across time points was plotted for each peptide, and a 10% cutoff was applied based on previous HDX-MS studies and the size of the peptides analyzed in this work.

HPLC-Based Enzyme Activity Determination

All enzymatic activity was performed at room temperature under anaerobic conditions. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding 50 μM McHemW to a reaction mixture containing 2 mM SAM and 1 mM DTH. Reactions were quenched by the addition of 5% formic acid before centrifuging at 14,800 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was heated at 80 °C for 5 min before centrifuging at 14,800 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatants were analyzed using a Thermo Vanquish HPLC equipped with a Thermo Scientific Hypersil Gold C18 column using a linear gradient elution method. The mobile phase consisted of A: 1 mM ammonium acetate, pH 5.0, and B: acetonitrile. The flow rate was maintained at 0.25 mL/min, and the absorption at 260 nm was monitored. The gradient began at 0% acetonitrile and increased to 15% over 10 min, followed by an increase to 80% B at 25 min. The percentage for solvent B was then increased to 100% at 25 min and was held at 100% B until 30 min, after which the gradient was returned to 0% B by 35 min. The column was re-equilibrated at 0% acetonitrile until the end of the 40 min run. An injection volume of 10 μL was used for both standards and samples. Authentic standards, including SAM, S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), and 5′-dAdo, were prepared at a concentration of 100 μM and subjected to the same HPLC method. Peaks corresponding to reaction products and standards were identified based on their retention times. Various concentrations of SAH and 5′-dAdo (1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 100 μM) were analyzed using the same method to establish a calibration curve for product quantification. Data acquisition and processing were performed using Thermo Chromeleon software.

LC-MS Enzyme Activity Analysis

The same reaction mixtures and standards used for activity determination by HPLC were also used for LC-MS analysis. The reaction mixture was injected into the Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with the Thermo TSQ Quantis Mass Spectrometer (LC-MS) system, equipped with a Thermo Scientific Hypersil Gold C18 Selectivity column. The gradient elution used 1 mM ammonium acetate, pH 5.0, and acetonitrile, both containing 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was maintained at 0.25 mL/min throughout the run. A divert valve was used for 1 min. After 1 min the gradient began at 0% acetonitrile and increased to 100% over 8.5 min and was held at 100% B until 11 min. After the gradient was returned to 0% acetonitrile, it was equilibrated for the remainder of the 14 min run. An injection volume of 10 μL was used. The sample was analyzed using an ESI source operated in positive-ion mode with Q1 full-scan acquisition. The scan range was set from m/z 200 to 1000 with a scan speed of 1000 Da per second. The LC flow rate was set to 250 μL/min. Ion source parameters were as follows: spray voltage 3500 V in positive ion mode and 2500 V in negative ion mode, sheath gas 50 arbitrary units, auxiliary gas 10 arbitrary units, sweep gas 1.0 arbitrary units, ion transfer tube temperature 325 °C, and vaporizer temperature 350 °C. Data acquisition and processing were performed using Thermo Xcalibur and FreeStyle software.
