Association between triglyceride glucose-body mass index and the staging of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis and fibrosis in patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
Authors: Fan Zhang, Yan Han, Yunfei Wu, Zuowei Bao, Guojun Zheng, Jianhong Liu, Wenjian Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to thoroughly investigate the clinical value of triglyceride glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) in patients diagnosed with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Specifically, we aimed to determine its association with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and the progression of liver fibrosis.MethodsThe study included 393 patients diagnosed with NAFLD after liver biopsy. The patients were divided into two distinct cohorts: a training cohort (N = 320) and a validation cohort (N = 73). The training cohort was further divided into four groups based on TyG-BMI quartiles. The clinical characteristics of the patients in each group were compared in detail, and the association between TyG-BMI and NASH, NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis was analyzed using multiple models. Additionally, we generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the predictive ability of TyG-BMI for NASH and fibrosis staging in patients with NAFLD.ResultsPatients with higher TyG-BMI values had a significantly higher prevalence of NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis (all p < .05). TyG-BMI was an independent predictor of these diseases in both unadjusted and adjusted models (all p < .05). ROC curve analysis further revealed the excellent performance of TyG-BMI in predicting NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis. The validation cohort yielded analogous results. Furthermore, we constructed three multivariate models of TyG-BMI in conjunction with elastography metrics, which demonstrated elevated diagnostic AUC values of 0.782, 0.792, 0.794, 0.785, 0.834, and 0.845, respectively.ConclusionThis study confirms a significant association between insulin resistance and NAFLD, including at-risk NASH and fibrosis staging, as assessed using the TyG-BMI index. TyG-BMI and its associated multivariate models can be valuable noninvasive indicators for NAFLD diagnosis, risk stratification, and disease course monitoring.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a metabolic stress liver injury closely linked to insulin resistance (IR) and genetic susceptibility. It is characterized by the presence of 5% or more steatosis in hepatocytes after the exclusion of alcohol and other definitive liver injury factors [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of NAFLD is increasing every year and is now the leading cause of liver disease in 20%–30% of the global population [2].

The progression of NAFLD typically begins with simple non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFL). Without timely intervention, NAFL may progress to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which is accompanied by the development of liver fibrosis [3]. Fibrosis is a crucial factor in the progression of chronic liver disease to cirrhosis, and its progression is more rapid in NASH patients. A meta-analysis has revealed that fibrosis staging in NASH patients worsens by one grade every 7.1 years on average, compared to 14.3 years in NAFL patients [4]. Patients with NASH, particularly those with high histologic activity and fibrosis staging of 2 or higher, experience a significant increase in liver-related events and mortality [5]. For patients with cirrhosis associated with NASH, the annual risk of developing hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is approximately 1.5%–2% [6]. Early diagnosis of NASH and close monitoring of liver fibrosis progression are crucial.

While liver biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing NASH and evaluating disease activity and fibrosis, its invasive nature, associated risks, and limitations make it impractical [7]. In recent years, serum noninvasive diagnostic markers have gained widespread attention due to their noninvasive, low-cost, and highly reproducible advantages [8]. In the population with NAFLD, it is crucial to find biomarkers that can accurately diagnose NASH, at-risk NASH, and specific fibrosis thresholds. This will reduce the need for unnecessary liver biopsies and improve the efficiency of clinical interventions [9].

The pathogenesis and progression of NASH are complex and involve multiple factors, including IR, inflammation, oxidative stress, and hepatocyte death [10]. IR plays a central role in the development of NAFLD and progressive liver fibrosis [11]. However, assessing IR through traditional methods, such as the high-insulin positive glucose clamp technique, can be accurate but complex and costly. These methods are not suitable for widespread clinical use [12].

The Triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) is a widely used reflective index of IR in clinical settings [13]. TyG-BMI combines the information of body mass index (BMI) and TyG index, providing a more comprehensive reflection of the metabolic status of patients [14]. Studies have shown that TyG-BMI is closely related to insulin sensitivity and can effectively predict the occurrence of NAFLD [15]. However, studies examining the relationship between TyG-BMI and NASH activity and fibrosis staging in patients with NAFLD still need to be completed.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association of TyG-BMI with the progression of NASH and liver fibrosis in patients with NAFLD. This study aims to determine whether TyG-BMI can serve as a reliable, noninvasive indicator for diagnosing and stratifying risk and monitoring the progression of NAFLD. The findings of this study may offer new insights and approaches for the clinical management of NAFLD.

Materials and methods

Research participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from April 2020 to May 2024 at the Changzhou Third People’s Hospital. The study included patients who underwent liver biopsy and were diagnosed with NAFLD. To ensure accuracy and consistency in the study, we established exclusion criteria. These criteria included patients under 18 years of age, male patients who consumed more than 140 g of ethanol per week, and female patients who consumed more than 70 g of ethanol per week. Patients co-infected with hepatitis viruses, human immunodeficiency viruses, aspergillus viruses, cytomegaloviruses, or other viruses were excluded. The study investigated the development of severe infections, autoimmune diseases, chronic kidney diseases, biliary tract diseases, cardiovascular system diseases, active malignant tumors, and drug-induced hepatitis. Incomplete data recording was also considered. After rigorous screening, 393 eligible participants were recruited for this study. The study adhered strictly to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Changzhou Third People’s Hospital. Before participating in the study, all participants were informed of the study and provided written informed consent.

Liver biopsy procedures

To assess the pathological state of the liver, including the degree of steatosis, NASH, and fibrosis, we utilized the Menghini technique for liver biopsy. Liver tissue fragments obtained by biopsy were fixed in 10% formalin, followed by paraffin embedding and microtome sectioning. We processed the sections with hematoxylin-eosin, van Gieson, Gomory, and Masson trichrome stains to enhance the visualization of histopathological changes. The histologic grading and staging of the pathological features, including steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, and fibrosis, strictly followed the classification proposed by Kleiner and Brunts in 2005 [16]. Fibrosis was quantified and graded in detail, with clinically significant fibrosis defined as a fibrosis score of F ≥ 2, advanced fibrosis as F ≥ 3, and cirrhosis as F = 4. Furthermore, the NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) was calculated by adding the scores for steatosis, hepatocyte ballooning, and lobular inflammation. The diagnosis of NASH was based on a specific combination of pathologic features [17]. At-risk NASH was defined as the presence of NASH, NAS ≥ 4, and fibrosis stage ≥ 2 [9].

Disease definition criteria

This study clarifies the criteria for defining the following diseases. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was diagnosed based on a previous diagnosis by a specialized physician, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level ≥ 6.5%, or the use of diabetes medications [18]. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) over 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) over 90 mmHg. Dyslipidemia was defined based on abnormal levels of total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides (TG), or a history of treatment for dyslipidemia [19].

Measurement indicators and methods

To ensure accurate and consistent data, we took routine height and weight measurements from all participants. We calculated the BMI using the formula BMI = weight/height2 (kg/m2) from these measurements. Trained investigators took blood pressure measurements using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer, and we used the average of three measurements for analysis. The methods recommended by the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) were used for several biochemical parameters, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin transaminase (AST), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), TC, TG, HDL-c, LDL-c, C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine, uric acid (UA), and glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), for the blood tests. FPG was measured using dry chemistry. All participants were required to fast for 8-10 h before undergoing early morning fasting venous blood sampling. Additionally, we measured levels of HbA1c using high-performance liquid chromatography and conducted routine blood tests with a fully automated hematology analyzer.

Noninvasive assessment of liver stiffness

We used a FibroScan-502 probe to measure liver stiffness measurement (LSM). Patients had to fast for at least eight hours and lie down during the measurement. We placed the probe vertically on the skin in the intercostal region to get accurate data on LSM and controlled attenuation parameter (CAP). To ensure the reliability of the data, we set guidelines that included a specific range for the interquartile spacing to median ratio. We calculated the final LSM based on the median of ten successful measurements. This result was crucial for our analysis [20].

Noninvasive diagnostic indicators

The TyG index is calculated using the following formula: TyG = Ln [TG (mg/dl) × FPG (mg/dl)/2] [21]. The TyG-BMI is calculated by multiplying the TyG value by the BMI value. Furthermore, three multivariate models were constructed by combining TyG-BMI with CAP and LSM. These were calculated as follows: TyG-BMI-CAP = TyG × BMI × CAP; TyG-BMI-LSM = TyG × BMI × LSM; TyG-BMI-CAP-LSM = TyG × BMI × CAP × LSM. The FibroScan-AST (FAST) score was calculated using the following formula: [e^((−1.65 + 1.07 × In(LSM) + 2.66 × 10−8 × CAP3 − 63.3 × AST−1)])/[1 + e^((−1.65 +1.07 × In(LSM) + 2.66 × 10−8 × CAP3 − 63.3 × AST−1)])] [22].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation or expressed using the median (25th and 75th percentile), assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, depending on normal distribution. Categorical variables were displayed as frequencies and percentages. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous data, and the chi-square test was used for categorical data to compare group differences. Logistic regression models were utilized to investigate the relationship between TyG-BMI and various conditions in patients with NAFLD, including NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-Risk NASH, significant Fibrosis, advanced Fibrosis, and Cirrhosis. Three models were created: model 1 was the crude model with no adjustments, model 2 was adjusted for age and gender, and model 3 was adjusted for model 2 plus CRP, PLT, TBIL, TP, TC, eGFR, AST, and Hypertension. The reference was defined as the lowest quartiles of TyG-BMI. Odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated and presented separately for quartiles and continuous variables. Additionally, we used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to assess the predictive value of TyG-BMI and other variables for various conditions, including NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis. We evaluated diagnostic accuracy by calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and sensitivity and specificity. Significance was attributed to P values below 0.05 in two-tailed tests. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (SPSS, IBM, Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.0 (http://www.r-project.org, The R Foundation). Figures were plotted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Study subgroups and participant screening

The study screened 424 patients diagnosed with NAFLD by liver biopsy between April 2020 and May 2024 at the Changzhou Third People’s Hospital. After applying strict exclusion criteria, including excessive alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, autoimmune diseases, active malignancy, and incomplete data, 393 eligible participants were included in the study. The patients were divided into a training cohort (N = 320) and a validation cohort (N = 73). To ensure scientific validity and accuracy, we divided the training cohort subjects into four groups based on TyG-BMI quartiles (Figure 1).

Study flowchart.

Clinical characterization of participants in the training cohort

The study participants had a mean age of 50 (38-57) years, with 129 (40.31%) male. The prevalence of NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis were 245 (76.56%), 248 (77.50%), 80 (25.00%), 92 (28.75%), 44 (13.75%), and 22 (6.88%), respectively. Subjects with higher TyG-BMI values exhibited a trend towards an increased proportion of males, higher BMI, higher SBP, and a significantly higher prevalence of T2DM and dyslipidemia (all p < .05). Most laboratory indicators were consistent with clinical trends, except for HDL-c, which exhibited an inverse trend with TyG-BMI. Higher TG, HbA1c, FPG, UA, and CRP levels were found in participants with higher TyG-BMI values. Additionally, FibroScan findings confirmed that subjects with higher TyG-BMI values had higher LSM and CAP (all p < .05). The liver biopsy results revealed a significant increase in the prevalence of NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis in subjects with higher TyG-BMI values (all p < .05) (Table 1).

The relationship between TyG-BMI and disease severity in NAFLD patients

To investigate the relationship between TyG-BMI and disease severity in NAFLD patients, we analyzed TyG-BMI as a continuous and categorical variable. Multiple models were used to adjust for confounding factors, and the results consistently showed that TyG-BMI was an independent predictor of NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis, with or without adjustment (all p < .05) (Table 2). Furthermore, our additional analysis, after dividing TyG-BMI into quartiles, revealed higher ORs and 95% CIs for NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis in the fourth quartile of TyG-BMI compared to the first quartile in three different models (all p < .05). This association was significant in model 3 after adjusting for age, gender, CRP, PLT, TBIL, TP, TC, eGFR, AST, and Hypertension. The ORs and 95% CIs of superiority for the disease above states were significantly higher in the fourth TyG-BMI quartile than the first. Specifically, the ratios were 4.82 (2.00–11.58), 5.78 (2.28–14.61), 5.84 (2.39–14.29), 5.67 (2.43–13.26), 6.63 (1.95–22.57), and 86.60 (5.08–1475.34) (all p < .01) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study utilized a cross-sectional research design to examine the potential correlation between TyG-BMI and various aspects of NAFLD, such as NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis. The subsequent data analysis revealed the following significant findings: The study data showed a significant increasing trend in the probability of subjects developing NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis as the TyG-BMI value increased. This finding suggests that TyG-BMI may be an important clinical indicator closely related to disease severity in patients with NAFLD. To accurately assess the relationship between TyG-BMI and disease status, we included several potential confounders in the model for adjustment. The adjusted analyses revealed a dose-response relationship between different quartiles of TyG-BMI. Specifically, the prevalence of each disease state increased significantly as the TyG-BMI quartile increased. The results of the ROC analysis indicate that TyG-BMI is a reliable predictor for NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis. Furthermore, the multivariate models combining TyG-BMI with CAP and LSM demonstrated superior predictive capabilities for each disease state, with AUC values of 0.782, 0.792, 0.794, 0.785, 0.834, and 0.845, respectively, exceeding those of other variables and models. This finding supports the use of TyG-BMI as a screening tool for NASH and fibrosis staging in patients with NAFLD.

Identifying NASH and its high-risk forms in patients with NAFLD is crucial. NASH is a significant pathological stage of NAFLD, characterized by hepatocellular injury, necroinflammation, and fibrosis development [23]. It is important to note that 5% to 15% of patients in the NASH population have combined liver fibrosis, which is a concerning factor for disease progression in this group of patients. Statistically, they have a 30% chance of developing cirrhosis within ten years, and 3% may even experience acute liver failure, which puts them at risk of transformation to hepatocellular carcinoma [24]. At-risk NASH patients, especially those with significant liver fibrosis, have a higher risk of liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma [22]. Early identification and intervention of at-risk NASH patients is crucial for improving their prognosis and quality of life.

Liver fibrosis staging is crucial in predicting disease progression during NAFLD [25]. Fibrosis is a critical aspect of chronic liver disease progression to cirrhosis, and its various stages indicate different levels of liver damage and repair [26]. Advanced fibrosis, in particular, is a prognostic indicator of worsening liver-related outcomes and increased overall mortality and is strongly associated with an exponential increase in the risk of liver-related death [27]. Liver fibrosis staging is crucial in determining the treatment strategy for patients with NAFLD [28]. Non-pharmacological interventions, such as lifestyle modifications and metabolic factor control, can reverse or delay the fibrotic process in patients with early-stage fibrosis. However, patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis may be misdiagnosed as lacking these profound phenotypes, leading to a lack of necessary surveillance for hepatocellular carcinoma or gastroesophageal varices during follow-up. Accurately staging liver fibrosis in patients with cirrhosis is particularly important to ensure timely management and control of the disease. This has significant implications for predicting disease progression, assessing patient prognosis, and guiding the development of clinical treatment strategies for patients with NAFLD.

Liver biopsy pathology is currently considered the gold standard for diagnosing NASH and its high-risk forms, as well as for staging liver fibrosis. However, it has limitations due to its invasiveness, sampling errors, subjective differences between observers, and inability to continuously and dynamically monitor changes in lesions in chronic diseases such as NAFLD [29]. Therefore, it is unsuitable for long-term follow-up and monitoring. Therefore, research in this field has increasingly focused on noninvasive diagnostic methods. In recent years, several noninvasive diagnostic models and serologic markers have been developed and validated in clinical studies to assess liver health. However, the accuracy of their independent validation has often been questioned, which limits their widespread use in clinical practice [22]. Meanwhile, noninvasive imaging techniques such as abdominal ultrasound, transient elastography (e.g. FibroScan), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are widely used to assess the degree of steatosis and fibrosis in the liver. These methods have gained significant attention in clinical practice due to their noninvasive nature, ease of use, and reproducibility. However, they still have limitations in diagnosing NASH and performing large-scale clinical dissemination [30]. Although noninvasive methods can detect simple steatosis and advanced fibrosis, they are generally ineffective in detecting the presence or severity of NASH or assessing the progression of NAFLD, such as changes within the fibrosis stage. Therefore, clinicians must find screening biomarkers that can successfully identify most NASH patients, as well as NAFLD patients with different fibrosis stages, in order to formulate treatment plans and predict patient prognosis.

The development of NASH is a multifactorial and complex process. It involves imbalanced nutritional intake, dysregulation of the endocrine system (particularly abnormalities of insulin, leptin, adiponectin, and gastrin), alterations of the gut microbiota (such as endotoxemia), and the influence of epigenetic factors [31]. Despite years of research, the exact molecular mechanisms underlying the development of NASH and its associated fibrosis have yet to be fully revealed. IR is strongly associated with NASH and liver fibrosis [32]. It reduces the responsiveness of adipose tissue to insulin, accelerating lipolysis and increasing the plasma concentration of free fatty acids (FFA). IR is a significant factor in this process. Hepatocytes subsequently take up these FFA, leading to abnormal accumulation of intracellular fat and ultimately triggering the development of NASH [33]. Hepatic insulin resistance disrupts the average glucose metabolic pathway, impairing insulin function in activating glycogen synthase. This results in increased glucose shunting to the lipogenic pathway, thereby worsening the development of NAFLD [34]. The accumulation of fat in the liver not only causes hepatocellular dysfunction and injury but initiates a cascade of inflammatory responses, which play a crucial role in the development of fibrosis. In cases of IR, fat accumulation in liver cells leads to dysfunction of the mitochondria and the production of high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS can cause oxidative stress and damage to liver cell membranes and DNA, which not only exacerbates the pathological process of NASH but also accelerates the progression of fibrosis. In addition, IR induces the release of inflammatory factors from adipose tissue, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1 (IL-1) [35]. These factors are then transported to the liver through the bloodstream and activate inflammatory cells, including macrophages and Kupffer cells, which worsen the inflammatory response in the liver. A chronic inflammatory environment can cause hepatocellular damage and fibrosis, leading to the development of NASH. It is important to note that insulin resistance can also affect the expression of various cytokines and growth factors, such as transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), which play a significant role in liver fibrosis [36]. IR and its related molecular mechanisms are crucial in the onset and development of liver fibrosis. They promote fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. Therefore, gaining an in-depth understanding of these mechanisms for the pathogenesis and treatment of NASH is clinically significant.

The TyG index is a widely recognized tool for assessing insulin resistance [37]. It is calculated as the log-transformed product of triglycerides and glucose. A derivative called TyG-BMI has been developed to broaden its clinical application by combining the TyG index with BMI. Recent studies have consistently shown that TyG-BMI is a better predictor of insulin resistance than TyG alone [38]. TyG-BMI is an innovative metric that considers essential health parameters such as triglyceride levels, glycemic status, and obesity. Its accuracy and usefulness in predicting NAFLD risk have been demonstrated [39]. Additionally, the study suggests a potential correlation between TyG-BMI, an indicator of insulin resistance, and the occurrence and progression of NASH [15]. Previous research has shown that higher TyG-BMI values are associated with more severe hepatic steatosis and liver fibrosis, increasing the risk of NASH development in patients [40]. A recent study by Khamseh et al. has further confirmed the value of TyG-BMI in overweight and obese populations. The study found a significant correlation between TyG-BMI and NAFLD, with an even stronger correlation between liver fibrosis and TyG-BMI. This finding emphasizes the critical role of TyG-BMI in identifying patients at high risk of liver fibrosis [41]. In recent years, there has been a growing recognition of sarcopenia as a significant pathological factor in the development of NAFLD [42]. There is a robust correlation between sarcopenia and insulin resistance, primarily evidenced by skeletal muscle atrophy, the primary target organ of insulin action [43]. Furthermore, sarcopenia may result in sarcopenia obesity, which in turn increases the risk of NASH, liver fibrosis, and cardiovascular disease [44]. Notably, elevated TyG and its related markers have been demonstrated to show a significant positive correlation with the prevalence of sarcopenia and frailty [45]. The present findings provide further evidence in support of the correlation between TyG-BMI and NAFLD. However, it is essential to note that current studies on the correlation between TyG-BMI and NASH and liver fibrosis have mainly relied on noninvasive assessment methods. Although informative, these methods may still be subject to potential limitations and biases. Moreover, there are relatively few studies that use TyG-BMI to predict the stage of NASH and liver fibrosis in patients with NAFLD.

This study analyzed the effectiveness of TyG-BMI as a noninvasive index in predicting NASH and its associated liver fibrosis staging in patients with NAFLD. The results showed that TyG-BMI demonstrated good predictive ability for NASH, NAS ≥ 4, at-risk NASH, significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis. The corresponding AUC values for TyG-BMI were 0.650, 0.661, 0.652, 0.645, 0.627, and 0.715. The validation cohort yielded analogous results. These findings indicate that TyG-BMI, a measurement that combines data on triglycerides, glucose, and body mass index, has the potential to identify individuals with NAFLD who may develop NASH or have varying stages of liver fibrosis. This measurement can effectively screen patients for clinical trials and emerging therapies and may reduce the need for unnecessary liver biopsies. Additionally, our study suggests that using TyG-BMI alone for identification and staging is suboptimal (AUC < 0.80). Consequently, we constructed three multivariate models combining TyG-BMI with CAP and LSM, demonstrating high AUC values of 0.782, 0.792, 0.794, 0.785, 0.834, and 0.845 in predicting each disease state. This result surpassed TyG-BMI, CAP, LSM, and FAST scores alone. These findings indicate that integrating multiple diagnostic modalities can markedly enhance the diagnostic precision of NAFLD and its associated complications.

The present study has several strengths in exploring the relationship between TyG-BMI and NASH and liver fibrosis staging in patients with NAFLD. Firstly, this report is the first to link TyG-BMI with NASH and liver fibrosis staging in patients with NAFLD. This study provides a theoretical basis for developing biomarkers for diagnosing at-risk NASH and its related components. This study improves our understanding of NASH pathogenesis and provides clinicians with a more straightforward and more effective diagnostic tool. Secondly, the study used liver biopsy as the gold standard for assessing NAFLD lesions, which allowed for a precise definition of histologic changes and provided an accurate and reliable diagnostic basis. This method ensured the accuracy and credibility of the study results. Thirdly, the TyG-BMI is a non-invasive, simple, low-cost index that only tests TG, FPG, and BMI to produce results. Compared to liver puncture biopsy, transient elastography, CT, and MRI, TyG-BMI offers a superior cost-benefit ratio. Consequently, TyG-BMI has a broad range of applications in clinical practice, particularly in regions with limited resources or restricted diagnostic capabilities. In areas where it is available, the combination of TyG-BMI with transient elastography represents an effective alternative to liver biopsy, offering a superior diagnostic performance.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, as a single-center cross-sectional study, the sample may only partially represent the characteristics of the general population. Additionally, due to the lack of follow-up data, it is difficult to have a comprehensive view of NAFLD patients’ disease course and clarify the causal relationship. Therefore, to validate the findings of this study, future multicenter prospective studies and in-depth mechanistic investigations are necessary. Additionally, it is essential to note that the subjects in this study were all NAFLD patients who underwent liver biopsy. As a result, these patients may have had more severe symptoms or complications and a longer disease duration, which could have led to an overestimation of the prevalence of NASH and fibrosis in the NAFLD population. Therefore, it is essential to carefully consider this factor when generalizing the study results to a broader population. Additionally, since all study participants were from eastern China, there may be differences in body composition among other populations. Thus, it is necessary to consider this potential limitation when generalizing this study’s results. The study’s external validity could be enhanced by broadening the sample source and including participants from diverse races and regions. Additionally, we observed wide 95% confidence intervals in the interquartile analyses, particularly between TyG-BMI and the fourth quartile of cirrhosis (5.08–1475.34). The study may have been limited by the small sample size, indicating the need for larger sample sizes in future studies to improve the accuracy of statistical inference. Additionally, it is crucial to note that discovering and validating a biomarker is lengthy and complex, requiring extensive research, clinical validation, and regulatory approval before it can be widely used in diagnostic practice. Therefore, it is unrealistic to expect to achieve these goals in a single study. However, this study provides valuable insights and establishes a foundation for developing this field.

Conclusions

This study uncovered a significant correlation between the TyG-BMI index and the histopathologic components of NAFLD, including NASH, at-risk NASH, and fibrosis stages (F2, F3, F4) through in-depth analysis. This finding provides a foundation for validating the TyG-BMI index in other study cohorts. It offers a simple, low-cost biochemical measurement tool for identifying at-risk NASH and its associated components in NAFLD patients on a large scale. The multivariate models constructed by combining TyG-BMI with transient elastography demonstrate favorable diagnostic performance and are anticipated to serve as a productive alternative to liver biopsy. Future studies should investigate the biological link between the TyG-BMI index and NAFLD to clarify its specific role and mechanism of action in disease onset and progression.
