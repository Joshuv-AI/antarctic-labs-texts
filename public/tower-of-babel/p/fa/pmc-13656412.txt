Roles and professional boundaries of ward-based registered nurses without specialist wound care credentials in orthopedic surgical wound management: a qualitative study
Authors: Jianli Lu, Huafen Wang, Kaiyan Fan, Lili Jin, Kezhen Zhou, Mengwen Wu, Ling Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing volume and complexity of orthopedic surgery have expanded the involvement of ward-based registered nurses in postoperative wound care. Although registered nurses receive foundational wound care education as part of their professional preparation, those without specialist wound care credentials may encounter challenges when managing complex orthopedic surgical wounds. Their experiences, role boundaries, and perceived support needs remain insufficiently explored. This study aimed to explore ward-based registered nurses’ perceptions of their roles and professional boundaries in orthopedic surgical wound management and to identify perceived strategies for supporting their practice.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted in an orthopedic ward of a tertiary hospital in China. Fourteen ward-based registered nurses were purposively recruited and participated in semi-structured interviews. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach.ResultsFour themes were identified: negotiating professional boundaries within clinical hierarchies, evolving role identity in orthopedic surgical wound care, developing professional capacity for safe and responsive wound management, and sustaining practice development through continuing education and organizational support. Participants described surgical wound care as an expanding but ambiguously defined area of nursing practice. Their accounts highlighted a mismatch between foundational wound care education and the demands of complex orthopedic surgical wounds, variability in physicians’ treatment preferences and local ward routines, limited decision-making authority, and inconsistent interprofessional collaboration. Participants also emphasized the importance of timely nursing involvement, clinically relevant continuing education, standardized protocols, and clearer escalation pathways for supporting safe and coordinated wound care.ConclusionsWard-based registered nurses without specialist wound care credentials make important contributions to orthopedic surgical wound management through ongoing assessment, timely dressing care, patient education, and communication with physicians and wound care specialists. However, their expanding role is shaped by unclear professional boundaries, variable local practices, limited authority in wound-related decision-making, and uneven access to continuing education. Clearer role delineation, standardized protocols, structured continuing education, and collaborative escalation pathways are needed to support safe and consistent wound care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05050-8.Keywords: Orthopedic nursing, Registered nurses, Surgical wound care, Professional boundaries, Qualitative research

Introduction

The global burden of musculoskeletal disorders has increased in recent years, contributing to rising demand for orthopedic surgery and placing additional pressure on healthcare systems [1]. Orthopedic surgical wounds require careful postoperative management because they may be complicated by implant-related risks, soft tissue trauma, and patient-related factors such as age, comorbidities, and impaired mobility [2]. Surgical site infections (SSIs), which may involve superficial incisional tissue or extend to deeper tissues and implanted materials, remain a major concern after orthopedic surgery [3]. SSIs are associated with increased healthcare costs, longer hospital stays, delayed recovery, pain, and reduced mobility [4]. Therefore, effective postoperative wound management is essential for preventing complications and supporting safe recovery after orthopedic surgery [5].

Nurses have a central role in postoperative surgical wound care, including wound observation, dressing care, patient education, early recognition of complications, and communication with surgical teams [6]. Within this area of practice, registered nurses, wound care specialist nurses, and advanced practice nurses may contribute in different ways according to their educational preparation, clinical experience, institutional policies, and scope of practice [7]. In this study, ward-based registered nurses without specialist wound care credentials refer to registered nurses working in orthopedic surgical wards who participate in postoperative wound care but do not hold formal wound care specialist certification or an advanced wound care role. This term is used to distinguish them from wound care specialist nurses or advanced practice nurses, rather than to imply a lack of professional nursing education. As surgical volumes increase and care delivery models evolve, these ward-based registered nurses are increasingly involved in surgical wound management, particularly in routine assessment, dressing care, patient education, and escalation of concerns to physicians or wound care specialists [8–10].

The scope and organization of nurses’ involvement in surgical wound care differ across healthcare systems. In Greece, nurses have been reported to contribute to surveillance of orthopedic surgical site infections, although diagnostic responsibilities and authority may remain closely linked to medical roles and local role definitions [11]. In the United Kingdom, some nurses undertake extended surgical roles, such as Surgical Care Practitioners, after completing additional education and supervised preparation, enabling them to contribute to selected aspects of perioperative and postoperative care [10]. Studies from other settings also suggest that nurses’ wound care practice is shaped by access to continuing education, availability of evidence-based guidelines, local protocols, and opportunities for interprofessional collaboration [12, 13]. For example, research from Italy reported variation in registered nurses’ exposure to wound care education and self-efficacy in advanced wound care activities [14], while an Australian chart audit highlighted variability in postsurgical wound assessment and documentation practices [15]. Collectively, these studies indicate that the issue is not a simple absence of nursing education, but rather the need to align foundational nursing preparation, continuing professional development, clinical governance, and clearly defined interprofessional responsibilities in surgical wound care.

In China, surgical wound management is commonly organized within a physician-led model, while recent nursing policy and practice initiatives have sought to expand nurses’ roles in specialized and procedure-related care [16]. In China, registered nurses enter practice through formal pre-registration nursing education pathways, which have historically included secondary, associate-degree, and baccalaureate-level programmes, and the National Nursing Licensure Examination has served as an important component of the nurse registration process since its establishment in 1995 [17, 18].

Existing Chinese studies have mainly focused on chronic wound care, nurse-led wound management models, or adherence to surgical site infection prevention guidelines, with less attention paid to how ward-based registered nurses understand their roles and professional boundaries in orthopedic postoperative wound care [19, 20]. This gap is important because orthopedic surgical wounds may require timely observation, dressing care, escalation of abnormal findings, and coordination between nurses, surgeons, and wound care specialists [2, 6]. Understanding nurses’ perceptions may help clarify how role boundaries are negotiated in daily practice and what forms of educational, procedural, and organizational support are needed. Therefore, this study aimed to explore ward-based registered nurses’ perceptions of their roles and professional boundaries in orthopedic surgical wound management and to identify perceived strategies for supporting their practice.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative design was adopted to explore ward-based registered nurses’ perceptions of their roles and professional boundaries in orthopedic surgical wound management and to identify perceived strategies for supporting their practice. Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted to generate in-depth accounts of participants’ experiences, role expectations, and support needs. Data were analysed using thematic analysis to identify patterns of meaning across participants’ accounts. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines to enhance transparency and completeness of qualitative reporting [21]. This study was conducted in accordance with the international ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2024-Research-1404).

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted in an orthopedic ward of a tertiary hospital in China. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to capture the perspectives of ward-based registered nurses involved in orthopedic postoperative wound care. Eligible participants were registered nurses who had at least three years of clinical experience in orthopedic nursing and routinely participated in postoperative wound observation, dressing care, patient education, or escalation of wound-related concerns. Nurses who held formal wound care specialist certification or were employed in a designated wound care specialist role were excluded. Attendance at general wound care continuing education or ward-based in-service education did not constitute specialist wound care credentialing and therefore was not an exclusion criterion. Fourteen participants were interviewed. Recruitment and data collection continued until inductive thematic saturation was judged to have been reached. During concurrent data collection and analysis, no substantially new codes relevant to the research aim were identified after the twelfth interview. Two additional interviews were conducted to confirm the stability of the developing themes, after which the research team agreed that further interviews were unlikely to generate new insights related to the study aim.

Interview guide

The semi-structured interview guide was developed by the research team based on a review of relevant literature and the team’s clinical and research experience in orthopedic nursing and wound care. The guide was designed to explore participants’ experiences of orthopedic surgical wound management, perceived roles and responsibilities, professional boundaries, interprofessional collaboration, and support needs. Open-ended questions were used to encourage participants to provide detailed accounts of their clinical experiences, and probing questions were used to clarify meanings and elicit examples. The interview guide was pilot tested with two registered nurses to assess clarity, relevance, and flow. These two nurses were not included in the final sample. No major changes were required after the pilot interviews. The full interview guide is provided as Additional file 1. Sociodemographic and professional data were collected, including age group, education level, professional title, years of orthopedic nursing experience, wound care continuing education or ward-based in-service education, and participation in wound management activities.

Data collection

Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted between December 2024 and February 2025. After written informed consent had been obtained, interviews were arranged at times convenient for participants and conducted in quiet, private settings. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese by trained members of the research team. Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 60 min. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded; video recording was used only when agreed by participants and was intended to support accurate transcription and contextual understanding. Field notes were written during and immediately after each interview to document contextual information, non-verbal cues, initial analytic reflections, and issues that required follow-up in subsequent interviews. These notes were used to contextualize interview data and inform team discussions during analysis, rather than as an independent data source.

Data collection and preliminary analysis were conducted concurrently. Recruitment ceased when inductive thematic saturation was judged to have been reached, that is, when later interviews generated no substantially new codes or themes relevant to the study aim. Audio-recordings were transcribed verbatim in Mandarin Chinese by a professional transcription service and checked against the recordings by members of the research team. Identifying information was removed during transcription and data preparation. Analysis was conducted using the Mandarin Chinese transcripts. Representative quotations selected for publication were translated into English by one bilingual researcher and checked by another bilingual member of the research team for semantic accuracy and consistency. Back-translation was not conducted; however, discrepancies in wording were discussed among the research team to preserve the meaning and tone of the original Mandarin accounts. Minor grammatical adjustments were made to improve readability in English while preserving participants’ intended meanings. All anonymized data were stored securely and were accessible only to the research team.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach to thematic analysis: familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [22]. Analysis was conducted primarily using the Mandarin Chinese transcripts to preserve participants’ original meanings and contextual expressions. L.J.L. and W.H.F. independently read the transcripts several times and recorded preliminary analytic notes. They then generated initial codes inductively from the data. Codes were compared and discussed to identify similarities, differences, and areas requiring refinement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with input from W.M.W. when needed.

Field notes were reviewed alongside the transcripts to support contextual interpretation of participants’ accounts, particularly when clarifying references to local routines, clinical situations, or interactions with physicians and wound care specialists. Field notes were not coded as a separate data set. F.K.Y. and J.L.L. reviewed the coding decisions to enhance analytic consistency. Z.K.Z. and Y.L. supported the organization of related codes into subthemes, which were then refined into overarching themes through team discussion. Theme names and definitions were revised iteratively to ensure that they reflected both the semantic content of participants’ accounts and the study aim concerning roles and professional boundaries. NVivo 12 software was used for data organization and management.

Reflexivity

The interviewers and analysts had nursing research and clinical backgrounds, which informed their understanding of orthopedic nursing and wound care practices. At the same time, the team recognized that these backgrounds could influence interpretation of participants’ accounts. Reflexive notes were used to document assumptions, emerging interpretations, and possible influences of professional experience. These reflections were discussed in team meetings to challenge individual assumptions and refine interpretations. None of the interviewers had direct managerial authority over the participants during data collection, and participants were encouraged to describe both supportive and problematic aspects of wound care practice.

Rigor and trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was supported through strategies addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. To enhance credibility, two researchers independently reviewed the transcripts, generated initial codes, and compared interpretations through repeated discussion. Disagreements were resolved through team discussion until consensus was reached. Representative quotations were used to illustrate the connection between participants’ accounts and the themes. To support dependability and confirmability, the research team maintained an audit trail documenting key decisions during recruitment, data collection, coding, theme development, and refinement of theme names. NVivo 12 was used to organize transcripts, codes, and analytic notes.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine before data collection began (Approval No. 2024-Research-1404; expedited review process). All participants received information about the study purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw before providing written informed consent. Identifiable information was removed from transcripts and quotations, and participants were assigned numerical identifiers to protect confidentiality.

Overview of Themes

Four overarching themes were identified from the thematic analysis: (1) negotiating professional boundaries within clinical hierarchies (2), evolving role identity in orthopedic surgical wound care (3), developing professional capacity for safe and responsive wound management, and (4) sustaining practice development through continuing education and organizational support. Together, these themes describe how ward-based registered nurses perceived their expanding involvement in orthopedic surgical wound management, the boundaries that shaped their practice, and the forms of support they considered necessary for safe and coordinated care. Thirteen subthemes were identified and are presented in the thematic map (Fig. 1).

Thematic map of ward-based registered nurses’ perceived roles and professional boundaries in orthopedic surgical wound management. In Theme 4, professional autonomy refers to role-appropriate autonomy within defined clinical boundaries, rather than unrestricted independent authority over complex wound treatment decisions

Theme 1: Negotiating professional boundaries within clinical hierarchies

This theme describes how participants understood and negotiated their responsibilities in orthopedic surgical wound management within physician-led clinical structures and ward-based routines. Participants did not portray their role as fixed or clearly delimited. Instead, their accounts suggested that nursing involvement in wound care was shaped by the complexity of the wound, physicians’ treatment preferences, local procedural expectations, and the extent of nurses’ perceived authority to act. Professional boundaries were therefore experienced as negotiated in everyday practice rather than formally defined.

Misalignment between foundational wound care education and dynamic clinical demands

Participants described a perceived misalignment between the foundational wound care knowledge acquired through nursing education and the dynamic, practice-based demands of orthopedic surgical wound management. This misalignment became particularly apparent when they were required to adapt to physicians’ individualized treatment preferences, which often varied across clinicians and introduced uncertainty into everyday wound care practice.

We need training just to keep up with each doctor’s habits—some use iodine, others use gel. (Participant 3: CN)

Participants’ accounts suggested that such variability extended beyond differences in individual preference and was experienced as a challenge to care consistency. When treatment choices differed across physicians, nurses were required not only to follow orders but also to interpret how these differences related to routine ward practice and patient recovery. For some participants, this created uncertainty about whether commonly used approaches were sufficient in specific clinical situations.

We don’t know if iodine is enough. Doctors use hydrogen peroxide, but we don’t. It makes us concerned about the patient’s recovery. (Participant 5: CN)

This uncertainty was also evident in participants’ descriptions of technical aspects of wound care that required practical judgement. One participant referred to applying elastic bandages as an example of a task that could not be reduced to routine execution alone, because inappropriate pressure might compromise circulation or wound stability. In this sense, participants’ accounts highlighted not simply a gap in knowledge, but a perceived need for more detailed procedural guidance and practice-based learning in relation to complex wound care tasks.

We haven’t been trained. If the bandage is too tight, it affects circulation; too loose, and it falls off. (Participant 5: CN)

Participants further described wound documentation as an area in which clinical expectations were not fully supported by standardized procedures. In the absence of clear institutional guidance for wound photography, some participants reported relying on personal mobile devices to document wounds. They perceived that variation in image angle, size, and clarity reduced the consistency of documentation and limited the reliability of subsequent wound assessment.

Wound photos vary in size, angle, and clarity. It’s hard to keep them consistent. (Participant 10: OSRN)

Taken together, these accounts suggest that participants experienced orthopedic surgical wound care as an area in which foundational nursing preparation did not always fully equip them for the variability, technical specificity, and interpretive demands of daily practice. Their concerns were not limited to performing wound care tasks themselves, but extended to making sense of differing clinical expectations and maintaining consistency in care processes.

Weaknesses in interprofessional collaboration

Participants consistently described unclear role expectations and inconsistent communication as major barriers to effective interprofessional collaboration in orthopedic surgical wound management. Although physicians were generally expected to assess wounds during rounds and take primary responsibility for complex wound-related decisions, participants reported that this involvement was not always reliably enacted in practice.

Ideally, doctors would check wounds during morning rounds and handle complex ones… but most don’t. (Participant 12: NE)

This perceived gap between expected and actual medical involvement left nurses uncertain about how wound-related concerns should be managed, particularly when ongoing assessment or timely review was required. Participants described situations in which nurses performed dressing care and monitored wound changes, yet medical follow-up did not always occur in a visible or timely manner.

We change the dressing, but doctors often don’t even look at it again… It reduces their attention to the wound’s condition. (Participant 1: CN)

These difficulties became more pronounced in cases involving acute deterioration or cross-disciplinary complexity, where the absence of clear accountability could delay review and treatment. In such situations, participants’ accounts suggested that interprofessional collaboration depended not only on willingness to cooperate, but also on timely access to the appropriate clinician and clearer pathways for responsibility allocation.

We had a patient with significant wound bleeding after colorectal surgery… the physician couldn’t come immediately… eventually, the head doctor assigned someone, but I was really concerned about the delay. (Participant 10: OSRN)

Participants generally regarded complex wound care as a shared clinical responsibility requiring input from both nurses and physicians. However, they also emphasized that collaboration was often constrained by physicians’ competing clinical commitments, particularly when doctors were unavailable during critical moments.

Complex wounds should be managed together by doctors and nurses. I always hope the doctor can come when we face difficult cases, but in reality, they are often in the operating room and just cannot come. (Participant 2: NE)

Taken together, these accounts suggest that interprofessional collaboration was not absent, but was experienced as variably enacted and insufficiently supported by clear role delineation, consistent communication, and dependable escalation processes. Participants’ narratives indicate that when collaboration relied heavily on individual availability rather than structured coordination, nurses were left to manage uncertainty at the point of care.

Decision-making constrained by hierarchical clinical boundaries

Participants described wound-related decision-making as constrained by hierarchical clinical boundaries, particularly when caring for complex orthopedic surgical wounds. Although nurses were closely involved in wound observation and routine dressing care, they perceived that formal authority over treatment-related decisions remained predominantly with physicians.

Doctors hold more authority than nurses. (Participant 7: CN)

This unequal distribution of authority shaped how participants responded to wound-related concerns in practice. Even when nurses identified signs suggesting that a wound required more than routine care, they often hesitated to act beyond basic dressing management because decisions about advanced products or treatment adjustments were regarded as falling outside their legitimate scope of practice.

Sometimes the wound has a lot of exudate, and the doctor might use something like a silver dressing… I don’t really have the qualification or knowledge to decide if I can use that, so I just do a simple dressing change and wait for the doctor the next day. We don’t really have decision-making power. (Participant 14: CN)

This account suggests that participants’ limited decision-making authority was not experienced simply as personal uncertainty, but as a boundary produced through the interaction of clinical hierarchy, perceived scope of practice, and differential access to specialist wound care knowledge. In this context, nurses remained responsible for ongoing surveillance and immediate basic care, yet their capacity to intervene more substantively was constrained when wound management required products, judgements, or actions understood to belong to medical or specialist authority.

Taken together, these accounts indicate that nurses’ involvement in orthopedic surgical wound care was both active and restricted: participants monitored wounds, recognized deterioration, and maintained basic care, but their authority to make treatment-related decisions did not expand in parallel with their practical responsibilities. As a result, professional boundary negotiation was experienced not only as role ambiguity, but also as a misalignment between clinical responsibility and formal decision-making authority.

Constraints in standardizing operational processes

Participants described the absence of standardized operational processes as a further constraint on their involvement in orthopedic surgical wound management. In addition to unclear decision-making boundaries and inconsistent interprofessional coordination, participants reported that key aspects of wound care practice were not always supported by unified procedural expectations. As a result, wound management was often shaped by individual clinicians’ preferences, ward-level habits, and situational judgement rather than by a consistently applied process.

We need training just to keep up with each doctor’s habits—some use iodine, others use gel. (Participant 3: CN)

This variation in treatment routines suggested that even basic aspects of dressing care were not always governed by shared operational standards. For participants, this meant that carrying out wound care required continual adjustment to physician-specific preferences, rather than reliance on a stable and transparent procedural framework. Such variability made it more difficult to achieve consistency in practice and contributed to uncertainty about what constituted appropriate care in a given situation.

Participants also identified wound documentation as an area in which standardization was lacking. In the absence of clear institutional guidance for wound photography, some participants reported using personal mobile devices to document wounds, which they perceived as resulting in inconsistency in image size, angle, and clarity.

Wound photos vary in size, angle, and clarity. It’s hard to keep them consistent. (Participant 10: OSRN)

Participants’ accounts suggest that these inconsistencies were not merely technical inconveniences, but reflected broader limitations in the procedural infrastructure supporting wound care. When documentation methods, product selection, and aspects of dressing practice were not standardized, nurses were required to rely more heavily on personal experience and informal coordination. In this context, the lack of standardized operational processes further complicated efforts to maintain continuity, consistency, and clarity in wound management.

Taken together, these accounts indicate that participants experienced orthopedic surgical wound care within a procedural environment that was only partially standardized. This lack of operational consistency intensified uncertainty in daily practice and reinforced the need for clearer protocols to support coordinated care.

Some participants linked this lack of standardization directly to the absence of formal ward-level policy for nurse-performed dressing care.

Our hospital currently has no standard policy for nurses performing wound dressings. (Participant 7: CN)

This absence of explicit policy limited nurses’ ability to judge when routine dressing care could be managed independently and when medical or specialist review should be sought.

Theme 2: Evolving role identity in orthopedic surgical wound care

This theme describes how participants understood their role in orthopedic surgical wound care as evolving beyond routine bedside tasks, while remaining only partially formalized within existing clinical structures. Participants’ accounts suggested that their role identity was shaped by their increasing involvement in wound observation, dressing care, patient education, and communication with physicians. However, this expanded involvement also generated uncertainty, as nurses were expected to assume more responsibility without always receiving corresponding authority, recognition, or procedural support. Role identity was therefore experienced as both developing and contested.

Expansion and ambiguity of the professional role

Participants described wound care as an area in which nurses’ responsibilities had expanded in practice, but where the formal boundaries of nursing work remained ambiguous. Some participants emphasized that ward-based registered nurses were not yet positioned to lead surgical wound management independently, particularly when complex wounds required specialist knowledge or medical decision-making.

At this stage, ward nurses can’t lead surgical wound management. We lack the necessary capacity. Technically, dressing changes aren’t even considered a nursing duty. Traditionally, this has been the doctor’s responsibility. (Participant 4: NE)

This account reflects the tension between historical role expectations and emerging clinical realities. Although dressing care had traditionally been associated with physicians in this setting, participants reported that nurses were increasingly expected to undertake wound-related tasks in daily practice. The expansion of nursing involvement was therefore not experienced as a clearly authorized role transition, but as a gradual redistribution of work that left nurses uncertain about the limits of their responsibility.

Participants also distinguished between routine wounds that nurses could manage and complex wounds that required specialist or medical input. However, they noted that this division of labor was difficult to maintain when wound care specialists were not readily available on the ward.

We mainly handle routine wounds. Complex ones are supposed to be treated by specialists… but there are few of them, and they’re rarely on the ward. In fact, we’re the ones who interact with patients the most. (Participant 1: CN)

This quotation highlights a central feature of participants’ evolving role identity: nurses were not necessarily positioned as formal wound care leaders, yet their continuous proximity to patients meant that they often became the most immediate and accessible providers of wound-related care. Their role was therefore shaped less by formal designation than by clinical presence, patient contact, and practical necessity.

The ambiguity of this expanded role was further evident when wound care tasks were delegated to nurses without consistent medical review. One participant explained that the original purpose of involving nurses in dressing care was to improve efficiency and allow physicians more time for patient communication. In practice, however, this arrangement sometimes resulted in reduced medical attention to wound assessment and increased workload for nurses.

Originally, nurses were to change dressings so doctors could spend more time with patients. But now doctors rarely check the wounds. They just ask us to do it. The rule that doctors must review the wound first is often ignored, especially with rushed discharges. This increases risks and defeats the purpose. Patient satisfaction may be higher, but nurses bear a heavier workload. (Participant 5: CN)

This account suggests that role expansion was experienced as double-edged. On the one hand, nurses’ involvement could improve efficiency and responsiveness. On the other hand, when delegation occurred without clear review mechanisms or shared accountability, nurses perceived that they were carrying greater responsibility without sufficient structural support. Thus, the expansion of the nursing role did not simply strengthen professional identity; it also exposed unresolved tensions around responsibility, workload, and role legitimacy.

Some participants nevertheless viewed the expansion of wound care responsibilities as a potential opportunity for professional and organizational development, particularly in newer service models such as home-based wound care.

I think it’s a positive direction. We now offer home dressing services and accept cases through online platforms. It’s also a development opportunity for our organization. (Participant 7: CN)

At the same time, participants emphasized that expanded roles required clear boundaries to prevent inappropriate delegation of complex wound care. One nurse manager stressed the importance of adhering to existing guidelines and declining tasks that exceeded the nursing scope of practice.

We have clear guidelines… complex wounds should be managed by doctors or wound care teams. But some doctors take shortcuts and shift the responsibility to us. We try to stick to the rules. If it’s beyond our scope, we won’t handle it. (Participant 9: NM)

Taken together, these accounts indicate that participants experienced role expansion as both necessary and problematic. Their wound care responsibilities were growing, but this expansion was accompanied by uncertainty regarding authority, accountability, and the distinction between appropriate nursing involvement and inappropriate task shifting.

Nurses’ integration into wound care processes

Participants described themselves as increasingly integrated into the ongoing processes of orthopedic surgical wound care. This integration was largely grounded in their continuous bedside presence, repeated contact with patients, and responsibility for monitoring changes over time. Nurses were often the first to recognize that a dressing needed to be changed or that a wound required further attention.

In the past, it was always the doctors who would open and check the wound themselves. But now, doctors sometimes don’t look at it at all. Ward nurses are the ones reminding them when the dressing needs to be changed, like after three days or when the patient is discharged. (Participant 10: OSRN)

This shift positioned nurses as active participants in maintaining the continuity of wound care. Participants were not only carrying out dressing changes, but also monitoring timing, identifying when review was needed, and prompting physicians to reassess wounds. In this sense, nursing involvement became embedded in the coordination of wound care, even when final treatment decisions remained medically led.

Participants also described how increased involvement in wound care encouraged more independent clinical reasoning. Rather than relying solely on physicians to interpret postoperative symptoms, some nurses began to consider wound-related explanations when patients developed fever or other signs of deterioration.

Now, when a patient has a fever, we consider whether it might be inflammation, normal postoperative absorption, or perhaps a wound infection. We used to rely on doctors to make that judgment, but now we begin to think independently. (Participant 14: CN)

This account suggests that nurses’ integration into wound care processes was associated with a gradual shift in professional self-understanding. Participants began to see wound assessment not merely as a delegated task, but as part of a broader process of clinical observation, interpretation, and early recognition. However, this developing judgement remained situated within a context where nurses’ formal authority to initiate treatment was limited.

Participants also emphasized that patients themselves often provided important cues that prompted nurses to assess wounds more proactively. Patient reports of pain, delayed dressing changes, or abnormal wound appearance became part of nurses’ everyday wound assessment.

Sometimes patients tell us things like ‘the dressing hasn’t been changed in days,’ or they feel pain or the wound doesn’t look right. That prompts us to take a look, especially if the doctor hasn’t yet. (Participant 14: CN)

This illustrates how nurses integrated patient-reported information into wound care processes. Their role involved not only technical wound management, but also responsiveness to patient concerns and the translation of those concerns into clinical attention. Through close patient contact, nurses became important intermediaries between patients’ experiences and the wider wound care team.

Another participant described how direct involvement in dressing care allowed nurses to see wounds themselves, identify problems earlier, and provide patient education during care.

Previously, we didn’t even see the wound because the doctor changed the dressing. Now, we handle it ourselves, which allows us to identify issues early and deliver health education to patients simultaneously. This has enhanced communication and trust. (Participant 9: NM)

This account shows that integration into wound care strengthened nurses’ relational and educational roles. By seeing the wound directly and interacting with patients during dressing care, nurses were able to build trust, deliver health education, and detect potential problems earlier. Thus, participants’ evolving role identity was closely linked to their continuous presence at the bedside and their capacity to connect wound observation, patient communication, and care coordination.

Taken together, these accounts suggest that nurses’ involvement in wound care was becoming more clinically integrated and interpretive. Their role extended beyond task execution to include surveillance, patient communication, early recognition, and coordination. However, this integration remained uneven because greater practical involvement did not always correspond to formal decision-making authority.

Emotional responses to expanded wound care responsibilities

Participants described emotional and physical strain associated with their expanding wound care responsibilities. These responses were closely related to workload intensity, time pressure, patient turnover, and the need to perform dressing care alongside existing nursing duties. One participant summarized this expansion directly:

One downside is that it’s definitely increased the nurses’ workload. (Participant 6: CN)

Another participant described how dressing care was added to already demanding morning routines, including ward rounds, handovers, transfers, intravenous therapy, discharges, and new admissions.

Each of us handles 8 to 10 patients, and mornings are already crazy with rounds, handovers, transfers, IVs, discharges, and new admissions. Now we also have to do dressing changes during this time—it’s overwhelming and makes us really anxious. (Participant 10: OSRN)

This account indicates that emotional responses were not simply individual reactions to wound care tasks, but were produced within a broader workload context. Dressing changes became stressful when they were inserted into already compressed workflows without corresponding adjustments in staffing, time allocation, or task prioritization. Participants’ anxiety therefore reflected the pressure of maintaining wound care quality under conditions of competing demands.

Emotional tension also emerged during interactions with patients’ family members, particularly when family members closely observed or questioned nurses’ wound care techniques. One participant described a situation in which a family member compared her dressing technique with the physician’s previous method.

When I was changing the dressing, the family member was very cautious and kept telling me what to do—like how to insert the gauze at a certain angle. I told her, ‘I’ve been doing this for years, I know how.’ But she said, ‘The doctor didn’t do it this way yesterday. (Participant 11: CN)

This quotation illustrates how nurses’ evolving wound care role could expose them to direct scrutiny from patients and families. Such scrutiny was not only technical but also relational and symbolic: the comparison between the nurse’s method and the doctor’s method reflected uncertainty about the legitimacy of nurses’ wound care role. For participants, this could generate frustration, defensiveness, and emotional pressure, particularly when they were expected to perform wound care but their authority or competence was questioned by others.

Taken together, these accounts show that expanded wound care responsibilities were accompanied by emotional complexity. Participants experienced pressure and anxiety because wound care added to already demanding workloads, but they also experienced tension when their professional competence was challenged during care interactions. These emotional responses reflected the broader uncertainty of an evolving role: nurses were assuming more visible responsibility for wound care while still negotiating recognition, trust, and professional legitimacy.

Theme 3: Developing professional capacity for safe and responsive wound management

This theme describes how participants understood professional capacity in orthopedic surgical wound care as developing through practical role differentiation, timely patient-centered responsiveness, and a strengthening sense of nursing contribution. Rather than presenting wound care capacity as a fixed individual attribute, participants portrayed it as something enacted within team structures, shaped by clinical exposure, and gradually strengthened through repeated involvement in wound observation, dressing care, communication, and escalation. Across interviews, participants suggested that the development of nursing capacity was closely tied to the ability to deliver safe, timely, and context-sensitive wound management within existing clinical constraints.

Optimizing team division of labor

Participants described wound care as requiring a differentiated division of labor according to wound complexity, patient risk, and staff expertise. In their accounts, nurses were generally positioned to take the lead in routine or uncomplicated wound care, whereas more complex wounds required physician involvement or specialist consultation.

For simple wound care, we usually take the lead. But for more complicated cases, we play more of an assisting role. (Participant 12: NE)

This account suggests that participants understood professional capacity not as unrestricted independence, but as the ability to work safely within a graded structure of responsibility. Nurses’ wound care capacity was therefore expressed through appropriate role positioning: taking responsibility for simple wounds, supporting physicians in complex cases, and recognizing when escalation was necessary.

Participants also described how wound care tasks were often initiated by physicians, but nurses remained responsible for ongoing observation and timely reporting of abnormal wound changes. One participant explained:

The doctors usually let us know which wounds need to be dressed, and then one of us is assigned to take care of it. If everything looks fine, I just proceed with the dressing. But if I notice signs of infection like redness, swelling, or excessive exudate, I might take a picture and send it to the doctor, or ask them to come have a look if they’re available. (Participant 13: CN)

This quotation shows that nurses’ contribution extended beyond performing dressing changes. Their role involved wound surveillance, identification of potential infection, documentation through photographs, and communication with physicians. In this sense, professional capacity included both technical performance and clinical judgement. Nurses were expected to recognize when a wound remained within the scope of routine care and when it required further medical attention.

Some participants described this division of labor as a tacit but recognizable boundary between nurses, physicians, and wound specialists. Clean surgical wounds without infection were generally considered manageable by nurses, while infected wounds, revision cases, or more complicated surgical wounds remained under physicians’ responsibility.

In general, doctors assess whether a wound can be managed by nurses. Clean surgical wounds without signs of infection are usually handled by us. But more complex wounds or infections, especially in joint revision cases, are still managed by doctors. Wound specialists mainly focus on chronic wounds like pressure injuries, while surgical wounds remain under physicians’ care. (Participant 7: CN)

This account indicates that participants did not seek to replace physicians or wound specialists. Instead, they emphasized the importance of a clear and clinically appropriate division of labor. Such a structure could help nurses develop their wound care capacity while maintaining patient safety and avoiding inappropriate task shifting.

At the same time, participants expressed openness to further role development if additional responsibilities were supported by continuing education, supervised practice, and medical guidance.

If registered nurses are allowed to perform tasks like suture or drain removal in the future, we’re definitely willing to learn. We’re eager to acquire these skills under doctors’ guidance and apply them in clinical practice. (Participant 5: CN)

Taken together, these accounts suggest that optimizing team division of labor was central to developing nursing capacity in wound care. Participants valued an arrangement in which nurses could lead routine care, assist with complex cases, escalate concerns appropriately, and gradually expand their skills under structured guidance.

Enhancing patient experience through timely nursing involvement

Participants linked nursing involvement in wound care to improved patient experience, particularly through more timely responses to wound-related needs. Because nurses were continuously present on the ward and had frequent contact with patients, they were often able to notice problems and respond faster than physicians who were occupied with surgery, rounds, or other clinical duties.

There are fewer complaints from patients now. Previously, when there was drainage or wound detachment that required dressing changes, the doctors weren’t as quick as nurses. If I noticed detachment, I would immediately address it. Similarly, if there was exudate, I would deal with it right away. (Participant 3: CN)

This account shows that timely nursing involvement was perceived as having direct value for patients. Participants associated nursing responsiveness with fewer complaints, quicker management of drainage or dressing detachment, and reduced delays in routine wound care. In this way, wound care capacity was not only about technical skill, but also about the ability to provide immediate, patient-facing support.

Another participant similarly emphasized the proactive nature of nursing involvement:

A positive aspect of wound care is that we can be more proactive. If a patient needs something, we can take care of it right away. (Participant 13: CN)

This quotation highlights an important shift in how participants understood their role. Nurses were not merely waiting for instructions; they were increasingly able to respond to visible patient needs in real time. This proactive involvement strengthened the continuity of wound care, especially when patients experienced discomfort, wound exudate, dressing loosening, or other routine problems that required prompt bedside attention.

Participants’ accounts suggest that patient experience improved not only because care was faster, but also because nurses were more accessible. Their continuous presence enabled them to identify problems early, respond to patient concerns, and reduce the gap between patient need and clinical action. Therefore, timely nursing involvement became a key expression of professional capacity in surgical wound management.

Taken together, these accounts indicate that participants viewed responsiveness as a distinctive nursing contribution. By being available, observant, and able to act promptly, nurses enhanced the patient experience and supported safer, more continuous wound care.

Strengthening nursing professional identity

Participants’ accounts suggested that increased involvement in wound care contributed to a stronger sense of nursing professional identity. As nurses took responsibility for simple wound care, participated in the observation of wound changes, and supported communication between patients and physicians, they began to view wound care as a meaningful part of nursing practice rather than merely an additional delegated task.

We typically take charge of simple wound care. However, for more complicated cases, we play a supporting role. (Participant 12: NE)

This statement reflects a professional identity based on differentiated contribution. Participants did not describe themselves as fully independent wound care decision-makers in all situations. Instead, they articulated a more balanced understanding of their role: nurses could lead routine wound care, while also recognizing the need to support or collaborate with physicians in complex cases. This clarity helped reinforce nursing identity within appropriate professional boundaries.

Participants also emphasized that nursing contribution extended beyond wound dressing itself. Their role included attending to patients’ overall condition, supporting patient education, and maintaining communication between patients and doctors.

It’s not just about checking vital signs or the patient’s condition. Nurses also look after the overall well-being of patients and make sure they’re receiving the care they need. Part of our job is teaching them how to care for themselves and helping keep communication smooth between them and the doctors. (Participant 7: CN)

This quotation broadens the meaning of wound care capacity. Nurses’ professional identity was strengthened not only through technical competence, but also through their relational, educational, and coordinating functions. Participants understood their role as connecting clinical observation with patient support, self-care education, and interdisciplinary communication.

In this sense, wound care became a site where nursing identity was made more visible. Through direct participation in wound observation and care, nurses were able to demonstrate their clinical value, contribute to patient recovery, and strengthen trust with patients. Their professional identity was therefore not only grounded in task completion, but also in the broader nursing functions of continuity, communication, education, and patient-centered care.

Taken together, these accounts suggest that the strengthening of nursing professional identity occurred alongside the development of wound care capacity. As participants became more involved in routine wound management and patient support, they gained a clearer sense of the distinctive contribution nurses could make within orthopedic surgical wound care.

Theme 4: Sustaining practice development through continuing education and organizational support

This theme describes how participants understood sustained development in orthopedic surgical wound care as dependent on the interaction between individual learning and organizational support. Participants associated continued engagement in wound care with opportunities to connect theoretical knowledge with clinical practice, develop professionally within clearly defined role boundaries, and work in an environment supported by structured educational and procedural systems. Their accounts suggest that motivation for continued involvement in wound care was shaped not only by personal willingness to learn, but also by institutional conditions that enabled safe practice, role development, and coordinated support.

Integrating theory and practice in dynamic nursing contexts

Participants consistently linked their motivation to engage in orthopedic surgical wound care with educational support that could be applied directly to clinical practice. Although theoretical knowledge was considered necessary, they emphasized that it was insufficient without repeated opportunities to apply that knowledge in diverse wound care situations. In their accounts, meaningful learning occurred when continuing education was closely connected to the clinical realities of orthopedic wards.

One training session is definitely not enough. You really need practical experience, and guidance should vary depending on the type of wound. (Participant 2: NE)

This account suggests that participants did not view knowledge development as the outcome of a single educational event. Rather, they described wound care learning as an ongoing process that required practical exposure, repeated application, and contextualized guidance. Their motivation to continue developing in wound care was therefore strengthened when education was responsive to the variability of wounds encountered in daily practice.

Another participant similarly emphasized that educational support should reflect differences in disease conditions and wound characteristics rather than rely on generalized instruction alone.

Training should not be considered sufficient after just one session. For orthopedics, I believe it’s crucial to differentiate by disease and wound type. (Participant 7: CN)

This perspective indicates that participants valued continuing education that was clinically specific and sensitive to the diversity of orthopedic wound care scenarios. In this sense, the integration of theory and practice was not described simply as a pedagogical preference, but as an important condition for sustaining confidence and engagement in wound care. Participants’ accounts suggest that when education was better aligned with clinical complexity, it was more likely to support ongoing role development and motivate nurses to remain actively involved in wound management.

Sustaining professional autonomy through continuing education and institutional support

Participants described professional autonomy in orthopedic surgical wound care as a bounded and practice-based form of professional agency rather than unrestricted independent decision-making in complex wound treatment. In their accounts, ward-based registered nurses could exercise role-appropriate judgement in routine wound care, participate in wound observation and communication, and recognize situations requiring escalation. At the same time, they emphasized that more complex orthopedic wounds continued to require the involvement of clinicians with advanced wound care expertise or designated specialist roles. In this sense, professional autonomy was understood as developing within clearly defined clinical boundaries rather than beyond them.

Registered nurses who’ve had some continuing education in post-surgical wound care can usually take care of basic wounds, but when it comes to more complex orthopedic wounds, that’s really where nurses with advanced wound care expertise are better suited. (Participant 14: CN)

This account indicates that participants did not equate autonomy with unrestricted responsibility. Rather, they associated it with the ability to manage routine wounds safely, contribute to ongoing assessment, and work with greater confidence within an appropriately delimited scope of practice. The recognition of more advanced expertise for complex wounds did not diminish the role of ward-based registered nurses; instead, it reinforced the importance of a clear division of responsibility supported by appropriate escalation.

Participants also emphasized that professional development should not be limited to formal specialist credentialing. They suggested that ward-based registered nurses could strengthen their contribution to wound care through continuing education, even when they were not preparing for specialist roles. In this regard, professional autonomy was closely connected to opportunities for ongoing learning and skill development within everyday practice.

While specialization is important, I believe continuous learning should be encouraged. Each unit should provide opportunities for professional growth, even if nurses are not formally pursuing specialist certification. (Participant 10: OSRN)

This perspective suggests that participants viewed continuing education as an important condition for maintaining professional confidence and strengthening nursing contribution within defined role boundaries. Their accounts did not frame role development as a shift toward independent management of all wound types, but rather as a process of improving competence in routine wound care, communication, and appropriate escalation.

A further aspect of sustaining professional autonomy concerned the role of institutional support in connecting theoretical knowledge with practical application. Participants noted that wound management knowledge alone was insufficient if nurses lacked structured opportunities to apply that knowledge in clinical settings. They therefore emphasized the need for institutional mechanisms that could support hands-on learning and progressive skill development.

Registered nurses may acquire theoretical knowledge in wound management, but hands-on practice still requires institutional support. A workshop could help nurses from each ward specialize in wound care, supported by advanced education to enhance their skills. (Participant 4: NE)

This account highlights that participants understood professional autonomy as dependent not only on individual learning, but also on organizational conditions that enabled nurses to translate knowledge into safe practice. Institutional support was therefore seen as essential for sustaining nurses’ role-appropriate judgement, strengthening their participation in wound care, and supporting professional development across wards.

Taken together, these accounts suggest that sustaining professional autonomy depended on continuing education, practical learning opportunities, and institutional support. However, autonomy was not presented as independent authority over complex wound treatment decisions. Rather, it was understood as a bounded and supported form of professional agency through which ward-based registered nurses could practise more confidently, contribute more effectively to routine wound care, and escalate concerns appropriately within clearly defined clinical and interprofessional structures.

Establishing structured systems for individual and collective practice development

Participants further described sustained practice development as dependent on structured organizational systems rather than on informal learning alone. Although individual motivation and continuing education were considered important, participants emphasized that ongoing improvement in wound care required coordinated institutional support. In their accounts, a more standardized framework could help reduce inconsistency, improve communication, and support both individual and collective development in wound care practice.

To ensure effective wound care throughout the hospital, a unified and standardized management model is necessary. Appointing a dedicated coordinator could help address issues and promote communication across departments, thereby supporting consistent implementation. (Participant 5: CN)

This account suggests that participants regarded organizational coordination as an important condition for sustaining practice development. A dedicated coordinator or comparable mechanism was valued not only for oversight, but also for facilitating communication across departments, supporting problem-solving, and promoting greater consistency in wound care processes.

Another participant similarly emphasized the need for a formalized management structure to support standardization across different units.

A standardized approach is essential, possibly through a quality control team dedicated to wound management. This would ensure consistency across different units. (Participant 6: CN)

These accounts indicate that participants did not view practice development as something that could be maintained through individual effort alone. Rather, they associated long-term improvement in wound care with systems that linked education, coordination, and quality oversight. Such structures were perceived as important for supporting nurses in everyday practice, reducing procedural variability, and creating a more consistent environment for wound care delivery.

Taken together, these accounts suggest that sustaining practice development depended on the interaction between individual learning and organizational infrastructure. Participants were more likely to remain engaged in wound care when educational opportunities, communication pathways, and standardized support systems were aligned to facilitate both personal development and collective consistency in practice.

Discussion

This study explored the experiences and perceived role boundaries of ward-based registered nurses without specialist wound care credentials in orthopedic surgical wound management. The findings show that these nurses were actively involved in routine wound observation, dressing-related care, patient communication, and early identification of wound-related concerns. However, their role was not experienced as fixed or clearly bounded. Instead, it was shaped by wound complexity, perceived clinical risk, professional hierarchy, physician decision-making, access to specialist support, and organizational routines. This suggests that the contribution of ward-based registered nurses to orthopedic surgical wound care should be understood not simply as a matter of individual competence, but as a practice situated within broader educational, clinical governance, and interprofessional systems.

The findings indicate that ward-based registered nurses occupy an important frontline position in orthopedic surgical wound management. Although they did not hold specialist wound care credentials, participants described regular involvement in monitoring wound appearance, recognizing abnormal changes, implementing routine wound-related care, and communicating concerns to physicians or more experienced colleagues. This frontline role is clinically important because postoperative wound care often depends on timely observation, continuity of bedside assessment, and early escalation when signs of delayed healing, infection, drainage, or dehiscence are suspected [6]. Therefore, the role of ward-based nurses should not be interpreted as peripheral. Rather, they function as an essential point of continuity between patients, physicians, and specialist wound care resources [20, 23, 24]. In the physician-led model described by participants, written medical orders and locally agreed wound care protocols may function as important mechanisms for clarifying when nurses should provide routine dressing care, when specific products such as iodine, hydrogen peroxide, gels, or silver dressings should be used, and when medical or specialist review is required.

A key finding of this study is that professional boundaries in orthopedic surgical wound management were experienced as dynamic rather than fixed [23, 25]. Participants did not describe their role as simply divided between “basic” and “specialist” tasks. Instead, they described a more fluid practice environment in which routine wound care could be performed independently, whereas complex wounds, uncertain healing trajectories, or situations requiring treatment decisions often prompted escalation to physicians, senior nurses, or wound care specialists. This reflects a form of role negotiation in which nurses’ practical responsibilities may extend beyond what is formally recognized, while decision-making authority remains shaped by institutional hierarchy and professional accountability [16, 24, 26, 27]. Such findings suggest that uncertainty in wound care practice may arise not only from limited individual knowledge, but also from unclear procedural boundaries and uneven access to specialist support.

The findings also highlight the need to distinguish between general nursing education and continuing education relevant to orthopedic postoperative wound care. Participants’ accounts did not suggest an absence of nursing knowledge, but rather a gap between general preparation and the specific clinical judgements required in complex postoperative wound situations [14]. Confidence appeared to be strengthened when nurses had repeated exposure to wound cases, opportunities to discuss uncertain situations, and access to senior or specialist guidance. This suggests that educational support should move beyond isolated theoretical sessions and instead include case-based learning [28], supervised bedside practice [16], and continuing professional development for ward-based registered nurses in orthopedic wards, particularly in relation to postoperative wound observation, routine wound-related care, and early recognition of abnormal wound changes. Although not wound-specific, evidence linking nurses’ eHealth literacy with professional knowledge, attitudes, and practice further suggests that digital literacy may serve as a supplementary resource within broader competency development [29].

Beyond education, the findings point to the importance of clinical governance in supporting safe and consistent wound management. Participants’ descriptions of uncertainty around when to manage wounds independently, when to seek medical review, and when to involve wound care specialists suggest that clearer procedural support may be needed. In this context, information literacy may also support nurses in navigating clinical uncertainty by enabling more effective use of available guidance, documentation systems, and specialist resources [19]. Standardized wound assessment tools, documentation requirements, escalation criteria, and referral pathways could help reduce variability in practice and provide ward-based nurses with more reliable guidance in situations of clinical uncertainty [15, 19, 30, 31]. Importantly, such governance mechanisms should not be understood as restricting nurses’ practice, but as providing a safer structure within which role-appropriate judgement can be exercised.

Interprofessional collaboration emerged as another important condition shaping nurses’ wound care practice. Participants’ accounts suggest that ward-based nurses often acted as the first point of observation, while treatment decisions for complex or deteriorating wounds were commonly dependent on physicians or wound care specialists. In this context, unclear communication channels or inconsistent access to specialist advice may increase uncertainty and delay timely decision-making. More structured communication between physicians, ward-based nurses, and wound care specialists may therefore help clarify responsibilities, support timely escalation, and improve continuity of postoperative wound care [32, 33].

Taken together, these findings have practical implications for nursing education, clinical governance, and interprofessional collaboration. First, continuing education for ward-based registered nurses should be clinically specific, longitudinal, and closely connected to supervised practice in orthopedic wound care [34, 35]. Second, hospitals may need to strengthen clinical governance through clearer wound care protocols, standardized documentation, explicit escalation criteria, and more accessible specialist consultation [36]. Third, interprofessional arrangements should clarify how responsibilities are shared between ward-based nurses, physicians, senior nurses, and wound care specialists, particularly when wounds become complex, delayed, or clinically uncertain [16]. These measures may help align nurses’ everyday wound care responsibilities with appropriate authority, support, and accountability.

Overall, this study shows that the professional boundaries of ward-based registered nurses in orthopedic surgical wound management are negotiated within everyday clinical practice rather than determined solely by formal credentials. Their contribution is shaped by wound complexity, experiential knowledge, hierarchical decision-making, access to specialist support, and organizational routines. Strengthening their role therefore requires not only individual competence development, but also clearer procedural systems and more reliable interprofessional support. This interpretation may help inform future efforts to improve orthopedic surgical wound care by aligning nurses’ frontline responsibilities with education, governance, and collaborative decision-making structures.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its in-depth qualitative exploration of how ward-based registered nurses without specialist wound care credentials perceive their roles and professional boundaries in orthopedic surgical wound management.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-site study conducted in a tertiary hospital in China, and the findings may reflect the characteristics of a specific organizational setting and physician-led model of care. Second, only ward-based registered nurses were included; therefore, the findings capture nurses’ perspectives but do not incorporate the views of other relevant stakeholders, such as physicians, wound care specialist nurses, patients, or family members. Third, the study relied on self-reported interview data and did not include direct observation of wound care practices or analysis of relevant clinical documents. Finally, although the interview quotations were translated into English and checked by bilingual members of the research team, some nuances of meaning may have been attenuated in the process of translation.

Future research could usefully include multiple clinical sites, a broader range of stakeholders, and observational or documentary methods to further examine how professional boundaries and organizational support shape orthopedic surgical wound management in practice.

Conclusions

Ward-based registered nurses without specialist wound care credentials play an important but only partially formalized role in orthopedic surgical wound management. Their contribution includes wound observation, timely dressing care, patient education, and communication with physicians and wound care specialists, but remains shaped by dynamically negotiated professional boundaries, limited decision-making authority, and uneven organizational support. Clearer role delineation, role-appropriate autonomy, standardized protocols, consistent interprofessional communication, and clinically relevant continuing education may better support safe and coordinated wound care in orthopedic wards.
