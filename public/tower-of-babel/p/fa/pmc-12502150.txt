Non-linear effect of physical activity intervention on blood pressure in children and adolescents: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis
Authors: Hengtai Yuan, Wenying Shao, Yanan Ren, Minmin Jiang, Jingxia Kong, Fangyuan Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe noticeable increase in the prevalence of elevated blood pressure (BP) among children and adolescents has been a public health concern. Although physical activity (PA) intervention has been widely applied in children BP controlling programs, the effectiveness of PA intervention in adolescents over 12, the dose-response relationship between PA intervention and BP and the optimal dose of exercise required for BP lowering are all remained unclear. Current systemic review and meta-analysis aimed to offer a comprehensive evaluation of the evidence linking PA intervention to BP reduction in children and adolescents.MethodsAccording to PRISMA and MOOSE guidelines, we searched PubMed, Embase, MEDLINE Complete, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library up to Jul 22, 2025, for randomized controlled trails examining the association between physical activity intervention and blood pressure in children and adolescents aged 6–24 years old. Pooled weighted mean differences (WMD) were calculated using a random-effects model. Restricted cubic splines method was used to assess the dose-response association between PA intervention dose and blood pressure change.ResultsA total of 22 RCT studies involving 3456 subjects were included in this meta-analysis. Pooled results revealed that PA intervention was significantly associated with mean reduction in systolic blood pressure (SBP) (MD=-4.05, 95% Confidence Interval (95%CI), -6.05 to -2.06) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (MD=-1.95, 95% CI, -3.12 to -0.78). Both SBP and DBP showed a non-linear relationship with PA intervention dose with a max decrease at 700 MET-min/week. While the exercise intervention dose is within 0-700 MET-min/week, both DBP (β=-0.0054, p = 0.015) and SBP (β=-0.0111, p = 0.003) decrease as the exercise dose increases. However, when the exercise dose exceeds 700 MET-min/week, diastolic (β = 0.0059, p = 0.049) and systolic blood pressure (β = 0.0113, p = 0.029) rebound upward with further increases in exercise volume.ConclusionPhysical activity intervention could reduce blood pressure in children and adolescents. There is a V-shape dose-response relationship between physical activity intervention dose and changes in blood pressure among adolescents, with an optimized dose of 700 MET-minutes per week.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24640-w.Keywords: Physical activity, Children and adolescent, Blood pressure, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Over the past few decades, there has been a noticeable increase in the prevalence of elevated blood pressure (BP) among children and adolescents [1, 2]. This trend has raised significant public health concerns, as elevated BP in early life is increasingly recognized as a precursor to adult hypertension and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [3]. These concerns underscored the urgent need for effective preventive and therapeutic interventions [4]. Among various lifestyle modifications, physical activity (PA) has been identified as a crucial factor in reducing BP [5, 6].

Physical activity (PA) has been described as a “polypill” due to its preventive and therapeutic effects in decelerating cardiovascular aging, enhancing physiological functioning, and reducing the global health burden of cardiovascular disease [7, 8]. Based on evidence from adult studies, experts recommend that children and adolescents mitigate their cardiovascular risk by minimizing sedentary behaviors and engaging in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) [9]. The association between PA and BP in adolescents is of particular interest due to its potential long-term health implications [10, 11].

Recently, several longitudinal studies in youth have investigated the relationship between PA and BP with mixed findings [12, 13]. For instance, Wellmen et al. [14] found that engaging in moderate to vigorous PA was associated with a lower likelihood of having BP in the hypertensive range, suggesting that regular PA may help prevent high BP in adolescents. Another study demonstrated that PA measured by accelerometer was inversely associated with diastolic BP, whereas self-reported PA did not show a significant association [15]. A recent systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) evaluated the effectiveness of various PA interventions for reducing BP and the results indicate that interventions which integrate physical activity, nutrition, and behavior modification are more effective at lowering blood pressure compared to other physical activity interventions [16]. This meta-analysis provided a systematic conclusion to the effect of PA on blood pressure control in children. However, this study only investigated the effects of physical activity on lowering blood pressure in children aged 6 to 12, while the impact of PA in adolescents over 12, and the dose-response relationship between PA and blood pressure as well as the optimal amount of exercise required are all remained unclear. Although previous researchers have focused on the association between PA intensity and risk of hypertension and derived different conclusions [17–19], these results from observational studies were limited because of confounders, and may not appropriate for guiding PA intervention for blood pressure control in children and adolescents. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are widely regarded as the gold standard for evaluating the effectiveness of interventions due to their methodological rigor and ability to minimize bias [20, 21].

Here, we conducted a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis on randomized control trials of PA intervention, aimed to offer a comprehensive evaluation of the evidence linking PA interventions to BP reduction in children and adolescents. By identifying the most effective PA intervention dose, this meta-analysis could contribute to the development of strategies that can help prevent and manage hypertension in children and adolescents, ultimately reducing their risk of cardiovascular diseases in adulthood.

Method

Literature search strategy

According to PRISMA and MOOSE guidelines, we conducted a systematic review. We searched PubMed, Embase, MEDLINE Complete, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library up to Jul 22, 2025, for randomized controlled trails examining the association between physical activity intervention and blood pressure in children and adolescents. Detailed search terms are provided in Table S1. Our search was limited to English-language publications and human studies. Our study was registered under the international prospective register of systematic reviews (INPLASY (https://inplasy.com/), register number: INPLASY202570089, supplementary material File Registry_details.PDF).

Studies were included if they met all of the following inclusion criteria: 1) Patients had not received any medication or other intervention measures prior to the study; 2) Participants were aged between 6 and 24 years; 3) The intervention period lasted for at least one month; 4) The study design was a randomized controlled trial (RCT). Studies were excluded if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: 1) Participants’ ages fell outside the specified range; 2) Patients had secondary hypertension or comorbid cardiovascular diseases; 3) Patients suffered from severe organic diseases or psychiatric disorders; 4) Outcome variables were unreported or involved duplicate reporting. We included studies with control conditions such as no intervention, educational control, nutritional therapy, or other. Only studies with interventions lasting at least 4 weeks were included. To ensure the representativeness of the results, we selected the general population and excluded patients with organic diseases and psychiatric disorders. Two authors independently reviewed each study using pre-established criteria, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Data extraction

For selected studies, relevant data was independently extracted by two investigators using a priori designed tables. The following information was included: participant and trial characteristics to determine their association with physical activity interventions and blood pressure. Data extracted included age, BMI, and gender at baseline; blood pressure values for the intervention and control groups before and after the study; intervention duration; and the number of participants at the study’s end. Blood pressure values were extracted as means and standard errors (or standard deviations). For ages provided as ranges, we averaged them to represent the study population’s age. Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers, with questions resolved through consultation with another author.

Physical activity intervention dose extraction

In order to evaluate the dose-response relationship between PA intervention and blood pressure change, we introduced metabolic equivalent (MET) to unify measurements across different studies. PA intervention levels were transformed into MET-min per week, using exercise evaluation criteria for children [22] and adolescents aged 18 and above [23]. A single MET-min equates to the energy expended while sitting at rest for one minute. For instance, a person walking 3 h per week at an intensity of 3 METs expends 540 MET-minutes weekly, calculated as 3 METs * 60 min * 3 h. For each study, the median or mean level of PA intervention was assigned to the corresponding mean BP differences. When these values were not available, the midpoint between the upper and lower boundaries of each category was used. For the open-ended exposure category, the PA level was defined by multiplying the reported upper boundary by 1.25 [24].

Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool [25] for randomized trials. Studies with low risk of bias in key domains—random sequence generation, allocation concealment, and missing participant outcome data—were classified as low overall risk of bias, while others were considered high risk. Two investigators (Yuan HT and Shao WY) independently reviewed each study. Discrepancies in evaluation were resolved by a third reviewer (Ren YN) through consensus. The results of the risk-of-bias assessment for each study are available in Figure S1.

Statistical analyses

Pooled weighted mean differences (WMD) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the main outcomes of included studies were calculated using a random-effects model. Standard errors were calculated from sample sizes when not provided in the original articles. Restricted cubic splines method was used to assess the dose-response association between PA intervention dose and blood pressure change. Funnel plots along with Egger’s test were applied to assess the impact of potential publication bias on the overall effect size. Heterogeneity tests were performed to assess variability among studies, and Kendall’s tau test was used to assess inter-study agreement. The Leave-One-Out method was used to assess the stability of results by excluding one article at a time.

All analyses were performed using R 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) unless otherwise specified. The WMD analysis was performed using the “metafor” package, dose-response meta-analyses were conducted with “dosresmeta” and “rms” packages. The “forestplot” and “ggplot2” package was used for visualization of the results.

Results

A comprehensive literature search was conducted, resulting in the identification of 10,643 references, of which 10,526 articles were excluded based on their abstracts. We then selected 97 articles after removal of 20 duplicates. Subsequent evaluation led to the full-text assessment of 97 articles for eligibility, of which only 22 studies [26–47] satisfied the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the meta-analysis. The literature search and selection process is depicted in a flow diagram presented in Fig. 1.

Systematic review of the literature examining the blood pressure lowering effect of physical activity intervention in children and adolescents

The analysis included a total of 3456 participant, with 1757 subjects in the control group, and 1699 subjects assigned to the PA intervention group, Most of participants had elevated blood pressure or were diagnosed with hypertension. As detailed in Tables 1 and 19 of total 22 studies included both male and female participants, 5 studies included only female participants, and 4 studies included only male participants. Among these studies, 16 focused on individuals with overweight or obesity, while 12 targeted individuals from the general population. The mean duration of the prescribed physical activity programs was 21.57 weeks, ranging from 4 to 76 weeks. The frequency of physical activity varied from one to five times per week, with the maximum frequency being five sessions per week. The average duration of each physical activity session was 54.75 min, with most sessions lasting less than 60 min. All included interventions were supervised, with some being single-mode exercise interventions and others being multi-exercise comprehensive interventions.

Mean effect of PA intervention on blood pressure

The mean effect of the PA intervention on systolic blood pressure was − 4.05 (95% CI, −6.05 to −2.06; P < 0.01). Among the 22 studies on diastolic blood pressure, 15 studies (83.3%) had WMDs less than 0, suggesting that exercise also reduced diastolic blood pressure, albeit to a lesser extent than systolic blood pressure. The mean effect of exercise on diastolic blood pressure was − 1.95 (95% CI, −3.12 to −0.78). Collectively, PA intervention demonstrated a hypotensive effect on both systolic and diastolic blood pressure (Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis of the weighted mean difference (WMD) of systolic and diastolic blood pressure between physical activity intervention group and control group

Subgroup analyses

Results of subgroup analyses are presented in Fig. 3. For systolic blood pressure, we found that the subjects aged 19–24 (WMD =−6.65, 95%CI: −10.51 to −2.80) and 13–18 years (WMD =−7.10, 95%CI: −11.31 to −2.89) in intervention group showed a significant decrease in systolic blood pressure compared to the control group, while subjects aged 6–12 years(WMD =−2.12,95%CI: −4.34 to 0.11) exhibited no significant difference. Significant decreases of systolic blood pressure were also observed in subjects with obesity (WMD =−7.38, 95%CI: −10.61 to −4.15) or overweight (WMD =−6.14, 95%CI: −10.37 to −1.92), while no statistical significance was observed in normal group.

Subgroup analysis for blood pressure lowering effect of physical activity intervention in different age groups, sex, weight status and intervention durations

For diastolic blood pressure, adolescents aged 19–24 years (WMD =−5.89, 95%CI: −9.11 to −2.67) and subjects with obesity (WMD =−3.53, 95%CI: −5.55 to −1.50) still showed a significant decrease in diastolic blood pressure compared to the control group.

Besides, PA intervention lasted shorter than 12 weeks turned out the most efficient for both SBP (WMD =−7.48, 95%CI: −10.18 to −4.77) and DBP (WMD =−3.54, 95%CI: −4.99 to −2.10) lowering according to the results of subgroup analyses.

Dose-response relationship between PA intervention and blood pressure

Figure 4 illustrates the dose-response relationship between PA intervention and blood pressure and indicated the optimal dose for blood pressure control in children and adolescents. Both SBP (WMD=−2.12, 95%CI:−3.52 to −0.72) and DBP (WMD=−4.56, 95%CI:−7.00 to −2.11) showed a non-linear relationship with PA intervention dose with a max decrease at 700 MET-minutes per week. While the exercise intervention dose is within 0-700 MET-minutes per week, both DBP (β=−0.0054, p = 0.015) and SBP (β=−0.0111, p = 0.003) decrease as the exercise dose increases. However, when the exercise dose exceeds 700 MET- minutes per week, diastolic (β = 0.0059, p = 0.049) and systolic blood pressure (β = 0.0113, p = 0.029) rebound upward with further increases in exercise volume.

Dose-response relationships between physical activity intervention and systolic and diastolic blood pressure in children and adolescents

Heterogeneity and bias

Egger’s test and Funnel plots (Figure S2/S3) illustrated no significant publication bias for either systolic blood pressure (Egger’s t=−0.9945, p = 0.34) or diastolic blood pressure (Egger’s t=−1.2968, p = 0.22). This indicates that the methodological rigor of our meta-analysis yields reliable conclusions.

To evaluate the robustness of the association between physical activity and blood pressures in children and adolescents, a sensitivity analysis was performed by iteratively removing individual studies from the meta-analysis and recalculating the pooled standardized mean difference (SMD). The results demonstrated that the overall SMD estimates remained consistent across all iterations, with no significant variation observed upon exclusion of any single study (Figure S4). These findings collectively confirm the methodological rigor and generalizability of the conclusions drawn from the pooled data.

Discussion

This meta-analysis demonstrated significant blood pressure-lowering effect of physical activity interventions in children and adolescents. Pooled results from 22 RCTs revealed a significant mean reduction in systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure, with greater blood pressure reductions in older age groups, individuals with obesity, and short-term interventions. We further observed a V-shape dose-response relationship between physical activity intervention and changes in blood pressure among adolescents, with an optimized dose (700 MET-min/weeks) yielding maximal benefits for blood pressure –lowering. These findings pointed out the overall effect of PA on blood pressure lowering in children and adolescents, and figured out an optimized dose of PA intervention for clinical use of blood pressure lowering.

The effect of PA on reducing blood pressure in children and adolescents has been a subject of extensive research, with varying results across different studies [48, 49]. A longitudinal study focusing on primary school children found that higher levels of physical activity were inversely associated with diastolic blood pressure, highlighting the evidence of a causal relationship between increased physical activity and lower blood pressure in children as young as five years old [50]. Aguilar et al. [51] conducted randomized clinical trial in Granada, Spain and demonstrated the positive effects of physical activity on blood pressure in children who are overweight. Weston et al. [52] examed the acute effects of interrupting prolonged sitting with intermittent PA in preadolescent children, and found no significant differences in systolic blood pressure across different PA intensities. However, it noted a general decrease in systolic blood pressure throughout the day, suggesting that even low-intensity PA might have beneficial effects over time. Previous network meta-analysis also showed that interventions combining physical activity with nutrition and behavior change were the most effective in reducing both systolic and diastolic blood pressure in children and adolescents [16, 53]. These studies collectively illustrate that while PA can be an effective means of reducing blood pressure in children and adolescents, the outcomes can vary depending on the type, intensity, and combination of interventions used. Likewise, our meta-analysis revealed that PA intervention in children and adolescents could result in a mean reduction of −4.05 mmHg for systolic blood pressure and 1.95 mmHg for diastolic blood pressure. This finding is partly in line with prior studies, which indicated that PA-only interventions may provide only slight effect on reducing BP in children aged 3–12 years.

Compared to children under 12 years old, the blood pressure reducing effect of PA intervention in adolescents aged over 12 years has gotten less attention. Previous studies reported diverse results, and the conclusion was not stable. Some of these studies found that physical activities could significantly reduce the systolic blood pressure but not the diastolic blood pressure [26, 43], some revealed PA intervention was effective for both systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure reducing [40], while the meta-analysis by Guillem et al. [54] assessing the effects of resistance training on blood pressure in preadolescents and adolescents showed there was no significant decrease between training and control groups. Our meta-analysis demonstrated the blood pressure reducing effect of PA was greater in adolescents aged over 12 especially for systolic blood pressure, suggesting age specific effect of PA on blood pressure which may relating to the onset of puberty [55, 56].

The dose-response relationship between physical activity and blood pressure in youth has been evaluated previously in different single observational studies. A study focusing on the dose-response relationship between physical activity and cardio-metabolic health in adolescents found significant nonlinear associations, indicating that even moderate increases in physical activity can lead to substantial health benefits, including improved blood pressure profiles [57]. Guan et al.’s [19] found in Chinese adults that increased levels of physical activity were inversely related to blood pressure levels, with the most significant benefits observed at moderate levels of activity. Optimal durations of physical activity were recommended at 120–420 min/week across different studies [58–61]. A meta-analysis investigating the relationship between physical activity and hypertension risk in longitude observational studies concluded that regular physical activity significantly reduces the risk of developing hypertension, emphasizing the importance of maintaining an active lifestyle from a young age [62]. Our study demonstrated a V-shape dose-response relationship between physical activity intervention and changes in blood pressure among adolescents, with an optimized dose (700 MET-min/weeks) yielding maximal benefits for blood pressure lowering, which is slightly different from the suggestion of Physical Activity Guideline for American Children and Adolescents [58]. The purpose of this study is to determine the most efficient physical activity intervention dose for controlling blood pressure, whereas the guidelines target the overall health status of adolescents (particularly musculoskeletal health). Thus, the differences between our findings and the guideline-recommended quantities are justified. Notably, the V-shape dose-response curve does not necessarily mean that physical activity levels above the inflection point will negatively impact adolescent blood pressure, although it might imply a reduced blood pressure-lowering effect.

Few studies has conducted to assess the blood pressure-lowering effect of PA in children and adolescents aged over 12, and the dose-response relationship between PA and blood pressure as well as the optimal amount of exercise required are all remained unclear. Our study provides reliable and high-quality evidence that PA intervention could significantly reduce the blood pressure with the inclusion of studies from various countries, different age, and PA intensities into our meta-analysis. Furthermore, our study figured out an optimal PA intervention volume which can be subsequently applied for blood pressure control in children and adolescents.

Nonetheless, this study does have some limitations. Firstly, our study did not impose specific restrictions on blood pressure measurement methods and criteria. While the methods used before and after the experiment remained consistent within the same study, they might still affect the study’s results. Secondly, the intensity of regular physical activity for the subjects was not measured in most articles included in our study, so that the conclusion could be affected. Thirdly, although the funnel plots did not show significant publication bias, the possibility of bias cannot be completely dismissed.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that physical activity intervention could efficiently reduce blood pressure in children and adolescents. There is a V-shape dose-response relationship between physical activity intervention dose and changes in blood pressure among adolescents within the 330–2340 MET-min/week range, with an optimized dose of 700 MET-minutes per week. Both diastolic and systolic blood pressure decrease with the PA dose increases within 0-700 MET-minutes per week, while they will increase with the PA dose when the dose is over 700 MET-minutes per week.

With the increasing incidence of elevated blood pressure and hypertension in young individuals, there is a growing need for interventions promoting physical activity in children and adolescents. Our study provided a most effective PA intervention dose for blood pressure control in children and adolescents, and contributed to prevent and manage hypertension in children and adolescents, ultimately reducing their risk of cardiovascular diseases in adulthood. Moreover, future studies are warranted to investigate the impact of physical activity on diastolic and systolic blood pressure across a broader range of metabolic equivalents.
