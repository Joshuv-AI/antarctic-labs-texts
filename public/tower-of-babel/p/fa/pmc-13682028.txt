Sharing general practice data: a qualitative study with Australian general practitioners
Authors: Heidi Green, Justin Beilby, Carolyn Adams, Alberto Nettel-Aguirre, Felicity Flack, Anthony Brown, Allison Clarke, Christian Jung, Belinda Fabrianesi, Lucy Carolan, Annette Braunack-Mayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing demand for access to general practice data for research and policy development presents ethical challenges for Australian general practitioners (GPs), particularly around patient consent, privacy, and trust. As frontline custodians of patient information, GPs must navigate the tension between supporting public benefit and upholding their professional obligations to confidentiality and autonomy.MethodsThis qualitative study involved interviews with 19 Australian GPs who reported limited experience with secondary use of general practice data. Participants were recruited via professional networks and interviewed using open-ended questions informed by prior community jury recommendations. Thematic analysis was conducted using NVivo 12, with an ethical lens applied to identify key themes related to trust, consent, and data governance.ResultsGPs expressed broad support for using general practice data in research but emphasised the centrality of trust in the doctor–patient relationship. Most participants preferred an opt-in consent model, citing transparency and respect for patient autonomy. Concerns were raised about privacy breaches, re-identification risks, and the ethical implications of remuneration. Organisational constraints, such as time pressures and limited decision-making authority within practices, were identified as barriers to implementing ethical data-sharing practices.ConclusionAustralian GPs are pivotal in shaping ethical and effective models for data sharing in general practice. However, substantial policy, educational, and ethical work is needed to address their concerns. Building trust, ensuring transparency, and respecting patient autonomy are essential to securing GP support for routine data collection. A collaborative approach involving government, community, and general practice stakeholders is necessary to develop acceptable and sustainable data-sharing frameworks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03490-4.

Introduction

Data sharing in general practice is at an ethical and practical crossroads. Long-standing foundational principles that support trust within the doctor-patient relationship [1] are being challenged by the increased demand for access to patient data for research, policy design, evaluation and quality assurance [2, 3]. On the one hand, general practitioners (GPs) must meet obligations of confidentiality – the professional and legal obligation to protect patient information from unauthorised disclosure - and respect for patient autonomy [4]. On the other, the comprehensive nature of general practice care makes these data especially useful, and there is considerable potential for general practice data to improve the quality of care and benefit individual health service users [5, 6].

General practice data in Australia are already routinely shared for purposes beyond direct patient care. For example, data from patient records are shared with Primary Health Networks (PHNs) for service planning, to the Australian Immunisation Register for vaccination tracking, and other government bodies for public health monitoring [7]. These existing practices illustrate how data sharing is already embedded in general practice workflows, often without explicit patient consent. However, when general practice data are shared for purposes that seem unrelated to clinical care, such as for research, and particularly without patients’ knowledge or agreement, it may be perceived as a breach of trust, raising ethical concerns about transparency, autonomy [7, 8], and the integrity of the doctor–patient relationship.

Australia’s health data sharing landscape is rapidly evolving, with national initiatives such as the secondary use framework for My Health Record and emerging data integration infrastructures seeking to enable the use of routinely collected health data for research and policy, although incorporation of general practice data remains uneven and under development [2, 5]. These developments have also been accompanied by public debate and policy backlash, particularly in relation to My Health Record, highlighting ongoing tensions around consent, trust, and governance that underscore the importance of understanding community preferences for data use [2, 5].

Contemporary codes of medical ethics, including codes specifically related to general practice [9–11] attempt to balance the potential benefits of data sharing with the protection of patient privacy – the patient’s right to control how their health information is collected, used, stored and shared [4]. These codes acknowledge that sharing patient information beyond the immediate doctor-patient relationship is a reality, while also aiming to maintain a robust, trust-based relationship between GP and patient [12–14]. Both elements are critical to the quality of patient care and the credibility of general practice.

In Australia, GPs are legally obligated to comply with both the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and the professional duty of confidentiality [15, 16]. The duty of confidentiality allows patient information to only be shared in very narrow circumstances, for example, with explicit patient consent or where required by law, whereas the Privacy Act allows for certain legally defined conditions under which patient information may be disclosed more broadly without such consent [15, 17]. An opt‑in consent approach requires individuals to take an explicit action to agree to participation or data use, whereas an opt‑out approach to consent includes individuals by default unless they actively decline. These regulatory obligations, along with the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) Code of Conduct [10], underscore the importance of respecting patient autonomy through safeguarding health information, while also highlighting the tension between respect for autonomy and public benefit [15–17]. This study explores these issues by investigating the benefits and challenges that increased data usage poses for the ethical responsibilities of Australian GPs.

This study offers a novel contribution by focusing on GPs with limited experience in data sharing or research, an underrepresented group in existing literature. Previous Australian studies [4–6] exploring GP perspectives on the secondary use of general practice data have not specified whether participants had prior research involvement, leaving a gap in understanding the views of those outside established research networks. However, what these studies did find was that agreement to share general practice data was conditional, emphasising the need for strong privacy protections, trustworthy data custodians, transparent governance, acceptable consent arrangements, and safeguards against commercial misuse [4–6].

By deliberately engaging GPs who are less familiar with data-intensive research, this study captures perspectives that are critical for designing ethically robust and practically feasible data-sharing frameworks. These insights provide more inclusive evidence base for policy development, ensuring that strategies reflect the realities of everyday general practice rather than the views of research-engaged clinicians alone. We define data-intensive research as research using large‑scale, complex, diverse or high‑frequency data such as administrative records, surveys, digital traces, or linked datasets to investigate social processes and outcomes.

Methods

Study design, recruitment, and participants

This study forms part of a larger, multiphase, multi-institutional, and multi-year project aimed at building confidence in the use of general practice data for research. The project comprised several phases, of mixed methods research including using deliberative, qualitative and quantitative methods, these were conducted through a national community survey; community focus groups; a desktop legal and policy analysis; two community juries exploring public perspectives on the secondary use of general practice data; a Delphi study with GPs who had self-reported experience in data sharing and research; interviews with policymakers and regulators to identify governance priorities; interviews with practice managers and owners to capture operational considerations; and interviews with Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) chairs to examine ethical oversight and consent frameworks. The final phase is a stakeholder roundtable conference designed to translate research findings into policy and practice.

We recruited GPs who self-reported limited experience and/or interest in data sharing for the secondary use of general practice data for research. We were focusing on this group because it is an important component of the general practice workforce in Australia. These GPs may not be actively involved in research networks or academic collaborations. Perspectives of GPs with limited experience in sharing general practice data for research are underrepresented in existing studies, as researchers often recruit participants through established data-sharing networks or collaborations, limiting the inclusion of those less engaged and their potentially differing concerns or perspectives. Therefore, the objective of this study was to explore the perspectives of GPs with self-reported limited experience in sharing general practice data for research, specifically the ethical and legal tensions between respect for autonomy and public benefit.

We used the active membership lists of the RACGP and PHNs to recruit GP participants through their email channels as we believed this would reach a wide network of GPs. The RACGP sets and maintains education, training, clinical practice quality and research standards for general practice in Australia [18]. Australia’s 31 PHNs are independent organisations funded by the Commonwealth Government to support the delivery of tailored health services to the community and streamline health care across Australia [19].

GPs who expressed interest in the study were sent a participant information sheet and asked to complete a screening tool on their experience in sharing general practice data. We used purposive sampling to select GPs into the study to ensure diversity of participants such as age, geographical location, and years of experience as a GP. GPs with little or no experience in data sharing were offered an interview and verbal consent (audio-recorded) was obtained before the interview. All GPs were reminded that participation in the study was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. Remuneration, through a $250 AUD visa e-gift card, was provided to GPs who participated.

The interviews used a case study and open-ended questions (see supplementary material) based on the recommendations developed by two community juries completed in previous research exploring public views on the secondary use of general practice data for research [20]. The case study described a university-led health study in partnership with a not-for-profit diabetes organisation to analyse linked, de-identified general practice records from 600,000 patients with type 2 diabetes to determine stroke risk. We asked GPs to consider consent options (opt-in, opt-out approach and waiver of consent), governance models, implications of data sharing for patient relationships, remuneration options and legislative requirements. All interviews were conducted by HG via Zoom and lasted between 45 and 60 min. Interviews were audio recorded and de-identified before verbatim transcription by a professional transcription service. This study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines.

Data analysis

We analysed the interview data applying an inductive thematic analysis approach [21], with the use of NVivo 12. The initial step involved thorough immersion in the data, which included repeated readings of the transcripts and attentive listening to the audio recordings. Subsequently, initial codes, meanings, and patterns were systematically generated. As the analysis advanced, these initial codes were organised into potential themes, with pertinent coded extracts collated. To ensure that the emerging themes were firmly anchored in the data, the coding framework underwent a review and was cross-checked against the transcripts. The first author (HG) conducted the initial coding in consultation with the last author (ABM), and then with the broader research team, who met to review coding strategies, compare interpretations, and recode random samples to establish concordance. HG is a qualitative researcher with a background in public health. ABM is an expert in empirical ethics, contributing a deep understanding of ethical frameworks and considerations relevant to the study. Their collaboration enabled a balanced interpretation of the data, with regular discussions to reflect on assumptions and ensure ethical sensitivity. The broader research team included members with diverse disciplinary backgrounds, including a privacy lawyer with extensive experience in qualitative research, a general practitioner with extensive academic and clinical experience, a health data leader with experience in the intersection of health research, data governance, public policy, and ethics, and a health consumer leader with experience of consumer engagement and health systems research, which enriched the thematic analysis and helped mitigate individual biases.

During analysis, we noted that the GPs we interviewed in this study drew on their understanding of their ethical obligations when discussing their perceptions on sharing general practice data. We used these understandings to identify themes and sub-themes to describe and summarise the data through an ethical lens.

The quotations used in this paper were chosen as illustrative examples of the main themes. All quotations are anonymised; however, used key demographic characteristics at the end of the quote to represent gender, age range, remoteness index [22] and state or territory of Australia.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the University of Wollongong Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number 2023/348). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines from the ethical committee whose principles are in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

We interviewed 19 GPs, with most GPs identifying as female (n = 15) and in a younger age range 25–39 years. Most of the participants (n = 13) worked in non-corporate general practices. A non-corporate practice is independently owned by a GP or small partnerships and is run like a private business, whereas a corporate practice is owned and managed by large companies or corporate groups, often with centralised management structures. The GPs were a diverse group, ranging in levels of experience as a GP from 0 to 10 years (n = 9), 11–20 years (n = 4), to more than 20 years (n = 6). This diversity also extended to geographical locations with GPs based in all states of Australia (NSW, VIC, SA, QLD, WA, TAS) and working in major cities, inner and outer regional, and remote areas [22]. Most received their medical qualifications in Australia (n = 11), with two obtaining their qualifications in the UK, and one each in India, South Africa, Iraq, Hong Kong, China and Pakistan.

The GPs supported using general practice patient data for research, but they highlighted issues that need to be addressed. The central and recurring theme that seemed to shape their views was the importance of the trust relationship between doctor and patient. Our analysis focuses on this relationship and its connection to consent and privacy and confidentiality. We also examine two organisational factors – time constraints and practice structure - which the GPs acknowledged as limitations on their ability to meet their ethical ideals. Upon reviewing the data analysis, we could not find evidence that gender was important to GPs’ views.

Risks to the doctor-patient relationship

Almost all GPs in this study were concerned that sharing patient data without consent would undermine the doctor–patient relationship because it conflicted with their understanding of their role as trusted custodians of sensitive personal information. They explained that patients rely on GPs not only for clinical expertise but also to exercise judgement on their behalf, particularly in matters relating to privacy; acting without patients’ knowledge or agreement was a violation of this stewardship role and a likely trigger for loss of trust that would be difficult to restore.

“That’s why they say doctor-patient relationship. There is that so-called power imbalance because there is that whole total trust in you. We recognise that that is very, very significant in the relationship. That’s why we tend to be like super careful in real life, about what we are actually introducing to the patient” GP 14 (F, 40–54, major city, WA).

GPs believed that a breakdown in the doctor-patient relationship could have direct implications for patient care because trust underpins open and effective communication in clinical encounters. They explained that a breach of trust could lead to patients being less forthcoming or deliberately withhold sensitive information from the GP that could be vital to their ongoing medical management. As GP18 explains below, the simplest way to be transparent was to ask patients for their consent. “… I think it would affect the relationship if I was to go behind their backs, say, and just release the information. Otherwise, you will fracture that relationship between the doctor and the GP [patient]. That person may only have the GP to talk to, so you don’t want to do that in general practice at all. … you do need to be transparent with your patient. You do need to get their permission. Otherwise, it will have repercussions afterwards and it won’t be desirable. That patient may never see a doctor again and then they end up having a misdiagnosed cancer and dying. Worst case scenario but you never know, it could just lead onto a domino effect. I do think transparency and being open with your patients is vital for sure.” GP 18 (F, 25–39, major city, QLD).

GPs emphasised that transparency and clear open communication were crucial to minimising the risk of undermining trust in the doctor-patient relationship. They explained that providing explicit assurances about de-identification helped reassure themselves and the patients that personal information would be protected, thereby aligning data sharing with their professional responsibility to safeguard confidentiality. The inclusion of de‑identified data use in the case study discussed during interviews reinforced this expectation and was seen as an important signal that appropriate privacy safeguards were in place. “Probably five per cent of my patients aren’t part of My Health Record because they don’t believe that their health data should be shared with anybody, even for the purpose of their own health. That’s got nothing to do with research. So, I think those sorts of people, it could impact the trust that they have with the GP and I think there you just have to be very, very clear about the de-identification of any identifying elements of their health. So, I think that that’s a risk.” GP 16 (F, 25–39, major city, NSW).

GPs emphasised that being honest and open with their patients about the use of health information was fundamental to sustaining trust, as they believed trust was strengthened when patients were able to make informed decisions rather than discovering data use retrospectively. They reasoned that informing patients about research use in advance, before their consultation, would give patients the opportunity to raise any concerns and ask questions. This approach was seen as a way to alleviate the GPs concerns, they had about patients not understanding how their health information could be used, reducing the risk of perceived deception or inadvertent breaches of trust. “So, we have really low health literacy, actually, just literacy in general in Tasmania….I think that’s where it needs to be a conversation, not just a piece of paper or not just a letter. Because I actually don’t think a lot of my patients would understand. They would need me to talk them through it.” GP 6 (F, 25–39, inner regional, TAS).

Preference for opt-in consent

Most GPs viewed an opt-in approach to consent to secondary use of patient data is the most appropriate because it requires explicit patient agreement and aligns with their commitment to transparency and respect for patient autonomy. They believed that actively involving patients in the decision-making assists in safeguarding the integrity of the doctor-patient relationship. “If I went for a preference, I would prefer opt in because then everyone is excluded until they make the decision that they are happy for the information to be included… So, I just think the opt in approach is the safest way because then it needs to be mentioned. There’s definite guarantee that they’re getting proper informed consent around that and then there is documentation saying, well, they have chosen to have their data included in the study.” GP 7 (F, 25–39, major city, VIC).

Some GPs argued that alternatives to an opt-in approach, such as an opt-out approach, risked undermining trust, because they were perceived as containing an element of coercion. This concern was heightened for patients whom GPs believed already distrusted health systems and government. “I’m actually a little bit worried about an opt out model and I’d prefer very much an opt in. My practice in particular sees a lot of very vulnerable people, who are financially in difficult situations, domestic violence for example, who need urgent, timely care…. I’d be quite concerned about some element of coercion, in that if it was an opt out model they’d feel like they have no option but to sign in, just to continue receiving their usual care.” GP 13 (F, 25–39, major city, NSW).

The GPs were also concerned that some patients would forget to opt out or not completely understand how to opt out. They recognised that patients lead busy lives and that they may be distracted and never opt out even if they intended to do so. This could also lead to distrust by patients, especially if patients did not understand how their data were being collected and used.

“Just the nature of life is that when you have a choice, it means that I made the choice as opposed to I forgot to take myself out. Invariably, people will not take themselves out, and they will assume that they haven’t given consent. Which is I think what’s happened before in the past already.” GP 14 (F, 40–54, major city, WA).

A minority of GPs emphasised that an opt-out approach could be feasible within general practice, but they acknowledged that it would come with challenges and require an extensive communication campaign. Two GPs in this study indicated that if the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) approved a waiver of consent, they would accept that decision and follow the approved process, noting their limited understanding of consent requirements for research.

“I assume the ethics have already gone through and part of the ethics would include what the data handling, how they de-identify and connect it back, and all that would be information that I’d want. But I would also assume that if it’s come down to me, that’s already done.” GP 15 (F, 25–39, major city, VIC).

Confidentiality and privacy as a vehicle for trust

Ensuring patient privacy and confidentiality was a primary concern to the GPs because they understood trust in the doctor-patient relationship to depend on their ability to protect sensitive information. Although the GPs viewed de-identification of data as a necessary safeguard, they were also aware that re‑identification remained possible, particularly through data linkage, which heightened their sense of risk. These concerns were heightened if ‘sensitive data’, as determined by the participants, such as mental health or domestic violence information, were involved, as such data were seen as more personal and more likely to be identifiable, especially when drawn from detailed clinical notes. “I mean, that would be a breach of confidentiality between the practice and the patients. The doctor and patient relationship is essential. When we breach that confidentiality, the trust is gone.” GP 9 (F, 65+, remote, NSW).

GPs were concerned about the potential for malicious data breaches, in light of recent data breaches, such as those involving health insurance companies in Australia [23]. They reasoned that once a breach occurs, the damage is irreversible, cannot be undone and would ultimately affect trust.

“…once data is shared, data’s shared. It’s out there, it’s gone forever. No matter what you do, you can’t fix it anymore. It’s out there.” GP 14 (F, 40–54, major city, WA).

Some GPs explained that they would be guided by their patients’ preferences regarding which data could be extracted, as this exemplified the concept of trust, with the patient deciding how their privacy should be managed. They emphasised that, while in principle the GPs themselves might agree to sharing data, they would defer to the patient’s decision, as this would ensure patient autonomy was respected.

“I think the main concern would be mainly the data required from each individual patient. What kind of data you try to collect, extract. That’s the most important thing from my personal point of view. I support this research, but on the other hand, I also need - I’m the patient’s advocate. I have to be guided by the patient’s privacy. That’s also very important”. GP 9 (F, 65+, remote, NSW).

The realities of general practice

While trust between doctor and patient was central, the GPs highlighted that there were factors that might make it difficult for them to fulfil their goals of respecting patient autonomy and seeking fully informed consent. First, some GPs acknowledged that they did not make the decisions about data sharing. In some practices, consensus among GPs determined data sharing but individual GP opinions carried little weight if the majority agreed. In others, practice managers and owners decided, consulting GPs afterward. Some GPs felt they were unable to make personal decisions as responsibility rested with practice leaders.

“Usually what happens is the directors will have a meeting about it first, and then if they come to a consensus that it’s something worth doing, they will present it to the rest of the GPs in the clinic as well.” GP 11 (F, 40–54, outer regional, SA).

“We have six owners of the practice and they along with the practice manager form an executive team, and that’s the team that would make a decision about research in our practice.” GP 2 (M, 65+, major city, SA).

Some participants considered that these practice-level decision making arrangements were appropriate and provided consistent governance across the practice, while others perceived them as potentially constraining individual clinical autonomy.

Second, GPs recognised that seeking opt-in consent and providing clear and transparent information created extra workload for already time poor practitioners. They emphasised that it was already difficult to balance patient care with non-related patient tasks such as administrative tasks and adding a consent process would further increase their workload with extra measures needed to ensure their patients understood data sharing.

“I think because most of the GPs are quite time poor, it really depends on what sort of abilities or resources that the organisation has to assist us with taking part in research like this as well. Currently, I spend an hour and a half each day completing paperwork after consulting - seeing patients face to face.” GP 10 (F, 25–39, major city, VIC).

The GPs offered solutions to time barriers which were: remuneration and practice or financial incentives. Some GPs suggested that Continuous Professional Development (CPD) points would increase their willingness to participate in sharing general practice data.

“One of the things that GPs love is being able to find a way of getting their CPD up to date, and this would be another way of doing it, is to collaborate with the College of GPs or whichever other college they might be involved with to say if you’re involved in research, well, this is one of the ways that you can get your points.” GP 2 (M, 65+, major city, SA).

“I think remuneration would help engage the general practitioner. I think with - if it takes time or it takes time out of that consult or whatever, I think it would be a motivator.” GP 6 (F, 25–39, inner regional, TAS).

But, additional remuneration carried with it further ethical obligations to be transparent with patients:

“I think if a general practice is getting paid to extract their data, there is a high level of need to let the patient know that, and to be just very opaque, very transparent about that process, of the amount of money that changes hands, who benefits.” GP 12 (F, 65+, inner regional, NSW).

Discussion

GPs are key stakeholders in providing access to general practice data for research. As demand for access continues to grow, the cooperation of GPs who have limited experience of data-intensive research will become increasingly important. The GPs interviewed in our study were in this category and, in principle, they broadly agreed to share general practice data for research. Most participants favoured an opt-in consent model with full disclosure of data sharing, viewing themselves as trusted advocates for their patients, safeguarding patients’ rights to understand and consent to how their data is used.

The finding that trust is central to GPs’ views about sharing patient data is not unique to this study. Earlier data-sharing initiatives, in the UK with care.data [24] and in Australia with ‘My Health Record’ [25] which is an opt-out approach, aimed to improve healthcare delivery and research through enhanced data access and integration. However, the benefits of these programs were not necessarily clear to patients. The lesson from these initiatives is that clear communication, robust governance, and respect for patient autonomy to build and sustain trust are essential for data-sharing efforts [26]. The key point here is clear: trust is a foundational determinant of GPs’ support for data-sharing initiatives, with ethical acceptability contingent not solely on technical safeguards such as de-identification, but on transparent processes that preserve the integrity of the doctor–patient relationship.

Our study also illustrates that trust is not the only thing that matters. The participants acknowledged that they were not always well-positioned to contribute to decisions about sharing patient data. Fully informed, opt-in consent was an ideal that was challenging in busy practices and many GPs recognised they had little control over data-sharing decisions. Further exploration of the ways in which practice organisational structures may support or constrain GPs’ involvement in communicating with patients will provide additional context for decision-making in this area.

The participants’ dual focus on their ethical obligations and on the business of general practice highlight the challenges of contemporary general practice and reflect the differing views on its role. The ethical professional concept of general practice is reflected in codes of conduct which present a model of the GP as the provider of continuous, comprehensive and holistic care to individual patients. This model can be at odds with how general practice work is structured and financed in Australia. Time-based payments for consultation, and operational models which place key decision-making in the hands of practice managers and principals are more consistent with a conceptualisation of general practice as principally a business unit.

Calls for increased access to general practice data are challenging because they bring these two models into tension with each other. Fully informed opt-in consent models may enhance patient trust, but implementation will require significant effort and finances. Asking each patient for consent, particularly if that consent is time-limited and specific, will be extremely burdensome for GPs and other practice staff. Developing a standardised consent process for an entire practice will be difficult if individual GPs and their patients are permitted to make independent decisions regarding data sharing.

Recent reforms in general practice in Australia have, at least, the potential to decrease the dissonance between the professional and business understandings of general practice. The recently launched MyMedicare reforms encourage voluntary patient registration with a preferred general practice, which may foster stronger patient-doctor relationships and promote continuity of care and personalised service delivery [27]. This funding also includes supporting improved data management and administrative processes, ensuring that practices can implement systems that prioritise patient consent and privacy. However, the reforms do not explicitly address the fact that there is a tension between efficient processes for the collection of general practice data and the ethical responsibilities of individual GPs to respect privacy and maintain confidentiality.

In general, these GPs’ views were very similar to those of patients reported in many studies, including our own [28–30]. Members of the public share concerns about transparency, consent, and trust - emphasising the need for clear governance and individual control over how personal health information is accessed and used [7, 8, 31]. Despite advances in digital health, these long-standing concerns are not going away; instead, as O’Neill has shown, increased data sharing appears to heighten, rather than diminish, concerns about privacy and trust [32]. As GPs and patients increase participation in data-sharing environments their awareness of vulnerability and misuse also will increase, intensifying concern about privacy protections even as disclosure continues.

Implications and next steps

The findings of this study will inform ongoing engagement with key stakeholders, including general practice bodies, PHNs, policymakers, and ethics committees, to ensure that proposed data-sharing frameworks align with clinical realities and ethical expectations. As part of this process, a Stakeholder Roundtable Conference will be convened to translate these findings into actionable policy and practice recommendations. This collaborative approach aims to bridge the gap between research insights and implementation, fostering trust and sustainability in the secondary use of general practice data. These findings have broader relevance beyond Australia, suggesting that as health systems internationally expand the secondary use of clinical data, aligning governance frameworks with practitioner expectations around transparency, autonomy, and trust will be critical to maintaining professional support and social licence.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it provides rich, ethically grounded insights into the perspectives of GPs who have limited experience with data-intensive research - an underrepresented yet critical group in discussions about secondary data use.

Second, the diversity of participants enhances the credibility and relevance of the findings. The sample included GPs from all Australian states and territories, working across a range of practice types, sizes, and geographical locations, including remote and Aboriginal Medical Services. This diversity allowed for a nuanced understanding of how differing GPs perspectives can influence ethical concerns and practical barriers to data sharing.

The use of case-based interviews informed by prior community jury recommendations provided a structured yet flexible framework for exploring complex ethical issues. This design facilitated meaningful engagement with GPs and allowed for the emergence of themes that are directly relevant to policy development and implementation.

We must also acknowledge that this study had its limitations. This qualitative study was designed to provide insights and understanding of specific issues within the chosen sample of GPs. Consequently, the findings are not generalisable to all GPs, and the applicability of the results to other general practice settings remains undetermined. Additionally, while our analysis did not identify gender as a significant influence on GPs’ views, this finding should be interpreted with caution given the low proportion of male participants in the sample, which may limit the ability to detect gender-related differences.

Conclusion

General practitioners are crucial stakeholders in building successful models to collect and use general practice data. This study provides new insights into how general practitioners with limited research experience perceive the ethical and practical dimensions of data sharing in general practice. Our findings show that trust between doctor and patient is essential, with GPs expressing strong concerns about maintaining transparency, protecting patient autonomy, and ensuring confidentiality. While supportive of research in principle, GPs emphasised that data-sharing models must not compromise the integrity of the doctor–patient relationship. Australia has reached a pivotal point in its debate about the secondary use of general practice data and future initiatives should prioritise clear communication with both GPs and the public. By engaging GPs as active partners, data-sharing efforts can better align with clinical realities and ethical expectations, fostering greater trust and legitimacy.
