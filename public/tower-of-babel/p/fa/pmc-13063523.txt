Contexts and concepts influencing general practitioners’ and patients’ decision-making on antibiotic prescriptions for upper respiratory tract infections in the Northern Dutch-German cross-border region – a qualitative study
Authors: Vaitiare Mulderij-Jansen, Aida Bedri, Till Herdeg, Mieke Andre, Luuk Holterman, Marit Boer, Michael H. Freitag, Adriana Tami, Nienke Beerlage-de Jong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRedundant antibiotic prescribing in primary care is a key driver of antimicrobial resistance. In the northern Dutch-German border region (Ems-Dollart region), antibiotic prescription rates increase as one approaches the border, and the reasons for this trend still need to be fully understood. The first step to address inappropriate antibiotic prescribing is to understand the aspects influencing the decision-making of those involved, especially for common conditions like upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) that are often associated with redundant antibiotic use. Therefore, we examined and compared the concepts and contexts influencing the decision-making of Dutch and German general practitioners (GPs) and patients in the Ems-Dollart region concerning antibiotic prescription for URTIs.MethodsFrom December 2023 to mid-April 2024, GPs (n = 20) and patients (n = 21) were interviewed. The interviews were recorded, transcribed, translated into English, and coded using MAXQDA 24.2.0 software. Two deductive and inductive coding cycles using primary- and sub-codes were applied. The coded data was analyzed using thematic discourse analysis methodology.ResultsWhile there were reported differences in guideline adherence, this study found that German and Dutch GPs employ quite similar approaches when diagnosing and treating URTIs. Antibiotic prescription was based on consistent aspects, including diagnostic results (such as elevated C-reactive protein levels), the severity of symptoms (including fever and prolonged duration), time constraints, and perceived patient pressure. Additionally, patient age (young children and older adults) and the presence of comorbidities were key considerations in antibiotic prescription for URTIs. Moreover, there are prevalent misconceptions among patients regarding the etiology of URTIs and the appropriate use of antibiotics. Some patients believe that bacteria primarily cause the common flu or think antibiotics are necessary to treat viral infections.ConclusionsThis study explores GPs’ and patients’ perspectives on URTI treatment, including their management preferences and the utilization of home remedies and over-the-counter medications. In addition, it highlights the wide-ranging non-medical aspects influencing GPs’ decisions, underscoring their critical role in antimicrobial resistance stewardship initiatives. Furthermore, the study identifies patient misconceptions regarding URTIs, which may lead to inappropriate antibiotic requests. Addressing these aspects is essential for optimizing URTI management and promoting prudent antibiotic prescribing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03239-z.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance is among the top ten global public health threats [1]. It occurs when microbes change over time and no longer respond to drugs, making infections more complicated to treat and increasing the risk of disease spread, severe illness and death [1]. Resistance to antimicrobial medications causes an estimated 700.000 deaths globally annually. Without effective action, it might generate 10 million deaths annually globally by 2050 [2]. Therefore, the World Health Organization (WHO) urges all countries to reduce this public health threat to protect humanity and secure a sustainable future for all [1]. One of the most important measures to be taken is to reduce the inappropriate use of antibiotics, as this amplifies antimicrobial resistance [3–4].

Since 2011, European citizens have been able to seek healthcare in other European countries, resulting in increased cross-border mobility of healthcare professionals and patients [5–7]. This dynamic complicates the fight against antimicrobial resistance, facilitating the spread of drug-resistant microorganisms across national borders. This situation is exemplified by differing antimicrobial resistance rates between neighboring countries such as Germany and the Netherlands, given that antimicrobial resistance rates are higher in Germany [5]. Additionally, Germany exhibits a higher antibiotic prescription rate [8]. Within the northern Dutch-German border region (Ems-Dollart region), antibiotic prescription rates become progressively greater as one approaches the border, and the reasons for this are not yet fully understood [9–10].

Most antibiotics are prescribed in primary care [11], and upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) is a common condition for which antibiotics are often prescribed [12–13]. This tendency is apparent even in low-prescribing countries like the Netherlands [13–14]. URTIs are commonly caused by viral infections, which are not directly treated with antibiotics, while only 15% of URTIs are caused by bacteria. Yet, antibiotics are still overprescribed for URTI, or “the common cold”, promoting antimicrobial resistance [13]. Despite strict antibiotic policies, inappropriate prescribing remains substantial. For example, 46% of antibiotics prescribed for respiratory tract infections in the Netherlands deviate from Dutch guidelines [15]. To facilitate targeted interventions, understanding the reasons behind prescribing practices is crucial. Prioritizing URTIs - highly prevalent in primary care settings - in studying these reasons can greatly impact efforts to mitigate antimicrobial resistance.

Antibiotic usage is influenced by various determinants, including patients’ age, demographic and socio-economic characteristics, interactions with healthcare professionals, treatment access, disease characteristics/severity, and knowledge [16]. Antibiotic therapy decisions are also associated with healthcare providers’ clinical experience and risk attitudes [17]. Furthermore, general practitioners (GPs) are more likely to prescribe antibiotics for lower respiratory tract illnesses when under perceived patient pressure [18]. GPs also tend to prescribe antibiotics when they believe their patients expect them to do so, assuming they will be more satisfied [15, 19]. However, it is essential to note that patients’ expectations do not always align with GPs’ assumptions about patients’ expectations, underscoring the potential for miscommunication between GPs and patients [20]. Yet, many studies on antibiotic prescription decision-making for URTIs have been quantitative and have not offered a nuanced understanding of the participants’ decision-making process. Moreover, limited studies have assessed and compared the perspectives of both patients and GPs, with even fewer focusing on cross-border regions [21–22].

This study addresses these gaps by offering detailed insights into the concepts and contexts influencing antibiotic prescription decision-making for URTIs. By collecting data from the northern Dutch-German Ems-Dollart cross-border region, we will explore the perspectives of both GPs and patients, enabling a comparative analysis to reveal cultural similarities and differences in diagnosing and treatment practices. The regions mentioned above have much to gain from each other, as they can share valuable insights to strengthen their antimicrobial resistance management strategies.

Methods

Study design

From December 2023 to mid-April 2024, a qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with GPs (n = 20) and patients (n = 21) practicing or residing in the Ems-Dollart region. The interviews focused on collecting data related to the experiences and contexts in which potential antibiotic prescription occurs. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ethical committees of the Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg (2023 − 170) and University Medical Center Groningen (METc 2023/459). The research team comprised experts from various disciplines, including psychology, medical microbiology, epidemiology, and infectious disease prevention and control. The team leveraged its diverse expertise to collaboratively develop topic guides, ensuring that comprehensive data on decision-making processes were gathered. Their expertise was used to interpret the data through insights from behavioral science, microbiology, and epidemiology. Finally, insights from medical doctors were used to reflect on and compare our data with common antibiotic prescribing practices in primary care.

Setting and study population

The Ems-Dollart region includes the Dutch northern provinces of Friesland, Groningen, Overijssel, and Drenthe and the German districts of Emden, Aurich, Wittmund, Wilhelmshaven, Leer, Friesland, Wesermarsch, Ammerland, Delmenhorst, Cloppenburg, Vechta, Emsland, Grafschaft Bentheim, and Oldenburg. GPs from specified Dutch provinces and German districts were recruited through formal and informal professional networks (e.g., at conferences and via research partners). Patients residing in these areas were recruited via social networks, posters, and online advertisements. All participants were invited by phone, email, or in person and provided written informed consent before joining the study.

Patient’s inclusion criteria were that they had been diagnosed with or had experienced symptoms associated with URTIs up to three months before the data collection date; had to be 18 years or older; and had to reside in the Ems-Dollart region at the time of the study and sign a written informed consent. URTI cases were identified based on participants’ self-reports to maintain an agile study design and minimize unnecessary data-access risks. As URTIs are common and carry little stigma, the potential for self-report bias was considered minimal. Access to medical records was therefore not included in the study design. Patients were not eligible for study enrolment if they could not understand, read, and write German or Dutch (depending on the country), suffered from psychological or psychiatric illness, were hospitalized, and/or were otherwise incapable of signing the informed consent form. All the recruited GPs and patients received 50 euros as an incentive after participation.

Data collection

Data was collected through in-depth interviews (IDIs). IDI is a one-to-one data collection method involving an interviewer and interviewee discussing specific themes in depth. The following themes were discussed in the IDI: (1) diagnostics of URTIs, (2) treatment options, (3) aspects that influence the discretion to request (patients) or prescribe (GPs) antibiotics, and (4) recommendations to reduce inappropriate antibiotic usage. This data collection method allows the study of individuals’ beliefs, perceptions, and meanings attached to experiences, motivations, and decision-making processes [23]. The interviewer used a semi-structured interview guide to gain insight into specific themes (See Additional file 1). The interview guides contain open-ended main and sub-questions, providing a loose structure that ensures all necessary topics are covered and that the interview data are comparable between participants. This structure also allows for flexibility in adapting the interview if needed. Prior to the commencement of the study, the interview guides underwent a pilot phase involving three patients and two GPs, to ensure the clarity of the questions and the inclusion of all relevant themes. One team member piloted the interview guide, and the study results were subsequently discussed with the team. Following these discussions, minor wording adjustments were made to the interview guide. Interviews were audio recorded using Microsoft Teams (version 2023.43.01.22) in the Netherlands and BigBlueButton (version 2.4) in Germany. Depending on the interviewee’s preference, the IDIs were held in person, via telephone or video conference. In cases where in-person interviews were not possible, video calls were proposed to allow for the continued observation of body language and facial expressions. During these video interviews, deliberate efforts were made to probe in depth, establish rapport, and obtain comprehensive insights from participants. Interviews were planned to last a maximum of 60 min. The number of interviews was determined based on the point at which data saturation was reached, meaning that additional interviews no longer generated new themes or insights [24]. Furthermore, each study participant’s identity was anonymized and assigned a unique code based on the order in which their interview was conducted. For example, the first ten participants, all GPs, received codes numbered 1 through 10.

Data analysis

The recorded audio data was transcribed, and the interviews were translated from Dutch or German into English. Three team members familiarized themselves with the data by reading the transcripts. They independently coded the data simultaneously using the qualitative data management software MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2022 (version 24.2.0). The codes were documented in a codebook (See Additional file 2). Two deductive and inductive coding cycles using primary- and sub-codes were applied. Deductive coding was based on concepts related to aspects of antibiotic prescriptions discussed in scientific literature. Inductive coding was applied to uncover insights that had not been anticipated at the outset of the study, allowing for the emergence of new patterns and themes directly from the data [25]. After each coding phase, the data was cross-checked. In case of doubts, the team members discussed their disagreements until consensus was reached or another team member was consulted to resolve the disputes. Cohen’s Kappa statistical test was used to demonstrate the level of agreement (inter-rater reliability). The obtained kappa value of 0.62 indicates good inter-rater agreement between the independent coders. The information pertaining to each code was analyzed using thematic discourse analysis methodology [26–27]. Combining thematic and discourse analysis offers a comprehensive approach, where thematic analysis identifies patterns and themes in the data, while discourse analysis explores how language shapes social realities and power dynamics. Numerical data was described using percentages, means or medians when appropriate.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The age distribution, gender ratio, GP visit rates, GP working experience, and geographical distribution of the Dutch and German study participants are outlined in Tables 1 and 2. A total of 20 GPs were interviewed, with an equal split between the German and Dutch sides. The median age was 50.5 years for German GPs and 38 years for Dutch GPs, while 60% of Dutch GPs and 20% of German GPs were female. Regarding practice locations, 70% of the Dutch GPs were based in the province of Groningen. On the German side, the participating GPs were spread across various districts, with 20% practicing in Ammerland and another 20% in Oldenburg.

A total of 21 patients were interviewed, ten on the German side and eleven on the Dutch side. The Dutch patients’ median age was 34.0, while the German patients’ median age was 27.5 years. In this study, 50% of the German patients visited their GPs for a URTI, compared to 36.4% of the Dutch patients. Of the total of Dutch patients, 63.6% resided in Groningen, and 70.0% of the German patients resided in Oldenburg.

The results are organized according to the thematic order outlined in the interview guides. Initially, the findings related to GPs diagnostic procedures are presented, followed by their treatment options and the decision-making processes for antibiotic prescriptions. Presenting the themes in this sequence provides a foundational context that aids in understanding the aspects influencing the decision-making of study participants. Subsequently, the patients’ perspectives on the same themes are presented, allowing for a comparison between GPs’ and patients’ perspectives and experiences.

General practitioners’ perspectives

Diagnostic procedures for URTI

According to the interviewed Dutch GPs, the diagnostic process for URTIs begins with a preliminary anamnesis conducted by their medical assistant, primarily via phone consultation. This preliminary anamnesis aims to identify alarm symptoms while also considering the patient’s medical history. If the patient’s illness is severe or prolonged, they are asked to come for an in-person consultation. The quote below demonstrates how a Dutch GP and medical assistant determine when a patient needs an in-person consultation.

I: When a patient comes to you with a fever, sore throat, cough and/or nasal cold what do you think of first and what is your approach?GP008: I think of an infection first and it could be viral or bacterial.I: And what is your approach?GP008: My assistant who answers the phone does the triage and tries to determine if the patient can be dismissed with advice. […] If the assistant feels, possibly in consultation with me, that the patient needs to be seen, a consultation is planned at my office, or I drive to the patient’s house. I: How severe do the symptoms have to be before a patient is invited? GP008: It varies, there are many factors contributing. So, the severity of the complaints and fever is an important parameter, namely fever above 38 degrees, longer than three days. And how sick someone is, so that's subjective data. Then age, so the elderly, people over 45 years of age and babies, especially under the age of one can come sooner. People who are immunocompromised - so people with cancer or other diseases that can suppress the immune system - are also seen sooner. Dutch GP008, Male, 2 years of working experience

This quote reflects a sentiment commonly shared by the interviewed Dutch and German GPs. They reported a higher likelihood of scheduling in-person consultations for patients with comorbidities, severe symptoms, or those at the extremes of age (children and elderly) compared to patients without these characteristics.

All interviewed GPs stated that they diagnose URTIs based on clinical presentation and physical examinations. They indicated that, during the in-person consultation, a physical examination is performed to check for throat inflammation, swollen lymph nodes, and lung sounds. All GPs noted that when patients exhibit symptoms such as fever, sore throat, and coughing, they initially consider a viral infection, as portrayed by the quote above. However, in cases of doubt, most GPs indicated that they use the C-reactive protein (CRP) test. CRP test is a diagnostic test that measures the level of C-reactive protein in the blood, a biomarker of inflammation. It is used to help distinguish between bacterial and non-bacterial infections and to guide clinical decisions, such as whether antibiotic treatment may be necessary.

Specifically, ten GPs from the Dutch side and seven from the German side reported using the CRP test, either as a point-of-care test or through laboratory analysis. The reasons reported by GPs for performing a CRP test are presented in Table 3.

Additionally, some GPs expressed their desire to perform the CRP test but noted that they do not have the financial means to purchase the necessary tools due to their small practice size.

All Dutch GPs reported basing their diagnostic procedures and treatment for URTIs on the guidelines provided by the Dutch Society of General Practitioners (NHG). Among the German GPs, half of the interviewees reported adhering to the German Society for General Practice/Family Medicine (DEGAM) guidelines for acute and chronic coughing. Additionally, some German GPs mentioned following guidelines from the Association of the Scientific Medical Societies in Germany or the German Respiratory Society. Two German GPs also indicated consulting documents about URTIs published by the Public Health Agency of Lower Saxony. Some German GPs did not follow the mentioned guidelines. As noted above, Dutch GPs demonstrate greater consistency by adhering to a single set of guidelines (NHG). In contrast, German GPs exhibit more variation in their guideline usage, referencing multiple sources.

Treatment options

German GPs often recommend a rest-and-wait approach, home remedies, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) once a URTI caused by a virus is suspected or diagnosed. The quotes below illustrate the treatment options suggested by German GPs:

GP022: […] if I believe it to be a simple common cold, I recommend home remedies. So, gargling with saltwater, drinking tea, resting, giving the body time to regenerate and recover, and one can recommend a few therapeutics. For example, I suggest paracetamol or ibuprofen for the pain or if the fever goes above 38.5. Ibuprofen also helps quite well with sore throat. German GP022, Male, 27 years of working experience

Dutch GPs also recommended treatment options similar to those of German GPs. Two GPs suggested using vitamins. A Dutch GP recommended a high dose of vitamin C, while a German GP indicated that patients should maintain a vitamin-rich diet. Some German and Dutch GPs also recommended nasal spray, with or without corticosteroids, drinking fluids (e.g., tea), and steaming. One Dutch GP indicated that steaming could help, but patients must be careful with heat burn. Additionally, one Dutch GP mentioned cough syrups since patients report experiencing relief.

Antibiotic prescription for URTIs

During the interviews, GPs were asked whether they prescribe antibiotics for URTIs. All the interviewed GPs indicated that antibiotics were prescribed when physical examinations and/or CRP tests showed evidence of bacterial infection. In Germany, GPs frequently mentioned using amoxicillin or penicillin, which aligns with the German guidelines for treating URTIs, such as rhinosinusitis and tonsillitis. Most Dutch GPs’ first and second choices are amoxicillin and doxycycline, which align with NHG guidelines. The following quote from a Dutch GP indicates that the first choice of antibiotic fluctuates over time, possibly due to regional variability in bacterial prevalence and resistance patterns.

I: When you give antibiotics, which ones do you prescribe? GP010: That changes. […] by season and year/period and that really has to do with, for example right now there are a lot of pneumonia that are fine to fight with amoxicillin. Decision-making on antibiotic prescriptions

This study explored the contexts and concepts influencing GPs’ decision-making after confirming that they sometimes prescribe antibiotics for URTIs, regardless of whether a bacterial infection is confirmed. The following section outlines the contexts and concepts that were assessed.

Clinical assessments

All German GPs indicated that their decision-making is primarily guided by clinical assessments (such as the duration of symptoms and the suspicion of pneumonia, severe bronchitis, and tonsillitis), including diagnostic testing. The following quote illustrates how clinical assessments primarily influence German GPs’ decision-making in determining the most appropriate treatment.

Patient characteristics

As mentioned previously, GPs indicated that there are situations where antibiotics are prescribed for URTIs without a clear diagnosis of bacterial infection. This was most often due to patient characteristics such as age and comorbidities. Most of the interviewed GPs indicated that they are more likely to consider prescribing antibiotics for young children and older adults than for young adults with URTIs. Antibiotics are also more likely to be considered for immunocompromised patients or patients with comorbidities such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) or Diabetes Mellitus (DM) than patients without comorbidities. Most GPs indicated that they tend to offer delayed antibiotic prescriptions to these patients, who have a reduced ability to cope with the disease. These considerations are driven by the belief in avoiding potential complications among these patient groups. (See Additional file 3 for quotes related to this theme).

Most GPs emphasized the importance of shared decision-making and considering patients’ expectations during the consultation. According to interviewed GPs, this approach often leaves patients more satisfied with the decision not to prescribe antibiotics when they leave the GP’s office.

Furthermore, GPs on both study sites indicated that patients from foreign countries who have used antibiotics to treat URTIs or who are used to easily getting antibiotics are more likely to expect antibiotic treatment and may coerce their GPs if antibiotics are not offered. Moreover, language barriers, such as a patient’s inability to communicate in the country’s official language, can hinder GPs from effectively educating foreign patients. In some cases, a few GPs noted that the combination of language barriers and patient pressure results in the unnecessary prescription of antibiotics for URTIs (See Additional file 3 for quotes related to this theme).

Time constraints

When we inquired about aspects and contexts influencing GPs’ decision-making, time constraints emerged as a prominent concern. More than half of the interviewed GPs reported experiencing limited time for patient treatment decisions, primarily due to high patient volumes and restricted consultation durations, such as the typical 10-15-minute appointments in the Netherlands. These time pressures were noted to influence antibiotic prescribing practices. GPs indicated an increased tendency of delayed antibiotic prescriptions, particularly on Fridays, and reduced opportunities for patient education about antibiotics and their appropriate use. To compensate for the lack of time for in-person patient education, a few Dutch GPs recommended that patients seek information on the website “Thuisarts.nl” (in English “homedoctor.nl”). This independent website is created by GPs and specialists that provide reliable health information. Moreover, some GPs admitted to occasionally prescribing antibiotics to avoid time-consuming arguments with patients, effectively saving time during consultations. Pharmaceutical influence

When German and Dutch GPs were asked about the potential influence of the pharmaceutical industry on their decision-making processes for prescribing antibiotics for URTIs, all of them affirmed that the industry does not influence their prescribing decisions. Some GPs mentioned that their practices have strict policies prohibiting meetings with pharmaceutical representatives (See Additional file 3 for quotes related to this theme). Others noted that they avoid reading materials sent by the industry or attending events organized by pharmaceutical companies.

Patient’s perspectives

Diagnostic procedure

When patients were asked to describe their diagnostic procedures for URTIs, most patients on both sides of the border attempted self-diagnosis based on their symptoms, past experiences with URTIs, and information shared within their social circles. All patients perceived URTIs as self-limiting conditions with a low risk of complications. However, the ones who faced severe or prolonged symptoms sought assistance from their GPs to confirm their self-diagnoses and rule out potential complications. Some patients with medical backgrounds (e.g., working in a healthcare facility, having an education related to medical sciences, or having prior experience with the CRP test) were aware of the CRP test and requested it as part of their diagnostic process. While most German and Dutch patients expressed satisfaction with their GPs, a minority reported difficulties securing appointments because of the preliminary anamnesis evaluation or felt they did not receive sufficient information about treatment options or explanations for their severe symptoms. Treatment options

In this study, most patients did not visit their GPs for URTI symptoms, believing they could manage the symptoms themselves. Instead, they primarily relied on home remedies, rest, and a wait-and-see approach. Reported home treatment options included NSAIDs, paracetamol, and metamizole. Patients also mentioned drinking fluids, using nasal spray or steam, dressing warmly, taking vitamins, and using candy or honey for sore throat. For phlegmy coughs and mucus in the bronchi, they used remedies such as fluid extracts of thyme herb and ivy leaves or tablets containing acetylcysteine.

While all patients agreed that antibiotics are unnecessary for the common cold, nearly half of those interviewed (four from each study site) were uncertain or believed that antibiotics could treat viral infections. Patients who did not receive antibiotics were also asked what might influence their decision to request them for URTIs. Most cited symptom severity and duration, while some mentioned high CRP test results. These perceptions were consistent across both study sites and similar to those of patients who received antibiotics.

On both study sites, all patients indicated that they do not want to use antibiotics unless necessary and that they generally trust their GPs’ decisions regarding treatment options for URTIs. In this study, most patients’ social circles discouraged antibiotic use for URTIs, raising awareness about antibiotic resistance (See Additional file 3 for quotes related to this theme). Many patients cited their understanding of the adverse effects of antibiotics, such as depleting beneficial gut bacteria and contributing to antibiotic resistance, as reasons for avoiding antibiotic requests (See Additional file 3 for quotes related to this theme). Almost all interviewed patients, except for one, had heard about antibiotic resistance. Most patients on both sides could accurately explain what antibiotic resistance means.

Discussion

This qualitative cross-border study investigated the decision-making processes of Dutch and German GPs and patients in the Ems-Dollart region regarding antibiotic prescriptions for URTIs. The study explored and compared procedures and perceptions of URTI diagnosis and treatment among GPs alongside patient perspectives, yielding several pertinent discussion points.

This study found that German and Dutch GPs employ similar approaches when diagnosing and treating URTIs, with the rest-and-wait approach being the primary recommendation. Regarding antibiotic usage, amoxicillin (a broad-spectrum antibiotic) was among the top 2 choices for German and Dutch GPs, highlighting a common approach in treatment practices. Although most GPs on both sides of the border used guidelines to manage URTIs, Dutch GPs consistently used a single set of guidelines (NHG), whereas German GPs showed more variation by referencing multiple sources. Despite this difference, the German guidelines offer similar recommendations, though they are tailored to various target groups, including regional, national, and specific medical specialties. Furthermore, we also observed a lack of awareness regarding guidelines in some German GPs. These findings align with previous research indicating higher guideline adherence in the Netherlands compared to Germany [9]. Another possible explanation for the higher guideline adherence among Dutch GPs in this study is their younger age, as they may have received more recent education emphasizing the minimization of unnecessary antibiotic use. Despite this difference, our study showed that the interviewed Dutch and German GPs employ comparable methods in assessing and managing patients suspected of having URTIs. This similarity can be attributed to the evidence-based nature of guidelines in both countries, which are aligned with international standards and emphasize prudent antibiotic use.

In our study, Dutch GPs reported using CRP tests slightly more frequently than German GPs. Based on our knowledge, there are no direct comparative statistics on this matter, but one study in Germany on point-of-care CRP testing in outpatient emergency medical services noted that only one-third of the recruited physicians used the CRP test, suggesting that point-of-care CRP testing is not widely adopted in Germany [28]. The findings of this study cannot fully explain the observed differences, and more research is needed to identify the underlying attributes. It is worth mentioning that our findings highlight the issue faced by GPs in solo or small group practices, who often do not perform point-of-care CRP tests due to a lack of financial resources to purchase the necessary equipment. In the Netherlands, diagnostic facilities typically provide CRP point-of-care equipment free of charge to larger GP practices. However, 30–40% of GPs, predominantly from smaller practices, do not perform CRP point-of-care testing due to cost recovery issues [29]. In cases where CRP point-of-care testing is unavailable, GPs can send patients’ blood samples to external laboratories. These facilities typically provide CRP test results by the afternoon or the following day. However, it’s important to note that CRP tests are not without cost to the patient. In the Netherlands, patients are subject to an annual deductible, meaning they must pay a fixed amount each year before their health insurance covers medical expenses. Thus, either GPs or patients incur costs when CRP tests are performed. This financial aspect underscores the need for a more sustainable approach to support high-quality care across practices of all sizes [29]. Moreover, relying on laboratory testing can delay result availability, potentially postponing antibiotic prescription decisions. This delay may create tension, particularly in cases where patients are anxious to know if medication is necessary.

Our data indicated that the interviewed GPs aimed to prescribe antibiotics judiciously; however, some GPs reported instances where antibiotics were prescribed without a clear indication of a bacterial infection. Various concepts, including the patient’s age, existing health conditions, patient pressure, past antibiotic experiences, ability to communicate in the country’s official language(s), and time constraints, could influence GP’s decision to prescribe antibiotics for URTIs. These findings align with other studies that also examined aspects influencing antibiotic prescription [30–34]. Our study underscored the medical and non-medical reasons for antibiotic prescriptions and demonstrated the complexity of antibiotic prescribing practices and the difficulties in fully adhering to guidelines.

While examining patients’ decision-making processes concerning treatment options for URTIs, this study found that people often relied on self-management and home remedies. An antibiotic was considered not needed or as a last resort to treat URTIs. In this study population, most patients were aware of the adverse impact of antibiotics and understood that antibiotics were unnecessary for the common flu. Although this awareness is important in managing URTIs, some misconceptions were still found. Some interviewed patients believed that antibiotics could treat viral infections. This highlights possible gaps in knowledge that could influence decisions to request antibiotics for URTIs. The findings of this study also underscored the need for enhanced patient education about the difference between bacteria and viruses, their link with URTIs, the appropriate use of antibiotics, and the consequences of antibiotic misuse, including antimicrobial resistance. Communication campaigns that translate medical evidence into accessible language on accessible platforms could help address misconceptions and empower patients to make informed healthcare decisions. In the Netherlands, some GPs recommend patients to seek information on the website Thuisarts.nl. However, interviewed patients indicated a preference for more personal contact with their GP. This emphasizes that patients’ needs regarding the information distribution method are not fully met, indicating there is room for improvement in providing more accessible and understandable information.

Another noteworthy finding reveals a misalignment between GP perceptions and patient expectations. GPs often felt pressured to prescribe unnecessary antibiotics to satisfy patients, while patients indicated their satisfaction was more linked to (i) personalized information about symptoms, (ii) reassurance about treatment options, and (iii) feeling heard and understood by their GP. Another Dutch study revealed that patient satisfaction was more strongly associated with receiving information and reassurance than being prescribed antibiotics [35]. This disconnect further highlights the importance of effective communication in primary care. In the Netherlands, a study showed that engaging in shared decision-making, where GPs and patients collaboratively discuss treatment options, can help reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions, particularly for younger adults [36]. This highlights the importance of GPs adopting patient-centered communication strategies and embracing shared decision-making to reduce unnecessary antibiotic use and enhance patient satisfaction.

Strengths and Limitations

One of our study’s strengths is its inclusion of both patients and GPs, which allowed for a comprehensive exploration of their perspectives on antibiotic prescribing for URTIs. This dual approach uncovers potential socially undesirable aspects from both sides of the consultation, highlighting discrepancies between healthcare providers’ perceptions and patients’ actual experiences. Moreover, the study’s cross-border design is a unique strength, enabling a comparative analysis between Dutch and German healthcare contexts. This comparative approach offers valuable insights into cross-national variations in antibiotic prescribing practices, thereby enhancing the credibility and applicability of our conclusions.

Our qualitative study recognizes several limitations. First, we must acknowledge the potential influence of social desirability bias on our findings. This bias may have led participants to report more conservative antibiotic prescribing practices or requests than they employ, potentially skewing our results. Nevertheless, our study has yielded valuable data indicating instances of redundant antibiotic prescriptions, gaps in guideline awareness, and misconceptions among participants. These findings highlight the relevance and importance of this study in identifying areas for improvement in antibiotic stewardship practices. On the German side, the patient cohort primarily consisted of young adults currently or previously enrolled at the University of Oldenburg. This demographic focus enhances the data validity, particularly for young adults, but a different participant demographic might yield contrasting insights that could complement our findings. Although we did not include different patient groups, this study still uncovered the struggles of those not included, such as those with communication difficulties or language barriers, through the GP interviews. By acknowledging this limitation, we stress the importance of considering diverse populations in future studies while demonstrating the relevance of our current findings to underrepresented groups. Moreover, there may be observer bias among GPs, as indicated by at least two GPs who mentioned refreshing their knowledge in preparation for the interviews. Conversely, on both study sides, some patients and GPs were affiliated with the Academic Hospital in Groningen or the University of Oldenburg, which resulted in the recruitment of participants with above-average medical knowledge or GPs who were more conscientious about antibiotic prescribing. This recruitment strategy may have influenced the perspectives of our participants, so caution is needed in generalizing our findings. However, as stated before, our study still uncovered misconceptions, confirmed knowledge gaps among patient groups, and highlighted unnecessary antibiotic prescribing for URTIs on both study sides.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated persistent challenges in antibiotic prescribing practices, including prescribing antibiotics without clear indications of bacterial infections. Aspects such as patient age, comorbidities, previous antibiotic use, and time constraints play important roles in these decisions, reflecting the complex nature of clinical decision-making in primary care settings. This study also showed misconceptions among patients, particularly concerning the effectiveness of antibiotics against viral infections. This highlights the importance of ongoing patient education initiatives to bridge knowledge gaps and promote more informed healthcare decision-making. In conclusion, GPs working in the Dutch and German Ems-Dollart region have made commendable efforts to align with international antibiotic stewardship guidelines, but there is still room for improvement. Addressing these challenges requires a continued focus on enhancing diagnostic capabilities in smaller GP practices, more attention to training that refines communication strategies, and improving GPs’ skills to consider patient expectations.
