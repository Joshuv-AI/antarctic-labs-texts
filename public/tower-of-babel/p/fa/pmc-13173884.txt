Mosquito vector composition and biting behavior of malaria vectors in forest versus humid savanna eco-epidemiological settings of Cameroon
Authors: Aline G Bouopda-Tuedom, Claire B Kiam, Luc Aabate, Albert N Bayibeki, Ibrahima Ibrahima, Charlène Tina Nanssong-Vomo, Brigitte F Tumamo, Clément Onguene, Parfait H Awono-Ambéné, Lawrence S Ayong, Carole E Eboumbou Moukoko, Isabelle Morlais, Sandrine E Nsango
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe effectiveness of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLIN) depends largely on the biting behaviour of the mosquito vectors. Therefore, understanding vector bionomics in a given epidemiological setting is essential for evaluating the impact of these malaria vector control tools. This study presents the entomological profile of malaria in two ecologically distinct settings in Cameroon, Mfou, a forest region, and Tibati, a savannah region.MethodsAdult mosquitoes were collected both outdoors and indoors in randomly selected households between April and July 2019 using Human Landing Catches (HLCs). After morphological identification of the mosquitoes, a subset of anopheline mosquitoes from each species was dissected to determine parity status. DNA from the head and thorax was used for molecular analysis to identify individual mosquitoes to the An. gambiae complex and An. funestus group, and to detect Plasmodium infection. Entomological Inoculation Rate (EIR) was estimated based on Plasmodium infection rate as revealed by qPCR.ResultsA total of 3,651 Anopheles mosquitoes were collected (1,817 in Mfou and 1,834 in Tibati) and identified as belonging to 9 vector species: Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.), An. coluzzii, An. funestus s.s., An. leesoni, An. moucheti, An. nili, An. ziemanni, An. paludis and An. pharoensis. The predominant vector was Anopheles gambiae s.l. in Mfou (68.96%; 1253/1817) with An. gambiae s.s. representing 79.22% of the An. gambiae complex. In Tibati, An. funestus s.l. was the major vector, accounting for 49.95% (916/1834) of the total Anopheles collection, and An. funestus s.s. was the main species within the An. funestus group (97.41%). Overall, host-seeking activity was not significantly different between indoor and outdoor collections at either site, with 48.71% of females caught indoors in Mfou and 46.89% in Tibati. In both sites, a peak of aggressiveness was observed outdoors between 4–5 h in the morning, with a rate of 2.68 bites per person per hour in Mfou and 2.31 in Tibati. The EIR was higher in Mfou, with an overall EIR of 1.47 infectious bites per person per night indoors and 1.00 ib/p/n outdoors, than in Tibati (indoors EIR = 0.25 ib/p/n and outdoors EIR = 0.23 ib/p/n). In Mfou, An. gambiae s.s. was the primary vector (indoors EIR = 0.84 ib/p/n and outdoors EIR = 0.60 ib/p/n), while in Tibati, An. coluzzii and An. funestus s.s. contributed equally to malaria transmission (An. coluzzii: indoors EIR = 0.11 ib/p/n and outdoors EIR = 0.05 ib/p/n; An. funestus s.s.: indoors EIR = 0.15 ib/p/n and outdoors EIR = 0.05 ib/p/n).ConclusionOur study confirmed the key epidemiological role of An. gambiae s.s., An. coluzzii and An. funestus s.s. as major malaria vectors in Cameroon. Notably, we recorded substantial outdoor and early morning biting activity by Anopheles mosquitoes in the two surveyed localities. These findings underscore the limitations of bed nets as a standalone intervention and emphasize the need for complementary control strategies targeting both indoor and outdoor mosquito populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05861-9.Keywords: Malaria, Mosquito vectors, Diversity, Biting behaviour, Cameroon

Background

Malaria remains the deadliest vector-borne disease globally and the leading cause of mortality in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The World Health Organization’s most recent estimates report 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 related deaths worldwide in 2024, with approximately 94% of cases occurring in the WHO African Region and 80% of fatalities among children under five [1]. Since the early 2000s, malaria control in SSA has relied heavily on insecticide-based interventions, notably long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) [2]. The rapid scaling up of these strategies has been the major driver of the decline in malaria morbidity and mortality observed over the past two decades [2]. In Cameroon, these vector control strategies contributed to a decline in malaria prevalence from 41% in 2000 to 24% in 2015, alongside a 54% decrease in malaria deaths [3].

However, progress on malaria control has stalled since 2015, with resurgent cases in several countries, including Cameroon, underscoring the diminishing efficacy of control measures [4]. A major challenge is the rise of insecticide resistance in key malaria vectors [5–7]. Since the introduction of LLINs in Cameroon, multiple resistance mechanisms have been documented in An. gambiae sensu lato (s.l.) and An. funestus s.l., including target site mutations [8, 9], cuticular resistance [6] and metabolic detoxification [10, 11].

Additionally, the success of vector control depends critically on mosquito behavior, such as biting and resting patterns [12]. Shifts in these behaviors in response to LLIN pressure have been increasingly reported in malaria vectors across endemic regions, including Cameroon [13–16]. Such changes include altered biting times [17], a shift from endophagy to exophagy [14–16] and from endophily to exophily [18].

In Cameroon, over 30 million LLINs have been distributed nationwide through 4 major campaigns, and ownership rates exceed 70% [19–21]. In this context of widespread LLIN coverage, entomological surveillance must not only track insecticide resistance but also monitor vector behavior and transmission dynamics. While insecticide resistance in Cameroonian mosquito population is well documented, much less is known about behavioral adaptations and their epidemiological implications, particularly in savannah and forest regions such as the Adamawa and Center.

Despite high LLIN coverage, malaria transmission remains intense in these areas. According to the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP) [20], Adamawa and Center regions recorded the highest incidence rates in the country (141 and 120 per 1,000 inhabitants, respectively) in 2019 [20], coinciding with the period of the present study. This persistence of malaria transmission may be linked to vector behavioral changes, which can only be captured through real-time entomological monitoring [15–17, 22–25]. Such investigations are essential to update knowledge on vector population dynamics and behavioral responses to control interventions.

This study assessed malaria vector bionomics and key mosquito behaviors in Mfou (Center region) and Tibati (Adamawa) following intensive LLIN deployment. Entomological surveys were conducted in both sites, evaluating parameters including vector species diversity and abundance, biting rhythm, feeding behavior, parity, Plasmodium infection rate and entomological inoculation rate (EIR). The findings aim to support the NMCP in refining vector control strategies tailored to these high-burden settings.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Cameroon National Ethics Committee for Research in Human Health (CNERSH, No. 2018/05/1011/CE/CNERSH/SP and CNERSH, No. 2019/05/1161/CE/CNERSH/SP). Volunteers for collections were recruited through community information meetings and during these community sensitizations, the study objectives, procedures, and participants' right to withdraw were clearly explained, with opportunities for questions. Written informed consent form was obtained from all volunteers participating in HLCs. Agreement from heads of households was obtained prior to mosquito collection in all selected households. Collectors were monitored throughout the study and at the end of collections, all collectors were given doxycycline as malaria prophylactic treatment.

Description of the study sites

Entomological surveys were conducted in two ecologically distinct settings, during rainy season, in April 2019 in Mfou (3°72N; 11°64E) and July 2019 in Tibati (6°46’N, 12°63’E) (Fig. 1).

Map showing the collection sites in the two different eco-epidemiological settings of Cameroon

Mfou is a semi-urban area located in the Mefou et Afamba Division, Center region of Cameroon. The region is situated in a forested South of Cameroon. It is characterized by a dense forest vegetation and an equatorial climate with two dry seasons (November–February and June–July) and two rainy seasons (August–October and March–May). In Mfou, annual rainfall average is around 1,500 mm, while the average monthly temperature varies between 19 and 29 °C and the mean annual humidity between 66 and 80%. Malaria transmission is perennial with higher prevalence during the rainy seasons [3, 20].

Tibati is a semi-urban area located in the Djerem Division, Adamawa region. The region is in the Cameroon’s highland massifs that form a transition between the equatorial forest in the south and the large shrubby savannah in the north. The climate is temperate, with shrub savannahs and gallery forests. It is a wet tropical type with two seasons, a dry season (November–February) and a rainy season (March–October) [19]. The average annual temperature is around 23.6 °C and the annual rainfall in Tibati ranges from 1192 to 2023 mm. Malaria transmission is seasonal in the locality, peaking during the rainy season [3, 20].

The hydrographic profile of both sites is characterized by the presence of several rivers such as Djerem and Mefou rivers. The villages around are crossed with lakes, ponds, agricultural sites and swamps favorable to the reproduction of the main vectors An. gambiae s.l. and An. funestus s.l. Human activities are mainly agriculture and fishing in both sites.

Study design and mosquito collection

Adult mosquitoes were sampled using Human Landing Catches (HLCs). Collections were performed during ten and fourteen consecutive nights in Mfou and Tibati, respectively. Mosquito collections were carried out from 18:00 to 9:00. In Mfou and Tibati, a total of 25 and 35 houses were selected, respectively, and used for HLCs. At each house, two collectors were assigned, one indoors and one outdoors. Each night, collectors were placed from 18:00 to 1:00 and shifted for another group of collectors from 1:00 to 9:00. A supervisor ensured that collectors switched locations every two hours overnight to reduce collection bias. Mosquitoes landing on collectors’ legs were captured using hemolysis tubes, which were stored in a bag for later identification.

Mosquito processing and identification

The day following collection, adult mosquitoes from HLCs were morphologically identified to genus level (Aedes, Anopheles, Mansonia, Culex and others) using standard taxonomic keys. Further classification to species or complex was performed using established identification keys according to Gillies and De Meillon and Coetzee [26, 27]. All identified Anopheles specimens were individually preserved in labeled Eppendorf tubes containing silica gel for subsequent molecular analysis. To determine parity rates of Anopheles vectors, we dissected ovaries from a representative sample (> 60%) of collected female Anopheles following the method described by Detinova in 1962 [28]. Only unfed female mosquitoes were selected for dissection.

DNA isolation, identification of Anopheles species and detection of Plasmodium infection

A randomly selected subset of mosquitoes from the An. gambiae complex and An. funestus group was identified to species-level using molecular tools. Genomic DNA was extracted from individual head-thoraxes using a 2% cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol [29]. Multiplex PCR was performed with species-specific primers targeting the ribosomal DNA intergenic spacer (rDNA IGS) for An. gambiae s.l. and the internal transcribed spacer region 2 (ITS2) for An. funestus s.l. [30, 31].

Briefly, PCRs were performed in a 25 µl of mix reaction volume, containing 1X Hot-Start PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 100 nM of each primer (Eurogentec, Belgium) and 2 µl of template DNA. The amplification was carried out in a PCR system (Applied Biosystems). For An. gambiae s.l., PCR conditions were: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 10 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. For An. funestus s.l., PCRs were run as follow: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 45 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 40 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. A restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis was subsequently performed to separate An. gambiae and An. coluzzii species. Digestion reactions were set at a final volume of 15 µl with 0.15 µl of Hha I restriction enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1.5 µl of enzyme buffer and 8 µl of PCR product. Digestion was carried out at 37 °C for 3 h. A 8 µl volume of the digested fragments, for An. gambiae s.l. or PCR products, for An. funestus s.l., was loaded on a 2% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide and the gel was observed on a transilluminator with UV light.

Plasmodium infections were detected and identified using High Resolution Melting (HRM) real-time PCR targeting the 18S ribosomal RNA gene (18S rRNA), following the protocol of Mangold et al. [32]. Reactions were performed in 10 μl volumes using 5 × HOT Pol EvaGreen® qPCR Mix Plus (Euromodex, Souffelweyersheim, France) with 1 μl template DNA. Amplification was conducted on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California, USA) with the following program: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min; 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s (ramp rate 4.4 °C/s), 50 °C for 5 s (2.2 °C/s), and 72 °C for 20 s (4.4 °C/s); followed by melting curve analysis (95 °C for 2 min (2.2 °C/s), 68 °C for 2 min (2.2 °C/s) and 90 °C for 1 s with 15 readings/°C), concluding with a stepwise temperature increase (0.03 °C/s) to 95 °C. The melting curves profile analysis allowed differentiation of Plasmodium species within positive samples.

Statistical analysis

Entomological parameters were derived from HLC data collected by site and included: vector diversity; species abundance (number of specimens of each species); endophagy rate (ER, proportion of mosquitoes caught indoors relative to the total collection); human bite rate (HBR, mean number of Anopheles females collected per person per night); parity rate (PR, proportion of parous females among dissected mosquitoes); infection rate (IR, percentage of Plasmodium-positive mosquitoes among mosquitoes analyzed by PCR analysis); and entomological inoculation rate (EIR, calculated as IR × HBR and expressed as infective bites per person per night). Hourly human-biting activity was defined as the number of Anopheles females collected per person per hour.

Data were analyzed using R statistical software (version 4.5.1) [33]. We assessed ER, PR and IR using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with binomial distribution, modeling individual mosquito status as the response variable with location (indoors/outdoors), parity (parous/nulliparous) and infection status (infected/non-infected) as explanatory variables. For count data, HBR (human biting rate) and EIR (entomological inoculation rate) were modeled using negative binomial distributions to account for overdispersion. HBR models used Anopheles mosquito counts as response, while EIR models used Plasmodium-infected mosquito counts. Study site (Mfou/Tibati), location, and mosquito species were included as fixed effects. The significance of fixed effects was assessed via Type II Wald chi-square tests, with post-hoc Tukey tests for multiple comparisons. The variable “village” (5 villages in Mfou, 7 in Tibati) was included as a random intercept in all models. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Mosquito diversity and abundance

A total of 6,951 mosquitoes were sampled during 100 person-nights in Mfou and 140 person-nights Tibati. Out of these, 3,373 (48.53%) were collected in Mfou and 3578 (51.47%) in Tibati. Morphological identification classified mosquitoes into five genera, namely Aedes, Anopheles, Coquilletidia, Culex, and Mansonia. Anopheles genus was the most abundant both in Mfou (n = 1817, 53.87%) and Tibati (n = 1834, 51.26%). Culex represented 45.89% (1642/3578) of the mosquitoes captured in Tibati, and Mansonia 28.05% (946/3373) of the specimens caught in Mfou. The others genera, namely Aedes and Coquilletidia, were found in both settings but at a low proportion (< 1%) (Fig. S1).

Anopheles species diversity and abundance

Anopheles mosquitoes were identified morphologically and caught specimens were classified into six anopheline species, including An. gambiae s.l., An. funestus s.l., An. moucheti, An. ziemanni, An. paludis, An. nili and An. pharoensis (Fig. 2 and Table S1). In Mfou, An. gambiae s.l. was the most abundant vector, representing 68.96% (n = 1253) of the total anopheline caught. Anopheles funestus s.l. constituted 24.22% (n = 440) of the Anophelinae collection and An. moucheti 6.38% (n = 116). Anopheles ziemanni and An. paludis accounted for less than 1% of the specimens captured. By contrast, in Tibati, An. funestus s.l. and An. gambiae s.l. were the most represented, 49.95% (n = 916) and 42.91% (n = 787), respectively. Anopheles moucheti and An. ziemanni were less abundant, accounting for 3.38% (n = 62) and 2.94% (n = 54) of the captured Anopheles. Anopheles nili and An. pharoensis were found at lower percentages (< 1%) (Table S1).

Indoor and outdoor human-biting behavior

The human biting behavior of the different Anopheles species is presented in Table 1. The overall proportion of female anopheline mosquito vectors caught indoors was 48.71% [46.38–51.03] (885/1817) in Mfou and 46.89% [44.59–49.21] (974/1834) in Tibati, and the difference is not significant (LRT X21 = 1.152, P = 0.283). However, the endophagy rate was significantly different between anopheline species (LRT X26 = 14.757, P = 0.022) and there was a significant species-by-site interaction (LRT X23 = 16.536, P < 0.001). An. funestus were caught indoors more frequently in Mfou (58.18% [53.42–62.84]) as compared to Tibati (47.49% [44.21–50.78]) (LRT X21 = 13.17, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

The biting patterns differed significantly across Anopheles species (LRT X26 = 340.58, P < 0.001) (Table 2). In Mfou, An. gambiae s.l. contributed the most to mosquito bites with an average of 12.5 bites/person/night, while in Tibati, An. gambiae s.l. and An. funestus s.l. exhibited similar biting densities, 5.62 bites/person/night and 6.54 bites/person/night, respectively.

Human-biting activities of Anopheles species

The hourly human-biting activity of the total Anopheles species, An. gambiae s.l., and An. funestus s.l. in Mfou and Tibati is shown in Fig. 2. Overall, anopheline mosquitoes bite throughout the night both outdoors and indoors, from 18:00 until 9:00. The highest biting activities occurred outdoors between 4:00 and 5:00 AM both in Mfou (HBR = 2.68 b/p/h) and in Tibati (2.31 b/p/h). Indoors, the peak of activity was reached a bit earlier, between 3:00 and 4:00 AM, HBR = 2.54 b/p/h in Mfou and HBR = 1.79 b/p/h in Tibati (Fig. 2A). A certain proportion of bites, 22% in Mfou and 27.8% in Tibati, occurred before 22:00 and after 5:00, when people are most likely awake and unprotected. Specifically, 29.8% of An. funestus s.l. in Mfou and 31.1% of An. gambiae s.l. in Tibati were biting before 22:00 or after 5:00 (Table S2). In addition, the proportion of mosquitoes biting outside human sleeping hours was negatively correlated with the HBR at both sites (Fig. S2).

In Mfou, An. funestus s.l. had a higher biting rate during the second part of the night and presented a unique peak early in the morning, between 4:00 and 5:00 AM both indoors (HBR = 1.14 b/p/h) and outdoors (HBR = 0.9 b/p/h) (Fig. 2B). Anopheles gambiae s.l. showed a bimodal activity, with a first peak recorded between 23:00 and 24:00 for indoor bites HBR = 1.42 b/p/h and between 24:00 and 1:00 for outdoor bites HBR = 1.56 b/p/h. The strongest peak was recorded in the second part of the night, between 2:00 and 3:00 for indoor catches (HBR = 1.96 b/p/h) and 3:00–4:00 for outdoors (HBR = 2.02 b/p/h) (Fig. 2C).

In Tibati, the biting activity of An. funestus s.l. was predominantly outdoors during the second part of the night, and An. funestus s.l. was more aggressive between 4:00 and 5:00 AM, with an HBR of 1.46 b/p/h. Indoors, the peak of aggressiveness of An. funestus s.l. occurred between 3:00–4:00 AM (HBR = 0.96 b/p/h) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, the highest biting rates of An. gambiae s.l. were recorded during the second part of the night, reaching 0.69 b/p/h between 3:00 and 4:00 AM indoors and 0.73 b/p/h from 4:00 and 5:00 AM outdoors (Fig. 2C).

Parity rate

Ovaries of 2330 Anopheles females were dissected (1149 in Mfou and 1181 in Tibati, respectively) and 1,388 (59.57%) were identified as parous (Table S3). The parity rate was similar between the two sites, 59.79% (n = 687) in Mfou and 59.36% (n = 701) in Tibati (LRT X21 = 0.059, P = 0.809). The average parity rate did not differ significantly between indoor and outdoor catches, 647/1113 (58.13%) and 741/1217 (60.89%) respectively (LRT X21 = 3.165, P = 0.075), despite rates were higher outdoors when considering An. gambiae s.l. and An. funestus s.l. only (LRT X21 = 6.153, P = 0.013) (Table S3). Overall, the parity rate differed among Anopheles species (LRT X26 = 15.611, P = 0.016). While the parity rates were similar between An. gambiae s.l. and An. funestus s.l. (60.33% and 61.56%; LRT X21 = 2.238, P = 0.135), the rate was lower in An. moucheti, 34.91% (37/106). In addition, the parity rate of An. moucheti was significantly different between collection sites (26.58% in Mfou and 59.26% in Tibati; LRT X21 = 8.899, P = 0.003) and from indoors and outdoors (46.67% indoors and 26.23% outdoors; LRT X21 = 4.748, P = 0.029).

Plasmodium infection rate

A subset of 2,144 Anopheles mosquitoes (905 from Mfou and 1,239 from Tibati) were tested for Plasmodium infection (Table 3). The overall Plasmodium infection rate was significantly higher in Mfou, 6.85% [5.38–8.69], than in Tibati, 1.78% [1.18–2.67] (LRT X21 = 26.322, P < 0.001). Infection rate was higher indoors (5.39% [4.06–7.11]) as compare to outdoors (2.95% [2.15–4.02]), but the difference did not reach statistical significance (LRT X21 = 2.932, P = 0.087). None of the other Anopheles species, An. ziemanni, An. paludis An. nili and An. phaorensis were found infected. An. funestus s.l., An. gambiae s.l. and An. moucheti carried all Plasmodium infections, and infection rate varied according to species (LRT X22 = 6.912, P = 0.031), An. gambiae being the most infected (Fig. 3A).

Entomological inoculation rate (EIR)

Malaria transmission varied between Anopheles species and sites (Table 3). Entomological inoculation rate (EIR) was 1.25 infectious bites per person per night (ib/p/n) in Mfou and EIR was significantly lower in Tibati, 0.23 ib/p/n (LRT X21 = 6.534, P = 0.002). Anopheles gambiae s.l. with an overall EIR of 0.47 ib/p/n was the most important contributor to malaria transmission. Its EIR was significantly higher than that of An. funestus s.l. (0.14 ib/p/n, Tukey’s P = 0.025) and An. moucheti (0.03 ib/p/n, Tukey’s P = 0.004).

Species composition of the Anopheles gambiae complex and the Anopheles funestus group

A sub-sample of 568 An. gambiae s.l. specimens from Mfou and 439 from Tibati were randomly selected to identify sibling species of the An. gambiae complex. Anopheles gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii were unevenly distributed in the study sites (OR = 4.39 [3.30–5.85], P > 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). Anopheles gambiae s.s. represented the most frequent species of the complex in Mfou (79.22%; n = 450) while in Tibati, the proportion of both species was similar, 53.53% of An. coluzzii (n = 235) and 46.47% of An. gambiae s.s. (n = 204). Within the An. funestus group, almost all specimens tested were An. funestus s.s., 98.89% (269/272) in Mfou and 97.41% (564/579) in Tibati (Fig. 4A).

Composition (A) and relative transmission intensity (B)of the species of the An. gambiae complex and the An. funestus group. EIR, entomological inoculationrate expressed as the number of infective bites received per person per night

Infection rate of the different species differed between study sites (LRT X21 = 24.68, P < 0.001), being higher in Mfou, and infection rate varied according to species (LRT X23 = 13.62, P = 0.003) (Table S4). This was particularly pronounced in Mfou, where infection rate of An. coluzzii was almost 3 times than of An. funestus s.s., 14.12% versus 4.68% respectively (Tukey’s P = 0.021). But, because of the higher density of An. gambiae s.s., the EIR of this species was twice higher than that of An. coluzzii, 0.73 ib/p/n versus 0.37 ib/p/n (Fig. 4B). In Tibati, An. coluzzii (EIR = 0.08 ib/p/n) and An. funestus s.s. (EIR = 0.07 ib/p/n) equally contributed to malaria transmission.

Discussion

Entomological investigations are essential for providing information on vector populations, including their distribution, density, and behaviour in a given region or country. Consequently, these studies are crucial for developing and deploying appropriate tools to combat vector-borne diseases [34].

Our study, carried out in Mfou and Tibati, two ecologically distinct settings in Cameroon confirmed the high diversity of Anopheles species, with seven distinct malaria vectors identified across the two sites. This observation aligns with the known complexity of the vector system in Cameroon, although older studies reported higher diversity [35–38]. All the Anopheles species identified in this study have already been reported as Plasmodium vectors [36, 38, 39]. An. gambiae s.l. and An. funestus s.l. were the most abundant in our collections, consistent with their wide geographical distribution in Cameroon [36, 37, 40]. A higher abundance of An. gambiae s.l. was observed in the forested area of Mfou, whereas An. funestus s.l. was predominant in the savannah region of Tibati. The higher density of An. funestus s.l.in Tibati might be linked to the presence of numerous lakes (Pang Antenne, Mbela Assom, Maissaba and Panyere lakes) and permanent water pools that offer suitable breeding sites for larval development [37, 41]. Additionally, climatic conditions at the time of collection may have influenced the abundance of the different Anopheles species, as seasonality impacts the dynamics of malaria vectors [42–44].

In Mfou, An. gambiae s.l. was twice as abundant as An. funestus s.l., and secondary vectors were caught at low densities. This may indicate that environmental changes, such as deforestation and urbanization, have created ecological niches more favorable for the development of An. gambiae s.l. larval stages. Indeed, previous studies in nearby forested areas performed reported an overabundance of An. moucheti, whose larvae thrive in lentic river habitats with abundant riparian vegetation [37, 45–47]. It has been reported that changes in land use and land cover are detrimental to An. moucheti, resulting in its competitive displacement by An. gambiae [48].

Molecular identification of members of the funestus group indicated that the vast majority of specimens (98%) were An. funestus s.s., with the remaining 2% being An. leesoni. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Cameroon, which have consistently reported An. leesoni at low densities when collected by HLCs [3, 36, 37, 39, 49]. This pattern can be attributed to its known zoophilic behavior, with a primary preference for cattle [50, 51], making it less likely to be sampled by HLCs. The An. gambiae complex comprised only two species, An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii, as expected for the study sites [3, 37, 40, 42, 47, 49, 52, 53]. In Tibati, the two species were equally represented, in line with a previous study in the same area [42]. In Mfou, An. gambiae s.s. was the predominant species, as previously reported [52]. Anopheles gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii exhibit habitat segregation along gradients of urbanization, with An. coluzzii being the predominant species in urbanized settings due to its ability to develop in more polluted larval habitats [54]. Mosquito collections were performed in the Mfou district, which extends from the city of Mfou to rural neighborhoods. Mfou is located 30 km from Yaoundé, and this proximity to the capital city has led to significant demographic growth and socio-economic changes in recent years. The urban expansion of Mfou and its periphery will likely modify the malaria vector composition, highlighting the importance of implementing entomological monitoring and adapting control interventions.

Biting behaviour is an important parameter for understanding the dynamics of malaria transmission in the field. Overall, half of the Anopheles mosquitoes were caught outdoors in both sites. However, host-seeking behaviour differed between sites and species; An. funestus exhibited more pronounced endophagic behaviour in Mfou compared to Tibati. Behavioural changes in biting activity have been reported following the large-scale deployment of LLINs, with host-seeking shifting from indoors to outdoors and biting activity occurring earlier in the evening or later in the morning, when individuals are unprotected by bednets [16, 17, 25]. Several LLIN distribution campaigns have taken place in Cameroon since 2004 [20] and behavioural adaptations due to widespread use of LLINs may have occurred. However, a study undertaken near Mfou in 2000, before LLIN implementation, found endophagy rates of 60.1% for An. gambiae and 61.5% for An. funestus [45]. In Tibati, 58% of female anopheline mosquitoes were caught outdoors in 2021 [55]. Thus, Anopheles populations in our study sites did not experience a clear shift in their biting activity.

Interestingly, we observed that the proportion of mosquitoes biting outside human sleeping hours was negatively correlated with the relative species density at both sites. Specifically, about 30% of An. funestus in Mfou and 31% of An. gambiae in Tibati were biting before 22:00 or after 5:00, when people are not sleeping under their bednet. This result may represent behavioural resilience to ensure blood-feeding and maintain mosquito populations under high LLIN coverage [22]. Anopheline mosquitoes exhibit remarkable plasticity and behavioural strategies to avoid vector control interventions have been widely documented [15–17, 22–25, 56]. According to theoretical models, biting activity patterns under LLIN pressure are expected to shift towards periods when human hosts are unprotected, earlier in the evening and later in the morning [57]. In our study sites, a peak of aggressiveness was recorded between 3:00 and 4:00 AM for indoor collections and between 4:00 and 5:00 AM for outdoor collections. However, in Tibati, no clear peak was observed for An. gambiae, which could indicate changes in the biting rhythm of this species. Longitudinal studies in our field sites are necessary to confirm these putative changes in biting activity following the scale-up of LLIN coverage. Additionally, surveys of human behaviour would help to better estimate the proportion of human exposure to vector bites.

None of the specimens of An. ziemanni, An. nili, An. paludis, or An. pharoensis were found infected with Plasmodium. These species were collected at low densities, and larger collection efforts would be necessary to determine their potential contribution to residual transmission. Indeed, previous works reported that all these species can carry malaria parasites, although infection rates in secondary vectors were lower than that in major vectors [3, 36, 45, 47]. Only three mosquito species were found infected with Plasmodium, An. gambiae s.l., An. funestus s.l. and An. moucheti. Infection rates varied by species and study site. Mosquito infections were almost four times more frequent in Mfou compared to Tibati. This result is consistent with a previous study that reported lower infection rates in Tibati than in a forested village in southern Cameroon [58]. Malaria is driven by a combination of environmental, geographic, socioeconomic, and epidemiological factors and parasite prevalence, in both human and mosquito hosts, varies between locations [59, 60]. Multiple lines of evidence indicate that asymptomatic infections are a major contributor to mosquito infections [61–63]. In our study areas, previous works reported over 60% prevalence among asymptomatic individuals [42, 64, 65]. Therefore, malaria prevalence in human hosts is unlikely to explain the differences in mosquito infection rates between our study sites.

In Cameroon, insecticide resistance has expanded in malaria vector populations across the country and the main vectors, An. gambiae s.l. and An. funestus s.l., show high levels of resistance to most insecticide classes [5, 6]. However, the impact of insecticide resistance on vector competence remains controversial [66–70]. Anopheles gambiae strains carrying insecticide resistance mutations were found to have higher salivary gland infections than susceptible strains, both in experimental and natural conditions [66]. Other studies reported no differences [67] or lower oocyst infections [71] in An. funestus. Further studies at local scales are needed to better understand how insecticide resistance mechanisms might affect parameters linked to parasite transmission in different vector species. In our study sites, ITN ownership was higher in Mfou than in Tibati [64]. Exposure to LLINs has been shown to induce delayed mortality in resistant mosquitoes [72, 73], which could reduce transmission potential. However, the higher EIR in Mfou indicates a higher level of exposure to infectious mosquito bites in this locality. It is possible that transmission occurs at a household scale, preferentially in unprotected houses. Indeed, hotspots of transmission have been widely described in different epidemiological settings [74–76]. This would explain why the protective effect of LLINs on human malaria prevalence was observed only in Mfou [64].

Anopheles gambiae s.l. was the most infected species in our study. This contrasts with Annan et al. (2007) who found higher infection rates in An. funestus as compared to An. gambiae [58]. Other works reported similar infection rates in both species [36, 42]. In areas where different malaria vectors sustain transmission, the role of each species varies with season and from one year to another [77, 78]. Particularly, An. funestus is known to persist during the dry season, sustaining malaria transmission throughout the year [77–81]. Upon molecular characterization of the species within the An. gambiae complex, we found that the infection rate was higher in An. coluzzi, consistent with Boissiere et al. (2013), who reported a higher susceptibility of An. coluzzii as compared to An. gambiae s.s. in experimentally infected mosquitoes [52]. Nonetheless, in Mfou, despite a higher infection rate in An. coluzzii, An. gambiae s.s. was the major vector, responsible for two-thirds of the transmission, and this was due to its higher density in the area. However, the accelerated urbanization currently observed in the Mfou district is likely to favor the proliferation of the more competent An. coluzzii, which may have important epidemiological consequences.

In Tibati, two specimens of An. leesoni from outdoor collections were found infected with P. falciparum. Anopheles leesoni has previously been reported in Cameroon [36, 37, 39, 49], and its potential role as a secondary vector has recently been suggested [39]. Our result confirms the ability of An. leesoni to transmit P. falciparum parasites. However, we cannot exclude the misidentification of these two specimens, as it has been shown that members of the An. gambiae complex can be incorrectly classified as An. leesoni when using the An. funestus multiplex PCR [82]. Although morphological identification of mosquitoes was performed by well-trained technicians in our study, it will be necessary to perform ITS2 and COI sequencing to accurately determine the species status of these specimens and confirm whether An. leesoni can transmit malaria parasites.

Among the three Plasmodium species identified, P. falciparum was the main circulating parasite within the mosquito vectors, consistent with previous studies from Cameroon [3, 38, 42, 49, 55]. No mixed infections were recorded in infected mosquitoes. This was surprising as an epidemiological study among asymptomatic individuals in the same sites and at the same period, found that half of the P. malariae infections were mixed with P. falciparum and one-third of P. ovale infections were co-infected with P. falciparum [64]. However, a previous survey in Tibati also reported mixed infections in human hosts and none in mosquitoes [42]. This trend could suggest that interspecific competition occurs during transmission to the vector [83]. Regardless, the circulation of P. malariae and P. ovale in the studied locations suggests that control programs should also pay attention to non-falciparum malaria parasites to improve malaria elimination efforts.

Our study has some limitations. Mosquito collections were performed during a single period in both sites and longitudinal studies are necessary to better understand the transmission dynamics of the different vector species. Indeed, malaria transmission exhibits seasonal and interannual variability [43, 44], and it will be important to consider long-term time scales to identify the role of each vector species throughout the year and adapt targeted interventions accordingly. Additionally, meteorological parameters such as temperature and rainfall were not measured in this study, though these climatic factors are key drivers of malaria transmission, impacting vector composition, mosquito survival and parasite development [79, 84, 85].

Conclusion

This study described the composition of anopheline mosquitoes in two distinct malaria transmission settings in Cameroon, Mfou and Tibati and confirmed the high diversity of malaria vectors. Our results highlight the important epidemiological role of An. gambiae s.s., An. coluzzii and An. funestus s.s. as the main malaria vectors in the studied areas. Anopheles vector species were unevenly distributed between the study sites underscoring different epidemiological contexts. The higher EIR in Mfou may reflect greater transmission intensity in this forested area—a setting undergoing rapid urban and demographic expansion. Nevertheless, locally resolved surveillance data on human parasite prevalence, clinical incidence, and intervention coverage will be essential to determine whether heterogeneous human exposure underlies the observed EIR difference between the two sites. Outdoor and early morning biting by Anopheles mosquitoes presents a significant challenge to current vector control measures and calls for additional tools that can target both indoor and outdoor mosquito populations.
