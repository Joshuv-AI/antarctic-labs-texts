The specific mediating role of social anhedonia in the relationship between childhood emotional abuse and depressive symptoms in bipolar disorder
Authors: Peiyun Zhang, Qianqian Qin, Kaihong Tang, Weihong Jiang, Chao Liu, Qi Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood trauma is a well-established risk factor for greater severity and poorer prognosis of bipolar depression. While anhedonia particularly in the social domain is theorized to link early adversity to depressive symptoms, this specific pathway remains underexplored in bipolar disorder.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 201 patients diagnosed with bipolar I disorder (current depressive episode). Childhood trauma was assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ). Anhedonia was evaluated multidimensionally, including Revised Social Anhedonia Scale-Chinese Version (RSAS-C) and Revised Physical Anhedonia Scale-Chinese Version (RPAS-C). Depressive symptom severity was rated using the 17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-17). Mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro, controlling for age, sex, years of education, age of onset, illness duration, and psychotropic medication.ResultsMediation analysis revealed that social anhedonia showed a significant indirect effect in the association between childhood trauma and depression severity (indirect effect B = 0.02, 95% CI [0.00, 0.04]). This mediating effect was most robust for emotional abuse (indirect effect B = 0.06, 95% CI [0.01, 0.14]). Notably, the direct path from emotional abuse to depression became non-significant after controlling for social anhedonia, suggesting that the effect of emotional abuse on depression is largely statistically accounted for by social anhedonia. In contrast, physical anhedonia showed no significant mediation effect.ConclusionsThis study identifies social anhedonia as a critical mediator in the pathway from childhood emotional abuse to depressive severity in bipolar disorder. These findings highlight a specific psychosocial mechanism, suggesting that deficits in social reward processing may serve as a key link between early relational trauma and adult mood pathology.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08004-1.

Background

Bipolar depression, the depressive phase of bipolar disorder, is a debilitating condition characterized by high recurrence rates, significant functional impairment, and considerable treatment challenges [1]. Elucidating its underlying pathological mechanisms is therefore critical for developing more effective interventions. Among various risk factors, childhood trauma has emerged as a potent environmental contributor, robustly predicting the onset, severity, and adverse course of bipolar disorder [2–4]. A recent meta-analysis in affective disorders confirmed that exposure to adverse childhood experiences nearly doubles the risk of suicidal behavior (OR = 1.98), with sexual abuse showing the highest risk [5]. Specifically, emotional abuse and neglect are strongly linked to earlier illness onset, more severe symptomatology, and increased psychiatric comorbidity [2, 4].

Anhedonia, a diminished capacity to experience or anticipate pleasure—is a core transdiagnostic feature of depression, reflecting underlying dysfunction in the brain’s motivation and reward systems [6, 7]. Early adverse experiences are theorized to disrupt the maturation of key reward neural circuits, including the prefrontal cortex and striatal regions such as the nucleus accumbens, potentially leading to enduring deficits in hedonic capacity [8, 9]. Consistent with this view, studies across healthy and clinical populations have demonstrated associations between childhood trauma and various dimensions of anhedonia [10, 11]. For instance, emotional neglect has been specifically linked to heightened anhedonic traits, suggesting a potential pathway from early adversity to reward processing deficits [10]. Critically, anhedonia is not merely a symptom; it is intricately connected to depression severity, treatment resistance, and functional impairment [12, 13]. Among these dimensions, social anhedonia, the reduced ability to derive pleasure from interpersonal interactions may be particularly relevant to bipolar depression for several reasons. First, neuroimaging studies have shown that social reward processing engages specific neural circuits (e.g., ventral striatum, medial prefrontal cortex) that are particularly vulnerable to early life stress [14]. Second, longitudinal research has demonstrated that social anhedonia persists even during remission phases in bipolar disorder, suggesting it may represent a trait-like marker rather than a transient state-dependent symptom [12]. Third, from a psychosocial perspective, emotional abuse characterized by rejection, humiliation, and invalidation directly attacks the child’s sense of social safety and self-worth, potentially leading to enduring deficits in social reward processing through disruptions in attachment bonds [15].

Previous research indicates that anhedonic symptoms are closely tied to the overall burden of depressive episodes and poor functional outcomes [16, 17]. Furthermore, anhedonia may represent a critical “psychological internalization pathway,” translating the impact of childhood trauma into the profound amotivation and pleasure deficits characteristic of bipolar depression [18]. The assessment of anhedonia has evolved to recognize its multidimensional nature, encompassing distinct components such as anticipatory versus consummatory pleasure and social versus physical anhedonia [12, 19]. Importantly, different subtypes of childhood trauma may exert their effects through specific anhedonia dimensions. For example, emotional abuse might be more strongly linked to deficits in anticipatory pleasure and social engagement, whereas physical neglect could be associated with reduced consummatory pleasure [3, 10].

However, most existing studies on these mediating mechanisms have focused on major depressive disorder or schizophrenia, leaving the unique pathways in bipolar depression relatively unexplored [4, 20]. Notably, research in other diagnostic populations has highlighted the specific impact of emotional abuse on symptom severity and clinical presentation. For instance, a systematic review in obsessive-compulsive disorder found that emotional abuse and neglect were significantly associated with greater symptom severity and specific obsession dimensions [21], suggesting that emotional maltreatment may exert transdiagnostic effects on psychopathology.The heterogeneous and complex nature of bipolar disorder, with its distinct neurobiological underpinnings and clinical course, suggests that mechanisms identified in other disorders may not be directly translatable [22, 23]. For instance, neuroimaging studies have revealed enhanced global brain network efficiency in bipolar disorder compared to major depressive disorder, hinting at distinct neuropathological processes that could influence how childhood trauma and anhedonia interrelate [24]. Furthermore, evidence that childhood trauma moderates treatment response in bipolar depression underscores the clinical relevance of understanding these mechanisms [25].

Critically, most prior mediation studies have relied on unidimensional measures of anhedonia. To address these gaps, the present study employs a multidimensional assessment of anhedonia (social, physical, anticipatory, consummatory) to provide a more nuanced understanding. We aim to identify which specific anhedonia domain primarily mediates the association between of childhood trauma particularly its subtypes on depression severity in bipolar disorder. We hypothesize that: (1) childhood trauma severity, particularly emotional abuse, will be positively associated with increased depressive symptoms and greater anhedonia, especially in the social domain; and (2) social anhedonia will significantly mediate the relationship between childhood trauma (especially emotional abuse) and depressive symptom severity.

Methods

Participants

A total of 232 patients with bipolar depression who met the enrollment criteria were consecutively recruited from September 2023 to September 2025 as outpatients or inpatients at Nantong Fourth People’s Hospital, Jiangsu Province. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria for bipolar I disorder, and were currently experiencing a depressive episode; (2) Han ethnicity, aged 18–50 years, with a junior high school education or above; Exclusion criteria were: (1) Presence of mental disorders other than bipolar disorder; (2) Presence of serious physical diseases (e.g., neurological, cardiovascular, or metabolic diseases); (3) History of substance dependence or abuse. Initially, a total of 31 patients were excluded for the following reasons: declined to participate (n = 5), enrolled in other studies (n = 6), did not meet age criteria (n = 5), presented with suicidal ideation or attempt (n = 10), and had comorbid physical or other mental disorders (n = 5). Finally, 201 patients were included in the analysis (see Fig. 1).

Measures

Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale (TEPS)

The Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale is an 18-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess the trait capacity for pleasure across two distinct neuropsychological dimensions: anticipatory pleasure and consummatory pleasure. Respondents rate each item on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = “Very false for me” to 6 = “Very true for me”). Higher scores on each subscale indicate a greater capacity for that type of pleasure. The TEPS has demonstrated good internal consistency and construct validity, Cronbach‘s α > 0.75 [26].

Revised Social Anhedonia Scale-Chinese Version (RSAS-C)

Revised Social Anhedonia Scale-Chinese Version(RSAS-C) is a 40-item true/false questionnaire that measures a trait-like deficiency in the ability to experience pleasure from interpersonal interactions and social relationships. Items assess indifference towards social activities, lack of pleasure in conversation, and a general detachment from social rewards. A higher total score indicates greater social anhedonia, Cronbach‘s α typically > 0.80 [27].

Revised Physical Anhedonia Scale-Chinese Version (RPAS-C)

Revised Physical Anhedonia Scale-Chinese Version (RPAS-C) is a 61-item true/false questionnaire designed to measure a diminished capacity to experience pleasure from sensory and physical activities, such as eating, touching, moving, or experiencing nature. Like the RSAS-C, it assesses a stable, trait-like characteristic. The scale has been widely used to study reward deficits in psychiatric populations. Higher scores reflect a greater degree of physical anhedonia, Cronbach‘s α typically around 0.75–0.80 [27].

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ)

The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire includes a 28-item retrospective self-report inventory. It measures the frequency and severity of five types of maltreatment experienced before age 16: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect, each assessed by 5 items. Three additional items constitute a Minimization/Denial scale. Participants rate items on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never True to 5 = Very Often True). Subscale scores (range 5–25) and a total score are calculated, with higher scores indicating more severe trauma. The CTQ has shown excellent psychometric properties, including good internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and convergent validity [28].

17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-17)

This semi-structured interview assesses the severity of core depressive symptoms (e.g., depressed mood, work and activities, guilt, insomnia, psychomotor changes) over the past week. Items are scored from 0 to 2 or 0–4. The total score ranges from 0 to 52, with higher scores indicating greater depression severity [29].

Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS)

The YMRS is an 11-item clinician-rated scale designed to assess the severity of manic symptoms. Items are rated based on patient self-report and clinical observation during the interview. It is widely used in clinical trials for bipolar disorder and has excellent psychometric properties [30].

All clinician-rated assessments, specifically the 17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-17) and the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS), were administered by two experienced attending psychiatrists. Prior to the initiation of the study, both evaluators underwent standardized training to ensure consistency in symptom evaluation and scoring. Inter-rater reliability was established using a subset of patients (e.g., n = 20) prior to formal data collection, yielding an excellent Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of > 0.85 for both scales. Importantly, to prevent any potential observer bias, the clinical evaluators were strictly blinded to the participants’ self-reported childhood trauma histories (CTQ scores) and anhedonia self-assessments during the clinical interviews.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). First, descriptive statistics were computed to characterize the sample, with continuous variables presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.Second, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the bivariate associations among childhood trauma subtypes, anhedonia dimensions, and depressive symptoms. To control for the risk of Type I errors due to multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to all correlation tests, with a false discovery rate set at 0.05. Third, to test the study hypotheses, mediation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Version 3.5, Model 4) developed by Hayes. In these models, childhood trauma (total score or subtypes) served as the independent variable, depressive symptom severity (HAMD-17) as the dependent variable, and anhedonia dimensions as mediators. All mediation models controlled for age, sex, years of education, age of onset, illness duration, and daily defined doses (DDDs) of four psychotropic medication classes: mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, antidepressants, and benzodiazepines.

Prior to mediation analyses, we assessed key statistical assumptions. All variables showed acceptable normality. Linearity was confirmed via partial regression plots, and the Breusch-Pagan test indicated homoscedasticity (p > 0.05 for all models). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were all below 2.5, indicating no concerning multicollinearity. These diagnostic checks support the appropriateness of the linear mediation models employed.

For all analyses, statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p value < 0.05. The significance of indirect (mediation) effects was determined by examining whether the bias-corrected 95% bootstrap confidence intervals did not include zero. Unstandardized regression coefficients (B), standardized regression coefficient (β), standard errors (SE), and model fit indices (R²) are reported.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nantong City (Approval No.: 2023-K018). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 201 participants (139 females, 62 males) with a mean age of 23.4 years (SD = 7.68) were included in the analysis. The mean years of education was 13.17 (SD = 3.77). Clinical measures indicated moderate levels of childhood trauma, with a mean CTQ total score of 49.77 (SD = 13.99). Among the trauma subtypes, Emotional Neglect (EN) had the highest mean score (14.64, SD = 5.47), followed by Emotional Abuse (EA) (11.38, SD = 5.18). The sample showed a moderate level of depressive symptoms, as reflected by a mean HAMD-17 score of 19.39 (SD = 7.78). Anhedonia symptoms were evident, with mean scores of 23.64 (SD = 4.39) on the RSAS-C and 31.10 (SD = 6.68) on the RPAS-C. Mean scores on the TEPS-Total and its subscales were within the typical range for a clinical population (see Table 1).

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the bivariate associations among childhood trauma subtypes, anhedonia dimensions, and depressive symptom severity. To control for the risk of Type I errors due to multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied across all correlation tests (see Supplementary Tables 1 and Fig. 2).

The total score of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) was significantly positively correlated with social anhedonia (RSAS-C: r = 0.20, pFDR = 0.027) and with physical anhedonia (RPAS-C: r = 0.19, pFDR = 0.036). However, the correlation between CTQ total score and depressive symptoms (HAMD-17: r = 0.16, uncorrected p = 0.020) did not survive FDR correction (pFDR = 0.068).

Among childhood trauma subtypes, emotional abuse (EA) demonstrated the most robust correlation pattern. EA was significantly positively correlated with social anhedonia (RSAS-C: r = 0.27, pFDR < 0.001) and with depressive symptoms (HAMD-17: r = 0.21, pFDR = 0.025), both of which remained significant after FDR correction. In contrast, while EA showed a nominal correlation with physical anhedonia (RPAS-C: r = 0.17, uncorrected p = 0.014), this association did not survive FDR correction (pFDR = 0.057), highlighting the domain-specific impact of emotional abuse on social, rather than physical, anhedonia.

Physical abuse (PA) was significantly correlated with physical anhedonia (RPAS-C: r = 0.19, pFDR = 0.031), but not with social anhedonia or depressive symptoms after correction. Sexual abuse (SA) showed no significant correlations with any anhedonia measures or depressive symptoms following FDR adjustment. Emotional neglect (EN) and physical neglect (PN) were not significantly associated with any anhedonia dimensions or depressive symptoms after correction.

Social anhedonia (RSAS-C) was significantly positively correlated with depression severity (HAMD-17: r = 0.27, pFDR < 0.001), strongly supporting its hypothesized role as a potential mediator. In contrast, physical anhedonia (RPAS-C) was not significantly correlated with depressive symptoms (r = -0.04, pFDR = 0.763).

Regarding the temporal experience of pleasure, both anticipatory (TEPS-ant: r = -0.19, pFDR = 0.031) and consummatory pleasure (TEPS-con: r = -0.29, pFDR < 0.001) were significantly negatively correlated with depressive symptoms, as was the TEPS total score (r = -0.27, pFDR < 0.001). However, none of the TEPS subscales showed significant associations with any childhood trauma measures after FDR correction (all pFDR > 0.05), indicating that while pleasure capacity is closely linked to current depressive symptoms, it may not directly reflect the long-term impact of childhood trauma in this clinical population.

Mediation analyses of anhedonia in the relationships between Childhood Trauma and Depression Symptoms

Mediation analyses were conducted to examine whether social anhedonia (RSAS-C) statistically accounted for the associations between childhood trauma and depressive symptom severity. All models controlled for age, sex, education, age of onset, illness duration, and daily defined doses (DDDs) of mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, antidepressants, and benzodiazepines (see Table 2; Fig. 3). Models were systematically organized to first examine total childhood trauma, followed by specific trauma subtypes.

Model 1: Total Childhood Trauma (CTQ) → Social Anhedonia → Depressive Symptoms.

Using the CTQ total score as the predictor, the path from CTQ to RSAS-C (a path) was significant (B = 0.05, t = 2.33, p = 0.021), indicating that greater childhood trauma severity was associated with higher levels of social anhedonia. The path from RSAS-C to HAMD-17 (b path) was also significant (B = 0.32, t = 2.58, p = 0.011), demonstrating that social anhedonia was positively associated with depressive symptom severity. The indirect effect of CTQ on depressive symptoms through social anhedonia was significant (B = 0.02, 95% bootstrap CI [0.00, 0.04]), confirming a mediating role. The direct effect of CTQ on HAMD-17 (c’ path) became non-significant after including the mediator (B = 0.05, t = 1.28, p = 0.203), suggesting that social anhedonia fully mediated this relationship. The full model, including the mediator and all covariates, explained 17% of the variance in depressive symptoms (R² = 0.17, F = 3.54), indicating a small-to-moderate effect size meaning that while social anhedonia is a significant pathway, other factors also contribute to depression severity in this population.

Model 2: Emotional Abuse (EA) → Social Anhedonia → Depressive Symptoms.

Given the robust correlations observed for emotional abuse in the bivariate analyses, a second model specified EA as the predictor. The path from EA to RSAS-C (a path) was significant (B = 0.19, t = 3.23, p = 0.001), indicating that emotional abuse was strongly associated with increased social anhedonia. The path from RSAS-C to HAMD-17 (b path) remained significant (B = 0.31, t = 2.43, p = 0.016). The indirect effect of EA on depressive symptoms through social anhedonia was significant (B = 0.06, 95% bootstrap CI [0.01, 0.14]), representing a small-to-moderate indirect effect. Notably, the direct effect of EA on HAMD-17 (c’ path) was not significant (B = 0.15, t = 1.45, p = 0.149), indicating that social anhedonia fully mediated the relationship between emotional abuse and depression. The full model explained 17% of the variance in depressive symptoms (R² = 0.17, F = 3.59). To provide clinical context, the indirect effect accounted for approximately 28.6% of the total effect (calculated as indirect effect/total effect: 0.06/0.21), suggesting that social anhedonia is a substantial mechanism through which early emotional abuse influences adult depressive symptoms.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence that social anhedonia, a trait-like deficit in the ability to derive pleasure from interpersonal connections plays a significant and specific mediating role in the association between childhood emotional abuse and depressive symptom severity in patients with bipolar depression. Notably, the direct effect of emotional abuse on depression became non-significant when social anhedonia was accounted for, suggesting that the influence of early relational adversity on adult mood pathology is largely explained statistically by this specific psychological mechanism. In contrast, physical anhedonia did not demonstrate a significant mediating effect, underscoring the specificity of the social reward domain in this pathway. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as statistical mediation rather than causal inference.

From a neurobiological perspective, early adverse experiences are theorized to disrupt the maturation of key reward neural circuits. Specifically, research indicates that bipolar disorder is characterized by distinct dysfunctions in reward processing, particularly within the ventral striatum [14]. Furthermore, childhood trauma has been linked to enduring structural alterations in prefrontal regions [31, 32] and a pro-inflammatory state that selectively impairs social motivation [33]. These biological disruptions may provide a neural basis that is consistent with the specific social anhedonia observed in our study, suggesting that early maltreatment compromises the brain’s capacity to anticipate and enjoy social rewards.

Beyond neurobiological explanations, psychosocial frameworks particularly attachment theory and cognitive models of interpersonal functioning offer valuable insights into the link between emotional abuse and social anhedonia. First, from an attachment theory perspective, childhood emotional abuse, characterized by rejection, humiliation, and invalidation, severely disrupts the formation of secure attachment bonds [15, 34]. Unlike physical abuse, which poses a direct threat to bodily integrity, emotional abuse specifically undermines the child’s sense of self-worth and social safety. This may lead to the development of insecure-avoidant strategies, whereby social anhedonia may function as a defensive mechanism: by dampening the anticipation of social reward, the individual protects themselves from potential rejection and further emotional pain [35].

Second, heightened interpersonal sensitivity and rejection sensitivity likely play a pivotal role. Victims of early emotional maltreatment often internalize maladaptive social schemas, perceiving others as critical or untrustworthy [36, 37]. This “negative social bias” transforms social interactions from potential sources of pleasure into sources of anxiety or threat. Consequently, the reduced capacity to experience social pleasure may not reflect a primary deficit in the reward system per se, but rather a learned devaluation of social stimuli driven by chronic interpersonal vigilance [18, 38].

The stronger indirect effect observed for emotional abuse, compared to overall childhood trauma, warrants particular attention. Characterized by verbal hostility and emotional invalidation, emotional abuse uniquely impairs the development of social reward processing systems by fundamentally altering how interpersonal relationships are experienced and valued [39]. This finding aligns with recent research indicating that specific trauma subtypes exert differential effects on psychological outcomes [11]. The predominance of emotional abuse in our sample further underscores the clinical importance of this often-overlooked form of childhood maltreatment.

The absence of significant mediation effects for physical anhedonia (RPAS-C) is a critical finding that supports the specificity of the trauma-anhedonia link. The RPAS-C primarily measures responses to sensory and physiological stimuli [27]. The fact that emotional abuse predicted social but not physical anhedonia suggests that the observed deficits are not merely a generalized consequence of depression severity or psychotropic medication. Psychotropic medications, particularly antipsychotics and mood stabilizers used in bipolar disorder, are known to induce ‘secondary anhedonia’ or emotional blunting, largely through dopaminergic antagonism. However, medication-induced anhedonia typically manifests as a global reduction in reward responsiveness, affecting physical (e.g., sensory, consummatory) and social domains indiscriminately. If the anhedonia observed in our sample were solely a pharmacological side effect, we would expect a generalized suppression of hedonic capacity across all dimensions. Instead, the specific dissociation found here is consistent with the view that social anhedonia may represent a distinct “psychological scar” of relational trauma, separate from the physiological or vegetative symptoms often seen in bipolar depression. Similarly, the TEPS, while methodologically precise in decomposing pleasure into anticipatory and consummatory components, may dilute the explanatory power of each narrower component in capturing the broader motivational deficit that translates childhood adversity into clinical depression [40]. This underscores the specificity of social anhedonia as the key intermediary in our model.

Clinical implications

The clinical implications of these findings are notable. Identifying social anhedonia as a mediator suggests that interventions specifically designed to enhance reward processing and social engagement such as Behavioral Activation Therapy (BAT) tailored to social goals, Cognitive Remediation Therapy (CRT) targeting social cognition, or Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT) may be particularly beneficial for patients with bipolar disorder and a history of childhood trauma [41, 42]. Brief screening for social anhedonia in clinical settings could help identify individuals who might benefit most from such targeted approaches.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences regarding the temporal relationship between childhood trauma, social anhedonia, and depressive symptoms. Second, as this was a naturalistic clinical sample, the confounding effects of psychotropic medications cannot be entirely ruled out. However, the distinct dissociation observed in our model—where social anhedonia, but not physical anhedonia, mediated the impact of emotional abuse—provides robust evidence that our findings capture a core psychological mechanism rather than a generalized pharmacological blunting effect. Third, our stringent selection criteria and sample demographics (e.g., recruitment from a single clinical center, exclusively Han Chinese adults) limit the broad generalizability of the results. Most notably, while excluding individuals with severe comorbidities and current suicidal ideation or attempts (n = 10) strengthened internal validity, it likely truncated the range of symptom severity within our sample. Given that suicidality is deeply intertwined with both early trauma and anhedonia in bipolar disorder, excluding this high-risk subgroup may have attenuated the observed associations. It is highly plausible that the mediating role of social anhedonia is even more pronounced in patients experiencing suicidality, or that complementary mechanisms (such as impulsivity) become more prominent. Future multi-center, longitudinal studies with more inclusive recruitment strategies are warranted to validate these mechanism-informed pathways across diverse and more complex clinical populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that social anhedonia is a specific psychological mechanism associated with the link between childhood emotional abuse and depressive symptom severity in bipolar disorder. Our findings shift the focus from a general association between trauma and depression to a precise pathway centered on deficits in social reward processing. This pathway is supported by integrated neurobiological and psychosocial frameworks and is distinct from deficits in physical pleasure. By highlighting social anhedonia as a key intervention target, this research points the way toward more personalized, mechanism-informed strategies for alleviating the burden of bipolar depression in those who have experienced early relational trauma.
