Risk prediction of cardiometabolic diseases with depression in the community of Xinjiang production and construction corps
Authors: Ziqi Wang, Jinhe Fan, Jianing Fan, Mingxia Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDepression not only diminishes the quality of life and self-care abilities of the elderly but also exacerbates the burden of pre-existing physical diseases. This study aims to explore the influencing factors of depressive events in patients with cardiometabolic disease (CMD) within a division of the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps (XPCC). Additionally, it seeks to establish a depression risk prediction model to provide a foundation for hierarchical management of this comorbidity in the community and for the stratification of the population.MethodUtilizing baseline data from 2016 and follow-up data from 2018 to 2024, a total of 1,648 individuals were included after excluding those with baseline depression, cognitive impairment, or loss to follow-up. Data on demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, chronic diseases, and health service utilization were collected. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) and adaptive LASSO regression were employed to identify longitudinal predictors. Depression prediction models for the total population and subgroups (non-multimorbidity/multimorbidity) were constructed using GEE and generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), and model performance was evaluated.ResultThe findings revealed that as of 2024, the cumulative incidence of depression was 68.93% (67.02% for non-multimorbidity patients and 74.30% for multimorbidity patients). Model comparisons indicated that the discrimination and accuracy of GLMM were superior in the total population. GEE demonstrated better performance in non-multimorbidity patients, while GLMM consistently outperformed GEE in the multimorbidity population. Self-rated health status and metabolic equivalent emerged as core predictors, with chronic disease treatment and emergency medical treatment identified as significant characteristics for the general population and non-multimorbidity population, respectively.ConclusionThe incidence of cardiometabolic diseases accompanied by depression among the elderly in a division community of the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps (XPCC) is higher than the national average. Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) are more suitable for screening individuals with cardiometabolic diseases. This study emphasizes the importance of physical activity, social security, and health service indicators in predicting the risk of depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24777-8.

Introduction

China’s population is aging rapidly. As the “second baby boomer” generation enters retirement age in 2022, the public health challenges posed by aging are further exacerbated [1]. According to the seventh national population census, the number of elderly individuals aged 60 and above in China reached 264 million, accounting for approximately 18.7% of the country’s total population, while those aged 65 and above reached 190 million (13.5%) [2].It is estimated that by 2050, there will be 395 million people over the age of 65 in China, including 135 million over the age of 80. Concurrently, Life expectancy in China has significantly increased, from 35 years at the founding of the People’s Republic of China to 78.6 years [3], and is expected to exceed 81.3 years by 2035 [4]. This demographic shift has resulted in a substantial increase in the burden of chronic noncommunicable diseases, particularly multimorbidity [5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) National Assessment Report on China’s Aging and Health, the prevalence of chronic noncommunicable diseases among older adults in China is projected to increase by at least 40% by 2030, with more than 75% of them expected to experience multimorbidity.

Cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) encompass a group of chronic conditions that include cardiovascular disease (CVD) and metabolic health issues [6], such as ischemic heart disease (IHD), stroke, hypertension, and diabetes. The rising prevalence of CMDs has positioned them as the leading contributors to the economic burden of diseases in China and globally. CVD is the primary cause of mortality within the Chinese population [7], with IHD identified as the deadliest condition [8, 9]. Notably, the standardized mortality rate for IHD increased by 20.6% from 1990 to 2017 [10]. In 2020, the incidence of new strokes in China surpassed that of the United States (610,000) [11]and the European Union (1.12 million) [12], accounting for nearly a quarter of the annual global stroke events [13]. Additionally, the weighted prevalence of stroke in China (2.6%) significantly exceeds the global average (1.2%) [9]. The prevalence of hypertension remains above 20% [14], resulting in over 10 million deaths worldwide each year [15]. In China, the number of individuals diagnosed with diabetes has reached 140 million [16], with nearly half already identified. Alarmingly, a Markov projection suggests that CVD events in China may increase by over 50% from 2010 to 2030 [17], underscoring the urgent need for effective prevention and management strategies.

At present, many patients can maintain a long survival time after suffering from a type of CMD. Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) is becoming more prevalent in patients with two or more CMDs at the same time. The prevalence of CMM in the Chinese adult population increased from 2.41 to 5.94% between 2010 and 2016. Compared with CMD alone, CMM has a more severe health impact [18], which not only significantly increases the risk of multiple adverse outcomes, including decreased general health [19], depression [20], disability [21], cognitive function [22], and social adjustment [23], but also leads to a 3.7-6.9-fold increase in mortality and a 12–15 year reduction in life expectancy [24], resulting in a heavy family and socioeconomic burden [25]. This state has become an important health concern that cannot be underestimated in the process of CMD. At the same time, due to the complexity of multimorbidity, it is necessary to treat patients not only with regard to the disease itself, but also with regard to their psychological problems [26]. CMD promotes depression through pathophysiological mechanisms, and depression may result from both behavioral changes [27, 28] and biological mechanisms such as dysregulation of the thalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [29, 30].

Reducing the burden of depression among older adults is a global health priority and is essential for promoting healthy aging [31]. Depression is the leading cause of disability worldwide [32], particularly affecting older individuals with chronic diseases. The global average incidence of depression in the elderly population is approximately 31.74% [33], while it is about 10% in China [34]. Notably, depression affects around one-fifth of the chronically ill population [35]. The risk of depression is two to three times higher in patients with diabetes and hypertension compared to the general population [36], and it is 1.5 to 2 times higher among patients with these conditions, with rates of 63.4% versus 36.6% in men [37].In a multi-country study conducted by the World Health Organization (WHO), patients with multimorbidity exhibited a significantly higher association with depression (23.0%) compared to those with depression alone (9.3%) [29]. In China, the incidence of depressive symptoms among middle-aged and elderly patients with chronic disease comorbidity reached as high as 45.65% [38]. This indicates that the risk of depression is notably elevated in individuals with chronic multimorbidity, underscoring the substantial amplifying effect of comorbid conditions on mental health. The model of ‘psychosomatic comorbidity’ is characterized by mutually reinforcing mechanisms, where inadequate primary mental health services result in 50–70% of depressed patients remaining unidentified within the healthcare system [39, 40]. Additionally, at the individual behavior level, depression often co-occurs with other chronic diseases, adversely affecting daily functioning, reducing medication adherence, and increasing the burden of physical illness [41]. Biologically, factors such as chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and autonomic disorders play a significant role in the development of chronic multimorbidity (CMD) and depression [42, 43]. The prevalence of depression among patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) has been reported to range from 18.4 to 45%, although this prevalence varies based on nationality, measurement instruments, study quality, gender, and ethnicity [44]. Therefore, the early identification of depressive symptom risk in individuals with CMD is critical to prevent and delay the progression of this form of psychosomatic comorbidity.

The Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps (XPCC) faces unique challenges in chronic disease management due to its aging population [2](12.78% vs. 11.28% in Xinjiang), limited primary medical resources, and geographical remoteness [45]. While its militarized vertical management system may facilitate chronic disease interventions [46], the risk of cardiometabolic diseases (CMD) with depression in this population remains understudied. Current research primarily examines single CMD to depression associations, lacking evidence for multimorbidity patterns in CMD populations. Additionally, most studies use static predictors, ignoring temporal variability. Cohort-based predictive studies are needed to better understand CMD to depression multimorbidity and guide prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

A longitudinal survey was used in this study. The cohort was established in July-August 2016, using a typical sampling method to select hypertensive and diabetic patients over the age of 60 who were under community chronic disease health Management from 3 urban communities and 2 surrounding regimental farms (including 1 suburban community and 1 regimental community) of a division of the XPCC in China. A total of 1900 community residents were included at baseline after those who were unable to communicate with hearing and vision impairment and sequelae of the disease, as well as those who did not obtain informed consent. The study followed GCP principles and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University (Approval No.: KJ2024-210-01), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study follow-up

The community population cohort of a division of the Xinjiang XPCC has completed five follow-up surveys in 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021, and 2024. The outcome events for this study were collected in 2018 and 2024, with the survey content mirroring that of the baseline survey. Given the recurrence and fluctuation of depressive symptoms, multiple outcome events measured by individuals during the follow-up period were included in the analysis as outcomes at different time points. Four patients with depressive symptoms at baseline were excluded, along with 213 individuals who were lost to follow-up and died during the follow-up period, and 35 individuals with cognitive dysfunction. Ultimately, 1,648 participants were included in the analysis, comprising 1,216 patients with baseline non-multimorbidity and 432 patients with baseline multimorbidity.

Quantitative assessments

The questionnaire comprised general demographic information (age, gender, marital status, education level, etc.), daily living habits (smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, etc.), the prevalence of chronic medical disorders (CMD) including hypertension, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, and stroke, as well as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Additionally, it assessed social policy and health utilization, encompassing medical insurance, chronic disease treatment, hospitalization, and emergency visits. The respondents self-reported whether they had been diagnosed by a doctor with conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, stroke, or ischemic heart disease.

In this study, the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) was employed to evaluate the depressive symptoms of the respondents, reflecting their mental and psychological states over the past two weeks. A total score of 27 was possible, and participants with a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10 were classified as exhibiting depressive symptoms [47, 48].

In this study, the EQ-5D-5 L scale was used to evaluate the health status of the respondents, and the health utility value was converted through the integral conversion system studied by Guo et al. [49]. The formula for the health utility value is (U = 1-MOn-SCn-UAn-PDn-ADn) [50], where n represents different levels (values 1–5) and the health utility value ranges from − 0.391 ~ 1. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) is a quantitative description of the respondent’s overall perception of their health (0-100 points), with higher scores representing better self-rated health.

Through the questionnaire survey, respondents were asked about their regular participation in physical exercise, the typical methods of exercise (each session lasting more than 10 min), the frequency of exercise per week, and the duration of each exercise session. To ensure comparability of physical activity levels among individuals, the intensity of daily physical activity was uniformly quantified using the Metabolic Equivalent (MET) [51]. It is calculated as MET = mean metabolic equivalent of physical activity × number of exercises per week × number of hours of exercise per week/7, and the average metabolic equivalent of Light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity being 3.3, 4.0, and 8.0, respectively [52]. Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R4.4.2 language, STATA18.0. Statistical description: For data that conform to normal distribution, it is represented by ±s; Non-normally distributed data were expressed as medians and quartiles, while categorical and hierarchical data were represented by rates and composition ratios. Comparisons between groups were conducted using t-tests, chi-square tests, or rank sum tests. Predictor screening was performed using the ‘geepack’ and ‘glmnet’ toolkits in R version 4.4.2. The panel model GEE and GLMM were employed to construct the longitudinal data. The GEE model was constructed using the ‘geepack’ toolkit in R Studio, whereas GLMM modeling was implemented with the ‘glmm’ toolkit in R Studio. The model fitting effects were evaluated using AIC, BIC, CIC, and QIC indicators. Finally, performance evaluation metrics such as recall, specificity, and the area under the ROC curve were utilized to compare the model effects in R Studio. A two-sided test with P < 0.05 was applied for statistical analysis.

Missing data

In this study, some variables had less than 25% of their data missing, necessitating the treatment of these missing values. Directly eliminating observers with missing values would result in information loss, adversely affecting the final analysis results. Therefore, this study employs multiple imputation to address the missing values in the database, following the exclusion of missing data from the outcome variables. This approach ensures the accuracy of the research results and mitigates the selection bias that could arise from only including complete observations, thereby enhancing statistical efficiency.

Results

Basic information of the research population

The study cohort comprised 1,648 participants with a mean age of 71.11 ± 7.44 years. Over the follow-up periods, the cumulative incidence of cardiometabolic disease (CMD) with depression was 44.30% (730/1,648) at 2 years (incidence density: 221.48 per 1,000 person-years) and 68.93% (1,136/1,648) at 8 years (incidence density: 86.17 per 1,000 person-years). The increased incidence at 8 years reflects the progressive nature of CMD-depression multimorbidity over time. In this study, participants were stratified into non-multimorbid (single CMD at baseline, n = 1,216) and multimorbid (CMD multimorbidity [CMM] at baseline, n = 432) groups. Significant baseline differences (P < 0.05) were observed between the groups: a higher prevalence of females in the comorbid group (68.29% vs. 58.88%), lower MET scores (6.38 vs. 5.15), and poorer healthcare accessibility (distance/time) in the multimorbid group, as well as higher emergency visits (2.04 vs. 1.84), lower health utility values (0.79 vs. 0.82), and greater chronic disease treatment needs (27.08% vs. 16.69%) in the multimorbid group. These findings suggest that there are significant differences in various risk factors between the multimorbid and non-multimorbid groups, indicating that the risk prediction model should differentiate between the presence of cardiometabolic comorbidity (Table 2). Furthermore, the differences in baseline risk factors between cardiometabolic multimorbidity and non-multimorbidity suggest the necessity for separate construction of risk prediction models.

Construction of a prediction model for cardiometabolic disease with depression

Variable filtering

This study utilizes predictor screening using an adaptive LASSO regression model and a multivariate GEE model for screening. Adaptive LASSO adjusts the penalty terms in LASSO regression by introducing weights, so as to achieve different penalties for different variables, thereby improving the accuracy and interpretability of the prediction model. In this study, the adaptive LASSO regression analysis was carried out based on the R language “glmnet” package, and the minimum λ parameter value was selected by 5-fold cross-validation, and the variables screened by the adaptive LASSO were finally determined, and then the 16 variables screened were included in the multivariate GEE model (Fig. 1, Supplemental Tables 1, and Supplemental Table 2 for details) for analysis.Fig. 1Screening predictors using an adaptive LASSO regression model

Screening predictors using an adaptive LASSO regression model

GEE model estimated

The logistic regression function was used to construct the model, and the variables screened out were analyzed based on multivariate GEE and the interaction with time was considered, and the GEE equation was used to further analyze the data. Discussion

Current status of cardiometabolic diseases with depression in a division of Xinjiang production and construction corps

In this study, the cumulative incidence of depression in patients with CMD reached 44.30% and 68.93% at 2-year and 8-year follow-up, respectively, which was significantly higher than that of the general population (24.7%−43.82%) [53, 54] and the elderly population (8.5%−50%) [55].This difference may be related to the special environment, insufficient social support and limited medical resources of a division of the Corps exacerbate mental health risks and also be related to the overall age of the study population. In addition, the incidence of depression in the comorbid group (CMM) was higher than that in the non-comorbid group (67.02%), consistent with previous studies [56–59], demonstrating the dose-response effect of increased CMD on the risk of depression (OR = 1.12) [20], and this modest association may underestimate the risk due to unmeasured confounders (e.g., socioeconomic status, medication adherence). The incidence rate in females (70.52%) was higher than that in males (66.41%), which was consistent with gender-specific factors such as hormonal fluctuations and social role stress [60–62].

Longitudinal predictors of cardiometabolic disease with depression

This study analyzed the longitudinal predictors of cardiometabolic disease with depressive psychosomatic comorbidity using the Generalized Estimation Equation (GEE) model in conjunction with adaptive LASSO regression. The predictors were classified into four categories: sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical service utilization, and psychosocial factors.

Age and gender, as immutable demographic factors, are critical characteristics of the population, and the analysis results align with conclusions from previous studies. Notably, older women exhibited the highest risk, underscoring the need for tailored interventions, such as integrating mental health services into menopause care [63].

At the level of lifestyle factors, smoking and low metabolic equivalents (MET) have been identified as significant risk factors for cardiometabolic diseases associated with depressive psychosomatic multimorbidity. Conversely, regular exercise has been shown to exert a protective effect, aligning with findings from prior studies [64, 65]. Consequently, community-based interventions, such as group exercise and smoking cessation programs, may effectively mitigate these risks.

Studies have found a paradoxical correlation between frequent physical exams and an increased risk of depression, potentially due to health anxiety. It is suggested that primary care providers receive training to address psychological distress during routine visits.

Among the psychosocial factors, suburban living, poor self-rated health, and low health utility were found to significantly elevate the risk of depression. These findings are consistent with those of our earlier study [66] and the research conducted by Gray et al. [67] on Alabama residents. A decline in quality of life may serve as an early indicator of psychosomatic multimorbidity. Therefore, expanding mental health services in rural areas and subsidizing chronic disease treatments to alleviate financial strain could enhance both the physical and mental health of patients.

Comparison of GEE vs. GLMM models

Model prediction ability

In this study, the generalized estimation equation (GEE) and the generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) were used to predict the cardiometabolic disease with depression in the total population, the baseline non-multimorbid population, and the baseline multimorbid population. The GLMM model outperformed GEE in the total population and multimorbid groups (precision: 0.852 vs. 0.824), and GLMM was able to better handle random effects between individuals, thereby improving the accuracy of prediction, which was inconsistent with the results of Guo Jing’s study using clinical longitudinal data [68]; in the non-multimorbid group, the GEE model was better (AUC: 0.807). The difference probably because GEE captured time-dependent effects (e.g., treatment delays), while GLMM identified stable individual-level predictors, thus GEE could make more efficient use of longitudinal data by assuming intra-population correlations and avoid unnecessary random effects in GLMM.

Predictive power of variables

Both models identified significant effects of CMD number, BMI, MET, health utility value, self-rated health status, age, gender, and community type on depression risk. However, GEE further revealed the key role of time interaction, in which the effects of CMD number, SBP, frequency of physical examinations, and number of emergency visits can be reduced with longer follow-up time, which may reflect the treatment delay effect, that is, the expected effect is not immediately produced after treatment or chronic disease management, but gradually emerges after a period of accumulation or physiological (metabolism, absorption, and distribution of drugs in the body)/psychological adaptation [69, 70]; however, MET, health utility value, and self-rated health status gradually increased the protective effect on outcomes over time, which was related to the accumulation of health status and the time effect. Individuals who maintain long-term exercise, positive psychological state, and social support gradually improve their physical and mental resilience and continue to reduce the risk of depression [71, 72]. In addition, a positive attitude towards life and good social support are conducive to better coping with the stress and disease burden of life in older adults [73].

Although GLMM does not directly model the temporal interaction term, its random slope can partially reflect the individual change trend. For example, the effect of MET in the comorbid group was more significant in GLMM (β = −0.21, p < 0.01), suggesting that the long-term benefit of exercise interventions for high-risk individuals may be better than that of the general population.

Subgroup analysis

Patients with cardiometabolic multimorbidity exhibit a higher risk of depression and greater disease complexity compared to those with a single cardiometabolic condition. Furthermore, the management strategies implemented by primary medical institutions for these two patient categories should differ accordingly. Consequently, this study models the two patient groups separately.

In this paper, we analyze non-multimorbid and multimorbid patients independently and find that the predictive performance of each model surpasses that of the combined population. This improvement may be attributed to the reduction of heterogeneity within each subgroup, allowing the model to more accurately capture the true associations pertinent to specific groups. The findings indicate that the risk of depression in the non-multimorbid group was not significantly influenced by age, community type, chronic disease treatment, number of hospitalizations, number of emergency department visits, or their interactions over time. This lack of significance may be due to the relatively younger demographic of the non-multimorbid group, the more uniform distribution of individuals across community types, and their favorable health status, which may obscure the impact of these variables on psychosomatic multimorbidity. Given the limited sample size in the multimorbid group, we employed a preliminary variable removal method to construct a model that minimizes rank loss. The Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) demonstrated superior performance in the multimorbid group; however, only metabolic equivalents, self-rated health status, gender, and community type significantly influenced the outcomes. Implications

Optimize the allocation of medical resources and service capabilities

In the development of public health policies and the management of chronic diseases, hierarchical management is key, that is, differentiated interventions for non-multimorbid and multimorbid patients. For patients with single CMD conditions, we should prioritize smoking cessation programs and physical activity promotion initiatives. For multimorbid patients, particularly elderly women and suburban residents, routine mental health screenings should be systematically integrated into standard care protocols. This requires strengthening primary care capacity through enhanced training for general practitioners in psychosomatic medicine while optimizing referral pathways to prevent treatment delays. The expansion of “Internet + mental health” services, including tele-counseling platforms, can significantly improve access for remote populations.

Promote lifestyle intervention and multi-level linkage

Community-level interventions should focus on creating health-promoting environments. Practical measures include establishing smoke-free public spaces with dedicated cessation hotlines, developing “15-minute walkable neighborhood” concepts, and organizing diverse fitness activities. These efforts demand close collaboration between health authorities and urban planning/environmental departments to design communities that inherently support physical and mental wellbeing through green spaces, pedestrian-friendly infrastructure, and accessible recreational facilities.

Improve data-driven precision interventions

For precision intervention, we recommend employing the GLMM model to identify high-risk individuals for targeted programs, while utilizing the GEE model to dynamically monitor risk factor evolution in non-multimorbid patients. Complementing this, we should implement robust long-term follow-up mechanisms featuring electronic mental health records that enable regular depression assessments and timely care plan adjustments.

Cross-departmental collaboration and policy guarantees

Policy frameworks should adopt a “One Community, One Policy” approach to ensure equitable resource allocation based on local socioeconomic and health profiles. This requires cross-sectoral coordination to embed health considerations into urban development plans - for instance, through strategic placement of healthcare facilities alongside recreational centers to address social determinants of health.

Limitation

The sample size and follow-up frequency of this study were small, and the trend between more real variables and outcomes may be ignored;The geographical representation of the study data sample is limited, which may not fully reflect the incidence and risk factors of cardiometabolic disease with depression in other regions or broader populations, and the extrapolation is limited, which can be externally validated in other populations in follow-up studies;The measurement methods we used about self-rated health and self-reported lifestyle factors may lead to some potential measurement bias;This study did not include the data related to laboratory tests and other auxiliary examinations available in primary public health services, which may lead to a certain degree of bias in the prediction results of the prediction model, suggesting future research with larger, more diverse samples and objective biomarkers.

Conclusion

This study reveals a significantly elevated burden of depression among elderly patients with cardiometabolic diseases (CMD with depression) in XPCC communities, with an overall incidence of 68.93% — substantially higher than national averages. Notably, the risk escalates with disease complexity, as demonstrated by the 74.30% incidence in patients with baseline cardiometabolic multimorbidity compared to 67.02% in non-multimorbid individuals. These findings underscore an urgent public health priority, particularly given the region’s unique challenges of geographic isolation and limited healthcare access.

Key predictors identified include cardiometabolic disease burden, BMI, physical activity levels (metabolic equivalents), health utility values, self-rated health status, age, gender, and community type. For clinical practice, we recommend: (1) implementing stratified screening using the GLMM model, which showed superior performance in detecting depressive symptoms among multimorbid patients, and (2) prioritizing high-risk subgroups (e.g., elderly women, suburban residents, and multimorbid patients) for integrated mental-physical healthcare interventions.

These results highlight the critical need for targeted policies that address both medical and social determinants of health in XPCC communities, including strengthened primary care capacity and community-based mental health programs. Our study provides an evidence base for developing precision interventions to reduce the dual burden of cardiometabolic and mental health disorders in this vulnerable population.
