Perceptions of the Benefits and Risks of Novel Therapies for Type 1 Diabetes: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Stephen R. Karpen, Richard Mellor, Linda A. DiMeglio, Peter Senior, Jennifer L. Sherr, Cooper Bussberg, Carol Mansfield, Marjana Marinac, Kelley Myers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNovel therapies, including disease-modifying and cell replacement therapies, may preserve or replace beta cells in people with type 1 diabetes. This study sought to understand how people living with type 1 diabetes or caring for someone with type 1 diabetes perceive the benefits and risks of novel therapies.MethodsSemistructured qualitative interviews were conducted with 26 participants in the United States: four adolescents and 12 adults with type 1 diabetes, and 10 caregivers of children with type 1 diabetes. A description of the benefits and risks of disease-modifying and cell replacement therapies, developed with a steering committee of patients and clinicians, was presented during interviews to facilitate discussion among people living with type 1 diabetes and caregivers. A qualitative directed content analysis was conducted.ResultsParticipants reported that type 1 diabetes and insulin therapy regimens impacted many life areas, with some participants reporting diabetes burnout. Most participants expressed that they would have considered trying disease-modifying therapies, most frequently citing perceived benefits such as reduced insulin reliance and an extended post-diagnosis “honeymoon period” providing time to prepare for life with diabetes. Cancer risk was the most frequently reported risk of concern for disease-modifying therapies. All participants expressed willingness to consider cell replacement therapies, with insulin independence and restored pancreatic function perceived to offer greater normalcy and freedom from the constant demands of diabetes. Participants reported concerns about the use of immunosuppressants and the risks and drawbacks of the cell replacement surgical procedure.ConclusionsDespite concerns about the risks and drawbacks of novel therapies, most participants reported that they would consider trying disease-modifying and cell replacement therapies. There is no substitute for consulting people living with or caring for someone with type 1 diabetes when considering new therapies with novel risks and benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01783-y.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes results in a considerable burden for people living with the condition and for their caregivers [1–4]. Daily exogenous insulin administration and glucose monitoring substantially impact psychological well-being, diet, sleep, work productivity, and interpersonal relationships [5–7]. Diabetes burnout—defined by exhaustion, detachment, and loss of control associated with the demands of living with type 1 diabetes—is a significant issue faced by those living with and caring for people with this complex and chronic medical condition [8, 9]. Furthermore, despite pharmacologic advances in insulin therapy and technological breakthroughs such as continuous glucose monitoring and automated insulin delivery systems [10, 11], most people with type 1 diabetes are unable to achieve glycemic targets [12]. This results in substantial residual risk for acute and chronic complications and reduced life expectancy [13, 14].

Recent technological advances and an increased understanding of the pathogenesis of type 1 diabetes have led to the development of novel therapeutic approaches [15]. Clinical work is underway to develop disease-modifying and cell replacement therapies that have the potential to preserve existing beta cells or replace beta cells that have been destroyed in people with type 1 diabetes, thus reducing or eliminating the need for exogenous insulin administration and glucose monitoring while also improving glycemia [16, 17].

The three stages of type 1 diabetes include stage 1 (the presence of two or more islet autoantibodies), stage 2 (the presence of two or more islet autoantibodies and dysglycemia), and stage 3 (the presence of hyperglycemia) [18]. Disease-modifying therapies are designed to preserve beta cell function by modulating the underlying autoimmune process for individuals with stage 1 or 2 or recently diagnosed stage 3 type 1 diabetes [19]. In November 2022, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the first disease-modifying therapy for those with stage 2 type 1 diabetes, teplizumab (Tzield®) [20]. Teplizumab deactivates a subset of immune cells that attack insulin-producing cells, thereby delaying the time to onset of stage 3 type 1 diabetes [21]. Teplizumab is associated with an increased risk of cytokine release syndrome, serious infection, lymphopenia, and other adverse reactions such as rash and headaches [21]. Furthermore, as a general class of therapies across different diseases and conditions, chronic use of immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory agents has been associated with an increased risk of cancer, which may be a relevant concern when used in type 1 diabetes [22].

Cell replacement therapies are designed to restore functioning beta cells in people with type 1 diabetes who have little or no remaining beta cell function [23]. The infused islet cells from cell replacement therapies, through the action of beta cells, can produce enough insulin to mitigate the need to take exogenous insulin, at least for a period of time, and can provide insulin independence while eliminating hypoglycemia [23, 24]. In June 2023, the FDA approved the first allogeneic (deceased donor) pancreatic islet cell replacement therapy for the treatment of adults with type 1 diabetes, donislecel (Lantidra®) [25]. Donislecel involves a single infusion of allogeneic islet beta cells into the liver, which may be repeated if the initial dose is inadequate [25]. Substantial ongoing research also aims to develop islet cell therapies that overcome key challenges of deceased donor islet cell transplantation, including cell supply limitations and the need for immunosuppression. Substantial ongoing research aims to overcome these challenges by leveraging alternative islet cell sources (e.g., pluripotent stem cells or porcine cells) and employing various strategies for immune protection (e.g., cell encapsulation or gene editing) [26]. Multiple phase 1 or phase 1/2 clinical trials of these products are underway, with one product (Zimislecel, an allogeneic stem cell-derived islet cell therapy requiring immunosuppression) having begun a phase 1/2/3 pivotal study [27, 28].

While research into novel therapies for type 1 diabetes has primarily focused on glycemic outcomes [19, 21, 23, 25], in this study, we sought to understand the perception of novel therapies from the perspective of individuals living with type 1 diabetes and caregivers of children with type 1 diabetes. Understanding patients’ and caregivers’ perspectives on potential new treatments, particularly their perceptions of treatment benefits and risks, is of utmost importance for informing medical product development and regulatory decision-making [29]. The specific objectives of this study were to understand the following among people with type 1 diabetes and caregivers of children with type 1 diabetes: (1) experiences with insulin therapy, including impacts on quality of life, unmet needs, and treatment burden; (2) perceptions of the potential benefits and risks of novel therapies and the likelihood of trying these treatments; and (3) the willingness to accept certain risks associated with novel therapies in order to achieve certain benefits.

Methods

Sample

Semistructured interviews were conducted with adolescents and adults with type 1 diabetes and caregivers of children with type 1 diabetes who reside in the United States. Eligible participants had a self-reported diagnosis of type 1 diabetes and were aged 15–17 years (adolescents) or 18 years or older (adults). Eligible caregivers were 18 years or older and were a self-reported parent, guardian, or primary caregiver living full-time with a child (aged younger than 18 years) with type 1 diabetes. Sampling criteria were set to identify a mix of adult and adolescent participants who were newly diagnosed (diagnosed less than 2 years ago) and those with long-standing type 1 diabetes (diagnosed more than 5 years ago). For those diagnosed more than 5 years ago, sampling criteria were set to identify a subset of participants either satisfied with how often they are able to meet glycemic targets (defined by a self-reported satisfaction score of 4 or above on a scale of 1–5) and/or were currently meeting their glycemic target (defined by being in range more than half of the day, on an average day, over the past week or their most recent hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] being at or below their self-defined target HbA1c).

Interview participants were identified and screened by L&E Research, a market research firm specializing in healthcare. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. The study was approved by RTI International’s institutional review board (approval no. 22757). All study participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and additionally, for adolescent interviews, verbal parental consent and adolescent assent were obtained prior to participation. All participants were informed that participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw from the study at any time, and that the interviews would be audio recorded and transcribed. Additionally, all participants were informed that if the study findings were presented at scientific meetings or published in journals, no information would be included that could identify them or their answers personally.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from participants before each adult interview, and verbal parental consent and adolescent assent were obtained before each adolescent interview.

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted by two experienced interviewers, using semistructured interview guides that were tailored to each population (people with type 1 diabetes and caregivers). Prior to the interview, a document describing each of the novel therapies was sent to participants. Participants were told that disease-modifying therapies are intended for people recently diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, while cell replacement therapies are for people with long-standing disease (Supplemental Figures S1 and S2). A steering committee of patient representatives and clinician-researchers who work directly with individuals with type 1 diabetes and their families provided direct input on the content of the interview guides, including the treatment descriptions.

To better understand the impact of having type 1 diabetes, participants were first asked questions about the impacts of diabetes on their life (or their child’s life) and their experiences managing glucose as well as their (or their child’s) daily insulin routines. These questions were followed by the interviewer and participant reviewing the descriptions of disease-modifying and cell replacement therapies that had been sent in advance to the participants. Participants were then asked questions about their perceptions of the benefits and risks of each therapy.

Analysis

A directed content analysis was conducted [30, 31]. A formative categorization matrix of deductive and inductive codes was iteratively developed throughout the analysis process [31]. In accordance with guidelines for inter-coder reliability assessment [32], a subset of transcripts was independently double-coded; Cohen’s kappa estimates for each code included in the final coding matrix ranged between 0.88 and 1.00, indicating acceptable reliability [32]. Once agreement on the coding matrix was established, all interview transcripts were independently coded in full. The qualitative data management software ATLAS.ti (version 9) was used to facilitate the coding process.

Results

The final sample comprised 26 participants: 12 adults and four adolescents diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, and ten caregivers of children diagnosed with type 1 diabetes. The interviews were completed between March 4 and April 2, 2024. The duration of the interviews ranged between 37 and 81 min (mean: 53 min). In the reporting of results in this article, verbatim quotations are presented with an interview number and an abbreviation to denote participant type: adolescent (ADO), adult (ADU), or caregiver (CAR).

The Impacts of Type 1 Diabetes on Daily Life

Participants reported that multiple aspects of life were impacted by the disease itself and by the daily glucose management that exogenous insulin therapy requires. Impacts to diet, physical activity, and sleep were the most frequently reported life domains impacted by type 1 diabetes (Table 3). Half of the participants (n = 13; 50.0%) reported impacts to their diets, which centered around the demands of food calculation (such as counting carbohydrates and calories) and food planning (such as having a strict eating schedule); participants also reported challenges with eating at social events where dietary information was not readily available. Approximately half of the participants (n = 12; 46.2%) reported impacts to their physical activity, which centered on possible changes to glucose levels that required vigilant glucose monitoring, or actual changes to glucose levels that required having to pause or stop physical activity. Beyond the impacts of diabetes on individual life domains, participants also described diabetes burnout, which arises from the constant demands of type 1 diabetes on daily life. Participants who reported this impact described the wide reach and omnipresence of type 1 diabetes, a condition that is “always there”:“Diabetes is always there. No matter what I do, no matter what I say, no matter how I exercise, no matter what, it’s always there. It’s always right there so that’s a pain in the butt.” (ADU3)

Due to the constant demands and wide-reaching impacts of diabetes on daily life, approximately one-quarter of participants (n = 7; 26.9%) reported diabetes burnout, characterized as being tired, exhausted, overwhelmed, and detached, or “just done with everything” (ADU4).

Perceptions of Disease-Modifying Therapies

Disease-modifying therapies were explained to participants as a treatment option that could extend the “honeymoon period” or period of time after diagnosis, when the body still has healthy insulin-producing cells. Most participants (n = 22; 84.6%) reported that they would have considered trying disease-modifying therapies at the time of diagnosis. This includes all adolescent participants (n = 4; 100.0%), almost all adult participants (n = 11; 91.7%), and most caregivers (n = 7; 70.0%).

The most frequently reported reasons why participants perceived disease-modifying therapies as beneficial were that they would result in less reliance on exogenous insulin and that they would allow them to have more time to prepare and adjust to life with type 1 diabetes after diagnosis.

Approximately three-quarters of participants (n = 18; 69.2%) reported that less reliance on exogenous insulin would be a benefit of disease-modifying therapies (Table 3). Participants noted that less reliance on daily insulin would be valuable as it would reduce the out-of-pocket expenses associated with insulin and would decrease the time spent on managing daily insulin administration. Participants also mentioned the negative effects of exogenous insulin, which centered around two themes: the negative impact of exogenous insulin on appetite and the unpredictable impacts of exogenous insulin to glucose levels. As explained by two adult participants, exogenous insulin had a negative impact on appetite, which led to a cycle of increased insulin and hunger:“The more insulin you take, it seems like the more your body would want more food. And the more food you have, the more insulin you need to take in. So, I could see that [taking less exogenous insulin] being really beneficial.” (ADU8)“There are challenges when you’re taking more insulin. I’ve found it can make you feel more hungry, which then you’re now fighting a cycle, so the less insulin you need, you’re just going to see a lot more positive impact in terms of lifestyle choice.” (ADU6)

“The more insulin you take, it seems like the more your body would want more food. And the more food you have, the more insulin you need to take in. So, I could see that [taking less exogenous insulin] being really beneficial.” (ADU8)

Participants also contrasted exogenous insulin’s unpredictable and uncertain effects on glucose levels with the accuracy and certainty of endogenous insulin production. Participants valued less reliance on “synthetic insulin” (CAR2), as they preferred instead “the body making its own insulin” (CAR2). As noted by one caregiver:“I think that anytime your body can respond to what you’re eating instead of relying on additional insulin or the intervention, outside intervention, then that’s going to be much more desirable. Yeah. I don’t think that there is a diabetes protocol that works as well as a working pancreas.” (CAR1)

“I think that anytime your body can respond to what you’re eating instead of relying on additional insulin or the intervention, outside intervention, then that’s going to be much more desirable. Yeah. I don’t think that there is a diabetes protocol that works as well as a working pancreas.” (CAR1)

Just under half of the participants (n = 11; 42.3%) reported that extending the honeymoon period or delaying diagnosis would be beneficial, as it would have helped them prepare for life with type 1 diabetes. Participants who reported this benefit explained that the time of diagnosis was distressing and a “big shock” (ADU6); they also reported that the attendant lifestyle changes were drastic, noting that they felt like they were “jumping right into it” (ADO1) and “going headfirst” (ADO2). In this context, having more time to prepare for life with diabetes was reported to provide more time for education (“just for the prep time, a little bit more time to educate myself” [ADU7]) and skill building (“it would’ve given me more time to learn how to give myself shots and prick my finger and make the calculations on how much insulin I need to take” [ADO1]).

On the other hand, some participants (n = 7; 26.9%) reported that extending the honeymoon period would delay the inevitable and prolong uncertainty. In terms of delaying the inevitable, one participant stated, “if you’re only buying time, I don’t know that the trade is worth it. Eventually you’re going to end up having diabetes” (CAR1). In terms of prolonging uncertainty, participants mentioned that the honeymoon period was marked by “constant tweaking” (CAR6), during which the insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio was a “moving target” (CAR1); it was like “invisible juggling balls, like we don’t know how many islet cells he’s got left” (CAR7). One caregiver said, “I would almost rather it be steady, whatever that amount is, and have a constant flow of something” (CAR6). For 3 caregiver participants (CAR 1, 7, 9), these perceptions resulted in hesitation or unwillingness to consider trying disease-modifying therapy at the time of diagnosis.

Overall, except for cancer, the risks associated with disease-modifying therapies did not frequently concern participants (Table 3). Half of the participants reported the risk of cancer associated with disease-modifying therapies to be a concern (n = 13; 50.0%). The notion of cancer intrinsically evoked concern as “the big C word” (CAR3); it was a risk that “stands out” (ADO4) and is “scary” (ADU9) and that immediately caused “fear” (CAR2) for participants.

Perceptions of Cell Replacement Therapies

Cell replacement therapies were explained to participants as therapies that are designed to place healthy insulin-producing cells into the body, with most people treated with the therapy becoming insulin independent, which can translate to freedom from hypoglycemia and more stable glucose levels. The description immediately elicited an emotionally positive response among participants: “It’s amazing. And to be someone that would get that procedure, I would feel I was an astronaut or something” (ADU4); “Too good to be true” (ADU9); “That sounds like a beautiful thing” (ADO3); “This sounds amazing” (CAR1); “First of all, that sounds almost like a cure” (CAR2). All participants (n = 26; 100.0%) reported that they would consider trying cell replacement therapy, and the perceived benefits of cell replacement treatment as presented outweighed any concerns about the perceived risks and drawbacks of the procedure and treatment.

The benefits of cell replacement therapy centered on two thematic categories: freedom from the demands of diabetes and an increased sense of normalcy. Half of the participants (n = 13; 50.0%) reported that insulin independence would offer freedom from the constant demands of type 1 diabetes. This theme not only reflects the benefits of insulin independence to individual life domains such as diet, physical activity, and sleep but also illustrates how insulin independence was perceived by participants to be uniquely beneficial because it offered overall freedom from the wide-reaching demands of diabetes on daily life. This is illustrated in the following quotes:“I think what people don’t understand is that how the percentage of the day that is spent making decisions based on diabetes. Right. And it is not just three times a day when you eat. It is every…we get a new Dexcom number every 5 minutes. It’s constantly changing and we’re constantly watching it. And so just everything, absolutely every decision all day long is made for her and for me. And so, this just sounds like freedom.” (CAR1)“I can eliminate the pump, I could eliminate the insulin every day, I could eliminate the worry. I would still have it, I would still know I have type 1 in my head, but now, it’s not a full thought from the moment I wake up, lunchtime, dinnertime, it’s just there.” (ADU3)

Similarly, one caregiver mentioned that in their role as a caregiver, they essentially “have to be the pancreas,” but that cell replacement therapy would offer a way for their child to get “back to normal” (CAR7). One adolescent patient said it would be “really good” to “just [get] rid of diabetes and [have] a normal pancreas again” (ADO4).

The reported benefits of freedom and normalcy were observed to be unique to cell replacement therapy, which could explain why the descriptions of cell replacement therapy immediately elicited an emotionally positive response among participants. Moreover, while it was explained to participants that cell replacement therapies would likely provide benefit for up to 3 years but could potentially produce benefits for up to 20 years, participants reported that even a few years of benefit would provide a sense of freedom or normalcy: “If it can cure for at least a few years that would be great” (ADO4); “If there’s an option to be become insulin independent for even 3 years, that’s a little bit more freedom” (CAR8); “To feel normal, even if it’s for 3 years would be outrageously awesome” (ADU4).

In terms of the risks and potential drawbacks of cell replacement therapies, participants reported being concerned about the use of immunosuppressants (n = 11; 42.3%) and about life-threatening complications related to bleeding (n = 9; 34.6%). The possibility that the body would reject the cells was also reported as concerning by participants (n = 10; 38.5%); undergoing the procedure without achieving benefit was suggested to be “very disappointing” (CAR2) and to provoke the feeling that it “would kind of be for nothing” (ADO4) or “we did this for nothing” (CAR5). While the surgical procedure associated with cell replacement therapy was considered concerning for some participants (n = 10; 38.5%), others felt that the procedure itself and the risks associated with cell replacement therapy were “standard” and thus accepted the risks: “all of those are just standard surgical potential risks” (ADU6); “I feel like it’s pretty standard for a surgery or transplant type thing” (CAR10); “that sounds like the spiel you get with every surgery” (ADU10).

Discussion

This qualitative study aimed to understand the recent experiences that people living with or caring for someone with type 1 diabetes have had with standard therapeutic approaches, and to explore participants’ perceptions of novel therapies that have the potential to preserve existing or replace destroyed beta cells in individuals with type 1 diabetes. Participants reported that multiple aspects of life—mostly including diet, physical activity, and sleep—were recently impacted by type 1 diabetes and the daily requirements associated with insulin therapy. Recent experience of diabetes burnout [8, 9], defined as exhaustion and detachment associated with the demands and struggles of living with type 1 diabetes, was reported by 27% of study participants.

As reported by participants in this study, the main benefit of the novel therapies was a reduction or elimination of daily exogenous insulin requirements. Reduced reliance on insulin in the context of disease-modifying therapies was valued by participants, as it would reduce the time spent managing daily insulin administration while also offsetting the negative effects of and uncertainty around exogenous insulin’s impact on glucose levels. Insulin independence in the context of cell replacement therapy, even if limited to a few years, was highly valued, as it offered freedom from the constant demands of type 1 diabetes. These findings suggest that addressing insulin requirements is of utmost importance to those most affected by the development of novel therapies for type 1 diabetes, thus supporting the inclusion of outcomes related to reducing or eliminating insulin dependence in clinical research into novel therapies.

Many participants in this study valued extending the honeymoon period using disease-modifying therapies, because doing so would provide more time to prepare for life with diabetes. Processing the disruptions at the time of diagnosis is a key step when adapting to life with type 1 diabetes [33]; our study has demonstrated that disease-modifying therapies have the potential to facilitate the early process of adaptation by easing the burden of insulin therapy after diagnosis. However, some participants saw no benefit in extending the honeymoon period, as this would only delay the inevitable and prolong uncertainty. This has implications for clinical practice, as some people living with type 1 diabetes and their caregivers may have views that differ from those of their healthcare providers with respect to the importance of extending the honeymoon period after diagnosis. This research has further implications for medical drug development and regulatory decision-making [29]. Despite being concerned with the risks and drawbacks of novel therapies, most participants (n = 22; 84.6%) reported that they would consider trying disease-modifying therapies, and all participants (n = 26; 100.0%) reported that they would consider trying cell replacement therapies; these data indicate that the benefits of the novel therapies, as presented and explained to participants in this study, could potentially outweigh the perceived risks.

This study has several limitations. The sample size within each participant group were small and therefore the study does not address possible similarities and differences in treatment perceptions between groups of participants. However, the overall sample size is consistent with sample sizes from other qualitative research evaluating patient and caregiver perspectives in type 1 diabetes [34] and thus has yielded meaningful insights about all groups’ perspectives and preferences. The small sample size also limits the generalizability of the findings. However, in qualitative research, where representative sampling is typically not reached or expected, diverse sampling helps ensure that the phenomenon of interest is adequately captured [35]. The findings of this qualitative study are based on interview data from a diverse sample that included adolescents, adults, and caregivers, as well as a range of newly diagnosed participants and those with long-standing diagnoses, including individuals who meet and do not meet their glycemic targets. This diverse sampling has helped ensure that the most important perceptions related to novel therapies have been identified and explored in this qualitative study. Furthermore, although participants in this study indicated a willingness to consider trying novel therapies, the study did not identify participants’ exact risk thresholds. To further understand risk thresholds, as well as whether treatment preferences vary between subgroups, an upcoming quantitative stated-preference survey is planned, which will be informed by these qualitative findings and will aim to quantify the risk–benefit tradeoffs of individuals living with or caring for someone with type 1 diabetes.

Conclusions

Reducing or eliminating daily exogenous insulin was considered a key benefit of novel therapies from the perspective of people living with or caring for someone with type 1 diabetes, as this would reduce the time spent managing daily insulin administration, offset the negative effects of insulin therapy and uncertainty around exogenous insulin’s impact on glucose levels, and offer freedom from the constant demands of type 1 diabetes, including the need for frequent decision-making. Overall, participants reported a strong desire for novel therapies beyond insulin, despite the potential risk of side effects and procedural complications. When considering and developing new therapies, it is essential to consult people living with type 1 diabetes and caring for someone with type 1 diabetes. Understanding their perspectives on potential new treatments, particularly their perceptions of treatment benefits and risks, is of utmost importance to inform medical product development and regulatory decision-making.
