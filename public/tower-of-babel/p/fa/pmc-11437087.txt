Cryo-EM structure of human class C orphan GPCR GPR179 involved in visual processing
Authors: Yaejin Yun, Hyeongseop Jeong, Thibaut Laboute, Kirill A Martemyanov, Hyung Ho Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGPR179, an orphan class C GPCR, is expressed at the dendritic tips of ON-bipolar cells in the retina. It plays a pivotal role in the initial synaptic transmission of visual signals from photoreceptors, and its deficiency is known to be the cause of complete congenital stationary night blindness. Here, we present the cryo-electron microscopy structure of human GPR179. Notably, the transmembrane domain (TMD) of GPR179 forms a homodimer through the TM1/7 interface with a single inter-protomer disulfide bond, adopting a noncanonical dimerization mode. Furthermore, the TMD dimer exhibits architecture well-suited for the highly curved membrane of the dendritic tip and distinct from the flat membrane arrangement observed in other class C GPCR dimers. Our structure reveals unique structural features of GPR179 TMD, setting it apart from other class C GPCRs. These findings provide a foundation for understanding signal transduction through GPR179 in visual processing and offers insights into the underlying causes of ocular diseases.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, G protein-coupled receptors

Introduction

In the mammalian retina, visual processing begins through parallel vertical pathways that form at the synapse between photoreceptors and bipolar cells (BCs)1 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). In the dark, photoreceptors continuously release glutamate from the synaptic terminals. However, when exposed to light, photoreceptors hyperpolarize, resulting in a decrease in glutamate release2. BCs are classified as either ON- or OFF-bipolar cells3,4, with ON-bipolar cells (ON-BCs) depolarizing in response to increases in light intensity, while OFF-bipolar cells undergo hyperpolarization3. GPR179 is expressed at the dendritic tips of ON-BCs and essential for their depolarizing response5–11. GPR179 plays a crucial role in visual processing, as evidenced by its causal role in complete congenital stationary night blindness (cCSNB), a genetically diverse group of retinal disorders. Additionally, GPR179 mutations have been linked to high myopia, the most prevalent ocular dysfunction that can potentially lead to blindness and other ocular diseases5,7,9,12,13.

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are typically involved in signal transmission through G proteins14 or β-arrestins15,16. However, certain GPCRs can modulate signaling by interacting with regulator of G protein signaling (RGS) proteins8,17–21. GPR179, expressed in ON-bipolar cells, is also involved in transsynaptic signal transduction by binding with RGSs6,8,9 (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In the dark, glutamate released by the photoreceptors binds with mGluR6 to activate G protein Gαo. The activated Gαo then inhibits TRPM122–24. The rapid inactivation of Gαo requires the GTPase activating protein (GAP), RGS6. GPR179 also interacts with the pre-synaptic dystroglycan glycoprotein complex (DGC) through its binding with Pikachurin, an HSPG member secreted from photoreceptors. This interaction facilitates the recruitment of RGS at the post-synaptic specializations of ON-BC dendritic tips6,7,9. The levels of RGS protein accumulation at the dendritic tips of ON-bipolar neurons determine the kinetics and sensitivity of their response to changes in photoreceptor inputs25. As a result, the formation of the DGC-Pikachurin-GPR179-RGS complex appears to regulate the timing of mGluR6 inactivation and the activity of RGS, thereby influencing the speed of the post-synaptic response to light. Despite the functional significance of GPR179, its molecular architecture remains elusive, impeding the molecular-level understanding of visual signal transduction at the synapse between photoreceptors and BCs.

GPR179 is an orphan GPCR belonging to class C. Class C GPCRs are characterized by a large N-terminal extracellular domain (ECD) and a seven-helix transmembrane domain (TMD), and they have the ability to form homo- or heterodimers. Recent advances have revealed the inactive and active structures of various class C GPCRs, including mGluR26–33, GABAB34–39, CaSR40–44, and mGlyR45,46. Notably, GPR179 shares sequence similarity with mGlyR, but it serves distinct functions. GPR179 is selectively expressed in the retina, playing a crucial role in visual processes, while mGlyR is expressed in multiple tissues and particularly prominent in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)47, a region critically involved in depression8. Both GPR179 and mGlyR possess TMD and ECD composed of a Cache domain and a cysteine-rich (CR) domain, distinct from the typical venus flytrap (VFT) domain found in other class C GPCRs45,46. The structures of apo mGlyR and mGlyR-RGS7-Gβ5 complex have been recently elucidated, revealing the organization of the signaling complex18,45,46. While GPR179 also interacts with RGS7, unlike the RGS7-specific binding of mGlyR, GPR179 has been shown to interact with all members of the R7 RGS subfamily8,18,48,49.

Here, we show the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of human GPR179. Surprisingly, GPR179 forms a dimeric arrangement that is completely different from the previously characterized class C GPCRs. This dimeric arrangement of the TMD is distinct from that of the highly homologous mGlyR. Furthermore, the two TMD protomers are covalently linked by a disulfide bond between cysteines in each protomer. Additionally, the TMD exhibits architecture more suitable conformation for a curved membrane of the dendritic tip and different from the flat membrane arrangement observed in previously known GPCR dimers.

Results

Structure determination of human GPR179

For our analysis using single-particle cryo-EM, we employed a biochemically stable construct of human GPR179 by truncating the highly flexible C-terminal loop region (residues 736–2367). We will refer to this construct as GPR179 for simplicity. The initial 3D reconstruction of GPR179 resulted in a 3.6 Å map with C2 symmetry imposition (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). From the consensus map, we successfully resolved a homodimer assembly (50 × 84 × 154 Å3) consisting of a large N-terminal ECD and a seven-helix TMD. The dimerization occurs at the interfaces of both ECD and TMD (Fig. 1). Notably, the TMD exhibits flexibility, as indicated by the observed relatively poor density in the consensus refinement map (Supplementary Fig. 3). To obtain more detailed structural information, we performed local reconstructions of ECD and TMD, resulting in improved maps with resolutions of 3.1 Å for the ECD and 3.5 Å for the TMD, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). In the final composite map, the overall densities of the ECD and TMD are well-resolved, although the local resolution of the TMD is slightly lower compared to the ECD (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). Nonetheless, the density of TM1 and TM7, which constitute the dimer interface, along with other essential features of the TMD, can be clearly distinguished. Additionally, we observed continuous density for TM1 through TM7, encompassing all extracellular loops (ECLs), except for a few portions of the intracellular loops (ICLs). Finally, we successfully constructed the atomic model of the GPR179 homodimer, which consists of an extracellular Cache domain and a TMD linked by a flexible CR domain exhibiting an epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like fold (Fig. 1).

Unprecedented dimerization mode of GPR179 TMD with an inter-protomer disulfide bond

Remarkably, an unexpected dimerization mode of the TMD was observed in GPR179, despite its sequence homology with mGlyR (41%), which belongs to the same class C GPCR family (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). The two protomers of GPR179 TMD displayed a distinct arrangement, forming a dimeric interface through the TM1/7 helices (Fig. 2a, b). The closest distance between the TM1 helices was found to be 5.1 Å, with Cα of A3831.42 (based on the residue numbering of class C GPCRs from Pin50,51) used as a reference. Notably, compared to the distances between the two closest TM helices in other inactive or active states of class C GPCRs (10 Å for mGlyR TM4, 13 Å for the inactive state of GABAB TM5, and 8 Å for the active state of GABAB TM6), the distance of the two closest TM helices (5.1 Å) formed by TM1 in GPR179 was notably short (Supplementary Fig. 8a)26,28,30,32,36,39,40.

Within the TMD dimer interface (a buried surface area of 585 Å2)52, TM1 plays a crucial role in mediating interactions (Fig. 2b). Residues located on the upper part of the TM1 helix (V379, A382, A383, and A386) engage in dimerization through hydrophobic interactions with TM1 of the other protomer. Additionally, a disulfide bond between C390 of chain A and C390 chain B further increases the rigidity of the TMD dimer by stapling two protomers (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4c)53. While C3901.49 is a conserved residue in GPR179 across species, most class C GPCRs exhibit Leu instead of Cys at that position (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Therefore, the C390-C390 disulfide bond appears to be unique to GPR179. The lower part of the TMD dimer interface consists of hydrophobic clusters between TM1 (chain A)-TM7 (chain B) and TM7 (chain A)-TM7 (chain B) (Fig. 2b). The C-tail extending from TM7 bends towards the TMD dimer interface, bringing F631 in close proximity to F631 of the other protomer. Furthermore, I628 of the C-tail is also situated near V401 of TM1 in the other protomer. Overall, GPR179 exhibits an unprecedented dimerization mode of TMD, primarily mediated by TM1 with a disulfide bond, a characteristic that has not been observed in other class C GPCRs.

Comparison of the dimerization mode of GPR179 TMD with other class C GPCRs

To compare the dimeric arrangements of GPR179 TMD with other class C GPCRs, we superimposed one TMD protomer (chain A) of mGlyR, mGluR5, and GABAB dimer onto the TMD protomer (chain A) of GPR179. We then assessed the relative positions of the other protomer (chain B) in order to evaluate the structural differences (Fig. 2c, d). It is known that the TMD chain B in mGlyR rotates counterclockwise by ~90° with respect to that of inactive GABAB45,46. In contrast, the TMD chain B of GPR179 further rotates counterclockwise by ~180° compared to mGlyR (PDB code: 7EWL) (Fig. 2c)46. Furthermore, it is rotated clockwise by ~150° and ~120° when compared to mGluR5 (PDB code: 6N52)26 and GABAB (PDB code: 7C7S)34, respectively. When compared to the active forms of mGluR5 (PDB code: 6N51)26 and GABAB (PDB code: 7C7Q)34, which exhibit the TM6 interface, the TM1/7 interface of GPR179 also displays distinct characteristics. Superimposing the TMD protomer of GPR179 chain A onto those of GABAB and mGluR5 in their active forms reveals that the GPR179 chain B is positioned in a clockwise rotation of ~90° (Fig. 2d). Taken together, the noncanonical binding interface (TM1/7) between the two TMDs in GPR179 results in significantly different relative positions of its protomers compared to other class C GPCRs.

Another noticeable feature of GPR179 TMD dimer is that it forms a curved fan-like shape (Fig. 2a). We reasoned that this configuration of the TMD dimer may fit well with the curved membrane of dendritic tip of ON-BCs, where GPR179 is functionally expressed (Supplementary Fig. 1). To investigate this further, we calculated the radii of intrinsic membrane curvature predicted for GPR179 dimer and compared it with other class C GPCRs including mGlyR, mGluR5, and GABAB (Fig. 2e) by using PPM 3.054,55. Interestingly, GPR179 demonstrated a significantly smaller radius (80 Å) compared to mGlyR (210 Å) and mGluR5 (360 Å), indicating that the GPR179 dimer is better suited for a more curved membrane. Conversely, GABAB exhibited a structure suitable for a nearly flat membrane without curvature. Overall, the extraordinary, curved shape of GPR179 TMD dimer might fit well with the curved membrane on the dendritic tip of ON-BCs56,57.

Structural dissection of the TMD protomer

Next, we explored the structural characteristics of the TMD protomer that contribute to the notable curved fan-like shape observed in the GPR179 TMD dimer by conducting a detailed analysis of its structural features. The positions and lengths of each helix within the TMD appeared to be intricately adjusted to facilitate the formation of the curved TMD dimer. Specifically, while the TM3-7 helices aligned well with the inactive state of mGlyR and GABAB, TM1 and TM2, located near the dimer interface, displayed an outward movement of the cytosolic portion (Fig. 3). Moreover, GPR179’s TM4 exhibited an elongation of approximately one and two turns at the extracellular end compared to mGlyR and GABAB, respectively (Fig. 3a), which serves as a highly distinctive feature setting it apart from other class C GPCRs (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Notably, TM4 occupies the outer position of the TMD dimer, making its elongation suitable for adapting to the overall curved shape of the TMD. In contrast, GPR179’s TM3 was shortened by two turns at the extracellular end compared to GABAB, while it exhibited an elongation of approximately one to two turns at the intracellular end compared to other class C GPCRs (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Additionally, an intramolecular disulfide bond (C4453.29–C537) played a vital role in stabilizing the TMD conformation, and hydrophobic and polar interactions were also observed between ECD-TM1 loop and ECL2/ECL3 (Fig. 3b). Taken together, our analysis revealed that the precise adjustments in helix positions and lengths collectively contribute to the highly curved shape of the TMD in GPR179.

We further analyzed various polar interactions between TM helices. To facilitate illustration, we divided it into the upper, middle, and lower parts from top to bottom (Fig. 3c). In the upper part, TM3, TM5, and TM6 formed electrostatic interactions through R4493.33, D5435.40, and R5956.57, respectively (Fig. 3d). This is consistent with previous observations in mGlyR, where the R4853.33, D5795.40, and R6316.57 network interacts with Y6477.32 through hydrogen bonding45,46. However, in the case of GPR179, the residue corresponding to mGlyR Y6477.32 is F6117.32, which lacks polarity and thus does not participate in polar interactions with the R4493.33, D5435.40, and R5956.57 network (Fig. 3d). As a result, TM7 in GPR179 did not participate in the polar interactions and moved outward by ~1.4 Å compared to mGlyR (Supplementary Fig. 8b). This outward movement of TM7 potentially contributes to the formation of the dimer interface, as TM7 is involved in the dimer interface of GPR179’s TMD. In the middle part, unusual polar interactions involving residues in TM3, TM5, TM6, and TM7 were observed (Fig. 3e). E5505.47 in TM5 formed polar interactions with R4523.36 in TM3 and H5926.54 in TM6. Additionally, Y4603.44, E5846.46, S5886.50, H6147.35, and T6187.39 participated in polar interactions. Finally, in the lower part, the ionic lock commonly observed in inactive GPCRs was found (Fig. 3f)39,45,46,58,59. In most GPCRs, the inactive state of an individual TM domain is stabilized through the connection of TM3 and TM6 known as the ionic lock37. In GPR179, the ionic lock is formed by K4663.50 of TM3 and E5736.35 of TM6. The residue K6307.51, belonging to the conserved FxPKxx motif found in class C GPCRs except for mGlyR (which has the LxPKxx motif), interacts with the ionic lock, which corresponds to the NPxxY motif observed in class A GPCRs60. This ionic lock provides additional evidence that the apo form of GPR179 is in an inactive state.

Distinct dimerization mode of the ECD

The ECD of GPR179 consists of a Cache domain and a CR domain, each containing one and five pairs of disulfide bonds, respectively (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 9). The ten cysteine residues within the CR domain engage in intradomain disulfide bridges contributing to the stabilization of the ECD. The Cache domain comprises six β-strands (βI-βVI) and three α-helices forming a core region, and two additional β-strands (βIII′ - βIII′′) that interact with the outer surface of another protomer were observed (Fig. 4a). The superposition of the Cache domain protomer between GPR179 and mGlyR revealed structural similarity, with an RMSD of 1.6 Å (Supplementary Fig. 9a). However, notable conformational differences were observed upon dimerization, even though GPR179 ECD exhibits a high sequence homology with mGlyR (~40%) (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 7).

The dimer interface of the ECD buries a surface area of 1918 Å2, formed by the Cache domain52. The primary interactions at the ECD dimer interface are mediated by the αI and αII helices, which constitute the core region of the Cache domain by forming hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 4b, d). In the bottom half of the αI and αII helices, it appears that the S136 in αI interacts with Q104 in αII of other protomer. Notably, the bottom half of αI at the ECD dimer interface in GPR179 is predominantly composed of Gly residues without bulky side chains, while the bottom half of αI in mGlyR consists of Asp and Arg residues, leading to a closer distance between the αI helices in GPR179 compared to mGlyR (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, the R119 of αII’ in GPR179 engages in electrostatic interactions with D126 in αII and E128 in αII of other protomer, further stabilizing the dimer interface (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 9b). In contrast, in mGlyR with a Cache domain, hydrophobic interactions are observed similarly, but a flexible loop interacts with the other protomer instead of αII’ (Fig. 4d).

The overall architecture of the apo GPR179 Cache domain closely resembles the recently revealed Cache domain structure of GPR179 bound to pikachurin61. However, upon superimposing the Cache domains of apo GPR179 and the GPR179-pikachurin complex, distinctive structural changes emerged in regions directly involved in binding to pikachurin (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Notably, the βI-βII loop of GPR179, responsible for binding to pikachurin through polar interactions with the S12-S13 loop and H1-S3 loop, exhibited an inward compression in the GPR179-pikachurin complex compared to the apo form. Similar alterations were observed in the βV-βVI loop of GPR179, which interacts with the S8-S9 loop and S10-S11 loop of pikachurin. Furthermore, the β-hairpin loop, initially disordered in apo GPR179, exhibited structural stabilization in the GPR179-pikachurin complex, suggesting its role in binding to pikachurin. Additionally, the inter-protomer distance in apo GPR179 measured ~18 Å, whereas in the GPR179-pikachurin complex, it increased to ~21 Å (based on P286).

The CR domain of GPR179, which connects the Cache domain and TMD through an EGF-like fold linker, closely resembles that of mGlyR and maintains stability through conserved disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 9f, g). However, the CR domain of GPR179 differs from those of mGluR and CaSR, as it consists of TNF-like A1 and B2’ modules, indicating a divergence in the CR domain composition among class C GPCRs46,62. The ECD-TM1 loop, which serves to bridge the ECD to the TMD, establishes robust interactions via residues E361 and E375 with R532 of ECL2 and H603 of ECL3. These interactions indicate their crucial role in upholding structural stability between the ECD and the TMD (Fig. 3b).

Taken together, these differences in ECD interactions between GPR179 and mGlyR contribute to overall structural disparities. The distance between αI helices in GPR179 (based on P93) is ~7 Å, whereas in mGlyR (based on P120), the distance between αI helices is relatively larger, ~14 Å (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, along with the variations in ECD interactions, differences in the TMD dimer arrangement also lead to variations in the direction of the loop connecting the ECD and TM1, resulting in a shorter distance between CR domains in GPR179 (25 Å) compared to mGlyR (42 Å) (Fig. 4e). Collectively, these disparities in the ECD dimer interface and TMD dimer interface significantly impact GPR179, causing it to adopt a conformation that differs not only from conventional class C GPCRs but also from mGlyR.

Mapping of complete congenital night blindness-related mutations

Mutations in the gene encoding GPR179, located in the dendrites of ON-bipolar cells, lead to the development of complete congenital stationary night blindness (cCSNB)5,7,9,10. These missense alterations induce structural changes in GPR179 by altering conserved amino acid residues. Based on our apo GPR179 structure, we investigated how four well-known missense mutations (D126H, Y220C, G455D, and H603Y) associated with cCSNB induce molecular-level structural changes in GPR179 (Fig. 5)7,9.

Missense mutation sites associated with cCSNB in GPR179.

The structure of GPR179 with cCSNB mutations. Disease-causing mutation sites identified in cCSNB are highlighted as black circles and magnified views are shown in black boxes. The mutated residues (His126, Cys220, Asp455, and Tyr603) in cCSNB are shown in violet sticks.

Each mutation site was identified in the Cache domain and TMD. Firstly, the D126H mutation located in the Cache domain is positioned on αI, which plays a crucial role in Cache domain dimerization. The D126 interacts with R119 on αII’ through electrostatic interactions, contributing to the rigid structure of the dimer interface formed by αI and αII. In the D126H mutant, the substitution of Asp with His might eliminate the electrostatic interaction with R119, causing αII’ to move away from αI due to the presence of a basic charge. Secondly, the Y220C mutation disrupts the interaction between Y220 on βIII” and K199 on βIII’, which is observed around the Cache domain, thereby affecting dimerization stability61. Thirdly, in the case of the H603Y mutation, the electrostatic interaction between H603 in ECL3 and E375 in the ECD-TM1 loop is disrupted as His is replaced by Tyr. Consequently, TM1, which forms the dimer interface of the TMD, is expected to exhibit increased dynamicity. Lastly, the G455D mutation replaces the Gly located in TM3 with Asp, which has a negatively charged side chain. This substitution may lead to clashes between G455D on TM3 and L428 and L429 on TM2, causing TM2 to move away from TM3. This displacement of TM2 from TM3 may potentially lead to overall structural changes in the TMD, as previously predicted in the 3D model7. Overall, our cryo-EM structure of GPR179 elucidates how cCSNB-related mutations compromise its structural integrity, which could be related to the dysfunction of GPR179.

The putative ligand-binding site and functional assays for monitoring the activity of GPR179

The ECD of GPR179 displays unique structural characteristics. Recent studies have revealed structural requirements for the binding of GPR179’s ECD to the extracellular matrix protein pikachurin61. However, the natural ligand responsible for activating GPR179 remains unidentified. Consequently, we conducted investigations to determine whether glycine, the ligand for mGlyR, could also be a ligand for GPR179 and influence deactivation of Gαo in a manner similar to mGlyR. Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)-based reporter assays were employed to examine the effect of glycine on the GAP activity of the GPR179–RGS7-Gβ5 complex. The results demonstrate that the addition of glycine had no discernible influence on the kinetics of Gαo deactivation (Fig. 6a, b), despite significantly inhibiting the GAP activity of mGlyR analyzed in parallel (Supplementary Fig. 10). We also examined the impact of GPR179 on cAMP formation, given the role of its associated RGS7/Gβ5 complex in cAMP production63, using both conformational cAMP biosensor (Camyel) and transcriptional reporter (CRE) (Supplementary Fig. 11). We found that introduction of the RGS7-Gβ5 complex significantly reduced cAMP formation both at baseline and upon elevation of cAMP by forskolin, as expected from its regulation of Gαo. However, the introduction of GPR179 either alone or in combination with RGS7-Gβ5 failed to additionally influence cAMP production, suggesting a lack of constitutive activity (Supplementary Fig. 11). Furthermore, application of glycine had no significant impact on GPR179-expressing cells as compared to control.

When we compared the ECD of GPR179 with that of mGlyR, we found that while several of the residues predicted to interact with glycine in mGlyR were also conserved in GPR179, the organization of the putative ligand-binding pocket in GPR179 was drastically different in both its shape, size and the distribution of electrostatic potentials on the surface (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9e). There was also a notable substitution: S172 in mGlyR was replaced with Y145 in GPR179 leading to additional constraints in the ligand-binding pocket (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9e). Given these differences, it seems unlikely that GPR179 shares the same ligand, glycine, with mGlyR. Instead, GPR179 might be activated by different small molecule ligands, despite the overall sequence similarity between GPR179 and mGlyR.

We further tested the constitutive GAP activity of GPR179 in the BRET assay and analyzed the functional impact of cCSNB-related mutations (D126H, Y220C, G455D, and H603Y) as well as a mutation designed to prevent disulfide linking of the protomers (C390A). The introduction of GPR179 did not significantly impact deactivation kinetics of Gαo, except for G455D (Fig. 6d). Because the C-terminus of GPR179 contains tandem repeats of PDE-gamma-like (PGL) sequences that can impact G protein dynamics at the basal state48,49, we also examined BRET parameters in the basal state before initiating or terminating Gαo signaling (Fig. 6e). Interestingly, while cCSNB-related mutations in the ECD of GPR179 and disulfide disrupting C390A had no impact on the basal BRET, the mutation affecting the intracellular surface (H603Y) significantly diminished the signal. We observed a similar effect upon disrupting the PGL sequences suggesting that this alteration may affect the dynamics of Gαo association with GPR179 in the basal state.

Discussion

GPR179, an orphan GPCR belonging to class C, plays a crucial role in transmitting visual signals, and its deficiency is linked to severe ocular diseases. Thus, elucidating the structure of GPR179 is fundamental for deorphanizing GPR179 and offers valuable insights into understanding a range of ocular diseases, including cCSNB. In this study, we unveiled the structure of human GPR179, which exhibits a unique architecture, deviating not only from other previously characterized class C GPCRs but also from mGlyR, despite initial expectations of a similar overall structure based on sequence homology.

The GPR179 TMD elucidated noncanonical structural features distinct from those found in other structurally-characterized class C GPCRs. Most strikingly, the TMD dimer interface of GPR179 is formed by TM1/7, representing a nonclassical dimeric arrangement not observed in other class C GPCRs. While it has been reported that mGluR5 and GABAB formed dimer interfaces through TM526,35,36, CasR through TM5/640–42,44, and mGlyR through the interaction of TM4/545,46, recent studies have revealed that inactive state mGlu2/3 heterodimers can exhibit various dimerization modes, including TM2/3/4, TM5, and TM4/5 interfaces33. However, exclusively, noncanonical dimerization mediated by TM1/7 was observed in GPR179 (Fig. 2), and the inter-protomer distance in GPR179 was much shorter compared to other class C GPCRs. These characteristics suggest the possibility of distinct conformational changes in the activation process of GPR179 compared to other class C GPCRs.

In class C GPCRs, the hallmark of activation appears to be a TM6–TM6 interface driven by a substantial conformational change from their inactive state mediated by TM3, TM4 or TM526–32,34–36,38–44. Consequently, introducing a disulfide bond at the TMD interface would impede the TMD rearrangement, stabilizing the inactive dimer53. Indeed, it was shown that the introduction of a disulfide bond at the TMD interface of mGluR2 led to a considerable reduction in receptor activity28. Remarkably, the observed disulfide bond (C390–C390) in the TMD interface of GPR179 is a distinctive characteristic not identified in other class C GPCRs. Given the substantial conformational changes required for the activation of class C GPCRs, the presence of the disulfide bond in the GPR179 TMD interface could inherently favor the inactive conformation, making conformational rearrangements that modify the protomer orientations more challenging. This suggests that GPR179 might encounter difficulties in forming a TM6–TM6 interface, as seen in other active class C GPCRs. This observation aligns with the established understanding that GPR179 functions as a noncanonical receptor, differing from canonical class C GPCRs by not activating heterotrimeric G proteins but rather recruiting the RGS protein complex6–9,61. In this regard, it is interesting that two CSNB-causing mutations slightly affected parameters of the G protein response. This may indicate that these mutations influence the conformational dynamics of the GPR179 complex that controls Gαo signaling in ON-BC. However, our ability to investigate the underlying mechanisms is limited by the absence of knowledge regarding its ligand.

The other notable feature is the curved conformation of the GPR179 TMD, which is tailored for the highly curved membrane of the dendritic tip. This unique TMD shape, distinct from other class C GPCRs, appears to be attributed to at least three structural characteristics. Firstly, the positions and lengths of each helix within the TMD seem to be intricately adjusted to facilitate the formation of the curved TMD dimer, as discussed above (Fig. 3a). Secondly, the dimer interface is narrower, and both protomers are held together by a disulfide bond, enhancing the flexibility of the TMD dimer. The TMD dimer interface of GPR179 (585 Å2) primarily comprises weak hydrophobic interactions mediated by alanine and one disulfide bond (C390–C390). This is in contrast to the dimer interface of mGlyR (1062 Å2), resulting in a smaller contact area. Consequently, the interaction between dimers is relatively weak, and the disulfide bond seems to act as a hinge, facilitating dynamic movements of the TMD dimer (Supplementary Movie 1). This dynamic behavior is hypothesized to assist GPR179 in maintaining a stable conformation within the curved structure of the dendritic tip. Thirdly, the overall architecture of the GPR179 ECD is well-suited for accommodating the curved architecture of the TMD dimer (Fig. 4e). A shorter distance between CR domains in GPR179 (25 Å), compared to mGlyR (42 Å), is particularly well-suited for connecting both C-terminal ends of ECD dimers to both TM1s within the TMD dimer. Additionally, the ECD-TM1 loop on the inside of the TMD dimer interface also contributes to linking the ECD to TM1.

There is a significant interest not only in investigating which ligands activate GPR179 but also in determining how the resulting signal alters depolarizing response of ON-BC. In this context, the GPR179 structure will provide a crucial foundation for such investigations and could enhance our comprehension of GPR179’s role in the visual signal transduction process, potentially contributing to a better understanding of ocular diseases.

Methods

Protein expression and purification

A gene fragment of truncated human GPR179 (amino acids 1 to 735) was subcloned into an in-house-modified pFastBac vector in frame with a C-terminal HRV-3C recognition site followed by EGFP and Twin-StrepII-tag. Escherichia coli DH10Bac strain (Gibco, cat #10361012) was transformed with pFastBac-GPR179 to produce GPR179 bacmid, which was transfected into Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) obtained from ThermoFisher (Catalog #11496015) to produce baculovirus containing the GPR179 expression cassette, according to manufacturer’s instructions. Sf9 cells were grown at 27 °C in suspension in ESF293 medium (Expression Systems) supplemented with 0.06 mg ml−1 penicillin G and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (sigma). At 48 h after infection, the cells were centrifugated at 500 × g for 10 min.

All purification steps were carried out at 4 °C or on ice unless indicated otherwise. The collected cells were resuspended in buffer A (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, and 20% glycerol) supplemented with protease inhibitors [1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 2 μg ml−1 leupeptin, 2 μM pepstatin A, and 2 μM aprotinin] and solubilized with 1% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG), and 0.1% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS) for 2 h. After ultracentrifugation at 50,000 × g for 1 h, the supernatant was incubated with Strep-Tactin resin (IBA Lifesciences) for 3–6 h. The resin was washed with 10 column volumes of buffer B [Buffer A supplemented with 0.001% (w/v) LMNG and 0.0001% (w/v) CHS]. The protein was eluted with two column volumes of buffer B with 5 mM d-desthiobiotin. The eluates were concentrated and further purified using Superose 6 Increase 10/300 column equilibrated with buffer B. Peak fractions were pooled, concentrated to ~1.5 mg ml−1, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for EM grid preparation.

Fluorescence detection size-exclusion chromatography

The oligomerization state of GPR179 was evaluated by fluorescence detection size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC)64. GPR179-eGFP was expressed as described above and solubilized with buffer A supplemented with protease inhibitors, 1% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.1% (w/v) CHS for 2 h at 4 °C. The insoluble fraction was removed by high-speed centrifugation at 50,000 g for 1 h. The soluble fraction was loaded onto Superose 6 Increase 5/150 GL size-exclusion column, and the fluorescence signal was monitored by a fluorescence detector (Agilent Technologies, cat #DEAEJ00102).

Cryo-EM specimen preparation and data collection

Purified GPR179 was concentrated to 1.5–2.0 mg ml−1. The cryo-EM grids were prepared by applying 3 μl of protein sample to a glow-discharged Quantifoil 1.2/1.3 300-mesh Cu holey carbon grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) under 4 °C and 100% humidity. The grids were blotted for 7.0 s and then plunged into liquid ethane using Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI). Cryo-EM images were collected at Institute of Membrane Proteins (IMP) using Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; TFS, USA) equipped with a Gatan K3 summit direct electron detector. Automated data acquisition was performed in electron counting mode using EPU software (TFS, USA). For the apo GPR179 structure, 22,837 movies were collected with a pixel size of 0.8512 Å/pixel using a defocus range of −0.8 to −2.25 μm. Each movie (50 frames) was acquired using a total exposure of 60 e−/Å2.

Image processing and reconstruction

The cryo-EM image processing was performed with cryoSPARC version 3.2.065 or Relion 3.1 softwares66. The apo GPR179 dataset was processed using cryoSPARC (v.3.2.0.). Patch-based pre-processing (Patch motion correction & Patch CTF estimation) was performed for the dataset containing 22,837 movies. The cryoSPARC exposure curation module was employed to sort through the images, thereby eliminating micrographs that contained outliers in defocus value, ice thickness, and CTF fit lower than 10. This process left 21,884 micrographs that were subsequently processed. TOPAZ, a convolutional neural network algorithm implemented in cryoSPARC, was utilized for particle picking67. A training set of 100 micrographs was used to develop the output model, which was then applied to the entire dataset for particle picking. Next, the picked particles were subject to three rounds of reference-free 2D classification for each of the three views (i.e., front, top, and side views) to discard bad or junk particles. After the 2D classification, duplicate particles were removed, and the remaining good particles were re-extracted into 400-pixel boxes. Finally, 1,157,942 particle images were selected and subjected to 3D reconstruction using ab initio reconstruction followed by heterogeneous refinement with C2 symmetry imposed in cryoSPARC.

A set of 1,122,857 particles was selected and subjected to non-uniform refinement with C2 symmetry in cryoSPARC. Subsequently, local refinement was performed on this subset, resulting in a map of apo GPR179 with a final resolution of ~3.6 Å. The particles were then transferred to Relion version 4.068 using the ‘csparc2star.py’ command to classify them based on their conformations. Focused 3D classification and particle sorting processes were carried out for this purpose. The particles used to obtain the consensus map were subjected to particle subtraction to generate subtracted particles with the density of ECD or TMD only. Further focused 3D classification without image alignment was performed using the same soft mask used for the particle subtraction. Several rounds of focused classifications of ECD or TMD subtracted particles were performed in Relion, and the particles with the best class were transferred back to cryoSPARC for local refinement, yielding a GSFSC resolution of 3.1 Å for the ECD or 3.5 Å for the TMD. These two individual reconstructions were then used to obtain a composite map of apo GPR179 using the vop maximum utility in Chimera.

The reported resolutions of the final maps were estimated using the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (GSFSC) in cryoSPARC65. Local resolution predictions were calculated using ResMap69, and the resolution range was estimated using the FSC = 0.143 criterion70. EM density visualization was carried out in UCSF Chimera version 1.1571. Local resolutions for all maps were assessed using the local resolution estimation in cryoSPARC.

Model building and refinement

The atomic model of human GPR179 was built from the composite map. AlphaFold model of GPR179 were used to generate initial templates for model building. Each of ECD and TMD for AlphaFold GPR179 was fitted into the composite EM density maps using UCSF chimera and then manually adjusted, built and real space refined in COOT version 0.9.8.772. The overall model was further refined and validated by Phenix version 1.1973. All structural figures and movies were visualized and generated by PyMOL, UCSF Chimera71 or UCSF ChimeraX version 1.374.

GPR179 (Origene: #NM_001004334) was purchased from Origene and cloned in pcDNA3.1+ vector. masGRK3ct-Nluc, Venus-156-239-Gβ1, and Venus-1-155-Gγ2 were synthesized by GenScript in pcDNA3.1+. pCMV5 plasmids encoding GαoA was gifts from H. Itoh (Nara Institute of Science and Technology, Japan).

The following chemicals were used. Dopamine hydrochloride (MilliporeSigma Cat# H8502), Haloperidol (MilliporeSigma Cat# H1512), Glycine (National Diagnostic # EC405), Forskolin (MP Biomedicals #66575-29-9), Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) (Gibco Cat#10010-023) with 0.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% glucose, TE-buffer: 50 mM Tris-HCl 5 mM EDTA pH=7.4, glycine ultrapure (EC405 National Diagnostic), OptiMEM (Gibco # 31985070), Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# 11965-092), Fetal bovine serum (Genesee Scientific Cat# 25-550), Sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# 11360-070), MEM non-essential amino acids (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# 11140-050), Penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat#15140-122), Matrigel (Corning Cat# 356230), METAFECTENE® PRO (RKP203/RK092820, Biontex Germany), Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (MilliporeSigma Cat# D5652), Nano-Glo luciferase (N113B Promega).

Culture and transfection of clonal cell lines

HEK293FT cells (Catalog #CRL-1573) were obtained from ATCC and grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (v/v), minimum Eagle’s medium non-essential amino acids, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and antibiotics (100 units/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Cells were transiently transfected using Metafectene Pro (Biontex, Germany) in 96 well plate following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell-based GAP assay

The assay performed to monitor the GAP activity of RGS7 were conducted as described previously75. Shortly, HEK293FT cells were seeded in a white flat-bottom 96 well plate (Greiner Bio-One) at 50,000 cells per well and transiently transfected using metafectene pro with the plasmid coding for dopamine D2 receptor, RGS7, Gβ5, GαoA, GPR179 WT or GPR179 mutant (D126H, Y220C, G455D, H603Y, C390A or PGL-AAAAA), masGRK3ct-Nluc, Venus-156-239-Gβ1, and Venus-1-155-Gγ2. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were washed twice with BRET buffer (Dulbecco’s PBS containing 0.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% glucose) and incubated 5 min at 37 °C with the NanoLuc (Nluc) substrate, furimazine (Promega: #N1110) at the manufacturer’s instructions with or without glycine at 100 µM. In order to activate and deactivate dopamine D2 receptor, respectively, dopamine and haloperidol at the final concentration of 100 µM ligands were injected sequentially and automatically on the cells by the plate reader (PHERAstar FSX, BMG Labtech) and dual luminescence were measured at 475 ± 30 nm and 535 ± 30 nm. All measurements were performed at 37 °C. The BRET signal was determined by calculating the ratio of the light emitted by the Venus-Gβ1γ2 (535 nm with a 30 nm band path width) over the light emitted by the masGRK3ct-Nluc (475 nm with a 30 nm band path width). The average baseline value (basal BRET ratio) recorded prior to agonist stimulation was subtracted from the experimental BRET signal values and the resulting difference (∆BRET ratio) was normalized against the maximal ∆BRET value recorded upon agonist stimulation. The rate constants (1/τ) of the deactivation phases were obtained by fitting a one phase exponential decay curve to the traces with Graphpad Prism 9.0.

Cell-based cAMP assay (Camyel)

Cells were co-transfected with the cAMP sensor Camyel and with untagged receptors (respectively: empty vector, GPR179, RGS7/Gβ5, D2R, mGluR5 or mGlyR cloned in pcDNA3.1+ vector) in DMEM complete medium supplemented with 0.1% of Matrigel (Corning). 24 h after transfection, cells were washed once with BRET buffer (Dulbecco’s PBS containing 0.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% glucose). The Rluc (Renilla luciferase) substrate, coelenterazine H (Research Products International Corp, IL), was diluted to 5 μM in BRET buffer and added to cells with or without 1 µM of forskolin. BRET measurements were made using a microplate reader (PHERAstar FSX; BMG Labtech) equipped with two emission photomultiplier tubes. All measurements were performed at 37 °C and dual luminescence was measured at 475 ± 30 nm and 535 ± 30 nm. Since this cAMP BRET-based sensor (Camyel) is inversely proportional to cAMP, 1/BRET signal was determined by calculating the ratio of the light emitted by the RLuc (475 nm with a 30 nm band path width) over the light emitted by Venus (535 nm with a 30 nm band path width) in order to measure an increase in the signal when cAMP increase. The percentage of response was plotted as a function of the pcDNA3.1-empty vector condition.

Cell-based transcriptional assay (CRE reporter)

Cells were transfected with CRE, reporter cloned in the pNL(NlucP/reporter/Hygro) vector and with untagged receptors (respectively; empty vector, GPR179, RGS7/Gβ5, D2R, mGluR5 or mGlyR cloned in the pcDNA3.1+ vector) in DMEM complete medium supplemented with 0.1% of Matrigel (Corning). 24 h after transfection, cells were washed once with BRET buffer (Dulbecco’s PBS containing 0.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% glucose) and incubated with optiMEM [±1 μM of forskolin] for 6 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. The Nluc (Nanoluciferase) substrate, fumirazide (Nanoglo from Promega), was diluted according to the manufacturer’s instruction in DMEM and added to the cells. Luminescence measurements were made using a microplate reader (PHERAstar FSX; BMG Labtech). All measurements were performed at 37 °C.

SDS-PAGE analysis

10 μl of each sample at a concentration of 2 mg/ml was combined with either 2.5 μl of 5× SDS loading buffer (comprising 60 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 25% glycerol, 2% SDS, 0.1% bromophenol blue, and 720 mM β-mercaptoethanol) or 5× non-reducing SDS loading buffer (comprising 60 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 25% glycerol, 2% SDS, and 0.1% bromophenol blue). The mixture was then incubated at 100 °C for 5 minutes. After incubation, 5 μl of each sample was loaded into the wells of a 4–20% precast polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad). The dimerization of GPR179 was measured by western blot analysis using an anti-strep tag conjugated monoclonal antibody (IBA: Catalog# 2-1509-001; 1:4000).

Data analysis and statistics

The functional data shown represent the average ± SEM of 6–12 individual experiments each performed in triplicate or indicated in each figure and expressed as means ± standard error means (s.e.m). In the GAP assay, cells with the same transfection conditions were compared on their treatment, and a one-way ANOVA test was performed. The non-linear regression curve was used to fit on the dose response curve giving the IC50 value. For cAMP assays and CRE reporter assays, cells transfected with empty vector were used as control and one-way ANOVA test was performed. Values of *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001 ***p < 0.0001 were considered to be statistically significant. Calculations, graphs, and statistics were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 software (San Diego, CA, USA).
