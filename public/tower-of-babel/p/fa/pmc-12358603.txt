Interobserver agreement in BPPV diagnosis using eye movement recordings
Authors: Ali A Melliti, Rajneesh Bhandari, Anita Bhandari, Andrea Castellucci, Marie-Cecile Gerards, Bre Myers, Vipin Ola, Peter Oostenbrink, Hans Van Kampen, Sophie Vanbelle, Raymond van de Berg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV) is one of the most prevalent peripheral vestibular disorders seen in specialized dizziness clinics. Despite being a well-understood condition with effective treatment options, BPPV remains associated with significant diagnostic delays and healthcare costs. If proven reliable, telemedicine approaches could help address these challenges by improving diagnostic accessibility and efficiency. To investigate the interobserver agreement in BPPV diagnosis, when using eye movement recordings. Six vestibular medicine specialists (Specialist 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) were recruited to participate in this study. The specialists were asked to evaluate the recordings of 240 patient cases who underwent assessment for BPPV (first assessment). After viewing the recordings of each case, they were required to make a BPPV diagnosis. Five specialists (2, 3, 4, 5, 6) agreed to repeat the procedure twice, to additionally evaluate the intraobserver agreement (second assessment). The proportion of agreement and Cohen’s kappa were calculated for both interobserver and intraobserver agreement. Furthermore, agreement with the original diagnoses was evaluated. The interobserver agreement between experts was fair to moderate with a Cohen’s kappa value of 0.40 (CI 95% [0.35, 0.45]) and a proportion of agreement of 60% (CI 95% [54, 67]). Specialists 2, 4, and 5 exhibited substantial intraobserver agreement. In contrast, Specialist 3 demonstrated fair intraobserver agreement, while Specialist 6 showed almost perfect intraobserver agreement. Regarding the first assessment, agreement with the original diagnoses ranged from fair to substantial, with kappa values between 0.40 and 0.70, and corresponding percentages between 58 and 78%. Similar results were observed for the second assessment. The interobserver agreement between specialists diagnosing BPPV using eye movement recordings, was fair to moderate. The suboptimal agreement could be related to missing clinical information (e.g. patient history and symptoms during positional maneuvers). Introduction

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is an inner ear disease which is believed to be caused by displacement of otoconia from the utricle to the semicircular canals1. Patients with BPPV experience brief spinning sensations when changing head position, caused by free-floating otoconia stimulating the cupula. This triggers aberrant vestibulo-ocular reflex signals leading to nystagmus1–3. Clinicians diagnose BPPV using positional tests such as the Dix-Hallpike and supine roll test which are meant to provoke symptoms and generate nystagmus. The direction of the resultant nystagmus pattern provides information to identify the affected canal. Consequently, the correct repositioning maneuver can be performed1,3. BPPV is reported to account for 20–30% of patients presenting to specialized vestibular clinics with complaints of dizziness. It is therefore one of the most prevalent vestibular disorders4. Despite being well understood, BPPV remains associated with significant healthcare costs3. The main reason for the high cost is the delay in patient diagnosis, with many patients undergoing unnecessary procedures before they are referred to specialists5.

The use of video-oculography is recommended by BPPV clinical guidelines, as it improves evaluation of nystagmus and facilitates eye movement recordings and analysis1,3,6. These recordings can be transmitted digitally, allowing vestibular specialists to remotely assess eye movements through telemedicine platforms. The use of telemedicine services to review eye movement recordings can improve diagnostic efficiency in the vestibular medicine7–10. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, current telemedicine approaches for diagnosing BPPV do not systematically incorporate critical clinical elements, such as head positioning. This may be essential for accurate and reliable diagnosis9,11. Hence, evaluating diagnostic agreement among specialists using eye movement recordings, may support the development of a robust protocol to guide standardized telemedicine practices for BPPV assessment. This approach allows for assessment of the current protocol’s effectiveness. Furthermore, it helps identifying challenges in evaluating eye movements remotely. Previously, moderate to almost perfect levels of interobserver agreement were found between specialists evaluating BPPV12,13. However, these studies were limited in sample size. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the interobserver agreement in BPPV diagnosis when solely using eye movement recordings from a large cohort of patients.

Methods

Study design

Six vestibular medicine specialists evaluated eye movement recordings of 240 patient cases. These recordings were collected during BPPV assessment. Each specialist was required to provide a diagnosis after reviewing every case. Based on the collected diagnoses, the interobserver agreement was determined. Additionally, five specialists evaluated the 240 patient cases a second time (first and second assessment respectively), which allowed determination of the intraobserver agreement. Finally, the level of agreement with the original diagnoses was determined for each specialist.

Specialists and patient cases

Specialists

Six vestibular medicine specialists were recruited for the study. They were selected based on their expertise in Vestibular Medicine. This was reflected by years of experience (mean 16.7 years, range 7–33 years) and setting (tertiary referral clinics). In order to attempt to increase generalizability, participants were selected from four countries. Two were affiliated with the same institution, while the others were from different organizations. Among these specialists, four practiced in a hospital setting, one practiced in both a hospital and private practice setting, and one was affiliated with a university hospital. The specialists included one ENT surgeon, one neurologist, two laboratory technicians, one audiologist, and one physician assistant. On average, they evaluated 22 patients per month for BPPV.

Patient cases

The eye movement recordings of 240 patients with vestibular symptoms were used in this study. These patients had previously undergone assessment for BPPV at Vertigo and Ear clinic, Jaipur. Their data were retrieved from the NeuroEquilibrium database, which includes standardized eye movement recordings collected during clinical evaluations. Patient characteristics are described in Table 1. Initially, patient selection aimed for an equal distribution across diagnoses (30 per diagnosis). The primary reason for this approach was to ensure a comprehensive representation of all diagnoses, including rare conditions such as anterior canal BPPV. However, due to a technical issue during video data extraction, recordings from patients’ most recent visits were used instead of their initial visit/evaluation. This led to some cases being captured post-symptom resolution, increasing the proportion classified as having ‘no BPPV’. Nonetheless, the overall distribution still reflected the full spectrum of BPPV subtypes, preserving the study’s integrity. In addition, for each specific diagnosis, cases were randomly selected. For this study, the original diagnosis was considered as a reference, as the original clinician had access to patient history, symptom reporting and recorded traces of eye movements.

Presentation of patient cases to specialists

The specialists were instructed to evaluate 240 patient cases, using the NeuroEquilibrium software platform (NeuroEquilibrium Diagnostic Systems Pvt Ltd, Jaipur, India). Each patient case consisted of six eye movement recordings during BPPV assessment: Spontaneous Nystagmus, Supine Roll to the Right, Supine Roll to the Left, Dix-Hallpike Maneuver to the Right, Dix-Hallpike Maneuver to the Left, and the Deep Head Hanging test (see Fig. 1). Accompanying head position, patient feedback, or patient case history were not included. Head position was not included because it was not captured in the retrospectively collected videos. This could impact diagnostic accuracy. It however also reflected most of current telemedicine protocols.

Each specialist had their own individual login credentials, allowing them to assess the cases independently at their own pace. The nomenclature of the diagnoses was determined by consensus among four authors (AM, RvB, AB, RB), which was also based on used terms in The American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery guideline3. Although the Bárány Society recommends distinguishing canalithiasis from cupulolithiasis, these were excluded due to diagnostic complexity and limited consensus1,14. Furthermore, Anterior, and apogeotropic posterior canal BPPV were grouped together due to overlapping features (e.g., downbeat nystagmus) and the limitations of single video recordings without follow-up15. Noteworthy, cases with ‘No BPPV’ could still demonstrate positional nystagmus. This resulted from the fact that only patients with vestibular symptoms (and other vestibular disorders than BPPV) were included.

Five out of six specialists agreed to evaluate all 240 patient cases for a second assessment. This allowed to determine the intraobserver agreement. To reduce recall bias during the second assessment, cases were randomized again, and a minimum interval of three weeks was maintained between the first and second assessment.

At the end of this study, the specialists were requested to provide feedback about evaluating the patient cases. An open form was used.

Statistical analysis

The interobserver and intraobserver agreement for the BPPV diagnosis (8 categories) were evaluated using the proportion of agreement, Cohen’s kappa for two raters and its extension (Conger’s kappa) to multiple raters16. Confidence intervals were constructed based on the delta method. Additionally, given the large number of BPPV negative cases, the mean agreement between pairs of specialists was calculated for the overall presence and absence of BPPV, as well as the positive agreement (BPPV present), and negative agreement (BPPV absent). We found the benchmark scale established by Landis and Koch (1977), which categorizes kappa values as follows: almost perfect (0.81–1.00), substantial (0.61–0.80), moderate (0.41–0.60), fair (0.21–0.40), slight (0–0.20), and poor < 0 appropriate for our study to interpret kappa coefficients (accounting for confidence intervals)17. All calculations were made in R (v4.1.2) and SPSS (v28.0.0.0).

Results

Interobserver agreement in the diagnosis of BPPV

The interobserver agreement for the first assessment in the diagnosis of BPPV, based on eye movement recordings alone, is presented in Table 2. The overall agreement was fair to moderate with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.40 (CI 95% [0.35,0.45]) and a proportion of agreement of 60% (CI 95% [54,67]). Specialist 3 demonstrated the lowest agreement with the other specialists. When excluding specialist 3 from the analysis, the interobserver agreement slightly increased to moderate with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.46 (CI 95% [0.41, 0.52]) and a proportion of agreement of 67% (CI 95% [61,73]). The proportion of diagnoses for each rater at the first assessment is shown in Supplementary Table S1. Additionally, the mean agreement between pairs of specialists on the presence versus absence of BPPV was 68%. The percentage of positive agreement (presence of BPPV) was 64.0% (CI 95% [59,69]) while the negative agreement (absence of BPPV) was 71.0% (CI 95%, [68,75]).

Intraobserver agreement in the diagnosis of BPPV

Table 3 presents the intraobserver agreement in the diagnosis of BPPV (first versus second assessment), based on five specialists, who conducted a second assessment of the 240 patient cases. Considering the confidence intervals, specialists 2, 4, and 5 exhibited a moderate to substantial intraobserver agreement, with kappa’s between 0.62 and 0.65. In contrast, specialist 3 exhibited a fair agreement with a kappa of 0.32 (95% CI [0.23,0.40]), while specialist 6 demonstrated an almost perfect intraobserver agreement with a kappa of 0.93 (95% CI [0.89,0.97]). Discussion

The overall interobserver agreement among vestibular medicine specialists when diagnosing BPPV solely based on eye movement recordings was fair to moderate. In addition, agreement with the original diagnoses ranged from fair to substantial.

This agreement is lower than previously reported, where moderate to almost perfect agreement levels were found between specialists evaluating BPPV12,13. This could be explained by several factors related to this study: (1) the higher prevalence of different BPPV types, including anterior canal BPPV, and (2) the absence of other patient data, including head position, patient feedback, patient case history and nystagmus tracings. These factors will now be discussed.

The higher prevalence of rare and complex BPPV variants, namely horizontal and anterior canal types, may have also reduced agreement. Horizontal and anterior canal BPPV are less well understood than posterior canal BPPV, and can present with atypical features3. For example, horizontal canal BPPV might produce different types of nystagmus than previously reported18. Anterior canal BPPV can mimic central vestibular disorders19. Including more of these challenging BPPV variants, most likely resulted in a lower agreement between specialists. It should also be noted that specialists do not always agree when evaluating vestibulo-ocular responses20. This implies that the absence or presence of nystagmus is interpreted differently by different specialists, leading to contrasting BPPV diagnoses. Moreover, specialists may even disagree with themselves, when evaluating the same eye movement recordings multiple times21. These previous findings were also confirmed by this study.

Regarding the absence of other patient data in this study, the assessments made by the specialists were only based on the eye movement recordings. Head position, patient feedback during the diagnostic maneuvers, patient case history and nystagmus tracings, were not included. This may have challenged each specialist differently, potentially contributing to variability in their results with the original diagnoses.

Most specialists independently indicated that evaluating eye movement recordings without information about head position was difficult. Knowing the head position, could help differentiating between physiological nystagmus when lying down during the Dix-Hallpike maneuver, and nystagmus related to BPPV11,22,23.

Furthermore, patient feedback is valuable when evaluating patients with BPPV. It can be particularly important in cases with subtle nystagmus during positional testing, cases with suboptimal quality of eye movement recordings (e.g., blinking, goggle slippage), and in cases of (suspected) subjective BPPV24,25. A previous study including head position and audio recordings, did show a higher agreement than this study12.

Additionally, only one of the specialists mentioned the importance of patient case history, a key element of the Bárány diagnostic criteria1. History taking is a crucial diagnostic tool and would normally contribute to the final diagnosis26. However, the main objective of this study was to investigate the interobserver agreement in BPPV diagnosis, when using eye movement recordings only. Patient case history was therefore not included.

Recently, it was also proposed to include nystagmus tracings when evaluating eye movement recordings. These tracings could support decision making in case of subtle nystagmus patterns, especially in horizontal canal BPPV7.

The abovementioned factors should be considered, when implementing a telemedicine approach for diagnosing BPPV. After all, if vestibular medicine specialists were to remotely assess vestibular patients, access to head position information, patient feedback, patient case history and nystagmus tracings, might improve the diagnostic process11,27,28. Therefore, it would be advised to incorporate these aspects in the equipment (hardware and software) used for telemedicine in vestibular patients.

Limitations

There are a few limitations to this study. Firstly, the duration of the recordings was not standardized, which could have introduced bias (see Table 5). As noted by one specialist, shorter recording durations might suggest a negative BPPV diagnosis. Secondly, the large sample size may have imposed a burden on the specialists, potentially affecting focus. Thirdly, the large number of BPPV-negative cases may have influenced agreement rates. However, the mean agreement between pairs of specialists remained lower than expected for the presence (64%) and absence (71%) of BPPV. This suggests challenges in identifying negative cases, as positional nystagmus can occur in healthy individuals or patients with other vestibular disorders11,29.

Conclusion

The overall interobserver agreement between vestibular medicine specialists, when diagnosing BPPV solely based on eye movement recordings, was fair to moderate. The disagreement might be related to the absence of clinical information such as head position, patient feedback and patient case history. Incorporating these elements into telemedicine equipment could enhance diagnostic accuracy and improve patient outcomes, paving the way for more effective and comprehensive remote care in the future.
