Outcomes of patients with cervical spine metastasis treated with different surgical approaches—decision based on location
Authors: Jiasheng Chen, Ben Wang, Liang Liu, Yang Luo, Fangzhi Liu, Yanchao Tang, Panpan Hu, Hua Zhou, Zihe Li, Xiaoguang Liu, Zhongjun Liu, Feng Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe low incidence of cervical metastases and complex anatomical structure of cervical spine complicate surgical strategy formulation and make it controversial. Background

With continuous advances in systemic therapy, the number of patients with spinal metastatic tumors and their overall survival have gradually increased [1]. This change has shifted the clinical priorities to encompass not only effective tumor management but also preservation of functional outcomes and local disease control. With respect to surgical treatment, advances have evolved from initial treatment decisions based on tools such as Tomita and Tokuhashi scores to the introduction of separation surgery and the neurologic, oncologic, mechanical, and systemic (NOMS) framework [2–5], hereby establishing a robust theoretical foundation for spinal metastatic tumor management.

The treatment of thoracolumbar metastases follows the principles of separation surgery, which aims to achieve circumferential decompression of the spinal cord (with a minimum distance of 3 mm between the postoperative residual epidural tumor and the spinal cord) to mitigate radiation-induced neurotoxicity from stereotactic radiosurgery [5, 6]. In contrast, cervical metastases pose clinical challenges. Compared with thoracolumbar metastases, the incidence of cervical metastases is lower (8–25%), and the prognosis of patients is worse [7, 8]. Research on the treatment remains limited to case reports or small series studies [9, 10], resulting in comparatively restricted clinical experience. Furthermore, the complex anatomical structure of cervical spine complicates the formulation of surgical strategies and treatment integration. Cervical metastases often cause severe neck pain or limb numbness. If tumor progresses, it may even lead to severe consequences such as high-level paralysis and death [11, 12]. Surgical treatment for cervical metastases tumors is more effective than non-surgical approaches in alleviating pain and helps reverse neurological deficits [13]. However, optimal surgical strategy selection and effective adjuvant therapy integration continue to be debated in current clinical practice.

As one of the largest spinal tumor centers in China, our institution has managed a substantial number of patients with metastatic spinal diseases. This study aims to review and summarize the cases of cervical metastases treated at our center in recent years and analyze the treatment decisions of separation surgery and outcomes of different approaches to provide insights for clinical practice.

Methods

Study population

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (No. M2023797) on December 15, 2023 and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 148 patients with cervical metastases treated surgically between January 1, 2018 and June 30, 2024 were analyzed. Owing to incomplete clinical data or follow-up records, 24 patients were excluded; consequently, final analysis included 124 patients.

All patients underwent X-ray, CT and MRI scans preoperatively and postoperatively. Additionally, PET‒CT was routinely performed in patients suspected of having metastatic tumors. Recorded data included demographic data (gender, age, pathology, tumor location, and preoperative neurological status), operative data (surgical approach, operation time, blood loss, and perioperative complications), and postoperative data (hospital stay, neurological outcomes, postoperative therapy, survival). Planned structural sacrifices, such as nerve root resection for tumor-related purposes, are not considered complications.

The degree of spinal cord compression was graded based on MR images via Epidural Spinal Cord Compression (ESCC) scale [14]. Predicted survival was evaluated via Tomita and Tokuhashi scores [2, 3]. Additionally, we also referred to Tomita score when we classified the growth rate of tumors into three categories: slow growth, intermediate growth, and rapid growth. Preoperative and postoperative functional status were assessed via Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score [15], and neurological function was evaluated via Frankel grade at admission and at discharge [16]. Neck pain was assessed using visual analogue scale (VAS), which ranged from 0 (no pain) to 10 (most extreme pain) [17].

Treatment and surgical strategy

The surgical indications were as follows: (1) cervical metastases confirmed by history, imaging and/or pathology; (2) epidural spinal cord compression with associated neurological impairment; (3) severe damage leading to pathological fracture or spinal instability; (4) estimated survival time not less than 6 months; and (5) absence of apparent surgical contraindications. The assessment of spinal instability referred to the Spinal Instability Neoplastic Scale (SINS) [18], which included tumor location, pain, type of bone destruction, presence of dislocation or deformity, degree of vertebral collapse, and extent of involvement of posterior-lateral structures. The selection of surgical approach and reconstruction method primarily depended on the location of tumor as identified on preoperative CT and MRI scans. Occipitocervical fixation with decompression (OPFD) was performed for atlantoaxial metastases [19–20]. For metastases in lower cervical spine, anterior cervical corpectomy (ACCP) was preferred when only vertebral body was involved. In cases where tumor involved appendices, appropriate approach was posterior cervical fixation with decompression (PCFD). The anterior approach involved vertebral corpectomy, prosthetic implantation, and anterior cervical plate fixation for reconstruction. The posterior approach included laminectomy at the affected level followed by stabilization using either pedicle screws or lateral mass screws coupled with rod systems. During posterior approach, we not only performed laminectomy but also resected the bilateral facet joints, removed tumors within the intervertebral foramen, and decompressed nerve roots. In some cases, excision of tumors located inside vertebral body may also be necessary. When vertebral body and appendices were simultaneously involved, surgical approach is controversial. In our previous work, the choice of surgical approach was primarily based on surgeons’ personal preference and patient’s specific condition. In this study, we explored the advantages and disadvantages of anterior and posterior approaches. Our surgical principle was to maintain a distance of > 3 mm between the tumor and spinal cord to to meet the standards of separation surgery and create space to separate the tumor from neural structures in preparation for subsequent radiotherapy. On postoperative axial and sagittal MRI images, we selected the plane where the distance between the tumor (or resection cavity wall) and the spinal cord was minimal. Along a direction perpendicular to the spinal cord surface, we used the measurement tool in the image workstation to measure the shortest distance three times and took the average. According to the classification by McDonnell et al., complications were categorized as major or minor [21]. Any complication that significantly altered the recovery process and prolongs hospital stay was defined as major complication, while the rest was considered minor. Postoperative treatment was determined by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) after thorough discussion, which included prompt intervention by the rehabilitation department, timely radiotherapy, chemotherapy and other treatments, ensuring that patients received comprehensive and personalized care plans. Ideally, all patients should receive radiotherapy within two to four weeks postoperatively. The primary outcomes encompass neurological recovery, pain improvement, perioperative complications, and survival.

Follow-up

Follow-up was performed regularly every three months during the first year, every six months during the second and third years, and annually thereafter. Overall survival was calculated from the date of surgery to last follow-up or death due to any cause.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented in terms of absolute quantity and frequency. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed to access the normality of distribution. Statistical comparisons of pre- and post-operative data were conducted via two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann‒Whitney U test. Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess the significance of potential predictors of interest. Factors significant at the p < 0.05 level on univariate analysis were included in the Cox proportional hazards multivariate model. P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Time-to-event data were defined as the time interval from the date of surgery to the corresponding event (e.g., death). Kaplan‒Meier survival curve was performed to evaluate overall survival.

Results

Study population

This study included 124 patients, comprising 66 males (53.2%) and 58 females (46.8%), with an average age of 58.8 (22–83) years. Neck pain was the most common symptom (116/124, 93.5%), whereas 58 patients (46.8%) had neurological deficits. The average time from symptom onset to surgery was 3.6 ± 4.0 months (1 week to 2 years). The average Tomita and Tokuhashi scores were 6.0 ± 2.3 (1–10) and 7.9 ± 3.1 (0–14), respectively. Regarding preoperative functional status, 22 patients (17.7%) were unable to live independently (KPS 0–40), 49 patients (39.5%) could perform daily activity but were unable to maintain normal work and life (KPS 50–70), and 53 patients (42.7%) were capable of performing normal activity (KPS 80–100). Sixty-six patients (53.2%) exhibited no significant neurological deficit (Frankel E), while 58 patients (46.8%) demonstrated varying degrees of neurological impairment, including 44 patients with Frankel D (35.5%), 9 patients with Frankel C (7.3%), and 5 patients with Frankel B (4.0%). None was completely paralyzed before surgery (Frankel A) (Table 1).

Patient demographics (n = 124)

Primary tumor and cervical metastasis conditions

The primary histological types were lung (n = 40, 32.3%), breast (n = 20, 16.1%), and kidney (n = 18, 14.5%) (Fig. 1). Twenty-three patients (18.5%) were unaware of the presence of primary tumor before admission, and symptoms of cervical metastases were the first clinical manifestations. Among 101 cases with confirmed primary tumors, 58 (57.4%) had undergone surgery before admission. The affected regions were as follows: only upper cervical spine in 23 patients (18.5%), only lower cervical spine in 89 patients (71.8%), and both upper and lower cervical spine in 12 patients (9.7%). Single segment involvement was observed in 80 cases (64.5%), while two segments and multi-segment involvement accounted for 24 cases (19.4%) and 20 cases (16.1%), respectively. Regarding the degree of spinal cord compression, the most frequent grade was ESCC 1c (41 cases, 33.1%), followed by ESCC 2 (31 cases, 25.0%), ESCC 3 (19 cases, 15.3%), ESCC 0 (13 cases, 10.5%), ESCC 1a (12 cases, 9.7%), and ESCC 1b (8 cases, 6.5%).

Surgical approaches

All surgeries were completed successfully after strict pre-operative assessment. Twenty-three patients (18.5%) with upper cervical spine involvement underwent posterior OPFD. For lower cervical metastases, 47 patients (37.9%) and 42 patients (33.9%) received ACCP and PCFD, respectively. Posterior approach was also performed in 12 patients (9.7%) with both upper and lower cervical spine involvement. No significant intraoperative complications were observed. The average operation time was 123 ± 57 (46–363) minutes, and the average blood loss volume was 287 ± 260 (50–1000) ml. After surgery, no patients exhibited neurologic deterioration, and their pain was alleviated to varying degrees. No perioperative death was observed. The average hospital stay was 7.4 ± 4.1 (2–30) days. Postoperative MRI revealed that 97 of 124 patients (78.2%) achieved a circumferential decompression of > 3 mm between the tumor and spinal cord, with an average of 4.72 ± 0.67 mm.

Perioperative complications

Thirty-three patients (26.6%) experienced perioperative complications, including 8 cases of major complications and 27 cases of minor complications. Two patients (1.6%) experienced cerebrospinal leakage and recovered with bed rest and hypertonic saline infusion. Three patients (2.4%) developed impaired wound healing, which resolved after debridement. Four patients (3.2%) developed unexplained fever, and treated with intravenous empiric antibiotics and hydration treatment. One patient (0.8%) developed dyspnea due to tracheal edema and immediately received to emergency tracheotomy. One patient (0.8%) experienced dysphagia, 1 patient (0.8%) experienced both dysphagia and dysphonia, and 2 patients (1.6%) developed liquid aspiration, and all resolved without special intervention. 1 patient (0.8%) experienced gastrointestinal hemorrhage and underwent emergency arterial embolization for hemostasis. Hypokalemia was observed in 2 patients (1.6%), hypocalcemia in 1 patient (0.8%), nausea and vomiting in 4 patients (3.2%), urinary tract infection in 2 patients (1.6%), muscle weakness due to neuroedema in 3 patients (2.4%), headache in 1 patient (0.8%), laryngeal edema in 3 patients (1.6%), and delirium in 2 patients (1.6%), all of whom improved with symptomatic therapy. Additionally, 1 patient (0.8%) experienced internal fixation failure one year after surgery but had no symptoms and is under observation with regular follow-up. In the three comparisons, there was no significant difference in the rates of major or minor complications between anterior and posterior approaches (p > 0.05).

Comprehensive therapy

At discharge, 76 patients showed no neurological impairment. Frankel D, C, and B accounted for 40, 5 and 3 cases respectively. The average KPS score was 79, indicating significant improvement in functional status (p < 0.05). Based on clinical principles and MDT discussions, all patients were recommended to receive comprehensive therapy after surgery. However, owing to factors such as patient’s financial condition, compliance, and/or family decisions, 88 patients (71.0%) received conventional external-beam radiotherapy (EBRT) or stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) at a dose of 40–50 Gy, meeting the requirements of separation surgery. Eighty-two patients (66.1%) received targeted therapy, chemotherapy, or other tumor-related drug therapies. Sixty-four patients (51.6%) received both radiotherapy and systemic therapy.

Follow-up

The average follow-up time was 21 ± 17 (6–84) months. At final follow-up, 61 patients were alive, and 63 had died. The average KPS score of the surviving patients was 90.2 ± 9.2 (60–100). With respect to neurological function, 47 patients had no neurological impairment (Frankel E), and 14 were classified as Frankel D. The Kaplan‒Meier survival curve demonstrated that overall survival rates at 1, 2, and 3 years after surgery were 79.5%, 60.9%, and 48.5%, respectively (Fig. 2).

Outcomes

To analyze the outcomes of different surgical approaches for lower cervical metastases, we conducted three comparative studies (Comparisons One, Two, and Three) (Tables 2, 3 and 4). No significant differences were observed in terms of patient age, gender, tumor grade, prognostic scores, Frankel grade, recurrence conditions, perioperative complications, and so on between patients receiving ACCP or PCFD.

When the KPS was categorized into three segments (0–40, 50–70, and 80–100), the preoperative KPS of patients who underwent posterior approach was lower than that of those receiving anterior approach in three comparisons (p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences in postoperative KPS were observed. No matter which approach, significant improvement was found in postoperative KPS compared to preoperative assessment (p < 0.05). In Comparison Two, patients who underwent posterior approach had poorer preoperative Frankel grades (p = 0.009), but showed apparent improvement postoperatively, with no significant differences observed.

Regarding long-term tumor control, four cases of local recurrence were observed, with two cases each occurring after anterior and posterior approaches. The time intervals from surgery to recurrence were 5 months (anterior), 23 months (anterior), 12 months (posterior), and 46 months (posterior), respectively. Due to the limited number of recurrence cases, no statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between surgical approach and recurrence. Anterior approach had significantly shorter operation time and less blood loss (p < 0.001).

Not all patients received comprehensive therapy postoperatively, but there was no significant difference in the administration of radiotherapy or systemic therapy among patients with different surgical approaches. Postoperative pain level has significantly improved, and the VAS scores of patients who underwent posterior approach decreased more significantly (p < 0.05). At final follow-up, there was no significant difference in the proportion of surviving patients or their KPS scores.

Among 51 patients (41.1%) with simultaneous involvement of the vertebral body and appendices, 20 patients underwent ACCP, and else underwent PCFD. Preoperative and postoperative functional status of the two groups were not significantly different (Table 4). All patients showed improvement in neurological function or no deterioration upon discharge. Two patients who underwent PCFD had no motor function at admission (Frankel B), but both showed improvement postoperatively (Frankel C and D, respectively). Patients who underwent posterior approach showed a more significant improvement in neck pain (2.06 ± 1.11 vs. 1.45 ± 0.83, p = 0.038). These phenomena were similarly observed when analyzing patients with tumor involvement confined to the vertebral body and those with involvement of both the vertebral body and appendices (Table 3). The same was true when all patients with lower cervical metastases were compared (Table 2). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves of the three comparisons were shown in Fig. 3. Case presentation

Case 1: Occipitocervical fixation with decompression

This was a case of invasive ductal carcinoma of the breast that metastasized to the C2 vertebra. The patient was a 50-year-old woman who had experienced limited cervical range of motion for 6 months and neck pain for 2 months without apparent cause. Imaging examination revealed a space-occupying lesion and pathological fracture in the C2 vertebra (Fig. 4). Tomita and Tokuhashi scores were 7 and 11, respectively. Preoperative Frankel grade was E, and ESCC grade was 1c. In November 2021, OPFD was performed, with operation time of 115 min and blood loss of 200 ml. She had no significant complications after surgery and received radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and endocrine treatment. She died in December 2023 due to advanced tumor spread, with overall survival of 25 months.

Preoperative imaging suggested osteolytic bone destruction of the C2 and its attachments, with a surrounding soft tissue mass, part of which protruded into the spinal canal, causing secondary spinal stenosis (A, B, C). After posterior cervical laminectomy with decompression, and occipitocervical fixation, the X-ray showed proper positioning of the internal fixation device (D). Postoperative CT indicated complete decompression of the spinal canal (E). Postoperative pathology confirmed breast cancer spinal metastasis

Case 2: Anterior cervical corpectomy

A 41-year-old female patient presented with neck and left upper limb pain for 2 months. Gallbladder adenocarcinoma metastasized to the C4 vertebra, leading to destructive lesions. The patient had no neurological deficits (Frankel E) and was able to take care of herself (KPS 80). Both Tomita and Tokuhashi scores were 7. Preoperative imaging revealed osteolytic metastasis at the C4 vertebra with vertebral pathological fracture (Fig. 5). In March 2018, she underwent ACCP with 3D-printed artificial vertebral body reconstruction. The surgery lasted 50 min, with blood loss of 80 ml. No perioperative complications were observed, and postoperative muscle strength remained normal (Frankel E). She received radiotherapy and chemotherapy but succumbed to multiple systemic metastases in October 2020.

Preoperative X-ray suggested bone destruction in the C4 and local low-density shadows (A), and CT and MRI showed mild compression, bone destruction, and pathological fracture of the C4 (B, C). After anterior cervical C4 vertebrectomy, tumor curettage, and 3D-printed artificial prosthesis implantation, the X-ray indicated proper positioning of the internal fixation device (D). CT scan showed satisfactory resection of vertebral lesions and complete decompression of the spinal canal (E). Postoperative pathology confirmed that the primary tumor was gallbladder adenocarcinoma

Case 3: Posterior cervical fixation with decompression

A 66-year-old male who underwent subtotal nephrectomy for renal clear cell carcinoma 7 years prior presented with right neck and shoulder pain for 2 months. The patient had neurological deficits (Frankel D), and KPS score was 70. Tomita and Tokuhashi scores were 4 and 8, respectively. Preoperative imaging revealed an osteolytic metastatic tumor at C6 with vertebral pathological fracture and spinal cord compression (ESCC 1c) (Fig. 6). In June 2021, he underwent PCFD, with fixation extending from C4 to T1. The surgery lasted 105 min, with blood loss of 500 ml. No perioperative complications were documented. Postoperatively, he actively received radiotherapy and chemotherapy. At last follow-up, the patient was still alive, with normal neurological function (Frankel E), KPS score of 90, and no signs of tumor recurrence.

Preoperative X-ray showed no significant bone destruction (A), while CT showed expansive osteolytic bone destruction of the C6 spinous process and right-sided vertebral arch root, with uneven density and slight posterior convexity, leading to spinal canal narrowing (B). MRI showed signal changes in the C6, significant bone destruction of the attachments, and soft tissue mass protruding into the spinal canal, causing secondary spinal stenosis (C). Posterior tumor resection from the C6 wall, spinal canal decompression and fixation were performed. The internal fixation device was well-positioned (D), and the spinal canal decompression was adequate (E). Postoperative pathology confirmed metastatic renal clear cell carcinoma

Discussion

In recent years, treatment strategies for metastatic spinal tumors have significantly evolved. Compared to surgical resection, relatively conservative treatment used to be the main option for treating cervical metastases, primarily due to limited life expectancy, high surgical risks, and the inadequacy of existing techniques [9]. In 2013, Laufer et al. [4] performed epidural tumor circumferential decompression followed by postoperative radiotherapy on 186 patients with spinal metastases and first introduced the concept of “separation surgery”, which was a technique that separated the anterior sulcus in the spinal canal from the posterior vertebral body [22]. Separation surgery did not aim to achieve complete tumor resection, instead, it focused on reducing compression and preserving as much neurological function as possible. Through limited posterolateral tumor resection and posterior segmental fixation to decompress the dural sac, this approach not only alleviated dural compression but also restored the cerebrospinal space around the spinal cord and established a radiation dose gradient zone. In combination with radiotherapy, it can achieve long-term local control, neurological function improvement and pain relief, which was often used for spinal cord decompression and spinal stability reconstruction in patients with spinal metastasis [13]. After establishing a safe gap, radiation-sensitive tumors can be treated with conventional radiotherapy, whereas SBRT can be safely applied to severe ESCC cases caused by radiation-insensitive tumors [23].

At present, some studies have described the application of separation surgery in the treatment of thoracolumbar tumors. Patients who underwent separation surgery experienced less blood loss, fewer complications, and shorter operation time [24–31]. However, owing to the complex anatomical structure surrounding cervical spine and relatively low incidence in this region, studies on surgical strategies for cervical metastases were limited. OPFD for upper cervical metastases has been widely accepted [32–34], but treatment options for lower cervical metastases remain controversial, particularly regarding surgical approaches. Although the Weinstein-Boriani-Biagini (WBB) staging system is specific to primary spinal tumors, we refer to this principle for classification and surgical decision-making to achieve favorable outcomes. For tumors involving vertebral body, ACCP is performed; when appendices are involved, the patients receive PCFD. When both vertebral body and appendices are affected, the choice of approach depends primarily on surgeon and patient’s specific condition. At our center, we prefer posterior approach to relieve nerve compression.

This study evaluated perioperative and oncological outcomes of cervical metastases treated with different surgical approaches. We hoped that these analyses could provide references for decision-making in the surgical treatment of this unique and complex type of metastasis. The results of this study revealed that when tumors simultaneously involved vertebral body and appendices, patients who underwent posterior approach had significantly longer survival (29.4 ± 14.9 vs. 19.7 ± 12.5, p = 0.028). Kaplan-Meier survival curves also showed this phenomenon (Fig. 3). It was worth noting that there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of preoperative baseline characteristics (such as age, prognostic score, tumor grade), perioperative complications, or postoperative comprehensive treatments. Moreover, postoperative functional status of all patients improved or did not deteriorate. While Cho et al. [35] found that factors such as the growth rate of primary tumor, Tomita score, radiotherapy, the timing of radiotherapy, and postoperative adjuvant therapy were associated with longer survival. Our univariate Cox regression analysis also observed some similar results, while the multivariate Cox analysis showed that the surgical approach and multiple bone metastases were independent risk factors affecting prognosis. Posterior approach provides more extensive decompression of the spinal canal, especially in cases of multi-segmental or extensive tumor involvement. There is a lower likelihood of pulling and damaging structures such as blood vessels, nerves, and the esophagus in the neck, making the procedure relatively less invasive, reducing surgical trauma and being more conducive to patients receiving comprehensive therapy. Additionally, during ACCP, a titanium plate needs to be placed anterior to the vertebral body, which may not only obscure surgical field but also pose a risk of esophageal fistula after radiotherapy due to friction against the esophagus. Although previous literature has reported that tumors located at the C3-C7 levels and prolonged operative time may increase the risk of neurological dysfunction, such an association was not observed in our study [36].

Comparative analysis based on KPS categorization (0–40, 50–70, and 80–100) revealed that patients who underwent posterior approach demonstrated consistently worse preoperative KPS scores compared to the anterior approach group, however, apparent postoperative improvement was observed in these patients, with no statistically significant differences observed. Additionally, patients who underwent posterior approach had worse overall Frankel grades at baseline, but there was also no significant difference in postoperative outcomes. The overall incidence of perioperative complications was also similar to that reported in previous studies [37, 38]. Chanbour et al. [39] reported a higher rate of new neurological deficits associated with anterior approach, which was not observed in our study. Luksanapruksa et al. [40] also found no significant differences in perioperative complications between anterior and posterior approaches. Functional status at last follow-up was also similar among surviving patients.

When we included patients with only vertebral body involvement who underwent ACCP in comparison, the result was also observed (Table 3). The same was true when all patients with lower cervical metastases were compared (Table 2). We presumed that differences in survival outcomes are likely attributable to unrecognized additional benefits conferred by surgical approach itself. Posterior approach allows for more extensive laminectomy and resection of accessory structures, theoretically enabling a more thorough removal of tumor-infiltrated soft tissues (e.g., muscles and ligaments), thus reducing the risk of microscopic residual lesions. In contrast, anterior approach offers limited capability to manage lesions in the posterior elements following vertebral body resection. Anterior surgical field is constrained, with maneuvers performed in close proximity to critical structures such as the esophagus, trachea, and major blood vessels. During resection of tumor-involved vertebral bodies, this approach may elevate the risk of tumor cell shedding and local implantation. Conversely, posterior surgical field is relatively “clean” and benefits from more abundant soft tissue coverage, which may help mitigate such risks. However, it was undeniable that ACCP had a shorter operation time, less blood loss, less trauma, and enabled direct resection of vertebral body–based metastases, achieving thorough ventral decompression of spinal cord, and allowed immediate reconstruction of anterior load-bearing column with structural grafts or cages—thereby restoring cervical height, preserving lordosis, and reducing the risk of postoperative kyphotic deformity [41, 42]

. In our study, local recurrence occurred in two patients from each surgical approach group. Some patients who might have developed recurrence were not observed due to poor overall prognosis. In the study by Chanbour et al. [39], neither the local recurrence rate nor time interval from surgery to recurrence demonstrated a significant association with the surgical approach. However, recurrence is only one factor influencing prognosis, which is also influenced by patient’s systemic factors. Other potential contributing factors may not have been observed, which represents a limitation of our study. Postoperative radiotherapy is the component of comprehensive therapy. Posterior approach, with its thicker muscular flap coverage, may allow for earlier initiation of radiotherapy or tolerance of higher radiation doses, while posing comparatively lower risks to the esophagus and trachea. Aggressive and effective radiotherapy further enhances local control outcomes [43–45]. Furthermore, mortality in patients with spinal metastases often results not directly from the local tumor, but from systemic wasting and multi-organ failure [46, 47]. Pain is a major contributor to this wasting process [48]. The continuous expansion of a tumor can stretch the periosteum, resulting in persistent local pain and nerve root compression, which leads to radicular pain. Axial pain is associated with pathological vertebral fractures that cause spinal instability [49]. Surgical intervention can reduce tumor size and stabilize spine, thus alleviating pain. Cho et al. [35] analyzed the outcomes of 46 patients who underwent surgical treatment for cervical metastases, and they found that pain relief was observed in 37 out of 44 patients (84.1%). The average preoperative VAS score decreased significantly from 7.86 ± 1.05 to 4.48 ± 2.09 postoperatively, with an average reduction of 3.39 ± 2.14 (p = 0.001). Gallazzi et al. [50] reported clinical outcomes of 30 patients with cervical metastases treated with posterior laminectomy combined with stabilization. Their results demonstrated a significant improvement in pain scores (from 5.5 ± 1.8 to 2.1 ± 1.0, p < 0.01). In our study, neck pain showed significant improvement regardless of whether patients underwent anterior or posterior approach. However, patients who underwent posterior approach had significantly lower VAS scores postoperatively (p < 0.05). For example, when tumors simultaneously involved vertebral body and appendices, postoperative VAS scores for patients who underwent anterior and posterior approaches were 2.06 ± 1.11 and 1.45 ± 0.83, respectively (p = 0.038). We proposed that PCFD offered superior stability and decompression, and its long-segment posterior fixation was highly effective at eliminating abnormal spinal micro-movements, thus alleviating mechanical pain. Luksanapruksa et al. [42] also demonstrated that extensive decompression combined with robust stabilization contributes to improved pain management in patients.

The findings in Chanbour et al.‘s study [39] differed from ours. They observed similar functional outcomes at last follow-up among patients, but no differences in overall survival or one-year survival rates. They concluded that outcomes and long-term tumor control were comparable across different surgical approaches, and either approach is acceptable for managing cervical metastases. The divergence between our observation of improved overall survival with posterior separation surgery and the equivalent oncologic outcomes reported by Chanbour et al. warrants a nuanced interpretation grounded in differences in case selection philosophy, procedural execution, and interplay between surgical decompression and adjuvant therapy. A distinction lies in how surgical approach was linked to tumor anatomy. In our practice, PCFD was indicated for patients with radiographic involvement of appendices. This anatomy-driven algorithm could ensure posterior strategy is applied to cases where disease extends into the posterior compartment—a pattern that inherently carries higher risk of circumferential cord compression and often necessitates more extensive bony resection. In their study, patients were grouped according to the surgical approach received. 13 patients with tumors only involving vertebral body underwent posterior surgery, therefore, it may be hard to assess the independent impact of tumor location and surgical approach on survival, whereas in our study, all such patients received anterior surgery. Consistent with this individualized decision-making, their posterior-alone cohort exhibited a higher proportion of patients with Frankel D preoperatively (24% vs. 9% in anterior group), suggesting that posterior approaches may have been preferentially employed in cases with more advanced neurologic compromise—thereby introducing a prognostic imbalance that could mask intrinsic benefit of the technique. our posterior procedure routinely includes resection of involved facet joints and clearance of foraminal disease when indicated by imaging. SBRT was administered to patients with radiosensitive histologies (e.g., renal cell carcinoma) in whom adequate dural separation was achieved; conventional radiotherapy was used for other patients. Chanbour et al. described their posterior approach as “laminectomy and instrumented fusion”, while they did not report whether resection of posterior bony elements beyond the lamina was performed. Most patients received adjuvant radiotherapy but did not specify modality, dose, or timing. These differences may represent contextual factors that may influence the measurable association between surgical approach and survival, but it should be acknowledged that the above analysis did not represent the causal relationship of differences. Future prospective studies that prospectively capture tumor location, extent of bony resection, and details of adjuvant therapy may help clarify which patients benefit most from each strategy.

However, in a systematic review, Fehlings et al. [51] discussed the selection of surgical approaches when different regions of cervical spine are affected. They proposed that when tumor involved the C3-C6 vertebrae, anterior approach is the preferred in most cases, while at the cervical-thoracic junction (C7-T1), either anterior or posterior approach can be employed for decompression. These different results underscored the importance of careful patient selection and the necessity of personalized surgical strategies to optimize or maintain quality of life in patients with cervical metastases. It should be noted, however, that the surgical techniques described across these studies were not entirely consistent. For instance, the type of graft and fusion status are associated with improvements in patient-reported outcomes, as well as a reduction in the incidence of complications, revisions, and adverse events [52]. This represents a potential area for further research. It must be acknowledged that these are observational conclusions drawn from retrospective studies. Analyzing potential causes based on such conclusions is not entirely accurate. Ultimately, these findings require validation through more rigorous prospective studies with fewer confounding factors. With a larger patient cohort and more standardized stratification along with multivariate analysis, the results may be different. Further research is necessary to better elucidate the causal relationship between surgical approach and patient outcomes. We proposed that, under an anatomy-driven selection framework and with comprehensive decompression enabling precision radiotherapy in eligible patients, posterior separation was associated with improved outcomes.

During surgery, we decompressed nerves and separated tumor in specific areas to meet the requirements for separation surgery. The primary objective of separation surgery is to relieve nerve compression and restore spinal stability. Compared with traditional decompression surgery, separation surgery can achieve more complete circumferential decompression. Currently, there is no consensus on the optimal distance between the spinal cord and the tumor postoperatively. BARZILAI et al. [53] suggested that the distance between the epidural tumor and the spinal cord should be 2 to 3 mm. MELEIS et al. [54] proposed that a distance of > 3–5 mm may provide a better prognosis, whereas JAKUBOVIC et al. argued that the optimal distance should be 6 mm to achieve ideal local tumor control. Gong et al. [55] conducted a retrospective study of 36 patients with spinal metastases who underwent separation surgery followed by radiotherapy. The results revealed that all 5 patients who experienced local progression had a distance of < 3 mm between the epidural tumor and the spinal cord. There was a significant difference in 1-year local tumor recurrence rate between patients with a distance of < 3 mm and those with a distance > 3 mm (48.1% vs. 0%, p = 0.053). In our cases, the average separation distance was maintained at > 3 mm, which met the distance requirement for radiotherapy. This technique reduces surgical trauma and is more conducive to patients receiving comprehensive therapy.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study without a pre-specified protocol for patient selection, treatment allocation, or follow-up, making it susceptible to selection and information bias. Crucially, surgical approach, including posterior circumferential decompression, was determined by clinical judgment rather than randomization, which may have introduced imbalances in unmeasured prognostic factors (e.g., systemic disease burden or tumor biology). Therefore, the observed survival difference should be considered hypothesis-generating and not indicative of a causal effect. Second, the sample size was limited due to the low incidence of surgically eligible cervical metastases. Consequently, we report demographic and functional data for the entire cohort without stratification by surgical indication, tumor location, or ESCC grade, and we did not perform multivariate regression analysis to adjust for potential confounders. Additionally, although separation surgery aimed to achieve adequate dural clearance for radiotherapy, postoperative imaging was not routinely used to objectively verify the extent of decompression. Given these constraints, particularly the non-randomized design and potential residual confounding, prospective validation in a multicenter setting, ideally through a randomized trial or a standardized prospective cohort, is needed to determine whether posterior separation surgery confers a true survival benefit.

Conclusions

For patients with cervical metastases, treatment plans are developed based on the specific location of tumor and whether they meet the criteria for separation surgery. Although there were no significant differences in postoperative therapy, patients who underwent posterior approach showed better clinical outcomes, including more significant pain relief and longer survival. When vertebral body and appendices are involved simultaneously, posterior approach is generally preferred to achieve nerve decompression. This strategy achieves satisfactory outcomes and facilitates patients’ subsequent comprehensive therapy.
