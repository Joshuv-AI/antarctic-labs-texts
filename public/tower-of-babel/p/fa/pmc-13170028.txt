A comparative analysis of topological domain callers over RNA-associated interactome
Authors: Tuna Alaygut, Emre Sefer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTopological domains (TADs) are consecutive genomic locations with denser local interactions to a certain extent, and they are important for cellular gene expression control and modulation. TADs were first identified when studying three-dimensional genomic structures over Hi-C interaction datasets. Many studies have focused on developing approaches in inferring TADs, which led to multiple TAD-caller approaches development. On the other hand, the number of RNA interactome datasets, such as RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interactions, has recently been increasing. Even though TADs have been extensively studied in Hi-C datasets, they have not been studied across these RNA interactomes.ResultWe conducted a systematized comparison of 28 TAD-callers across mammalians over RNA-associated interactomes (RAIs), especially RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interaction datasets at a high resolution. Our findings highlighted the significant enrichment of Cohesin/CTCF proteins at RNA-TAD boundaries for both RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interactomes, especially those with corner dots, which are similar to the results for original TADs. The sizes and numbers of RNA-TADs vary significantly between different TAD caller approaches and RNA interactome resolution, suggesting the importance of considering RNA-TADs as hierarchical domains rather than distinct intervals.ConclusionWe examined the core principles and assumptions behind TAD-callers over RNA interactomes. To our best knowledge, this is the first time that many TAD inference methods are adapted to infer TAD-like domains on RNAs. Our results provide valuable guidance in selecting the most suitable methods for TAD inference over RNA interactomes.Keywords: RNA-Associated Interactions, RNA-RNA Interactome, RNA-DNA Interactome, Topological domains

Introduction

Chromosomes store both genetic and epigenetic information within cells and have a complex 3D structure that is intricately folded into a small spatial space. The 3D conformation of chromosomes is crucial for genomic organization, and it is also important for regulating DNA replication as well as transcription, which is demonstrated via multiple up-to-date studies. To detect the interaction count quantitatively among the whole pairs of genomic positions over the genome, sequencing-based High-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) technologies have been introduced. Those Hi-C experimental approaches are proximity ligation techniques that are based on chromatin proximity. Those techniques are used in studying multiple species genomic structures, including yeast, Drosophila, plants, and mammals.

There are multiple types of chromosome intervals and areas that are inferred over those Hi-C datasets, which are interacting significantly more with one another [1]. These chromosome parts are high-order regions, such as chromosome territories, A/B compartments, and topologically associating domains (TADs). Those chromosome parts have distinct sizes, and each of them is correlated with the genome’s distinct biomolecular attributes. Moreover, these compartments are often smaller than CTCF loop domains, can form their own domains, and indeed can often be subgenic, a few kilobases, or segregate loci inside CTCF loops [2–4]. For instance, TADs are large chromosomal organization units where the same domain DNA sequences interact higher than the DNA sequences over neighbouring topological domains [5–9]. Size of a TAD is usually within megabases. As a key feature, TADs are demarcated by their boundaries. Even though they are also generally preserved remarkably and in a stable way across distinct sets of species and cell lines, recent investigations on single-cell Hi-C reveal major variations in topological domains. Topological domains are critical in terms of biochemical and genetic processes, where they are associated with genetic diseases as well as being stable as part of cellular differentiation and development. Even though there is still a debate going over the relationship between topological domains and gene expression differences, topological domains are connected to cellular development [10, 11]. As a result, accurate topologically associating domain inference is important in analyzing the association between the three-dimensional organization of the chromatin and biocellular functions.

Recent detailed analysis of TADs has revealed subTADs, which are less-than one megabase chromatin domains [12–16]. In mammals, those subTADs are encapsulated inside TADS in a hierarchical way. Those subTADs cannot be identified in lower resolution interaction dataset, but can only be detected on higher resolution interaction datasets [17–20]. SubTADs have a similar structure to TADs since their boundaries also separate them apart. One difference is that subTAD boundaries insulate signals more strongly, so attenuation of longer-distance interactions between domains is less common. Another difference is that dynamic folding behaviour of subTADs change more frequently according to cell types.

RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interactomes refer to the complex networks of interactions between RNA molecules and between RNA and DNA within a cell. RNA-RNA interactomes involve the interactions between different RNA species, such as mRNA, rRNA, tRNA, and various non-coding RNAs, which play crucial roles in regulating gene expression, RNA stability, and the formation of RNA-protein complexes. These interactions are essential for processes like RNA interference, splicing, and translation regulation. On the other hand, RNA-DNA interactomes encompass the interactions between RNA molecules and DNA, which are vital for gene regulation and chromatin organization. This includes mechanisms like RNA-mediated transcriptional regulation, where non-coding RNAs can guide proteins to specific DNA sequences, influencing gene expression and epigenetic modifications. Together, these interactomes contribute to the dynamic regulation of cellular processes, ensuring the proper function and adaptation of cells to various conditions. Physical associations of RNAs with distinct RNAs and chromatin exist primarily by 2 processes [21–26]. Via cis interactions, interactions between newly-transcribed RNAs and nascent RNAs and chromatin are observed while these RNAs are remaining in their template DNA sequence locations. Once they no longer belong to the transcription site, interactions between RNAs and other RNAs or particular genome loci occur via RNA. These trans-interactions may also be formed by mediating proteins or hybrid formation with DNA. Various molecular interaction mechanisms are part of these RNA-associated interactions (RAIs), such as formation of fusion transcripts in high-frequency interaction regions [27], regulation of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) [23, 25, 26, 28], and methylation of DNA [29]

Up to date biological experiments could detect set of all interactions between DNA and RNA at a genome scale [30]. Some examples are RADICL-seq [31], iMARGI [32], ChAR-seq [33], GRID-seq [28], and MARGI [34]. In general, those experimental techniques detect the chimeric fragments between RNAs that are associated with chromatin and their close genome sequences in 3D space. Those techniques could deal with both non-coding RNAs and coding RNAs [35]. As a result, they contribute a broader and genome-wide perspective of interactions between RNA and chromatin. Moreover, other techniques have been introduced in inferring spatially close associations among distinct RNAs via mediating RNA-binding protein and chromatin [24, 36]. In this study, we refer the whole set of RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interactions together as RNA-associated interactome.

According to a number of studies, there is an association between three-dimensional chromatin structures and RNA-associated interactome, particularly topological domains of chromatin structure [27, 28, 33]. Topological domains have a hierarchy where neighbouring domains can be involved in forming another topological domain. According to [28], there exists a higher harmony and concord between Hi-C data and GRID-seq in S2 cells and mouse embryonic stem cells. Moreover, confinement of RNA-TADs are mainly inside TADs. According to [26], the formation of topological domains could be prevented via long non-coding RNA Xist by the impact of Cohesin and positive chromatin factors. The statistical enrichment of RNA-DNA interactions DNA tags across topological domain boundaries have been observed in [31]. Since the effect of transcription is constrained by topological domain boundaries, RNA migration’s faster diffusion is also prevented by topological domains. Overall, RNA-associated interactome’s impact on RNA regulation and generation is observed by strong evidence. On the other hand, the comprehensive analysis of RNA-associated topologically associated domains (RNA-TADs) is missing and challenging.

In terms of TAD prediction, [5] has initially proposed Directionality Index (DI) technique. Until then, topological domain prediction has received notable attention, and it has become a favourable topic. A number of computational TAD calling methods have been introduced to infer those topological domains. Together with the development of these approaches, a number of experimental Hi-C approaches have been introduced, which became a critical component in TAD inference. In this case, a comprehensive comparison of numerous TAD calling methods has become important to speed up enhancements in topological domain inference. Up to date, we have identified 31 TAD calling methods where the summarization of those methods and their relative attributes are presented in Table 1.

Here, we make a comprehensive evaluation and comparison of 28 TAD identification methods by using high-resolution experimental RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interaction datasets across mammals. Through this comparison, we assess the methods along with their biostatistical features. Three strategies are employed in our comparison: comparing RNA-based topological domains with inferred Hi-C topological domains, evaluating the consistency among multiple replicate experiments by using the identical TAD calling method, and comparing different TAD inference methods on the identical interaction datasets. The performance of the TAD calling methods are evaluated by using six evaluation criteria, including the Jaccard Index (JI), p-value () ratio, fold change, boundary tagged ratio, average peak, TAD adjacency, false positive rate, and true positive rate. The analysis focuses on three main criteria: the reproducibility of RNA-TADs and RNA-TAD boundaries across multiple RNA interactome experiments, the functional elements/factors enrichment and depletion adjacent to RNA-TAD boundaries, and the decay of interaction counts with increasing genomic interval. Additionally, we emphasize the quality of TAD caller implementations. The key contributions of our research are to come up with novel and the first overview of the current TAD identification methods across RNA interactomes, highlighting the dynamics of various topological domain inference approaches and identifying possible limitations in topological domain detection across RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interaction datasets.

From RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interactomes perspective, this research is the first benchmarking study and discusses the reproducibility and robustness of results, providing valuable guidelines for designing molecular and computational RNA studies. In addition to being the first study to analyze TADs in depth across RNA interactomes, our research also makes several extra benefits: 1- TAD inference methods are partitioned into 3 clusters of algorithms: Attribute-based methods, Community-based methods, and Network-partitioning methods, where attribute-based methods generally performed better than the other two types of algorithms in domain identification over RNA interactomes. 2- We evaluate TAD callers using recent higher-resolution RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA datasets. 3- Our evaluation extends across mammals rather than being limited to humans. 4- The performance of topological domain inference methods has been assessed in identifying both corner-dot domains (similar extrusion loops in DNA domains) and topologically associating domains without corner dots, which have been previously identified in TADs over Hi-C interaction datasets. 5- Finally, topological domain inference methods are evaluated by using 8 distinct qualities. Overall, we provide researchers who will be using RNA-TADs, a detailed roadmap to choose the best domain inference method according to their needs as well as the considered data.

Materials and methods

TAD callers in Hi-C

Hi-C datasets result in the number of interactions between genomic positions over the whole genome. After binning at a certain resolution, Hi-C experiments generate interaction matrix, where the value in (i, j)’th entry defines number of interactions between bins i and j. This matrix is symmetric as well. Topological domains are the most common structures which are inferred over these genome-wide Hi-C datasets. Practically, TAD-calling methods identify topological domains by extracting square submatrix with their diagonal regions having significantly higher number of interactions. More recent approaches can infer topological domains hierarchically by recognizing the hierarchical nature of topological domains, which cannot be done by older approaches. Even though Hi-C studies up to now have made considerable progress in our understanding of TADs, exact topological domain definition does not exist where the definition differs according to domain size, domain count, and hierarchy. In Hi-C domain identification, there is a quite limited number of benchmarks and prediction performance quality is not assessed directly but via making association between domains and biological features.

TAD callers in RNA interactomes

Similarly, in RNA interactomes such RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interaction datasets, we have interaction matrix, where n is the number of loci after binning at a certain resolution, the matrix is symmetric, and the value in (i, j)’th entry defines number of interactions between bins i and j. For instance, if we form the interaction matrix by binning at 100 kilobase (kb) resolution for a given chromosome, segment 0-100000 corresponds to the first loci in the matrix, whereas 100000-200000 corresponds to the second loci, etc. Even though it has not been studied before in detail, topological domains are potentially among the most frequent densely-interacting structures in RNA interaction matrices similar to Hi-C matrices [67].

As discussed previously, even though the studies up to now have made considerable progress in our understanding of TADs, an exact topological domain definition does not exist, where the definition differs according to domain size, domain count, and hierarchy. In common, TADs are highly self-interacting contiguous chromosome parts. In summary, we will be partitioning the existing topological domain inference methods into 3 groups, as also discussed in [68, 69]: 1- Attribute-based methods, 2- Community-based methods, and 3- Network partitioning-based methods. Attribute-based approaches will utilize the patterns extracted from the interaction dataset via optimization-based, dynamic programming-based, and statistical filtering-based methods. Community-based methods will focus on grouping contiguous genome parts together into clusters which will potentially become topological domains. These techniques will focus on distance or similarity values between segments to model dependencies. Our last group, which is network partitioning-based methods, will model a symmetric interaction matrix as an undirected weighted graph, and will focus on decomposing the graph into smaller subgraphs via network partitioning methods, where the subgraphs will correspond to topological domains.

RNA-DNA interaction matrices are intrinsically asymmetric, with rows and columns corresponding to RNA genomic origins and DNA loci, respectively. For TAD callers that require symmetric inputs, we constructed a symmetric interaction matrix by explicitly adding the transpose of the RNA-DNA matrix:

SuperTLD

[67] formulates RNA–chromatin interaction data as a 1D genomic signal analogous to Hi-C contact matrices, but asymmetric and RNA-centric. Starting from RNA–DNA proximity ligation assays (e.g., iMARGI [32]), they construct RNA–chromatin interaction matrices where rows correspond to RNA genomic origins and columns to DNA genomic bins. By marginalizing or aggregating along the RNA dimension, they derive RNA-associated interaction density profiles along the genome. To detect domain structure, they adapt TAD-calling strategies, specifically, directionality index–like statistics and insulation-style scores, to quantify local changes in RNA contact enrichment. Domain boundaries are identified at positions where upstream vs. downstream RNA interaction biases shift significantly, yielding contiguous genomic intervals termed RNA-associated domains (RADs) or TAD-like domains. Algorithmically, SuperTLD applies a hierarchical domain detection strategy.

Attribute-based methods

Armatus: Armatus, introduced in [38], is designed to identify non-overlapping topological domains across multiple predefined scales using a dynamic programming optimization approach. The motivation behind Armatus stems from the understanding that topological domains can exist at various length scales. The method includes a resolution parameter within its objective function, which is crucial for detecting topological domains. This objective function factors in the resolution parameter by applying it to contact frequency densities calculated across all pairs of genomic loci. The primary input to Armatus is an symmetric interaction matrix, where n represents its dimension, along with thirteen additional parameters. A key parameter among these is t, which defines the maximum resolution for detection. The algorithm’s time complexity depends on both t and n, specifically . Armatus produces partitions of topological domains at various resolutions, although it does not generate a final hierarchical TAD partition that integrates these multi-scale partitions.

Arrowhead: Arrowhead, introduced in [39], processes interaction data using a specialized transformation designed to enhance the data and make the boundaries of topological domains more distinct. Following this transformation, topological domains emerge in shapes resembling arrowheads, with the corners of these shapes corresponding to three specific criteria. Arrowhead identifies a single partition of domains through a dynamic programming approach. Besides the input interaction matrix, its key parameter controls the size of the sliding window used during analysis. The algorithm operates with a time complexity of . A notable limitation of Arrowhead is its dependence on a predefined set of thresholds during domain detection, which can result in some topological domains being overlooked.

CaTCH: CaTCH, presented in [40], focuses on distinguishing individual topological domains from their neighboring domains. It introduces the reciprocal insulation (RI) metric to assess how well domains are separated. During the algorithm’s process, if the RI value between two adjacent domains falls below a specified threshold, those domains are merged into a single larger domain. This threshold serves as the primary adjustable parameter in the method, allowing for hierarchical identification of topological domains. Besides this parameter, CaTCH requires a sparse-format interaction matrix as input.

chromoR: chromoR, introduced in [43], identifies topological domains by detecting transition points through wavelet analysis. The method assumes that the interaction matrix follows a Poisson distribution. Initially, it sums the rows of the interaction matrix to create an interaction profile vector. Then it applies a wavelet-based Poisson transition-point detection algorithm to this vector. The resulting set of detected transition points are designated as the boundaries of topological domains. The two main parameters of chromoR are the lowest and highest levels at which transition points are identified. In addition to these parameters, chromoR requires an interaction matrix as input.

The method optimizes this scoring function to produce a set of non-overlapping topological domains. GMAP: GMAP, introduced in [47], stands for Gaussian Mixture model And Proportion test. The algorithm operates through three main steps: first, it applies a two-component Gaussian mixture model to distinguish interactions occurring inside topological domains from those outside. Second, it uses a proportion test to identify significantly enriched topological domain boundaries. Finally, the method defines topological domains based on these inferred boundaries. GMAP supports a hierarchical approach, recursively identifying smaller topological domains within larger ones. The method involves ten key parameters and requires an interaction matrix as input.

HiCExplorer: HiCExplorer, introduced in [49], transforms the interaction matrix into a z-score matrix. Each element of this matrix is calculated using the mean and standard deviation of entries at the same genomic distance. The resulting z-scores are organized into a vector of bins, where the topological domain separation score for each bin is computed as the average of z-scores from both upstream and downstream regions within a defined window. These separation scores are then averaged across multiple window sizes to produce a final score for each bin. HiCExplorer employs the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare the upstream and downstream z-scores and selects the higher of the two. Consequently, the method identifies and visualizes a set of non-overlapping topological domains.

HiCKey: HiCKey, developed in [50], identifies hierarchical topological domains using a two-step statistical method. The first step involves applying a generalized likelihood-ratio test to detect change points in the interaction matrix, assuming the data follows either a negative binomial distribution or a general mixture distribution. The second step employs various statistical techniques to infer hierarchical topological domains, where the generalized likelihood test assigns p-values to these domains. The method takes an interaction matrix as input and includes three key parameters: (a) the threshold for inferring hierarchical domains, (b) the significance threshold for transition points, and (c) the minimum length for a topological domain. The overall computational complexity of HiCKey is on the order of .

HiCseg: HiCseg, presented in [51], identifies topological domains by determining the start and end points of diagonal blocks within the interaction matrix. It transforms the matrix segmentation problem into a vector segmentation task, which is then solved through maximum log-likelihood estimation. The method takes an interaction matrix as input and involves three additional parameters: the type of statistical model used, the assumed data distribution, and the maximum allowed number of transition points. The time complexity of HiCseg is , where K represents the upper limit on the number of diagonal blocks corresponding to topological domains. A limitation of this approach is its assumption that the interaction data follows a specific probability distribution, which may not always hold true.

InsulationScore: InsulationScore, introduced in [54], determines transition points through a three-step process. First, it calculates insulation scores by measuring the average interaction count between upstream and downstream regions within a specified window. Next, it identifies local minima on the insulation score curve. Finally, it filters out local minima that fall below a predefined strength threshold, with the remaining minima representing topological domain boundaries. The method takes an interaction matrix as input and relies on five additional parameters. A drawback of InsulationScore is its lack of flexibility, as the fixed parameter settings reduce its effectiveness in filtering out false positives.

OnTAD: OnTAD, introduced in [58], identifies transition or turning points within the interaction matrix to detect topological domains. Unlike the TopDom method, which relies on a fixed window size and struggles with identifying TADs of varying lengths and hierarchical levels, OnTAD employs an adaptive strategy that searches for local minima across multiple window sizes to infer potential domain boundaries. It sets a dynamic, data-driven threshold to effectively filter out false positives. Using dynamic programming, OnTAD recursively assembles topological domains from the candidate boundaries it identifies. The method takes an interaction matrix as input and involves five additional parameters. Its computational complexity is , where d represents the maximum possible domain length and m is the number of candidate boundaries. Compared to TopDom and InsulationScore, OnTAD demonstrates improved performance due to its ability to utilize multiple window sizes and its more flexible false positive filtering.

Data and materials

We categorize the datasets into two groups: RNA-DNA and RNA-RNA interaction datasets. RNA-DNA and RNA-RNA datasets are part of broader RNA interactomes, and they were utilized to thoroughly examine features below the TAD (topologically associating domains) level, which has not been previously assessed systematically using standard analysis. We sourced high resolution human RNA-DNA and RNA-RNA interactions from RNAInter (RNA Interactome Database) version 4.0 [71], which creates a comprehensive repository of RNA-associated interactions that is crucial for achieving a system-level understanding of RNA functions. Similarly, mouse RNA-RNA interactions are also obtained from the same repository [71]. Datasets in all cases are a combination of various RNA types such as mRNA [72], tRNA [73], long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) [74], miRNA [75], circRNA [76], etc.

We mainly employ HiCNorm [77], a normalization method originally developed for Hi-C data, to standardize RNA interaction contact matrices, as it offers a clear, single-sample normalization approach specifically suited for RNA interactome data [77]. HiCNorm uses a Poisson regression framework to adjust the contact matrices. For our analysis, we normalized RNA interaction matrices, drawn from pooled samples representing various RNA types in the RNAInter database at both 50 kb and 100 kb resolutions. To examine the influence of sequencing depth, we created several subsampled datasets by randomly reducing the original pooled sample to different proportions (0.01, 0.1, 0.5, and 0.9). We then applied normalization to these subsampled contact matrices at 50 kb and 100 kb resolutions. In addition, we constructed normalized RNA-RNA interaction matrices at 10 kb resolution for mouse data using KR normalization [78]. These processed matrices served as the basis for evaluating both the reproducibility of TAD boundaries and the enrichment of functional genomic features. Among these techniques, HiCNorm is a normalization method, whereas KR is a correction method.

For the analysis of biological features, we collected peak data for RAD21, CTCF, SMC3, H3K9me3, H3K36me3, H3K4me3, and RNA Polymerase II (Pol II) in humans from the NIH Roadmap Epigenomics Project [79] and the UCSC ENCODE database [80]. Our study focused on human embryonic stem cells and fibroblasts mapped to the hg19 reference genome. Among these features, H3K9me3, H3K36me3, and H3K4me3 represent histone modifications, while RAD21, CTCF, and SMC3 are classified as structural proteins. We chose embryonic stem cells and fibroblasts because they offer the most comprehensive datasets for both epigenomic marks and structural protein binding. Additionally, housekeeping genes were aligned to the hg19 genome [81], and we obtained data for SINE elements and transcription start sites (TSSs) from the UCSC Genome Browser. Following the same approach, we gathered comparable biological feature datasets for mouse embryonic stem cells using resources from both the NIH Roadmap Epigenomics Project and UCSC ENCODE.

TAD performance evaluation metrics

To evaluate the effectiveness of TAD-calling methods, we employ six different metrics, following the criteria described in [68, 69]. Our assessment emphasizes the Jaccard Index (JI), the proportion of statistically significant p-values (), fold change, the boundary-tagged ratio, the mean peak signal, and the TAD score as key indicators of performance.

The TAD metric is especially useful for validating predicted TADs, as it takes into account the natural decline in contact frequency with increasing genomic distance. A higher TAD score suggests more accurate TAD segmentation and indicates the extent to which TADs capture variability in the Hi-C interaction data. This measure reflects both how well the identified TADs explain the overall interaction patterns and the effectiveness of the genome partitioning. Notably, TAD is crucial because it specifically evaluates the consistency of interaction frequency variation with distance.

Beyond TAD, we further assess TAD boundary accuracy by examining the presence of regulatory and functional genomic elements—a widely used validation strategy in previous research. To analyze their distribution in relation to TAD boundaries, we use metrics such as the proportion of significant p-values (), fold change, boundary tagged ratio, and average peak intensity. These features are typically either enriched or depleted near TAD boundaries, and the chosen metrics help quantify this behavior from various angles. In particular, the boundary tagged ratio and average peak values offer more direct insight into the density and frequency of enrichment or depletion. Meanwhile, the p-value ratio and fold change highlight the statistical strength of these enrichments or depletions relative to a background expectation.

We also compute the Jaccard Index between regulatory elements and TAD boundaries to mitigate the potential bias introduced by the average peak metric, which can be disproportionately influenced by signals located near established TAD boundaries. The Jaccard Index quantifies the overlap between two sets of TAD boundaries, allowing us to assess the agreement among different TAD-calling approaches as well as the consistency or similarity of identified boundaries. A more comprehensive description of these evaluation metrics is provided in the following sections.

Comparison of RNA-TADs with Hi-C TADs

To assess whether RNA-associated domains inferred from RNA–RNA and RNA–DNA interactomes approximate classical topologically associating domains (TADs) identified from Hi-C data, we performed a direct comparative analysis using Hi-C and RNA interactome datasets derived from the same or closely matched cell types. Specifically, we focused on human embryonic stem cells and fibroblasts, for which both RNA interactome data and high-quality Hi-C contact maps are available [39]. Hi-C contact matrices were processed at resolutions matching the RNA interactome matrices (50 kb and 100 kb) and TADs were inferred using established Hi-C TAD callers following standard pipelines. We quantified the similarity between RNA-TAD boundaries and Hi-C TAD boundaries using the Jaccard Index (JI). Boundaries were represented as genomic bins, and JI was computed as the ratio of shared boundary bins to the union of boundary bins across the two domain sets. This analysis was performed chromosome-wise and aggregated across chromosomes to obtain global similarity estimates.

TAD inference across callers, resolutions, and sequencing depths

Key factors such as the choice of TAD-calling method, data resolution, and sequencing depth significantly influence the distribution of topologically associating domains (TADs). We examined these variables to assess the precision of TAD detection. Our study specifically investigated how these factors affect TAD identification in human RNA-RNA interaction datasets. Comparing resolutions of 50 kb and 100 kb, we found that a lower resolution typically results in fewer TADs but larger average domain sizes across many TAD-calling algorithms, as illustrated in Figures 1A–1B. In addition to analyzing TAD count and size distributions, we evaluated the consistency of TAD boundary predictions across different tools, a common measure of inference reliability. We observed that, for many TAD callers, reducing resolution improved the agreement of predicted TAD boundaries with those generated by other methods. This pattern is shown in Figure 1C, where error bars indicate variability across chromosomes. Consequently, lower resolution settings tend to produce more consistent domain predictions among different algorithms. Furthermore, some methods create gaps between neighboring TADs, and with decreasing resolution, these gaps become fewer but longer on average.

Reducing the interaction matrix resolution from 50 kb to 100 kb consistently resulted in fewer but larger domains and improved cross-method boundary agreement. This increase in concordance should be interpreted in a scale-dependent context. Chromatin domains are inherently hierarchical, with smaller substructures nested within broader architectural units. At higher resolutions, TAD callers are more sensitive to fine-scale interaction variability and may detect subTAD-level features whose precise boundaries depend on algorithmic assumptions, window sizes, and statistical thresholds. Consequently, small differences in modeling strategies can lead to boundary shifts and reduced inter-method agreement. When the resolution is lowered, adjacent fine-scale domains are effectively aggregated into larger intervals, emphasizing higher-order structural units that are more stable and less sensitive to local interaction fluctuations. This aggregation naturally increases agreement across methods because dominant structural signals outweigh fine-grained variability. At the same time, lower resolution also smooths the interaction matrix, reducing sensitivity to weak or transient substructures. Therefore, the observed improvement in agreement at 100 kb likely reflects both the stabilization of broader hierarchical domains and a reduced detection of fine-scale domain variability. Importantly, enrichment analyses of regulatory and architectural elements near boundaries indicate that these larger domains retain biological coherence, suggesting that the increased agreement is not merely a technical artifact of smoothing but reflects robust higher-level organizational features of RNA-associated chromatin structure.

In this case, corner dots are detected by HiCCUPS [84]. To evaluate its effect, we created samples by randomly downsampling reads from pooled data at different proportions (0.01, 0.1, 0.5, and 0.9) and assessed how well TADs were inferred on these subsets. Unlike Spectral and MrTADFinder, which showed less sensitivity, increasing sequencing depth resulted in a greater number of topological domains identified by IC-Finder and Arrowhead, as depicted in Figure 3. Furthermore, with deeper sequencing, the average size of TADs detected by Spectral and Arrowhead also increased. Examining the consistency of TAD boundaries across methods, boundaries called by MrTADFinder and Arrowhead became less similar at lower sequencing depths, whereas Spectral and CaTCH displayed the opposite trend. Among the evaluated algorithms, Spectral, CaTCH, MrTADFinder, and Arrowhead were most influenced by sequencing depth, affecting their TAD size and quantity outputs. As sequencing depth approached , the TAD boundaries identified closely matched those from downsampled datasets for most callers, as shown in Figure 3. The error bars in Figure 3C represent variability across different chromosomes. These findings indicate that TAD inference from datasets with low sequencing depth may be more prone to bias.

Within the comparative analysis of TAD inference across callers, resolutions, and sequencing depths, SuperTLD, which is RNA-DNA interaction specific approach, displays a characteristic behavior that distinguishes it from most Hi-C–adapted methods. As shown in Figure 1A-B, SuperTLD typically infers a moderate number of TAD-like domains with intermediate domain sizes, positioning it between feature-based callers that tend to produce many smaller domains (e.g., Armatus, HiCseg, TADbit) and clustering or network-partitioning approaches that often yield fewer but substantially larger domains. Importantly, the resolution-dependent trends observed across callers also apply to SuperTLD: reducing resolution from 50 kb to 100 kb results in fewer but larger RNA-TADs, indicating that SuperTLD captures hierarchical domain aggregation similarly to classical TAD callers, despite operating on RNA-associated interaction signals rather than symmetric Hi-C matrices. In terms of boundary agreement (Fig. 1C), SuperTLD exhibits lower Jaccard similarity with other methods, reflecting its RNA-centric formulation and one-dimensional aggregation of RNA–chromatin contacts. This divergence suggests that SuperTLD identifies a complementary set of domain boundaries that are not optimized for consensus with Hi-C–derived callers, but rather for transcription-associated interaction structure. When sequencing depth is varied (Fig. 3), SuperTLD shows stable TAD counts and boundary positions relative to highly depth-sensitive methods such as Spectral, Arrowhead, and MrTADFinder, indicating robustness to subsampling. However, its boundaries do not converge toward the dominant consensus even at high depth, underscoring that SuperTLD captures a distinct organizational layer of genome architecture. Collectively, these results position SuperTLD not as a high-consensus caller under these metrics, but as a methodologically orthogonal approach whose inferred RNA-TADs reflect RNA-associated organization patterns that are only partially aligned with conventional TAD definitions.

Biological assessment of TADs and TAD boundaries

Evaluating the accuracy of TAD-calling algorithms is challenging due to the difficulty of reaching consensus among methods—even when applied to the same dataset—and the lack of standardized benchmark datasets for validation. Nevertheless, recent studies have emphasized the importance of various regulatory functional elements and their close relationship with topological domain boundaries [5, 82, 85–93]. Key components involved in TAD formation include cohesin complexes such as SMC3 [82] and RAD21, along with the chromatin insulator protein CTCF [82, 85–89, 94, 95], whose binding sites are often enriched near domain boundaries. In addition, TAD boundaries show enrichment of specific histone modifications like H3K36me3 and H3K4me3, as well as promoter-associated factors such as RNA polymerase II [5, 90, 91]. In contrast, the repressive histone mark H3K9me3 tends to be depleted at these boundaries [5, 90]. Housekeeping genes [66] and transcription start sites [5] are also frequently located close to TAD boundaries. Furthermore, Alu SINE elements are notably enriched at TAD boundaries in the human genome [5]. Taken together, these associations highlight TADs as fundamental genomic structures intricately linked to complex gene regulation. Investigating these connections offers valuable biological insights that can aid in assessing the effectiveness of different TAD-calling methods.

We investigated whether regulatory functional elements are depleted or enriched near TAD boundaries by analyzing genomic regions within 1 kilobase of the boundary and extending 500 kilobases both upstream and downstream, considering all boundary-calling methods. Although the general patterns of enrichment and depletion were similar (refer to Figure 5), we conducted further analyses using several metrics, including the p-value (with a significance cutoff of ), fold change, boundary-tagged ratio, and average peak signal. These measures helped quantify the density of regulatory element peaks adjacent to topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries. In particular, the boundary-tagged ratio and average peak metrics provided precise estimates of the degree of enrichment or depletion in these regions. To determine the statistical significance of these observations, we relied on p-values and fold change values. Additionally, to correct for any potential bias related to the exact placement of TAD boundaries, we calculated the Jaccard Index (JI) within a kilobase window to evaluate the overlap between regulatory elements and TAD boundaries.

In the biological assessment of TAD boundaries, RNA-RNA interaction-specific approach SuperTLD demonstrates a biologically coherent yet distinct enrichment profile compared to classical Hi-C–adapted callers. As shown in Figure 5 and Tables 2-4, SuperTLD boundaries exhibit clear enrichment for architectural proteins such as CTCF and cohesin components (RAD21 and SMC3), consistent with the established role of these factors in delimiting transcriptionally active chromatin domains. However, relative to top-ranked feature-based methods such as OnTAD, HiCseg, HiCExplorer, and the Directionality Index, SuperTLD generally achieves moderate enrichment scores across metrics including average peak, boundary-tagged ratio, and fold change. This difference is also reflected in the Jaccard Index (JI) analyses, where SuperTLD shows lower overlap with functional genomic elements than methods optimized for diagonal block detection in symmetric interaction matrices. Importantly, SuperTLD boundaries consistently display depletion of the repressive histone mark H3K9me3, aligning with canonical boundary-associated chromatin signatures and reinforcing the biological validity of RNA-TAD boundaries. The comparatively lower JI values observed for SuperTLD should not be interpreted as reduced biological relevance, but rather as a consequence of its RNA-centric formulation, which prioritizes RNA–chromatin interaction landscapes over maximizing spatial concordance with DNA–DNA–derived boundaries. In this context, SuperTLD captures a complementary subset of biologically meaningful boundaries that are likely enriched for transcription-associated and RNA-mediated organizational features, which may be underrepresented by conventional TAD callers. This interpretation is further supported by its intermediate performance (Fig. 6), indicating that while SuperTLD does not maximize variance explanation across all genomic distances, it reliably captures biologically grounded interaction decay patterns characteristic of RNA-associated domain organization

Although SuperTLD achieves the highest spatial concordance with Hi-C TAD boundaries, its architectural protein enrichment scores are moderate relative to certain attribute-based callers. This observation does not imply a fundamental trade-off between spatial agreement and transcription-associated organization. Instead, it reflects methodological differences in optimization objectives. Attribute-based callers are tuned to maximize insulation contrast in symmetric contact matrices, which naturally aligns with loop-extrusion–mediated CTCF/cohesin binding. In contrast, SuperTLD emphasizes RNA-associated interaction asymmetry and transcription-linked contact density. The resulting RNA-TAD boundaries therefore capture a complementary organizational layer that is partially aligned with, but not fully determined by, classical loop-extrusion architecture.

Concordance and reproducibility of TADs

Beyond evaluating topological domains in terms of their biological relevance to functional regulatory elements, it is equally important to assess the agreement and consistency of TADs when comparing different TAD-calling methods. To measure the similarity and reproducibility of TAD predictions, we examined experimental datasets that include both technical and biological replicates. While it is known that TAD boundaries can undergo changes during processes such as cellular differentiation [101] and organismal development [102, 103], prior research has shown that TADs are generally highly reproducible, with domain boundaries remaining relatively consistent across replicate samples [5, 39].

We began by evaluating how consistently TAD boundaries were reproduced across both biological and technical replicates at a 50 kb resolution, using the Jaccard Index (JI) as a similarity metric. This analysis was performed on a human RNA-RNA interaction dataset where the replicate samples had nearly the same sequencing depth. As anticipated, TAD boundaries demonstrated higher reproducibility across technical replicates compared to biological ones, as illustrated in Figure 7A. A similar trend was observed in both the human RNA-DNA interaction dataset and the mouse RNA-RNA interaction dataset.

Topological domains that share identical start and end coordinates across samples are classified as exhibiting perfect concordance. We quantified how the number of TADs varies with different levels of reproducibility at 50 kb resolution using the human RNA-RNA interaction dataset. In this context, reproducibility reflects how frequently the same TAD is detected across multiple replicate samples. As illustrated in Figure 7B, the majority of TADs are not consistently detected across replicates, indicating that domains identified by a particular TAD-calling algorithm in one replicate are often not found in others. This trend also appears in the human RNA-DNA interaction dataset and in mouse RNA-RNA interaction data. Among the evaluated methods, the feature-based approach Armatus and the graph-based method MrTADFinder produced topological domains with notably higher reproducibility than others. The ability of some algorithms to consistently detect a significant subset of domain boundaries reinforces the idea that many TADs represent conserved genomic structures. Importantly, we observed that smaller domains with stronger interaction signals tend to be more reliably reproduced across replicates.

To uncover potential trends in TAD identification, we examined both the size and average interaction frequency of topological domains across varying levels of reproducibility. As illustrated in Figure 7C, domains that are consistently identified across replicates tend to exhibit significantly higher mean interaction frequencies than those with lower reproducibility, based on the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with a significance threshold of . This finding implies that TADs with weaker interaction signals are generally less stable. Moreover, methods that perform more evenly across samples tend to show lower variability in the mean interaction frequencies of their inferred domains. In contrast, for many TAD-calling algorithms, domains that appear less reproducibly tend to be larger on average than those identified more consistently, as shown in Figure 7D. This suggests that larger domains may be inherently less stable than smaller ones. These observations were consistent across both the human RNA-DNA interaction data and the mouse RNA-RNA interaction data. Overall, the results reinforce the idea that smaller domains with stronger internal interactions are more reproducible, in line with canonical definitions of topological domains.

Concordance between RNA-TADs and Hi-C TADs

To evaluate whether RNA-TADs capture chromatin domain organization comparable to that inferred from Hi-C, we compared RNA interactome heatmaps with matched Hi-C contact maps and Hi-C–derived TAD annotations over identical genomic regions. Quantitatively, the overlap between RNA-TAD boundaries and Hi-C TAD boundaries, measured using the Jaccard Index, was significantly higher than expected by chance and comparable to the level of agreement observed between different Hi-C TAD callers reported in prior benchmarking studies. While the overall similarity is moderate rather than complete, this behavior is consistent with the known variability among TAD callers and reflects the distinct interaction modalities underlying RNA-based and DNA-based contact maps.

Notably, RNA-TAD boundaries frequently emphasize boundary regions rather than uniformly dense intra-domain interactions. This observation aligns with recent experimental evidence suggesting that RNA–RNA and RNA–DNA interactions are preferentially enriched at or near chromatin domain boundaries [104, 105], rather than across entire TAD interiors. As a result, RNA-TADs tend to highlight transcription-associated and boundary-centric organizational features that are only partially captured by Hi-C data.

Attribute-based methods generally exhibit higher Jaccard similarity, with OnTAD, Arrowhead, TADtree, and the Directionality Index achieving the strongest agreement with Hi-C–derived TAD boundaries. This trend is consistent with their explicit optimization for diagonal block structures in symmetric Hi-C contact matrices. In contrast, community-based and network-partitioning approaches show lower but non-negligible overlap, reflecting their emphasis on broader clustering and graph modularity rather than sharp boundary detection. Importantly, it supports the interpretation that SuperTLD captures a distinct yet biologically meaningful organizational layer rather than a degraded approximation of Hi-C TADs.

Implementation assessment

All TAD-calling algorithms evaluated in this study are publicly accessible, either as downloadable tools or via web interfaces. While user-friendliness plays a role in their practical adoption, computational efficiency, specifically runtime, is equally crucial due to the high cost of processing power.

To assess performance, we measured how long each method takes to process data from a single chromosome. The results revealed substantial differences in runtime across methods. Each caller was tested using predefined parameter settings on chromosome 6 of the human RNA-RNA interaction dataset at a 50 kb resolution, as detailed in Table 9. Among all the methods, TADtree had the longest execution time, whereas OnTAD—a feature-driven approach—was the fastest. Discussion

The performance of various TAD-calling methods on RNA interactomes differs because each method is typically applied to different datasets and with distinct parameter settings. This variability complicates fair comparisons of their effectiveness. Nonetheless, when standardized datasets and appropriate parameter choices are employed, these methods tend to yield largely comparable outcomes. In our study, we maintained uniform parameters across all callers to reduce discrepancies caused by parameter variations. The advent of Hi-C interaction datasets has led to the development of numerous domain-calling algorithms, significantly advancing our insight into the fundamental organization of the genome.

In this research, our primary aim was to adapt existing TAD-calling techniques for RNA interactome data. Initially, we investigated how factors such as sequencing depth and resolution influence the topological domains identified. For most methods, adjusting the resolution parameter consistently changes the size and number of RNA-based TADs, as well as the spacing between adjacent domains. However, different RNA-TAD inference tools respond differently to sequencing depth, resulting in varied outcomes in terms of domain size and quantity. Both sequencing depth and resolution typically affect the consistency of RNA-TAD boundary detection. Despite differences in inference criteria among these methods, the variety of RNA-TAD segmentations they generate remains valuable for deeper genome analyses due to inherent RNA-TAD variability. Although there is no definitive benchmark dataset for RNA-TAD inference, performance can still be assessed by examining RNA-TAD specificity and counts, such as through measures of signal variation in RNA-RNA interaction datasets and the locations of RNA-TAD boundaries. In the absence of a standard reference, validating the biological relevance of the inferred RNA-TADs offers a meaningful way to confirm domain accuracy, similar to approaches used for TADs in Hi-C data.

Across the comprehensive benchmark, SuperTLD exhibits a distinctive performance profile that reflects both its RNA-centric design and its methodological assumptions. Unlike conventional Hi-C–oriented callers that operate directly on symmetric contact matrices, SuperTLD leverages RNA–DNA interaction asymmetry by aggregating RNA-associated contacts into a one-dimensional genomic signal, enabling it to detect TAD-like domains driven by transcriptional proximity rather than purely chromatin contact density. In terms of variance explanation, SuperTLD achieves moderate-to-high values across genomic distances 6, indicating that its inferred domains capture a substantial fraction of distance-dependent interaction decay, though it does not consistently outperform leading attribute-based methods such as OnTAD or HiCseg, which rank highest overall on this metric. Similarly, while SuperTLD boundaries show biologically meaningful enrichment for architectural proteins such as CTCF and cohesin components, this enrichment is generally less pronounced than that observed for top-performing feature-based approaches (e.g., DI, TADbit, OnTAD) that were originally optimized for diagonal block detection in symmetric matrices. Notably, SuperTLD’s strengths emerge in its conceptual alignment with RNA-associated interactomes, as it directly integrates RNA localization signals rather than treating RNA interactions as Hi-C–like proxies. This likely explains its ability to recover coherent TAD-like structures even in settings where classical corner-dot patterns are weak or absent. However, this same design choice may contribute to lower boundary concordance with other callers and reduced reproducibility across replicates compared to Armatus or MrTADFinder, which favor highly stable, interaction-dense domains. Overall, SuperTLD should be viewed not as a universal replacement for established TAD callers, but as a complementary RNA-aware method that captures a distinct layer of genome organization, emphasizing transcription-associated domain architecture rather than maximal agreement with canonical Hi-C–derived TAD definitions.

Some RNA-TAD inference methods demonstrate superior performance in two main respects: 1- confirming the relationship between topological domain boundaries and established regulatory elements, and 2- validation using the TAD metric, which quantifies how much of the signal variability in RNA-RNA or RNA-DNA interaction data is explained by the identified domains. Since topological domains are known to be stable genomic features, an effective caller should consistently detect reproducible domains across replicate samples. Generally, RNA-TAD boundaries show higher reproducibility between technical replicates compared to biological replicates. Moreover, smaller domains with stronger interaction frequencies are typically more reproducible across RNA-TADs exhibiting different reproducibility levels. In general, consistent with findings from Hi-C data analyses, TAD callers that aim to detect hierarchical domains tend to underperform compared to those that focus on identifying non-hierarchical domains, as the task of inferring hierarchical structures is inherently more complex.

We explored the underlying causes for the notable differences among various TAD-calling methods, especially regarding reproducibility, domain size, and the total count of RNA-TADs. Such disparities often stem from differences in the assumptions each method makes about the hierarchy, size, and quantity of RNA-TADs, which influence the specific topological domains they identify. Furthermore, variations can also result from distinct criteria used to finalize domain detection. These criteria may include factors like the chosen window size for computing average interaction frequencies between neighboring bins, and the threshold set for excluding false positive domain calls.

Among the three types of TAD-calling approaches examined here for RNA-TADs, attribute-based methods are notable for their streamlined techniques, scalability, and wide-ranging ability to capture chromosome interactions. These approaches effectively identify characteristic patterns and features of TADs within interaction data and incorporate statistical methods into the domain inference process. In contrast, clustering-based methods primarily focus on grouping neighboring bins based on similarity, which plays a crucial role in detecting TADs. However, a significant challenge for clustering approaches lies in selecting suitable criteria for cluster identification, often relying on heuristic judgments. Network-partitioning methods use graph-theoretic strategies by treating the interaction matrix as an adjacency matrix, but they tend to place less emphasis on extracting TAD-specific patterns and features from the data. Our evaluation shows that attribute-based callers generally achieve better performance than the other types, highlighting the importance of capturing TAD features within the interaction data. Nevertheless, many existing methods have drawbacks, such as using fixed window sizes that may limit their ability to detect diverse topological domain structures and overlook larger-scale patterns. Additionally, parameters that set the maximum number of TADs pose difficulties, especially for users unfamiliar with the characteristics of the experimental data.

In this work, we assessed the effectiveness of twenty-seven TAD-calling methods across a range of sequencing depths and resolutions, focusing on their agreement, reproducibility, and overall performance. We further examined how specific characteristics of the callers influenced their results and highlighted key challenges in inferring RNA-TADs, proposing potential avenues for enhancing current approaches. From our thorough comparison, we established a framework aimed at improving RNA-TAD inference methods by addressing aspects such as computational efficiency, usability, reproducibility, and result quality. Unlike earlier reviews [68, 69, 82, 83, 106] that concentrated primarily on Hi-C data rather than RNA interactomes, our objective was to develop a systematic, comprehensive, and streamlined evaluation framework specifically tailored for TAD-calling methods applied to RNA-RNA and RNA-DNA interaction datasets.

For future research, we suggest several pathways to enhance the study of RNA interactomes and their analysis. Primarily, the accuracy of RNA-TAD detection can be greatly improved. A fundamental step involves identifying meaningful patterns while minimizing noise, since experimental datasets often contain inherent noise. Although many sophisticated normalization methods [107–111] have been developed to reduce noise in Hi-C interaction data, comparable studies focusing on RNA interactomes are lacking. Network partitioning techniques hold promise for methodological advancements. These approaches can reveal essential genome structures by utilizing a global understanding of interaction data and have recently attracted increased interest in the context of Hi-C TAD detection. Compared to attribute-based methods, graph partitioning strategies better capture the overall topology of interaction datasets. Moreover, most network partitioning algorithms do not require pre-specifying the number of clusters, which makes them more adaptable and flexible for RNA-TAD inference.

Moreover, the connection between topological domains and gene regulation requires deeper investigation. Numerous functional elements are closely linked to the boundaries of topological domains, helping maintain the stable architecture of gene expression and regulation [5, 90, 91]. Yet, many questions remain unanswered regarding the association between RNA-TADs and gene regulatory processes. Integrative analyses that combine inferred RNA-TADs with other genomic datasets are essential for uncovering the relationships between various regulatory elements and mechanisms controlling gene function, such as replication, transcription, and translation. This integrative approach can also clarify how three-dimensional chromosome organization, RNA structural features, and RNA interactomes collectively impact gene expression. For instance, a more detailed examination of ChIP-seq data around domain boundaries might yield richer insights.

Topological domains also help explain phenotypic traits. These TAD boundaries and domains are dynamic rather than fixed, differing across species, cell types, and stages of the cell cycle. Alterations in TAD boundaries caused by conditions such as cancer, genetic mutations, or inherited disorders can result in changes to phenotypes. Similar patterns observed in Hi-C TADs are also evident in RNA-TAD domains. Further study of gene expression differences across cell phases or types could improve understanding of gene-driven phenotypic variation. Controlled experiments in RNA-TAD detection may aid in identifying genes related to diseases. As essential spatial genome structures akin to Hi-C TADs, RNA-TADs likely play key roles in normal cellular development, underscoring the importance of their detailed analysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the task of identifying topological domains from RNA interaction data poses considerable computational difficulties. Like many TAD callers originally developed for Hi-C datasets, most RNA-TAD detection methods rely on the premise that interaction frequencies are higher within a topological domain compared to those between different domains. Despite this common foundational assumption, a diverse range of approaches has been created to infer topological domains from various angles. We introduce a comprehensive comparison framework designed to quantitatively assess inferred RNA-TADs, evaluate the effectiveness of multiple RNA-TAD callers, and highlight their distinct characteristics and strengths. While certain methods showed superior performance in our evaluations, each has provided important contributions to RNA-TAD detection, collectively pushing the field forward and revealing key insights about RNA interactome topological domains. As one of the pioneering efforts focused on RNA interactomes, this study aims to showcase the current landscape of RNA-TAD inference and pinpoint existing challenges. Although research in this area remains limited, there is an urgent need for continued investigation to enhance understanding and improve techniques.
