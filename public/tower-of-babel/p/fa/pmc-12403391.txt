Establishing a research advisory committee in addictions services with a focus on social needs and social isolation
Authors: Marik Moen, Kathena Storm, Donald Young, Rosalind Haines-Jones, Darin Loveland, Sarah Kattakuzhy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubstance use disorders and unmet social needs (like housing instability and social isolation) are major contributors to U.S. morbidity and mortality. We, patients, staff and researchers, established a research advisory committee (RAC) in an addictions treatment setting to guide research and interventions based on lived-experience.MethodsWe employed community engaged outreach methods to recruit, train, and establish the RAC, and the 10-Step Framework for Continuous Patient-Engagement to advise research activities, and to initiate our own research.ResultsThe RAC contributed unique insights, advising on 12 research projects. We present the process and challenges of establishing the RAC and the benefits to researchers and advisors. We also summarize a case example of a co-developed a research project related to housing stability and social connection.ConclusionsThis experience shows that establishing a patient/community advisory committee in addictions treatment settings is doable and valuable. Co-authoring this manuscript has allowed us to share how we are shaping research that is more likely to be acceptable to community participants and feasible to implement, especially for populations with addictions and facing barriers related to social determinants of health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-025-00770-8.

Introduction

Substance use disorders (SUD) and social determinants of health (see Definitions and Clarifications inset) contribute to hundreds of thousands of deaths each year, and unequal rates of death and disease for different racial and income groups [1–4] Drug and alcohol addictions are related to over 100,000 deaths annually in the U.S. since 2010, with our city of Baltimore having among the highest overdose deaths in the nation, especially among older Black/African American adults [5–7]. Further, social determinants of health (SDOH) are causal factors driving health outcomes and contributing to addictions [8–10] and 1 in 4 U.S. patients are unable meet these basic social needs [8–11]. Despite the need, only 1 in 4 people receive effective, evidence-based treatment or successfully address their social needs [12–14].

Resources to address addiction and social needs are insufficient, fragmented– that is, access to mental, behavioral, and physical health and social care services are rarely integrated– and not patient-centered or trauma-informed [15–18].

When interventions to address addictions or social needs are patient-centered, there is greater service uptake, increased patient-engagement in care, positive satisfaction with care, and improved physical and social wellbeing [15, 19–21]. Definitions and clarifications1. Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) are non-medical factors or systems (e.g., economic, healthcare, racism) and environments that shape our lives and affect our ability to meet our social needs(e.g., adequate food, housing, and transportation) [22].2. Substance use disorders (SUD) is often a preferred term to refer to complex brain disorders related to consuming drugs and alcohol. Addiction, defined below, can be associated with negative moral judgments. We use the term addiction(s) as that is what we are most comfortable with, and this term is often more understood by many people with lived experience.3. Addiction is a treatable, chronic disease that affects the brain and behavior. People with addiction engage in behaviors that become compulsive and often continue despite harmful consequences. Addiction is not a moral failing or lack of willpower—it is a condition that can be managed with appropriate treatment and support [23].

Similarly, a review of articles about engagement of populations with lived experience in mental health and substance use research reports that results from these projects tend to be more aligned with the needs and priorities of the target population- therefore, more likely to be feasible, easily adopted, implemented, and sustainable [24] (p2). Also, populations with lived experience benefit from being involved in the research process, and from feeling heard, valued, and gaining deeper understanding [25]. Going forward, services, programming, and research to address addictions and social needs mustincorporate patient perspectives and adapt interventions based on their feedback [15].

The Research Advisory Committee (RAC) was established in end November 2021 to address gaps in appropriate research and services for people with SUD via a collaboration between the interdisciplinary (medicine, nursing, and pharmacy) clinician-researchers at University of Maryland Addictions Treatment Center and patients and staff, supported by institutional research funding support (see funding section) [26].

The goal of this project was to support patient-centered research by involving advisors with lived experience in shaping studies from start to finish. This manuscript describes the development and evaluation of the RAC and a case example of a co-created research pilot.

Patient and public involvement in research—patient/community-engaged research (P/CEnR)

Our definition of P/CEnR is this: We are the RAC advisors who represent a diverse group of patients and staff with experiences of living with addictions and striving to meet basic social needs. In the RAC, we work as patients, staff, and researcher collaborators to build our skills to co-create research and programming that is relevant and meaningful to our communities. We do so because we believe that researchers, healthcare systems, community organizations, and employers truly want to improve the care and services they provide and to meet community and patient priorities. (See Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP 2) (long form) in Supplementary Material 1) [26].

Methods

Design

This is a descriptive study of the methods and results of establishing our Research Advisory Committee (RAC) comprised of patients and staff to guide research at UMATC, an innovative approach to community engaged research in an addiction treatment setting.

Setting

The University of Maryland Addictions Treatment Center (UMATC) includes an opioid treatment (methadone) program (OTP), Alcohol and Drug Addiction Program (ADAP) offering buprenorphine, talk therapy, intensive outpatient treatment, and Deaf Addiction Services of Maryland (DASAM) with a co-located comprehensive primary care clinic, the Health and Recovery Practice (HARP). HARP is one of a few such programs nationally [27], offering infectious disease, psychiatry and harm reduction services in addition to adult primary preventive and acute care services.

Methodology

Our work is guided conceptually and methodologically by prior studies2626–29283022(p2)23 on engaging people with lived experience in substance use research, and the 10-Step Framework for Continuous Patient and Stakeholder Engagement in Patient Centered Outcomes Research (PCOR) (10-Step Framework) [24, 25, 28–31].The 10-Step Framework is an evidence-based model for inclusive and reciprocal engagement of patients/public in patient-centered outcomes research developed by the University of Maryland School of Pharmacy PATIENTS (PATient-centered Involvement in Evaluating the effectiveNess of TreatmentS) Program.26–29 This framework guides patient/community engaged research teams to apply multiple community engagement strategies with different approaches and implications depending on step, setting, or population (see Fig. 1) [30]. To create the RAC, researcher collaborators relied on trusted messengers, staff and patients at UMATC, to recruit, install, orient, and train a patient and staff research advisory committee.

Recruitment

Researcher collaborators were committed to recruiting a diverse group of advisors based on age, gender, race/ethnicity, and experience with addiction. Recruitment occurred over 4 months with advertising via flyer and (primarily) via word of mouth and on-site networking. Research study staff reached out to clinic counselors to recommend potential patients and staff as RAC advisors; they asked staff of other studies to ask patients who regularly participated in research projects to refer themselves or other patients. Clinic healthcare staff recruited patients, and the referred patients also recruited other patients by word of mouth. During recruitment, potential advisors were assessed (screened) for meeting eligibility criteria established to assure a highly functional advisory committee (see Table 1). Research advisory committee

Recruitment efforts resulted in roughly 20 people referred, with a final advisory group of 14 people who met criteria, who accepted the role, and who were available to participate at the time of the first RAC meeting. These RAC advisors were three staff (two counselors and one nurse) and eleven patients, including women (n = 8) and men (n = 6); who identify as Black (n = 10) and white (n = 4) and range in age from early 30 s to late 60 s. Ultimately, an average of 8 advisors attended most RAC meetings.

RAC advisors had a diversity of addictions treatment and recovery experience: some received methadone or suboxone treatment and some did not receive medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD); and advisors had days to years of stable sobriety. In addition to patients and staff, two or three rotating scientists and research staff joined as researcher collaborators (RAC project principal investigators and staff) in the first year (November 2021-October 2022).

Participation and compensation

RAC advisors were compensated at $100/hour for general research advisory meetings and at $75/hour for sub-study meetings based on the National Health Council Patient Engagement Fair-Market Value Calculator [32].

Orientation and training

We started the RAC kick-off launch meeting by introducing ourselves and stating why we wanted to participate in the RAC. Advisors attended kick-off and training meetings that reviewed research basics, ethics, and the value of community input. Meetings opened and closed with patient-led reflections.

Evaluation

RAC advisors completed anonymous surveys (WE-ENACT) [33] and shared spoken feedback to researcher collaborators in interviews and focus group meetings.

Results

Below we share findings from the evaluation surveys (WE-ENACT) and interviews, focusing on key issues of trust, the research environment, communication dynamics, and areas for improvement.

Trustworthiness in non-scientist and scientist collaborator interactions

In general, evaluation results demonstrate a strong feeling of community established through the RAC; and the feeling of growth, confidence, and purpose created within the advisors [34]. As patient and staff advisors, we perceived that researcher collaborators demonstrated commitment to our collaborative partnership. They were accountable: they kept coming to meetings, they did what they said they would do or explained why they could not. They were respectful and caring in interactions to build relationships with non-scientist advisors. They were competent and ethical in their approach to the RAC and research. They shared resources, they explained processes, they compensated us, they clarified how they acquired funding, and shared opportunities for future joint research projects.

Environment

The meetings were held in a location that we were familiar with, and at a time that was generally convenient for us to attend. Together we created an environment that was welcoming, non-judgmental, and tolerant of diverse perspectives. There was mutual respect between all participants, and a come as-you-are acceptance to the group.

Different ways of sharing

Our approach to P/CenR allowed all advisors to bring our authentic selves to the group and provide valuable insights from our lived experience to inform the work. Everyone was allowed to speak their piece. At times, this meant the direction of the conversations were diverted from the main purpose and took longer. However, many rich insights may have been missed without these diversions. At times two or three people dominated the conversations. Attempts were made to keep this in check by reminding individuals to allow space for others to share, and by using other means to give everyone a chance to equally participate, such as round robin discussions, having people write down their thoughts, or voting. Some of us patient/staff advisors found it difficult to contribute and to follow what researchers were presenting, or what was asked of us. Researcher and fellow patient/staff advisors attempted to clarify and engage all RAC members, with limited success in the beginning, and with more success as engagement went on.

Areas for improvement

The RAC experience and utility could be improved by better defined roles and more clarity in specific advising needs to reduce confusion and improve efficiency. As a RAC patient advisor noted, “nobody likes to have their time wasted.”

Enhancing RAC efficiency and capacity could also be facilitated by more active management of group dynamics and providing more and on-going training.

Going forward, attention to RAC composition will be important as different people with specific experiences may offer more targeted insights for various research (e.g., younger advisors for research on addictions and youth).

Another main finding in this evaluation was that advisors were confused about the distinction between the RAC’s general advisory role and its specific work on a sub-study (PURPLE CARES described in the case study below). In future, splitting these responsibilities or recruiting subgroups for project-specific advising is recommended.

Finally, establishing continuous funding, by integrating a RAC line item into departmental or research budgets or as part of institutional indirect support, will be critical to sustain our work.

Overall RAC experience

In general, the research advising experience has been valuable for all of us. Researcher collaborators emphasized repeatedly the value of the RAC in informing and improving their approach to research. They also remarked on the growth that occurred because of participating in the RAC, for themselves as scientists and community members, as well as the growth they saw in non-scientist advisors. “The most valuable aspect of working with the RAC was the amazing insights we gained collaborating on equal ground with RAC advisors who could offer fresh perspectives to improve the work” (RAC researcher collaborator).

As persons in various stages of recovery, some RAC advisors were struggling with addiction and striving to meet our own social needs, plus, participating in the RAC could be challenging, yet it was a good experience. “This was a good experience for me, even if at times it was challenging. I learned about the different problems that other addicts faced, I learned some background from doctors and other staff I wouldn’t normally learn about as a patient, which allowed me to gain more respect of their perspective when it comes to patient care and the work they put in” (RAC patient co-author).

We also shared how that being involved in research and “giving back” had a positive impact on us. “This was an excellent experience for me. I’m uplifted by people asking for my thoughts on how to make this environment a better place for everyone as a whole” (RAC patient co-author).

In the first year (November 2021-October 2022), five researchers/teams presented projects to the RAC. This expanded to six teams in year two and, at the time of writing, the RAC has advised on 12 research projects in various stages of development (see Table 2). Discussion

The development of the RAC builds on a growing body of scholarship related to engaging patients with substance use disorders in co-creating research. As others in this work have found, there is great merit in bringing multiple perspectives to the table and there are also challenges to this approach [35, 36]. Our experience affirms the importance of key recommendations for successful community-engaged research including establishing and maintaining trustworthiness and inclusiveness and adhering to pay equity standards for advisors [36, 37]. Our experience also aligns with other scholars who state it is at times difficult to work together, the process can take a long time, and the path is not straightforward [35, 36].

We can further improve the experience and functioning of entities like our RAC by clarifying project goals and the advisor role, equipping RAC teams to actively manage group dynamics, investing in advisor capacity building and funding, and doing more to address power inequities [36, 38].

Limitations

Our work has limitations. Our context is very unique, our setting may not be representative of other addiction treatment programs, however, we feel that the diversity of services and programs offered at UMATC may lend our findings broader relevance. As advisors, we only represent ourselves and not the general experiences of other community-engaged research advisors or other persons with lived experience of addiction or unmet social needs. There may be selection bias in terms of who was invited join and structural bias, in who was able to join and to sustain membership in the RAC, (e.g., our work was only conducted in English, excluding non-English speakers, and meetings were generally Wednesday afternoons). However, we worked with researcher advisors to accommodate meetings to patient preferences and to be as inclusive as possible of people with different learning and engagement styles. Further, as mentioned briefly, power imbalances may have resulted in social desirability in responses posed by researchers. Yet, we feel we were honest and forthright, and we felt heard in sharing our opinions and insights. Finally, as RAC members co-authoring this manuscript, we are not able to be completely objective in our evaluation of the RAC.

Strengths

The trustworthiness and approach of research advisors along with our own expertise helped us to overcome many challenges and deliver on our mission, by working together and listening deeply and applying multiple strategies. Our initial RAC membership achieved great diversity in age, race, gender, education, and addiction experience. Our unique setting, one of the only SUD treatment programs with such an array of services and a primary care clinic integrated on site, allowed recruitment of patient and staff advisors representing varied treatment experiences, allowed us to engage in advising on research spanning many addiction and treatment types.

Our input has already affected many research projects, from changing wording in recruitment materials, to modifying how surveys are conducted and how results are interpreted, and by improving the relevance of research to our communities. We made a crucial discovery for social needs researchers: that social relationships and connections are just as, or more important for health and quality of life, as our need for stable housing. In addition, advising on research lead to personal fulfillment and other positive effects on us: “I love the idea that my input is useful and motivates others to continue to achieve the goals, dreams that was lost but still lives” (RAC co-author).

Implications

As noted above, it is recommended that researchers engage community members with lived experience, as a feasible and valuable initiative to improve research in population with substance use disorders. Community advisors can influence everything from what the research focuses on, to how the questions are phrased, recruitment, incentives, interventions, and interpretation of results, and more [39]. Strategically engaging advisors based on the specific needs of the research project is recommended. For healthcare providers in similar settings, keenly reviewing literature for interventions developed via patient/community engaged research and seeking input of patients and staff with lived experience of addictions as you implement new practices and quality improvement initiatives will likely facilitate the adaptation of interventions into practice. Healthcare systems and public health departments can establish policies, guidance, and incentives that encourage conducting community-engaged research, and implementing community-informed interventions, and operationalizing sustained funding for patient/community research advisory committees.

Next steps

In 2023, the University of Maryland Baltimore launched the Kahlert Institute for Addictions Medicine to integrate and elevate addictions research across campus and the university system. Under its new leadership, Kahlert has committed to establishing an advisory board that will build on and the work of our initial RAC.

Conclusion

In this time of urgent focus addictions and overdose, especially among older adults [7, 40], and the influence of social determinants on health outcomes, we, the RAC, are helping to shape and improve the quality and relevance of science related to these topics that are vital to our community. Co-authoring this manuscript allows us to share with others in the field about our process with deeper insights than would be possible with a traditional report or typical scientific manuscript- as we do so by using our own words. These findings can inform researchers, community advisors, clinicians, scholars, and policy makers who are working to address addictions or social needs by authentically incorporating community advisors to partner with and guide the work.

To summarize the impact of this project, we conclude by sharing a quote from one of our co-authors: “This has given me the tools necessary to engage my community in conversations about research and the benefits it has in our community. It helped me realize the importance of having community involved in every stage of research” (RAC patient co-author).
