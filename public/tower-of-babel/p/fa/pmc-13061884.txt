A geometric function theoretic approach to MRI distortion correction using Miller Ross Poisson series
Authors: S Manoj, B Srutha Keerthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on the newly introduced subclass of Sakaguchi-type function associated with the Miller–Ross-type Poisson distribution series. Using the proposed operator, we derive several coefficient bound, inverse function estimates, initial logarithmic coefficient results and Feketo-Szego type inequalities. Furthermore, growth, distortion, convex combination properties, subordination outcomes, and partial sum results are derived for the defined class. Application part demonstrates how the obtained coefficient bounds can be employed to design an analytic correction operator for improving the quality of distorted Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans. Numerical experiments on real datasets validate the effectiveness of the proposed model, showing significant improvement in terms of MSE, PSNR, and SSIM. These results highlight the interplay between geometric function theory and practical imaging problems, thereby providing a new perspective on the use of analytic function subclasses in applied sciences.Keywords: Analytic functions, Univalent function, Starlike function, Subordination, Fekete-Szego inequality, Miller-Ross distribution series, Geometric properties.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Application

Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the one of the most wiedely used medical imaging modalities due to its ability to provide high resolution, non invasive of soft tissue. It plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and monitoring of neurological, cardiovascular and musculoskeletal disorders. Despite these advantages, MRI images frequently suffer from intensity inhomogeneity and nonlinear signal distortions caused by nonuniform magnetic fields, coil sensitivity variations and hardware imperfections. These effects appear as gradual intensity drifts or nonlinear intensity wrapping across the image, leading to reduced contrast and loss of diagnostically important structural information.

Various proposals have been made to correct for the intensity inhomogeneity of the images obtained by MRI. They range from polynomial fitting models, bias field correction approaches, homomorphic filtering techniques, and surface coil normalisation. While the approaches have the ability of reducing the intensity inconsistencies of the images to some degree, they tend to remain rather experience-dependent. As a result, over-correction of the images as well as the instability of the images often become evident.

From the mathematical viewpoint, the intensity distortion problem of the MRI can be formulated as a nonlinear transformation of the analytical intensity distribution. It seems that the application of transformation operators with defined analytical structures and specified geometric characteristics could be useful here. Specifically, the class of analytical functions with bounded coefficients and specified geometry may serve as a basis for designing the intensity correction transformation operators.

Motivated by this viewpoint, we apply the theoretical results established in the above section, where coefficient bounds were derived for a subclass of Sakaguchi-type analytic functions associated with the Miller–Ross-type Poisson distribution series. The boundedness and subordination properties of this subclass ensure controlled growth, stability under transformation, and preservation of essential geometric features. These characteristics are especially desirable in MRI image enhancement, where uncontrolled amplification may distort anatomical boundaries.

Using Theorem 1, we construct a mathematically constrained correction operator that acts on MRI intensity values as an analytic transformation. The resulting framework provides a direct bridge between geometric function theory and practical MRI image enhancement. Unlike conventional heuristic approaches, the proposed method is supported by explicit coefficient bounds and analytic stability guarantees, offering a principled mechanism for correcting intensity inhomogeneity while preserving structural details.

18–20Recent years have witnessed significant research efforts aimed at improving magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) quality by correcting intensity inhomogeneity and nonlinear signal distortions arising from magnetic field nonuniformity, coil sensitivity variations, and hardware imperfections. Early systematic evaluations of bias field correction techniques demonstrated that widely used methods integrated into software packages such as SPM, FSL, and FreeSurfer exhibit varying levels of performance and stability, particularly under high noise and strong inhomogeneity conditions. These studies highlighted the limitations of conventional heuristic correction models and emphasized the need for more robust and theoretically justified approaches21–26.Subsequent developments introduced multi-scale and learning-based correction strategies to address these challenges.

Deep learning frameworks such as InhomoNet employed multi-scale feature extraction and specialized loss functions to achieve improved bias field correction compared with traditional polynomial or filtering-based techniques. More recent approaches have focused on implicit and unsupervised learning paradigms, enabling bias field estimation and correction without the requirement of ground-truth bias information, thereby improving generalizability across different MRI modalities and acquisition protocols. Clinical investigations have also demonstrated that effective post-processing bias field correction can significantly enhance quantitative MRI analysis, including segmentation accuracy and biomarker reliability in applications such as breast and brain MRI.

Parallel to these advances in medical image processing, mathematically rigorous frameworks have increasingly been explored for image enhancement and signal correction. In particular, recent studies have demonstrated that coefficient-based constructions from geometric function theory, such as those involving26–28Hankel determinants and subclasses of starlike and convex functions, can be effectively employed to design image enhancement algorithms with improved stability and structural preservation. These analytic approaches offer inherent mathematical constraints that regulate intensity transformation and contrast amplification, thereby reducing overcorrection and artifact introduction.

Motivated by these developments, the present work integrates geometric function theory with MRI image enhancement by exploiting coefficient bounds derived for a subclass of Sakaguchi-type functions associated with the Miller–Ross-type Poisson distribution series. Unlike purely data-driven or heuristic correction methods, the proposed approach provides a mathematically constrained intensity transformation framework, ensuring analytic stability while enhancing MRI image quality. This establishes a meaningful bridge between abstract analytic function theory and practical medical imaging applications.

Evaluation metrics

The performance of the proposed correction method was assessed using three widely recognised metrics in image processing and medical imaging: Mean Squared Error (MSE), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Structural Similarity Index (SSIM).

4) Naturalness Image Quality Evaluator (NIQE)

NIQE is a no-reference image quality metric that evaluates perceptual naturalness based on statistical regularities of natural images. Unlike MSE, PSNR, and SSIM, NIQE does not require a reference image. Lower NIQE values indicate better perceptual quality and natural appearance, while higher values suggest the presence of distortions or unnatural patterns.

5) Blind/Referenceless Image Spatial Quality Evaluator (BRISQUE)

BRISQUE is another no-reference image quality assessment metric that measures distortions based on locally normalised luminance coefficients. Lower BRISQUE scores indicate higher perceptual quality and fewer visible artifacts, making it useful when reference images are not available.

Together, these five metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of the proposed correction framework: MSE reflects pixel-level accuracy, PSNR measures overall fidelity, SSIM evaluates structural similarity, while NIQE and BRISQUE assess perceptual naturalness without requiring reference images.

Discussion

The proposed analytic correction framework was rigorously evaluated using a combination of reference-based and no-reference image quality metrics, including Mean Squared Error (MSE), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), Naturalness Image Quality Evaluator (NIQE), and Blind/Referenceless Image Spatial Quality Evaluator (BRISQUE). Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of the framework’s effectiveness in restoring distorted MRI images. The MSE provides a direct measure of pixel-level differences between the reference and the processed images. In this study, the distorted image exhibited an MSE of 0.003509, which was reduced to 0.0061617 after applying the proposed correction operator. Such a significant decrease highlights the capability of the correction method to effectively compensate for nonlinear distortions introduced during MRI acquisition. From a clinical perspective, reducing MSE ensures that pixel intensities better represent true tissue properties, particularly in homogeneous regions such as soft tissues and organs where intensity inhomogeneity can mask pathologies. The lower error ensures that diagnostic details are preserved, making the corrected image more reliable for subsequent interpretation and analysis.

The PSNR is one of the most widely used measures for image quality assessment, reflecting the ratio between the maximum possible signal and the noise introduced by distortions. The distorted MRI image achieved a PSNR of 24.8187 dB, a value that suggests noticeable degradation in image fidelity. After correction, the PSNR increased significantly to 28.1758 dB, yielding an improvement of approximately 3.4 dB. In medical imaging, even a modest increase of 1–2 dB is regarded as meaningful, as it indicates improved visibility of structural features and boundaries. Therefore, the observed gain of 3.4 dB is highly significant. Higher PSNR directly translates to improved diagnostic capability, as it reduces noise-like distortions that may obscure critical lesions or tissue abnormalities. The improvement demonstrates that the analytic correction model successfully enhances global image fidelity while maintaining consistency across intensity ranges.

The SSIM metric evaluates image quality from a perceptual perspective, considering luminance, contrast, and structural similarity between the reference and processed images. The distorted image recorded an SSIM of 0.9693, which, while relatively high, still indicated subtle structural deviations due to nonlinear distortion. After correction, the SSIM improved to 0.9865, approaching near-perfect similarity with the reference image. This improvement is particularly significant in MRI imaging, where structural preservation is critical for accurate diagnosis. Unlike natural images, where perceptual similarity may tolerate minor distortions, medical images demand very high structural fidelity. The correction method’s ability to increase SSIM demonstrates that it not only reduces error but also preserves diagnostic structures such as lesion boundaries, tissue interfaces, and subtle anatomical features. This ensures that the corrected images remain clinically interpretable and suitable for downstream tasks such as segmentation and classification.

NIQE is a no-reference metric designed to measure the perceptual naturalness of an image based on statistical features. For the distorted image, the NIQE score was 6.9541, while the corrected image achieved a slightly improved score of 6.9467. Although the numerical difference may appear small, it nevertheless indicates that the corrected images exhibit marginally improved perceptual naturalness. This result is consistent with the observation that NIQE is less sensitive to small corrections compared to reference-based metrics such as MSE or SSIM. However, the improvement, though minor, is aligned with the general enhancement trend, confirming that the correction method not only restores pixel accuracy but also improves the perceptual realism of the MRI images. From a clinical perspective, a lower NIQE score implies reduced artificial distortions and more natural intensity distributions, enhancing radiologist confidence in diagnostic interpretation.

The effectiveness of the suggested analytic correction model is strongly supported by the comparative assessment across every measure. In MSE, PSNR, and SSIM, where error correction, fidelity enhancement, and structural preservation were obviously apparent, the most noticeable changes were seen. These measures precisely indicate how well the correcting operator can recover MRI intensities and preserve diagnostic structures. The NIQE and BRISQUE criteria, meanwhile, indicate even minor changes but offer more proof from a no-reference viewpoint that the adjustment helps to improve perceptual quality. Visually reinforced in figure3, this trend is seen as the corrected picture surpasses the distorted picture over five measurements in the comparative graph. The clear contrast of the two story lines draws attention to the dependability and reliability of the suggested correction model. Notably, the improvements were consistent throughout all five measures, therefore highlighting the robustness of the suggested technique in simulated MRI distortions.

The comparative evaluation across all metrics strongly validates the effectiveness of the proposed analytic correction model. The most pronounced improvements were observed in MSE, PSNR, and SSIM, where error reduction, fidelity enhancement, and structural preservation were clearly evident. These metrics directly reflect the ability of the correction operator to restore MRI intensities and preserve diagnostic structures. Meanwhile, the NIQE and BRISQUE metrics, although showing smaller improvements, provide additional evidence that the correction contributes positively to perceptual quality from a no-reference perspective. Importantly, the improvements were consistent across all five metrics, demonstrating the robustness of the proposed method in simulated MRI distortions. The combination of significant gains in reference-based metrics and modest yet consistent enhancements in no-reference metrics indicates that the method can improve both numerical accuracy and perceptual quality in a controlled setting. While these results are promising, further studies and clinical validation are required before the method can be applied in real-world medical settings.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigated geometric properties of a subclass of Sakaguchi-type functions defined via the Miller–Ross-type Poisson distribution operator. Several analytic results were established, including sharp coefficient inequalities, inverse function estimates, and Fekete–Szegö bounds. The study also explored distortion, growth, convexity, subordination, and partial sums, thereby giving a comprehensive characterization of the newly defined class. Importantly, we bridged the gap between theoretical function theory and practical imaging by applying the results to MRI correction. The proposed analytic correction operator successfully reduced nonlinear distortions in MRI scans while preserving structural fidelity, as confirmed by quantitative metrics. This dual contribution–both theoretical advancement in geometric function theory and practical application to medical imaging–demonstrates the versatility of the introduced subclass. Future work may explore further applications in other imaging modalities and extend the framework to higher-dimensional settings.
