Cardiac surveillance of childhood cancer using artificial intelligence-enabled electrocardiograms
Authors: Ivor B Asztalos, Amy Li, Victoria L Vetter, John K Triedman, Joshua Mayourian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo assess the potential for artificial intelligence-enabled electrocardiogram (AI-ECG) to serve as a long-term cardiac surveillance tool and predict left ventricular systolic dysfunction in childhood cancer patients.Methods and resultsWe assessed performance of our previously established AI-ECG model to predict left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤50% and ≤40% in patients with childhood cancer during internal testing (Boston Children’s Hospital) and external validation (Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia). The external test cohort comprised 2964 patients [55.4% male; 7054 ECG–echo pairs at median age 11.6 (IQR 6.8–15.1) years; 2.5% with LVEF ≤50%; 0.9% with LVEF ≤40%]. Introduction

Significant progress in childhood cancer treatment has led to >80% survival of this population into adulthood.1 Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in this growing population of long-term childhood cancer survivors (CCS), underscoring the need for lifelong cardiac surveillance for early detection of left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) to prevent or delay heart failure onset. To date, long-term surveillance of long-term CCS is performed via echocardiogram,2 with recent work demonstrating the potential role of cardiac biomarkers to rule out LVSD and reduce unnecessary CCS echocardiogram surveillance.3

In adult cardio-oncology, artificial intelligence-enabled electrocardiograms (AI-ECGs) have shown promise as a non-invasive, inexpensive, and convenient tool to also reduce echocardiogram utilization and detect early signs of cardiovascular disease progression.4 However, similar technology has not yet been developed for childhood cancer.

In this study, we aim to address this technological gap by leveraging our previously established AI-ECG model5 and evaluating its internal and external performance on childhood cancer patients.

Methods

Details of our previously established AI-ECG model—trained to predict LV ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 50% (indicative of at least moderate cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction6) and ≤40% (indicative of severe cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction6) in paediatric and adult congenital heart disease patients—are shown elsewhere.5 Briefly, this model was developed on patient data from Boston Children’s Hospital using ∼125 000 ECG–echocardiogram pairs ≤2 days apart from ∼50 000 unique patients.

The internal test cohort comprised of oncology patients from Boston Children’s Hospital with ≥1 echocardiogram reporting LVEF that is ≤2 days apart from an ECG. Given our aim to assess longitudinal surveillance, we included echoes performed before, during, and active cancer treatment. There was no patient overlap between training and test cohorts. The external test cohort comprised of oncology patients from Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia with similar inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Oncology patients were identified via Fyler codes (internal cohort) and ICD-9 codes (external cohort). Oncological characteristics were identified for the internal cohort using Fyler codes. IRB approval was obtained.

Model performance was evaluated on the overall internal and external test groups using one randomly selected ECG–echo pair per patient. To optimize AI-ECG as a screening tool to reduce routine echocardiograms, performance metrics were calculated based on a cutoff achieving a 95% sensitivity in the training set. Confidence intervals were obtained via resampling with 1000 bootstraps.

If an ECG has an AI-ECG probability of LVEF ≤50% that is above the cutoff, then the ECG is deemed to have a high AI-ECG risk score. If an ECG has an AI-ECG prediction of LVEF ≤50% that is below the cutoff, then the ECG is deemed to have a low AI-ECG risk score.

Time-to-event analysis was performed on the internal test cohort as previously described5 for the following outcomes: All-cause mortality and LVSD onset (defined as LVEF ≤50%). Time-to-mortality was performed by: (i) stratifying patients into two groups (low or high AI-ECG risk score) based on a single randomly selected ECG per patient; (ii) assessing time-to-event after ECG within each group. Patients who did not experience all-cause mortality were censored at time last known alive. Time-to-LVSD onset analysis (where the event of interest was LVEF ≤50%) was performed by: (i) stratifying patients into two groups (low or high AI-ECG risk score) based on the initial ECG–echo pair; (ii) assessing time-to-event after ECG within each group. The initial ECG–echo pair per patient includes ECG–echo pairs before cancer treatment. Patients who did not experience LVSD onset were censored at the time of last echo. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate the association between AI-ECG risk score groups and time from ECG to the event of interest (all-cause mortality or LVEF ≤50%). Hazard ratios (HRs) were adjusted for age.

Results

The internal test cohort comprised 447 patients [57% male; 1553 ECG–echo pairs at median age 13.5 (IQR 7.7–17.9) years; 6.4% with LVEF ≤50%; 1.3% with LVEF ≤40%], 28% with leukaemia, 16% with lymphoma, 8% with neuroblastoma, 8% with sarcoma, 2% with gastrointestinal cancers, 3% with genitourinary cancers, 6% with central nervous system cancers, 11% with other/unspecified cancers, and 18% with missing/unknown cancer labels. Treatment strategies included anthracyclines (35%), bone marrow transplant (7%), and radiation (1%). In the internal test cohort, there were 68 (15%) mortalities at a median age of 14.5 (IQR 8.3–19.7) years. The external test cohort comprised 2964 patients [55.4% male; 7054 ECG–echo pairs at median age 11.6 (IQR 6.8–15.1) years; 2.5% with LVEF ≤50%; 0.9% with LVEF ≤40%].

Internal and external model performance for predicting LVEF ≤40% and LVEF ≤50% is shown in Figure 1A. For LVEF ≤50%, similar AUROCs (0.80–0.85), sensitivities (0.75–0.82), negative predictive values (NPVs) (0.986–0.996), and percent predicted negative (51–65%) were obtained across institutions. Notably, these AUROCs outperform a prior model utilizing the cardiac biomarkers n-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide and high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T and clinical characteristics (AUROC 0.73 for LVEF <50%).3

As shown in Figure 1B, AI-ECG classifications for LVEF ≤50% also hold prognostic value. Patients with high AI-ECG risk scores for LVEF ≤50% had higher rates of mortality [HR 3.1 (95% 1.8–5.3), P < 0.001] compared to patients with low AI-ECG risk scores. In patients whose LVEF was initially ≥50%, high AI-ECG risk scores still conferred a HR of 2.3 (95% CI 0.9–6.1) for future LVSD, although this trend did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.10).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first AI-ECG model to predict LVSD in children undergoing chemotherapy and in those who have survived cancer. Our model was validated both internally and externally, and outperforms a recently developed model that leverages both cardiac biomarkers and clinical characteristics as inputs.3 As shown in Figure 1A, an NPV of ≥99% is achieved across institutions for LVEF ≤50% and LVEF ≤40%, with the potential to decrease echo utilization by 51–90%. Our model holds prognostic value for predicting future risk of all-cause mortality, and even potentially future risk of LVSD (Figure 1B). Altogether, this demonstrates the promise of AI-ECG to serve as a screening tool to assist in the early diagnosis of cardiac sequalae in CCS.

Areas for future AI-ECG investigation in paediatric cardio-oncology include: (i) multi-centre external validation, with assessment of model performance across oncological disease and treatment subgroups; (ii) aggregating data across multiple centres to improve model performance, and refining thresholds to achieve clinically significant metrics (e.g. sensitivity); (iii) incorporating clinical characteristics and/or cardiac biomarkers to improve performance; (iv) development of a model using ECG photos as inputs (to improve accessibility for centres without access to digital waveforms)4; and (v) pragmatic randomized clinical trials to help guide clinical implementation and inform the cost-effectiveness of AI-ECG.
