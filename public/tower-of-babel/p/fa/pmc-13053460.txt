Chronic hypertension diagnosed in a previous pregnancy is associated with recurrent preeclampsia
Authors: Ying Gu, Yu Chen, Xiaohui Cao, Lingli Hu, Yingrui Fu, Jingyang Li, Yihui Pan, Qi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA history of preeclampsia is a significant risk factor for recurrence, especially in cases of early-onset or severe preeclampsia. Additional maternal risk factors present in the subsequent pregnancy include advanced maternal age, interval between pregnancies, and newly diagnosed chronic hypertension. However, the impact of chronic hypertension diagnosed during the previous pregnancy on recurrence risk remains underexplored.MethodsNinety-four women with a history of preeclampsia were followed into their subsequent pregnancies. The association of clinical parameters in their previous pregnancy and the subsequent pregnancy outcomes was analysed.ResultsAmong the 94 women, 56 (60%) did not develop any current hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), while 4 (5%) developed chronic hypertension, 12 (13%) developed gestational hypertension, and 22 (24%) developed recurrent preeclampsia. Notably, among the 10 women who were diagnosed with chronic hypertension in the previous pregnancy, 80% developed recurrent HDP, compared to 36% of those without it (odds ratio [OR], 7.200; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.538–34.71; P = 0.0133). In addition, 87% of women with recurrent hypertensive disorders had a cesarean section in their previous pregnancy, compared to 68% of those without recurrence (OR, 3.126; 95% CI, 1.077–8.279; P = 0.0356).ConclusionsWomen with preeclampsia complicated by chronic hypertension diagnosed in their previous pregnancies and those who underwent cesarean section in their previous pregnancies are at increased risk for recurrent HDP, including recurrent preeclampsia.

INTRODUCTION

Preeclampsia is a multi-organ, pregnancy-associated disorder, and is the leading cause of maternal and perinatal mortality and morbidity, affecting approximately 5–8% of all pregnancies [1]. Additionally, the risk of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies is relatively high, with 13% to 65% in hospital-based studies, depending on the population, region, and ethnicity [234]. Treatment with low-dose aspirin, low molecular weight heparin, and calcium supplementation may moderately reduce the risk of recurrence [56789].

Previous preeclampsia remains one of the strongest risk factors for developing recurrent preeclampsia in subsequent pregnancies [1011], including specific characteristics of the previous pregnancy, such as early onset or severe preeclampsia [12]. Cumulative risk factors for recurrent preeclampsia include a previous cesarean section [13], a higher maternal body mass index [12], advanced maternal age [12], newly diagnosed chronic hypertension [14], and a longer interval between 2 pregnancies [1516]. Notably, these risk factors can vary depending on the specific populations being studied.

Among these risk factors for developing recurrence, one cause for newly diagnosed chronic hypertension in a subsequent pregnancy can be previous preeclampsia. Increasing evidence indicates that women with a history of preeclampsia are at increased risk of developing cardiovascular diseases later, including hypertension [14]. A study reported a 14% incidence of developing hypertension in subsequent pregnancy in women with a previous hypertensive disorder of pregnancy (HDP) [17]. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis study reported a 2.26-fold increase in developing chronic hypertension in a subsequent pregnancy 5 years after previous preeclampsia [18]. Despite this, however, it remains unclear whether the presence of chronic hypertension during a previous pregnancy is associated with the recurrence. Chronic hypertension in pregnancy is defined as elevated blood pressure (BP) diagnosed before pregnancy or before 20 weeks of gestation, or that persists more than 12 weeks postpartum [19]. Therefore, we undertook this study to investigate whether chronic hypertension diagnosed before 20 weeks of gestation in a previous pregnancy is associated with the recurrence of HDP, including preeclampsia.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Wuxi Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital, Jiangnan University, Wuxi, China (approval numbers: 2022-01-0726-12). The consent form from individuals was waived due to the nature of the retrospective study. This study was performed in accordance with the Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Study cohort

A total of 94 women with a previous diagnosis of preeclampsia at a single hospital who had a subsequent pregnancy were included in this study. None of these women had chronic hypertension before their previous pregnancy. All these women received aspirin for the prevention of recurrent preeclampsia after the first prenatal visit in subsequent pregnancy. Additionally, these women received calcium from the second trimester (500 to 1,000 mg daily). Women who had normal BP before pregnancy but developed chronic hypertension before 20 weeks of gestation received anti-hypertensives, such as nifedipine or labetalol, for controlling BP. Data on maternal age at previous and subsequent pregnancy, parity, gravidity, gestational age of onset of previous and recurrent preeclampsia, gestational age at previous and following delivery, systolic and diastolic BP at the time of onset of previous and recurrent preeclampsia, interval between 2 pregnancies, BP at last discharge, and birthweight were collected from the hospital databases. Additionally, data on renal function from the 2 pregnancies were also collected.

According to International Society for Study Hypertensive Pregnancy guidelines, preeclampsia was defined when maternal systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg on 2 occasions between 6 hours with or without proteinuria (> 300 mg in 24 hours) and/or impaired liver function and/or lower platelet count, after 20 weeks of gestation [20]. Systolic BP ≥ 160 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 110 mmHg with impaired liver or renal function and/or lower platelet count was defined as severe preeclampsia. The onset of gestational week before 34 weeks was defined as early-onset preeclampsia, and after 34 weeks was described as late-onset preeclampsia. Gestational hypertension is defined as elevated BP (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg) occurring after 20 weeks of gestation, without proteinuria or other signs of preeclampsia, when previous BP readings were normal. Chronic hypertension was defined as elevated BP (systolic ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic ≥ 90 mm Hg) that was present before pregnancy (referred to as pre-existing chronic hypertension), or that develops before 20 weeks of gestation [19]. Elevated BP at discharge was defined as when systolic BP was ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP was ≥ 90 mmHg. HDP was defined as a group including chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, and preeclampsia. Renal dysfunction was defined as an elevated value of creatinine above 1.0 mg/dL.

Statistical analysis

Data on maternal age, interval between pregnancies, delivery weeks, and birth weight were expressed as means and standard deviations (Tables 1 and 2). Other data were expressed as numbers and proportions. A t-test (Mann-Whitney test) was performed to analyse maternal age and intervals between pregnancies using Prism GraphPad (version 10). The χ2 tests, including odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), were performed for data presented in Tables 3, 4, 5, 6 using Prism GraphPad (version 10). A statistically significant difference was considered when P < 0.05.

RESULTS

The clinical demographics of 94 women with a history of preeclampsia in a previous pregnancy are summarised in Table 1. The median interval between the 2 pregnancies was 46 months (ranging from 14 to 99 months). The mean maternal age at the time of the previous pregnancy was 27 years (± 3.5). Among these women, 21 (22%) experienced early-onset preeclampsia, and 31 (33%) had severe preeclampsia. Additionally, 10 women (11%) developed chronic hypertension. 29 (31%) women remained with elevated BP at discharge. Two of them had elevated creatinine at the time of discharge. In the subsequent pregnancy, the mean maternal age was 31 years (± 4.0). Pregnancy outcomes in the subsequent pregnancy are shown in Fig. 2. Of the 94 women, 56 (60%) did not develop any HDP, while 4 (4%) developed chronic hypertension, 12 (13%) developed gestational hypertension, and 22 (23%) developed recurrent preeclampsia. The overall incidence of HDP in the subsequent pregnancy was 40%. In total, 12 (13%) women were diagnosed with chronic hypertension during the subsequent pregnancy. There were no significant differences in maternal age in the subsequent pregnancy and interpregnancy between women who developed recurrent HDP and those who did not (P = 0.9463 or P = 0.9709, respectively).

We first grouped all women who developed any HDP in the subsequent pregnancy and assessed the association with chronic hypertension in the previous pregnancy. In total, 10 preeclamptic women developed chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy. As shown in Table 3, 8 (80%) women who developed chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy developed recurrent HDP, which was significantly higher than those women who did not develop chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy (36%, P = 0.0133). The OR for developing recurrent HDP was 7.200 (95% CI, 1.538–34.71) for women who developed chronic hypertension in the previous pregnancy, compared to those who did not develop chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy.

We then specifically examined recurrent preeclampsia as the outcome. As shown in Table 4, 5 (71%) women with chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy developed recurrent preeclampsia, which was significantly higher than those women who did not develop chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy (24%, P = 0.017). The OR for developing recurrent HDP in women who developed chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy was 7.941 (95% CI, 1.417–41.37).

We also examined the association of elevated BP at the time of discharge after the previous pregnancy and recurrent HDP. As shown in Table 5, 38% of women who had elevated BP at discharge in their previous pregnancy developed recurrent HDP, but this was not significantly different from the rate among women who had normal BP at discharge (45%, P = 0.6507).

Finally, we assessed whether the mode of delivery in the previous pregnancy is a cumulative risk factor for developing recurrent HDP. As shown in Table 6, 87% of women who had a cesarean section in their previous pregnancy developed recurrent HDP, which was significantly higher than that of women who did not develop recurrent HDP (68%, P = 0.0356). The OR for developing recurrent HDP in women with a previous cesarean section was 3.126 (95% CI, 1.077–8.279).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective study, we found a significantly higher OR for developing recurrent HDP in women who developed chronic hypertension in their previous pregnancy. This association was particularly evident among women who developed recurrent preeclampsia. Additionally, we observed significantly higher OR for developing recurrent HDP in women who had a cesarean section in their previous pregnancy. In contrast, elevated BP at the time of discharge following their previous pregnancy was not associated with an increased risk of recurrent HDP.

There is overwhelming evidence that previous preeclampsia is a major risk factor for developing recurrent preeclampsia, including specific clinical parameters of the previous pregnancy, such as early onset or severe preeclampsia [101112]. Increasing evidence suggests that women with a prior preeclampsia are more likely to develop chronic hypertension in the years following pregnancy [1718], which may further increase the risk of recurrence [14]. However, it remains unclear whether chronic hypertension developed during the previous pregnancy is a cumulative risk factor for recurrence. In our current study, we found that women who had chronic hypertension diagnosed before 20 weeks in a previous pregnancy had an OR of 7.200 (95% CI, 1.538–34.71; P = 0.0133) for developing recurrent HDP in the subsequent pregnancy, compared with women who did not have chronic hypertension. This elevated risk was most pronounced among those who developed recurrent preeclampsia. Our findings suggest that when preeclampsia occurs in the context of chronic hypertension, it may be identified as a high-risk factor of recurrent HDP, including preeclampsia, in future pregnancies.

Although the underlying mechanism of our findings is unclear, women who developed chronic hypertension during pregnancy may already have early impaired vascular function, inadequate remodelling of spiral arteries, and systemic endothelial dysfunction before clinical diagnosis of preeclampsia, all of which precede the clinical onset of preeclampsia [21]. These pathological changes can result in increased vulnerability to recurrence of preeclampsia or gestational hypertension in subsequent pregnancies, suggesting these women carry a higher recurrence risk. Therefore, preeclampsia caused by chronic hypertension may provide a critical opportunity for early risk stratification and targeted surveillance in subsequent pregnancies.

Previous studies reported that a significant proportion of preeclamptic women experience persistently elevated BP after delivery or at discharge [22]. Specific recommendations to closely monitor and/or control BP after delivery have been suggested to reduce the risk of long-term cardiovascular diseases [23]. Whether persistently elevated BP is associated with the risk of developing recurrent preeclampsia has not been fully explored. In our current study, we found that there was no significant difference in the proportion of women with elevated BP at the time of previous discharge between women who developed recurrent HDP and those who did not. Although persistently elevated BP after delivery or discharge can lead to serious complications, such as cardiovascular diseases, including chronic hypertension later in life, our findings suggest it may not be a valuable predictor of developing recurrent HDP.

A previous study indicated that a previous cesarean section, even following a normotensive pregnancy, is a risk factor for developing preeclampsia in the subsequent pregnancy [13]. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that preeclamptic women who had a previous cesarean section are at significantly higher risk of developing recurrent HDP. One possible explanation is that uterine scarring from cesarean section may impair placental implantation and spiral artery remodelling, contributing to abnormal placentation, which are well-known mechanisms in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, data on cesarean sections collected from the hospital database were not classified into planned or emergency cesarean sections groups. This limitation suggests that the observed significant association between cesarean sections and recurrent HDP may be influenced by a decision bias, where emergency cesarean sections were potentially performed due to the severity of preeclampsia.

This study has several limitations. First, although the total sample size was moderate, the number of women with recurrent preeclampsia is relatively small, limiting our ability to conduct detailed subgroup analyses. Second, due to the retrospective nature of our study, data on interpregnancy lifestyle factors were not available, which may represent confounding variables associated with the development of recurrent HDP, including preeclampsia. Third, recent studies led by Kitt highlighted the importance of postpartum blood control in preventing future vascular diseases, particularly physician-guided medication titration, which significantly improves long-term BP control and regresses adverse cardiac remodelling [242526]. Unfortunately, specific data on individual medication adherence and BP management during the previous and current pregnancy were not available. Future follow-up studies are required to confirm the observed association.

In conclusion, our study reported that preeclamptic women with chronic hypertension in the previous pregnancy represent a higher risk for recurrent HDP, including recurrent preeclampsia. Furthermore, the previous cesarean section appears to be an additional cumulative risk factor for recurrent HDP. These findings underscore the importance of early identification and close monitoring of high-risk women in subsequent pregnancies.
