Unraveling the mechanism of action of the PepGAT peptide against Candida albicans through proteomics approaches
Authors: Francisco I R Gomes, Queilane L S G Chaves, Nicholas S S Filho, João L T Mororó, Patrícia G Lima, Rômulo F Carneiro, Celso S Nagano, Débora C B S C Maia, Raquel C Montenegro, Marlon H Cardoso, Felipe P Mesquita, Octavio Luiz Franco, Pedro F N Souza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandida albicans is a major opportunistic human pathogen, frequently implicated in infections with increasing antifungal resistance. As such, the search for new therapeutic alternatives is essential. Therefore, synthetic antimicrobial peptides (SAMPs) have emerged as promising alternatives because resistance development against these molecules appears to occur less frequently than against many conventional antimicrobial drugs. Here, the mechanisms of action of the chitinase-derived synthetic peptide PepGAT, which is bioinspired by the chitinase of Arabidopsis thaliana, were evaluated against C. albicans. Proteomic analysis, enzymatic activity assays, ergosterol interaction studies, and membrane permeability assessments were performed to provide insights into how PepGAT affects C. albicans. Proteomic analysis revealed that PepGAT alters the protein profile of C. albicans cells, disrupting several key pathways involved in cell development, lipid metabolism, and drug resistance. Moreover, PepGAT decreased superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity while increasing catalase (CAT) activity, suggesting an impact on the cellular redox system. Additionally, PepGAT inhibits ergosterol biosynthesis, potentially leading to the formation of membrane pores with a size of at least 6 kDa, as suggested by fluorescence microscopy. Altogether, these findings indicate that PepGAT exhibits multiple mechanisms of action against C. albicans, highlighting its potential as an alternative antifungal agent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57568-1.Keywords: Mechanism of action, Chitinase-derived peptide, Synthetic peptides, Candida albicansSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

The first report of antifungal resistance (AFR) dates back to the 1960s, when a dermatophyte acquired resistance to griseofulvin1. Since then, AFR has caused severe problems with food security and public health worldwide. This emergence of resistance is a result of the natural process of evolution, which poses several threats to immunocompromised patients2. This is a complex phenomenon that encompasses everything from hospital care to environmental and societal issues. Nowadays, 150 million people are infected with fungi, with 1.8 million deaths. In 2023, more than 3.8 million deaths were attributed to fungal infections3.

In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) classified C. albicans as a critical-priority pathogenic fungus for human health. Denning (2024) reported candidemia as the third most threatening invasive mycoses and the second leading cause of death3,4. C. albicans colonizes human skin; however, it becomes an opportunistic microorganism that mainly affects immunocompromised patients, causing superficial and systemic infections that can even lead to death5. Furthermore, C. albicans exhibits several virulence factors, including biofilm formation, a transition from its yeast-like morphological form to pseudohyphae or hyphae, and the production of hydrolytic enzymes6,7.

Recently, a second-generation echinocandin, rezafungin, has emerged as a potential treatment for fungal infections, including those caused by Candida spp., including those with FKS mutations8,9. Moreover, some fungi are intrinsically resistant to rezafungin (e.g., Fusarium spp. and Cryptococcus spp.) due to the F684Y mutation in FKS, which is equivalent to the mutation present in C. albicans (F641X)8. This data suggests that although no resistance has occurred yet, C. albicans may develop resistance to rezafungin10. Thus, by combining the limited number of antifungal classes currently available, with numerous reported side effects and the resistance of C. albicans, the development of new drug therapies has become imperative11.

In this scenario, the use of antifungal protein-derived synthetic antimicrobial peptides (SAMPs) emerges as an alternative, as they have shown effective antifungal potential and are less likely to induce fungal resistance12. Recently, our research group evaluated the antimicrobial potential of plant protein-derived SAMPs against pathogenic fungi and bacteria13. As a result, a synthetic peptide (PepGAT) bioinspired by a chitinase of Arabidopsis thaliana showed strong antifungal potential against C. albicans13. Therefore, the present study focused on investigating the mechanisms of action of the PepGAT peptide toward C. albicans using a suite of proteomic analyses, revealing significant alterations in the protein profile. Morphological alterations indicating pore formation in the fungal cell membrane and disturbances in redox homeostasis were assessed by measuring the activity of enzymes involved in detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS). Altogether, these findings provide insights into the multifactorial antifungal activity of PepGAT and support its prospects as a lead peptide candidate with promising clinical translation potential.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

Not applied for this study.

Biological material and synthetic peptide chemical synthesis

The C. albicans strain (ATCC 10231) was obtained from the Laboratory of Applied Bioinformatics in Health, at the Center for Research and Development of Medicines (NPDM), Federal University of Ceará, Fortaleza, Brazil. The synthetic peptide PepGAT was designed as described previously13 and chemically synthesized by ChemPeptide (Shanghai, China) with a purity of greater than 98% 13.

Antifungal activity

Antifungal activity was evaluated according to the methodology previously described by Dias et al. (2020). Initially, the C. albicans strain was cultured on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar plates for 24 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, a yeast colony was transferred to liquid Sabouraud Dextrose medium. After 24 h, the cells were standardized to a concentration of (2.5 × 10³ CFU mL⁻¹). Then, a 50 µL aliquot of the C. albicans cell suspension was mixed with 50 µL of PepGAT peptide at a concentration of 50 µg mL⁻¹ (minimal inhibitory concentration to reach 50% of inhibition) as determined in previous work. (Souza et al., 2020). The mixture was prepared in a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After this period, cell growth was measured using a microplate reader at 600 nm (Epoch, Biotek, Santa Clara, CA, USA). All assays were performed in triplicate, and the negative control was a 5% DMSO solution in 0.15 M NaCl.

Protein extraction

To obtain the protein extract, 1 mL of each sample was collected, as described in the antifungal activity assay. Then, following Branco et al.14, three washes were performed using 500 µL of acetate buffer (500 mM, pH 5.2), followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Samples were resuspended in 300 µL of acetate buffer and frozen at -20 °C for 24 h. After this period, samples were thawed by sonication for 30 min. Following thawing, samples were centrifuged again (10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was discarded, and the supernatant was used for enzymatic activity analysis.

Mass spectrometry

Protein extraction was performed as described above. Control and treated cell samples were incubated with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark to reduce protein disulfide bonds. After this step, iodoacetamide was added to a final concentration of 15 mM, and the samples were incubated in the dark for 30 min to alkylate the reduced proteins. Proteins were then digested with trypsin gold (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at a 1:20 (w/w) ratio according to the manufacturer’s instructions for 16 h at 37 °C. After digestion, samples were dried using a speed vacuum (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) for 3 h and analyzed by ESI-QUAD-TOF mass spectrometry14.

Protein identification

Protein identification was performed according to the method previously described by Branco et al.14. Tandem mass spectra were exported as .pkl files and submitted to MS/MS ion search using MASCOT (MATRIXSCIENCE, https://www.matrixscience.com/cgi/search_form.pl?FORMVER=2&SEARCH=MIS, accessed on April 15, 2023), using the UP2311_S_cerevisiae, UP219602_F_oxysporum, and SwissProt databases. Search parameters included carbamidomethylation (C) as a fixed modification and oxidation (M) as a variable modification; peptide charge states set to 2+, 3+, and 4+; and the instrument set to ESI-QUAD-TOF. Identified proteins were researched in UNIPROT and grouped into three categories: (1) unique to the control (proteins identified only in control samples), (2) unique to PepGAT-treated cells (proteins identified only in treated samples), and (3) shared proteins (proteins identified in both control and treated samples). Proteins with a fold-change value ≥ 1.5 (p < 0.05, Tukey’s test) were considered upregulated (increased abundance), whereas those with a fold-change ≤ 0.5 (p < 0.05, Tukey’s test) were considered downregulated (decreased abundance) and included in the comparisons. Proteins with fold-change between 0.5 and 1.5 were considered unchanged. For each protein, the corresponding .fasta file was downloaded. The Blast2GO software (https://www.blast2go.com/, accessed on June 6, 2025) was used to categorize the detected proteins using Gene Ontology (GO) annotations for molecular function, biological process, and subcellular localization.

Interaction with ergosterol and inhibition of ergosterol biosynthesis

The assay was conducted in 96-well plates containing yeast inoculum (2.5 × 10³ CFU mL⁻¹), ergosterol diluted in Tween 80 at different concentrations (200, 400, and 800 µgmL⁻¹), PepGAT (50 µg mL⁻¹), and Sabouraud Dextrose medium in a 1:1:1:1 (v/v) ratio, totaling 200 µL per well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, fungal growth was evaluated by spectrophotometry at 620 nm. The negative control consisted of 96% ethanol and Tween 80 (1:1, v/v), which were the solvents used to prepare the sterol solutions15.

Redox system enzymatic activity

Catalase (CAT)

CAT activity was determined following the methodology described by Souza et al.16, in which 800 µL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) was incubated with 100 µL of protein extract from treated and untreated samples at 30 °C for 10 min. Then, 100 µL of 112 mM H₂O₂ was added to initiate the reaction. The mixture was immediately transferred to a quartz cuvette (1 cm path length) for absorbance readings at 240 nm using a spectrophotometer (Biochron, Libra 394 S12). Absorbance decrease was measured every 10 s for up to 1 min. A reduction of 1.0 absorbance unit per minute was considered equivalent to one unit of catalase activity per mL (U mgP⁻¹).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD)

SOD activity was determined as previously described by Van-Rossum et al.17. In a 96-well plate, the reaction included 10 µL of 1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 20 µL of 1 mM EDTA, 10 µL of 0.25% Triton X-100, 20 µL of 130 mM L-methionine, 100 µL of protein extract, 20 µL of 100 mM riboflavin, and 20 µL of 750 µM NBT. The reaction was mixed and kept in the dark for 5 min. Then, the first absorbance reading was taken at 630 nm (time 0). The plate was then exposed to 32 W fluorescent light for 5 min, with absorbance readings taken every minute at 630 nm. The experiment was conducted in triplicate for both treated and untreated (5% DMSO) control samples.

Membrane pore formation assay

Membrane integrity was evaluated as previously described by Dias et al.18 to assess pore size formation. C. albicans cells were treated as described in the antifungal activity assay, washed with 0.15 M NaCl solution, and centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cells were then incubated with 10 µM fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated dextran (6 kDa) (Sigma-Aldrich, São Paulo, SP, Brazil) for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark. After incubation, cells were re-washed, and fluorescence was measured using a Cytation 3 Cell Multi-Mode Reader (BioTek®) with excitation at 490 nm and emission at 520 nm.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The samples for SEM analysis were prepared as described in the antifungal assay section, following the methodology previously published by Staniszewska et al.19. The images were taken using a low-energy equipment model FEI Inspect TM50.

Antibiofilm potential of PepGAT

The antibiofilm potential of PepGAT was tested against the unformed biofilm (early stage) and preformed biofilm (later stage) of C. albicans following the methodology of Neto et al.20. The experiment was performed in three biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Prior to statistical analysis, all datasets were tested for normality and met the assumptions required for parametric analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 5.01) for Microsoft Windows. Data obtained from the assays were compared by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s test (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Proteomic profile of C. albicans cells treated with PepGAT

Proteomics, combined with mass spectrometry, has emerged as a powerful tool for studying antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and their mechanisms of action against various microorganisms21. This approach enables researchers to analyze subtle changes in the protein profile in microorganisms after treatment with AMPs, providing detailed insights into the alterations triggered by interactions with a particular AMP14. Additionally, to better understand the mechanism of action in this work, we performed proteomic analysis to evaluate changes in C. albicans treated with PepGAT.

Our results showed interesting alterations in the protein profile. In control cells, 319 proteins were detected (Supplementary Table 1). By contrast, in treated cells, 117 proteins were observed (Supplementary Table 2). Following antifungal treatment, a reduction in proteins involved in vital processes, including cell growth, metabolism, and cell wall formation, was observed, which could lead to cell death22–24.

Additionally, about six proteins showed overlap between the two groups analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 3). Among these overlapping proteins, a fold-change value (relative variation of accumulated proteins) was considered; proteins with an abundance variation ≥ 1.5 were classified as up-accumulated (increased abundance), whereas values ≤ 0.5 indicated down-accumulated proteins (decreased abundance) (Supplementary Fig. 1)13, and proteins with values between 0.5 and 1.5 were considered that did not change. All the proteins were analyzed and classified by molecular function and biological process using Gene Ontology information.

The shared proteins (Supplementary Table 3) were classified regarding their molecular function into six groups, with each protein assigned to one of the following categories: DNA and RNA Binding, Transport or Structural Activity, Oxidoreductase, Unknown, Multifunctional Enzyme, and Ion Binding, resulting in an equal distribution of 16.6% for each group (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Regarding biological processes, four groups were observed: two proteins were assigned to the Other Metabolites group, two to the Unknown group, and one protein to each of the Transport and Regulation Factor or Signaling groups. This corresponds to approximately 33% for the first two groups and 17% for the remaining groups (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

For the proteins exclusively found in the control and PepGAT-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 2C-F), classification was also performed based on Gene Ontology results. In terms of Molecular Function, 13 categories were identified: Carbohydrate Binding, Chaperone, DNA and RNA Binding, Hydrolase, Transport or Structural Activity, Oxidoreductase, Ligase, Protein Binding, Transferase, Ubiquination, Unknown, Multifunctional Enzyme, and Ion Binding. Regarding Biological Process, 13 categories were also identified: Pathogenicity, Lipid Metabolism, Energetic Metabolism, Cell Function and Structure, Protein Folding and Metabolism, Nucleic Acid Metabolism, Amino Acid Metabolism, Carbohydrate Binding, Stress and Defense Response, Other Metabolites, Transport, Regulation Factor or Signaling, and Unknown.

Six proteins were shared; only three proteins had their abundance altered: Methylmalonate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase (CoAacylating) decreased in abundance (0.08-fold-change – Supplementary Table 3). This enzyme catalyzes the irreversible oxidative decarboxylation of malonate- and methylmalonate-semialdehydes to acetyl- and propionyl-CoA, being part of the valine and pyrimidine catabolic pathways25. Studies have demonstrated that the deletion of this enzyme severely affects conidiation, appressoria formation, and pathogenesis in Magnaporthe oryzae26. The downregulation of this protein in PepGAT-treated cells could disrupt amino acid homeostasis and affect protein synthesis.

Ras-related protein Sect. 4 was another protein from the overlapping group that was also down-accumulated after PepGAT treatment. This protein is involved in the secretory pathway in yeast, specifically in targeting and fusing secretory vesicles from the Golgi to the plasma membrane27. Disruption of Golgi-derived secretory vesicles was observed, resulting in their accumulation27,28. Additionally, cell division was impaired, and morphological changes were observed in the mutated cells. The cells exhibited a swollen and misshapen morphology, indicating that this protein is crucial for maintaining a healthy cell shape.

Proteins related to lipid metabolism

Two proteins, phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase LSB6 (PI4K) and Squalene monooxygenase, essential for sterol transport, such as ergosterol, in the cell membrane, were exclusively detected in the control cells. By contrast, the protein Membrane-anchored lipid-binding protein LAM4 was solely detected in the PepGAT-treated group. PI4K participates in the synthesis of phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P), a crucial component of the fungal plasma membrane that affects hyphal growth, cell wall organization, vesicular trafficking, and the maintenance of lipid homeostasis in membranes, including ergosterol29,30. The presence of this protein in the control group suggests a structurally stable cell membrane, whereas its absence in PepGAT-treated cells suggests a weak, possibly damaged membrane.

Similarly, squalene monooxygenase (SQMO), exclusively found in the control group, catalyzes the introduction of an oxygen atom to the squalene substrate, converting it to 2,3-oxidosqualene, considered a rate-limiting enzyme in steroid biosynthesis31, indicating that the absence of this protein in PepGAT-treated cells led to a perturbation in steroid biosynthesis, including ergosterol. Studies showed that disruption of both alleles of ERG1 in C. albicans was lethal32. This caused growth defects and increased susceptibility to antifungals (e.g., fluconazole, ketoconazole, cycloheximide, nystatin, amphotericin B, and terbinafine)32.

In cells treated with the PepGAT peptide, the membrane-anchored lipid-binding protein LAM4 was identified, suggesting an adaptive response to treatment. LAM4 is involved in sterol homeostasis in response to stress induced by azole antifungals, increasing the total sterol concentration and their accumulation in cells33. Therefore, the presence of LAM4 may indicate a compensatory mechanism induced by cells to regulate sterol metabolism after exposure to the PepGAT peptide, which was confirmed to interact with ergosterol (Fig. 1).

As indicated by proteomic analysis, PepGAT interferes with ergosterol metabolism. As previously reported by our research group34,35, PepGAT inhibits 75% of ergosterol biosynthesis in Cryptococcus neoformans. Here, it was observed that PepGAT inhibited ergosterol biosynthesis in C. albicans by 66%, compared to 82% inhibition by Itraconazole (Fig. 1A). Additionally, our data indicate that PepGAT causes membrane damage and pore formation; we further investigated how PepGAT interacts with ergosterol in the fungal membrane (Fig. 1B).

To assess this, we evaluated its interaction with ergosterol in the C. albicans membrane. The anticandidal activity of PepGAT (MIC50) was markedly reduced in the presence of ergosterol, with inhibition levels of 18.98% at 200 µg mL− 1 and 23.32% at 400 µg mL− 1, compared to the control (Fig. 1B). However, at the highest tested concentration (800 µg mL− 1), the inhibition rate reached 45.95%, which did not differ significantly from the control (Fig. 1B).

Ergosterol is a crucial sterol component of fungal cell membranes, essential for maintaining membrane structure, function, and permeability36. It plays a vital role in fungal adaptation to environmental stress, nutrient transport, and host–pathogen interactions, and is closely linked to antifungal resistance and virulence36–38. Our results indicate that PepGAT has a higher affinity for free ergosterol molecules; however, in their absence, it likely interacts with ergosterol in the fungal membrane. Suggesting that PepGAT not only interacts with ergosterol, but also that its interaction is crucial for its anticandidal activity against C. albicans. Similar results were obtained by Aguiar et al.35 using the same peptide against C. neoformans.

Found exclusively in the control group, the protein Lysophospholipase ARB (LyARB) is involved in the hydrolysis of phospholipids, which is crucial for epithelial invasion by various classes of fungi39. Furthermore, in C. albicans, this protein plays a critical role in phospholipid remodeling and membrane composition40. Therefore, its absence in the PepGAT-treated group may indicate disruption of the phospholipid recycling pathway and reduced virulence in infections.

By contrast, two essential virulence proteins were found in cells treated with PepGAT: phosphatidylserine decarboxylase proenzyme one mitochondrial (Psd1) and Patatin-like phospholipase (PLP). Psd1 is responsible for the synthesis of phosphatidylethanolamine from phosphatidylserine and is essential for C. albicans virulence41, and PLP is associated with membrane damage repair, antifungal resistance, oxidative stress response, and maintenance of virulence42.

Studies have shown that the lipid composition of the plasma membrane, influenced by Psd1 and PLP, affects membrane permeability and increases drug susceptibility to azoles, making cells more sensitive to these antifungals42. Therefore, although the presence of Psd1 and PLP is vital for virulence, their presence in the PepGAT-treated group may reflect a cellular attempt to remodel the membrane to resist the antifungal actions of the peptide.

Previously, it was reported that PepGAT induced ROS overproduction and propidium iodide uptake in C. albicans cells13. Here, we aimed to delve deeper and understand whether, in addition to affecting membrane permeability, the PepGAT peptide could also induce pore formation. Therefore, we used dextran with a molecular weight of 6 kDa, conjugated with FITC (fluorescein isothiocyanate), to estimate pore size (Fig. 2). Thus, green fluorescence detection showed that PepGAT (50 µg mL− 1 Fig. 2D) induced pores of at least 6 kDa, allowing dextran to pass through the C. albicans membrane. Cell membrane integrity is essential for fungi, as it affects their pathogenicity, resistance to antifungal agents, and overall cellular functions.

Pore formation can cause apoptosis43, dissipation of ionic gradients, and a loss of membrane potential, resulting in cell lysis44. These findings highlight the complexity of the cellular response to PepGAT, suggesting that exposure to it may prompt an attempt to reorganize phospholipid metabolism by modulating proteins involved in membrane degradation and remodeling, which may be directly related to PepGAT’s antifungal action.

Proteins related to energy metabolism

The isocitrate lyase (ISOL), exclusively found in control cells, is an enzyme involved in maintaining intracellular redox balance, which is essential for metabolic adaptations. Furthermore, it enables C. albicans to metabolically adapt by using alternative carbon sources, which is crucial for survival in nutrient-limited environments45. However, the absence of this protein in PepGAT-treated cells suggests that PepGAT disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, thereby reducing the likelihood of survival under stress conditions.

Another essential protein from the control group was alternative oxidase (AOX). This protein is a secondary terminal oxidase found in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it receives electrons from reduced ubiquinone and catalyzes the reduction of oxygen to water46. Studies have suggested that in C. albicans, Aox may also function in resistance to oxidative stress and tolerance to high iron environments, acting as a safety valve in the electron transport chain, since it is upregulated under these stress conditions47.

Moreover, deletion of Aox in C. albicans leads to increased susceptibility to Fluconazole47,48. Corroborating Aox’s role in oxidative stress prevention. As Aox is not present in human mitochondria, it represents a relevant target for the development of antifungal drugs49. The absence of lyase (ISOL) and AOX in the PepGAT-treated cells may indicate a disruption of the cell’s redox homeostasis.

Proteins related to transport function and cell structure

In this class, the following proteins were exclusively identified in control cells: Serine/threonine protein kinase (KIN1), azole resistance protein 1 (AZR1), oligomycin resistance ATP-dependent permease YOR1, and KNR4/SMI1 homolog. The presence of these three proteins is directly associated with essential processes for fungal adaptations, such as survival under adverse conditions, resistance to oxidative stress, and maintenance of cell wall integrity50.

KIN1 is associated with stress responses and cell growth regulation, which are crucial for fungal virulence. Studies have shown that kinase proteins like KIN1 play roles in fundamental cellular processes related to biofilm formation and adaptation to hostile environments in C. albicans51. Additionally, the deletion of KIN1 affects the susceptibility of A. fumigatus to antifungals targeting the cell wall and membrane, suggesting an essential role in maintaining cell wall integrity52.

In addition, the AZR1 protein, a membrane transporter from the major facilitator superfamily (MFS), is associated with resistance to azole antifungals, promoting the efflux of these compounds to reduce their toxicity53. In S. cerevisiae, AZR1 confers resistance to azoles, enabling survival under high selective pressure from these antifungals54. Similarly, Oligomycin resistance ATP-dependent permease YOR1 is a plasma membrane ABC drug transporter involved in oligomycin and organic anion resistance, contributing to drug resistance development55,56. The absence of both AZR1 and YOR1 in PepGAT-treated cells suggests a break in the resistance phenotype of C. albicans, rendering them susceptible to antifungals once more.

In this regard, a combined application of PepGAT likely reverses C. albicans resistance to ITR. A previous published study by our research group has already reported the antifungal potential of PepGAT against C. albicans.13 Here, a combined action of ITR alone and combined with PepGAT at a MIC50 concentration (50 µg mL− 1) was performed. Alone, in curve with 11 ITR concentrations, only the 500 µg mL− 1 concentration barely reached 15% inhibition of C. albicans growth (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the combination of ITR (some concentrations) with PepGAT, but the best combination was between ITR 250 µg mL− 1 + PepGAT 50 µg mL− 1 (Fig. 8B). PepGAT has already shown combined activity with 5-FU against colorectal cancer57 and with ITR against C. neoformans.

PepGAT may exert this effect in two ways: (1) as revealed by proteomics, reducing the accumulation of proteins involved in resistance against ITR; or (2) by inducing the membrane permeability and pore formation. Turns out, PepGAT not only acts against C. albicans but also could help a currently useless drug to become the game again.

The fungus-specific protein KNR4/SMI1 homolog plays a crucial role in cell wall integrity and is essential for stress resistance in various species. Its deletion results in hypersensitivity to antifungal agents and cell wall stress, whereas its overexpression increases cell adhesion58. The absence of these proteins in cells treated with PepGAT suggests an effect on the cell wall integrity, stress response, and antifungal resistance mechanisms. The reduced abundance of KIN1 and KNR4/SMI1 homologs may indicate weakened cell structure, making C. albicans more susceptible to antifungal agents and environmental stress.

By contrast, the peroxisomal membrane protein PEX17, a peripheral membrane protein associated with peroxisomes, was exclusively in PepGAT-treated cells. Peroxisomes are organelles essential for lipid metabolism and the response to oxidative stress, participating in fatty acid β-oxidation and maintaining redox homeostasis59. In yeasts, PEX17 is involved in ROS detoxification through catalase activity35. The presence of this protein in treated cells supports the hypothesis that PepGAT induces oxidative stress, stimulating the activation of antioxidant pathways to compensate for cellular damage, as demonstrated by redox enzyme assays.

It has already been described that another possible mode of action of PepGAT against C. albicans is the induction of ROS overproduction13. Souza et al. 13 reported H2O2 accumulation in C. albicans treated with PepGAT, as detected using fluorescence microscopy with 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Together with the proteomic data presented here, we reasoned that PepGAT could interfere with the redox enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) in C. albicans (Fig. 4). The control cells showed an activity of 0.80 AU mgP⁻¹. In contrast, PepGAT-treated cells showed an activity of 0.417 AU mgP⁻¹, a two-fold reduction in SOD activity after treatment with PepGAT (Fig. 4A). For CAT, an enzyme that scavenges H2O2, control cells showed an activity of 7.2 AU mgP⁻¹, and cells treated with PepGAT showed 12.26 AU mgP⁻¹ of CAT activity (Fig. 4B). The results presented here are consistent with other studies with PepGAT.

Previous work by our research group also showed a similar decrease in SOD activity of C. neoformans cells treated with PepGAT35. Although H2O2 is a more stable molecule than O2−, it can still cause damage to the cell. By contrast, the increased CAT activity in treated cells may reflect a compensatory response to oxidative stress induced by PepGAT; however, it did not prevent H2O2 accumulation13. We hypothesize that PepGAT disrupts the cellular redox balance, leading to oxidative stress. The observed increase in CAT activity represents a cellular defense mechanism, suggesting modulation of antioxidant responses to minimize damage caused by homeostatic imbalance, particularly in the context of decreased SOD activity.

Additionally, our proteomic analyses support these enzymatic findings, revealing that PepGAT-treated cells lack Isocitrate lyase, which typically maintains redox balance, and instead express D-lactate dehydrogenase [cytochrome], which regulates ROS. Additionally, the presence of PEX17 and YAP1 suggests activation of antioxidant pathways, whereas SAS3 may modulate stress-response gene expression. Together, these changes indicate that PepGAT affects redox homeostasis, leading to decreased SOD activity and a compensatory increase in CAT activity, reflecting a coordinated cellular response to oxidative stress aimed at maintaining cell viability.

These findings are consistent with the experimental results analyzed, which show that PepGAT, in addition to affecting redox homeostasis by stimulating antioxidant pathway activation to compensate for cellular damage, also interacts with the membrane, leading to pore formation. These observations further reinforce PepGAT’s potential as an antifungal agent that can enhance the activity of certain conventional drugs, such as azole-based agents.

Proteins related to signaling or regulation

In cells treated with PepGAT, proteins involved in transcription regulation and chromatin remodeling were exclusively accumulated, such as chromatin structure-remodeling complex protein (RSC30), AP-1-like transcription factor YAP1, and Mediator of RNA polymerase II. The presence of these proteins suggests that C. albicans activates adaptive mechanisms in response to PepGAT-induced stress, promoting changes in gene expression and adjustments in cellular homeostasis.

RSC30 is part of the RSC complex (Remodeling the Structure of Chromatin) and is crucial for regulating gene expression, maintaining cell integrity, and adapting to stress conditions60. Furthermore, the presence of RSC30 is crucial for repairing ROS-induced DNA damage, which is essential for cell survival and genomic integrity61. Thus, the presence of RSC30 in the treated group suggests that the imbalance in redox enzymes (Fig. 9) and overaccumulation of ROS13 induced by PepGAT may lead to DNA damage in C. albicans.

The AP-1-like transcription factor YAP1 plays a crucial role in the response to oxidative stress. In C. albicans and C. neoformans, YAP1 is essential for the hydrogen peroxide response, promoting the expression of antioxidant genes such as SOD1 and SOD2 to scavenge accumulated ROS and balance ROS levels to maintain homeostasis62,63. Therefore, an increase in YAP1 abundance in PepGAT-treated cells may activate YAP1, which could regulate the expression of antioxidant genes, including catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), whose activities were analyzed by biochemical assays. It appears that PepGAT interferes with both SOD and CAT activities (Fig. 4), leading to elevated ROS levels in Candida cells.

Proteins related to nucleic acid and carbohydrate metabolism

Histone acetyltransferase SAS3 is a key regulator of gene expression, modulating chromatin structure through histone acetylation. In A. fumigatus, SAS3 has functions beyond histone acetylation. It was reported that a non-canonical function of SAS3 is related to cell wall integrity, enabling resistance to external cell wall-perturbing agents64. As shown in Fig. 5, PepGAT-treated C. albicans cells exhibited a damaged cell wall and altered cell morphology. Additionally, it was noted that the absence of histone chaperone Asf1 and protein Elg1 in PepGAT-treated cells, whose deletions in S. cerevisiae disrupt DNA replication, repair, transcription, genome instability, and histone modification65–67.

Changes in the morphology of C. albicans cells induced by PepGAT revealed by Scanning Electron Microscopy. (A) Control C. albicans cells present a normal morphology. (B-D) PepGAT-treated cells exhibit damage to the cell wall and cell structure, leading to the formation of broken cells.

The absence of thymidylate synthase (TS) only in the PepGAT-treated cells suggests an interruption of the DNA biosynthesis pathway during the cell division cycle68. Once, TS catalyzes the methylation of deoxyuridylate to thymidylate, a precursor in DNA biosynthesis69. Regarding carbohydrate metabolism, alpha-1,2 mannosyltransferase (KTR1) is an enzyme involved in cell wall biosynthesis, specifically in the modification of glycans through the addition of mannose residues. This modification is crucial for maintaining cell wall integrity, promoting conidiation, enhancing stress tolerance, and influencing virulence70,71. Moreover, the deletion of genes encoding these enzymes generally results in attenuated virulence, highlighting their importance in the pathogenic development of fungi, which affects adhesion and biofilm formation72.

The accumulation of KTR1 in PepGAT-treated cells is particularly intriguing because this protein is involved in cell wall biogenesis and maintenance. Considering that PepGAT is a chitin-binding peptide13 and that SEM analysis revealed severe cell wall damage in treated cells (Fig. 5), the increased abundance of KTR1 likely reflects a compensatory cellular response aimed at repairing and maintaining cell wall integrity. Consistent with this interpretation, control cells displayed normal morphology, whereas PepGAT-treated cells exhibited pronounced structural damage, reinforcing the hypothesis that cell wall disruption is a mechanism underlying PepGAT’s antifungal activity.

Amino acid metabolism and other metabolites

Dihydrofolate synthetase (DHFS) is a protein detected only in control cells, absent in PepGAT-treated cells, and is involved in folic acid biosynthesis. It catalyzes the conversion of dihydropteroate to dihydrofolate in folate biosynthesis. Previous studies have investigated the potential of the folate-synthesizing enzyme system in fungi as an antifungal target, a concept already well established in other microorganisms73,74. As C. albicans also possesses the complete folate-synthesizing enzyme system, the inhibition of DHYS could suggest a promising target for PepGAT’s antifungal activity.

Thiamine pyrophosphokinase (TPK) is an enzyme that converts thiamine into thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), a biologically active coenzyme essential for several key enzymes75. In S. cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe, the absence of TPK is lethal76. Here, the lack of this protein in C. albicans cells treated with PepGAT indicates a harmful effect on cell life.

Proteins related to pathogenicity

The extracellular metalloproteinase (EXMP) was found exclusively in control cells. Literature data suggest that EXMP is strongly associated with processes linked to fungal pathogenesis77. EXMP is known for its ability to degrade extracellular proteins, especially in interaction with the extracellular matrix, an essential aspect of the virulence of pathogenic fungi and biofilm formation. For example, in the human body, it acts specifically by destroying host barriers and defenses, disrupting homeostasis due to its high proteolytic activity78. In C. albicans, these metalloproteinases have been linked to the fungus’ ability to invade host tissues, facilitate biofilm formation, modulate the immune response, and promote resistance to antifungal treatments78. The absence of this protein in PepGAT-treated cells suggests impaired biofilm formation or disruption of preformed C. albicans biofilms, making adaptation to hostile environments difficult.

Together, proteomics, biochemical assays, and scanning electron and fluorescence microscopies revealed cellular functions perturbed by PepGAT (Fig. 6). This study highlights the mechanisms underlying PepGAT’s anticandidal activity against C. albicans. By presenting multiple mechanisms of action (Fig. 6), PepGAT makes it difficult for C. albicans to develop resistance.

Overview of all process perturbed by PepGAT on C. albicans cells.

Conclusions

The results presented here provided new insights into the anticandidal action against C. albicans. PepGAT-induced changes in many biochemical pathways involved in C. albicans metabolism, virulence, biofilm formation, and drug resistance. In this sense, PepGAT enhances the action of ITR against C. albicans cells. Therefore, the results presented here suggest that further experiments may be warranted to evaluate this molecule as a potential alternative for treating fungal infections.
