Charge Modulation at Nucleic Acid/MoS2 Interfaces: Insights from Chronocoulometry for 2D Material-Based Biosensing
Authors: Mahmudun Noby, Prabhangshu K. Das, Mohammad A. Asad, Will Almy, Mohtashim H. Shamsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines the modulation of interfacial charge (Q sur) at nucleic acid/MoS2 interfaces using chronocoulometry with the goal of elucidating the roles of the charge on metal ions and properties of nucleic acids in developing adsorption-based platforms for nucleic acid detection. Specifically, we investigated the adsorption of locked nucleic acid and DNA oligomers on MoS2 surfaces in the presence of Mg2+ and Na+, employing [Ru­(NH3)6]3+ as an electrostatically adsorbed redox probe. Q sur was determined by removing the diffusion-controlled contribution and subtracting the adsorbed charge (Q ads) from the total measured charge (Q total). Our results show that interfacial charge is highly sensitive to metal ion identity, nucleic acid backbone chemistry, strand conformation, and nonspecific adsorptionfactors that may influence diffusion-limited charge transfer at the nucleic acid/MoS2 interface. Owing to its higher charge density, Mg2+ induces strong electrostatic interactions with both nucleic acids and MoS2, resulting in pronounced charge accumulation, whereas Na+ produces weaker and less predictable effects. Duplex adsorption and duplex formation on the surface reduced interfacial electropositivity relative to single-stranded oligomers, while nonspecific adsorption of noncomplementary strands caused large charge perturbations that could mimic hybridization, underscoring potential risks for false positives and the critical need for blocking layers in such biosensing interfaces. Overall, these findings emphasize that interfacial charge, not mere steric hindrance from probe–target complexes, governs redox probe diffusion on nucleic acid/MoS2 interfaces. By elucidating charge-dependent dynamics, this study establishes a framework for reducing artifacts and enhancing the specificity and reliability of 2D material-based nucleic acid biosensors.

Introduction

Interfaces comprising nucleic acids and two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials are rapidly growing as powerful platforms for electrochemical biosensing across a broad spectrum of target analytes such as nucleic acids, metal ions, proteins, antibodies, cells, etc. − These interfaces offer high sensitivity and selectivity in nucleic acid detection, rendering them particularly valuable for disease diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and point-of-care applications. − Among various strategies, adsorption-based nucleic acid electrochemical biosensors have been investigated due to their simple strategy, cost-effectiveness, and time efficiency. , 2D nanomaterials have been vigorously explored as a transducer surface for adsorption-based electrochemical biosensing. − Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) nanosheets are the most popular 2D nanomaterials after graphene and its derivatives as electrochemical biosensing substrates. MoS2 is a semiconducting transition metal dichalcogenide with a tunable bandgap that may contribute to higher sensitivity, while their differential affinity for nucleic acids enables adsorption of probes through supramolecular forces. , Theoretical and experimental studies have demonstrated that the adsorption and desorption of nucleic acids from the MoS2 surface influences interfacial charge transferelectrical as well as electrochemicalwith sensitivity down to the attomole level. ,, Furthermore, MoS2 2D nanosheets can be printed to fabricate miniaturized electrodes via inkjet printing or drop-casting techniques. ,, At large, label-free electrochemical detection on nucleic acid/MoS2 interface relies on registering diffusion-controlled charge transfer across the interface in the presence of a soluble redox probe. ,,

Metal ions play a critical role in stabilizing B-DNA through electrostatic interactions with the phosphate backbone. While metal ions can also influence the surface properties of nanomaterials like MoS2, particularly affecting nucleic acid adsorption and interfacial charge transfer processes. , At neutral pH, MoS2 exhibits a zeta potential of approximately −50 mV, indicating a negatively charged surface. This surface charge can be modulated by factors such as chemical functionalization, surfactants, pH, and the presence of metal ions. , Upon introduction of metal ions, the zeta potential shifts toward more positive values, indicating reduced surface negativity and enhanced nucleic acid adsorption. For example, exposure to 0.02 M MgCl2 shifts the zeta potential from −40 mV to +2 mV. It is critical to note that most common types of nucleic acids (DNA, RNA, etc.) and MoS2 surfaces are negatively charged; therefore, to minimize the electrostatic repulsion and facilitate the adsorption of nucleic acids onto the MoS2 surface, solutions of high ionic strengthparticularly those containing divalent cationsare often employed. Similarly, Na+ ions have been employed in both optical and electrochemical studies to facilitate probe adsorption on MoS2 surfaces. Mg2+ forms a bridging interaction between nucleic acids and MoS2, while Na+ does not offer such interaction. ,, Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that the presence of Mg2+ ions in the nucleic acid/MoS2 interface effectively reduces electrostatic repulsion, enhances diffusion of negatively charged redox probes, increases current response, and decreases charge transfer resistance. , These findings collectively underscore that electrostatic charge owing to the adsorption of charged species plays a critical role in governing interfacial charge transfer processes. Such effects may lead to misinterpretation of experimental results if they are not carefully studied. A quantitative understanding of charge modulation due to the adsorption of metal ions and nucleic acidsparticularly in the presence of metal ionsis essential to accurately characterizing and optimizing the interfacial charge transfer properties of these electrochemical biosensing platforms.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy is typically employed in the presence of a redox probe to evaluate Faradaic charge transfer across the DNA interfaces along with the double-layer capacitance. ,, However, in this study, we focus on measuring the surface charge at time zeroprior to the onset of diffusion-controlled Faradaic processesto capture how interfacial charge is modulated as charged species within the nanoenvironment initially interact and reorganize. Specifically, we measure the interfacial charge at the nucleic acid/MoS2 interface, which is modulated by the presence of metal ions in the nanoenvironment. In particular, DNAs and locked nucleic acid (LNA) oligonucleotides were studied to understand whether backbone stiffness plays any role in the interfacial charge property. LNAs are characterized by a rigid, negatively charged phosphate backbone, whose conformational constraint, owing to the locked sugar ring, enhances base stacking and preorganizes the backbone, resulting in high binding affinity to complementary DNA or RNA sequences. ,− Therefore, LNA probes have been considered promising due to their enhanced specificity compared to their natural counterparts. ,, Scheme A illustrates that the nucleic acids (DNA or LNA) adsorb on the MoS2 nanosheets printed on indium tin oxide (ITO) electrodes, in the presence of Na+ or Mg2+ ions, followed by monitoring surface charge in the absence and presence of [Ru­(NH3)6]3+ using chronocoulometry (CC). Scheme B represents an Anson plot that shows the interfacial charge as a function of time after applying a potential step. The charge at the intercept (t = 0) eliminates the charge due to diffusion of the redox probe, and the intercept value can be used to quantify the surface charge.

Experimental Section

Materials

All DNA and LNA oligonucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Iowa, USA), and their sequences and lengths are listed in Table S1. Magnesium chloride solution (molecular biology grade), sodium chloride solution, α-terpineol (90% technical grade), cyclohexanone (ACS reagent, ≥99%), acetone (ACS reagent, ≥99.5%), ethyl cellulose (EC; viscosity 4 cP, 5% in toluene/ethanol 80:20 (lit.), 48% ethoxyl content), MoS2 raw bulk powder (<2 μm, 98%), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and ruthenium hexaammine­(III) chloride ([Ru­(NH3)6]­Cl3) (98+%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Tris buffer (pH 8.0, molecular biology grade) was sourced from EMD Millipore Corp. (Burlington, MA). The 10× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (0.067 M phosphate, containing calcium and magnesium, sterile-filtered to 0.1 μm) was obtained from Cytiva (Logan, Utah). Isopropyl alcohol and 95% ethanol were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). ITO substrates (50 × 75 × 0.7 mm3) with 4–8 Ω resistivity were obtained from Delta Technologies (Colorado, USA). The Ag/AgCl reference electrode and platinum wire auxiliary electrode were sourced from Bioanalytical Systems Inc. (Indiana, USA). The passivating films of vinyl sheets featuring circular holes of 2 mm diameter were cut by using a Graphtec CE6000-40 cutting plotter (Yokohama, Japan). Sonication was carried out using a Branson 5800 ultrasonic bath (Virginia, USA). Surface characterization was conducted via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), employing a Quanta 450 FEG system (FEI Ltd., Hillsboro, OR), housed at the SIUC IMAGE Center. Electrostatic force microscopy (EFM)–atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were performed using an NX7 scanning microscope (Park Systems, Santa Clara, CA) equipped with a AC240TM probe (spring constant: 2 N/m) at noncontact mode. Electrochemical measurements were performed using an Autolab electrochemical workstation equipped with a PGSTAT204 potentiostat and an FRA32 M module (Metrohm, Riverview, FL), housed within a Faraday cage provided by Metrohm.

Formulation and Characterization of MoS2 Ink

MoS2 nanosheets were synthesized from bulk MoS2 via a liquid-phase exfoliation method using ethanol as a solvent. − EC was added as a stabilizing agent to maintain the dispersion quality of the exfoliated nanosheets. A 3% (w/v) MoS2 powder was subsequently dispersed in a cyclohexanone/terpineol solvent mixture (85:15 v/v) and sonicated for 2.5 h to formulate a stable ink suitable for electrode fabrication. The morphological, elemental, and layer characterization of the formulated MoS2 ink have been published previously by the authors. ,

Preparation and Characterization of Nucleic Acid-Modified MoS2 Electrodes

First, 5 μL of the 3% MoS2 ink was drop-cast onto precleaned ITO substrates, followed by drying at room temperature for 4–6 h. Then, the MoS2-coated ITO substrate was sintered at 315 °C for 1 h under an ultrapure Ar gas atmosphere to ensure proper adhesion and structural integrity of the MoS2 layer. A vinyl sheet with a 2 mm diameter circular aperture was placed over the MoS2-coated ITO substrate to define a working electrode area and ensure consistent surface passivation. To modify the MoS2 surface with oligonucleotides, 5 μL of single-stranded or double-stranded DNA and LNA, prepared in 10 mM Tris buffer at pH 7.0, was drop-cast onto the MoS2-modified ITO substrate. Double-stranded oligonucleotides were prepared as reported previously. To set the Na+ and Mg2+ strength, 1 mM NaCl and MgCl2 were added to the nucleic acid solutions. Previously, concentrations of Na+ and Mg2+ ions were studied to facilitate electrostatic screening between oligonucleotides, enhancing, and MoS2-modified electrode to enhance the adsorption, where 1 mM Na+ or Mg2+ in 10 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.0) were found to be optimal concentrations to screen electrostatic repulsion. Then, the topography of the nucleic acid/MoS2 interface was characterized using SEM with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and AFM with EFM. The surface potential was studied using EFM and an AC240TM cantilever (Olympus), which has a nominal resonance frequency of ∼70 kHz and a force constant of ∼2 N/m. During the second pass, the tip was lifted 10 nm above the recorded topography to minimize short-range interactions and isolate long-range electrostatic forces with 0 V tip bias and grounded sample. The phase and amplitude signals were recorded to map variations in surface potential and charge distribution across the sample. All measurements were carried out under ambient conditions. Figure S1A displays the SEM image of the MoS2-modified ITO surface, which is further validated by the characteristic peaks of molybdenum (Mo) and sulfur (S) in the EDS spectrum shown in Figure S1C. After exposure to 100 nM LNA containing 1 mM MgCl2, the SEM image in Figure S1B shows no significant morphological changes to the MoS2 surface. However, the EDS spectrum in Figure S1D confirms the presence of nucleic acids and Mg, which is evident by the characteristic peaks corresponding to carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), magnesium (Mg), and phosphorus (P). For electrochemical measurements, Ag/AgCl electrode served as a reference electrode and a platinum wire as a counter electrode. Electrochemical characterization of the MoS2-modified electrodes was previously reported in the presence of K4[Fe­(CN)6]/K3[Fe­(CN)6 (1:1) redox probe in PBS (pH 7.0), , where significant enhancement of anodic and cathodic peak currents was reported after MoS2 modification of the ITO substrates, thereby confirming the successful diffusion of soluble redox probes on the MoS2 surface.

Chronocoulometric Measurements

Chronocoulometry is an electrochemical technique that monitors the total accumulated charge (Q total) as a function of time, following the application of a potential step. To study the surface charge owing to nucleic acid adsorption, 5 μL aliquots of either single-stranded or double-stranded oligonucleotides were drop-cast onto MoS2-modified working electrodes and incubated for 15 min. After incubation, the electrodes were rinsed 3× with the buffer10 μL of 10 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.0)to remove unbound strands. For concentration-dependent adsorption studies, 5 μL aliquots of nucleic acid sequence containing 1 mM metal ions (Mg2+ or Na+) were incubated on the MoS2 electrodes for 15 min, followed by the rinsing step. To confirm the Mg2+ effect, the electrodes were rinsed with 1 μM EDTA prepared in 10 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.0). For target exposure experiments, 5 μL of the probe-modified MoS2 was exposed to specific and nonspecific target DNA oligonucleotide for 15 min, followed by the washing step. Finally, chronocoulometry was performed at an applied potential of 0.04 V using 1 mM Ru­(NH3)6 3+ in PBS buffer (pH 7.2) as the electrochemical indicator to quantify surface-bound charge.

As established in previous studies, chronocoulometry quantifies the total interfacial charge (Q total) in the presence of a redox mediator. This total charge comprises three components: diffusion-controlled charge (Q diff), adsorbed charge (Q ads), and double-layer capacitive charge (Q dl). The redox mediator, [Ru­(NH3)6]3+, used in this study electrostatically interacts with negatively charged surfaces and contributes to the Faradaic charge under an applied potential. [Ru­(NH3)6]3+ owing to its multivalent positive charge, is known to electrostatically interact with DNA and is expected to adsorb at the DNA/MoS2 interface. In contrast, the commonly usedin DNA biosensingnegatively charged redox probe, [Fe­(CN)6]3–/4–, is not expected to adsorb on the DNA/MoS2 surface due to electrostatic repulsion. Accordingly, Q ads reflects the Faradaic contribution arising from mediator adsorption on the MoS2 surface. In contrast, double-layer capacitance is conventionally measured in the absence of a redox mediator. In the present study, however, we substitute Q dl with Q sur, as all measurements were conducted in the presence of [Ru­(NH3)6]3+. This substitution is necessary because the extent of mediator adsorption cannot be assumed to remain constant following exposure to metal ions, and thus, Q sur more accurately represents the effective interfacial charge under these conditions. Thus, to estimate the surface charge (Q sur), the adsorbed charge (Q ads) was subtracted from the total charge (Q total). Results and Discussion

A previous study demonstrated that the presence of Mg2+ and Na+ ions significantly enhances charge transfer across the nucleic acid-modified MoS2 interface in the presence of a negative redox probe while impeding the charge transfer when a positive redox probe is applied. The previous observation proved that the metal ions may reverse the surface potential and, therefore, affect the interfacial charge transfer. Based on this, we used Q sur to better understand the charge character of the nucleic acid/MoS2 interfaces in the presence of Mg2+ and Na+the most commonly used cations to stabilize DNA structure and screen their negative charge. Figure a,b presents the Anson plots of the time-dependent effects of Mg2+ and Na+ (1 mM each) on MoS2 in the presence of [Ru­(NH3)6]3+, evaluating incubation times between 10 and 30 min. The intercept of the Anson plot for MoS2 only represents the Faradaic charge contribution (Q ads) from adsorbed [Ru­(NH3)6]3+ on the MoS2 surface. While the intercept after the metal ion exposure represents surface charge (Q sur) in addition to the Q ads. The 1 mM metal ion concentration was used based on the previous study showing the highest change in the charge transfer resistance at this concentration. From the Anson plots, the metal-modified MoS2 surface exhibited a higher interfacial charge compared to the unmodified MoS2, which can be attributed to an increase in surface charge (Q sur). Figure c compares Q sur following metal ion exposure at varying exposure times, where the highest Q sur was observed at a 15 min incubation time with respect to both ions. At 15 min, the signal increased markedly from approx. 12 μC to 17 μC for Mg2+ and from 7 μC to 12 μC for Na+ before subsequently decreasing to 5 μC for Mg2+ and 9 μC for Na+. At 15 min, the Mg2+-modified surface exhibited ∼42% higher Q sur than the Na+-modified surface, likely due to the greater charge density of Mg2+, which enhances its affinity for the negatively charged MoS2 surface. These results are aligned with a recent study showing that exposure of the MoS2 surface to Mg2+ brings a higher change in diffusion-limited charge transfer resistance compared to Na+ owing to its accumulation on the surface.

(c) Box-and-whisker plot illustrating the calculated Q sur, as a function of exposure time to Mg2+ and Na+. Number of replicates = 5.

To investigate the effect on the interfacial charge owing to adsorption of the nucleic acid sequence in the presence of metal ions, we next evaluated the Q sur of the ss-LNA and ss-DNA-modified MoS2 surface in the presence of Mg2+ and Na+ ions. Figure a summarizes the Q sur of ss-LNA-modified surfaces across the nucleic acid concentration of 0.1–100 nM in the presence of 1 mM Mg2+ and Na+, respectively. In the presence of Mg2+, Q sur progressively increases from approx. −10 μC to 20 μC. The negative value of Q sur indicates that the surface charge is lower than Q ads. This may be attributed to effective adsorption of ss-LNA in the lower concentration regime, and the increase in charge toward positive is due to the accumulation of Mg2+ and making the surface more positive. Moreover, at very high concentrations, charge drops after the maximum value, perhaps due to layer-by-layer accumulation or surface crowding of nucleic acids. These results are by large align with the study where the change in charge transfer resistance following the adsorption of ss-LNA on MoS2 in the presence of Mg2+ was the highest in the low concentration regime. In contrast, the adsorption of ss-LNA in the presence of Na+ shows no consistent trend within the same concentration range. This irregularity may stem from the monovalent nature and lower charge density of Na+, which limits its ability to mediate stable interactions between LNA and MoS2. , As Na+ tends to interact with either LNA or MoS2but not both simultaneouslyit increases the likelihood of nonspecific binding during diffusion. Conversely, Mg2+, being divalent with higher charge density, likely promotes a bridging interaction between LNA and MoS2, thereby enhancing surface binding. , To further support these findings, we analyzed the electrostatic force of ss-LNA-modified MoS2 in the presence of Mg2+ and Na+ using EFM. The EFM mapping and the extracted surface potential profile (Figure S2) revealed that the surface potential of ss-LNA/MoS2 in the presence of Mg2+ ∼ 1.7 mV versus ∼0.5 mV in the presence of Na+. These results reinforce the stronger electrostatic interaction of Mg2+ with LNA and the MoS2 surface simultaneously, consistent with its higher charge and enhanced surface coordination capability. To understand the effect of backbone flexibility, we modified the surface with 0.1–100 nM ss-DNA in the presence of 1 mM Mg2+. Figure b summarizes the Q sur ss-DNA/MoS2, which increased from approx. −10 to 35 μC with DNA concentration increased from 0.1 to 10 nM. The charge at the lower concentration regime is the same as ss-LNA/MoS2, and the upward trend with concentration was also similar. Moreover, ss-DNA adsorption yielded higher charge even more than ss-LNA in the Mg2+ only condition. This may be attributed to multiple interactions of Mg2+ involved in stabilization of flexible DNA structure, DNA folding, electrostatic interaction, and bridging between DNA and MoS2. ,,

To evaluate our hypothesis that Mg2+ accumulates excessively on the surface along with nucleic acid concentrations, the electrodes were rinsed with 1 μM EDTA. As shown in Figure a, the Q sur values for ss-LNA and ss-DNA (both 10 nM) decreased markedly after EDTA treatment. Specifically, Q sur dropped from ∼20 μC to ∼3 μC for ss-LNA and from ∼40 μC to ∼8 μC for ss-DNA, confirming significant Mg2+ accumulation. The similar trend was observed for ss-DNA across 0.1–100 nM concentration, as shown in Figure S3. This accumulation renders the surface highly electropositive and may alter interfacial diffusion-limited charge transfer by either facilitating or hindering diffusion of the redox probe, depending on the charge of the redox probe. To further investigate the influence of the nucleic acid conformation on the surface charge in the presence of metal ions, ss-LNA was hybridized with its complementary DNA strand in solution and adsorbed onto the MoS2 surface in the presence of 1 mM Mg2+ or Na+, followed by chronocoulometric measurements. Figure b compares the Q sur values of 10 nM ss-LNA and solution-hybridized ds-LNA in the presence of both metal ions. Adsorption of ds-LNA resulted in substantially lower Q sur compared to ss-LNA under both ionic conditions, consistent with the higher negative charge density of the duplex, which offsets surface electropositivity even in the presence of cations. In the case of Mg2+, Q sur decreased by approximately 50% (from ∼20 μC to ∼10 μC), whereas in the case of Na+, the reduction was nearly 4-fold (from ∼17 μC to −5 μC). This stronger effect with Na+ may reflect its lower charge density and weaker interaction with the MoS2 surface, whichwhen combined with the highly electronegative duplexyields a net negative surface charge.

(b) Comparison of Q sur of 10 nM ss-LNA and solution hybridized ds-LNA in the presence of 1 mM Mg2+ and Na+, respectively. Number of replicates = 5.

For biosensing applications, probe-modified platforms are typically exposed to target sequences, which can alter the interfacial charge and, in turn, influence diffusion-limited charge transfer. To investigate this effect, the ss-LNA/MoS2 surface was exposed to complementary (cDNA) and noncomplementary (NC) DNA sequences in the presence of Mg2+ or Na+. The surface charge comparison in Figure shows that under both ionic conditions exposure to cDNA and NC resulted in a marked decrease in Q sur, consistent with an increase in negative charge due to hybridization or nonspecific adsorption of DNA strands. Notably, the decrease was more pronounced with cDNA than with NC sequences, as expected for hybridization events. Theoretically, negligible change was anticipated with NC sequences; however, a substantial decrease was observed under both ionic conditions. This effect is likely attributable to nonspecific adsorption of NC sequences, facilitated by unoccupied surface sites and the absence of a blocking layer, which allow NC strands to interact with the MoS2 surface. Such charge modulation arising from nonspecific adsorption may generate false-positive signals in practical biosensing applications. Overall, our results demonstrate that the interfacial non-Faradaic charge of nucleic acid modified MoS2 undergoes substantial modulation under different conditions, thereby altering the balance of electrostatic attractive and repulsive forces experienced by a soluble redox probe during diffusion-controlled charge transfer.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated how the charges of metal ions and nucleic acids modulate the interfacial charge at the nucleic acid/MoS2 interface. Using chronocoulometry with a positive redox probe, we demonstrated that interfacial charge, rather than just steric hindrance, can also be the dominant factor governing redox probe diffusion and the resulting electrochemical signal in these biosensing platforms. Our results underscore the critical role of Mg2+ in exhibiting a much stronger interaction with nucleic acids and MoS2 compared to Na+. The higher charge density of Mg2+ promotes robust bridging interactions, leading to a significant positive charge accumulation on the surface. This accumulation of positive charge on the MoS2 surface is a key mechanism that facilitates the adsorption of negatively charged nucleic acids and influences charge transfer. Furthermore, we found that duplex formation (ds-LNA) substantially reduces surface electropositivity compared to that of single-stranded probes (ss-LNA), reflecting the higher negative charge density of the double-stranded complex. Our data show that noncomplementary sequences can introduce significant charge perturbations, perhaps causing probe displacement as reported earlier for other DNA/nanomaterials interactions. , This highlights a major challenge in developing reliable MoS2-based sensors and underscores the critical need for effective blocking strategies to prevent false positives. The findings emphasize that careful understanding of the surface charge and factors that may modulate the interfacial charge is essential for designing an adsorption-based assay on charged substrates (transducers) such as MoS2. In essence, understanding the charge-dependent dynamics may reduce artifacts and improve the accuracy of 2D material-based nucleic acid biosensors.
