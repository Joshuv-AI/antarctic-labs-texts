Stability of G/C DNA Tetraplexes at Physiological pH Formed at the Promoter-UTR Intersection of the RAP2C Gene: Structural Multitude of a G/C Rich Genomic Segment
Authors: Anju Singh, Shrikant Kukreti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Genomic guanine-rich segments are prone to adopting self-associated noncanonical structures termed G-quadruplex structures. An overabundance of reports reveals their existence prevalently at important genomic locations, including oncogenic promoters, exonic regions, immunoglobulin switch regions, telomeres, etc. Undeniably, these structures have been the subject of curiosity for many decades, leading to mounting evidence suggestive of their involvement in key cellular processes. Herein, we identified a 45-mer G-rich segment containing G5 tracts, present at the intersection of the promoter and 5′-UTR region of the RAP2C gene of the ras oncogene family, found to fold into a G-quadruplex structure. We employed biochemical and biophysical techniques for structural characterization of the G-rich segment (RAP2C45) and its C-rich counterpart (RAP2C45C). RAP2C45 adopts a bimolecular and unimolecular G-quadruplex structure in Na+, whereas the presence of K+ favors predominantly the unimolecular G-quadruplex. The counterpart C-rich segment RAP2C45C adopts an i-motif (C-tetraplex) at physiological and acidic pH. Bioinformatics analysis identified the presence of Sp1 and Rap1 transcription factors in the vicinity and overlapping throughout the studied sequence, indicating the role of these noncanonical structures in gene regulation. As noncanonical structures are promising targets for developing new drugs, the present study may add to the understanding of designing novel molecules with promising therapeutic applications.

Introduction

The nucleic acid architecture is not limited to just the double helix. However, DNA also exists in varied noncanonical conformations, which have been validated to play a crucial role in biological processes. Largely, the noncanonical G-quadruplex and C-tetraplex (i-motif) structures have received great attention from researchers worldwide for their involvement in gene regulation. The G-quadruplex has raised curiosity and considerable interest for more than seven decades, as it has been explored for its therapeutic purposes. − The formation of the non-Watson–Crick DNA G-quadruplex structure was validated decades ago. , Considerable interest in these noncanonical structures has driven the scientific fraternity to explore G-quadruplexes throughout the genome to establish their in vivo existence. However, in the recent past, in vivo evidence of these non-B-DNA structures has been confirmed. The Balasubramanian group has identified 716 310 distinct G4 structures at various genomic locations. , Guanine-rich segments have the propensity to self-associate through Hoogsteen bonding-mediated G-tetrads, which upon stacking adopt the G-quadruplex structure. The central core of the G-quadruplex harbors monovalent cations such as Na+ and K+, stabilizing these structures. − The order of stabilization by monovalent cations is as follows: K+ > Na+ > Li+. A diverse type of G-quadruplex structure can be formed based on the stoichiometry of strands, i.e., unimolecular (single-strand → intramolecular), bimolecular (two-strands → intermolecular), triple-stranded (three-strands → intermolecular) and tetramolecular (four-strands → intermolecular), strands polarity (parallel and antiparallel), as well as loop length and loop composition, etc. − Based on extensive biophysical techniques, a unique pattern, G≥3NX G≥3NX G≥3NX G≥3NX G≥3, was deployed to predict putative G-quadruplex structures at genomic locations; however, this was not found to be a universal pattern. Several G-quadruplexes were identified and explored, having discontinuous G-stretches with longer loop lengths. ,

In a DNA double-helical structure, the guanine-rich strand will always have its counterpart, a cytosine-rich strand. However, in the event that the guanine strand undergoes self-association to form a G-quad, the cytosine-rich sequence has two options: it can either exist as an unpaired strand or adopt a self-associated structure known as an i-motif or a C-tetraplex. Though it was initially thought that i-motif formation required a slightly acidic pH to facilitate cytosine protonation , and form C+●C mispairs, it is now well established that these structures can also form at or near physiological pH within the cellular environment and solution conditions. − In vivo evidence of these structures in human cells validates the existence and involvement of the i-motif in gene regulation and supports the fact that G4 and i-motif structures simultaneously may play a key role in gene modulation and expression. Alike the G-quad, the i-motif can be unimolecular, bimolecular, or tetramolecular, where two parallel-stranded C+●C base-paired duplexes interdigitate to each other, forming the i-motif or C-tetraplex. Extensive literature is available reflecting the coexistence of i-motif and G-quadruplex structures in the genomes. ,

Schematic representation of the formation of self-associated G-quadruplex and i-motif structures.

Similar to many oncogene promoters and 5′-untranslated regions, the RAP2C gene promoter also contains some contiguous guanine-rich stretches. The RAP2C gene belongs to the ras family and plays a key role in tumor migration and invasion. Also, the Akt signaling pathway may be involved in Rap2c-induced MMP2 expression. , The Rap group has demonstrated more than 50% overall identity, as well as three-dimensional structural similarity to Ras-related proteins. − It is well-documented that RAP2C plays a vital role in the activation of transcriptional activities of SRE and might act as a positive regulator in SRE-mediated gene transcription. RAP2C is crucially involved in the migration and invasion of U2OS cells, i.e., cancerous cells, and the downregulation of expression of RAP2C substantially inhibits cancer cell migration and invasion. In the present study, we attempted to investigate the possibility of the formation of noncanonical G-quadruplex and i-motif structures by a RAP2C genomic segment at physiological pH and salt conditions. We employed biochemical and biophysical studies to gain insight into the structural features of a 45-mer guanine-rich segment containing G5 tracts along with its complementary sequence of the RAP2C gene. We propose here that the G-rich segment of the gene adopts unimolecular and bimolecular G-quad structures. Interestingly, the C-counterpart exhibits substantial stability and adopts a unimolecular i-motif structure at physiological pH, which is in equilibrium with a homoduplex species. To our surprise, no duplex formation is observed in K+ containing heat-treated (HT) samples, whereas nonheat-treated (NHT) samples formed a combination of duplex along with self-associated structures of G- and C-rich sequences. This study may add to the understanding of the coexistence of self-associated structures formed simultaneously by counter G/C strands of duplex DNA at important genomic locations and their involvement in transcription as well as gene regulation.

Materials

The DNA sequences (deoxyoligonucleotides) under study, along with the control size markers synthesized at a 1 μM scale, were procured with PAGE purification in lyophilized form from Helix Biosciences. The oligonucleotide sequences were stored at −20 °C and were used further without purification. The stock solutions were prepared by dissolving the lyophilized powder of oligonucleotides in Milli-Q water. The stock concentration was determined spectrophotometrically by measuring absorbance at 260 nm at an elevated temperature (95 °C), and the extinction coefficient (ε) was calculated by the nearest-neighbor method already described in the literature. − All of the fine chemicals used were of analytical grade. Methods

Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

Denaturing or urea PAGE was used to check the purity of commercially synthesized oligomers by running them on 20% PAGE consisting of 7 M urea. By using 15% nondenaturing (native) PAGE, the molecularity (strandedness) and structural characterization of the used oligomers were investigated. The 15% native PAGE contained 20 mM sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 5.7/7.4) with 100 mM NaCl or KCl and 0.1 mM EDTA, whereas the running tank buffer consisted of 1× TBE with the same concentration of buffer, NaCl or KCl, and EDTA. The gel was run at approximately 8 °C for 15 h at 45 V. Each gel was pre-equilibrated for 1 h before loading the samples. Orange-G with glycerol was used as a tracking dye. After completion of gel electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Stains-All solution, ultimately visualized under white light, and photographed by the Alphaimager2200 (Alpha Infotech Corp.).

UV-Thermal Denaturation Studies

UV-thermal melting profiles of the used oligomer sequences were recorded to obtain information about the thermal stability of G- and C-rich sequences under study. Thermal melting profiles were measured at a wavelength of 295 nm (standard characteristic wavelength used for studying G-quadruplex and i-motif structures). A UV-2450PC Shimadzu UV–vis spectrophotometer (equipped with a Peltier thermoprogrammer TMSPC-8€-200) interfaced with a Pentium IV computer was used for data collection and analysis. The thermal denaturation experiment used a stoppered multicell quartz cuvette with a volume of 110 μL and a 1 cm optical path length. The heating rate was maintained at 0.5 °C/min, and the melting temperature was determined using the first derivative of the melting curve.

Thermal Difference Spectra

Thermal difference spectra were measured for the studied G-rich and C-rich oligomers on a UV-2450PC Shimadzu UV–vis spectrophotometer (equipped with a Peltier thermoprogrammer TMSPC-8­(E)-200) interfaced with a Pentium IV computer by using a 1 cm path length multicell quartz cuvette. Absorbance was recorded between 220 and 320 nm above and below the melting temperature of the used oligomers (G-rich and C-rich). TDS is the arithmetic difference between the absorbance spectra of the folded and unfolded states of the structure adopted by the used oligomers.

Database Searching and Sequence Alignment

Bioinformatics tools and biological databases were combined to characterize the RAP2C gene and extract the RAP2C gene sequence. 45mer RAP2C gene sequences were identified at the intersection of the coding region of the 5′-UTR and promoter location by using the UCSC Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu) and verified by the NCBI BLAST database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Alibaba2.0 software was used to identify significant transcription factors in the vicinity of the studied sequence. Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/) was used for multiple sequence alignment in five organisms, i.e., Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, Macaca mulatta, Canis lupus familiaris, and Bos taurus. Phylogenetic analysis was performed to gain insight into the evolutionary relationships among organisms.

Results and Discussion

Database Searching, Sequence Alignment, and Transcription Factor Binding Sites

To find the guanine-rich stretch in the RAP2C gene, the NCBI BLAST database and UCSC Human Genome Browser were used to search the human genome. Usually, G2, G3, and G4 guanine tracts have been well studied and reported for quadruplex formation. G5 stretches located in the MYH7 gene, forming G-quadruplexes, have been reported by our laboratory. Further, we found a 45-mer GC-rich stretch having G5 tracts located at the junction of the promoter and 5′-UTR of the human RAP2C gene. Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of the RAP2C gene was performed to demonstrate the conservation of RAP2C45 in humans, mice, monkeys, dogs, and oxen (Figure ).

(b) Transcription factor binding sites are present in the vicinity of the studied sequence RAP2C45. RAP2C45 is highlighted in yellow and green. The yellow-highlighted sequence shows its presence in the promoter location, and the green-highlighted sequence is located in the untranslated region.

This 45-mer consecutive guanine tract is fully conserved in humans and partially conserved in other organisms. Transcription factors Sp1, RAP1, TBP, NF-kappa, and CACCC-bi, etc., are present (upfield, downfield, or overlapping) in the proximity of the studied sequence. The human RAP2C promoter-5’UTR element encompasses the five evolutionarily conserved Sp1 binding sites, along with RAP1, which entails the biological significance of the structure adopted by this 45-mer sequence. It was also identified that the RAP2C gene, when compared to other organisms, is closely related to the mouse and ox as well. Sequence analogy depicts the structural similarity that may be involved in the same or related structural and functional aspects.

Non-Denaturing (Native) Gel Electrophoresis

Structural Status of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C at Neutral pH (Heat-Treated Samples)

Nondenaturing gel electrophoresis was used to decipher the structural status and exact molecularity/strandedness of the oligomers. A native gel is an informative and sensitive technique to gain insight into the folding pattern of multistranded structures. 15% native gel electrophoresis was carried out in 20 mM sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 0.1 M NaCl, 0.1 M KCl, and 0.1 mM EDTA. Figure a,b illustrates the electrophoretogram of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C at neutral pH (7.4). All samples were heat–cooled and equilibrated at 4 °C overnight before being loaded onto the gel. Lane 1 consists of lower and upper bands of duplexes of PAL12 (12 × 2 = 24) and PAL24 (24 × 2 = 48), respectively. A random single-strand size marker M35 (Figure a,b, Lane 5) and a duplex of M35 + M35C (70 nt) (Lane 6) were also used as control size markers. RAP2C45 (Figure a, Lane 2) migrated more slowly than the duplex of PAL24 and M35. Since it is a 45-nucleotide long sequence, there exists a possibility that it adopts dimeric folded structure (45 × 2 = 90). The structural status of RAP2C45 is seen to be different in K+, where it migrated as a single band lower than that of PAL24 (48 nt), reflecting its compact unimolecular status. Lane 3 (Figure a,b) showed a single band migrated retarded than the duplex of PAL24 and faster than the duplex of M35+M35C. It is a general notion that at neutral pH, C-rich sequences migrate as unstructured single strands; however, to our surprise, a 45 nt long cytosine-rich sequence RAP2C45C migrated slowly than the 24-mer duplex (48 nt) of PAL24 indicating the formation of a unimolecular species. It is only possible if the sequence experiences C-protonation at physiological pH and adopts a higher-order structure. An equimolar mixture of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C showed two bands in Lane 4 (Figure a), with the upper band corresponding to duplex species along with a lower, less intense band of RAP2C45 (G-rich sequence, residual). To our surprise, in the presence of K+, no duplex species band was observed in Lane 4 (Figure b), rather two bands migrated, corresponding to RAP2C45 (Figure b, the lower band (less intense)) and RAP2C45C (Figure b, upper intense band, Lane 4).

It is a well-established fact that G-rich sequences behave differently in varied cation solutions; the different behavior shown by the duplex here intrigues us to investigate it further; hence, PAGE was run at varied solution conditions. It is important to mention here that, in native gels, we noticed a few additional frail/feeble bands in some lanes. Similarly, some fainter bands are also visible in addition to the perfect duplex of PAL12, PAL24, M35 + M35C, and the single-strand control size marker M35. It is known that DNA sequences have a propensity to adopt some transient structural forms as well as some slipped structures. , Therefore, there exists a possibility that, at the used oligomer concentration, some slipped structures with retarded mobility may also have emerged owing to the mismatched pairing of nucleobases. These minor structures are reflected as faint bands along with intramolecularly folded species of G/C.

Structural Status of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C at Neutral pH (Non-Heat-Treated Samples)

It was intriguing to see the structural status of these oligonucleotides, prepared in a nonconventional way. For clarity on the sequence behavior under varied conditions, we performed 15% native gel electrophoresis in 20 mM sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 0.1 M NaCl, and 0.1 M KCl, and 0.1 mM EDTA. These samples were used in duplicate, where one set was prepared in a conventional way, i.e., heat-treated (heating followed by slow cooling), whereas the other set was used without heat and cool treatment (nonheat-treated). The samples were pre-equilibrated at 4 °C overnight before being loaded onto the gel. Figure a,b demonstrates the mobility patterns of all of the oligomers. Lanes 1 and 8 comprised control size markers PAL12 and PAL24 (Lane 1) and a duplex of M35 + M35C (Lane 8), respectively. Lanes 2–4 illustrated the heat-treated samples of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C, along with the duplex of RAP2C45 + RAP2C45C, giving the identical gel status as discussed earlier in Figure a,b. Lanes 5–7 comprised nonheat-treated samples of the G-rich, C-rich, and an equimolar mixture of the G sequence with its counterpart C. Interestingly, RAP2C45 showed the identical structural status as earlier, migrating as a unimolecular folded species in Na+ as well as in K+ ions (Figure a, Lane 5, NHT). The structural status of all the oligomers remained the same in the K+ solution as in the heat-treated samples, except for the differing behavior of the G–C duplex. Three distinct bands were observed in Lane 7 of Figure b, where the upper band migrated more slowly than the duplex of M35 + M35C, which can be assigned to the duplex species of RAP2C45 + RAP2C45C. The middle (light) band migrated with the band of RAP2C45C (folded intramolecular species), and the lower band migrated corresponding to RAP2C45 (intramolecular unimolecular species). Despite participating in duplex formation, some population of self-associated structures of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C also coexisted in solution. This indicated the disproportionation of strands and the coexistence of three structures in equilibrium.

Contrasting results of the formation of duplex species in the presence of K+ ions only in nonheat-treated (NHT) samples enabled us to contemplate why this happens. There is a fair possibility that when the samples were heated up to 95 °C and cooled down slowly (heat-treated) at room temperature, it facilitated the oligonucleotide to achieve the most probable type of secondary structure. It is well documented that G-quadruplexes have more affinity for potassium ions and facilitate stable G-quadruplexes. − It is also reported that K+ cations are significantly harbored and sandwiched between G-tetrads, resembling an octahedral geometry, or, in an alternate manner, within loops above tetrads, producing the probable hexacoordinate stereochemistry of cations. , Undeniably, the cationic radius also accounts for the preference toward a particular structure. Therefore, it is fairly possible that heat treatment of the sample containing G-rich oligonucleotides in the presence of K+ ions would engage the G-strands in coordination and preferential binding to K+ cations, resulting in a compact self-associated structure, making G-strands unavailable for duplex formation. On the other hand, in the case of nonheat-treated samples, almost all the G-oligonucleotide strands are possibly already in a structured form, except for some left-out (residual) unstructured free G-strands available for W–C hydrogen bonding with their counterpart C-rich strand present in solution, generating duplex species.

The literature is rich in reports indicating that if a complementary C-rich sequence exists with a G-rich sequence at genomic locations, i.e., in promoter or telomeric regions, they exhibit competition with self-associated G-quadruplex and i-motif structures for adopting Watson–Crick duplex structures. About three decades ago, Hardin et al. demonstrated an equilibrium at physiological conditions between Watson–Crick hairpin and a parallel intermolecular quadruplex structure. Similarly, other groups have also reported the interconversion of a B-form duplex into an intermolecular G-quadruplex under some cationic conditions. , Manzini et al. illustrated that an equimolar mixture of dAG3(T2AG3)3 and d­(C3TA2)3C3T at acidic pH predominantly adopts a G-quadruplex structure and prevents WC duplex formation. Sugimoto’s group has also described the competition between G-quadruplex and duplex species at physiological conditions, highlighting the fact that pH plays a key role in the structural competition between the G-quadruplex, i-motif, and WC duplex. Further, Maiti’s group has also illustrated the effect of loop length on structural competition under physiological conditions. Their findings indicate that an increase in the loop length of the quadruplex-forming sequence facilitates duplex formation over the quadruplex. Again, a recent report has further established that while a quadruplex and WC duplex both exist in equilibrium at physiological solution conditions, quadruplex and i-motif structures coexist at acidic pH. Intriguingly, the results from our present study are somewhat contrasting: in K+ containing solutions, the heat-treated samples gave only self-associated G-quadruplex and i-motif structures, whereas in nonheat-treated samples, all three (G-quadruplex, i-motif, and duplex) species were observed. Therefore, we concluded that the formation of stable structures at physiological conditions significantly depends on the nucleotide sequence (G- and C-tracts), loop length, and solution conditions. However, the structural status of these G/C-rich oligomers has also been investigated using gel assays in the presence of Li+ and the divalent cation Mg2+, respectively (Figure S1). The results are in good agreement with the gel assay observations in Na+ for heat-treated (HT) and nonheat-treated (NHT) samples. In the presence of both the monovalent and divalent cations, duplex species of RAP2C45 + RAP2C45C are well observed in HT and NHT samples, whereas duplex species are not observed in HT samples containing only the K+ ions.

UV-Thermal Denaturation Experiments of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C

UV-thermal denaturation is an informative tool to elucidate the formation of noncanonical structures by G/C-rich oligomers. Further, to establish the thermal stability of the used RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C sequences, we monitored UV melting studies. It is well documented that G-rich sequences that adopt G-quadruplex conformations display a characteristic inverted melting transition at A 295 nm. , Melting experiments were carried out in 20 mM sodium cacodylate (pH 5.7 and 7.4) containing 0.1 M Na+/K+ and 0.1 mM EDTA.

Thermal Stability of RAP2C45C at Physiological pH

Thermal stability was also monitored at neutral pH to investigate the structural status of RAP2C45C in 20 mM sodium cacodylate (pH 7.4) containing 0.1 M NaCl/0.1 M KCl, and 0.1 mM EDTA. At physiological pH, RAP2C45C exhibited a well-defined biphasic melting profile, as depicted in Figure S2. The melting transition temperature reflects the disordering of two structural species. This remarkable difference in T m provides a fair possibility of uncoiling the minor population of i-motif DNA and unimolecular C-type hairpin duplex species. The results of thermal melting studies are in good agreement with the gel assay. This depicts that RAP2C45C is not an unstructured/random coil at neutral pH; rather, it forms C●C+ mispair-mediated structural conformations.

Thermal Difference Spectra of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C

Thermal difference spectra (TDS) entail the characteristic features of higher-order DNA conformations and are considered fingerprint spectra to distinguish discrete multistranded DNA structures. , Specific TDS signature peaks for the G-quadruplex are manifested on the positive side at 243, 255, and 273 nm, along with a negative peak at 295 nm. Figure a displayed the characteristic TDS spectra of RAP2C45 in Na+ as well as in K+ and exhibited two positive maxima at 243 and 273 nm, along with a minimum at 295 nm. The C-rich RAP2C45C also depicted the signatures of classical i-motif DNA, with a positive peak at 239 nm and a negative peak at 295 nm (Figure b). TDS results are in good agreement with gel and T m results and indicate the formation of G-quadruplex and i-motif DNA structures by G-rich (RAP2C45) and C-rich (RAP2C45C) sequences, respectively.

Proposed Models of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C Structures at Varied Solution Conditions

Based on the results and fine correlation among the PAGE, UV–T m, and TDS studies, we proposed hypothetical models for the possible structures in the genome with varied molecularity by RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C sequences at physiological as well as acidic pH (Figure ). While the results of native PAGE depict the molecularity/strandedness of the oligomeric structures, UV-melting and TDS spectra entail the specific characteristic signatures of the G-quadruplex and C-tetraplex structure formation. Thus, secondary structural information obtained by UV-melting, TDS, and PAGE can be used to propose molecular models for the structures of G-quadruplex, i-motif, and duplex-forming sequences, even in the absence of higher-resolution information.

Hypothetical proposed structures adopted by RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C (G/C-rich sequences) located at the promoter-UTR intersection of the RAP2C gene at physiological pH in Na+ and K+ cations under HT and NHT conditions.

In PAGE Na+, an intense band of RAP2C45 is obtained along with additional feeble bands, whereas in K+, a single intense band is observed, reflecting the formation of a bimolecular G-quadruplex and a compact unimolecular G-quadruplex structure. Compactness is specifically mentioned because of the involvement of 6-tracts of G5–G6 along with an additional five guanines. Intriguingly, the counterpart C-rich RAP2C45C displays an interesting behavior at neutral pH. Its migration in the gel assay is quite intriguing, as it migrated above the duplex of PAL24 (48 nt), suggesting that it adopts a folded intramolecular structure as well as a hairpin-duplex, displaying a strong band in the gel. Based on experimental results, we suggested that RAP2C45C exists as an intramolecular i-motif at 4 °C via the involvement of protonated C●C+ base pairs, whereas at 25 °C (RT), it adopts hairpin-duplex species. The possible C●C+ base pairing is involved in intramolecular i-motif DNA and dimeric i-motif DNA (room temperature). Mounting evidence suggested that several hemiprotonated C●C+ base pairs determine the stability of i-motif DNA structures. Waller et al. and Burrow et al. have recently demonstrated that increasing the number of C-tract lengths (>5) promotes the stability of i-motif DNA. Besides enhancing the i-motif stability, the longer C-tracts also probe the formation of two discrete structural species at physiological pH (7.4). , Intramolecular i-motifs are formed preferentially with longer C-tracts. Further, Dzatko et al. have recently used NMR to investigate the stability of i-motif at physiological pH in a cellular environment. The group studied a C-rich oligomer of the human promoter region and reported the stability of the i-motif at neutral pH, up to 35 °C. Others have also established and validated the existence of i-motif DNA at physiological pH. Temperature also has a significant effect on the structural transition of C-rich oligomers. We observed the formation of the i-motif by RAP2C45C at a lower temperature, i.e., 4 °C, whereas the same adopted a unimolecular hairpin-duplex at 25 °C. Thus, apart from pH, temperature also plays a pivotal role in the structuration of i-motif DNA.

It is well-documented that the formation of individual G-quadruplex and i-motif structures is compromised owing to the formation of duplex structures by hybridization of G-rich strands and their complementary C-rich strands. Many groups have investigated and reported the competition between G-quadruplex/i-motif and duplex conversion at acidic and physiological pH. , To our surprise, in the present study, in the case of Na+, we observed a duplex species in HT and NHT, whereas in K+ in HT, individual bands of structured RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C were obtained in the gel assay (Figure a,b). Surprisingly, in the NHT sample, an intense band of duplex species was obtained along with individual bands of RAP2C45 (G-rich) and RAP2C45C (C-rich) structures. It is a well-established fact that G-quadruplex and i-motif formation is a slow kinetic process, so heating up to 95 °C and then allowing it to cool slowly at room temperature leads to the formation of the most stable higher-order structure. All hydrogen bonds break during the nucleation process, followed by proper base pairing during renaturation on slow cooling, thus facilitating the formation of thermodynamically stable conformations. We hypothesized and proposed that K+ predominantly promotes G-quadruplex formation, facilitates the uncoiling of G-rich strands, and adopts the most stable G-quadruplex conformation. Since RAP2C45 has a high content of Gs, it is prone to adopt compact intramolecular structures and is not available for duplex formation with its C-rich counterpart. Similarly, the C-rich oligomer also adopts compact intramolecular i-motif DNA at neutral pH; thus, both G/C-rich oligomers exist as their self-associated stable conformations. Though in the case of NHT samples, while G/C-rich sequences are almost self-aggregated, there lies a possibility of some residual strands making themselves available for duplex formation with C-rich counterparts. Therefore, some duplex species are identified along with individual G/C structural conformations.

Biological Relevance

A plethora of evidence has suggested the significant role of the RAP2C gene in the migration and invasion of cancer. , RAP2C belongs to the Ras-related Ras GTP-binding protein family. Ras-related protein is found to be involved in the regulation of various cellular functions, such as cell adhesion, cell differentiation, cell cycle control, cytoskeletal organization, and apoptosis. The connection among RAP2C, EMT markers, and glioma metastasis mediated by ERK is well-documented. Overexpression of RAP2C is crucially linked to the progression of glioma and might act as a prognostic factor for glioma. These reports indicate that RAP2C may be therapeutically targeted for the cessation of cancer. Augmentation of RAP2C intersection at the promoter and UTR with guanine and cytosine stretches points toward a possible involvement of self-associated structures in gene regulation. Structures adopted by guanine-rich stretches, i.e., G-quadruplexes, are immensely linked to the myriads of cancers and other ailments. In light of this evidence, we proposed that the 45-mer G-rich sequence RAP2C45 and its complementary RAP2C45C, at times, adopt unimolecular G-quadruplex and C-tetraplexes at physiological solution conditions. We demonstrated that in heat-treated samples, even in the presence of a complementary sequence, K+ stabilizes and promotes G-quadruplex structures, impeding duplex formation due to the involvement of RAP2C45 in the self-associated G-quadruplex structure. However, a minor population of C–C+ mediated i-motif structures is also evident at neutral pH. It is worth mentioning that various transcription factors are recognized in the vicinity of the RAP2C45 sequence in the RAP2C gene promoter. RAP1, SP1, and CACCC-bi directly bind to the sequence itself. Additionally, present near the sequence are NG-kappa, Hb, and TBP transcription factors, indicating a substantial role in gene regulation. Numerous studies indicate the recognition of these noncanonical structures by transcription factors, playing a regulatory role in gene expression. − NF-kappa is a well-established transcription factor that is crucially involved in inflammatory and immune responses, as well as in the regulation of genes related to cell survival and proliferation. It is quite possible that, being in the vicinity of the sequence, gene transcription regulation is affected. Similarly, RAP2C45 encompasses the Sp1 transcription factor, overlapping as well as being in the vicinity of the sequence, significantly reflecting the interlacing of Sp1 in the transcriptional gene expression of RAP2C. Raiber et al. have established the role of Sp1 in the specific recognition of noncanonical G-quadruplex structures.

Conclusion

To sum up, the results of PAGE, UV thermal denaturation (T m), and thermal difference spectra (TDS) employed to investigate the structural status of a 45-mer G-rich (RAP2C45) and its counterpart C-rich (RAP2C45C) segments of the RAP2C gene exhibited a fine correlation among the experimental findings. Using gel assays, we analyzed the molecularity (size)/strandedness and secondary structure adopted by RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C sequences. Extensive gel studies were carried out to gain insight into the structural polymorphism of these sequences. The characteristic TDS spectra reflect the G-quadruplex and i-motif formation by G- and C-rich oligomers. Thermal melting studies are also in accordance with gel assays, where two structural species are observed unfolding in Na+; whereas, in the presence of K+ the highly stable unimolecular compact G-quadruplex structure could not show proper melting. At neutral pH, the counterpart RAP2C45C also presented distinct behavior in gel assays. It adopts a self-associated unimolecular i-motif structure at neutral pH, while it adopts a bimolecular and unimolecular i-motif at acidic pH. Surprisingly, at lower temperatures, the same sequence exists as an i-motif, whereas at room temperature, it adopts unimolecular C–C-hairpin structural species. Besides this, each of RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C in heat-treated samples adopts compact self-associated structures that hinder duplex formation in the presence of K+. In contrast, the duplex and self-associated G-quadruplex and i-motif structures are formed with nonheat-treated samples.

The intriguing observation has been the absence of duplex formation in a heat-treated sample of an equimolar mixture of RAP2C45 (G-rich) and RAP2C45C (C-rich) sequences in the presence of K+ ions, owing to the competition between K+ cations and C-rich counterparts. We hypothesize that K+ shows a strong affinity for the G-quadruplex by fitting best in the G-tetrad core. However, this is facilitated by heat treatment, where all of the strands unwind and accommodate K+ in the core of the G-tetrad cavity. This leads to the formation of a compact unimolecular G-quadruplex, thus preventing G-rich strands from binding with their counterparts for duplex formation.

In this study, we have proposed the formation of G-quadruplex and i-motif structures by both strands in the RAP2C gene promoter, indicating the relevance of these structural species in critical biological and cellular processes. The presence of various transcription factors overlapping and in the vicinity leaves a fair possibility of recognition of TF-binding sites by the proposed G-quadruplex and C-tetraplex DNA structures. Despite the unifying origin of structural polymorphism of the G-quadruplex structure and the existence of multiple transient structural species in equilibrium under solution conditions, it reflects the recognition of these structures by various proteins and small molecules, thus depicting the biological importance of these findings. Though our study is far from the exact in vivo picture and detailed structural visualization adopted by RAP2C45 and RAP2C45C, we still strongly propose that in vivo biochemical and biophysical characterization in physiological solution conditions gives an approximation of the structural behavior of these oligonucleotides under in vivo solution conditions. For more clarity on the structural status of these sequences, extensive studies are needed to portray their exact biological role in cellular conditions. Further research can focus on targeting these structures to inhibit cancer and melanoma invasion and cell migration.
