Integrated methods for public health action tracking (IMPAcT): to understand and evaluate systems change in a public health context
Authors: Therese Lockenwitz Petersen, Knud Ryom, Jane Nautrup Østergaard, Steven Allender, Anne-Louise Bjerregaard, Peter Bentsen, James Nobles
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApproaches from systems science are increasingly being trialed in public health because the drivers of poor health are complex, unpredictable and difficult to disentangle. While a broad range of methods is available to study systems science, one method alone is often insufficient for evaluation, as each offer only a limited perspective. Yet, few examples exist showing how several methods can be pragmatically integrated to generate new and meaningful insights, which is vital within systems changes. This paper describes, exemplifies and discusses the Integrated Methods for Public Health action Tracking (IMPAcT) process, which integrates group model building, Causal Loop Diagramming (CLD), the Action Scales Model (ASM), an Action Registry (AR) and Ripple Effects Mapping (REM), to better understand and address complexity within public health interventions.MethodsWe used common approaches for understanding system organization and interconnections (e.g. through CLD), identifying places to intervene in the system (e.g. ASM), tracking actions implemented within the system (e.g. REM) and understanding the impact at individual level of actions. We illustrate how the IMPAcT process can be applied via a case from a Danish project, the Healthy Active Children Study.ResultsWe present a development process, that combines the above-mentioned approaches, to capture the behaviour, and allow tracking and evaluation of a system following several intervention efforts. Integrating complementary, participatory methods enabled a formative evaluation process that supported continuous learning, adaptation and improvement across complex systems. In the IMPAcT process, methods served both as evaluation tools and as means of stakeholder engagement and knowledge co-production. Embedding reflection and dialogue allowed stakeholders to examine practice and shape next steps. Visuals and narratives enhanced the clarity and impact of the evaluation.ConclusionsThe new process supports both those working at the front-line of systems change efforts, but also researchers, municipality staff and none the least, policymakers.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-025-01396-8.

Background

Techniques from systems science have increasingly been applied in recent years to address the recognized complexity of multiple drivers of public health [1–3]. A system approach views a situation as an interconnected whole, requiring explicit definition of system boundaries and purpose, as well as an understanding of its dynamics and functioning. Such systems are inherently subjective in interpretation and demand the inclusion of multiple perspectives. However, the very nature of these complex public health problems (such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, physical inactivity, smoking and vaping) means that they act in unpredictable ways, are difficult to disentangle and produce emergent outcomes that can be far greater than the sum of their parts [4]. Thus, when trying to intervene in these complex systems through intervention efforts, it can be challenging to evaluate how and why these efforts may contribute to changes in the system dynamics [3, 5]. In practice, such evaluation methods are often applied in isolation [1, 6]. For example, common methods include Group Model Building (GMB) for creating Causal Loop Diagrams (CLD) of complex systems [7–9], identifying places to intervene through models such as the Action Scales Model (ASM) [10]. Others have used Action Registries (AR) to track actions and their implementation [8], whilst methods such as Ripple Effects Mapping (REM) can help explore the wider impacts of intervention efforts [11, 12]. Network analysis is used to examine the relationships and interactions between different system actors, and to a lesser extent, component variables [13], while readiness to change measures the capacity of stakeholders to adopt new changes [14]. These different methods may be complimentary in exploring the function of systems as well as helping locate actors and actions to understand how initiatives create or hinder changes in a system. While each offers valuable insights, no single method fully addresses the multiple, interacting dimensions of complex systems change.

Given the wicked nature of complex systems, the evaluation of programmes in complex adaptive systems (ENCOMPASS) framework [15], which recommends combining complementary approaches to adequately capture systems change, and doing so in a way that remains accessible for practitioners. However, this integrated evaluation strategy increases the resources needed and workload for researchers and practitioners evaluating systems change [16].

Much of the existing literature remains theoretical, offering limited practical guidance for implementation in real-world, resource-limited settings [3, 17, 18]. This gap highlights the need for robust, integrated and accessible methodologies to support interdisciplinary teams in addressing public health challenges.

In this paper, we address the research question: how can an integrated, participatory, multi-method process be applied to evaluate public health interventions in complex systems, in a way that is both methodologically robust and practical for real-world use? We describe, discuss and exemplify the IMPAcT process which integrates GMB, CLDs, the ASM, an AR and REM into a comprehensive process. We also explain the rationale for selecting these five methods – each chosen for their ability to address a distinct aspect of systems change evaluation – and illustrate their combined use through a case from Denmark.

Theoretical foundation

The theoretical foundation of systems evaluation draws on methodological traditions and the theories or frameworks that inform them. We take direction from the general systems theory [19], which views systems as dynamic and interdependent entities, where changes in one component can impact the entire system. This approach has been pivotal in transitioning evaluation methods from reductionist approaches to more holistic, integrative analyses.

Evaluating interventions in complex health systems requires going beyond traditional randomized controlled trials [3], which often fail to capture the dynamic, context-dependent nature of these interventions. Three broad methodological traditions inspired our work:Systems approaches – general systems theory [19], complexity theory [20] and complex adaptive systems theory [21]; these approaches seek to understand the whole system, its boundaries, purpose and internal dynamics, and emphasize participation to capture multiple perspectives.Realist evaluation – a methodology starting from an explicit initial program theory [22] to explore what works, for whom, under what circumstances and why. Realist evaluation focuses on how understanding the system context can inform adaptive interventions.Implementation science – a methodological field aiming to identify barriers and facilitators for implementing predefined interventions, often using frameworks as structuring tools (e.g., normalization process theory as a mid-range theory applied within this field [23, 24]). It emphasizes the balance between fidelity and adaptation [25].

Within these traditions, various theories and frameworks offer practical structuring tools: ecological models [26] address multi-level influences in shaping public health outcomes, reinforcing the need for holistic evaluation approaches [27]. RE-AIM [28] and TIDieR-PHP [29] provide structured reporting and evaluation; design-based implementation research [30] and adaptive learning cycles [31] from innovation research promote continuous refinement through stakeholder engagement.

Incorporating these methodological perspectives calls for a dynamic, context-sensitive approach to systems evaluation, focusing on stakeholder engagement, iterative learning and real-time adaptation [17].

Methodological foundation

Overview of selected methods to evaluate systems change

Our integrated approach is firmly rooted in the systems tradition while selectively incorporating key elements from realist evaluation – particularly its context-mechanism-outcome – and implementation science’s focus on adoption and adaptation. This blend facilitates participatory, multi-perspective evaluation capable of capturing the dynamic, emergent and interdependent nature of chances within the public health systems. We integrated elements from GMB, CLDs, the ASM, an AR and REM, each offer distinct strengths in assessing complex systems. Together, these methods support iterative learning, active stakeholder engagement and a comprehensive multi-level understanding of systems change. Central to this integration are the use of CLDs, which visually map relationships between system variables, enabling the identification of feedback loops both reinforcing and balancing, and identifying places to intervene [7, 32].

Methods 1 and 2: group model building (GMB) and causal loop diagrams (CLDs)

GMB is a collaborative modelling approach rooted in system dynamics, designed to engage stakeholders in constructing causal models of the key relationships of cause and effect representing the variables that drive complex issues. It enhances shared understanding, promotes dialogue and can inform decision-making by integrating diverse perspectives [33, 34]. A central component of GMB is the development of CLDs, which map interactions between key system variables, helping participants understand interdependence, identify feedback loops, explore potential intervention points and consider unintended consequences. CLDs visually represent causal relationships within a system, highlighting feedback loops that influence outcomes of interest [9, 35]. Reinforcing loops amplify changes within the system, while balancing loops counteract them, creating stabilizing effects [36]. By structuring discussions around causal relationships through CLDs, GMB provides a framework for collectively identifying system drivers, constraints and leverage points for systemic change.

Together, GMB and CLD complement each other by depicting the mental models of stakeholders with the ability to consider places to intervene. GMB brings stakeholders together to ensure the model reflects multiple perspectives, while CLD provides the tools to visualize the complex interactions and feedback loops within the system. Collectively, these methods enable a more comprehensive approach to problem-solving, as they allow for both stakeholder engagement and systemic insight. Group-model building, and the subsequent CLD, fosters a shared understanding of the problem, facilitates more informed decision-making and increases the likelihood of successful, sustainable solutions [9, 35]. Furthermore, they also expose stakeholders and project partners to systems-thinking concepts [37].

Method 3: action scales model (ASM)

The ASM provides a structured framework for identifying, conceptualizing and analysing actions at different levels within a system, from individual behaviours to organizational and policy interventions. It enables a multi-level perspective on system change, ensuring that evaluation captures both micro– and macro-level effects [10, 38]. The ASM, alongside CLDs, can unpick how actions at different scales interact, showing how small-scale interventions can lead to broader systemic shifts or unintended side effects. ASM acknowledges that actions vary in depth and transformative potential, with some addressing immediate outcomes while others target foundational system structures, underlying assumptions or societal paradigms. The model draws on and complements established systems thinking frameworks, such as Donella Meadows’ 12 Places to Intervene [4] and the Intervention Level Framework [39, 40]. Both emphasize that meaningful and sustained change is more likely to occur when interventions engage with deeper aspects of the system, rather than focusing solely on surface-level indicators (i.e. the events). As such, the ASM provides a valuable lens for evaluating and prioritizing actions on the basis of their capacity to effect long-term systemic transformation.

Method 4: action registry (AR)

The AR is a participatory approach that systematically records and tracks stakeholder actions throughout an intervention process. By documenting decisions, activities and their consequences, AR facilitates iterative learning and continuous systems adaptation [41]. CLDs can be used alongside AR to visualize how recorded actions influence broader system change, helping stakeholders identify feedback loops that reinforce or hinder progress. Each action is recorded in the AR, which includes detailed information on its implementation, target group, timeline and outcomes, as well as the responsible parties, key actors, contributors and any additional relevant details. Each action is updated continuously, reflecting its implementation status (emerging, active, completed or ceased) [32]. The AR provides a systematic overview of all actions, documenting their progress, implementation status, responsible actors, target groups and outcomes. This structured approach supports both accountability and transparency, while enabling cross-cutting analysis of how individual actions contribute to broader system dynamics, particularly when linked to tools such as CLDs.

Method 5: ripple effects mapping (REM)

REM is a participatory evaluation method focusing on capturing the broader impacts of interventions beyond their immediate scope. By identifying secondary and tertiary effects, REM helps assess unintended consequences and long-term systems transformation, making it particularly useful for evaluating complex social interventions [11, 12, 42]. Participating in a REM workshop serves a dual purpose: while it documents ripple effects of an intervention, it simultaneously offers participants the opportunity to reflect on why certain outcomes occurred, how specific actions contributed to these results, what has worked, what has not and what could be adapted moving forward. In this way, methods such as REM become part of the intervention itself, shaping practice as much as evaluating it. Incorporating CLDs within REM allows for a structured visualization of how initial actions ripple through the system, identifying reinforcing or balancing loops that amplify or dampen effects over time.

Method integration: the IMPAcT process

By integrating GMB, CLD, the ASM, an AR and REM into the IMPAcT evaluation process, we draw on the unique strengths of each method to create an iterative, systems-oriented and efficient approach. This integrated process is designed to navigate the complexity and interconnectedness of real-world systems, enabling a more nuanced understanding of change processes. Our rationale for bringing these methods together lies in their applied, participatory and action-oriented nature. Each method is not only a tool for analysis but also a mechanism for engagement. Creating space for stakeholders to co-produce knowledge, reflect on their actions and directly inform their next steps. The integration of these participatory methods fosters shared ownership of both the process and its outcomes, ensuring that insights are not only generated but immediately actionable within the system being studied.

Ultimately, this paper will outline a process for how to apply these evaluation methods in a structured, combined and context-specific way. This will include a step-by-step process for engaging stakeholders, identifying system drivers, in order to track both direct and indirect impact. In addition to presenting a proposed evaluation process, our work will also contribute to the broader academic discussion on systems evaluation.

Applying the IMPAcT process in the Healthy Active Children Study

We draw on our experiences using this evaluation approach in the design, implementation and evaluation of a mental health-oriented systems approach in Denmark. We present our reflections, and the strengths, limitations and future use of this methods integration.

Setting and population

Our example draws on the Healthy Active Children Study on Moen, Denmark. Moen is a small island connected by bridges to the rest of Vordingborg Municipality, with a population of ~10 000 citizens, one larger city and four schools (two public, two private). Population density differs in line with socio-economic status across the island [43]. As with many rural areas, the municipality faces challenges such as higher rates of chronic diseases, lifestyle-related health issues and access to specialized healthcare. The population’s overall health is influenced by factors such as smoking, physical activity and dietary behaviours [44].

The Healthy Active Children Study aimed to conduct a system-oriented community initiative to improve childhood mental health. The study was conducted and evaluated between October 2021 and November 2024. The lead researcher (T.L.P.) was embedded full-time in Vordingborg Municipality, at the Secretariat of Health, during the whole project period. Our integrated approach was carried out as part of the wider natural experimental evaluation.

Step 1 and 2: Mapping the underlying system and identifying places to intervene

The initial step involved developing a shared understanding of the system, and how it leads to the observed problem of poor mental health resulting in a CLD created via GMB. The process included one preparatory workshop and three GMB workshops (workshops 1–3) [7, 45]. A municipal employee collaborated with the embedded researcher as a backbone office to identify and recruit key leaders – such as local politicians, department heads, municipal leaders and local stakeholders, including sports club representatives. In the preparatory workshop, participants choose what they perceived to be the most important health topic for the children in their community on the basis of local health data from the municipality (mental health was selected). In workshops 1 and 2, key leaders and stakeholders created a CLD – using the Allender et al. [36] process – depicting the local system affecting mental health in the local community [45].

Based on the CLD created in step 1, key leaders, stakeholders and all local citizens willing to engage in changing the local system met in workshop 3, to identify and prioritize areas for action. The workshop introduced the ASM [10] to encourage participants to critique the system from a wider perspective and identify actions that could change how the system operates. We described and discussed how the deeper levels of the model (goals and beliefs) offer greater potential to transform how the system operates [10]. The model drew attention to the participants’ curiosity about structural factors, while also facilitating broader thinking about actions within complex systems.

Step 3: combining action group formation with ongoing evaluation

Following workshop 3 (Fig. 1), local action groups were established to implement activities guided by co-creation principles [7]. The action groups independently selected which activity to begin implementing, guided by the ASM, as well as their own motivation, engagement and perceptions of where they could make the greatest impact. These action groups received support and supervision from a backbone office comprising a municipality coordinator and the embedded researcher.

Face-to-face action group meetings (with 5–10 participants) were held on a quarterly basis and facilitated by the backbone office. The main purpose of these meetings was to maintain the engagement and motivation of the stakeholders included and to contribute to their growing network. Every meeting started with participants sharing their successes and challenges with activities from the last meeting. Each activity was logged by the municipality employee in the AR (see Additional file 1), which contained inspiration and questions from different evaluation tools [8, 10, 11]. The register was continuously adapted to make it useable for practitioners while maintaining depth for research. During the development process, it became clear that it was necessary to include supporting questions, and clarification notes to help the facilitators explore the main question in more depth, e.g. the question about criteria of success, was adapted to include a note: that it is not just about achieving a high number of participants. Stakeholders would give updated information to the backbone office every quarter, which was added to the register.

Step 4: follow- up interviews

There was ongoing contact from the backbone office with action group members between meetings based on the needs of each group. Further, as recommended by Chazdon et al. [46], supplementary individual interviews were conducted with stakeholders who did not participate in the action group meetings to ensure the continuous updating of the AR. These interviews aimed to generate additional insights about specific activities and complete respective sections of the AR. Participants were initially asked to identify any outcomes they believed were missing. Afterwards, they were asked which activities they considered the most significant for the community because of the project. They were encouraged to share short narratives related to these outcomes and the specific sections of the CLD where they were involved. The insights gathered from these interviews were incorporated into the AR, which then fed into the REM timeline (step 5).

Step 5: understanding the wider impacts of actions

Every 6 months, the action group meeting included an REM activity to try to understand how activities may be contributing to change across the system. These workshops utilized a simplified version of the Nobles et al. [11] REM process (see Additional file 2). As participants became more accustomed to the REM method, the duration of each workshop decreased (from 60 to 30 min).

A Danish protocol was developed, inspired by the REM guidelines [11, 46] primarily focusing on capturing the ripple effects of actions on a timeline. A physical whiteboard was used to illustrate a pre-drawn timeline that we wanted the participant to relate to. The timeline covered the period from August 2022 to December 2022 (see Fig. 1). During the REM workshop, participants were asked to reflect on their work during this period, noting significant activities or actions along the timeline by writing them down on Post-it notes and placing them on the whiteboard.

The Post-it notes were then discussed in a plenary, followed by each stakeholder identifying the resulting impacts of these activities and actions, using arrows to demonstrate the “ripple effect” between activities and their corresponding impacts.

Following the first workshop, the REM output was digitalized on Miro (an online, collaborative whiteboard tool). For the second, third and fourth REM workshops, the overall process remained the same, with updated or new information being added to the REM output on Miro. This was done to maintain an overview of the activities as time went by, and to make it possible to share knowledge and results across locations and sectors as well as to make it more accessible for potential online evaluations and meetings in the future. Further, based on the first timeline, we added more of the information gathered in the AR (which contained information from ASM) between the different action group meetings to the timeline, before presenting it to the stakeholders for the second time (June 2023). The backbone office deeply engaged with the data from steps 3–5 to uncover impact pathways – sequences of actions, activities and their resulting impacts – within the REM output. Identifying these pathways was beneficial, as they provided a structured narrative pertaining to the project development and to identify potential synergistic effects that could emerge.

Additional data from the interviews and AR was incorporated into the timeline, to create a more visual and manageable result of the full evaluation. For example, different colour codes were added to each activity on the timeline to illustrate the four different levels (understood as places to intervene) from the ASM that the activity intervened within the system [10]. Further, details were included regarding whether the activity was managed by volunteers or professionals, along with insights into synergies between various activities and the status of each activity – whether it had ceased, was ongoing, completed or was still in development (Fig. 3).

For each activity, stakeholders’ narratives from the interviews were incorporated to provide a cohesive storyline when the visual output was presented (both the timeline and the CLD). The narratives served as a powerful tool for driving systemic change, keeping engagement and motivation, as well as for evaluating, understanding and highlighting the transformations taking place within the communities [47].

Furthermore, all activities and the individuals performing them were continuously fed back into the original CLD within the theme from the map that the activity was intended to impact (Fig. 2).

In total, 28 activities were registered during the evaluation period. Most activities were categorized as events (n = 15), the remaining activities (n = 10) were registered as working on/with the structural level of the system. Of the 28 activities, 13 was carried out by volunteers, 9 by professionals and 6 in collaboration between volunteers and professionals. Five activities manage to create synergies between different activities. The majority of the activities worked with the theme “communities” from the CLD, with only a few addressing the theme “role models.” The last theme from the CLD was “pressure of society,” which was a theme that all the participants felt was important, but they didn’t know how to address it (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Our findings demonstrate that the integration of these complementary, participatory methods supports a formative evaluation process that facilitates continuous learning, real-time adaptation and iterative improvement across complex systems. Central to the IMPAcT process is the dual function of the methods used serving not only as tools for evaluation but also as mechanisms for stakeholder engagement and knowledge co-production. By embedding reflection, dialogue and joint sense-making into the process, stakeholders are enabled to critically examine practice and contribute to shaping the next steps. The integration of visual illustrations and narrative descriptions enhanced the communicative power of the evaluation. This combination enables a more accessible and holistic understanding of complex processes for practitioners.

Comparison with the literature

While various frameworks such as the ENCOMPASS framework [15] emphasize the need for broader evaluation, [15, 48] most studies tend to focus narrowly on a single main outcome and a single evaluation approach rather than integrating multiple methods within a comprehensive framework. This limitation is primarily due to the substantial resource demands and methodological complexity associated with comprehensive evaluation [16]. Typically, research examines one specific aspect at a time, such as social network [7, 49], REM [12], process evaluation [50] or changes in CLDs [5], without combining them to obtain a more holistic view of system change.

Participatory action research (PAR) shares common ground with system methodologies, which is inherently oriented towards local action and stakeholder engagement [51]. PAR emphasizes co-creation of knowledge through active participation of those affected by the issues under study, fostering empowerment and contextual relevance [52]. IMPaCT and similar system approaches build upon and extend PAR by integrating multiple complementary methods, allowing for a more structured and comprehensive evaluation of complex systems. This combination supports not only participatory engagement but also addresses the dynamic, emergent and interdependent nature of public health systems change in a way that traditional PAR alone may not fully capture.

Recent work by Hall et al. [53] in the JU:MP programme and Mansukoski and colleagues [54] in the ActEarly programme, both provided protocol examples of innovative approaches to systems evaluation where multiple methods are applied. By blending complexity-driven and pragmatist evaluation methods, they aimed to create a more comprehensive and adaptable framework. Building on this emerging trend, our work takes the next step by not only proposing an integrated approach but also testing and applying it in practice. We have already implemented our process in real-world settings, demonstrating its ability to capture the dynamic and interconnected nature of system change. This practical application has allowed us to refine our process, ensuring that it is both robust and flexible, and capable of addressing the complexities of system change in a way that traditional methods alone cannot.

In addition, much of the existing research emphasizes specific outcomes, such as weight change, rather than evaluating how a system evolves over time. Conventional evaluation methods, such as randomized controlled trials, aiming to isolate the effect of specific interventions but often struggle to capture the dynamics, non-linear processes inherent in systems change [55]. This narrow focus overlooks the broader dynamics of system transformation, limiting insights into how interventions create lasting structural change.

Recognising the complementarity of these perspectives may support more holistic approaches to both intervention design and evaluation [56].

Strength and limitations

Our process could be viewed as a template for others to adapt in their own contexts on the basis of local resource, expertise and stakeholder preferences. The IMPAcT process can serve as a valuable tool for communication, offering an effective way to inform stakeholders about intervention progress. In our implementation, it has been utilized across municipal departments, for presentations to policy makers, and within local communities to convey developments in the project. By combining visual elements with storytelling, the process fosters engagement and a sense of ownership among stakeholders [47]. Storytelling, in particular, brings the narrative to life, integrating local experiences to make the project’s activities and impact more relatable and compelling to diverse audiences [47]. Storytelling was incorporated in the REM part of the process and in the dissemination of the results.

The visual component of the process provides a clear and accessible overview of progress, enabling us to demonstrate the achievements of a systems approach when outcomes, such as obesity rates or physical activity levels, are not immediately measurable. Furthermore, the AR has proven useful for internal reporting and evaluation within the municipality, complementing the visual outputs to provide a comprehensive overview of the project’s development.

In addition, a strength of the IMPAcT process is that it builds on the integration of various well-developed and tested methods. This allowed us to track both intended and emergent changes across different levels of the system, including shifts in relationships, practices and structures, while also creating spaces for collective reflection and learning. This approach aligns closely with the ENCOMPASS framework [15], which also advocates for the use of multiple methods to better understand changes within a system. By adopting a flexible, integrated approach, both our process and the ENCOMPASS framework recognize that systems are dynamic and multifaceted, requiring diverse evaluation strategies to capture their complexity. This parallel highlights how both the framework and our process are rooted in the need for comprehensive, adaptable approaches to systems evaluation, helping to bridge the gap between complex theory and practical application.

Evaluation should continuously “feed into” adaptation and innovation of the project. Our process facilitates this by ensuring that research findings, or even the process of data collection itself, such as through methods such as REM, continually contributes to an ongoing cycle of learning and adjustment. By integrating these findings into the evaluation process, we enable real-time insights to inform decision-making, driving continuous program evolution. This iterative loop of feedback not only enhances the relevance of the evaluation but also ensures that the system remains responsive to emerging challenges and opportunities throughout its development. Understanding the context and needs of the environment in which one is working is crucial. Adapting to these contextual factors is key to ensuring the relevance and effectiveness of the evaluation.

In implementing the IMPAcT process within such a complex, multi-actor initiative, it becomes particularly important to carefully consider the composition of the stakeholder group to ensure a broad representation of participants from the project. A diverse group can offer valuable insights into various aspects of systemic change, whereas a homogeneous stakeholder group may have a narrow perspective on the system. It is essential to emphasize that the process must always be adapted to the specific context in which it is implemented, as well as to the stakeholders with whom collaboration is undertaken. Contextual factors, such as organizational structures, local needs and cultural dynamics, may influence the success of the process. Moreover, the diverse perspectives and expertise of stakeholders necessitate a flexible approach to ensure that the process remains relevant and engaging for all parties involved. Tailoring the process to these variables not only improves its relevance but also strengthens stakeholder ownership and commitment, key factors for the initiative’s long-term success and sustainability.

Implications for policy and practice

Implementing systems approaches in policy and practice necessitates a fundamental shift in mindset [57]. Both practitioners and researchers must embrace openness, flexibility and a readiness to engage with complexity and uncertainty. Unlike traditional linear interventions, systems-based work is inherently non-linear and unpredictable, requiring the courage to proceed without predetermined outcomes and a commitment to iterative learning and adaptation throughout the process.

From a municipal perspective, the IMPAcT process helped build internal capacity for evaluation, especially among staff with limited prior experience. Traditional evaluation approaches, often linear, expert-driven and methodologically rigid, can be inaccessible or impractical for professionals embedded in the day-to-day complexities of public service delivery. In contrast, our proposed IMPAcT process, which integrates tools such as CLDs, REM and the AR, emphasizes co-creation, visual thinking and iterative reflection presents a tool which we hope make the process more intuitive and engaging for non-specialists. By actively participating, municipal staff both contributed local insights and developed transferable skills in monitoring and adapting complex interventions.

Adapting the integrated evaluation process to the municipal context required several iterations, particularly with the AR. It was crucial to ensure the tool was both functional and meaningful for local practitioners. This included tailoring how information was presented to support internal learning and decision-making. Data collection often relied on informal, trust-based channels, emphasizing the importance of relationship-building and shared ownership. These experiences highlight the need for time, flexibility and co-development when applying systems methods in practice. While the IMPAcT process shows strong potential, particularly in fostering cross-sectoral reflection and shared understanding, future applications should continue to attend to how individual methods are introduced, adapted and supported in local settings.

Future research

Despite increasing interest in applying systems approaches, evaluation has often been deprioritized or minimally reported [1] because of cost, participant burden and complexity. This limits opportunities for generating practice-based evidence and sharing learning across settings [1, 58]. Looking forward, the development and application of flexible, practice-oriented evaluation methods are essential not only to capture system dynamics but also to support practitioners in documenting, reflecting on and sharing their experiences in complex, real-world settings. This gap underscores the need for further research to validate proposed associations between system approaches, population and individual outcomes and deepen our understanding of the effective implementation and evaluation of systems approaches. Equally important is a deeper understanding of how research-practice collaboration in the evaluation of system changes can be strengthened to ensure mutual benefit for all stakeholders. This includes identifying effective forms of support that can enable sustained implementation without continuous external facilitation.

The IMPAcT process responds to these needs by offering a versatile, user-friendly and context-sensitive evaluation approach, applicable across both research and practice. By combining GMB, CLDs, ASM, AR and REM, the IMPAcT process supports the observation and interpretation of systemic change within dynamic environments. It also holds potential to inform policy and agenda-setting by translating complex system insights into accessible, actionable knowledge. While the IMPAcT process contributes meaningfully to addressing current evaluation challenges, further research is needed to explore its scalability, adaptability across settings and long-term value in fostering integrated research–practice–policy collaboration.

Conclusions

In this paper, we present a novel, multi-method and integrated process for understanding, describing and evaluating system changes. The IMPAcT process was intentionally designed to be both practical and accessible, supporting front-line practitioners, researchers and policymakers in identifying and leveraging opportunities for impactful intervention. The IMPAcT process not only enables a more nuanced understanding of systems chances behaviours but also supports stakeholders across practice, policy and research in reflecting on, designing and adapting interventions. A key contribution of this approach lies in its dual function: it informs intervention design while simultaneously supporting evaluation, reducing the need for parallel, resource-intensive processes. As such, the proposed IMPAcT process offers a valuable contribution to the advancement of systems-informed evaluation and implementation research and practice.
