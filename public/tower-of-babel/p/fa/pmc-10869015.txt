Paediatric Generalised Pustular Psoriasis with IL36RN Mutation: Successful Treatment with Adalimumab
Authors: Kexin Liao, Zhao Hou, Rongrong Jing, Xinyi Qian, Rongxin Zhang
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

A 3-year-old boy was referred to our hospital for generalised pustules, erythema, scales and pyrexia. He was diagnosed with GPP at the age of 6 months old. Initial treatments included systematic corticosteroids, acitretin, traditional Chinese medicine and topical steroids. Complete remission was maintained during a 2-year follow-up. He had no family history of psoriasis, and genetic analysis showed a heterozygous mutation at C.227C>T within the IL36RN gene. The diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis vaccine was injected before the recurrence of GPP. He was started on oral acitretin 15 mg daily before being transferred to our hospital. Unfortunately, his condition gradually worsened with generalised pustules, high fever and skin pain [Figure 1a and b]. GPP was diagnosed according to the clinical manifestations and pathological examination [Figure 2]. After admission, he received intravenous methylprednisolone 24 mg and oral acitretin 10 mg daily, along with topical moisture agents, and the condition gradually improved. However, his pustular psoriasis flared with high fever when the methylprednisolone dose was reduced to 8 mg daily [Figure 1c and d]. Excluding hepatitis B and tuberculosis infection, adalimumab subcutaneously (20 mg weekly for the first 2 weeks and 20 mg every two weeks thereafter) was given with unchanged doses of methylprednisolone and acitretin. His body temperature recovered to normal within 24 hours after the first injection of adalimumab. A few days later, improvement of erythema, pustules and scales was noticeable. About ten days after adalimumab treatment, his psoriatic lesions basically subsided. The doses of methylprednisolone and acitretin were gradually reduced and discontinued as the condition stabilised. The child is still receiving adalimumab monotherapy with no recurrence for about 8 months [Figure 1e and f]. No adverse events were found in regular follow-up and blood monitoring.

(a and b) Generalised sterile pustules based on erythema accompanied with partial scales before being referred to our hospital. (c and d) Psoriatic lesions recurred, presenting as pustules based on erythema and extensive scales all over the body before adalimumab therapy. (e and f) 8 months after adalimumab therapy with no recurrence

Pathological examination shows mild hyperkeratosis and acanthosis, diminution of the granular layer and spongiform pustules of Kogoj in the epidermis. Mild to moderate lymphocytic infiltration is present in the superficial dermis (HE×100)

GPP is an uncommon and severe form of psoriasis characterised by small sterile pustules on an erythematous basis, some of which may partially merge into a lake of pus.[1] Smoking, infection, drug withdrawal and pregnancy are predisposing and aggravating factors.[2] IL36RN mutation-positive patients usually demonstrated a more severe phenotype (absence of concurrent psoriasis vulgaris, onset at a younger age, and a higher risk of systemic inflammation) than those without mutation.[3]

In paediatric GPP, there is usually an acute onset and recurrent episodes, often accompanied by high fever, which can be life-threatening in severe cases. In this case, it is necessary to alleviate the symptoms as soon as possible in the short term due to the young age and urgent conditions. The triggers in our case may be the vaccination and glucocorticoid dose reduction. After the failure of traditional systemic therapy, we used adalimumab, a fully human anti-TNF-alpha monoclonal antibody, which significantly improved in children with severe plaque psoriasis.[4] Data on adalimumab in the treatment of paediatric GPP have been reported in some literature, but only one case mentioned IL36RN mutation [Table 1].[5] Our study further assessed the safety and efficacy of adalimumab in paediatric GPP with IL36RN mutation at a younger age. We suggest that adalimumab is a viable option for acute paediatric GPP. Long-term follow-up is needed to monitor recurrence and potential adverse reactions.
