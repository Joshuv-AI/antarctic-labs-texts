The influence of ligands on AlphaFold3 prediction of cryptic pockets
Authors: Maria Lazou, Felix Tuchscherer, Sandor Vajda, Diane Joseph-McCarthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryptic pockets—binding sites that are formed or exposed upon a conformational change—represent an important class of potentially druggable binding sites. Reliably predicting cryptic pockets capable of binding ligands, however, remains a challenge. Herein we examine the use of AlphaFold 3 (AF3) for generating realistic conformational ensembles that include known cryptic pockets. We find that AF3 is generally able to reproduce the scale of conformational change required for cryptic site formation. When given a cryptic-site ligand for the protein, AF3 predominantly predicts conformations competent to bind the ligand in the cryptic site; without the ligand, conformations lacking the cryptic pocket generally dominate. While the results may reflect a bias toward memorized structural priors, the level of detrimental memorization appears to be limited. We also show that the choice of the ligand can significantly impact the predictions, and that AF3 is able to produce models with the ligand correctly positioned. Variability in ligand position, however, suggests that generating ensembles of co-folded predictions is critical to enhancing the likelihood of obtaining a correct binding mode. Overall, AF3-generated protein-ligand structural ensembles have potential utility in cryptic-site drug discovery, and they can reveal ligands likely to bind to those sites.Subject terms: Computational models, Machine learning, Protein structure predictions

Introduction

Cryptic sites are hidden sites that become exposed upon a relatively small conformational change from the native structure. They are important in that they can expand the range of druggable targets1–4. Specifically, cryptic sites can extend an active site, act as allosteric sites, or serve as a binding site for PROTACS and/or molecular glues5.

As a result, considerable effort has been devoted to predicting cryptic sites through a variety of physics-based methods6–10. In particular, mixed-solvent molecular dynamics and related enhanced-sampling techniques have been widely adopted to identify transient, ligandable pockets by monitoring preferential cosolvent binding, sometimes in combination with approaches such as SWISH, Markov state modeling, or collective variable–based enhanced sampling to capture larger-scale conformational changes11. Others have sought to augment physics-based computational approaches with artificial intelligence, using models such as PocketMiner for residue-level crypticity scoring, AlphaFold-based conformational ensembles, and deep generative co-folding methods like AlphaFold3 and NeuralPLexer to propose ligand placements in putative cryptic pockets11–13. While significant progress has been made, challenges remain, including method-specific limitations such as probe aggregation and protein unfolding in mixed-solvent simulations, the need for carefully chosen collective variables in biased simulations, and the still-limited validation and generalizability of many AI-based tools for cryptic pocket discovery11,14.

In addition, it has long been known that proteins exist as conformational ensembles and that protein flexibility is key to understanding protein function15,16. Ligand binding (including cryptic pocket opening) is inherently coupled to protein flexibility. In fact, recent pharmaceutically derived benchmark datasets, such as the CASP16 experimental protein–ligand dataset, provide high-resolution structures of systems where accurate pose and affinity prediction critically depend on modeling protein flexibility17. Related to this, it has been shown that AlphaFold2 (AF2) models of proteins can have similar accuracy to solution NMR structures, which capture a certain level of protein flexibility18.

AlphaFold3 (AF3), compared to AF2, has the capability of predicting ligand-bound vs. ligand-unbound structures19,20. It operates by utilizing a single unified diffusion-based approach that treats all molecular components as part of an integrated system from the start. Although AlphaFold3 enables joint modeling of proteins and ligands, recent benchmarking of protein–ligand co-folding methods has shown that prediction accuracy can strongly depend on similarity to specific ligand poses observed during training, raising concerns about limited generalization to rare or sparsely represented binding modes21. Importantly, cryptic pockets fall into this category; even when the underlying protein has been structurally characterized, ligand-stabilized open conformations could still be underrepresented relative to closed states.

In this study, we examine the ability of AF3 to predict the ligand-bound conformation of cryptic sites both with and without the ligand present. This work builds on our previous study looking at the ability of AF2 to predict cryptic pockets22. We and others have shown that AF has a strong bias towards the dominant conformation and has memorized alternative conformations23–27. AF2 can nonetheless confidently predict different structures from the same MSA.

Herein, we compare the distribution of conformations generated by AF3 with and without an input ligand to the distribution of bound and unbound structures in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) for a test set of known cryptic pockets. We also examine the accuracy of the ligand placement by AF3, the local structure surrounding the ligand, and the effect of running AF3 with different ligands. A detailed analysis is provided.

Results and discussion

Our cryptic pocket test sets were primarily selected either from CryptoSite4 (for the original test set) or CryptoBench28 (for the second test set). As described in the Methods, we then applied a set of criteria to classify all PDB structures of those proteins as bound or unbound with respect to the cryptic site. In general, a cryptic site is thought to be one which forms upon ligand binding as a result of a relatively small conformation change. It has been shown, however, that cryptic sites that can form spontaneously without ligand binding exist but are rare, and that genuinely cryptic sites that never form without a ligand are equally rare29. In some cases in our original test set, there are unbound “cryptic sites” in the open conformation. Some are due to the determination of an unbound structure with the pocket open after the CryptoSite database was assembled, but many are not. In one case (BACE), the cryptic pocket forms when the protein closes around the site—in the open form, it is too shallow for ligand binding.

Agreement between AF3 prediction and PDB distributions

For cryptic sites, AF3 with multiple seeds tends to find the dominant bound conformation when predicting the protein structure with the ligand present and the dominant unbound conformation when predicting the protein structure without the ligand. The distribution of structural models produced using AF3 with a ligand is generally in agreement with the distribution of bound structures found in the PDB (in 13 out of 16 cases), while the distribution produced by AF3 without a ligand is generally in agreement with the distribution of unbound structures in the PDB (in 12 out of 16 cases). In 9 out of 16 cases, the predicted distributions match both the bound and unbound PDB distributions. The test set is described in Table 1, and the distributions and root mean square deviation (RMSD) plots are shown in Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3. This observation is consistent with previous results showing that AF2 predictions are driven by a memory of what is in the PDB22. Here, the distributions are sharpened somewhat by AF3 relative to what is observed in the PDB. It is clear that the same MSA is capable of predicting a range of conformations at the scale (side chain movements, loop movements, and helical shifts) necessary for cryptic site formation.

Accuracy of AF3 predictions for proteins in the AF3 training set

It is typical for most cryptic sites to be formed in the open conformation. In our dataset, for 15 of the 16 proteins, the bound reference structure is in the open conformation, and the unbound reference structure is in the closed conformation. For BLG, Myosin II, TEM, MAPK, Ricin, ALDBP, and PDK, AF3 correctly predicts the bound distribution with the ligand as predominantly open and the unbound distribution without the ligand as predominantly closed (Group 1a proteins, Figs. 1 and 2). As an example, the top-ranked model produced for BLG with the ligand (with a ligand RMSD to X-ray pose of 1.17 Å) is shown in Fig. 3. Top-ranked models for the other proteins in Group 1a are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Superposition of the bound reference structure and the AF3-predicted structure co-folded with the reference ligand.

Shown in (A) for BLG and in (B) for AR. The AF3 model with the ligand is shown colored by element with lilac carbons. The X-ray bound reference structure is shown colored by element with blue carbons. In (B) the DHT bound in the active site is shown in magenta.

For GluR2, Hsp90, and K-Ras, AF3 predicts the bound distribution with the ligand correctly but not the unbound distribution (Group 1b proteins, Figs. 1 and 2). For GluR2, AF3, without the ligand, incorrectly and almost exclusively predicts the open conformation; only two closed conformations (out of 500) are generated. However, many of the closed conformations in the PDB “unbound” distribution (210/340) actually have glutamate bound in the cryptic pocket. These are considered unbound in our test set because the glutamate does not clash with the moving segment, although it is possible that the closed position is more preferred when glutamate is bound at the site. For Hsp90, in the reference structures, there is a flexible loop (residues 106 to 113, which include the moving segment residues) that causes a break in a helix. A closer look at the distribution of structures in the PDB reveals that Hsp90 adopts at least three different conformations: one with the helix-loop-helix, another with a helix, and a third in which the protein has undergone a more significant conformational change. All three of these conformations are represented in the bound and unbound structures to some degree. AF3 with and without the ligand, models the whole segment (residue 100 to 124) as a helix with a slight kink in it. K-Ras is a structure with many very flexible regions. The helix spanning residues 65 to 74 (which includes the two residues in the moving segment) shifts from the closed to the open conformation. In the bound structures, the range of conformations of the helix is centered around the open conformation, while in the unbound structures, there is a continuous distribution from the closed to open conformations. When clashing with the reference ligand was added as a criterion for being closed (likely needed since the moving segment is in a helix, see Supplementary Fig. 5), the PDB distributions for bound and unbound were open and closed as anticipated.

For RNase A, AF3 predicts the unbound distribution without the ligand correctly, but not the bound distribution with the ligand (Group 1c, Figs. 1 and 2). The moving segment (His 119) is a single side chain in the middle of a beta-sheet. The bound PDB distribution is entirely open except that one of the structures has His 119 in both conformations, although there are very few bound structures. The unbound PDB distribution is mostly closed as expected, although some structures are open, and some have His 119 in both conformations. AF3 with the ligand predicts mostly the closed conformation, which may be an example of detrimental memorization. Herein, we describe several cases of “detrimental memorization” (RNase A, AmpC, AR, and BACE), where AF3 with the ligand incorrectly predicts the closed conformation, likely due to the fact that the total number of closed structures in the PDB is much greater than the total number of open structures.

For AR and AmpC, the PDB distribution is close to expected (Group 2 proteins, Figs. 1 and 2). The AF3 with the ligand incorrectly predicts the closed conformation, because AF3 places the ligand in the active site pocket (the orthosteric site) instead of the cryptic pocket. For AR, the bound PDB distribution is almost equally split between open and “other”, with “other” slightly outweighing open, but there are very few structures. Nonetheless, AF3 places the ligand at the dihydrotestosterone binding site, the active site, which is quite far from the cryptic site (3B). In fact, there are no models in which the ligand is placed in the cryptic pocket. Most of the “other” conformations in both the PDB distributions and the AF3 distributions are partially open with, for example, Met 734 open and the Lys 720 closed (e.g., 2PIO). In the reference-bound structure, the cryptic pocket ligand (RB1) and the active site ligand (dihydrotestosterone (DHT)), are bound simultaneously. In fact, in all of the bound structures, DHT is occupying the active site while another ligand is in the cryptic pocket. It is possible that without the DHT present, the RB1 would bind in the active site. In the closed reference structure, while the cryptic pocket is empty, a different ligand is bound in the active site. For AmpC, AF3 with and without the ligand predicts only the closed conformation. The ligand in the AF3 models is incorrectly placed in the nearby active site pocket, leaving the cryptic pocket empty and closed. In both of these cases, this result could also be due to detrimental memorization since the number of closed structures overall is much greater than the number of open structures.

For PKA, Thrombin and BACE (Group 3, Supplementary Fig. 3), there is almost a continuous distribution of loop conformations (the moving segment) from closed to open. For PKA, in the bound PDB distribution, there is a wide range of open conformations, while for the unbound PDB distribution, there is an almost continuous range of conformations spanning from closed to open. For Thrombin, there are distinct open and closed states, but a wide range of conformations around each. In the PDB unbound distribution, there are two clusters of closed conformations as well as many open conformations. Depending on the bound reference structure chosen, the conformations in the structures are either all classified as open (as in Supplementary Fig. 3) or all closed. For PKA and Thrombin, the cryptic pocket is open and accessible whether the ligand is bound or not. As a result, AF3 also predicts all open conformations. For BACE, most of the structures are classified as closed, although there is a significant fraction of open conformations in both the bound and unbound PDB distributions. The AF3 predictions are all entirely closed, which may reflect detrimental memorization.

While there are a few instances of detrimental memorization, the effects over this test set seem to be less prevalent with AF3 than with AF222. Over this test, we describe four cases of detrimental memorization (25%), all involving instances where AF3 with the ligand predicts the closed conformation instead of the correct open conformation. In our previous work utilizing AF2 over the same test set22, we present eight cases (50%) where either there is an imbalance in the total number of structures in the PDB and AF2 only finds the most prevalent conformation (25%), or there is a significant number of “other” conformations and AF2 only finds other (25%) (see Table S1). The enhanced performance of AF3 may be due to the presence or absence of the ligand, which is obviously not possible with AF2.

Accuracy of ligand pose predicted by AF3

The reference ligand is predominantly positioned correctly by AF3 in 10/16 of the proteins (see Table 2 and Fig. 4A). For four other cases, the correct pocket is identified; for three of these (PKA, TEM, and K-Ras), some poses are also correct, for one (RNase A), only a very small number of correct poses are. Finally, for the last two proteins (AR and AmpC), the ligand, as discussed above, is positioned in the orthosteric site. There is an overall correlation between ligand predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score and the ligand pose correctness, since with the decrease of ligand pLDDT values over the ensemble, the number of correct poses decreases as well (see Fig. 4B).

Notably, for three of the proteins, correct ligand poses are predicted in which the ligand still sterically clashes with the protein (see Fig. 4C and Table S2). For PDK, this represents only two of the models predicted by AF3 (<1%). For MAPK, it represents over 35 of the 500 models predicted by AF3 (still <10%), and for Hsp90, it represents over 400 models predicted by AF3 (~80%). In all three cases, the ligand pLDDT values were generally high (pLDDT >70), according to AlphaFold2-defined thresholds19; however, for MAPK and PDK, some predictions with lower pLDDT values corresponded to correct poses. Some but not all of the MAPK predictions with low ligand pLDDT scores had steric clashes, but all of the PDK models with clashes still have high ligand pLDDTs. Interestingly, ligand pLDDT for all Hsp90 models is very high ( > 95) despite most poses having a steric clash.

The data also indicate that if AF3 is run with a ligand, at least one conformation competent to bind a ligand in the cryptic site would be found for 14 out of 16 proteins in the test. This result is expected given that structures with ligands bound in the cryptic pocket exist and were in the AF3 training set. It also suggests that, despite some inaccuracies, the ensemble of predicted structures should be useful for drug design purposes. We also find that ensemble-level distributions converge rapidly with increasing numbers of seeds, with minimal changes observed beyond approximately 80 seeds (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 8). This indicates that the reported conformational populations are robust to stochastic variation in the diffusion process.

The effect of the ligand on AF3 predictions

The choice of the ligand used for the AF3 prediction can sometimes affect the results, especially when the ligand binds to a conformation significantly populated in the PDB. For example, when the ligand-bound AF3 prediction for GluR2 is performed with the smaller ligand, glutamate, instead of the reference ligand, ATPO30, the correct closed conformation is predicted. Glutamate is not large enough, given its position in the center of the cryptic pocket, to require the pocket to be open in order to bind. Similarly, X-ray structures of TEM-Xe show Xe simultaneously bound in multiple sites, including the cryptic pocket, all in the closed conformation. The AF3 predictions for TEM with one Xe bound also correctly predict the closed conformation, but with the Xe in a different pocket. Interestingly, if we ask AF3 to predict TEM with 3 Xe bound, it places all 3 Xe as overlapping in the cryptic pocket with the protein in the closed conformation. In our classification of bound vs. unbound structures in the PDB, the GluR2-glutamate and TEM-Xenon structures are all classified as unbound since there is no clash with the moving residues.

Furthermore, when AF3 is run for AR with both the cryptic site ligand (RB1) and the active site ligand (DHT) simultaneously, DHT is always predicted correctly to bind in the active site, but RB1 is still not placed correctly in the cryptic pocket. Instead, in the top-ranked prediction (and approximately half of the predictions overall) it is placed in a distinct third pocket on the surface of the protein (Fig. 5). We repeated the calculation with the order RB1 and DHT were input to AF3 reversed, and the results were essentially the same (Supplementary Fig. 9a). We also examined if the relative size of the ligands contributes to the misplacement. RB1 and DHT have similar sizes (22 and 21 heavy atoms, respectively), which may partly explain why AF3 places RB1 alone in the active site. The cryptic pocket ligands range in size from 4 to 23 heavy atoms. AF3 runs with the smaller ligand EDO (four heavy atoms) plus DHT and 2MI (ten heavy atoms) plus DHT, did not in any instance place the ligands in cryptic pocket (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Taken together, these results indicate that AF3 preferentially stabilizes ligand placement in the dominant active site pocket and struggles to correctly resolve simultaneous occupied binding sites, consistent with a bias toward dominant structural priors rather than context-dependent ligand discrimination.

While the choice of ligand has no effect on the MSA, AlphaFold3 predicts proteins and ligands jointly using a diffusion-based framework. As a result, different ligand inputs alter the initial conditions and constraints of the denoising process, leading to distinct denoising trajectories even for the same protein sequence. These ligand-dependent denoising paths can bias the model toward different local minima in the conformational landscape, which may explain why AF3 produces different structural ensembles depending on the ligand provided, despite identical MSAs. This sensitivity to denoising trajectories further motivates the use of extensive ensemble sampling with multiple random seeds to explore a broader range of conformations.

Conclusion

AF3 is generally able to reproduce the scale of conformational change required for cryptic site formation upon ligand binding. That is, over our test set, when given a cryptic site ligand for the protein, AF3 generally predominantly predicts conformations competent to bind the ligand in the cryptic site. Conversely, when AF3 predicts the same protein structures without a ligand, the closed conformation generally dominates.

More often than not, AF3 tends to reproduce the dominant conformations observed in the PDB for the bound distributions with the ligand and for the PDB unbound distributions without the ligand, even when there is a significant imbalance between the number of bound and unbound structures in the PBD (e.g., TEM, Ricin, ALDBP, GluR2, and K-Ras). Thus, while our results overall may reflect a bias toward memorized structural priors rather than flexible sampling of alternative conformations, the level of detrimental memorization appears to be reduced relative to what has previously been reported for AF222.

The results clearly show that the same MSAs can be used to predict different conformations depending on the presence of a ligand. Somewhat surprisingly, the choice of the ligand given to AF3 can also significantly impact the predictions. We saw this with GluR2, for example, where when a ligand known to open the cryptic site was used, the open conformation was predominantly predicted, while if glutamate (which binds at the same site but with the pocket closed) is used, the site is correctly predicted as closed. We have also seen that AF3 has no ability to discriminate when two ligands are directly overlapping (as in the case of TEM with multiple Xe ions predicted to bind in essentially the exact same site); AF3 is unaware that that is physically impossible. In the case of AR, when AF3 is given the active site ligand and the cryptic site ligand simultaneously, it rarely places both ligands in the correct pocket.

For all but two of the proteins, in our test set, we showed that AF3 was able to produce at least a few models with the ligand correctly position; the exceptions were AR and AmpC where AF3 incorrectly place the cryptic site ligand in the active site. Furthermore, we have also shown that ligand pLDDT scores over the AF3 structural models in the ensemble correlate somewhat with the ligand pose RMSD (with R2 = 0.35). That is, models with lower ligand RMSDs tend to have higher ligand pLDDTs. This suggests that ligand pLDDT can serve as a useful first-pass filter for prioritizing AF3 predictions. However, this relationship is not absolute. In particular, RNase A and TEM illustrate false-negative cases in which correct ligand poses are predicted with relatively low ligand pLDDT scores. In these systems, ligand pLDDT values are broadly distributed across the ensemble, suggesting that reliance on a single top-ranked model may be insufficient and that broader ensemble inspection is required. In contrast, cases where ligand pLDDT values are tightly clustered tend to yield more reliable top-ranked predictions.

Three systems—MAPK, PDK, and Hsp90—illustrate that high ligand pLDDT does not universally guarantee physical plausibility. For MAPK and PDK, steric clashes are observed in a small subset of models (<10% and <1%, respectively), while most high-confidence predictions are physically reasonable. In contrast, Hsp90 represents a more severe failure mode, in which the majority of models (~80%) exhibit steric clashes despite uniformly high ligand pLDDT scores. This distinction suggests that isolated clashes within an ensemble (as observed for MAPK and PDK) may reflect local side chain packing errors that could plausibly be resolved by light relaxation or minimization, whereas systematic clashes across the ensemble (as in Hsp90) indicate a deeper mismatch between the predicted pose and physical constraints, such as an incorrect loop/helix formation. This mismatch between apparent confidence and physical plausibility mirrors recent findings that co-folding models can produce high-confidence predictions that violate basic physical expectations, highlighting the need for independent plausibility checks (such as clash/strain filters or physics-based refinement) when interpreting ligand poses31. In practice, this indicates that ligand pLDDT should be complemented with independent plausibility checks, such as steric clash detection or light side-chain minimization, before downstream use in drug discovery. Taken together, these observations suggest that AF3 predictions are most effectively interpreted at the ensemble level: generating multiple seeds increases the likelihood of identifying a physically reasonable binding mode, while confidence metrics such as ligand pLDDT are best used as heuristic filters rather than definitive indicators of correctness.

Despite the limitations, using AF3 with extensive sampling to generate structural ensembles can reveal cryptic pockets that may be useful for drug discovery. The challenge will be in identifying those conformations with actionable cryptic pockets that are revealed among the many conformations generated.

Methods

Test set of proteins with structures released prior to the AF3 training date

The initial test set utilized for this study was the same as in ref. 22. All proteins in the test set (see Table 1) originate from the CryptoSite database4, except for K-Ras. In assembling CryptoSite, Sali and colleagues compared ligand-bound structures with the corresponding unbound structures from the PDB. They applied pocket-detection algorithms and retained only those pairs in which the pocket was minimal or absent in the unbound state but underwent a substantial conformational change—either expansion or closure—to accommodate the ligand upon binding. For the 15 CryptoSite proteins, these criteria defined the unbound and bound reference conformations and the moving segment. The moving segment of the protein undergoes a conformational change enabling cryptic site formation. We defined it for other cases, as the protein segment (such as a loop, a side chain, or a helix) that is in a different conformation in the bound and unbound reference structures and would sterically clash with the reference ligand in the unbound state but not in the bound state of the protein. Typically, the ligand-bound structure also represents the open state, as binding exposes or enlarges the pocket. For β-secretase—the binding region is too open or shallow in the unbound conformation, and ligand binding instead induces a closing of the site. In these cases, the bound state corresponds to the closed rather than the open reference. For K-Ras, the pocket and the relevant PDB entries were taken from a study by Fesik and co-workers32, which showed that the site becomes accessible when a small molecule binds. Overall, the bound and unbound structures provide complementary reference states, with the conformational transitions detailed in Table 1. Although these changes may involve multiple loops or even entire domains, in each case, a single loop movement or a side-chain rearrangement was sufficient to capture the main structural difference between the two states.

Test set of proteins with structures released after the AF3 training date

A benchmark of cryptic binding site structures identified after the AF3 training cutoff was constructed. The CryptoBench dataset28, which is comprised of curated apo-holo structure pairs involving cryptic pocket formation, was first filtered to remove structures released prior to the AF3 training cutoff date (September 30 2021). Next, any remaining proteins with ≥90% sequence identity to proteins released to the PDB prior to this cutoff that had a ligand-bound or displayed an open state at the cryptic site location were removed. Four of the remaining proteins were not analyzed because they were similar to BACE in that the cryptic pocket presents as a shallow surface that closes to form a pocket only upon ligand binding.

Protein Data Bank distributions

For each of the test cases, we assembled a set of experimental structures and categorized them into bound and unbound states. Experimental structures were retrieved from the PDB using the RCSB API by searching for entries with ≥90% sequence identity to the AF reference PDB ID (Table 1)33,34. Binding was determined through geometric criteria. A structure was considered bound if the distance between the ligand’s geometric center in the reference structure (ref_center) and the corresponding center in the test structure (test_center) was less than 5.0 Å, indicating that the ligand occupied a similar spatial position. Alternatively, binding was also assigned if any atom in the reference ligand was within 2.0 Å of any atom in the test ligand, a stricter overlap threshold designed to capture close atomic contacts. In cases where the cryptic site was adjacent to the active site, an additional criterion was applied: a structure was considered bound only if the ligand clashed with residues in the moving segment of the unbound structure. Clashes were calculated using a KDTree-based algorithm: for each atom, potential interacting atoms were identified and classified as clashing if their interatomic distance to the original atom was less than 0.63 of the sum of their van der Waals radii, ensuring that only substantial steric overlaps indicative of physically implausible geometries were counted as clashes.

AF3 predictions

AF3 code was obtained from the official AlphaFold3 repository (https://github.com/google-deepmind/alphafold3), and model weights were downloaded from Google DeepMind with their permission. Structure predictions were performed by first running the MSA search with MMseqs35,36, followed by converting the resulting alignments into JSON format using the alphafold3_tools repository (https://github.com/cddlab/alphafold3_tools). No structural templates were used, and predictions were generated with 100 random seeds, which resulted in a total of 500 models. For each of the test cases, the predictions were performed with and without a ligand present. For the ligand-bound predictions, the SMILES corresponding to the ligand listed for each case in Table 1 and 3 was used unless otherwise stated.

Analysis of conformations

For each of the test cases, the two sets of structures (with and without ligand) were aligned to both the reference closed and open states. The RMSD of the moving residues, as defined in Table 1 and 3, was then calculated. For each structure in the PDB distribution and for each model in the AF3 ensemble, the RMSD of the moving segment from both the open and the closed reference structures were determined. The moving segment was excluded during the alignment to the reference structures, and the RMSD was only calculated for the moving residues. For proteins with moving loop segments, only alpha carbons are considered in the RMSD calculations, while for systems with individual side chains identified as moving segments, all-heavy-atom RMSD is calculated. In Figures 1–3 and Supplementary Fig. 1, the structures are represented in a 2D local coordinate system that shows the RMSD of the moving segment from the closed reference structure on the X axis and the RMSD of the moving segment from the open reference structure on the Y axis.

To classify each structure as open, closed, or “other”, we first computed the RMSD between the open and closed reference structures and used half of this value as the cutoff. Structures with an RMSD to the open reference below this threshold were assigned to the open state, while those within the threshold of the closed reference were assigned to the closed state. Structures that did not meet either condition were classified as “other” state. “Other” state is present in the PDB throughout the dataset and represents experimentally observed alternative conformations, for example, partially open or closed conformations, alternative helical geometries with kinking, or different side chain rotamers. In the AF3 ensembles, the presence of “other” states likely reflects this underlying structural heterogeneity, although a portion of these “other” conformations could arise from stochastic diffusion noise.

In addition, for cases where the classification of the PDB structures did not result in open conformations dominant for bound and closed dominant for unbound as expected (K-Ras and Ricin), we added the criterion that any structure or model for which moving segment residues would clash with the reference ligand (in the aligned reference structure) would be classified as closed.

Convergence analysis

To evaluate the impact of stochastic sampling on AF3 predictions and to determine whether ensemble-level statistics were sensitive to the sampling, ensemble convergence was assessed by analyzing how the ensemble observables depend on the number of random seeds used. Subsets of models were incrementally and randomly sampled from the pool of 100 seeds, keeping the models from the same seed together, and the resulting distributions of conformational states and ligand poses were quantified. Statistical uncertainty associated with finite sampling was estimated using bootstrapping with replacement. For each subsample size, this procedure was repeated 100 times to assess variability in the estimated distributions.

Statistics and reproducibility

No inferential statistical tests were performed in this study. All analyses are descriptive, based on geometric and structural metrics (RMSD, clash detection, and ligand pose assessment) applied to computational predictions and experimentally determined structures retrieved from the PDB. AF3 predictions were generated using 100 random seeds, yielding 500 models per protein per condition (with and without ligand). These models represent stochastic samples from the AF3 prediction pipeline and are not biological or experimental replicates. Statistical uncertainty in convergence analyses (Supplementary Fig. 6–8) was estimated using bootstrapping with replacement, with 100 bootstrap resamples per subsample size, applied independently to each of the proteins in the test set. Shaded regions in Supplementary Fig. 8 represent ±1 standard deviation (SD) across these resamples. PDB structure sets were assembled per protein using a ≥90% sequence identity threshold and classified into bound and unbound states using the criteria described above. The number of structures per protein varied and is reflected directly in the counts shown in the figures.
