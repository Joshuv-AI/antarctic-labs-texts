Time-of-day modulation in mosquito response persistence to carbon dioxide is controlled by Pigment-Dispersing Factor
Authors: Linhan Dong, Richard Hormigo, Jord M. Barnett, Chloe Greppi, Laura B. Duvall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMosquitoes use rhythmic sensory responses to find human hosts, but the role of circadian rhythms in this behavior is not well understood. Using an automated assay, we show that Aedes aegypti mosquitoes’ responses to CO2, a critical host cue, vary with time of day. While activation is stable during daylight, persistence peaks at dawn and dusk, matching natural biting times. At night, activation is suppressed, suggesting internal circadian gating of host-seeking. We identify the neuropeptide Pigment-Dispersing Factor (PDF) as crucial in regulating this rhythm. Loss of PDF disrupts blood feeding success in the morning and impairs the mosquito circadian clock. These findings offer insights for vector control strategies that target natural periods of reduced biting to fight mosquito-borne diseases.

Female mosquitoes bite humans and consume blood to obtain nutriments for egg development. While blood-feeding, they can transmit pathogens that cause diseases including malaria, dengue, Zika, and West Nile fever, which infect hundreds of millions of people each year (1–4). Previous research has identified robust sensory strategies that enable efficient host-seeking behavior using multisensory cues (5–15). One of the most important host cues is carbon dioxide (CO2) from human breath, which can be detected by mosquitoes from over 10 m away (16). After CO2 detection, mosquitoes must navigate long distances through turbulent air and competing background signals to locate a host (17). CO2 not only activates mosquito flight but also primes them to integrate additional human host-associated signals that are essential for blood-feeding including visual contrast, skin odor, and body temperature (5–15, 18). Recent studies have shown that CO2 sensation induces prolonged flight activity, and synergizes with other sensory cues which allows mosquitoes to navigate the complex sensory landscape to increase their chances of landing on a host and successfully blood-feeding (5, 6). Optogenetic activation of CO2-sensing neurons elicits a persistent predatory-like state, although it remains to be demonstrated whether CO2 prestimulation alone can evoke this state (15).

Mosquitoes exhibit highly rhythmic biting behavior in the field, with species-specific peak biting times that maximize success while minimizing exposure to unfavorable environmental conditions and predators in their ecological niche (19–23). These daily rhythms play critical roles in disease transmission dynamics and understanding the temporal patterns of mosquito–host interactions is important for effective application of vector control approaches to target mosquitoes during periods of active biting (24–26). The rhythmic nature of biting activity suggests an underlying mechanism to gate behavioral responses to host cues outside of their peak biting times. Here, we investigate how Aedes aegypti mosquitoes regulate their response to the host cue CO2 throughout the day and report that although acute CO2 responsiveness is observed throughout daytime, response persistence is highly modulated and qualitatively mirror spontaneous locomotor activity profiles, suggesting central circadian control. Previous work on many insect species has revealed conserved genetic and neuronal mechanisms that modulate temporal activity profiles. Pigment-Dispersing Factor (PDF), a circadian neuropeptide coordinating central circadian clock neurons, has been implicated in temporal shifts in daily activity patterns (27, 28). We further examine the role of the circadian neuropeptide gene pdf in this rhythmic regulation.

Results

CO2-Evoked Activity and Response Persistence Are Modulated By Time of Day.

Field landing catch studies report that Aedes aegypti biting activity peaks at dawn and dusk (19, 23, 29). To confirm this general activity pattern, we used video-tracking (30) to quantify spontaneous locomotor activity of individual mosquitoes in the absence of host cues under 12:12 light/dark (LD) conditions with 30-min gradual dawn and dusk periods (Fig. 1A). Consistent with previous reports (30–33), we observed a bimodal activity pattern with morning and evening peaks separated by a midday “siesta” period of low activity (Fig. 1B). To assess daily changes in responses to host-associated cues, we developed a custom-made CO2 response assay (“MozzieDrome”), which combines timed lighting and videography with automated stimulus delivery (Fig. 1C). To induce reproducible changes in CO2 level, we delivered 30-s pulses of 10% CO2 throughout the day via a diffuser device that does not directly contact the mosquito arena. Combined with the built-in ventilation system, the delivery of this source CO2 can reliably raise the background CO2 level in the mosquito arena by ~1,800 ppm (Fig. 1D). Since CO2 is known to trigger sustained behavioral activation (9), we recorded mosquito activity from 2 min before stimulus onset to 10 min afterward to capture the persistence of this sensory response and track the movement of 9 to 10 individual females over days.

Aedes aegypti females are responsive to CO2 throughout the photophase and show clear increases in velocity, but responses to pulses delivered during the night are minimal (Fig. 1 E–G). These findings suggest that CO2 responses are reduced in the absence of light, in line with previous observations that nighttime biting activity in Aedes aegypti is enhanced by artificial light (34). A detailed analysis of the daytime response profiles of wild-type females reveals a rapid increase in activity within the first 2 min of CO2 exposure (Fig. 1F). To further investigate how CO2-evoked activity differs across the day without influence from baseline non-CO2-evoked activity, we excluded animals that were spontaneously active before CO2 exposure from subsequent metrics (SI Appendix). We quantified acute response by measuring activity 0–2 min post-CO2 (Fig. 1H), and among responding mosquitoes, acute response remains high across daytime, indicating that mosquitoes acutely respond to host-associated cues even at midday, when spontaneous locomotor activity is low. Small time-of-day differences in acute response were detected, with lower amplitudes at ZT3, as confirmed by independent replicates (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Following the acute peak, activity remains elevated above baseline levels, consistent with previously reported persistence of CO2-evoked host-seeking behavior. However, the duration of this persistent response varies across the day: A single CO2 pulse elicits >10 min of sustained activity at dawn (ZT0) and dusk (ZT12) but <6 min at midday (ZT6) (Fig. 1F). To quantify persistence, we measured velocity in minutes 6 to 10 as sustained CO2 response amplitude. Sustained flight velocity is significantly higher at dawn (ZT0) and dusk (ZT12) (Fig. 1I). To confirm that our temporal cut-offs do not influence these trends, we tested multiple time intervals (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) and consistently observed relatively stable acute response amplitudes and a U-shaped modulation in sustained response amplitude throughout the daytime.

We further established quantitative parameters to assess the persistence of the CO2 response. To measure overall response duration, we calculated the median time of activity during the 10-min trial for animals showing an acute response, which peaked at ZT0 and ZT12 (Fig. 1J). Using behavioral classification, we analyzed individual bouts of activity as either “walk” or “flight” based on displacement and velocity (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). This bout-level analysis revealed time-of-day differences: Bout counts were highest at ZT0 and ZT12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C) and bout duration was longest at ZT12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). It is important to note that because the MozzieDrome uses an enclosed arena, which may limit naturalistic movement, the measured bout lengths may not quantitatively reflect host-seeking behavior in natural settings. Overall, response persistence was significantly lower at ZT6 across multiple metrics including sustained velocity, median activity time, and bout count (Fig. 1 I and J and SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that wild-type Aedes aegypti females exhibit time-dependent response profiles to CO2. While responses are minimal at night, females respond rapidly to CO2 during the day, with response persistence following a “U-shaped pattern. The most sustained responses occur at dawn and dusk, coinciding with peaks in spontaneous locomotor activity and field-reported biting behavior (19, 23, 29–33).

Targeting a Neuropeptide Gene to Disrupt the Mosquito Central Circadian System.

Although daily rhythms in behavior and physiology can entrain to environmental cues like dawn and dusk, they are often sustained in the absence of environmental cues by the endogenous circadian clock (35). The insect circadian system consists of a central clock in the brain and peripheral clocks that function either dependently or independently of the central clock (36–38). Both central and peripheral oscillators rely on autoregulatory transcription and translation feedback loops (TTFLs) driven by highly conserved clock gene components (38–41). Recent studies have shown that sustained exposure to constant light, whole-body knock-outs, or RNAi-mediated knockdown of core components of the mosquito TTFL disrupt daily behavioral patterns and circadian rhythmicity (26, 42–44).

Although the hallmark of circadian control is the ability to maintain rhythmicity under constant conditions, circadian regulation is also evident under light/dark conditions in the modulation of behavioral intensity within the light/dark phases, often reflected in a biphasic peak of morning and evening activity and anticipatory behaviors (45–48). Our findings show that while acute CO2 responses exhibit little variation throughout the light phase of the day, sustained responses are highly modulated. This suggests that acute sensitivity to CO2 and response persistence are regulated through distinct mechanisms. Specifically, response persistence, which mirrors daytime trends in spontaneous locomotor activity, may be under control of the central circadian clock. In Drosophila, olfactory sensitivity rhythms are regulated by antennal oscillators (36), while the central circadian clock controls spontaneous activity, sleep, and arousal (27, 49–53). Based on these parallels, we reasoned that disrupting the central circadian clock may impair time-of-day modulation of CO2 response persistence.

In insects, the central circadian clock is composed of dozens to hundreds of individual clock neurons, organized into anatomically and functionally distinct subgroups that coordinate to generate coherent organismal rhythms (54–57). Intercellular coordination is mediated by PDF, a neuropeptide produced in certain clock cell subgroups that acts as a critical synchronizing agent for molecular, cellular, and behavioral rhythms (27, 28, 58, 59). In Aedes aegypti, PDF is produced by small and large ventral cells that coexpress the core clock protein PERIOD (26). To determine the role of PDF in controlling daily behavioral timing, we used a genetic approach to disrupt the pdf locus and provide genetic access to label cells that normally express the pdf gene using the Q-binary system (60, 61). Using CRISPR-Cas9, we created a “knock-in/knock-out” line by inserting a cassette containing the QF2 transcriptional activator and an eye-specific fluorescent reporter directly into the coding sequence of the pdf gene (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). This insertion results in an early truncation of the pdf locus, eliminating the mature peptide and completely disrupting PDF expression, as confirmed by the absence of PDF immunostaining (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). The integration of QF2 also enables pdf-specific cell labeling when crossed with a QUAS-driven fluorescent reporter (62) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E).

PDF Is Required for Spontaneous and CO2-Evoked Activity Profiles.

Loss of PDF has varying degrees of impact on daily locomotor activity timing across multiple insect species (27, 28). To ask how loss of PDF affects activity in mosquitoes, we recorded spontaneous locomotor activity under LD conditions. Heterozygous pdf mutants showed an activity profile similar to that of wild-type mosquitoes (Fig. 2A). However, in homozygous pdf mutants, we observed a loss of morning activity (Fig. 2 A and B). The remaining morning activity in pdf homozygotes occurs with a delayed phase compared to the control groups (Fig. 2C). The level of evening activity is maintained in pdf mutants and the onset of evening activity does not shift, in contrast to the advanced evening peak observed in Drosophila pdf mutants (27). However, pdf mutants displayed early termination of activity after dusk, becoming inactive within 3.23 ± 0.50 min of lights-off, compared to wild-type and heterozygous animals (20.60 ± 2.37 min and 13.25 ± 1.04 min, respectively) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). These findings indicate that pdf is a critical regulator of daily spontaneous locomotor activity and that PDF normally promotes morning locomotor activity, advances the morning peak, and sustains activity after lights-off.

Given the nuanced daily profiles of CO2 responses, including both acute reactions and response persistence, we next investigated how the loss of PDF affects CO2 responsiveness throughout the day. Specifically, we examined whether the loss of PDF causes a similar “flattened” profile in the morning as observed in spontaneous locomotor activity. As in wild-type animals, the overall amplitude of acute CO2 responses does not significantly differ across the photophase in pdf mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B; see also SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). To assess response persistence, we focused on the difference between dawn (ZT0) and midday (ZT6), as this contrast is particularly pronounced in wild-type females and pdf dramatically affects morning baseline locomotor activity amplitude and timing. Although sustained responses remain high at ZT12, pdf mutants show diminished modulation between ZT0 and ZT6 in terms of median time of activity, sustained activity in minutes 6 to 10 of the trial, and bout count (Fig. 2 D–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C–E). Surprisingly, while baseline locomotor activity is largely preserved in heterozygous pdf mutants, the CO2 response persistence profile is flattened between ZT0 and ZT6 in sustained CO2 responsiveness (Fig. 2 D–F; see also SI Appendix, Fig. S8) and bout count (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D). We also observed time-of-day specific defects in sustained CO2 response at ZT0 and more, but shorter, bouts of activity at ZT12 in animals with disrupted PDF (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C–F). However, median time of activity, which reflects overall response duration, remained significantly different between ZT0 and ZT6 in heterozygous mutants (Fig. 2 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D). Overall, the U-shaped modulation in daytime CO2 response persistence is disrupted by genetic mutation of pdf, which specifically flattens the downregulation between dawn and midday. This reduction in morning persistence is also reflected in the percentage of mosquitoes participating in both acute and sustained CO2 responses (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 F and G).

Loss of PDF Leads to Reduced Feeding Rate in the Morning and Midday.

Using automated activity monitoring and the MozzieDrome assay, we revealed changes in temporal modulation of behavior in pdf mutants in locomotor activity and CO2 responses. To determine whether changes in daily behavioral timing are also reflected in the biting pattern when multisensory cues are present, we assessed blood-feeding in a short-range assay using a standardized combination of host-associated cues. Female mosquitoes were placed in an environmentally controlled cabinet and exposed to a 30-s pulse of 10% CO2, which was allowed to dissipate for 1.5 min to increase levels of CO2 by ~1,000 ppm and the cabinet door was then opened to allow CO2 levels to return to baseline before offering sheep blood in an artificial feeder baited with human host odor and heated to 37 °C. Females were allowed 10 min to feed, scored for the presence of fresh blood in their abdomen, and the percentage of females that blood-fed was quantified (Fig. 3A). Wild-type females reliably blood-fed at dawn (ZT0–1), midday (ZT5.5 to 6.5), and dusk (ZT11–12) with highest levels of feeding at dusk (ZT11–12) (Fig. 3B). Notably, blood-feeding rate does not show a midday decrease in wild-type females (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), suggesting that when subjected to a combination of CO2 activation, human odor, and heat at close range, feeding does not follow the same midday downregulation observed in locomotor activity and sustained CO2 response. Since our blood-feeding assay requires experimenter handling, we cannot rule out the possibility that human presence influences the mosquitoes’ activation state. This may contribute to the relatively high midday biting rates observed here and in other laboratory studies (44), which contrast with the lower midday biting reported in field studies (23, 29). Heterozygous pdf mutants showed similar feeding rates as wild-type females across the day (Fig. 3B). Homozygous pdf mutant females showed no significant differences in evening feeding compared to other genotypes, though their feeding rates at ZT11–12 displayed a wider range. However, this variance was not statistically different from wild-type animals at ZT11–12 (P = 0.1055, F-test) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F). Strikingly, blood-feeding was significantly reduced in the morning and midday in homozygous pdf mutants (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that the loss of PDF impairs feeding rate at specific times of day, even when multiple host cues are present.

PDF Regulates Circadian Rhythmicity.

So far, our results indicate that the pdf gene regulates daily behavioral timing in mosquitoes. To further understand the impact of loss of PDF on the autonomous function of the mosquito clock, we recorded locomotor activity under constant dark conditions (DD) without external zeitgeber inputs. Wild-type Aedes aegypti females maintained rhythmic activity under constant dark (DD) conditions for more than 7 d with a period of 22.61 ± 0.08 h (Fig. 4 A–C). In contrast, both heterozygous and homozygous pdf mutants showed a higher percentage of arrhythmic animals under DD conditions (Fig. 4B). Free-running period in the rhythmic subset of the population was not significantly different between genotypes (Fig. 4C). Overall, the heterozygous animals showed higher levels of phase synchrony during the first days in DD compared to the homozygous mutants, suggesting an intermediate phenotype (Fig. 4 D and E). These findings indicate that the mosquito central clock fails to maintain proper circadian timing in spontaneous activity without PDF, and rhythmic defects associated with the loss of a single copy of the pdf gene become apparent under constant dark conditions, in the absence of environmental cues to entrain rhythms.

Genetic Disruption of pdf Alters Apatial Distribution of PDF Immunoreactivity.

Our results demonstrate that PDF plays a critical role in daily behavioral profiles and circadian rhythmicity. To investigate the neuronal mechanisms involved in PDF regulation of behavior, we characterized PDF-expressing neurons using immunohistochemistry. In insects, PDF is normally expressed in ventral clusters of clock neurons that appear during larval development (63, 64). After assessing PDF immunoreactivity across developmental stages, we identified three groups of PDF+ neurons with anatomically and developmentally unique characteristics (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). These include the small ventrolateral neurons (sLNv), which appear before the fourth larval (L4) stage, sending projections to the medial brain and the developing optic lobe and later branching to the dorsal brain in late L4 stage. This is different from the developmental pattern observed in Drosophila, where the small LNv cells and their dorsal projections develop early and persist, largely unchanged, into adulthood (63). Around the ventrolateral region, two groups of larger cells (lLNv-d and lLNv-v) emerge during late L4 stage densely arborizing the dorsal and ventral halves of the developing medulla (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–E). The three PDF+ cell groups and their projections persist into adulthood and we observe 8 to 10 cells in the sLNv group and 4 to 5 cells in each of the two lLNv groups, consistent with previous studies (26). We also noticed differences in the size of PDF immunoreactivity in the soma region across all three groups (Fig. 5 A and B) and differences in PDF staining intensity between small and ventral large cell populations in wild-type animals (Fig. 5C).

Since homozygous pdf mutants completely lack PDF expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), we compared PDF staining between wild-type and heterozygous animals at ZT2. While overall levels of PDF staining intensity did not differ significantly between these genotypes (Fig. 5C), we observed notable differences in the spatial patterns of PDF immunoreactivity. Heterozygous females showed smaller area of PDF immunoreactivity in the soma region and further lose staining intensity differences between clock cell subgroups and show abnormalities in the staining pattern of dorsal projections from the sLNv cells (Fig. 5 B–F). Normally, these sLNv projections are highly stereotyped and likely form critical connections between the PDF+ cells in the ventral brain and PDF- clock cells in the dorsal brain (26, 28, 59, 65–67). However, in pdf heterozygotes, the PDF immunostaining signal in these projections is spatially diffuse and signal is not observed in normal regions of innervation in the dorsal brain (Fig. 5 D–F). This phenotype is also present during larval development (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) and is similar to the defasciculation of the dorsal projection bundle observed in Drosophila cycle mutants, which also show reduced PDF expression (68). Overall, pdf heterozygous females show loss of size differences in PDF immunoreactivity in the cell body region and altered spatial distribution of PDF immunostaining.

PDF Is Required for Normal period Transcript Oscillation.

Previous work in Drosophila has demonstrated that PDF synchronizes oscillations in core clock transcripts and proteins in multiple clock cell subgroups (55, 56). Apart from the PDF+ ventrolateral neurons, we consistently observed period transcript in an anterior subset of dorsal cells (DNas, Fig. 5A) that has previously been reported to show clock protein expression (26). To ask whether loss of PDF changes temporal expression of core clock transcript levels, we used in situ hybridization to quantify circadian changes in period transcript in PDF+ ventrolateral neurons (LNvs) and PDF- anterior dorsal neurons (DNas) (Fig. 5G). In wild-type animals, period transcript levels are significantly higher at CT 14 compared to CT 2 in both groups of clock cells, similar to previous reports of transcript oscillations (40, 69) (Fig. 5H; see also SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Changes in period transcript levels are preserved in heterozygous animals, although amplitude is reduced and these changes are lost in homozygous pdf mutants (Fig. 5H). Interestingly, we also observed that period signal intensity often showed dramatic variation across hemispheres of the same brain in homozygous pdf mutants at CT 14 (Fig. 5G), suggesting phase disagreement. These data demonstrate that disrupting pdf interferes with the temporal regulation of period transcript levels under constant conditions across multiple clock cell subgroups.

CO2 Responsiveness Is Under Circadian Control, but Is PDF-Independent.

Although we identified a rhythm in mosquito response to CO2, it remains unclear whether this behavior is under true circadian control in constant darkness (DD), as host cue-elicited responses have not been previously profiled under such conditions. Our automated behavioral system provides an opportunity to study CO2 response under DD with minimal human interference. We subjected groups of 9 to 10 mosquitoes to two LD cycles followed by two DD cycles and analyzed CO2 response in the second day of DD. Wild-type Aedes aegypti females showed circadian variation in CO2-evoked velocity increases (Fig. 6A). Peak velocity increase occurs at CT9 by the second day in DD conditions, coincident with the evening locomotor activity peak in DD2, likely due to the relatively short free-running period (Fig. 4). Under DD conditions, wild-type females responsive to CO2 show reduced persistence compared to those in LD (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A), potentially due to the masking effect of light (see also SI Appendix, Fig. S14). As a result, we cannot quantify sustained CO2 response using our previously established temporal cut-offs. Wild-type females did not show significant circadian variation in median activity time or response bout number, (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 B and C). However, average bout length (SI Appendix, Fig. S13D) does show circadian fluctuation similar to the overall CO2 response trend (Fig. 6A). Notably, while the overall CO2 response profile remains intact in pdf mutants and still peaks at CT9 (Fig. 6B) bout length modulation is lost in these mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S13D). Direct statistical comparisons between genotypes further demonstrates that circadian CO2 response is not globally reduced in pdf mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). This suggests that aspects of CO2 response are under endogenous circadian control, but the maintenance of overall responsiveness does not require PDF, supporting the possibility that an independent peripheral oscillator regulates olfactory sensitivity to CO2.

Discussion

Mosquito host-seeking assays typically require carefully evaluated strategies and extensive cleaning procedures between trials to limit unwanted exposure to host cues (70). However, studying circadian behavior demands minimal handling to prevent unintentional exposure to zeitgebers that could alter the mosquito’s baseline activity state. Automated behavioral tracking systems like LAM or Flybox have been used to study spontaneous locomotor rhythms and provide valuable information on mosquito activity patterns, but are not designed for stimulus delivery (30, 32, 44). In this study, we developed a behavioral tracking platform that enabled us to examine mosquito responses to CO2 across the day. Previous studies have shown that CO2 is a potent activator of host-seeking and a single exposure to CO2 drives sustained flight activity and enhances detection and responses to multimodal host cues (6, 9, 10, 13, 15). Here, we report that not only is CO2 activation absent at night, but the persistence of the CO2 response also varies throughout the day. These time-of-day-dependent features in both acute and sustained CO2 responses cannot be fully explained by the timing of spontaneous activity. For example, while locomotor activity is low at both midday and night, acute CO2 response amplitude remains intact at midday but CO2 responsiveness is almost entirely diminished at night. This finding highlights how timing affects fundamental properties of CO2 responsiveness and underscores the importance of considering time-of-day context when studying sensory processes and behavioral responses. The absence of a CO2-evoked response at night suggests that light may play an important role in successful host-seeking behavior in mosquitoes. Future studies that introduce brief periods of darkness during the day could help determine whether this light-dependent gating can override the general daily temporal control of CO2 responsiveness. Our study uncovers daily variation of CO2 response persistence, but the correlation between this persistent behavioral state and downstream sensory integrations that induce blood-feeding remains unclear. In our blood-feeding assay, we used a combination of CO2 pre-exposure, and concurrent heat and human skin odorant cues to mimic ecologically relevant scenarios. However, whether the prolonged locomotor activity we observe after a brief period of prestimulation with CO2 facilitates attraction to unitary cues such as warmth or human odorants (akin to an apparent “predatory-like” state), or whether such increased locomotor activity as a result of prolonged mosquito response persistence to CO2 simply brings mosquitoes into the range of these other attractant cues to evoke blood-feeding remains to be characterized.

Although PDF has been implicated in the control of daily locomotor activity in many insects, its effects on behavior vary by species (27, 28, 59). Our data suggest that in Aedes aegypti, PDF promotes morning activity and maintains a U-shaped pattern in both locomotor activity and CO2 response persistence in the photophase. In homozygous pdf mutants, both spontaneous locomotor activity and biting behavior are reduced in the morning. This highlights the potential of targeting the mosquito clock for behavioral control of pathogen-vectoring mosquitoes by disrupting biting efficiency. The rhythmic defects associated with PDF loss are most apparent in locomotor activity recordings under constant dark conditions and we observed behavioral phenotypes in heterozygous animals, which express one copy of the QF2 transgene and have one intact copy of the pdf gene. While overall levels of PDF immunostaining remain unchanged in these animals, we observed anatomical differences in PDF immunostaining patterns in the dorsal projections of the small LNv cells, which are thought to be critical for communication between ventral populations of PDF+ clock cells and PDF- dorsal clock cells. Our findings suggest that, while these differences in heterozygotes do not cause locomotor activity defects when environmental timing cues are present, deficits are revealed under constant conditions and, although modulation of CO2 response is moderately affected, blood-feeding patterns are normal. Previous studies in Drosophila have associated changes in PDF levels with morphological abnormalities and behavioral deficits (71), and the mechanisms by which changes in pdf dosage may contribute to mistargeted projections are of interest for future study and require the development of membrane-associated markers that are not affected by pdf genotype. Notably, the expression of QF2 in our genetic construct may also contribute to abnormalities. We also showed that temporal regulation of period transcript is disrupted in pdf mutants and it will be an important area for ongoing work to determine whether and how protein components of the clock are affected in these mutants.

Last, we report that while overall CO2 response is under circadian control, it does not depend on PDF. This supports the hypothesis that timing of CO2 responsiveness is controlled by peripheral clocks in the maxillary palps that function independently of PDF, similar to the antennal control of olfactory responses in Drosophila (36). We hypothesize that maximal host-seeking efficacy requires the coordination of sustained locomotor activity, chemosensory sensitivity, and blood-feeding drive. Daily regulation of sensitivity to internal and external cues may coordinate periods of maximal peripheral sensitivity with internal motivation state to optimize temporal integration of host-associated cues (72). Understanding the relationship between sensory coding and locomotor activity to drive appropriate behaviors at specific times of day is a critical area for future research. Our findings suggest that PDF may act on downstream central brain cells that control overall response persistence (73). One area of ongoing work will be to determine whether this behavioral persistence is unique to host-seeking or generalizes to other behaviors. Understanding the mechanisms of rhythms in behavioral persistence can provide new targets to disrupt host-seeking effectiveness and potentially lock females into a “low host-seeking” state. In addition, understanding the behavioral features that predict biting can facilitate effective application of current mosquito control technologies by ensuring that they are deployed at the most effective times to protect people from disease.

Materials and Methods

Mosquito Rearing.

Wild-type Aedes aegypti Liverpool, pdf-QF2 (this study), and QUAS-mCD8-GFP (62) animals were maintained and reared at 28 °C, 70 to 80% relative humidity with a 12 h light: 12 h dark schedule, unless otherwise stated. Eggs were hatched using 1 L of TetraMin fish food suspension in deionized deoxygenated water (one tablet/L). Larvae were fed with TetraMin fish food tablets when needed. Adult female mosquitoes used for experiments were between 3 and 21 d old and cohoused with males, with ad lib access to 10% sucrose solution. For passaging and mutant generation, adult female mosquitoes were blood-fed on defibrinated sheep blood (Quad5) with 1 mM ATP at 37 °C using Hemotek artificial membrane feeders.

Generation and Maintenance of the pdf-QF2 Knock-In Knock-Out Line.

Single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed using CRISPR RGEN Tools (http://www.rgenome.net) to target the Aedes aegypti pigment-dispersing factor gene (AAEL001754) using the AaegL5 target genome. Efficiency of the sgRNAs were evaluated by injecting sgRNA and Cas9 protein into 200 Aedes aegypti Liverpool embryos. The synthesis of the sgRNA and the preparation of an injection mix was described in a previous publication (61).Guide RNA with the most efficient cutting rate (GAAGATGATAGAAGCACACACGG, PAM sequence underlined) as reflected in frequency of indel mutations was used to design a plasmid for homology-directed repair (HDR). The genetic insert contains the left homology arm (sequence 300 bp before the cut site) and followed by the ribosomal skipping factor T2A, transcriptional activator QF2, eye-specific promotor 3xP3, fluorescent marker dsRed, and the right homology arm (sequence 300 bp directly following the cut site). This 3,169 bp genetic insert was synthesized and cloned into a pUC57 plasmid (GenScript). Plasmid sequence can be found in Dataset S1. This donor plasmid (700 ng/μL at final concentration) was mixed with guide RNA (40 ng/μL at final concentration) and purified by ethanol precipitation before mixing with Cas9 protein (300 ng/μL at final concentration) and injecting into ~1,000 Aedes aegypti Liverpool embryos. All embryo injections were performed by the Insect Transformation Facility at the University of Maryland Institute for Biosciences & Biotechnology Research. Injected animals were outcrossed to wild-type virgin male/females in families of <25 injected animals to screen for germline transmission of the red eye marker (dsRed) under a Leica MZ10 F fluorescence stereomicroscope.

Transgene insertion was characterized by Sanger sequencing which showed complete integration of the genetic insert into the second exon of the pdf gene. We detected the presence of a highly repetitive fragment (~2.5 kb) in the intronic region preceding the second exon. However, this did not impact the pdf-specific expression of QF2-driven fluorescent marker and the transgene resulted in a null-allele as confirmed by the complete loss of PDF peptide in homozygous pdf-QF2 mutant. The pdf-QF2 mutant line was backcrossed to wild type for five generations before being used for experimentation. Homozygous animals were established by allowing siblings to interbreed and performing PCR on DNA extracted from single legs of virgin adults using Phire Tissue Direct PCR Master Mix following the manufacturer protocols. A primer pair (F: AGGCACGTCCCAAGTAACAG; R: CGCTGTTGGTGTGGTTAGGA) flanking the insertion was used, and the presence of the long, insertion-containing product and the absence of the short wild-type product was used to identify homozygous individuals (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). Homozygous lines were maintained for no more than six generations, at which time homozygosing procedures were repeated. We did not observe general mating, blood-feeding, or egg-laying deficits in either the heterozygous or the homozygous pdf-QF2 mutants.

Locomotor Activity Assay.

The locomotor activity assay (“MozzieBox”) was designed after the Flybox (30). The overall light-tight enclosure of the MozzieBox is 30 × 30 × 30 cm and is manufactured with expanded PVC sheets. Raspberry Pi computers were used to control light entrainment and image acquisition via a python code. Infrared (IR) LED strips (940 nm) were installed at the bottom of the box, and white LED strips were installed on the top of the box. Two 1/8” translucent acrylic panels were used to provide diffusion of the IR and white light sources. Raspberry Pi 5MP 1080p NoIR cameras were mounted on top of the box and longpass filters (Edmund Optics 43-948) were placed in front of the cameras. Custom printed PCB boards (Digikey) were used to connect IR and white light control to the Raspberry Pi computers. The MozzieBoxes were placed at room temperature, and 27 ± 2 °C temperature was maintained throughout.

Mosquitoes were individually loaded into the wells of 12-well tissue culture plates and each well was supplied with 1.5 mL of 10% sucrose in 1% agarose to provide humidity and a food source. Cohoused females were 3 to 14 d old when loaded into the 12-well plates under cold anesthesia, and loading was performed around ZT10 on the day prior to the first entrainment cycle. Two 12-well plates were placed on the lower diffuser panels to be recorded under programmed light conditions. Three LD cycles, with 30-minute dawn and dusk periods, that were synchronized to the light condition of the main rearing insectary were provided before seven days of DD recordings. Images were taken every minute throughout the recording and later compiled as a 30 frames-per-second video using custom Matlab code (Version 2020b). The videos were analyzed with EthoVision (Version 17), where individual mosquitoes were tracked using gray scaling. Distance traveled between frames were extracted for further analysis. All Mozziebox assays were performed at least 3 independent times and genotype, loading position, and the MozzieBox used were randomized between trials. All original codes for the MozzieBox assay are deposited at Github: (https://github.com/DongLinhan/LinhanDong2024). Calculation of rhythmic power and period (Fig. 4 B and C) were performed using the phase package (1.2.9) in R (version 4.2.3) (74). Further details on the analysis of locomotor activity can be found in SI Appendix.

MozzieDrome CO2 Response Assay.

The MozzieDrome CO2 response assay features a circadian entrainment enclosure, a video-recording platform, a mosquito arena with CO2 delivery components. The enclosure of the MozzieDrome was constructed with 1/4” black acrylic sheets. A 60 × 45 × 45 cm box with ventilation holes and a 60 × 45 cm hinged door was assembled with acrylic cement. Black foam tape was applied to the rims and secured with duct tape and black-out curtains were installed to ensure a light-proof seal. Four rubber feet were attached to the bottom of the box to provide elevation and soften vibration. Ventilation holes at the top were equipped with two USB cooling fans covered with a light-proof screen and ventilation holes at the bottom were covered with an elevated black acrylic platform to ensure darkness. The lighting system uses white LED strips covered with a 1/8’’ white translucent acrylic sheet to provide diffusion. The white LED light and the CO2 delivery system were controlled with Matlab code to provide animals with LD (12 h light: 12 h dark, with 30 min transitions) and DD conditions.

A camera mount was installed on a precut camera hole on top of the enclosure and an IR light platform was placed inside of the enclosure to provide illumination for videography. An IR longpass filter was secured inside the enclosure to block visible light and prevent visible light interfering with video acquisition. Back and side IR illumination was provided with a custom-built light box constructed with 1/4’’ clear acrylic sheets covered with two sheets of Kimwipe to provide diffusion. The 10 × 20 × 30 cm mosquito arena was constructed with 1/8’’ clear acrylic sheets and secured with acrylic cement. Two precut holes 13 mm in diameter were designed to accommodate sugar feeders. The open faces were covered with mesh sheets secured with hot glue. CO2 delivery was controlled by solenoid valves used to control the flow speed of CO2 through 1/4’’ OD tubing and 1/8’’ ID tubing by adjusting the duration of an on/off cycle according to readings from a flow meter. Custom mixed 10% CO2 was supplied to the solenoid valve and a T-splitter was used to divert the CO2 stream into two diffuser pads (Flystuff).

To avoid contamination with human odor, all handling was done with gloves and the arena was cleaned with deionized water and 70% ethanol between trials. Cohoused female mosquitoes (n = 9 to 10) reared under the same LD entrainment schedule as in the MozzieDrome were aspirated into the mosquito arena via the sugar feeder hole. Two sugar feeders with 5% sucrose solution were mounted on sugar feeder holders. IR light sources and ventilation fans were turned on at this point and stayed on throughout each experiment. All preparation for each experiment was done before ZT12 of the day prior to the testing period to allow animals to acclimate. For each experiment, testing consisted of 4 d of trials that are 3 h apart. Days 1 and 2 were under LD entrainment (12 h light: 12 h dark, 30 min transition) and days 3 and 4 were under DD conditions. For each trial, the camera recorded a 12 min 60 frames-per-second video at the beginning of each testing ZT/CT hour. A 30-sec supply of CO2 at a flow rate of 7 SCFH was programmed to start at minute two of the corresponding ZT/CT testing hour. Scheduling of video-recording was done with the Window Task Scheduler to call a start/end recording function from the StreamPix software. The StreamPix software was preconfigured with exposure time, gain, acquisition speed, and ROI. Light and CO2 delivery were programmed via custom Matlab program. Animals were cold-anesthetized and removed from the mosquito arena at the end of each experiment. No more than one death was recorded at the end of the recording. SLEAP was used to track animal activity and identity (75). Details on the downstream analysis of CO2-evoked response can be found in SI Appendix.

Blood-Feeding Assay.

Cohoused female mosquitoes were entrained for at least 3 d under a 12 h light: 12 h dark schedule and then loaded into a 24.5 × 24.5 × 24.5 cm cage 19 to 27 h prior to an experiment in groups of 27 to 31. The cage was supplied with a cotton wick soaked with DI water to prevent dehydration and kept under the same environmental and light condition until experimentation. Cages were placed inside an environmentally controlled cabinet (28 °C, 75 to 85% relative humidity) and acclimated for 10 min before the start of an experiment, when a 30-sec 25 SCFH pulse of 10% CO2 was supplied to the cabinet that elevated CO2 level by ~1,000 ppm. The cabinet door was opened by 3 cm 2 min after CO2 application to allow CO2 level to return to baseline level. Six minutes after CO2 delivery, a Hemotek blood-feeder at 37 °C was supplied to the testing cage for 10 min, and the cabinet door was closed. The Hemotek blood-feeder was assembled with 3 mL of defibrinated sheep blood and 30 μL of 200 mM ATP. The feeder was covered in a 4 × 4 cm piece of human-scented nylon. Nylon stocking was worn on the forearms of the same experimenter for 6–8 h then rubbed against the experimenter’s chest and arms prior to collection and storage at -20 °C until use. Gloves were used for all mosquito handling in the 24 h before testing to prevent contamination of host odor from other sources. After each experiment, cages were immediately moved to a cold room to be sorted and crushed on a piece of filter paper to determine blood-feeding status by scoring for the presence of fresh blood.

Generation of Polyclonal Guinea Pig Anti-ILP3 Antibody.

A custom polyclonal antibody was raised in guinea pig against Aedes aegypti ILP3, UniProt: A0EZR6 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Rockford IL). Epitope corresponded to residues 130 to 148 (RNDLIPPRFR KSPRGIVDE).

Brain Staining and Imaging.

Detailed procedures on brain immunohistochemistry and RNA in situ hybridization can be found in SI Appendix. All images shown and analyzed were obtained with a Zeiss LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope unless otherwise noted. All images used for quantitative analysis and representative images (Fig. 5 B–D, F–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4D, S10, and S11) were obtained with a 20× objective. Images used for the same cohort of quantitative analysis (PDF intensity quantification in Fig. 5C and period intensity quantification in Fig. 5H) were acquired at 1,024 × 1,024 pixels and stored as 16-bit images under the same imaging parameters and laser setups. Entire brains were captured with 2.3 μm z-step. Epifluorescent imaging was used for representative images shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4E with a Nikon Ti2E microscope with a 20× objective. Images were acquired with 0.9 μm z-step, at 3,892 × 3,884 pixels, and stored as 16-bit images. Details on image analysis can be found in SI Appendix.

Statistical Analysis.

All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism or custom python code. Nonparametric tests were used for data that do not follow normal distribution. Source data and statistical test details, including exact n and P values, can be found in Dataset S1.
