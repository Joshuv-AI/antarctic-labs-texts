A selective RCC1 inhibitor loaded in membrane-coated DNA nanocage for targeted suppression of TNBC progression
Authors: Yunbo Luo, Yali Wang, Xiaohan Su, Xue Li, Jiao Zeng, Shishan Deng, Wei Deng, Jia Xu, Lingmi Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) exhibits high recurrence rate and worse prognosis because of lacking effective targeted therapy. In this study, RCC1 was found to be highly expressed in TNBC and contribute to poor prognosis by promoting tumor progression through activation of Notch1 signaling pathway, suggesting that RCC1 is a potential therapeutic target for TNBC. Subsequently, Euphorbiasteroid (Ebd) was identified as a potent small-molecule inhibitor of RCC1 protein by high-throughput screening and can significantly suppress TNBC tumor growth and metastasis. To overcome the poor bioavailability and off-target effects of Ebd, a biomimetic nanodrug delivery system (TPRDN) was developed. Ebd was encapsulated within tetrahedral DNA nanocages assembled from four DNA oligonucleotide chains. These DNA nanocages were then coated with hybrid membranes composed of TNBC cell, platelet, and red blood cell membranes. Additionally, a Nectin-4 targeting bicyclic peptide was functionalized on the hybrid membranes to improve targeting precision. The TPRDN system can significantly target TNBC tissues and circulating tumor cells, thereby enhancing the inhibitory effects of Ebd on TNBC growth and metastasis, highlighting a promising strategy for precise and effective treatment for TNBC.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer (BC), characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression [1]. This unique feature renders TNBC unresponsive to endocrine therapy and anti-HER2 targeted therapies, leaving chemotherapy as the primary effective therapeutic option. The lack of actionable therapeutic targets further contributes to its poor prognosis, including higher rates of early recurrence and distant metastasis compared with non-TNBC [2]. Despite decades of extensive research aimed at identifying therapeutic targets for BC, effective targeted treatments for TNBC remain scarce. Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors are effective therapeutic agents for BC with germline BRCA mutations [3]. However, only a minority (9.3 %–15.4 %) of TNBC patients may benefit from this regimen due to the low prevalence of germline BRCA mutations in this population [4]. Immunotherapy has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in various malignancies, such as melanoma [5], non-small-cell lung cancer [6], and etc. However, its clinical efficacy in TNBC remains suboptimal, and it often necessitates combination with other therapeutic modalities, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which may increase the side effects for patients. Therefore, identifying highly effective therapeutic targets remains an urgent challenge to improve the treatment for TNBC.

As the only known guanine nucleotide exchange factor for Ran GTPase, regulator of chromosome condension 1 (RCC1) critically regulates nucleocytoplasmic transport by catalyzing the exchange of GDP for GTP [7], which plays a critical role in cell division and the maintenance of normal cellular functions. Recently, RCC1 was found to be highly expressed in multiple cancers and showed strong correlation with poor clinical outcomes [[8], [9], [10]]. Targeting RCC1 protein can inhibit the human soft-tissue sarcoma by disrupting nucleo-cytoplasmic trafficking of Skp2 [10]. However, the role of RCC1 on BC has seldom been studied. A previous study identified germline RCC1 mutations in 4.7 % patients with familial BC, suggesting its potential role as a BC susceptibility gene [11]. A pan-cancer analysis of TCGA data revealed that RCC1 was significantly upregulated in BC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues and was strongly associated with worse outcomes [12]. However, its expression patterns and prognostic significance across distinct molecular subtypes remain uncharacterized. In our study, RCC1 was found highly expressed in TNBC compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues and other molecular subtypes of BC. Additionally, high expression of RCC1 exhibited significantly worse outcomes for TNBC. Moreover, RCC1 may promote the progression of TNBC by activating the Notch1 signaling pathway. Therefore, RCC1 is a potential therapeutic target for TNBC and inhibition of RCC1 protein may serve as a promising therapeutic strategy for TNBC.

Functional suppression of target proteins constitutes a principal therapeutic paradigm for anti-cancer treatment, such as, monoclonal antibody blockade, RNA-mediated silencing, and CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing technology [13]. However, these techniques encounter substantial barriers to implementation, including technical complexity and high production costs, which considerably limit their clinical applicability. Recently, small molecular compounds have garnered much attention for their outstanding advantages in anti-tumor treatment. First, they exhibit better cell permeability, allowing efficient targeting of intracellular proteins [14]. Additionally, their synthesis is cost-effective compared to biologics, and many can be administered orally, offering a more convenient delivery method. Moreover, extensive preclinical characterization of small-molecule inhibitors, including comprehensive toxicity profiles and pharmacokinetic analyses, has facilitated their accelerated transition into Phase II oncology trials [15]. Therefore, small-molecule inhibitors targeting specific proteins represent a promising class of anticancer agents, leveraging their molecular precision and optimized pharmacological properties for targeted therapeutic intervention. While, lacking tumor-targeted delivery system may substantially compromise the bioavailability of small-molecule inhibitors.

In recent years, DNA nanocages have gained significant attention for their potential in encapsulating small molecular drugs. These nanostructures offer a promising platform for drug delivery due to their biocompatibility, programmability, and the ability to precisely control the release of therapeutic agents [16]. The unique properties of DNA nanocages allow for enhanced stability, targeted delivery, and controlled release of small molecules, making them a valuable tool in the development of advanced drug delivery systems. While, DNA nanocages face several challenges regarding their stability in the bloodstream, which can hinder their effectiveness in drug delivery applications. Enzymatic degradation by nucleases in the blood can rapidly break down the DNA structures, compromising their integrity [17]. Additionally, the immune system may recognize these foreign DNA structures as threats, triggering immune responses that lead to rapid clearance. To overcome these obstacles, strategies are needed to avoid immune recognition and improve their performance in therapeutic applications. Fortunately, the hybrid cell membranes can effectively encapsulate DNA nanocages and shield DNA nanocages from nucleases and other enzymatic degradation in the bloodstream. In addition, the hybrid cell membrane possesses specific functional properties derived from different cell source membranes [18]. Nanoparticles derived from tumor cell membranes possess excellent tumor-homing ability and are commonly used in targeted therapy for tumors [19]. The transmembrane protein CD47 on the surface of red blood cell membranes can bind to SRPα on the surface of macrophages, generating inhibitory signals that prevent phagocytosis and clearance by the immune system [20], which contributes to prolong circulation time for nanoparticles derived from red blood cell membranes [21]. Previous studies demonstrated that platelets can be activated and attach to circulating tumor cells (CTCs) to generate a protective cloak that helps cancer cells to escape immune surveillance [22,23]. Due to this characteristic, platelet membrane-coated nanoparticles were engineered to selectively target circulating tumor cells (CTCs) through biomimetic adhesion mechanisms, demonstrating significant metastasis suppression in multiple cancer models [24,25]. Therefore, the hybrid membrane system, integrating membranes from TNBC cells, platelets, and erythrocytes, may demonstrate prolonged circulation time, enhanced tumor-targeting capability, and selective affinity toward CTCs. Furthermore, previous study have shown that Nectin-4 is highly expressed on the cell membrane surface of TNBC cells [26]. Meanwhile, a Nectin-4 targeting Bicycle (N4TB) has been found to efficiently bind to the extracellular domain of the Nectin-4 protein [27]. Thus, attaching N4TB to the membrane surface may further enhance the targeting capability of delivery system for TNBC.

In our study, RCC1 has been found to promote the progression of TNBC, indicating that RCC1 can serve as an excellent therapeutic target for TNBC. Subsequently, high-throughput screening and drug toxicity assays have determined that Euphorbiasteroid (Ebd) is an excellent small-molecule inhibitor of RCC1 protein and can significantly suppress tumor growth by inhibiting Notch1 signaling pathway in TNBC. Moreover, the nanodrug delivery system (TPRDN) was designed to package Ebd and improve its targeting ability to TNBC tissues and CTCs. The results show that TPRDN can significantly target TNBC tumor tissues and enhance the inhibitory effect of Ebd on TNBC growth. Meanwhile, TPRDN can efficiently target CTCs and significantly enhance the anti-metastasis capacity of Ebd on TNBC. Therefore, TPRDN-encapsulated Ebd may be a promising targeted therapy for TNBC.

Results and discussion

High expression of RCC1 leads to worse outcome in TNBC

In recent years, RCC1 has been found to be aberrantly expressed in several cancers and has been demonstrated to promote the progress of these cancers [8,9,28]. In our study, bioinformatics analysis revealed that the expression level of RCC1 was significantly higher in BC tissues than that in adjacent noncancerous tissues (Fig. S1A). Moreover, the expression of RCC1 in TNBC is significantly higher than that in non-TNBC and adjacent noncancerous tissues (Fig. S1B). To verify the differential expression of RCC1 in BC, we cultured normal breast epithelial cells and BC cells of different molecular subtypes. Then, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and western blot (WB) assays were employed to detect the expression levels of RCC1. Consistent with bioinformatics analysis, the results showed that the expression level of RCC1 in TNBC cells was significantly higher than that in normal breast epithelial cell and non-TNBC cells (Fig. 1A). Moreover, fresh tumor tissues and adjacent noncancerous tissues were collected from BC patients and subjected to qRT-PCR and WB assays. As expected, RCC1 was highly expressed in both TNBC and non-TNBC tissues compared with adjacent noncancerous tissues (Fig. 1B). Additionally, RCC1 was expressed at higher levels in TNBC tissues compared to non-TNBC tissues. The expression of RCC1 in BC and its correlation with clinicopathological factors was further assessed by IHC analysis in 261 paraffin-embedded, archived BC specimens, including 131 cases of TNBC and 130 cases of non-TNBC. The results showed that RCC1 was either negatively expressed in adjacent noncancerous tissues or weakly expressed in Luminal A BC, moderately expressed in HER2-positive BC or some TNBC, whereas it was strongly expressed in most TNBC (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, the patients were categorized into high or low RCC1 expression groups based on the intensity and extent of RCC1 staining. The results indicated that 51 (39.2 %) patients had high expression of RCC1 in non-TNBC, whereas 82 (62.6 %) patients were found to have high expression of RCC1 in TNBC (Fig. 1D), which further demonstrated higher expression of RCC1 in TNBC. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the patients with high RCC1 expression exhibited higher proportion of young patients (14.3 % versus 6.3 %, P = 0.033), more lymph node positivity (48.1 % versus 35.9 %, P = 0.046), and later TNM stage (stage Ⅲ:29.3 % versus 16.4 %, P = 0.013) compared with patients of low RCC1 expression. Additionally, more patients were found to be estrogen receptor (ER) negative status (75.9 % versus 53.9 %, P < 0.001), high histological grade (Grade 3: 48.9 % versus 15.6 %, P < 0.001), and high Ki-67 expression (78.2 % versus 45.3 %, P < 0.001) in high RCC1 expression group compared to low RCC1 expression group. Collectively, these results suggested that high expression of RCC1 is closely related to adverse clinicopathological features in BC.

Previous researches have demonstrated that elevated RCC1 expression is associated with unfavorable prognosis in multiple tumors [[8], [9], [10],29]. In our study, bioinformatics analysis showed that BC patients with high RCC1 expression exhibited worse disease-free survival (DFS) (HR: 1.22, 95 % CI: 1.1–1.35) and overall survival (OS) (HR: 1.43, 95 % CI: 1.13–1.82) than patients with low RCC1 expression (Fig. S2A). Moreover, TNBC with high RCC1 expression exhibited significantly worse DFS (HR: 1.85, 95 % CI: 1.19–2.88) and OS (HR: 1.69, 95 % CI: 1.04–2.74) compared with TNBC with low RCC1 expression (Fig. S2B). To further validate the results from bioinformatics analysis, the survival data of 261 BC patients were used to analyze the impact of RCC1 expression on the prognosis of BC. Consistent with bioinformatics analysis, the patients with high RCC1 expression exhibited worse DFS (HR: 2.383, 95 %CI: 1.243–4.571) and OS (HR: 2.333, 95 %CI: 1.185–4.459) compared with patients of low RCC1 expression (Fig. S2C). Moreover, in TNBC, the patients with high RCC1 expression exhibited significantly worse DFS (HR: 4.095, 95 %CI: 1.420–11.805) and OS (HR: 4.153, 95 %CI: 1.430–11.892) compared to those with low RCC1 expression (Fig. 1E). However, in non-TNBC, there were no significant differences in DFS (HR: 1.065, 95 % CI: 0.379–2.992, p = 0.905) and OS (HR: 0.565, 95 % CI: 0.150–2.131, P = 0.399) between the high and low RCC1 expression groups (Fig. S2D). Thus, RCC1 may primarily exert adverse effects on the progression of TNBC. Subsequently, multivariate survival analysis revealed that high expression of RCC1 is an independent risk factor for DFS (HR: 3.271, 95 % CI: 1.018–10.511, p = 0.047) and OS (HR: 3.579, 95 % CI: 1.097–11.678, p = 0.035) in patients with TNBC (Supplemental Table 2). Taken together, these results suggested that high expression of RCC1 was associated with adverse prognosis in patients with TNBC.

RCC1 facilitates TNBC progression by worsening tumor behaviors through Notch 1 pathway

To explore how RCC1 promotes the progression of TNBC, we obtained RCC1 stable knockdown TNBC cells (MDA-MB-231 and BT-549) through lentivirus-mediated transduction of two distinct shRNAs targeting RCC1. The results of CCK-8 assay indicated that RCC1 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation of TNBC cells (Fig. 2A). Also, colony formation assays showed that RCC1 knockdown significantly suppressed the clonogenic formation of TNBC cells (Fig. 2B). Previous studies have shown that RCC1 promotes tumor progression by regulating the cell cycle [10,29]. Thus, flow cytometry was employed to assess the cell cycle distribution of TNBC cells after RCC1 knockdown. The analysis revealed that RCC1 knockdown resulted in a marked accumulation of TNBC cells in the G1 phase (Fig. 2C and Fig. S3A), suggesting that RCC1 promotes cell cycle progression in TNBC cells. Additionally, apoptosis rates were significantly increased in TNBC cells following RCC1 knockdown (Fig. 2D and Fig. S4A). These results indicated that RCC1 may promote the proliferation of TNBC cells by enhancing cell cycle progression and inhibiting apoptosis. Subsequently, wound healing and transwell assays were conducted to systematically assess the impact of RCC1 on the migration and invasion capabilities of TNBC cells. To further validate the effect of RCC1 on the growth of TNBC in vivo, xenograft models were established in BALB/c nude mice by subcutaneously inoculating MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells stably transduced with either shRCC1 or shNC. Tumor size was measured every three days, and tumor volume was calculated. Three weeks after establishing xenografts, the mice were euthanized, and the xenograft tumors were meticulously excised, imaged, and weighed. The shRCC1 group exhibited a marked reduction in both the average tumor volume and weight compared to the shNC group (Fig. 2G–H, Fig. S6A). In addition, immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the impact of RCC1 knockdown on the proliferative index (Ki-67 expression) in xenograft tumors. A significant reduction in Ki-67 expression was observed in tumors with RCC1 knockdown (Fig. 2I), indicating that RCC1 knockdown markedly suppressed TNBC cell proliferation. Taken together, these results confirmed that RCC1 can promote the growth of TNBC in vivo. While, further research is needed to clarify the mechanism by which RCC1 promotes TNBC progression.

In order to further investigate the mechanism that RCC1 promote TNBC, we acquired the transcriptomic data of TNBC from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Subsequently, differential gene expression analysis was conducted by stratifying samples into RCC1 high and low expression groups based on the median RCC1 expression level. The analysis revealed that 72 genes were upregulated and 159 genes were downregulated in TNBC samples with high RCC1 expression (Fig. S7A). To further validate the above results, qRT-PCR was conducted to assess the changes in key genes of the Notch signaling pathway in TNBC cells following RCC1 knockdown. The results showed the transcription levels of Notch1–4 were not significantly altered; however, the transcription of downstream target genes of Notch pathway, such as Hes1, Hey1, and Snail1, were suppressed (Fig. 3B). WB also showed that the expression levels of Notch1–4 were not significantly changed after RCC1 knockdown (Fig. 3C); while, the downstream target genes of Notch pathway, including Hes1, Hey1, and Snail1, were significantly suppressed in TNBC cells after RCC1 knockdown (Fig. 3D). By contrast, upregulation of these downstream target genes was observed in TNBC cells with RCC1 overexpression (RCC1-OE), suggesting that RCC1 may promote TNBC progression by activating the Notch signaling pathway. The cell cycle–related proteins, including Cyclin A2 and Cyclin D1 were markedly reduced in TNBC cells with RCC1 knockdown but upregulated in TNBC cells with RCC1-OE, which explains the above results that more TNBC cells arrested in the G1 phase after RCC1 knockdown. Previous study demonstrated that activation of the Notch signaling pathway can significantly inhibit tumor cell apoptosis [30]. In our study, RCC1 knockdown led to a significant upregulation of Bax and downregulation of Bcl-2 in TNBC cells. While, the oppositive change trends were observed in TNBC cells with RCC1-OE, suggesting that RCC1 may suppress apoptosis in TNBC cells by activating Notch signaling pathway. Furthermore, activation of the Notch signaling pathway can significantly promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in tumor cells [31]. In our study, knockdown of RCC1 led to a significant upregulation of E-cadherin in TNBC cells, accompanied by downregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin expression. Reversely, decreased E-cadherin but increased N-cadherin and Vimentin were observed in TNBC cells with RCC1-OE. These results suggested that RCC1 can promote EMT by activating Notch signaling pathway. In summary, RCC1 may promote cell cycle progression, inhibit apoptosis, and enhance EMT in TNBC cells by activating the Notch signaling pathway, thereby contributing to TNBC progression. However, how RCC1 activates the Notch signaling pathway remains to be investigated.

Notch1 is highly expressed in TNBC and contributes to the progress of TNBC [33]. In our study, bioinformatics analysis indicated that Notch1 expression was significantly higher in TNBC tissues than that in normal breast tissues, whereas no obvious upregulation of Notch2/3/4 expression was observed (Fig. S8A-D). These results were consistent with the WB results (Fig. S8E), suggesting that Notch1 may play an important role in the progression of TNBC. Notch1 signaling is typically activated through the binding of cell surface Notch1 receptors to membrane-bound ligands (such as Jagged1, Jagged2, DLL1, DLL3, and DLL4) on adjacent cells. Upon ligand binding, the Notch1 receptor undergoes a series of proteolytic cleavages, ultimately releasing the Notch1 intracellular domain (N1ICD), which translocates into the nucleus to regulate the transcription of target genes [32], indicating N1ICD is a key protein involved in the activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway. Therefore, WB was performed to assess the changes in N1ICD levels of TNBC cells following RCC1 knockdown. Surprisingly, the expression levels of N1ICD remained unchanged in TNBC cells following RCC1 knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 3D). Previous study suggested that N1ICD requires a nuclear localization signal (NLS) to translocate into the nucleus through the nuclear pore complex (NPC) [34]. Meanwhile, RCC1 facilitates the nucleocytoplasmic transport of macromolecular proteins through NPC by regulating the concentration of Ran-GTP [35,36]. Therefore, RCC1 may activate the Notch1 signaling pathway by controlling the nuclear transport of N1ICD. To verify this hypothesis, immunofluorescence staining and confocal microscopy analysis were used to detect the distribution of N1ICD. The results showed that RCC1 knockdown impaired nuclear translocation of N1ICD, resulting in its accumulation in cytoplasm (Fig. 3E and F). By contrast, an increased amount of N1ICD was translocated into the nucleus in TNBC cells with RCC1-OE (Fig. S9A-B). Moreover, we separately extracted proteins from the cytoplasm and nucleus of TNBC cells and assessed the differences in the nuclear-cytoplasmic distribution of N1ICD after RCC1 knockdown. The results showed a marked increase of N1ICD in the cytoplasm and decrease in the nucleus after RCC1 knockdown (Fig. 3G). Conversely, RCC1 overexpression led to a marked increase of N1ICD in the nucleus and a significant decrease in the cytoplasm (Fig. S9C). Therefore, RCC1 may activate the Notch1 signaling pathway by promoting nuclear translocation of N1ICD. Targeting RCC1 protein may inhibit the Notch1 signaling pathway and provide a promising treatment for TNBC.

High-throughput screening of targeted-RCC1 drugs

Currently, various approaches are available to target specific proteins, including monoclonal antibodies, RNA interference (RNAi), and CRISPR-Cas9–based gene editing strategies [37]. However, the complexity and high cost of these methods limit their clinical applicability, restricting their accessibility to a limited number of patients. Fortunately, high-throughput screening approaches may serve as an effective strategy for identifying potential small-molecule compounds targeting specific proteins due to their multiple advantages, including rapid processing, low cost, and high specificity. Therefore, virtual screening of potential small-molecule candidates was conducted by using the Topscience bioactive compound library through computer-based molecular docking simulations, and the top 50 small-molecule compounds were selected for next experiments (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) molecular interaction analysis was employed to evaluate the binding affinity between RCC1 protein and the selected small-molecule compounds. The results showed that Euphorbiasteroid (Ebd) exhibited the strongest binding affinity to RCC1 protein, with KD value of 2.51 μm (Fig. S10A). Moreover, the cytotoxicity of top 10 small-molecule compounds against TNBC cells was evaluated using CCK8 assay. The results showed that Ebd exhibited the strongest cytotoxic effect against TNBC cells (Fig. S11A-B). The chemical structure of Ebd is shown in Fig. 4B. Molecular docking revealed that Ebd can interact with multiple amino acid residues of RCC1 protein through hydrogen bonds, carbon–hydrogen bonds, and π-alkyl bonds (Fig. 4C). The binding strength between RCC1 protein and Ebd increased with higher Ebd concentrations (Fig. 4D). However, the mutation of certain amino acids of RCC1 protein, including SER, PHE, ALA, and LYS, led to a substantial increase in the KD values (Fig. 4E). These changes indicated that the interaction between Ebd and RCC1 protein involves the aforementioned amino acid residues. The drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay demonstrated that Ebd dose-dependently counteracted pronase-induced degradation of RCC1 in TNBC cells (Fig. 4F). Moreover, CETSA was conducted to assess the stability of RCC1 protein treated with Ebd. The results showed that RCC1 protein was denatured at 45 °C in DMSO-treated control cells (Fig. 4G). While, the denaturation temperature of RCC1 protein increased to 55 °C in TNBC cells treated with Ebd, suggesting that Ebd specifically stabilizes the RCC1 protein in TNBC cells. Taken together, Ebd shows potential as a novel RCC1-targeting compound for the treatment of TNBC. The inhibitory effects of Ebd on TNBC cells were assessed using the CCK8 assay at various drug concentrations. The results showed that the inhibitory effect on TNBC cells intensified with increasing concentrations of Ebd (Fig. 4H), with IC50 values of 10.8 and 9.4 μM for MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells, respectively. Therefore, subsequent experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of Ebd on TNBC cells at concentrations of 5, 10, and 20 μM. Consistent with the effect of RCC1 knockdown, Ebd exhibited dose-responsive effects: impaired clonogenicity, G1 phase arrest, and elevated apoptosis (Fig. 4I–K, Fig. S12-14A). Additionally, Ebd significantly suppressed the migratory and invasive capacities of TNBC cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4L–M and Fig. S15A-B). Moreover, the CCK-8 assay was conducted to compare the inhibitory effects of RCC1 knockdown and Ebd on the proliferative capacity of TNBC cells. The results shown that treatment of TNBC cells with 10 μM Ebd exerted a consistent inhibitory effect comparable to that of RCC1 knockdown (Fig. S16A). However, when the concentration of Ebd was increased to 20 μM, its inhibitory effect on TNBC cells was significantly stronger than that induced by RCC1 knockdown. These findings further support the potential therapeutic advantage of Ebd, as it can achieve more potent tumor inhibition with dose escalation, which may contribute to its priority in clinical application.

In order to further verify that Ebd alleviate the progression of TNBC through interaction with RCC1 protein, Ebd was administered to TNBC cells transfected with RCC1-OE plasmid or an empty vector control. Ebd can significantly block the cells in G1 phase in TNBC cells transfected with empty vector. However, the cell cycle arrest induced by Ebd was alleviated in RCC1-OE TNBC cells (Fig. 5A and Fig. S17A). Also, the apoptosis-promoting effect of Ebd was hindered in RCC1-OE TNBC cells (Fig. 5B and Fig. S18A). Furthermore, RCC1 overexpression partially reversed Ebd-mediated suppression of migratory and invasive capacities in TNBC cells (Fig. 5C–D and Fig. S19A-B). Collectively, these results indicated that Ebd may suppress TNBC progression by inhibiting RCC1 protein. Moreover, WB was conducted to validate the potential mechanism that Ebd-mediated inhibition of TNBC progression. Consistent with the effect of RCC1 knockdown, Ebd can significantly inhibit the expression of cyclin A2 and cyclin D1 in TNBC cells transfected with empty vector, but this inhibitory effect was significantly reversed in RCC1-OE TNBC cells (Fig. 5E). Also, Ebd significantly upregulated the expression of Bax and downregulated the expression of Bcl-2, but these effects were partly reversed in RCC1-OV TNBC cells. Moreover, Ebd markedly upregulated the expression of E-cadherin and downregulated the expression of N-cadherin and Vimentin, while these alterations were attenuated in RCC1-OE TNBC cells. These results further suggested that Ebd suppresses the progression of TNBC by inhibiting RCC1 protein. Our previous results demonstrated that RCC1 activates the Notch1 signaling pathway by regulating the nuclear translocation of N1ICD. Therefore, Ebd, as an inhibitor of RCC1 protein, may suppress Notch signaling pathway by inhibiting the nuclear translocation of N1ICD. As expected, Ebd significantly inhibited nuclear translocation of N1ICD in TNBC cells, resulting in substantial cytoplasmic accumulation of N1ICD (Fig. 5F). While, this inhibitory effect of nuclear translocation of N1ICD was attenuated in RCC1-OE TNBC cells. Moreover, TNBC cells treated with Ebd exhibited downregulated expression of downstream target genes of Notch1 signal pathway, including Hes1, Hey1, and Snail1 (Fig. 5G). However, the suppressive effects of Ebd on these downstream target genes were significantly attenuated in RCC1-overexpressing TNBC cells. In summary, these results demonstrated that Ebd can suppress the Notch1 signal pathway by inhibited nuclear translocation of N1ICD. Therefore, Ebd can be used for the treatment of TNBC for its excellent killing effect on TNBC by suppressing Notch1 signaling pathway. Design and characterization of Nanotargeted materials

Recent progress in DNA nanotechnology has facilitated the development of nanostructures with finely tuned size, geometry, and functional properties [38]. Due to their superior biocompatibility and exceptional structural stability, certain framework nucleic acids (FNAs), particularly those with compact dimensions, have emerged as a highly versatile nanoplatform for the targeted delivery of theranostic agents in biomedical applications [39]. Tetrahedral DNA nanocage (tDNAn) offers the highest number of interaction sites and the most robust salt bridge networks, thereby significantly enhancing cellular internalization efficiency [40]. Therefore, tDNAn was designed to as a drug-loading core through the self-assembly of four single-stranded oligonucleotides in our study. The stepwise assembly of tDNAn, achieved through the programmed hybridization of four single-stranded oligonucleotides (ssDNA1–4), was confirmed by the sequential mobility shift of discrete bands in agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 6A and B). Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization demonstrated the successful self-assembly of tDNAn adopting perfect tetrahedral geometry, with size of 10 nm (Fig. 6C). Despite the excellent drug-loading capacity of tDNAn, their clinical application is substantially limited due to nuclease-mediated degradation and efficient uptake by immune cells in blood circulation. Biomimetic cell membranes derived from a variety of cell membranes can be used to wrap DNA nanocage and protect it from enzymatic degradation and extend its half-life. Moreover, coating synthetic nanoparticles with hybrid cell membranes can impart various biological properties inherited from the source cells [41]. In our study, the hybrid cell membranes were constructed by fusing membranes derived from TNBC cells, platelets (PLT), and red blood cells (RBC). First, we obtained the RBC membranes (RBCm), PLT membranes (PLTm), and TNBC cell membranes (TNBCm) according to the way described in a previous study [42]. Then, the three types of membranes were combined in equal proportions (1:1:1) and fused into a hybrid membrane (TPRm) through membrane extrusion technology (Fig. 6D). To analyze the protein composition of TPRm, we performed SDS-PAGE with Coomassie blue staining on RBCm, PLTm, TNBCm, tDNA, and TPRm. The results showed that the protein profile of TPRm was consistent with the combined protein profiles of RBCm, PLTm, and TNBCm (Fig. S20A). Previous study has demonstrated that Nectin-4 is frequently expressed on the membrane of TNBC cells [26]. In addition, CD41 and CD235a serve as surface marker proteins for PLT and RBC, respectively. Thus, immunofluorescence was utilized to identify these specific proteins of TPRm to estimate the fusion of hybrid cell membranes. The results demonstrated that the aforementioned specific proteins were uniformly distributed across the surface of TPRm (Fig. 6E), suggesting successful fusion of the three cell membranes. Previous study have shown that a Nectin-4 Targeting Bicycle (N4TB) can efficiently bind to the extracellular domain of the Nectin-4 protein [27]. In our study, N4TB was covalently linked to DSPE-PEG through an amide bond, resulting in the formation of DSPE-PEG-N4TB. Then, N4TB was anchored onto the surface of TPRm via DSPE-PEG to further enhance its targeting capability toward TNBC cells. Moreover, to enhance the visualization of the drug delivery system's biodistribution, TPRm was labeled with DSPE-Rhodamine B (DSPE-Rho; red) for fluorescent tracking. Fluorescence imaging demonstrated that TPRm was effectively labeled with Rhodamine B (Fig. S21A).

To fabricate the biomimetic drug delivery system (TPRDN), the functional components, including tDNA, DSPE-PEG-N4TB, and TPRm, were co-extruded through 800, 400, and 200 nm polycarbonate porous membranes to generate membrane-coated nanoparticles (Fig. 6F). Then, TEM was applied to estimate morphological alterations in the membrane following the assembly process. The findings revealed that the membrane surface transitioned from a smooth outline to a more irregular, serrated appearance (Fig. 6G). Also, the particle size exhibited a notable increase, expanding from 100.41 ± 6.57 nm to 123.52 ± 8.25 nm (Fig. 6H). Moreover, the zeta potential also increased from −22.83 ± 3.52 mV to −9.31 ± 4.67 mV because of the positively charged DSPE-PEG-N4TB (Fig. 6I). Taken together, these changes suggested that DSPE-PEG-N4TB was successfully conjugated to the surface of TPRm. The stability of TPRDN was further evaluated after incubation in various media. TPRDN exhibited good stability in both PBS and PBS supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), showing no significant changes in particle size or zeta potential throughout a one-week period (Fig. S22A-B). The anti-phagocytic capability of TPRDN was further validated through subsequent experiments. Neutrophils were separately incubated with TPRDN(Rho) or Rho for a period of time to assess the anti-phagocytic capability. Immunofluorescence revealed differential cellular uptake amounts: free Rho exhibited substantial neutrophil internalization, whereas TPRDN(Rho) demonstrated markedly reduced neutrophil uptake (Fig. S23A), suggesting robust anti-phagocytic capacity of TPRDN. Then, TPRD(Rho), TPRDN(Rho), or Rho were incubated with TNBC cells to estimate the ability of TPRDN homing to TNBC cells. As expected, TNBC cells treated with TPRDN(Rho) displayed the stronger red fluorescence than TNBC cells treated with TPRD(Rho) lacking N4TB, while TNBC cells incubated with Rho alone showed weakest red fluorescence (Fig. S24A). Moreover, the targeting efficacy of TPRDN was assessed by intravenously injecting TPRD(Rho), TPRDN(Rho) or Rho alone into TNBC-xenografted BALB/c nude mice. Rho rapidly disseminated throughout the nude mice and eliminated quickly. By contrast, TPRD(Rho) showed significant aggregation around the tumor site, but to a lesser extent than TPRDN(Rho), and both exhibited a slow clearance rate (Fig. 6J). These results suggested that TPRD exhibits remarkable targeting capability toward TNBC tissue, and N4TB will further enhance the targeting capability of TPRD toward TNBC tissue. Previous study suggested that the incorporation of RBC membranes can prolong the circulation time of nanoparticles in the bloodstream [43]. To verify this effect, Rho or TPRDN(Rho) were injected into mice via the tail vein. Blood samples were then collected at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h to measure fluorescence intensity and assess the in vivo retention of the different formulations. The results showed that TPRDN(Rho) exhibited a significantly prolonged circulation time in the bloodstream compared to free Rho (Fig. S25A).

Previous studies have shown that PLTm can enhance the ability of nanoparticles to target circulating tumor cells (CTCs) [24,25]. Therefore, the fusion of PLTm may further enhance the nanoparticles' targeting ability to CTCs, potentially reducing distant metastasis of TNBC. In our study, female BALB/c mice were intravenously administered with 1x10^6 MDA-MB-231-GFP cells via the tail vein to establish a mouse model of CTCs. Ten minutes later, the mice received an intravenous injection of Rho or TPRDN(Rho) to detect their ability to target CTCs. Previous study showed that tumor cells mainly accumulated in the lung after intravenous injection [25]. Therefore, mice were euthanized, and their lung tissues were harvested for fluorescence imaging to assess the targeting capability toward circulating TNBC cells. The lungs of mice treated with TPRDN(Rho) exhibited stronger fluorescence compared to the lung of those treated with Rho (Fig. 6K). Moreover, the lung tissues were sectioned and examined under confocal microscope for co-localization of MDA-MB-231-GFP and TPRDN(Rho) or Rho. The results showed that much more TPRDN(Rho) co-localized with MDA-MB-231-GFP compared to Rho (Fig. 6L). Taken together, these results suggested that TPRDN can effectively target CTCs, which may further reduce distant metastasis of TNBC.

TPRDN (Ebd) was obtained by repeating the TPRDN preparation process and wrapping Ebd inside the tDNAn. Ultraviolet (UV) absorption spectra were obtained for both free Ebd and TPRDN (Ebd), revealing that Ebd had been successfully encapsulated within tDNAn through intercalation between the base pairs (Fig. 6M). Furthermore, the encapsulation efficiency (EE) was determined to be 64.15 ± 2.25 %, signifying the efficient incorporation of Ebd by TPRDN. The drug loading capacity (DLC) for Ebd within TPRDN was established at 17.31 ± 1.17 %, indicating an optimal equilibrium between the drug encapsulation and the integrity of the TPRDN. Tumor sites are commonly found to be a weakly acidic environment [45]; thus, PBS solutions with a range of pH levels were employed to assess the release profile of TPRDN (Ebd). The findings suggested that a mildly acidic environment substantially increased the release of Ebd from the TPRDN (Ebd) formulation, indicating the promising therapeutic potential of this nanocarrier for TNBC treatment. (Fig. S26A).

A pharmacokinetic (PK) investigation was conducted to evaluate whether TPRDN could enhance the systemic circulation of Ebd and prolong its half-life. After administering a single intravenous dose of different formulations to Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, blood specimens were gathered at pre-set time intervals and analyzed via HPLC. The plasma concentration-time profiles and PK parameters were depicted in Supplementary Fig. S27A and compiled in Supplementary Table 3. In contrast to the rapid elimination of Ebd (t1/2: 3.65 ± 0.08 h), TPRDN-loaded Ebd exhibited a notably extended blood circulation duration (t1/2: 22.14 ± 0.42 h). Additionally, the results derived from the area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) further validated the improved PK characteristics. These findings indicate that the biomimetic nanocarrier system endows Ebd with enhanced PK properties, which is likely due to its high stability and sustained drug release behavior.

This significant change in body weight suggests that effective antitumor treatment with TPRDN (Ebd) may improve the overall physiological condition of tumor-bearing mice. Conclusions

RCC1 is significantly upregulated in TNBC compared with non-TNBC tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues, leading to worse outcome for TNBC. Additionally, RCC1 was found to promote the progression of TNBC by activating Notch1 signaling pathway, indicating that RCC1 is a potential target for the treatment of TNBC. Therefore, high-throughput screening was performed to identify effective small-molecule compounds that target RCC1 protein and inhibit the growth of TNBC. Notably, the results demonstrated that Ebd significantly suppressed TNBC growth by targeting RCC1 protein and inhibiting its function. Moreover, the drug delivery system (TPRDN) was designed to specifically target TNBC tissues and CTCs, thereby enhancing the inhibitory effects of Ebd on TNBC growth and metastasis.

Materials and methods

Synthesis and characterization of tDNAn

In our study, the design and construction of tDNAn were carried out based on the previously reported method [40]. First, four ssDNAs (S1–S4) were combined in TM buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM MgCl2, pH 8.0) and mixed thoroughly to a final concentration of 1 μM. Subsequently, the mixture was heated to 95 °C for 10 min and rapidly cooled to 4 °C for 20 min using a PCR thermal cycler (Jena Bioscience, Germany). Finally, the synthesized products were tDNAn. The successful construction of tDNAn was confirmed by detecting the relative molecular mass distribution using PAGE. The morphological characteristics and particle stability of the tDNAn were estimated by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM).

Preparation of hybrid membrane (TPRm)

The cell membrane materials, comprising TNBC cells, erythrocytes, and platelets, were hybridized together in accordance with a previously reported method [42]. First, the harvested cells were resuspended in a membrane protein extraction buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and incubated on ice for 15 min. Subsequently, the cell lysis was achieved through three freeze-thaw cycles, followed by sequential centrifugation (3000×g, 10 min, 4 °C; then 12,800×g, 40 min, 4 °C) to isolate membrane fragments from the supernatant. The three membrane fractions were combined at equimolar ratios (1:1:1) and sonicated on ice (10 min) to generate TPRm. Immunofluorescence microscopy visualized the distribution of specific membrane proteins from the three kinds of cells, such as Nectin-4, CD41, and CD235a. SDS-PAGE resolved differential protein profiles between TNMm and the three constituent membranes. Because Nectin-4 Targeting Bicycle (N4TB) can efficiently bind to the extracellular domain of the Nectin-4 protein highly expressed in the membrane of TNBC cells [27], N4TB was conjugated to membrane surfaces via DSPE-PEG mediated amidation to augment TPRm tumor targeting. Moreover, the membrane system was labeled with DSPE-Rhodamine to evaluate its targeting and anti-phagocytic capabilities.

Synthesis and characterization of the biomimetic drug delivery systems (TPRDN)

The TPRm-coated tDNAn (TPRDN) were obtained by combining tDNAn with TPRm, followed by sonication in an ice-water bath for 10 min, and extrusion through polycarbonate membranes with pore sizes of 800, 400, and 200 nm. Subsequently, the suspension mixture was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm and 4 °C for 30 min to eliminate unassembled cell membranes and tDNAn. The acquired TPRDN were then resuspended in 10 mM HEPES buffer at pH 7.45 for next experiments. Moreover, TEM was used to evaluate the morphological changes of TPRDN, while the Zetasizer Nano ZS 90 was employed to assess the zeta potential and size of TPRDN.

Stability and targeting efficiency for TPRDN

In order to estimate the stability of TPRDN, PBS or PBS containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) were incubated with TPRDN for one week. Then, Zetasizer Nano ZS 90 was used to detect the zeta potential and size of TPRDN every day. To assess the anti-phagocytosis capacity of TPRDN, TPRDN(Rho) or Rho were incubated with neutrophils for 4 h, respectively. Subsequently, fluorescence microscopy was used to detect the phagocytosis of TPRDN(Rho) or Rho by neutrophils. Previous studies suggested that the incorporation of RBC membranes can prolong the circulation time of nanoparticles in the bloodstream [43]. To verify this effect, Rho or TPRDN(Rho) were injected into mice via the tail vein. Blood samples were then collected at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h to measure fluorescence intensity and assess the in vivo retention of the different formulations. Moreover, TPRD(Rho), TPRDN(Rho) or Rho were incubated with primary TNBC cells for 4 h. The uptake of TPRD(Rho), TPRDN(Rho) or Rho by TNBC cells was then analyzed using fluorescence microscopy, allowing assessment of the targeting capability of TPRDN towards TNBC cells. Besides, TPRD(Rho), TPRDN(Rho) or Rho were administered via tail vein injection into tumor-bearing mice. Fluorescent in vivo imaging was then conducted to evaluate the distribution of Rho, TPRD(Rho), or TPRDN(Rho) within the body.

Previous studies have shown that PLTm can enhance the ability of nanoparticles to target circulating tumor cells [24,25]. Therefore, female BALB/c mice were intravenously administered with 1x10^6 GFP-labeled MDA-MB-231 cells via the tail vein to establish a mouse model of CTCs. Ten min later, the mice received an intravenous injection of Rho or TPRDN(Rho) to detect their ability to target CTCs. Subsequently, the nude mice were euthanized, and their lung tissues were harvested for fluorescence imaging to assess the targeting capability of different nanoparticles toward circulating TNBC cells. Finally, the lung tissues were sectioned and examined under Confocal microscopy for co-localization of MDA-MB-231-GFP and TPRDN(Rho) or Rho.

In vivo antitumor efficacy evaluation of TPRDN-encapsulated Ebd

TPRDN(Ebd) was prepared by encapsulating Ebd into tDNAn following the assembly procedure described above. Patient-derived xenografts (PDX) model mice were established by transplanting the TNBC tissues into M-NSG mice, following the procedures outlined in the animal assessment section. Then, the mice were intravenously administered with saline, Ebd, TPRDN, and TPRDN(Ebd) every 3 days, respectively. Tumor volume and body weight of the mice were recorded every 3 days. Finally, the mice were sacrificed after three weeks to collect tumors and vital organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney. Immunofluorescence analysis of Ki-67 expression was performed to assess tumor proliferation across experimental groups. Additionally, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was employed to evaluate potential histological damage to major organs induced by the respective therapeutic agents.

To investigate the inhibitory effect of TPRDN(Ebd) on the metastasis of TNBC, we established pre-clinical metastasis model as described in animal studies. Then, the mice were treated with normal saline, TPRDN, Ebd, and TPRDN(Ebd) respectively through tail vein injection every 3 days. Finally, the mice were sacrificed and their lung tissues were harvested to estimate the lung metastasis.

Statistical analysis

In our study, Statistical comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for multi-group comparisons. These analyses were performed using Stata/SE 13.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) or GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). In bioinformatics analyses, continuous variables were compared between two groups using Student's t-tests for normally distributed data and Mann-Whitney Wilcoxon tests for non-normally distributed data. ANOVA was used to analyze variance across multiple groups. For survival analysis, Kaplan - Meier curves were plotted, and log-rank tests were carried out to compare the survival distributions among different groups. P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
