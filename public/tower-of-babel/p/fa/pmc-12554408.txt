Association of Long‐Term Body Mass Index Variability With the Development of Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction and Heart Failure With Reduced Ejection Fraction Across Patterns of Weight Change
Authors: Zeshui Yu, Yuqing Chen, Oshin Miranda, Meiyuzhen Qi, Manling Zhang, Ning Feng, Timothy P. Ryan, Nanette Cathrin Schloot, Yu Chen, Flora Sam, Lirong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBody mass index (BMI) variability is a risk factor for various adverse cardiovascular outcomes. However, the specific associations between BMI variability and the risk of developing heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) versus heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), particularly across multiple weight change trends, remain unexplored.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed electronic health records from a sample of patients with overweight or obesity. BMI variability was assessed over a 5‐year period using average successive variability, with patients categorized into 3 groups: weight loss, stable weight, and weight gain. We subsequently followed 50 812 eligible patients to monitor the incidence of HFpEF and HFrEF. Cox regression models were applied to examine the differential association between BMI variability and HFpEF and HFrEF risk.ResultsOver a median follow‐up of 4.52 years, 2128 patients developed HFpEF and 1121 developed HFrEF. After adjusting for relevant confounders, elevated BMI variability was associated with an increased risk of HFpEF. The hazard ratios (HRs) of HFpEF for each 1‐SD increment in average successive variability of BMI were 1.37 (95% CI, 1.17–1.59) in the weight loss group and 1.24 (95% CI, 1.11–1.40) in the stable weight group. Additionally, when analyzed as a binary variable divided by the median, BMI variability above the median was associated with higher risks of HFpEF compared with those below the median, with the corresponding HRs being 1.47 (95% CI, 1.21–1.79) for the weight loss group and 1.20 (95% CI, 1.06–1.35) for the stable weight group. Among patients who experienced weight gain, BMI variability was not significantly associated with the risk of incident HFpEF or HFrEF (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 0.88–1.53]; modeled as a continuous variable).ConclusionsIn this large cohort of individuals living with overweight or obesity, greater variability in BMI was significantly associated with a higher risk of developing HFpEF among those with reduced or stable weight.

Heart failure (HF) is a major public health problem, with an estimated global prevalence of >64 million adults. 1 , 2 , 3 Similarly, the prevalence and incidence of HF continue to increase in the United States, affecting ≈6.2 million adults. In the HF population, HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for >50%, compared with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), with the proportion of HFpEF relative to HFrEF increasing at a concerning rate of 1% per year. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Obesity‐associated cardiometabolic disorders in an aging population are accelerating and are associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. The obesity rate is expected to be ≈50% by 2035 in aging populations, but this is likely an underestimation, given that the burgeoning prevalence of overweight and obesity among children and adolescents is not considered in this calculation. 9 Almost 40 million children <5 years old and 340 million children between 5 and 19 years old are estimated to be affected by overweight or obesity worldwide. 10

There remains considerable heterogeneity in the trajectories of weight change among individuals living with overweight and obesity. 11 , 12 Distinct patterns of weight change differentially impact cardiovascular health. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Weight loss often reflects either exacerbation of existing illness or adherence to the lifestyle intervention, whereas weight gain frequently relates to worse metabolic profiles or physiological responses to the pharmacological treatment. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiology of HF in populations living with overweight or obesity, it is crucial to group patients according to their weight change patterns: weight loss, stable weight, and weight gain. Prior studies have demonstrated that both weight loss and weight gain independently increase the risk of various cardiovascular outcomes, highlighting the importance of distinguishing between these different weight change patterns when investigating their impact on heart failure subtypes. 17

Despite emerging evidence associating visit‐to‐visit body mass index (BMI) variability with various cardiovascular outcomes, including a composite of HF, the limited integration of BMI variability into clinical risk assessment arises from a lack of compelling evidence from real‐world clinical data. 18 , 19 , 20 Additionally, limited information is known about the differential association between BMI variability and the outcomes of incident HFpEF versus HFrEF in individuals with overweight or obesity. Similarly, HFpEF and HFrEF likely have distinct pathophysiological mechanisms that underlie each respective obesity phenotype. Evidence suggests that obesity may be a stronger risk factor for HFpEF, particularly in women. 21 We sought to investigate the association between long‐term BMI variability and the risk of developing a new onset of HFpEF versus HFrEF in patients with overweight and obesity, using longitudinal clinical data collected from an academic hospital setting in Pennsylvania.

METHODS

In this retrospective cohort study, we randomly selected 100 000 patients with overweight and obesity who had an initial BMI record ≥27 kg/m2 on an index date and were followed for at least 5 years between January 1, 2004, and October 31, 2021, from the electronic health records at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center. These patients were subsequently divided into 4 equal groups of 25 000 subjects based on their initial BMI values. The groups were: overweight (27 kg/m2≤BMI<30 kg/m2), class I obesity (30 kg/m2≤BMI<35 kg/m2), class II obesity (35 kg/m2≤BMI<40 kg/m2), and class III obesity (BMI≥40 kg/m2). This BMI record on the index date is defined as the initial BMI value, which represents the earliest BMI measurement that meets the criteria of the individual patient's group and serves as the beginning of the BMI variability measurement phase. The BMI variability measurement period was defined as the 5 years following the initial BMI value, using BMI records from electronic health records during this 5‐year period to create an independent variable representing each patient's BMI variability. As shown in Figure 1, the baseline was defined as the end of the BMI variability measurement period (ie, 5 years), from which incident HF was then evaluated. There were 2807 patients excluded from this analysis because of data input errors. Patients were excluded if they did not have at least 1 BMI record per calendar year during the 5‐year BMI variability measurement period (n=18 468). Given our focus on weight change patterns and BMI variability, this criterion was applied to ensure consistent and continuous BMI measurement throughout the study. Other exclusion criteria included a prior history of bariatric surgery (n=2647) and cancer (n=11 654), preexisting HF before and during the BMI variability measurement period of 5 years (n=8793), subsequent unclassified HF during the follow‐up period (n=953), missing covariates (n=2446), and missing information on zip code and related neighborhood socioeconomic status (n=1474). We ended up including 50 812 eligible individuals in this study (Figure 2). The study received approval from the institutional review board at the University of Pittsburgh (STUDY19020153, approved on March 13, 2019). The institutional review board concluded that the research activity does not meet the definition of human subject research as outlined by the US Department of Health and Human Services and US Food and Drug Administration regulations. Due to the inclusion of patient information and to uphold privacy standards, the data collected for this study are not available. The study protocol and programming code for this analysis are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines for cohort studies, with the use of the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data extension (Table S1). 22

Measurement of Variability in BMI

We calculated individual‐level BMI variability during the measurement period using average successive variability (ASV) as described in the study by Petria et al. 23 ASV is the mean of absolute differences between consecutive BMI measurements. This method emphasizes time‐sensitive changes by focusing on sequential changes as well as overall variability. ASV was selected as the variable of interest because a prior systematic review and meta‐analysis revealed that it is the most frequently used measure for assessing BMI variability in the current body of published research. 16 , 20

Definition of Incident HF Subtypes

The incidence of new‐onset HF was identified as the first‐time diagnosis of clinical HF based on the presence of corresponding International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10) codes during the follow‐up. ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 are code sets for classifying diseases and health problems (Table S2). Patients were followed from baseline to the earliest occurrence of an HF diagnosis or HF event, death, the last encounter date, October 31, 2021, or a maximum of 7.41 years of follow‐up, whichever occurred first. The 7.41‐year follow‐up maximum was selected based on the follow‐up distribution, at which point only 10% of patients remained at risk. The measure of left ventricle ejection fraction was obtained from the most recent echocardiography test within a year of incident HF and was used to determine HF subtypes. HFpEF was defined as left ventricle ejection fraction >50%, whereas HFrEF was defined as left ventricle ejection fraction ≤50%. 24

Covariates

Covariates included sex, race, baseline age, smoking status, the number of BMI records and mean BMI during the measurement period, baseline BMI categories, and comorbidities (atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, atrial fibrillation, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, and obstructive sleep apnea). The mean±SD of heart rate and systolic blood pressure during the BMI variability measurement period were included. Additionally, ∆BMI changes were measured by the difference between the initial BMI and the baseline BMI over the BMI variability measurement period. The use of any antiobesity medication during the BMI variability measurement phase, including phentermine, phentermine/topiramate, naltrexone/bupropion, orlistat, liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, lixisenatide, or semaglutide, was considered as a covariate. We calculated neighborhood socioeconomic status using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality socioeconomic status index formula, a weighted combination of housing density, home value, unemployment, poverty rate, median income, and educational attainment. 25 BMI, systolic blood pressure, and heart rate records below the 0.02 percentile or above the 99.8 percentile were removed. BMI and systolic blood pressure records with an absolute difference >1.5 times the interquartile range from the previous records were also excluded. Additionally, any BMI that remained unchanged from previous records was removed, because BMI was documented to 2 decimal points in the records.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized as means±SDs for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Baseline demographics and characteristics were compared across 3 groups of patients with distinct patterns of BMI change: weight loss (∆BMI change of <−5%); stable weight (∆BMI change between ≥−5% and +5%), and weight gain (∆BMI change of >+5%). The threshold of 5% weight loss was adopted based on evidence indicating improved cardiometabolic traits and cardiovascular outcomes in patients achieving it and is commonly used as the definition of clinically significant weight loss in clinical trials. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Statistical comparisons were performed using the χ2 test for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables among these groups. The granular percentage weight change patterns were analyzed alongside the percentage of patients within each initial BMI category. The percentage of weight change was calculated and classified into the following categories: −10% or less, >−10% to −5%, >−5% to −2.5%, >−2.5% to 0%, >0% to 2.5%, >2.5% to 5%, >5% to 10%, and ≥10%.

We first conducted a Cox regression analysis on the entire study cohort to explore the relationship between 5‐year BMI variability and the risk of developing either HFpEF or HFrEF during the follow‐up phase. The cause‐specific Cox regression models treated competing events, including death and alternative heart failure subtypes (eg, HFrEF was treated as a competing event when HFpEF was the outcome of interest), as censoring events. These models provided adjusted HRs for the risk of developing each HF subtype while accounting for the influence of competing risks on event occurrence. To address the clinically significant heterogeneity in weight trajectories among individuals with overweight and obesity, we conducted stratified analyses across 3 distinct BMI change patterns (weight loss, stable weight, and weight gain). This analytical approach revealed differential associations between BMI variability and HF subtype risk within specific weight trajectory phenotypes. By examining these relationships in stratified subgroups, we could uncover more nuanced insights into the complex physiological mechanisms through which BMI fluctuations may distinctly influence the development of HFpEF versus HFrEF, potentially reflecting different underlying pathophysiological processes across varying weight change patterns. Individuals were followed for up to 7.41 years, until the earliest occurrence of a HF diagnosis or event, death, last healthcare encounter, or October 31, 2021, whichever occurred first. BMI variability, measured by ASV, was examined as continuous values as well as medians and quartiles. In the subgroup analyses, the median and quartile cutoff values for BMI variability were recalculated based on the distribution within each subgroup. HRs were reported for each SD increase in continuous measures and compared across categories, using the below‐median group or the lowest quartile as the reference. All models were adjusted for the covariates listed above. Incidence rates of HF were determined by dividing the total number of patients diagnosed with first‐time HF by the total person‐years accumulated during the follow‐up period. We also sought to conduct analyses to assess the sex and race differences in the relationship between BMI variability and the incidence of HFpEF and HFrEF in the entire study cohort and across different weight change patterns.

Furthermore, we assessed the association between baseline BMI categories and the incidence of HF subtypes according to different BMI change patterns. These analyses were conducted using cause‐specific Cox proportional hazard models in 3 separate BMI ∆‐change pattern groups, adjusted for age, sex, race, smoking status, neighborhood socioeconomic status, the number of BMI records, and all comorbidities. Statistical significance was established at a 2‐sided P value of <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9 and SAS software.

RESULTS

During the mean follow‐up period of 4.52 years, a total of 3249 patients had a new diagnosis of HF, with 2128 cases classified as HFpEF and 1121 cases classified as HFrEF. Table 1 displays the baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the study patients by BMI change pattern. In comparison with individuals who either lost or maintained their weight, individuals who gained weight were younger (53.15±15.14 years). Female patients were more likely than male patients to experience weight gain or weight loss. Those who experienced weight loss had higher rates of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and type 2 diabetes at the baseline compared with patients with stable weight or those who gained weight. Conversely, individuals who gained weight had the highest rate of obstructive sleep apnea. Patients with weight gain or weight loss exhibited higher average heart rate (mean and SD) and greater BMI variability compared with those who maintained a stable weight. Additionally, individuals who gained weight had a significantly lower proportion of antiobesity medication use compared with those who lost their weight. Differences in baseline demographics and characteristics of the study cohort based on quartiles of ASV and HF subtypes are provided in Tables S3 and S4 in the Supplement. Detailed information on the use of antiobesity medications by weight change pattern is provided in Table S5.

Table 2 presents the adjusted HRs of ASV of BMI for the incident HFpEF and HFrEF in the entire study cohort and within subgroups of 3 patterns of BMI changes. In the entire study cohort, a significant association was found between increasing BMI variability and the risk of incident HFpEF and HFrEF. Association of BMI ASV With Incident HFpEF and HFrEF in the Study Cohort and Across Weight Change Patterns

The models were adjusted for age, sex, race, smoking status, nSES, the number of BMI records and comorbidities (atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, obstructive sleep apnea, and type 2 diabetes), the mean and SD of heart rate and systolic blood pressure, ∆ weight change, and use of antiobesity medications. ASV indicates average successive variability; BMI, body mass index; HFpEF, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction; HFrEF, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; nSES, neighborhood socioeconomic status; and Q, quartile.

When the study cohort was grouped by weight‐change patterns, continuous BMI variability was positively associated with an increased risk of developing HFpEF in both the weight loss and stable weight groups. The HRs of HFpEF for each 1‐SD increment in ASV of BMI were 1.37 (95% CI, 1.17–1.59) in the weight loss group and 1.24 (95% CI, 1.11–1.40) in the stable weight group. However, in the weight gain group, there was no significant association between BMI variability and incident HFpEF or HFrEF.

When BMI variability was measured as a categorical variable, patients in the above‐median BMI variability group had an increased risk of incident HFpEF compared with those below the median, with HRs of 1.47 (95% CI, 1.21–1.79) for the weight loss group and 1.20 (95% CI, 1.06–1.35) for the stable weight group. Additionally, among individuals in the weight loss group, those in the third and fourth quartiles of ASV had a higher risk of developing incident HFpEF compared with those in the first quartile, with an HR of 1.33 (95% CI, 1.01–1.74) and 1.69 (95% CI, 1.27–2.24), respectively. In the stable weight group, individuals in the third and fourth quartiles of ASV had an HR of 1.20 (95% CI, 1.01–1.42) and 1.31 (95% CI, 1.10–1.56) for developing incident HFpEF, compared with those in the first quartile. No significant associations were observed between categorical BMI variability and incident HF in the weight gain group. Association of Baseline BMI With HFpEF and HFrEF

We examined the relationship between baseline BMI categories and the risk of HF subtypes, as reported in Table 3. Patients with severe obesity at baseline, defined as obesity class III, exhibited a significantly increased risk of incident HFpEF in the entire study cohort and across all 3 subgroups. Specifically, the HRs for obesity class III, compared with those with a BMI <27, were 2.34 (95% CI, 1.83–2.98) in the entire study cohort, 2.30 (95% CI, 1.63–3.24) in the weight loss group, and 2.12 (95% CI, 1.39–3.24) in the stable weight group. Furthermore, the HR for HFpEF associated with obesity class III relative to obesity class I was 2.27 (95% CI, 1.41–3.56). Patients in obesity class II displayed a 1.67‐fold increased risk of incident HFpEF compared with those with a BMI <27 in the weight loss group. Those in obesity classes I and II faced higher risks for incident HFpEF compared with patients categorized as overweight in the weight gain group, with HRs of 1.46 (95% CI, 1.09–1.96) and 1.75 (95% CI, 1.09–2.81), indicating a trend of escalating HRs with higher obesity classes.

DISCUSSION

A comprehensive study was conducted to examine the relationship between long‐term BMI variability and the risk of developing HFpEF and HFrEF in a large cohort of individuals living with overweight and obesity, with particular consideration given to their weight change patterns. The primary findings revealed that greater BMI variability was independently associated with an increased risk of both HFpEF and HFrEF within this cohort. These associations remained significant even after adjusting for all potential confounding factors, including baseline BMI and ∆ changes in BMI over time. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated that the link between BMI variability and future HFpEF risk persisted in patients who lost or maintained their weight. Among patients who experienced weight gain, BMI variability was not significantly associated with the risk of incident HFpEF or HFrEF. These findings expand the previous evidence that has focused primarily on the association between BMI variability and other cardiovascular diseases and a composite of HF. 18 , 19 , 20 Our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the complex mechanisms of HF subtypes and may inform obesity management strategies.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to explore the link between BMI variability and incident HFpEF and HFrEF. Several previous studies provided evidence of the association of weight variability on the risk of a composite of HF, mostly in patients with type 2 diabetes, although granular analyses focusing on HF subtypes were not performed due to the absence of noninvasive imaging data, such as echocardiograms or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. 18 , 19 , 30 , 31 The availability of echocardiogram reports in this study permitted accurate identification based on the specific subtype of incident HF cases. It is important to note that HF is a clinical syndrome and cannot be solely identified by a normal left ventricle ejection fraction. Importantly, in our study, patients were selected from hospital electronic health records and cases identified with a confirmed diagnostic assessment of HF based on ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 coding. Therefore, we addressed the current gap in evidence on the relationship between BMI variability and the risks of HFpEF and HFrEF in a large cohort of individuals experiencing obesity and overweight over an extended period. Additionally, BMI variability was captured using frequent measurements, averaging 19.66 records per person, collected across various clinical settings (inpatient and outpatient visits). This approach provided a detailed and accurate picture of weight changes and BMI variability over time. Our finding that BMI variability significantly increases the risk of developing HFpEF and HFrEF is in line with previous studies that have shown a positive relationship between BMI variability and cardiovascular diseases, including HF in general. 18 , 19 , 20

The findings of this study have significant clinical implications for both healthcare providers and individuals with overweight and obesity. In clinical settings, weight reduction counseling is commonly recommended to control risk factors for obesity‐related diseases and mortality. 30 Previous studies have reported that BMI variability, regardless of the direction of weight change, can predict adverse cardiovascular events. 18 , 19 However, obesity and weight change patterns differ among individuals due to various factors like genetics, unsustainable diets, psychological influences, metabolic rate changes, medication effects, medical procedures, or hormonal changes. Our approach enables more granular analysis of how distinct weight change patterns, such as weight loss, stable weight, and weight gain, impact the risk and progression of HFpEF and HFrEF in patients with varying BMI trajectories. By addressing each group's unique weight trajectory, this approach offers valuable insights into patient outcomes and informs the development of tailored strategies for managing cardiovascular risks associated with BMI variability. Our findings offer real‐world evidence underscoring the importance of minimizing body weight fluctuations when attempting to lose weight or maintain a stable weight, thereby mitigating potential adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

Moreover, because antiobesity medications are increasingly used alongside lifestyle changes for obesity management, our findings provide valuable supportive evidence for the importance of adherence to these treatments for stable and continuous weight loss. Our study suggests that reducing weight cycling could help achieve the optimal effectiveness of intervention in preventing HF.

The patient characteristics of our randomly selected group of patients align with the widely recognized pattern of higher obesity rates among women. Our study also confirmed that baseline BMI is a risk factor for HF, with a stronger correlation to HFpEF than to HFrEF. 21 This association with HFpEF is particularly pronounced in patients who experienced weight gain, indicating that a higher BMI increases the risk of developing HFpEF. Interestingly, there was no significant association between BMI variability and the new onset of either HFpEF or HFrEF in the weight gain group.

The exact mechanisms between BMI variability and HF, particularly HFpEF, remain incompletely understood. These mechanisms are likely complex and involve multiple factors, especially considering the prevalence of comorbidities and the markedly different clinical features and outcomes of HFpEF and HFrEF, especially in response to standard medical treatments. One potential mechanism may involve the long‐term effects of body weight fluctuation on the development and progression of metabolic syndrome, because patients with metabolic syndrome are known to have a substantially higher risk of developing HFpEF. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Another potential pathway could involve chronic, low‐grade systemic inflammation, as evidenced by higher concentrations of C‐reactive protein in patients who experience large weight fluctuations. 36 Preclinical studies have shown that weight cycling (ie, repeated weight loss followed by regain) disrupts energy homeostasis, increasing hunger and decreasing satiety hormones, resulting in adipocyte hyperplasia and maladaptive excess visceral fat accumulation. 37 Thus, the adipocyte hyperplasia and weight regain associated with weight cycling enhance the inflammatory capacity of the adipose tissue mass. Further research needs to uncover precise mechanisms.

The influence of lifestyle factors and genetic variation on body composition and BMI variability over time is an important consideration when interpreting the results of our study. Although our observational design and use of real‐world data present challenges in directly assessing these factors, it is crucial to acknowledge their potential impact. Lifestyle factors such as diet, physical activity, stress, and sleep have been shown to substantially affect body composition and weight trajectories. 38 , 39 Moreover, genetic differences between individuals can contribute to variability in metabolism, fat distribution, and response to lifestyle interventions. 40 Addressing the complex interplay of lifestyle, genetics, and BMI variability in future studies, particularly prospective studies and randomized controlled trials, is helpful for developing targeted, evidence‐based weight management recommendations.

The present study has the following limitations. (1) It is unknown whether the observed weight changes in individuals were intentional or unintentional. This distinction is important, because intentional weight loss from lifestyle changes is seen to be beneficial, whereas unintentional weight loss may indicate serious illness or metabolic disorders. (2) This study specifically focused on classifying the HF by ejection fraction, but other HF classifications, such as New York Heart Failure classification, acute versus chronic, ischemic versus nonischemic, or primary myocardial disease versus secondary neurohormonal activity, were not available due to the lack of data. 41 (3) Because this was a retrospective observational study, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the results. Additionally, limited significant association was found between BMI variability and the incidence of HFrEF across weight change pattern, which may be attributed to the high heterogeneity among individuals with overweight or obesity and the diverse presentations of the HFrEF phenotype. (4) Although our analysis incorporated established risk factors for HF, we acknowledge that we lack comprehensive data on socioeconomic conditions, exercise habits, health care coverage, and menopausal status, all of which are now known to significantly impact cardiovascular outcomes independent of body mass. (5) The use of BMI as a measure of obesity has both strengths and limitations that are important to consider when interpreting our findings. BMI offers a quick, inexpensive, and widely used method to categorize weight status and estimate body fat in large epidemiological studies such as ours. However, BMI is an indirect measure that does not distinguish between lean mass and fat mass, nor does it capture the distribution of body fat. The results of our study should be interpreted in the context of the strengths and limitations of BMI as an obesity measure. (6) Electronic health records often contain data entry errors, which could lead to the underdiagnosis of certain comorbidities. Data were obtained from a single academic institution; therefore, generalizing our findings may present challenges.

CONCLUSIONS

In this retrospective study of a large cohort of patients with overweight and obesity, we observed that greater variability in BMI was associated with a higher risk of developing HFpEF and HFrEF, particularly with HFpEF in patients who maintained a stable weight or lost weight. Our findings suggest that these patients should minimize weight cycling while attempting to reduce or maintain their body weight.
