Impact of Intracranial Atherosclerosis and Cerebral Small Vessel Disease on Overt Vascular Events During Antithrombotic Therapy
Authors: Kaori Miwa, Masatoshi Koga, Kanta Tanaka, Yusuke Yakushiji, Makoto Sasaki, Kohsuke Kudo, Sohei Yoshimura, Masafumi Ihara, Shigeru Fujimoto, Tadashi Terasaki, Yukako Yazawa, Yoshinari Nagakane, Hiroshi Yamagami, Kazutoshi Nishiyama, Yoshiki Yagita, Shinichi Yoshimura, Teruyuki Hirano, Kazunori Toyoda, BAT2 Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionTo investigate the association of intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD), alone and comorbid with cerebral small vessel disease (SVD), with ischemic or hemorrhagic events in patients receiving antithrombotic therapy.Patients and MethodsIn this prospective, multicenter, observational study, baseline brain MRI was performed to assess SVD (white matter hyperintensities, cerebral microbleeds, lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces [PVS]), nonlacunar infarcts, and ICAD. SVD burden was defined as SVD score > 2. ICAD was classified as normal‐to‐mild, moderate, and severe stenosis‐to‐occlusion. The outcomes were any ischemic event, ischemic stroke, major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and all‐cause mortality. We assessed associations of ICAD with outcomes via Cox regression and mediation analyses, adjusting for SVD burden.ResultsAmong 5250 patients (mean age: 71 ± 11 years, 33% women), 3947 (75%) received antiplatelets and 1304 (25%) anticoagulants at baseline. ICAD was normal‐to‐mild in 3781 (72%), moderate in 571 (11%), and severe‐to‐occluded in 894 (17%). SVD burden was observed in 1400 (27%). ICAD was associated with a higher frequency of non‐lacunar infarcts and a lower frequency of PVS. There was no graded association between ICAD severity and SVD burden. During a median follow‐up of 2 years, 278 ischemic events, 197 ischemic strokes, 97 major bleedings, 55 intracranial hemorrhages, and 217 deaths occurred. Severe‐to‐occluded ICAD independently increased the risk of any ischemic event (adjusted hazard ratio: 1.39 [1.03–1.86]) and mortality (2.01 [1.48–2.74]); coexisting SVD burden further increased the risk of all outcomes. ICAD directly affected ischemic events, while mortality was mainly driven by its additive interaction with SVD.ConclusionsICAD increases the risk of any ischemic events and, with comorbid SVD, contributes to hemorrhagic events and excess mortality.

Introduction

Intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) is a leading cause of stroke worldwide and is a marker of systemic large artery atherosclerosis [1, 2]. Aggressive medical management is generally recommended for the acute and long‐term phases of ICAD‐related ischemic stroke based on the results of randomized controlled trials [3]. However, despite treatment advances in recent years, the risk of stroke recurrence remains high, with the 30‐day recurrence rates ranging from 10% to 20% in clinical settings [4]. This underscores the uncertainty regarding which subgroups in the ICAD population are at higher risk for vascular events, highlighting the need for further studies to identify these subgroups and refine risk stratification strategies.

ICAD coexists with other etiologies, including extracranial atherosclerosis or cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) [5, 6]. Among these, SVD refers to a group of pathologies affecting the small cerebral vessels and is a major pathological cause for future stroke [7]. The underlying mechanisms of SVD are multifactorial, with risk factors for SVD overlapping with those of ICAD [6]. Although SVD and ICAD are theoretically expected to be encountered with the growing aging population, few studies have assessed the prevalence and natural history of ICAD with concomitant SVD. Furthermore, previous findings regarding the increased risk of vascular events in ICAD comorbid with SVD have been conflicting [5, 6, 8]. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the association of ICAD with the risk of ischemic or hemorrhagic events and to determine whether SVD mediated the relationship between ICAD and clinical outcomes among patients receiving antithrombotic therapy.

Methods

The data that support the findings of the present study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Participants

The Bleeding with Antithrombotic Therapy 2 (BAT2) was an investigator‐initiated, prospective, multicenter, observational study involving 52 hospitals across Japan. Briefly, BAT2 was designed to determine the incidence and details of bleeding complications in patients treated with oral antithrombotic therapy. The overall protocol has been published elsewhere [9, 10]. Patients with cerebrovascular or cardiovascular diseases who newly started or continued taking oral antiplatelets or anticoagulants were enrolled from stroke clinics and hospitals between 2016 and 2019. The choice and dosage of antithrombotic agents were determined by the responsible treating investigator. Antithrombotic therapies at baseline included single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, clopidogrel, or cilostazol), dual antiplatelet therapy, warfarin, and direct oral anticoagulants (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban). Brain MRI was mandatory for all participants at registration, and contraindications for MRI were exclusion criteria. The study protocol permitted inclusion of participants who underwent brain MRI within 14 days after registration. This study adhered to the STROBE reporting guidelines for cohort studies. All procedures were approved by the ethics committees of the participating sites, and written informed consent was obtained before enrollment from the patients or their family members.

Data Collection

Baseline clinical information and blood test results were collected. Brain MRI scans were acquired using either a 3 T or 1.5 T magnetic field, with the imaging planes aligned parallel to the anterior commissure‐posterior commissure line or the orbitomeatal line. MRI was performed within 90 days before or up to 14 days after registration, with imaging protocols standardized across all facilities. MRI sequences, including T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, and T2*‐weighted imaging, were acquired along with three‐dimensional time‐of‐flight magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). Details regarding the MRI acquisition and interpretation are described elsewhere [10]. All MRI data were evaluated by a central diagnostic committee, blinded to clinical information, for cerebral microbleed (CMB), white matter hyperintensity (WMH), basal ganglia‐enlarged perivascular space (BG‐PVS), lacune, cortical superficial siderosis, and nonlacunar infarct, according to the established criteria [11, 12]. Each SVD feature of CMB, confluent WMH, extensive BG‐PVS, and lacunes on MRI was summed to obtain the total SVD score (range, 0–4) [10, 13]. ICAD was assessed using MRA and initially classified into four categories: (1) normal‐to‐mild (signal reduction < 50%), (2) moderate (≥ 50%), (3) severe (focal signal loss at the stenotic lesion with distal signal present), and (4) occlusion [10]. The overall ICAD grade was determined as the most severe stenosis observed across all evaluated intracranial arteries for each participant. We expected that few participants would be categorized with ICAD ratings of occlusion. The top two categories were combined for analysis, generating three categories corresponding to normal‐to‐mild, moderate, and severe‐to‐occluded ICAD.

The intracranial arteries included the C1 to C5 segments of the internal carotid artery, M1 and M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery, A1 and A2 segments of the anterior cerebral artery, intracranial vertebral artery, basilar artery, and P1 and P2 segments of the posterior cerebral artery [10]. Detailed kappa values and concordance rates for both interrater and intrarater reliability of MRI findings were reported [10].

Outcomes

The outcomes were major bleeding, any ischemic event, ischemic stroke, and all‐cause death. Major bleeding included ICH and extracranial major bleeding [14]. ICH was further analyzed as a separate outcome, encompassing intracerebral, subdural, subarachnoid, and extradural hemorrhages. Any ischemic event included ischemic stroke, acute coronary syndrome, systemic embolism, and unplanned revascularization due to ischemia. The patients were followed up at 6 (±2), 12 (±3), 18 (±3), and 24 (±4) months after enrollment. All patients were followed up for at least 6 months. Patients were censored at the last available follow‐up or at the time of the outcome event. When a patient experienced multiple events of the same type, follow‐up was censored at the first event. Follow‐ups were conducted either in person at the clinic or via telephone interviews with the patients or their caregivers. All follow‐ups were performed by the investigator or trained study coordinators at the participating sites [9]. The patients were instructed to promptly report any events to the investigator in charge to ensure timely data collection. The investigator adjudicated the determination of the occurrence and classification of events.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics. A cross‐sectional analysis was conducted to examine the clinical associations of ICAD, using multinomial logistic regression to estimate relative risk ratios. The incidence rates of the outcome events were calculated as the total number of events/100 patient‐years of follow‐up. The risk of events was determined by calculating the hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using adjusted Cox proportional hazards models with robust sandwich error variance. Variables for multivariable analysis were selected from the available cohort data based on current knowledge of risk factors for outcomes, including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, statin use, renal impairment, prior stroke/TIA, antiplatelet therapy, and anticoagulant therapy. SVD burden (SVD score > 2) was additionally included as a covariate in a further adjusted model. The combined association of ICAD (normal‐to‐mild, moderate, severe‐to‐occluded) and SVD score (dichotomized using the median: ≤ 2, > 2) with outcomes was also investigated. Both multiplicative and additive interactions were examined. The product term and the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) were employed to investigate multiplicative and additive interactions, respectively. To investigate whether the association between ICAD and outcomes was mediated by SVD burden, mediation analyses were conducted based on the four components of the decomposition model: controlled direct effect, pure indirect effect, reference interaction, and mediated interaction [15]. As a sensitivity analysis, the Fine and Gray model was applied, with mortality considered a competing risk, after imputing missing values using multiple imputations with chained equations (20 imputations). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 19.0; Stata Corp).

Results

Among the 5378 patients registered, 5250 patients (median age 73 [IQR: 66–79] years; 1736 [33.0%] women) were included in the analysis (Figure 1). In total, 4727 (90.0%) patients had a history of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack and were taking antithrombotic agents for secondary stroke prevention. The median interval between onset and registration was 73 (IQR: 15–1446) days. The remaining 523 (10.0%) patients received antithrombotic therapy for primary stroke prevention or secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases. Among the antiplatelet agents, aspirin was used in 1673 (32%) patients, clopidogrel in 1990 (37%), and cilostazol in 777 (15%). Clopidogrel use was consistently associated with ICAD severity (Table S1). Tables 1 and 2 summarize the baseline characteristics and MRI findings according to ICAD severity. Normal‐to‐mild ICAD was observed in 3781 (72.1%) patients; moderate ICAD, 571 (10.9%) patients; and severe‐to‐occluded ICAD, 894 (17%) patients. The median SVD score was 2 [IQR: 1–3] across the ICAD groups (Table 2). There was no graded association between ICAD severity and SVD burden (Table 2, Figure 2). Although moderate ICAD showed a modestly higher SVD score, severe ICAD was inversely associated. A similar pattern was observed for SVD score > 2. Among individual SVD markers, moderate ICAD was associated with a higher frequency of lacunes (≥ 5) and DSWMH (≥ 2), whereas these associations were not observed in severe‐to‐occluded ICAD. In contrast, the frequency of non‐lacunar infarcts increased, while that of BG‐PVS decreased with ICAD severity (Table 2).

During a median follow‐up of 2 [1.80–2.03] years, the frequently observed outcomes were 278 ischemic events (197 ischemic strokes, 37 acute coronary syndrome, 9 systemic embolism, 35 unplanned revascularization due to ischemia) and 217 all‐cause deaths (36 stroke deaths, 46 malignancies, 34 infections, 101 others or unknown). A total of 93 major bleeding events, including 55 ICH and 38 major extracranial bleeding events (24 gastrointestinal bleeding events), were observed. Of the 55 ICH, 24 (45.0%) were intracerebral hemorrhage; 20 cases were subdural hematoma; 9 cases were subarachnoid hemorrhage.

The cumulative incidence rates of any ischemic event and all‐cause death were higher in patients with severe‐to‐occluded ICAD than in those with normal‐to‐mild ICAD (Figure 3), whereas no significant association was observed for ischemic stroke or bleeding events. ICAD severity was associated with non‐stroke death (normal‐to‐mild 113 [3.0%], moderate 18 [3.2%], severe‐to‐occluded ICAD 50 [5.5%]; p for trend = < 0.001) but not with stroke death (22 [0.5%], 4 [0.7%], 10 [1.1%], respectively; p for trend = 0.09). After adjustment for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, statin medication, renal impairment, prior stroke/TIA, antiplatelet, anticoagulants, and SVD burden, severe‐to‐occluded ICAD remained independently associated with any ischemic event (HR 1.39 [95% CI: 1.03–1.86]) and all‐cause death (2.01 [1.48–2.74]). ICAD had a borderline significant association with ischemic stroke (1.34 [0.95–1.91]) but had no significant association with bleeding events (Table 3). In contrast, SVD burden was consistently associated with an increased risk of all adverse outcomes after adjusting for covariates (Table 3). The imputed Fine and Gray model yielded consistent results (Table S2).

In the joint associations of SVD burden and ICAD categories with outcome, normal‐to‐mild and severe‐to‐occluded ICAD, when combined with SVD burden, were associated with all events, whereas moderate ICAD with SVD burden was associated with ischemic stroke (Table 4). No multiplicative interactions were observed between ICAD and SVD across all events. In contrast, a significant additive interaction between ICAD and SVD was identified for mortality (RERI: 2.71; 95% CI: 0.68–4.72) (Table 4).

Discussion

The current study found that severe‐to‐occluded ICAD was associated with any ischemic event and mortality independent of SVD, exerted a direct effect on any ischemic event, and conferred an excess mortality risk through an additive interaction with SVD. When combined with SVD burden, ICAD was associated with ischemic or hemorrhagic events, with the latter outcomes primarily driven by the SVD burden. No dose–response relationship was observed between ICAD severity and SVD score, and ICAD severity was inversely associated with the frequency of BG‐PVS.

The current study supports the potential role of ICAD as a risk marker for generalized atherosclerotic burden associated with any ischemic event across multiple vascular territories, and with mortality. This study is the first to assess the effect of ICAD and SVD on adverse outcomes in patients receiving antithrombotic agents. The mechanisms underlying the association between ICAD and vascular events despite best medical treatments are poorly understood. ICAD can coexist with systemic atherosclerosis affecting different arterial beds. In addition to the atherosclerotic plaque and fibromuscular intima thickening in the large brain arteries, ICAD represents a chronic inflammatory disorder that predominantly affects arterial segments exposed to high endothelial shear stress. It is further associated with the propagation of inflammatory cytokines and activation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase, both of which precipitate clinical events [16]. Accordingly, ICAD is a proxy measure of underlying risk factors that include atherosclerosis and residual risks such as inflammatory, environmental, and genetic predispositions.

Our study demonstrates that SVD burden exerts a main effect on clinical outcomes, regardless of comorbid ICAD status, highlighting its potential as a predictive marker. These findings were confirmed using a large dataset, further underscoring the clinical significance of SVD burden. The coexistence of SVD burden shifted the risk profile toward hemorrhagic complications, including both intracranial and systemic hemorrhagic events in patients with ICAD. This suggests that in patients with coexisting ICAD and SVD, the hemorrhagic risk conferred by SVD may compound the conventional ischemic risk attributed to ICAD. Moreover, previous studies have shown that patients with large artery stroke or those receiving anticoagulation therapy for atrial fibrillation who have coexistent overt SVD have a twofold higher risk of recurrent stroke compared to those without SVD [8, 17]. SVD‐specific mechanisms that increase the risk of stroke include diffuse and variable arteriopathy; blood–brain barrier disruption; and the limited efficacy of antithrombotic therapy. Given the observed additive interaction in mortality, patients with both ICAD and SVD were more likely to have other comorbidities and may have been at an increased risk of mortality, reinforcing that large and small vessel diseases could amplify adverse outcomes.

Notably, there was no graded association between ICAD severity and SVD burden. Moderate ICAD was associated with non‐hemorrhagic SVD, including lacunes and severe DSWMH; these associations, though not fully elucidated, may be attributable to shared risk factors such as older age and hypertension. This lack of a dose–response relationship suggests that SVD is not necessarily driven by intracranial atherosclerotic disease, challenging the putative notion that ICAD specifically contributes to small vessel pathology. Most previous reports have focused on SVD prevalence based on ICAD with ≥ 50% stenosis (most often considered as binary variables of present or absent), thus limiting comparisons [18]. The pathological study indicates that intracranial atherosclerosis can cause luminal stenosis; however, most advanced atherosclerotic plaques in intracranial arteries demonstrate less than 40% stenosis [19]. Stenoses exceeding 40% are largely attributable to fibromuscular intimal thickening and are commonly unrelated to atheromatous plaques [19]. This pathological observation may explain the lack of a proportional relationship between the ICAD severity and SVD features. Notably, severe ICAD exhibited an inverse relationship with BG‐PVS, consistent with findings from population‐based studies indicating that PVS and other SVD features were linked to intracranial arterial dolichoectasia [20, 21, 22]. Brain arterial aging likely contributes to arterial stiffness by reducing the elastic modulus of the arterial wall, promoting the development of intracranial arterial dolichoectasia in large arteries. Subsequently, dilatation of the PVS around brain capillaries and arterioles reflects increased endothelial hemodynamic stress and shares a common pathophysiological mechanism with dolichoectasia. This supports the finding that the significantly lower prevalence of BG‐PVS in ICAD may reflect these divergent pathways.

The strength of our study lies in the large sample size and multicenter recruitment in a real‐world setting world clinical setting. We used standardized multimodal MRI sequences, rated for SVD and ICAD, using validated scales by a central diagnostic radiology committee, ensuring high reliability in neuroimaging interpretation. This study also has limitations, including its observational design. Residual confounding factors from unmeasured variables, such as socioeconomic status and inclusion of asymptomatic ICAD cases, may have affected the results. Second, the arterial wall could not be visualized on MRA. Thus, the lumen‐based measurement may have underestimated both atherosclerotic burden and plaque‐related outward remodeling. Third, ICAD severity was recorded as the worst stenosis grade across all evaluated arteries, and vessel‐specific data were not available. Therefore, we could not assess the spatial relationship between individual arterial stenosis and the territory of index or recurrent strokes, or the distribution of small vessel lesions such as lacunes and WMH. Fourth, bleeding or ischemic risk may vary between individual antithrombotic agents and their exposure times. Finally, almost all participants were Asians, which might limit the generalizability of the results to other ethnicities.

Individuals with severe‐to‐occluded ICAD remain at elevated risk of any ischemic event and all‐cause mortality despite long‐term antithrombotic therapy. Coexisting SVD burden was associated with an increased risk of clinical events. These findings underscore the need for an integrated approach incorporating ICAD and SVD to identify high‐risk groups. Given the absence of a graded correlation between ICAD and SVD burden, elucidating their respective pathogenic mechanisms and developing targeted preventive strategies are warranted. Rigorous large‐scale studies are necessary to identify and evaluate the efficacy of potential interventions in this population.
