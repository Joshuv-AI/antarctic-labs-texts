Design and Implementation of an Automated Interpretation Algorithm for Lupus Anticoagulant Functional Testing
Authors: Chiara Novelli, Arianna Gatti, Flora Ierna, Elena Riccardi, Irene Cuppari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionLupus anticoagulant (LA) testing is essential, albeit complex, in the laboratory diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). Given the multi‐step workflow and the variability introduced by anticoagulant therapy, reagent differences, and interpretive approaches, result interpretation requires expert evaluation. To address these challenges, we implemented a middleware‐based automated algorithm in our four‐hospital institution using the HemoHub system, guided by the latest ISTH recommendations. The objectives were to automate reflex testing, standardize interpretation, and support clinical decision‐making.MethodsThe algorithm incorporated rules aligned with good laboratory practice for LA diagnostics, including reflex testing, result interpretation, internal comments, integration of historical data, and auto‐validation of negative cases. Retrospective validation was performed on 190 historical cases in which the diagnosis was made conventionally and corroborated by follow‐up data. A subsequent prospective blind comparison with manual operator‐based workflow was conducted on 481 routine samples.ResultsConcordance between automated and manual interpretations reached 100% for LA negative and positive cases, with slightly lower agreement for first‐time positives (88.6% and 82.4%). When automated interpretation was not assignable, the system still provided useful internal comments. Additionally, 58.4% of samples were auto‐validated, significantly reducing manual workload. Time comparison demonstrated substantial savings for both experienced and less‐experienced pathologists.ConclusionThe implementation of the automated algorithm improved consistency, reduced interpretation time, and minimized intra‐ and inter‐laboratory variability. By integrating clinical context and historical data, it enhanced diagnostic accuracy. These findings support the use of middleware‐based, rule‐driven interpretation as a reliable and efficient approach to standardizing LA testing and optimizing laboratory workflow.

Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease defined when at least one clinical and one laboratory criterion are met [1]. The clinical criterion includes arterial, venous, or microvascular thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, or non‐thrombotic manifestations [2]. For the presence of the laboratory criterion, persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) must be demonstrable in a patient's plasma. The laboratory detection of aPL consists of phospholipid‐dependent coagulation tests for the detection of lupus anticoagulant (LA) and immunoassays for the measurement of anti‐cardiolipin antibodies and anti‐beta‐2 glycoprotein I antibodies [3, 4].

Among aPL assays, LA analysis remains the most complex analysis with several potential pitfalls [5].

The first problem is the lack of a gold standard [6]. Probably due to the heterogeneity of antiphospholipid antibodies, a single test is not enough to identify the lupus anticoagulant, but at least two tests are needed, with many different reagents commercially available [7].

Furthermore, LA consists of a multistep procedure with a first screening step including a diluted Russell's viper venom test (dRVVT) and an APTT‐based test (e.g., SCT = Silica Clotting Time) both with low phospholipid concentration. This is followed by a mixing test using normal pooled plasma (NPP) to demonstrate the inhibitor properties of LA (for both assays), only if one or both screening tests are prolonged. Finally, LA confirm test is done with high phospholipid concentration (for both test principles) to demonstrate that the clotting time shortens down to normal as the excess phospholipid overcomes LA inhibition [8], only for initially prolonged test(s).

Since LA is based on the coagulation pathway, the dRVVT and aPTT tests may be sensitive to anticoagulant drugs such as vitamin K antagonists (VKA), heparin or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) [9]. dRVVT and SCT (screen and confirm assays) usually contain a heparin neutralizer (e.g., polybrene) to counteract potential heparin interference, whereas routine APTT reagents generally lack this component. When the presence of DOACs is suspected or known, potential interference may be removed using a DOAC adsorption product [9, 10].

These are only a part of the difficulties in standardization, and we should certainly add the cut‐off calculations [11], between‐reagent sensitivity/specificity differences, and variability of NPP values [12, 13].

In the LA testing, different diagnostic approaches can be adopted [14]. The classical approach would be to promote the use of mixing studies and place them early in the investigative pathway without going any further if the mixing study is normal. An alternative approach, as recommended by the CLSI LA guidelines [15], postpones the mixing study until after the confirm test and only if the confirm results are above the reference interval. A third approach considers only integrated testing with the exclusion of mixing studies [16].

A fourth, more recent approach comes from the latest ISTH guidelines [17, 18] which recommend performing both mixing and confirming tests if the screening test on an undiluted sample is prolonged, regardless of the result of the mixing test. This is to avoid false‐positive and false‐negative results as pointed out in the guidelines and illustrated in recent studies [19, 20, 21].

Choosing different algorithms leads to different classifications, as pointed out by Moore et al. [22]. The number of samples classified as LA‐positive could differ depending on the interpretation method [23].

The recorded discrepancies can be mainly due to samples whose screen ratios were occasionally moderately elevated, particularly when the classical algorithm is used. In this approach, a design limitation of mixing tests can generate false‐negative mixing tests due to a dilution effect. However, this pitfall can also happen with the integrated testing approach [24].

Another critical step in the LA diagnosis is the inhomogeneity of the final reporting provided by the laboratories [25]. Reporting practices vary widely: some report only numerical values obtained; others include a global conclusion about the presence or absence of LA (with or without numerical values).

The laboratory practice and the results interpretation of LA assays require the constant presence of an experienced laboratorian and a close collaboration between clinicians and laboratories to minimize the possible diagnostic errors [26].

To streamline the LA process, we decided to implement a middleware‐based process algorithm with three main objectives: to automate processes and make them more reliable, faster, and easier to perform; to facilitate the results interpretation, reducing intra‐ and inter‐laboratory variability across our four‐hospital institution; and to streamline the clinical decision‐making that can be critical for less experienced laboratorians.

Materials and Methods

Six hundred eighty‐one samples were included in this study. Data were retrieved from the middleware system (HemoHub—Werfen) after anonymization. For each sample, dRVVT screen, SCT screen, and when available, the corresponding mix and confirmatory results were extracted.

Information on anticoagulation therapy, when available in the middleware or in the patient's medical record, was also considered. In DOAC cases, a neutralizer (DOAC‐Stop—Haematex) was used, and this was considered during data analysis.

We went through several phases from the choice of the algorithm, the creation of the different rules and the validation process:

Phase 2—Create the algorithm in the middleware.

Phase 3—Perform verification testing.

Phase 4—Conduct retrospective validation testing using historical data.

Phase 5—Conduct prospective validation testing using routine samples.

Phase 6—Establish the benchmark criteria for time comparison.

Phase 7—Perform a turnaround time comparison between manual and middleware‐assisted workflows.

From Phase 2, the results are interpreted centrally by HemoHub; the manual LA interpretation in Phase 5 has been done by the same expert who defined the rules.

Phase 1—Define the Algorithm to Implement

The algorithm logic and functional requirements were defined using the latest ISTH guideline [17, 18]. The diagnostic algorithm was already uniformly applied across our hospital network since 2020, including the same testing panels and following suggested interpretation criteria, reflecting our aim to harmonize.

The following tests were used: dRVVT (screen, mixing, confirm) and SCT (screen, mixing, confirm).

dRVVT and SCT screen, mixing and confirming results were expressed as normalized ratios; the dRVVT screen/confirm and SCT screen/confirm results were expressed as normalized test ratios, generally defined as paired test LA screen/confirm ratios.

The mixing test is performed on a mixture of patient's plasma 1:1 with NPP.

For all assays, local cut‐off values were established, and thresholds were applied across all laboratories and verified locally.

The panel assays for LA start with dRVVT and SCT screening tests.

If both screening results are under cut‐off no further testing is added. When the screening test is prolonged, mixing and confirming tests are included. These tests are added automatically to the patient's profile panel (both on the middleware and the analyzer).

In some cases, only one of the two screening tests (dRVVT or SCT) may be prolonged. While in others, both may be affected. Additionally, the results of the mixing and confirmation test can vary—being either above or below cut off points—leading to multiple possible results combinations.

Some abnormal results may be influenced by interfering factors such as the presence of anticoagulants or factor deficiencies. If such interferences are not properly identified, they can potentially generate a misinterpretation of the results and, consequently, an incorrect diagnosis [6, 19].

These potential sources of interference may occur in any step of the process and can affect both positive and negative cases across different diagnostic tests.

LA negativity is defined when both screening tests (dRVVT and SCT) are under the local cut‐off, or when a screening test is prolonged, and the LA screen/confirm ratio falls below the local cut‐off value. In this case, mixing studies are performed mainly to rule out other potential causes of abnormal screening results. Generally, if the clotting time does not correct after mixing (i.e., result is above cut‐off), the reason could be an anticoagulant effect on coagulation tests or the presence of an inhibitor (factor inhibitor or LA). Inversely, if the mixing test clotting time shows correction (i.e., result is under cut‐off), a factor deficiency (either congenital or due to VKA therapy) is the more likely cause.

LA positivity is defined when at least one of the two screening tests (dRVVT and/or SCT) is prolonged, and the LA screen/confirm ratio exceeds the local cut‐off.

Mixing studies help to differentiate the cause of abnormal screening test results. If inhibition persists after mixing (i.e., the mixing does not correct with normal plasma and result is above cut‐off), this supports the presence of inhibitor effect of lupus anticoagulant antibodies. If the mixing test shows correction (i.e., result is below cut‐off), a factor deficiency is more likely (congenital or due to VKA treatment) and the positive confirmatory result may be a false‐positive [21, 22].

The algorithm was successfully developed, and all the rules described above were integrated into the middleware platform.

Phase 3—Verification Testing

It was conducted to ensure that the developed algorithm operates according to the prerequisites described in Phases 1 and 2.

Different types of historical results were analyzed, performing preliminary tests of the implemented rules to ensure their consistency with the algorithm. This step served as a “feasibility phase” to verify that the data align correctly with the functionality of each rule.

Phase 4—Validation Testing Using Historical Data

Each rule was validated using retrospective datasets.

One hundred ninety historical LA assay results were analyzed by comparing the algorithm‐generated interpretations within the middleware to the original, manually validated results.

The dataset included 50 cases with both screening tests under local cut‐off and 140 cases with one or both screening tests above the local cut‐off.

Following confirmation of the accuracy of each implemented rule, the prospective validation phase was started.

Phase 5—Prospective Validation Testing Using Routine Samples

The algorithm's automatic interpretation was directly compared with manual LA interpretations performed by an experienced operator specialized in LA assay analysis.

Four hundred eighty‐one samples were prospectively evaluated by both methods: 281 with both screening tests results under the local cut‐off and 200 with one or both screening tests above the local cut‐off.

This validation phase was conducted between April 5, 2023, and September 21, 2023.

Phase 6—Establish the Benchmark Criteria for Time Comparison

To assess potential time savings from middleware implementation, benchmark criteria were established by comparing the time required for interpretation and validation by two professionals: a senior pathologist acting as supervisor and a less experienced laboratorian.

A LA testing session involving 35 samples was independently evaluated by both individuals. The time taken to interpret and validate results was recorded for both negative and positive cases. This served as the benchmark for comparing the two workflows.

Phase 7—Turnaround Time Comparison Between Manual and Middleware‐Assisted Workflows

Using the benchmark data, a sample processing time comparison was conducted across the full dataset of 481 samples. The total time required for manual validation was estimated using the average times from Phase 6 and compared to the time spent using the middleware‐assisted, rule‐based interpretation.

The objective was to determine whether the middleware reduced the total time required for result interpretation, validation, and results turnaround for both negative and positive cases.

Results

During the initial validation phase (Phase 4), a total of 190 historical samples were evaluated. Among the 50 samples where both screening tests were above the local cut‐off, the agreement between the middleware's automatic interpretation and the historical manual interpretation was 100%.

For the remaining 140 samples, which had one or both screening tests abnormal, concordance was 100% for samples classified as LA negative (L1) and LA positive (L2).

However, for cases identified as first‐time LA positive (L3), the concordance rate was 88.6% (Table 3).

Phase 5 involved the analysis of 481 routine samples. Of these, 281 samples had both screening tests negative, and once again, concordance between automatic and manual interpretations was 100%.

Among the 200 samples with at least one abnormal screening test, 130 were correctly interpreted automatically. In these cases, the concordance rate was 100% for LA Negative (L1) and LA Positive (L2), respectively. In L3 cases, the concordance found was 82.4%.

The difference between the two interpretation criteria in L3 was mainly due to the absence of previous LA data in the middleware or due to samples from patients under DOACs therapy that became negative after treatment with DOAC‐Stop (Table 3).

In 70 out of 481 samples (14%), no automatic interpretation was assigned. These were typically cases that did not meet the criteria for either a positive or negative classification. However, the middleware still generated internal comments in these instances to assist the pathologist in interpretation. Among these 70 samples, several variables were observed, but in most cases the SCT screen was positive (about 2/3) compared with the dRVVT screen.

Considering only the samples in which the middleware provided a clear interpretation, the overall discordance rate was just 1%.

Furthermore, 58.4% of all samples (281 out of 481) were auto‐validated, and they were automatically released to the LIS without the need for manual review.

Discussion

Our data show a high concordance rate between automatic interpretations generated by the middleware versus expert manual interpretations of LA assays. The highest concordance rate was recorded with negative and positive LA (100%), whereas a lower rate was found with LA present but without previous results available for comparison (82.4%).

Notably, 86% of samples with one or both abnormal screening tests were correctly interpreted using the rules implemented in the middleware, while for the remaining 14%, an internal comment was added to assist the pathologist in interpretation. However, 1% of the total samples showed discrepant results between manual and automatic interpretation, typically due to DOAC presence.

Defining a structured set of rules and encoded interpretations ensures consistency and reduces both intra‐ and inter‐laboratory variability across our four hospital institutions. This approach was also designed to incorporate supportive test results that may help in the interpretation of abnormal screening results, a process that is often challenging in LA testing.

The algorithm also integrates historical patient data, which may include different interpretations based on previous results. For example, in a case with a current result negative but a prior result positive, a specific comment is automatically added to suggest the possibility of transient LA and to recommend further testing and evaluation within the patients' clinical context and treatments.

Another significant advantage implemented in this middleware is the ability to display clinically relevant patient information—such as previous diagnoses and anticoagulant therapies—sourced from hospital departments for inpatients or general practitioners for outpatients. This greatly enhances the accuracy and clinical relevance of result interpretation, reducing the risk of misinterpretation and misdiagnosis.

Future development could be the capability to connect additional patient clinical information to the middleware, enhancing and impacting considerably the correct APS diagnosis.

Alternative algorithms by an international expert on LA, such as those proposing a more “integrated” approach, may increase sensitivity but have been associated with potential differences in classification in borderline cases [22]. The presence of pharmacological interference is one important aspect to consider; in our practice, DOAC removal is used, as opposed to plasma dilution or temporary discontinuation of therapy, as described by Favaloro [27].

Therefore, no diagnostic algorithm is entirely free from limitations in clinically complex cases or in the presence of pre‐analytical interferences.

The use of automated rules reduces the time required by both laboratory technicians and pathologists for interpreting results, making the interpretation process easier in case of complex abnormal results.

This approach also supports in developing expertise through repeated use and familiarization with the method. It serves both as a learning tool and a means of maintaining analytical skills.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, and the expert who defined the rules was also the supervisor responsible for interpreting the tests. Second, the algorithm was developed based on ISTH guidelines, which differ in certain aspects from other available guidelines, such as those from CLSI [15] or the British Society for Haematology [28]. Therefore, this approach may not be directly applicable in laboratories that follow alternative guidelines.

Third, if the patient information record is incomplete, this may affect the global efficiency of the algorithm for LA interpretation.

If applying the laboratory algorithm rules to different analytical systems, instruments and reagents will require further validation.

One additional future implementation could be the possibility to include other aPL assays in a new algorithm to complete the APS diagnosis.

The flexibility of the middleware is also remarkable, as the implemented rules are fully customizable, allowing adaptation to updated guidelines and evolving clinical practices. This is a key distinction from the study by Florin [29] where “the rules used in the algorithm cannot be adapted and are fixed.”

Conclusion

The application of an algorithm‐based process, integrating internal comments and clinical data within the middleware, significantly improves the interpretation of LA testing, which is often complex and influenced by several variables.

This approach provides essential support for less experienced laboratory professionals and enhances overall diagnostic accuracy.

Automatic interpretation not only reduces turnaround time (TAT) but also allows clinical staff to focus on the most complex cases. Standardization through middleware minimizes inter‐ and intra‐laboratory variability and ensures that interpretations are clear, consistent, and accessible to clinicians.

This automated system for LA testing proved to be a reliable, efficient, and user‐friendly process and demonstrated a high level of agreement with expert manual interpretation.
