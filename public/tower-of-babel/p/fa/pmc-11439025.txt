Comparison of the natural and surfactant-modified zeolites in the adsorption efficiency of sunset yellow food dye from aqueous solutions
Authors: Ahmad Bagheri, Shima H Khabbaz, Amir Abbas Rafati
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe removal of Sunset Yellow (E110) on natural zeolite and zeolite modified with the cationic surfactant cetyl pyridinium chloride (CPC) was studied using the adsorption method. The structural characteristics of the surfactant-modified zeolite (SMZ-CPC) were investigated using X-ray diffraction(XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis, and the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images. The effect of different parameters on the adsorption process, such as equilibration time and amount of adsorbent at 298 K, were determined using UV–Vis spectroscopy. The maximum dye removal percentage on SMZ-CPC was obtained with 0.08 g of adsorbent in 30 min. The results show that the zeolite modified with CPC surfactant (SMZ) has a higher adsorption capacity for Sunset Yellow than the unmodified zeolite (natural form). The experimental adsorption data were nonlinearly analyzed using isotherm equations such as Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, Langmuir-Freundlich (or Sips), and Extended Langmuir (EL). The experimental data were better fitted with the Sips isotherm. The adsorption kinetic data were analyzed using eight models in nonlinear forms: the pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), integrated kinetic Langmuir (IKL), Elovich, intraparticle diffusion (IPD), mixed order rate equation (MOE), fractal-like pseudo-first-order (FL-PFO) and fractal-like pseudo-second-order (FL-PSO). According to the results of the coefficient of determination (R2), Elovich kinetic model well expressed E110 adsorption onto SMZ-CPC. Most of the dye removal takes place in less than 5 min and the maximum adsorption capacity is 5.06 mg/g. The results of this study show that zeolite modified with cationic surfactant is an effective adsorbent for removal anionic dyes from an aqueous solution.Keywords: Sunset yellow, Food dye, Natural zeolite, Removal, Wastewater treatment KineticsSubject terms: Chemistry, Pollution remediation

Introduction

The various industrial products and their applications have caused significant environmental damage by producing plastic, glass, paper, heavy metals, drugs, toxic gases, and dye-containing effluents. Dyes are often used in productions such as food, cosmetics, leather, textiles, rubber, printing, plastics, pharmaceuticals, etc. Hazardous wastewater containing dyes in very high concentrations can be generated as a result of using these products. Most of the dyes have aromatic structures and are harmful and unsafe for human health (even in some cases < 1 mg/L), hence the treatment of dye-contaminated wastewater has become a genuinely challenging subject1–4.

Among the azo food dyes, Sunset Yellow FCF (E110 or orange yellow S) (Fig. 1) is the most important food dye. It is widely used in soft drinks, candies, juices, jams, jellies, chips, snacks, biscuits, ice creams, instant noodles, and food decorations5,6.

Molecular structure of sunset yellow (E110).

These types of dyes can enter the body through ingestion, inhalation, and skin contact. Excessive exposure to these dyes can lead to hormonal dysfunction, inflammation, DNA damage, and cancer7,8. Therefore, it is necessary to treat colored contaminated wastewater before discharging it into the environment. The acceptable daily intake (ADI) set by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) for Sunset Yellow is 2.5 mg/kg bw/day. Also, World Health Organization (WHO) has standardized the maximum permissible limitation of Sunset Yellow is up to 200 mg/kg9,10. Problems associated with dye contamination can be reduced or minimized by physical, chemical, and biological processes. For example, microbial degradation, photocatalytic degradation, coagulation, or membrane filtration and separation11. However, these processes have disadvantages and limitations such as high cost, production of secondary pollutants, and poor removal efficiency12. Among the proposed techniques, adsorption is a simple, cost-effective, facile, and powerful technique for dye wastewater treatment. Activated carbon is one of the most widely used adsorbents for this purpose13. However, activated carbon suffers from high production and regeneration costs. Alternatively, relatively inexpensive natural adsorbents or their modified products are proposed as suitable options. Clay minerals, as inexpensive natural materials, are natural purifiers of water pollutants through ion exchange and adsorption14. Natural zeolite (or clinoptilolite), as an abundant resource available globally, has found many applications due to its high cation exchange capacity and surface area, etc15. The aluminum and silica in the tetrahedral center are surrounded by four oxygen atoms and creates a negatively charged structure with an ability to adsorb cations on its surface16 (see Fig. 2). This negative charge is structured and stabilized in surface of zeolite. According to this argument, it is not able to adsorb anions, so if its surface sites can be modified by cationic compounds, its adsorption capacity will increase for anions17,18.

Basic structure of natural zeolite (clinoptilolite).

Zeolite surface modification was performed with cationic surfactants because they have an amphiphilic structure with a positively charged polar head (in the hydrophilic part) and a hydrocarbon chain (in the hydrophobic part)19. According to the general properties mentioned for surfactants, when the negative surface of zeolite is exposed to a solution of cationic surfactant, at concentrations lower than Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC), the surfactant monomers form a mono-layer of positive head on the zeolite surface (formation of a hemi-micelle at the solid/liquid interface). By increasing the concentration of surfactant to the point of CMC and concentrations above CMC, a second layer of surfactant molecules associate on the zeolite sites via a hydrophobic interaction between alkyl tails (formation of a bi-layer or ad-micelle at the solid/liquid interface)20–22.

Several clay minerals as adsorbent have been tested to remove synthetic dyes (especially food dyes) such as chitosan23–25, raw and modified montmorillonite26, cellulosic material27, bentonite28 and etc. As far as we know, very few research studies was reported for the removal of food dyes by the surfactant-modified zeolite (SMZ).

In the present research, the natural zeolite was modified using the cationic surfactant of cetyl pyridinium chloride (CPC). And the surfactant-modified zeolite (SMZ) was used for the adsorption of sunset yellow food dye (or E110) from aqueous solution. These studies were done in equilibrium and kinetic situations using nonlinear regression analysis, and the effect of different parameters such as time, adsorbent dosage, pH, and concentration, were investigated.

Materials and methods

Materials

The natural zeolite (clinoptilolite) was prepared from Semnan province in the North-East of Iran (Afrand Touska Company) with a minimum purity of 90%. The Si/Al ratio of this zeolite is equal to 4.32, which was obtained from the XRF technique. Cetyl Pyridinium Chloride-Monohydrate (CPC) and Sunset Yellow (E110) were purchased from Merck and Sigma-Aldrich companies, respectively, and were used without further purification. All solutions were prepared with double distilled deionized water (resistivity > 21.0 MΩ.cm). The specific details of all the materials are listed in Table 1.

Preparation of surfactant-modified zeolite by CPC

The dried zeolite was crushed and sieved with mesh number 100, and the resulting powder was washed several times with hot distilled water and then dried in an oven at 110 ° C for 24 h29,30. A solution of CPC cationic surfactant with 50 times CMC concentration (≈ 0.045 mmol/L) was prepared to modify the active surface of zeolite. 45 ml of CPC solution was added to 15 g of zeolite (3:1 w/v) in a suitable Erlenmeyer flask and was stirred for 24 h at 25 °C with a shaker at 800 rpm31,32. The mixture was filtered by vacuum pump and the resulting precipitate was washed several times with distilled water and dried. Finally, after drying, the obtained sample (SMZ-CPC) was considered as an adsorbent in this research (see Fig. 3).

Schematic model of the modified zeolite by cationic surfactant.

Characterizations of adsorbents

The Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the samples were recorded with a Perkin-Elmer spectrum GX spectrometer. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the sample was recorded by using a Bruker AXS-D8 Advance. The surface morphology of samples was performed by Scanning electron microscope (SEM, Tescan Vega3 Easyprobe). Brunauer Emmett Teller (BET) analysis was done using a Belsorp mini II (Bel Corp., Japan) to measure the specific surface area, pore size, and volume distribution in the adsorbents. Also, the Sunset yellow (E110) concentration was measured by using a double-beam UV/Vis. spectrophotometer (WPA Lightwave S2000, UK) at a maximum wavelength of 479 nm. The linear regression analysis was used to the relationship between the independent variable (concentration: x) and dependent variable (absorption: y) by this equation (y = 0.0469x + 0.0479, R2 = 0.9975). The linearity range was found to be 2–20 mg/L.

Determination of the point of zero charge pH (pHZPC)

The point of zero charge pH (pHpzc) is a point of pH in which the surface charge of adsorbent is equal to zero. The significance of this point is to obtain the properties of adsorbent surface. For determination of the pHZPC, 0.12 g of SMZ-CPC was added to six Erlenmeyer flasks, and initial pH (pHi) of each flask was adjusted between 2 and 12 using 0.1 N of NaOH or HCl solutions and stirred for 15 h by a shaker (200 rpm and 25 °C). The pH value was controlled by a pH meter (from Metrohm Company, model 827). After this time, the final pH value (pHf) was measured. By drawing the figure of the difference between pH values (ΔpH = pHf – pHi) versus pHi, the point of zero charge pH (pHpzc) was obtained from the point where the diagram reaches the x-axis (or ΔpH = 0)25,33.

Results and discussion

Characterization of natural zeolite and SMZ-CPC

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of natural zeolite, SMZ-CPC and dye-loaded by SMZ-CPC are presented in Fig. 4A. According to this figure, in the IR spectrum of the three samples, vibrations can be seen at the frequency of 950–1250 and 425–500 cm−1, which are caused by asymmetric bending T–O (T=Si, Al) and T–O fluctuations in the structure zeolites37. In addition, the band at ≈ 1636 cm−1 relates to the bending vibration peak of hydrated water molecules in the zeolite cavities38. The comparison between FTIR spectra of natural zeolite (a) and SMZ (b) shows a new peak in the latter at about 2925 and 2852 cm−1, which corresponds to C–H asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of hydrocarbon chain (due to the loading of surfactant molecules onto zeolite surface)39,40. From another point of view, since the spectrum obtained from SMZ is different from the spectrum of natural zeolite in the width and intensity of the resulting peaks, it can be confirmed that the surface modification of zeolite has been done well41.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was used to study the morphology of the natural zeolite and the SMZ-CPC and loaded dyes samples (see Fig. 4B). Flaky clusters and undefined disordered porous structures were formed after modification by surfactant. The image of Fig. 4B(d–f) shows that the cavities in the natural zeolite structure become filled with surfactant molecules after the modification process44. Also, the image of Fig. 4B(g–i) shows an aggregation of spherical nanoparticles with rough surface which corresponds to the dye loaded on SMZ-CPC. It indicates that many dye molecules has been aggregated on the surface of the adsorbent with a high density.

XRD analysis

Figure 4C shows the XRD patterns of the natural zeolite and SMZ. The crystalline species in both samples were recognized by comparing the characteristic diffraction peaks shown in the XRD pattern with the diffractometer database. The results indicated that the most intense peaks correspond to clinoptilolite, which is the dominant component. In the XRD pattern of the SMZ, a decrease in the intensity of the clinoptilolite characteristic peaks was observed, which can be attributed to the presence of the surfactant on the surface sites of the zeolite. On the other hand, the relative intensity of the quartz peak (2θ around 38) increased when the zeolite was modified with CPC surfactant. In other words, the CPC was mostly adsorbed on the zeolite phase and not on the quartz phase. This is because of that the cationic surfactant can be attached to the unbalanced negative surface of the zeolite phase.

BET analysis

The BET analysis is one of the most important tests for evaluating the structure of an adsorbent. It provides information on the specific surface area, average diameter, and pore volume of adsorbent47. Figure 4D shows N2 adsorption/desorption isotherm for natural zeolite and SMZ-CPC. According to the IUPAC classification, this behavior is classified as type IV of the classification scheme, which denotes a mesoporous structure. This structure suggests that the pore size distributions are mainly in the range of 2–50 nm for these adsorbents48. Hysteresis loops on natural and modified clinoptilolites (SMZ) are similar to type H3, signifying that slit-shaped pores are dominant in both samples. These results are confirmed by previous work on clinoptilolites obtained from other sites. The low amount of nitrogen adsorption in Fig. 4D is related to the SMZ structure, which due to the occupation of the porous space by cationic surfactants, has less space to adsorb other gas49.

Determination of the optimal amount of adsorbent

To determine the optimal amount of this adsorbent, different values of the desired adsorbent (0.005, 0.01, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, 0.10, 0.12, 0.15, 0.20, and 0.25) g were weighed. Finally, each of the solutions was filtered and the absorption of the sub-filter solutions was measured using UV–Vis. Subsequently, a plot of the dye removal percentage was drawn about the dosage of the adsorbent.

According to Fig. 5B, for 30 mg/L dye solution, more than 94% removal efficiency could be obtained when the dosage of SMZ-CPC was 0.1 g. The percentage removal remains constant with the addition of more adsorbent dosage (after this point) because the extra adsorption sites do not cause more dye molecules to be adsorbed. As seen, for SMZ, the maximum adsorption was obtained at 0.08–0.1 g of adsorbent (as the optimal dosage).

Adsorption isotherms modeling

In most prior studies, various linear forms of adsorption isotherms have been broadly used for the evaluation of experimental data. The transformation of non-linear isotherms to linear isotherms causes changes in parameters such as error functions and the normality assumptions of the least-squares regression methods.

This transformation misrepresents the experimental error, which affects the validity of the error and statistical functions. To avoid the errors discussed above, the non-linear regression method can be more appropriately used. Therefore, for a precise determination of parameters related to each of the isotherms, the use of the nonlinear fitting method is strongly recommended50,52.

The selected model (Elovich) can be used to describe the second-order kinetics, assuming that the surface sites of solids are energetically heterogeneous. It has also been accepted for fast adsorption kinetics78.

The effect of pH and pHzpc on the adsorption process

The pHpzc is one of the main parameters for discussion of pH effect. The pH at which the charge of the adsorbent surface is zero is defined to as the point of zero charges (pHpzc). Therefore, the adsorbent surface has a positive charge at pH < pHpzc, so that ions with negative charge (anions) are quickly adsorbed. In the country, the surface has a negative charge in pH > pHpzc, and ions with positive charges are adsorbed more rapidly. The point where ΔpH (pHf -pHi ) crossed the x-axis plotted versus pHi gave the the pHpzc25,33. Based on the results from this diagram in Fig. 11, the pHpzc of the SMZ-CPC is approximately 9.5. It denotes that the active sites of the modified adsorbent is positive in an aqueous solution with pH < 9.5 and is negative where the pH > 9.5. In the aqueous solution (without pH adjustment), the SMZ-CPC adsorbent has a pH lower than pHpzc and has a positive surface charge, which is very suitable for the removal of anionic species (such as E110 dye). For pH values below the pHpzc (used in this research), there may be two possible the mechanism for dye adsorption onto SMZ-CPC: one of them is anionic exchange of Cl− anions (as counterion of surfactant) by the sulfonate groups of the dye; the other is adsorption by means of association between the positively charged surface of SMZ-CPC and the anions of dye (that the latter is more likely).

Reusability of the modified zeolite (SMZ-CPC)

Initially, SMZ-CPC was used for the removal of E110 with a concentration of 20 mg/L in optimal conditions. Then, the solid adsorbate/adsorbent was separated by centrifuging the mixture at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the solid was dried for repeated use. The desorption of E110 from adsorbent particles was carried out in acidic conditions (1M H2SO4)79. The mixture was shaken to desorb E110 from the adsorbent. The recovered SMZ-CPC was used for removing dye from a new 20 mg/L solution, and the process was repeated three times to test the re-usability of SMZ-CPC. Overall, the results indicate that SMZ can be used as efficient adsorbent for the removal of the any anionic pollutants such as different types of dyes, drugs and anionic heavy metals form wastewater due to its cheap production cost, rapid removal and high adsorption capacity.

Conclusion

This manuscript reports on the facile method for the modification of natural zeolite and its potential application in water treatment. The adsorption of the anionic sunset yellow (E110) dye was investigated on the modified (SMZ-CPC) and natural adsorbents under optimal conditions. Compared with the unmodified adsorbent, the SMZ-CPC adsorbent exhibited a higher percentage of removal and a faster adsorption rate for E110 dye, due to the increase in the number of effective sites with positive charges on the zeolite surface. The average value of the removal efficiency of SMZ was found to be above 76% at various concentrations. Also, the estimated values of RL were less than 1 at different initial concentrations of dye, indicating the favorable adsorption of E110 on the adsorbent. Five isotherm models and eight kinetic models were used to describe the adsorption data of E110 by modified zeolite. The Sips adsorption model provided a better correlation for the adsorption of E110 onto SMZ-CPC (with qm = 5.06 mg/g), suggesting that the adsorption process occurred as a monolayer on various sites of the adsorbent structure. Also, the experimental kinetic data shows that the rate of E110 adsorption is fast, and this data (qt-t) was fitted very well by the Elovich model. This study provides new insights into developing natural zeolite as an efficient adsorbent for the removal of anionic dyes or other anionic contaminants from water.
