The probiotic Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ATCC10241 inhibits wound-associated pathogens through the secretion of organic acids
Authors: Sybren Van Ginneken, Bram Lories, Hans P. Steenackers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While probiotics are traditionally studied for their role in gut health, they are also being explored for their potential to treat wound infections and enhance wound healing. One probiotic strain commonly studied in the context of wound care is Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ATCC10241. Although its antimicrobial activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa has been well-established, data on the activity against other wound-associated pathogens is more limited. Here, we demonstrate that this probiotic also inhibits the growth of other clinically relevant pathogens such as Acinetobacter baumannii and, to a lesser extent, Staphylococcus aureus. While we identify a putative bacteriocin cluster encoding the well-characterized plantaricins PlnJK and PlnEF, these antimicrobial peptides did not substantially contribute to antimicrobial activity, even though they were weakly expressed in our in vitro setups. Instead, antimicrobial activity was completely lost when the pH was neutralized, and lactic acid alone was sufficient to mimic the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241. Consistent with previous work, these result confirm that acidification of the environment through the accumulation of organic acids is more important than plantaricins to explain the antimicrobial potential of L. plantarum ATCC10241.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38966-x.

Introduction

Probiotics, which are living microbes that confer a health benefit, have traditionally been studied for their role in improving gut health1,2. More recently, however, probiotics are also being explored to improve health conditions beyond the intestinal tract. One promising example is the use of probiotics to treat inflammatory skin conditions such as acne and atopic dermatitis3,4. Similarly, probiotics are being explored as a potential treatment for wound infections5. The rationale behind using probiotics in wound care is twofold. First, some microbes can directly promote wound healing by modulating host physiology, for example by downregulating excessive inflammation or by stimulating keratinocyte proliferation and migration6,7. In addition to these direct microbe-host interactions, microbes can also contribute to wound healing by competing with and antagonizing wound-associated pathogens8. Probiotics often produce a range of different antimicrobial compounds, including bacteriocins and organic acids, that inhibit growth of pathogenic microbes6. Additionally, they might have the ability to interfere with pathogenic virulence factors such as cell adhesion9 or quorum sensing10. As a result, the use of probiotics in wound care can combat wound infections while simultaneously stimulating wound healing.

Among the various probiotics explored for wound care, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ATCC10241, a natural isolate originally recovered from sauerkraut11, is the most commonly used strain. Topical application of this probiotic has been demonstrated to improve wound healing in different animal models8,12–14, and promising results have been obtained in small-scale clinical trials with patients suffering from burn wounds and chronic ulcers15–17. Moreover, L. plantarum ATCC10241 strongly inhibits growth both in vitro and in vivo of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, an opportunistic pathogen frequently isolated from infected wounds8,12,18–20. In addition to inhibiting growth, L. plantarum ATCC10241 also interferes with different virulence factors of P. aeruginosa, including biofilm formation and quorum sensing8,20,21. While the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 against P. aeruginosa has been well-established, data on the activity of this probiotic against other wound-associated pathogens remains limited22. Moreover, previous work has identified acidification as a major mechanism contributing to the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum, but other mechanisms have been suggested as well, including the secretion of extracellular DNAses, hydrogen peroxide and bacteriocins8,20–22. However, although bacteriocin genes have been reported in other L. plantarum strains, no bacteriocins have been formally identified or characterized for L. plantarum ATCC10241.

In this work, we therefore further characterize the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 in vitro. We first demonstrate that its activity is not limited to P. aeruginosa, but that this probiotic also inhibits the growth of other common wound-associated pathogens such as Acinetobacter baumannii and, to a lesser extent, Staphylococcus aureus. While we identify a well-characterized bacteriocin cluster in the genome of L. plantarum ATCC10241 that is weakly expressed under our in vitro conditions, the antimicrobial activity does not appear to be mediated by antimicrobial peptides. Instead, in agreement with previous work, acidification of the environment through the accumulation of organic acids seems the most important inhibitory mechanism of L. plantarum ATCC10241.

Materials & methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ATCC1024123 and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG24 were used as probiotic strains. As pathogens, we selected the model strains Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO125 and Staphylococcus aureus Newman26. We also included Acinetobacter baumannii NCTC13423, a multidrug-resistant clinical isolate, as reference strain27. For all three pathogens, fluorescently labeled reporter strains tagged with a genomic mCherry gene (see below) were used unless explicitly mentioned otherwise. In addition to these reference strains, a library of clinical (wound) isolates was used to further evaluate the antimicrobial spectrum of the probiotics (Supplementary Table S1).

Stationary phase precultures of the strains of interest were routinely prepared from single colonies and incubated at 37 °C. For the Lactobacilli, precultures were grown statically for ± 24 h in 10 mL de Man-Rogosa-Sharpe broth (MRS; Becton Dickinson). For P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, precultures were grown shaken (200 rpm) for ± 16 h in 5 mL Lysogeny Broth (LB; 10 g L-1 NaCl, 10 g L-1 tryptone, 5 g L-1 yeast extract), while for S. aureus Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. Bacterial cells were harvested from these precultures by centrifugation (6,000 rpm for 5’) and washed in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4; pH 7.4) before inoculating experiments.

To perform co-culture experiments, we screened some standard culture broths but found that most of the tested broths did not support growth of both pathogens and probiotics (Supplementary Fig. S1). We therefore used a 1:1 mixture of LB and MRS broth (LBMRS broth) for most experiments, as this supported growth of all species of interest. When required, culture broths were supplemented with 250 mM 3-(morpholin-4-yl)propane-1-sulfonic acid (MOPS; pH 7; TCI Europe) to buffer the pH (Supplementary Fig. S2). Agar plates were prepared by supplementing the broth of interest with 1.5% bacteriological agar.

Construction of fluorescent reporter strains

To distinguish between pathogens and probiotics in co-culture experiments, the pathogens were genomically labeled using a red fluorescent reporter gene. For P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, we genomically integrated a reporter cassette containing the mCherry gene driven by the constitutive Pem7 promoter downstream of the glmS locus using a mini-Tn7-based integration vector (pBG13)28. We first replaced the gfp gene in pBG13 with mCherry using type IIs restriction enzyme-based cloning, resulting in the pBG13_mCherry plasmid. Hereto, mCherry was PCR amplified (from pUC18-mini-Tn7-mCherry) and flanked with BsaI restriction sites followed by the appropriate overhangs. pBG13 was then amplified without the gfp gene, incorporating BsaI restriction sites with complementary overhangs. PCR products were purified (GeneJet PCR purification kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific), digested using BsaI (New England Biolabs) and assembled with T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For all PCR reactions, either high-fidelity Q5 or Phusion polymerase (New England Biolabs) was used. All primers are included in Supplementary Table S2.

Since our A. baumannii isolate demonstrated high levels of resistance to gentamycin27, we also replaced the native gentamycin resistance marker (aacC1) of pBG13_mCherry with a chloramphenicol acetyltransferase marker (cat), resulting in pBG13_mCherry_CmR. To achieve this, we used the same type IIs restriction enzyme-based approach, amplifying both the cat marker (from pLemo) and the pBG13_mCherry vector (without the aacC1 marker) and incorporating BsaI restriction sites with complementary overhangs. After restriction and ligation, the resulting plasmids were chemically transformed in Escherichia coli PIR2 using the rubidium chloride approach29. The transformation mixture was then plated on LB supplemented with either kanamycin (50 µg mL-1; for pBG13_mCherry) or chloramphenicol (30 µg mL-1; for pBG13_mCherry_CmR), and the final plasmids were purified (GeneJet Miniprep plasmid purification kit; Thermo Fisher Scientific) from positive transformants.

After construction of the integration vectors, pBG13_mCherry was introduced together with the helper plasmid (pTNS2) encoding the Tn7 transposase in P. aeruginosa through electroporation30. For A. baumannii, pBG13_mCherry_CmR and pTNS2 were introduced using four-parental mating instead30. Positive transformants were selected using either gentamycin (30 µg mL-1) or chloramphenicol (150 µg mL-1), after which successful genomic integration of the fluorescent reporter genes was validated using PCR and Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics) (Supplementary Fig. S3). To phenotypically confirm the red fluorescence of the reporter strains and ensure that genomic integration of the reporter cassettes did not impair bacterial growth, both growth and fluorescence of the reporter strains were compared to their wild-type, unlabeled counterparts (Supplementary Fig. S4). Hereto, precultures were diluted 100-fold in CAA broth supplemented with 15 µM FeCl3 to limit background fluorescence. Cultures were then dispensed per 100 µL in a 96-well plate, which was incubated shaken (300 rpm) for 15 h at 30 °C. Red fluorescence (Excitation: 570/15 nm; Emission: 620/20 nm) and cell density (Optical Density at 600 nm; OD600nm) were monitored every five minutes using a CLARIOstar plus (BMG Labtech).

Since the Tn7-based system described above is only efficient in Gram-negative bacteria, we used a different approach for S. aureus. Here, we used homologous recombination to integrate a reporter cassette containing the mCherry gene driven by the sarA-P1 promoter in the intergenic region between locus NWMN_0029 and NWMN_0030. Construction, genomic integration and validation of the required integration vector (pRN111) and the resulting reporter strains have been described in detail before31 (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Halo spot assay

Cell suspensions were diluted in PBS to an OD595nm of either 0.1 (for the pathogens) or 1 (for the probiotics) (Supplementary Fig. S5). Next, 400 µL of the pathogen suspension was homogeneously spread out over the surface of square (12 × 12 cm) LBMRS agar plates using sterile glass beads, after which plates were air-dried for 15 min. 2 µL of the probiotic suspension was then spotted on the LBMRS plates. Plates were incubated for 24 h at 30 °C, after which the diameter of inhibition zones was manually measured and corrected by subtracting the spot diameter. Three technical replicates were averaged per biological repeat.

Co-culture experiments

Cell suspensions were first diluted in PBS to an OD595nm corresponding to approximately 3*108 cells mL-1 (Supplementary Fig. S5). Normalized cultures were then diluted 100-fold, and 25 µL of each competitor was inoculated in 1 mL of the desired broth in a 96-deepwell plate. Plates were incubated shaken (750 rpm) at 30 °C for 24 h, after which cultures were homogenized and cell counts were determined using flow cytometry. Fluorescent reporter strains of the pathogens containing a genomic mCherry gene were used to distinguish them from probiotic cells.

Cell counts were quantified using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Culture samples were diluted in PBS, stained with ± 1 µM Syto9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and acquired at a flow rate of 10–60 µL min-1 until ± 30,000 stained events were recorded. Forward scatter (FSC), side scatter (SSC), green fluorescence (FITC; Excitation: 488 nm; Emission: 525/40 nm) and red fluorescence (ECD; Excitation: 561 nm; Emission: 610/20 nm) were determined for each particle. To reduce background noise, a size-based acquisition threshold was implemented (FSC ≥ 40,000). After data acquisition, samples were manually gated in CytExpert (Supplementary Fig. S6). First, the cell population was extracted from remaining background noise using green fluorescence (Syto9) (or SSC). Red fluorescence (mCherry) was then used to distinguish between pathogen and probiotic cells. As a benchmark, we compared cell counts obtained with flow cytometry to cell counts obtained by plate counting and observed a good correspondence between both methods (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Spent broth experiments

Spent broth was harvested from Lactobacilli cultures grown statically for 24 h at 30 °C in 10 mL LBMRS broth. To obtain the spent broth, cultures were centrifuged (15’, 4,000 rpm, 4 °C) and the supernatant was filter-sterilized (0.22 µm). Afterwards, pathogen cell suspensions were diluted to an OD595nm corresponding to approximately 3*108 cells mL-1 (Supplementary Fig. S5), diluted 1,000-fold in LBMRS broth and mixed with different volumes (0%—5%—10%—20%) of the spent broth obtained from the Lactobacilli cultures. As a control, cultures were treated with an equal volume PBS. Treated cultures (200 µL) were incubated shaken for 48 h at 30 °C and growth was monitored by measuring the OD600nm every 15 min using the Bioscreen C platform (Oy Growth Curves Ab Ltd). To investigate the nature of the antimicrobial compounds, spent broth was subjected to different treatments before being used to treat pathogen cultures. To test the thermal stability, spent broth was incubated for 10’ at 95 °C. In silico prediction of putative bacteriocin clusters

To identify putative bacteriocin clusters, the genomes of L. plantarum ATCC10241 (GenBank accession: GCA_001888655.1)23 and LGG (GenBank accession: FM179322.1)24 were analyzed using BAGEL434. For the identified clusters, BLASTP (v2.16.1) was used to predict protein functions based on sequence similarities with known proteins35. In addition, sequence alignments between identified bacteriocin-like peptides and known bacteriocins were performed using Clustal Omega (v1.2.4)36.

RT-PCR analysis of plantaricin expression

L. plantarum ATCC10241 was grown in 1 mL LBMRS broth (30 °C, 750 rpm), either in monoculture or in co-culture with one of the target pathogens, as described above. After 24 h of incubation, cultures were mixed with 200 µL Stop Solution (95% ethanol, 5% phenol) to arrest RNA synthesis, after which the RNA was extracted from culture samples using the SV Total RNA Isolation System (Promega). Afterwards, RNA samples were treated with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen) to remove genomic DNA contamination, analyzed with a Nanodrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) to confirm purity (A260/A280 = 1.7 – 2.1), and diluted to ± 50 ng µL-1. RNA samples were then converted to cDNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher). For each sample, an RT-negative control (in which the reverse transcriptase was omitted) was also included. To confirm expression of the two major plantaricins (plnEF & plnJK), a RT-PCR was performed on the cDNA samples using Taq polymerase (Invitrogen). PCR products were then resolved on an agarose gel (1%) stained with ethidium bromide and imaged under UV illumination. Two constitutively expressed housekeeping genes (rpoB and ldh) were included as positive controls37, and the RT-negative samples as negative controls. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The specificity of all primer pairs was first validated by performing a PCR on genomic DNA from the probiotics and the pathogens (Supplementary Figure S8).

Statistical analysis

Most experiments were performed in triplicate using independent precultures inoculated from a different colony, generally on a different day (i.e., independent biological repeats). The exact number of biological repeats is specified in each figure caption. To test for significant differences between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.3.0). For more complex datasets, statistical analysis was performed in R (v4.5.1) using a linear (mixed-effect) modeling approach based on the nlme package (v3.1–168)38. In brief, a linear (mixed-effect) model was first fitted on the dataset using all experimental factors of interest as explanatory variables and, when relevant, replicate as a random effect. To validate the constructed model, (i) normality and (ii) homoscedasticity were evaluated both visually (by plotting the residuals) and using a Shapiro–Wilk and Brown-Forsythe test. When substantial heteroscedasticity was observed, a variance function was included in the model to correct for this. Once a suitable model had been obtained, the effect of the factor(s) of interest was investigated in more detail by performing post-hoc pairwise comparisons with either Tukey’s or Holm’s multiple comparisons correction using the emmeans package (v1.11.2–8)39. For the flow cytometry experiments, the Limit of Detection (LOD) was determined as the average number of background events in blank samples plus three times the standard deviation40. Values below the LOD were replaced with LOD/2 for graphical representation and statistical analyses.

Results

L. plantarum ATCC10241 inhibits growth of wound-associated pathogens

While the antimicrobial activity of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ATCC10241 against Pseudomonas aeruginosa has been well-documented8,13,20,21, whether this probiotic also inhibits other wound-associated pathogens remains less well established. We therefore first explored the inhibitory activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 using a set of different assays against three reference strains of common wound-associated pathogens, including P. aeruginosa PAO1, Acinetobacter baumannii NCTC13423 and Staphylococcus aureus Newman. To preliminarily assess antimicrobial activity, we screened the probiotics for inhibition halos against the pathogens on agar plates (Fig. 1a). While we did not observe any inhibition zones surrounding LGG spots, L. plantarum ATCC10241 consistently produced small inhibition halos of a few mm against all three pathogens. Inhibition halos against S. aureus were slightly less clear than for the other pathogens (Supplementary Fig. S9). Interestingly, the inhibition halos observed for L. plantarum ATCC10241 were dose dependent, as they disappeared when the probiotic inoculation density was diluted tenfold. To more quantitatively assess antimicrobial activity, we inoculated pathogens and probiotics in an approximate 1:1 ratio in liquid cultures and quantified cell counts after 24 h of co-incubation (Fig. 1b). In agreement with the observed inhibition halos, L. plantarum ATCC10241 strongly inhibited growth of both P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, reducing their counts to below the detection limit of our system. While S. aureus counts were also significantly reduced, the inhibition was notably less pronounced than for the other two pathogens. In contrast to L. plantarum ATCC10241, LGG did not significantly reduce pathogen counts in this co-culture setup.

As an additional validation, we quantified the inhibitory activity of probiotic spent culture broth (Fig. 1c). Hereto, pathogen cultures were treated with 10% cell-free spent broth recovered from probiotic cultures, after which pathogen growth was monitored for 48 h. The obtained growth curves were then integrated to obtain the Area Under the Curve (AUC) as a measure for pathogen growth. In agreement with the previous experiments, the spent broth from L. plantarum ATCC10241 completely inhibited growth of both P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii. While the spent broth from LGG also inhibited growth, it was significantly less potent. At higher concentrations, LGG spent broth also more strongly inhibited pathogen growth, demonstrating a dose-dependent antimicrobial activity (Supplementary Fig. S10). Surprisingly, for S. aureus, probiotic spent broth did not substantially reduce the AUC, which suggests no inhibition of S. aureus growth. Nonetheless, when we investigated the S. aureus growth curves in more detail, we observed that the spent broth did moderately delay S. aureus growth in a dose-dependent fashion (Supplementary Fig. S11). Untreated S. aureus cultures exhibited a characteristic dip in optical density during the stationary phase (~ 24 h), which is likely due to cell aggregation because visual sedimentation was also observed in these wells at the end of the measurements. This was not observed in treated cultures and explains why the delay in S. aureus growth is not reflected in the AUC, as it is compensated by the elevated optical density during the stationary phase.

Given the promising inhibitory activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 against the reference pathogens, we further evaluated the clinical relevance by also quantifying the antimicrobial activity of spent culture broth against an extensive panel of 27 clinical (wound) isolates (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Table S1). Overall, the results mirrored those obtained for the reference strains. Growth of all tested P. aeruginosa isolates (± 99.67% average inhibition), and most of the A. baumannii isolates (± 97.99% average inhibition), was completely inhibited for at least 48 h following treatment with 10% probiotic spent broth. Two A. baumannii isolates (LMG1025 & RUH134), however, were not fully inhibited, although their growth was markedly delayed and only started after approximately ≥ 32–36 h. For the S. aureus isolates, probiotic treatment generally resulted in a modest growth delay rather than complete inhibition (± 11.61% average inhibition), which is consistent with the results obtained for the reference strain (S. aureus Newman). Remarkably, one of the S. aureus isolates (CL9) consistently showed enhanced growth following treatment with probiotic spent broth (Fig. 2b), resulting in a negative inhibition value. In summary, these results demonstrate that L. plantarum ATCC10241 consistently inhibits the growth of different wound-associated pathogens in a variety of experimental setups. While P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii are most strongly inhibited, growth of S. aureus is also affected. Moreover, L. plantarum ATCC10241 is significantly more potent in inhibiting these pathogens than the model probiotic LGG, making it an interesting strain to further investigate.

L. plantarum ATCC10241 encodes a biosynthetic gene cluster for plantaricin production

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the strong antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241, we used BAGEL4 to analyze its genome and identify putative biosynthetic gene clusters encoding antimicrobial peptides (Fig. 3). All identified Open Reading Frames (ORFs) in the putative clusters were then BLASTed against the non-redundant protein database to predict possible protein functions based on sequence similarities (Supplementary Table S4). This analysis predicted the presence of two putative bacteriocin clusters (Fig. 3). The first cluster is located at the end of contig 9 and has a size of ± 14 kb, while the second cluster is located at the start of contig 5 and spans approximately 12 kb. Remarkably, both clusters almost perfectly align with the well-characterized plantaricin locus of L. plantarum C1141, and the proteins encoded within the identified ORFs show almost 100% sequence identity to those of L. plantarum C11 (Supplementary Table S4). Interestingly, the alignment of the second cluster with the C11 plantaricin locus starts precisely where the alignment of the first cluster ends. However, we did not observe any obvious pathogen-dependent induction (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. S12). To test whether this was purely an effect of the pH, or whether lactic acid itself also plays a role in the antimicrobial activity, we performed the same experiments using HCl instead of lactic acid to acidify sterile broth. While sterile broth supplemented with HCl also inhibited pathogen growth in a pH-dependent fashion, it was substantially less potent compared to broth acidified with lactic acid (Fig. 7a). To formally test this, we fitted a dose–response model to both treatments to obtain the IC50-values, i.e. the pH values at which pathogen growth is inhibited by 50%. As expected, the IC50 values of sterile broth acidified with lactic acid were significantly higher than those for HCl, confirming that lactic acid inhibits pathogen growth more strongly than HCl (Supplementary Fig. S15). Moreover, sterile broth acidified with HCl to the same pH as L. plantarum ATCC10241 spent broth (pH ~ 3.8) inhibited pathogen growth significantly less than either sterile broth acidified with lactic acid or the spent broth from L. plantarum ATCC10241 itself (Fig. 7b). This indicates that an acidic pH alone is not sufficient to fully explain the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241, but that organic acids themselves also contribute to the antimicrobial activity.

In conclusion, while we do not fully rule out that the plantaricins partially contribute to the observed antimicrobial activity, these findings strongly suggest that an acidification of the environment through the accumulation of organic acids is the main inhibitory mechanism responsible for the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 in our in vitro setups.

Discussion

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ATCC10241 is a probiotic strain that has been mainly investigated for its ability to interfere with the growth and virulence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a common wound-associated pathogen56. Already 20 years ago, early work by the group of J. C. Valdéz demonstrated that L. plantarum ATCC10241 strongly inhibits the growth of P. aeruginosa when both strains are co-cultured in vitro8, findings which we recapitulate here (Fig. 1). Since then, subsequent studies have further confirmed and characterized the anti-Pseudomonas activity of L. plantarum ATCC1024112,13,18,20,21. For example, it has been confirmed that the antimicrobial activity is not limited to planktonic cultures and lab strains, but that L. plantarum ATCC10241 can also disrupt mature biofilms of clinical P. aeruginosa isolates18,20. This is especially relevant for wound care, as almost all wound infections are associated with biofilm development57. Moreover, L. plantarum ATCC10241 also modulates the virulence of P. aeruginosa by reducing the production of both rhamnolipids and pyocyanin, which is likely caused by interference with quorum sensing8,20,21. Consistent with the anti-Pseudomonas activity observed in vitro, topical application of L. plantarum ATCC10241 cultures to burn wounds in mice infected with P. aeruginosa reduces local pathogen load, improves wound healing and prevents lethal sepsis8,12.

Building on this prior work, we here show that L. plantarum ATCC10241 not only inhibits P. aeruginosa, but also consistently reduces in vitro growth of both A. baumannii and S. aureus, which are other common wound-associated pathogens58,59 (Fig. 1). While growth of S. aureus was more moderately reduced, growth of A. baumannii was strongly inhibited across different experimental setups. Importantly, this inhibitory activity was not limited to reference strains, but was reproducible across an extensive and diverse panel of 27 clinical isolates, mainly sourced from acute or chronic wound infections (Fig. 2). This finding is particularly relevant for A. baumannii, as clinical isolates are frequently multidrug resistant and therefore difficult to treat using conventional antimicrobials60. For example, the A. baumannii NCTC13423 isolate used as reference strain is a multidrug-resistant strain showing resistance to multiple β-lactams, fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides27. Despite this broad resistance spectrum, the strain remained susceptible to inhibition by L. plantarum ATCC10241. Taken together, these results indicate that the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 extends beyond P. aeruginosa, and may be particularly relevant for combating A. baumannii wound infections.

Interestingly, the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 was fully lost when culture broth was buffered (Fig. 6), or when the pH of spent broth was neutralized (Fig. 5). In agreement with previous work20–22, this suggests that acidification of the environment through the accumulation of organic acids is the major factor contributing to the observed antimicrobial activity against P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii. Lactic acid bacteria such as L. plantarum secrete large amounts of organic acids, mainly lactic acid, as a metabolic byproduct during the fermentation of carbohydrates. L. plantarum ATCC10241 specifically produces a mixture of D-lactic acid (~ 9 mg mL-1) and L-lactic acid (~ 3 mg mL-1) during in vitro culturing in MRS broth21. For many Lactobacillus-based probiotics, these organic acids play an important role in their ability to inhibit pathogens. For example, the ability of Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG to inhibit the enteric pathogen Salmonella Typhimurium can be fully attributed to the accumulation of lactic acid55. Similarly, when we acidified sterile broth with lactic acid, we observed the same antimicrobial activity as probiotic spent broth (Fig. 7), suggesting that lactic acid is indeed sufficient to explain the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241. In its protonated form, lactic acid crosses the cell membrane into the cytoplasm, where it dissociates into H+ and lactate. The intracellular accumulation of protons causes an acidification of the cytosolic pH, which interferes with enzyme activity and cellular homeostasis61. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that lactic acid compromises membrane integrity of pathogens, resulting in leakage of proteins and cell lysis62. However, it should be noted that in addition to lactic acid, L. plantarum ATCC10241 also secretes multiple other organic acids, including succinic acid, acetic acid and butyric acid, albeit in lower concentrations compared to lactic acid21. These other compounds could also contribute to the antimicrobial activity of this strain. A more detailed metabolomic analysis would be required to pinpoint the specific contribution of each of these metabolites.

While growth of P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii was strongly inhibited by L. plantarum ATCC10241, growth of S. aureus was only moderately reduced. Consistent with acidification being the dominant inhibitory mechanism in our in vitro setups, S. aureus is known to be well-adapted to acidic environments and possesses an extensive arsenal of acid stress response systems. These include a strong pH-dependent induction of urease and the arginine deiminase pathway, which both generate intracellular ammonia (NH3) that can be converted into ammonium (NH4+), consuming a proton in the process and thus increasing the intracellular pH63. These systems allow S. aureus to colonize acidic environments such as the urinary tract and the skin, and even survive in phagolysosomes inside macrophages64. In agreement with this, our dose–response experiments (Fig. 7) confirm that S. aureus is more tolerant to acidification. Even when treated with the highest HCl concentration (pH < 3), no substantial inhibition of S. aureus was observed, while both P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii were fully inhibited at this concentration. Similarly, for S. aureus, the IC50 value for lactic acid (IC50 = 3.39) was almost a full pH-unit lower than those observed for P. aeruginosa (IC50 = 4.35) and A. baumannii (IC50 = 4.40), confirming that S. aureus is more tolerant to lactic acid. These dose–response graphs might also explain why we observed small inhibition halos against S. aureus on agar plates, while inhibition in co-culture with L. plantarum ATCC10241 was rather limited. On agar plates, lactic acid likely accumulates to high local concentrations in the direct vicinity of the probiotic spot, which might be sufficient to also inhibit S. aureus growth.

In addition to its antimicrobial activity, the acidification by L. plantarum ATCC10241 might also promote wound healing beyond inhibiting pathogen growth. Chronic, non-healing wounds generally have a slightly alkaline pH (7.15–9.25), which is correlated with a poor healing outcome65. Treating these wounds with acidic substances, for example organic acids, has been shown to stimulate wound healing by enhancing fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition65,66. While we did not investigate the effect of L. plantarum ATCC10241 on wound healing in this study, previous in vitro and in vivo work has shown that topical administration of L. plantarum ATCC10241 in wounds is generally well-tolerated, with no notable adverse effects7,8,12,14–17. In contrast, most of these studies observe improved wound healing following L. plantarum treatment, further supporting the clinical potential of this strain.

Beyond organic acids, many probiotics with antimicrobial activity against wound-associated pathogens also rely on bacteriocins, small peptides secreted by the probiotic that interfere with pathogen growth67. Genomic analysis (Fig. 3) indeed revealed the presence of a putative bacteriocin cluster in the genome of L. plantarum ATCC10241 that is almost identical to the well-characterized bacteriocin cluster of L. plantarum C1141. In this cluster, we identified three previously described bacteriocins: plantaricin A (PlnA), plantaricin JK (PlnJK) and plantaricin EF (PlnEF)42. These plantaricins are short cationic peptides that can permeabilize cell membranes, resulting in a dissipation of the membrane potential and eventually cell death68. While these plantaricins are mainly active against closely related lactic acid bacteria42, activity against other Gram-positive and even Gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus epidermidis and S. aureus, has been documented44,45. In addition to their antimicrobial properties, these plantaricins have other interesting properties in the context of wound care as well. Specifically, at sub-inhibitory concentrations, they can re-sensitize drug-resistant pathogens like S. aureus to conventional antibiotics, for example by interfering with efflux pumps69,70. Moreover, they can directly promote wound healing by modulating inflammation, and by stimulating keratinocyte proliferation and migration71.

Despite detecting some weak transcriptional activity (Fig. 4), protease treatment did not reduce the antimicrobial activity of probiotic spent broth, suggesting that these plantaricins do not substantially contribute to the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 observed in our in vitro setups (Fig. 5). It should be emphasised, however, that the bands observed in our RT-PCR analysis were generally very faint, indicating that the plantaricin locus is tightly regulated and only weakly expressed under our in vitro culture conditions. This observation is in line with previous reports for other L. plantarum strains, in which plantaricin expression is generally limited under standard growth conditions and requires specific environmental cues for induction, such as exogenous addition of an inducer peptide (e.g. PlnA)48, supplementation with acetate72, or co-culturing with specific inducer strains49. Whether L. plantarum ATCC10241 is able to produce these plantaricins in vivo, and to what degree they contribute to the antimicrobial activity of this probiotic in a wound environment, therefore remains an open question. The knowledge that L. plantarum ATCC10241 has the genetic potential to produce these interesting plantaricins might guide future studies investigating their in vivo relevance, or may inspire engineering attempts to enhance plantaricin expression, thereby further improving the antimicrobial potential of this probiotic, particularly against more acid-tolerant pathogens like S. aureus. Similar strategies are currently being explored for other probiotics as well73.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that the antimicrobial activity of L. plantarum ATCC10241 is not limited to P. aeruginosa, but that this probiotic also inhibits other wound-associated pathogens such as A. baumannii and, to a lesser extent, S. aureus. While this activity appears to be primarily caused by the accumulation of organic acids, we also confirm the genetic potential of this probiotic to produce a range of different plantaricins with a broad activity spectrum and potential wound-healing properties. Although further research is needed to investigate the expression and relevance of these plantaricins in vivo, our findings support the potential of L. plantarum ATCC10241 as a probiotic to combat wound infections.
