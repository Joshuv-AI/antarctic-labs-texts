A Chromosome-scale Genome Assembly for the Broad-leaved Bamboo Indocalamus tessellatus
Authors: Xiaoqing Hu, Site Luo, Zhe Chen, Qian Xu, Chenjie Zhou, Xiaofang Yan, Daomin Chen, Xianjun Deng, Yikun Wang, Xianshixuan Liu, Yingjun Wu, Weicheng Zheng, Weiqing Qiu, Aiwu Jin, Qianggen Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndocalamus tessellatus, a broad-leaved bamboo species, holds significant economic and ecological value. However, the absence of a high-quality reference genome has constrained genetic and functional studies of this species. Here, we report a chromosome-scale genome assembly of I. tessellatus, constructed by integrating PacBio HiFi and Hi-C sequencing technologies. The final assembly is 2.89 Gb with a scaffold N50 of 117.66 Mb. BUSCO assessment against the poales_odb10 dataset revealed 99.0% completeness, indicating the high quality of the assembly. Additionally, a total of 41,765 protein-coding genes were predicted, offering a critical resource for functional annotation and comparative genomics. This reference genome establishes a foundation for studies on I. tessellatus biology, breeding, and conservation.Subject terms: Genome, Plant sciences

Background & Summary

Indocalamus tessellatus is a perennial bamboo species native to southeastern China1,2 (Fig. 1). It is characterized by exceptionally long and broad leaves, which can exceed 45 cm in length and 10 cm in width. This species is of both ecological and economic significance3. Locally known as “Ruo leaves”, the foliage is commonly used in Chinese cuisine to wrap zongzi (sticky rice dumplings). This practice is rooted in centuries of culinary tradition, which allows the leaves to impart a distinctive aroma, provide nutritional benefits and help inhibit fungal growth4,5. The leaves are also rich in bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, essential oils, phenolics and polysaccharides6,7. These compounds exhibit antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-aging activities, and are widely employed in traditional Chinese medicines8. Ecologically, I. tessellatus contributes to soil stabilization and carbon sequestration, owing to its extensive rhizome system and rapid biomass accumulation9. Moreover, it can remediate lead-contaminated soils, highlighting its potential for environmental restoration10.

The genus Indocalamus comprises at least 23 species, sharing a chromosome number of 2n = 481. Like Phyllostachys edulis, Indocalamus belongs to the subtribe Arundinariinae, which represents temperate woody bamboos (TWBs)1,11. However, the ploidy status of TWBs remains controversial. The first draft genome of P. edulis was assembled, and fluorescence in situ hybridization at mitotic metaphase suggested that the species is diploid12. Analysis of this draft genome also detected a whole-genome duplication (WGD) event 7–12 million years ago, indicating that P. edulis is a diploidized tetraploid derived from an ancient polyploidization. A follow-up study by the same group produced a chromosome-scale genome assembly comprising 24 pseudomolecules, with a scaffold N50 of 79.90 Mb13. Later, Nanopore-based assemblies of three TWBs, including P. edulis, Ampelocalamus luodianensis and Hsuehochloa calcarea, further highlighted the genomic complexity of this clade and suggested that their tetraploid nature likely originated from an ancient hybridization event11,14.

Despite these advances, genomic resources for Indocalamus remain scarce. To date, no reference genome has been published, and only flow cytometric estimation is available, indicating a genome size of approximately 2.3 Gb for I. decorus15. With the rapid development of genomics providing powerful tools for uncovering biological principles16–18, constructing a high-quality reference genome for I. tessellatus is therefore essential for elucidating the genetic basis of key traits, including leaf morphology, biosynthesis of bioactive compounds, and stress tolerance. Here, we report the first chromosome-level genome assembly of I. tessellatus. Using 159.97 Gb of PacBio HiFi reads, we generated a 2.89 Gb genome assembly comprising 699 contigs, with a BUSCO completeness of 99.0% (Table 1). Incorporation of 437.05 Gb of Hi-C data allowed 98.83% of the assembled genome to be anchored onto 24 pseudo-chromosomes (Fig. 2), yielding a scaffold N50 of 117.66 Mb. Repeat annotation identified 2,624 Mb (90.95%) of the genome as repetitive sequences, with long terminal repeat (LTR) transposons accounting for 66.05% (Table 2). We predicted 41,765 protein-coding genes, of which 40,978 were functionally annotated based on multiple public databases (Table 3).

Methods

Sample collection

I. tessellatus plants were collected from a naturally occurring population in Suichang County, Zhejiang Province, China (E119°09´, N28°35´). A representative individual was transplanted from the wild into the greenhouse at Lishui University for subsequent cultivation and sampling. The material was collected by Xiaoqing Hu and taxonomically confirmed by Professor Wengen Zhang from Jiangxi Agricultural University. The voucher specimen (No.0020) is preserved in the laboratory of the College of Ecology, Lishui University.

Plant tissues were collected, briefly rinsed with distilled water to remove surface debris, and gently blotted dry. Fresh young leaves were used for PacBio HiFi reads and Hi-C sequencing, while leaves, stems, roots, shoots, and leaf sheaths were prepared for transcriptome analysis. Harvested samples for both DNA and RNA extractions were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved at −80 °C in a deep freezer until further processing.

Short-read library construction and sequencing

High-molecular-weight (HMV) genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue using a modified CTAB protocol19. For short-read sequencing, genomic DNA was randomly fragmented into ~350 bp fragments using a Covaris S220 ultrasonic processor (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA). Library preparation involved end repair, 3′ A-tailing, and adapter ligation. The resulting library was sequenced on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China). Raw reads were processed with Fastp to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, yielding 317.3 Gb of clean data.

PacBio HiFi library construction and sequencing

Purified DNA was sheared to an average fragment size of approximately 20 kb using a g-TUBE device (Covaris, Inc., MA, USA). Size selection was then performed using the BluePippin system (Sage Science, Inc., MA, USA) to enrich for DNA fragments within the optimal size range of 18–21 kb for PacBio HiFi sequencing. The PacBio HiFi sequencing library was constructed using the SMRTbell® Express Template Prep Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was carried out on the PacBio Revio platform, producing a total of 8,764,096 high-fidelity circular consensus sequencing (HiFi CCS) reads20, corresponding to approximately 160 Gb of sequencing data (~55 × genome coverage; Table 1).

Hi-C library construction and sequencing

To achieve chromosome-level genome scaffolding, a Hi-C library was constructed from young leaf tissues. Samples were first cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, and the reaction was quenched with glycine. Nuclei were then isolated, and chromatin was digested using the restriction enzyme DpnII. Proximity ligation was subsequently performed to join spatially adjacent DNA fragments. After ligation, DNA was purified and processed into sequencing libraries using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The final Hi-C library21 was sequenced on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform, generating approximately 437 Gb of paired-end sequencing data.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 14 different tissues using R6827 Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity, integrity and concentration were assessed prior to library construction. Strand-specific cDNA libraries were prepared with the MGIEasy RNA Directional Library Prep Set (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China) and subsequently sequenced using paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. A total of 101.02 Gb transcriptomic data were generated, averaging approximately 7.22 Gb per tissue sample.

Genome estimate

The genome size of I. tessellatus was estimated in silico by analyzing the 21-mer frequency distribution using JELLYFISH (v.2.2.3)22 and GenomeScope v.2.023 (http://genomescope.org/genomescope2.0/). We performed the 21-mer frequency analysis using 317.3 Gb of short reads generated by the DNBSEQ platform. The analysis estimated that the haploid genome size is approximately 552.6–561.1 Mb, of which 294.9–299.4 Mb is repetitive. The K-mer profile suggests that I. tessellatus is likely tetraploid (or paleotetraploid), indicated by the high proportion of aaaa (91.1%) and aabb (6.96%) K-mers (Fig. 2).

Genome assembly

Prior to genome assembly, the quality and length distribution of PacBio HiFi reads were evaluated using SeqKit v2.8.124, yielding an N50 read length of approximately 18,157 bp. De novo genome assembly was then performed using Hifiasm v0.19.825 with the parameter -l 2 to apply a moderately aggressive purge level for removing haplotigs and heterozygous duplications. The primary contig assembly comprised 699 contigs, with a contig N50 of 40.51 Mb and a total size of 2.89 Gb. To remove potential contamination from symbiotic or environmental microorganisms, FCS-GX v0.5.026 was applied with default parameters. Contigs identified as non-host sequences were excluded from downstream analyses.

Hi-C sequencing data were used to scaffold the assembly with HapHiC v1.0.727, which iteratively maps, corrects, and scaffolds based on Hi-C contact frequency. The final chromosome-scale assembly was 2.89 Gb in size, with a scaffold N50 of 117.66 Mb and 438 gaps. Visualization of the Hi-C contact map demonstrated clear chromosomal-scale scaffolding, with 24 pseudo-chromosomes exhibiting clear chromosomal structures (Fig. 3). Interaction strength was represented using a red-white gradient based on KR-normalized contact counts, illustrating a clear chromosomal-scale organization consistent with the expected karyotype11,12.

Repeat element annotation

Repeat sequences were annotated using an integrated approach combining multiple tools. First, a species-specific repeat library was generated de novo using RepeatModeler v2.0.628. To further classify repeat sequences labeled as “Unknown”, the library was refined using the EDTA v2.2.229 and DeepTE pipelines30. EDTA applies both structural and homology-based methods, while DeepTE employs deep-learning algorithms to classify unknown transposable elements. This process resulted in a more complete and accurate custom repeat library.

The custom library was subsequently combined with Repbase (v20181026)31 to create a comprehensive repeat database. Genome-wide repeat annotation and soft masking were performed using RepeatMasker v4.1.532 (-xsmall parameter). In total, approximately 2,624 Mb of repeat sequences were annotated, constituting 90.95% of the assembled genome (Table 2).

Among the identified repeats, long terminal repeats (LTRs) were the most abundant, covering approximately 1,906 Mb (66.05%), followed by DNA transposons (617 Mb, 21.4%) and LINE elements (45 Mb, 1.55%). Other repeat types, including SINEs, rolling-circle elements, and unclassified repeats, collectively accounted for less than 2% of the genome. Tandem repeats, including small RNA, satellites, and simple repeats, occupied a minor fraction of the genome, totaling approximately 0.39% (Table 2).

Gene prediction and functional annotation

Gene prediction was performed using an integrative strategy that combined ab initio, transcriptome-guided, and homology-based approaches. BRAKER v3.0.833 was used to generate ab initio gene models by integrating RNA-seq and protein homology evidence, with final predictions produced by AUGUSTUS34. For transcriptome-guided prediction, RNA-seq reads were aligned to the genome using HISAT2 v2.2.135 (default settings) and assembled into transcripts with StringTie v2.2.136. Coding sequences (CDSs) were identified using TransDecoder v5.5.0 (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder; ORFs ≥ 50 aa), and only those supported by Pfam-A37 (HMMER, E ≤ 1 × 10−5) or Swiss-Prot (DIAMOND38 blastp, E ≤ 1 × 10−5) were retained, yielding high-confidence CDS and corresponding peptide predictions. Homology-based predictions were produced by GeMoMa v1.939, using reference protein sets from Oryza sativa (GCF_034140825.1), Triticum aestivum (GCF_018294505.1), and Zea mays (GCF_902167145.1).

All three evidence tracks were combined with EVidenceModeler v2.0.040 to create a consensus gene set. During this process, weight scores were assigned as follows: ab initio (AUGUSTUS) = 4, homology-based (GeMoMa) = 10, and transcriptome-based (StringTie + TransDecoder) = 12. This integration produced 41,765 high-confidence gene models. Gene density, GC content, and LTR densities across pseudo-chromosomes are illustrated in a circular diagram (Fig. 4).

The following tracks (moving inward) display the distribution of gene density (yellow), GC content (red), and repeat sequence density (blue) along each chromosome. The central ribbon links indicate syntenic blocks within the genome, highlighting regions of large-scale duplication and complex chromosomal rearrangements.

Functional annotation of the final gene set was performed by aligning predicted proteins to the UniProt and NCBI non-redundant (NR) databases using DIAMOND (blastp, E ≤ 1e-5)41. Further functional characterization was conducted with eggNOG-mapper using the eggNOG database42. Additionally, protein domains, motifs, and Gene Ontology (GO) terms were annotated via InterProScann v5.73 (parameters: -appl Pfam -iprlookup -goterms)43. Identification of tRNAs was performed with tRNAscan-SE v2.0.1244, and other non-coding RNAs were detected using Infernal v1.1.5 alongside the Rfam database45. In total, 40,978 genes (98% of predicted genes) were functionally annotated (Table 3), providing a solid foundation for future functional, evolutionary, and comparative genomic analyses.

Genome synteny and synonymous substitution rate (Ks) analysis between I. tessellatus and P. edulis

To assess whole-genome synteny between I. tessellatus and P. edulis, annotated protein-coding sequences of I. tessellatus were aligned against those of P. edulis using BLASTP (BLAST + v2.12.0)46, with an E-value cutoff of 1e−10 and a maximum of five target alignments per query. The alignments were formatted as input for MCScanX47, which was used with default parameters to identify collinear gene pairs and syntenic blocks between the two genomes. Visualization with TBtools v2.31548 revealed extensive chromosome-level syntenic conservation between the two genomes (Fig. 5a).

Synteny and genome duplication analysis between Indocalamus tessellatus and Phyllostachys edulis. (a) Synteny plot showing collinear relationships between the chromosomes of I. tessellatus (top) and P. edulis (bottom). (b) Distribution of synonymous substitution rates (Ks) for paralogous gene pairs within I. tessellatus (red) and within P. edulis (blue).

Following a similar strategy, self-synteny analyses were performed for both I. tessellatus and P. edulis. Based on the identified syntenic gene pairs, synonymous substitution rates (Ks) were calculated using ParaAT v.2.049 and KaKs_Calculator v.2.050. The intragenomic paralogous gene pairs of I. tessellatus exhibited a clear Ks peak at ~0.15 (Fig. 5b), similar to that of P. edulis, consistent with a potential recent polyploidy event11,12.

Visualization of I. tessellatus intraspecific synteny using TBtools revealed widespread intragenomic syntenic blocks, including prominent one-to-many and many-to-many relationships among chromosomes, which are indicative of ancient genome duplications (Fig. 4). Notably, chromosomes, including 7, 14, 23, and 24, exhibit weak or no synteny with other chromosomes, suggesting the loss of substantial chromosomal fragments during diploidization. Collectively, these observations suggest that I. tessellatus is likely a paleo-tetraploid that has undergone substantial diploidization.

Technical Validation

The quality and completeness of the I. tessellatus genome assembly were rigorously evaluated using multiple approaches. BUSCO analysis (v5.7.10)54 with the Poales_odb10 dataset (2024-01-08) identified 99.0% complete orthologs, including 39.0% single-copy and 60.0% duplicated, with only 0.1% fragmented and 0.9% missing (Table 1), indicating a highly complete but duplicated genome assembly. Assembly accuracy was further assessed through read mapping and K-mer-based evaluation. HiFi long reads achieved a 100% mapping rate, while paired-end short reads reached 99.86%, confirming that nearly all sequencing data were represented in the assembly. Consensus accuracy estimated with Merqury v1.355 (K = 21) yielded a quality value (QV) of 52.56, corresponding to an estimated base-level error rate of 5.55 × 10−6. These results collectively demonstrate that the assembly is highly contiguous, accurate, and reliable.

The predicted gene set also exhibited high completeness when evaluated using the same BUSCO configuration, with 93.8% complete BUSCOs (46.6% single-copy and 47.2% duplicated), 0.8% fragmented, and 5.4% missing (Table 1). This concordance between the genome and gene set evaluations supports the robustness of the assembly and provides a solid foundation for downstream functional and comparative genomic analyses.
