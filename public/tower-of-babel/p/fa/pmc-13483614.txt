Myeloid-specific Becn1 deficiency elicits spontaneous pulmonary injury and exacerbates acute lung injury
Authors: Jingwen Liu, Meiling Tan, Shuiwen Huang, Jun Zhang, Lejiao Mao, Chengzhi Chen, Yueqiang Fu, Xuejun Jiang, Zhen Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute lung injury (ALI) and its severe phenotype, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), represent devastating and highly lethal respiratory disorders. Hallmarked by unrestrained pulmonary inflammation predominantly driven by myeloid cells, these conditions ultimately culminate in profound disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier. The reciprocal crosstalk between autophagy and inflammation and its molecular underpinnings in ALI pathogenesis remain incompletely defined.MethodsThe role of myeloid Becn1 in maintaining pulmonary homeostasis and modulating susceptibility to lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI was defined, and the potential link between intestinal barrier integrity, gut microbiota, and ALI severity was further interrogated. The expression of BECN1 in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cells from patients with ARDS and in lung tissues of mice with LPS-induced ALI was detected by western blot and immunofluorescence respectively. Myeloid cell-specific Becn1 conditional knockout (cKO) mice were generated. Parallel analyses were performed in both steady-state and LPS-challenged mice, including quantitative lung histopathological analysis, inflammatory cytokine profiling of BALF, bulk RNA sequencing of lung tissue, histological assessment of intestinal architecture and intestinal tight junction integrity, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing of fecal microbiota.ResultsMyeloid Becn1 deficiency alone was sufficient to disrupt pulmonary homeostasis, leading to spontaneous lung injury characterized by increased alveolar-capillary permeability, inflammatory cell infiltration, and aberrant activation of immune-inflammatory pathways. When challenged with LPS, these pre-existing inflammatory priming effects translated to exacerbated pulmonary pathology and exaggerated cytokine storm. Beyond the lung, Becn1 cKO mice developed spontaneous intestinal barrier dysfunction and gut microbiota dysbiosis at steady state, including blunted intestinal villi, reduced goblet cells, impaired tight junction integrity, increased mast cell infiltration, and a characteristic microbial shift with depleted Actinobacteria and expanded Alistipes. All these intestinal and microbial perturbations were likely further amplified by LPS challenge, consistent with a potential association between intestinal-microbial dysregulation and exacerbated pulmonary injury.ConclusionsMyeloid Becn1 governs pulmonary-intestinal immune homeostasis, and its deficiency drives spontaneous lung injury, hyperinflammation, impaired gut-lung crosstalk, and exacerbated acute lung injury, establishing myeloid Becn1 as a critical determinant of acute lung injury severity.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03748-6.Keywords: Acute lung injury, Becn1, Myeloid cells, Intestinal barrier, Gut microbiota

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) and its severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) represent devastating respiratory disorders with substantial morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients, as no targeted therapeutics are clinically available [1, 2]. A defining pathological hallmark of ALI/ARDS is the dysregulated hyperactivation of innate immunity [3, 4]. Myeloid-lineage cells, including alveolar macrophages and neutrophils, are the primary mediators of pathogenic inflammation [5]. Their aberrant activation drives excessive proinflammatory cytokine and chemokine release [6], triggering a fulminant cytokine storm that disrupts the alveolar-capillary barrier [7], damages lung parenchyma [8] and causes irreversible respiratory dysfunction. Given the central role of myeloid inflammatory cascades in ALI/ARDS pathogenesis [9], clarifying the intrinsic molecular regulators of myeloid responses is critically important for understanding disease mechanisms.

Autophagy serves as a core homeostatic pathway governing myeloid cell development, differentiation, and function [10]. By clearing damaged organelles and misfolded proteins [11], autophagy maintains cellular stability and regulates innate immune response initiation and resolution [12, 13]. Becn1 was the first identified mammalian core autophagy gene [14]. It forms the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) complex with partner proteins [15], and Becn1 dysfunction is linked to tumorigenesis [16] and diverse inflammatory disorders [11]. Moderate autophagy protects cells by inhibiting inflammasome activation, partly via mitophagy [17, 18]. In contrast, impaired autophagic flux or loss of key autophagy genes amplifies inflammatory responses and worsens tissue injury [19]. However, the specific roles of myeloid Becn1 in pulmonary homeostasis and ALI pathogenesis remain incompletely defined.

In addition, the gut and lung engage in intimate immune crosstalk via the gut-lung axis [20, 21]. Disruption of the intestinal barrier permits translocation of bacteria and endotoxin into the systemic circulation [22], leading to broad immune cell activation and subsequent exacerbation of pulmonary inflammation [23]. Autophagy contributes to the preservation of intestinal epithelial integrity and immune homeostasis [24]. Autophagic insufficiency has been linked to inflammatory bowel disease and dysregulation of the gut microbiota [13]. The potential link between myeloid autophagy and pulmonary inflammation via modulation of the intestinal barrier and gut microbiota remains unexplored.

Despite the established role of the gut-lung axis in ALI, the myeloid-intrinsic molecular regulators that coordinate pulmonary and intestinal homeostasis remain poorly defined. Here, we show that myeloid cell-specific Becn1 is essential for maintaining immune and barrier homeostasis across these two anatomically distinct but functionally interconnected organs. Using a conditional knockout mouse model, we demonstrate that loss of Becn1 in myeloid cells causes spontaneous pulmonary inflammation, exacerbates LPS-induced ALI, and disrupts intestinal barrier integrity and microbial homeostasis. In this study, we explore the mechanism by which myeloid Becn1 modulates gut-lung crosstalk and governs the severity of ALI.

Materials and methods

Establishment of a murine model of acute lung injury

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and reported following the ARRIVE guidelines, and approved by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC-CQMU-2023-0088). The myeloid Becn1 conditional knockout mice (Becn1fl/fl; Lyz2-Cre) and their littermate control mice (Becn1fl/fl) used in the experiment were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6J genetic background, and both male and female mice aged 8 to 10 weeks were included in this study. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the animal facility of Chongqing Medical University. Mice were housed in polysulfone cages with corn cob bedding and nesting enrichment, at a controlled temperature of 23 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. All animals had ad libitum access to gamma-irradiated standard rodent chow and autoclaved drinking water throughout the study. After a 7-day acclimation period, mice were randomly assigned to different experimental groups.

The acute lung injury (ALI) model was established by intratracheal instillation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 0.5 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA, catalog no. L2630). All experimental parameters, including LPS dosage and sacrifice time point, were optimized and validated based on our previously published ALI mouse model protocols [25]. Briefly, after anesthetizing by 1% pentobarbital sodium, the mice were placed on their back on a 40 slope. The trachea was intubated by a BD insyte catheter Q5 with a shortened needle. LPS (0.5 mg/kg) were dissolved in 50 μL 0.9% sodium chloride and were instilled followed by 150 μL air using a syringe The mice were held on the head up until proper breathing was assured. Subsequently, the animals were carefully placed onto the 37℃ heating plate until theyrecovered from anesthesia. Mice were sacrificed 3 days after LPS instillation, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) as well as lung and intestinal tissue samples were collected for subsequent analyses.

Clinical samples

The clinical study was performed at University-Town Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, Chongqing, China. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of University-Town Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No.: LL-202146). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal surrogates. All ARDS patients met the Berlin Definition of ARDS. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples were collected from both non-ARDS and ARDS patients; cells within the BALF (predominantly myeloid cells) were harvested by centrifugation and then subjected to western blot analysis [26].

Processing and analysis of BALF

The collected BALF was centrifuged at 2, 000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used for protein concentration determination, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; DPB2004Y). A 10 μl aliquot of the resuspended BALF was pipetted onto a cell counting chamber, which was then inserted into an automatic cell counter (RWD; C100-SE) for observation, reading and recording of results.

Western blot

Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‑PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non‑fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against BECN1 (1:1,000 dilution; Proteintech, USA; 111306‑1‑AP). Following washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP‑conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent (Beyotime, P0018AM). Signals were acquired using a MiniChemi chemiluminescence imaging system (Sinsage, Beijing, China). GAPDH (1:10, 000 dilution; Proteintech, USA; 60004‑1‑AP) served as the loading control.

H&E staining

Lung and intestinal tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, baked at 65 °C for 1 h, then dewaxed in Xylene I and II (Chuandong Chemical Co., Ltd., Chongqing, China; 1330-20-7) for 30 min each sequentially, followed by hydration through gradient ethanol and distilled water. Staining was performed using a modified H&E staining kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; G1121) hematoxylin staining for 3 min, differentiation with 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 1–3 s, bluing with 0.1% ammonia water for 5 min, and eosin counterstaining for 1–3 min (the staining time for intestinal tissues was halved). After staining, the sections were rinsed with running water, dried at 37 °C for 30 min, and mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured using an upright microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan; BX53).

The degree of lung tissue injury was semi-quantitatively scored according to a previously reported method [27]. The scoring indicators included (A) neutrophils in alveolar spaces (B) neutrophils in the interstitium (C) hyaline membranes (D) proteinaceous debris in spaces and (E) alveolar septal thickening under 400 × magnification. The final scoring formula was: [(20 × A) + (14 × B) + (7 × C) + (7 × D) + (2 × E)]/(number of fields × 100).

For histological assessment, intestinal tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and cut into 5-μm-thick serial sections. All histological staining and quantitative analyses were conducted in a double-blinded manner by two independent observers to minimize experimental bias. Brightfield images were acquired using an inverted light microscope under standardized conditions, with consistent illumination intensity, objective magnification, and exposure time maintained for all samples. For each tissue slide, 5 to 6 representative, non-overlapping fields were selected according to predefined criteria, ensuring intact tissue architecture and absence of processing artifacts. Villus height (from villus tip to crypt-villus junction) and crypt depth (from crypt-villus junction to crypt base) were measured on 6 morphologically normal crypt-villus units per section, and the villus height/crypt depth ratio was derived [28]. Quantitative image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Data were averaged across multiple fields per mouse, and individual mice were treated as independent biological replicates for statistical comparisons.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin sections were baked at 65 °C for 1 h, then dewaxed in Xylene I and II for 30 min each. After dewaxing, the sections were hydrated in gradient ethanol and distilled water for 5 min each sequentially. Antigen retrieval was performed with 1 × citrate buffer (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China; ZLI-9064) by heating with medium-power microwave for 15 min, followed by natural cooling. After washing with PBS three times, the sections were blocked with peroxidase blocker (ZSGB-BIO; PV-9000; ZSGB-BIO; PV-9004) at room temperature for 20 min. The sections were incubated with primary antibodiesat 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies include CD68 (Proteintech Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; KHC0006), CD45 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.; sc-1187), and Ly6G (BioLegend, Inc.; 127632). After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with reaction enhancer and enzyme-labeled secondary antibody at room temperature for 20 min each sequentially. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining (ZSGB-BIO; ZLI-9018) was controlled in real time under a microscope (usually 5 s to 8 min). After counterstaining with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiation with hydrochloric acid ethanol and bluing with ammonia water, the sections were dried at 37 °C for 30 min and mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured using an upright microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan; BX53). According to a previously reported method [29], 5 different fields of view were randomly selected under a 40 × microscope for counting, and the results were expressed as the positive area per unit area. All image acquisition and analysis were performed using ImageJ software. Biological replicates were used as the statistical units for analysis.

Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) staining

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to AB-PAS staining (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; G1285) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were incubated in Alcian Blue solution for 10–20 min, washed, immersed in oxidizing agent for 5–8 min, washed again, and treated with Schiff reagent for 10–20 min. After subsequent washes, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, differentiated, and blued. Slides were dried at 37 °C, mounted with neutral balsam, and imaged using an Olympus upright microscope. (Olympus Corporation, Japan; BX53). Goblet cell counts were quantified on stained sections using a previously described method [30]. For each mouse, six intact crypts were randomly selected, and counts were averaged. The results from multiple fields of view for the same mouse were averaged to obtain the final data for that individual, and statistical analyses were performed with individual mice as biological replicates. All images were acquired and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Toluidine blue (TBO) staining

Staining was performed using a TBO staining kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; G3661). After paraffin embedding, tissue sections were baked at 65 °C for 1 h to enhance tissue adhesion. The sections were sequentially dewaxed in Xylene I and II for 30 min each, followed by rehydration through gradient ethanol and distilled water. Subsequently, the sections were immersed in preheated Toluidine Blue staining solution, covered, and incubated for 20–40 min. After staining, the sections were rinsed with distilled water and differentiated with 95% ethanol for 3–5 s. The sections were dried at 37 °C for 30 min and then mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured using an upright microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan; BX53). Mast cell counts were quantified using a previously described method [31]. Briefly, five random fields of view were selected at 40 × magnification, and counts were expressed as cells per field. The results from multiple fields of view for the same mouse were averaged to obtain the final data for that individual, and statistical analyses were performed with individual mice as biological replicates. All images were acquired and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

After rewarming the frozen sections pre-cooled acetone at 4 °C was added dropwise and the treatment lasted for 10 min. The sections were washed three times with PBS. This was followed by antigen retrieval using 1 × citrate antigen retrieval solution. The retrieval was conducted at medium power for 8 min and then at medium–low power for 7 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were washed again, blocked with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, USA; B2064-100G) for 1 h, and then washed. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 5% FBS at 4 °C overnight. The primary antiboies inclued CD68 (Proteintech Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; 28058-1-AP), CD45 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.; sc-1187), Ly6G (BioLegend, Inc.; 127632), BECN1(Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.; sc-48341), Occludin(Proteintech Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; 21260-1-AP), and ZO-1 (Proteintech Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; 21773-1-AP). After washing four times with PBST, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific; A-11008&A-11004/Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; SA00003-11) and DAPI (Invitrogen, USA; D3571) diluted in 5% FBS at room temperature for 2 h in the dark. The sections were then washed four times with PBST. After that the sections were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching agent (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; G1401). Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon Corporation, Japan; A1R+). According to a previously reported method [32], five different fields of view were randomly selected under a 60 × microscope for counting, and the results were expressed as the relative fluorescence intensity per unit area. All image acquisition and analysis were performed using ImageJ and Nikon NIS-Elements AR software. Biological replicates (n was indicated in the figure) were used as the statistical units for analysis.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using a commercial kit (Aifang Biotechnology, China; AFIHC024). Tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD68 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 28058-1-AP), Becn1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; sc-48341), and LC3B (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 81004-1-RR). Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1R+, Tokyo, Japan).

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptome sequencing and related bioinformatic analyses were performed by Majorbio (Shanghai, China) on the Illumina sequencing platform, with library construction conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing reagent kit. Total RNA was extracted from an appropriate amount of lung tissue, followed by assessment of RNA concentration, purity, and integrity. Messenger RNA (mRNA) was subsequently isolated via A-T base pairing between Oligo (dT) magnetic beads and the poly(A) tail of mRNA. After adding fragmentation buffer, mRNA was randomly sheared into approximately 300 bp fragments under optimized conditions. Using the fragmented mRNA as a template, double-stranded cDNA with sticky ends was synthesized with random primers under the action of reverse transcriptase. End-repair mixture was added to blunt the cDNA ends, followed by addition of an adenine (A) base at the 3’ end. The resulting products were purified, ligated with sequencing adapters, and subjected to fragment size selection. The size-selected products were amplified by PCR and purified to generate the final sequencing library. Library concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and libraries were mixed proportionally according to the required data volume and quality-checked. Clusters were generated by bridge PCR amplification on a cBot system, followed by high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Each sample was sequenced to a depth of no less than 6.5 Gb of clean data to meet the requirements for quantitative transcriptomic analysis.

Base distribution and quality fluctuation across all sequencing cycles were statistically evaluated to globally assess the quality of library construction and sequencing. Raw sequencing data were filtered using fastp software (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality reads (Q value < 20), reads containing more than 10% ambiguous bases (N), and short reads with a trimmed length of < 20 bp, yielding high-quality clean data. The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome to obtain mapped data for subsequent transcript assembly and quantification of gene expression levels.

Gene and transcript expression levels were quantified using RSEM software (http://deweylab.github.io/RSEM/). Transcripts Per Million (TPM) was used as the normalized expression index to provide a standardized data foundation for differential expression analysis. Normalized data were subjected to screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), heatmap construction, and pathway enrichment analysis. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (http://bioconductor.org/packages/stats/bioc/DESeq2) to identify DEGs between sample groups. Intergroup differential analyses were conducted using R packages including ggplot2 (v4.0.1), dplyr (v1.1.4), and ggrepel (v0.9.6). DEGs were screened with thresholds of absolute |log2 fold change|> 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Visualization of the identified DEGs was performed using the R packages pheatmap (v1.0.13) and RColorBrewer (v1.1.3).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from murine lung tissues was isolated using a commercial RNA extraction kit (Accurate Biology, China; AG21023) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using GoScript™ Reverse Transcription Mix with Random Primers (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; A2801). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad real-time PCR system using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China; Q711-02) with gene-specific primers. Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin as the internal reference. The sequences of the primers used in this study are listed as follows (5′ to 3′): β-actin forward, GTGCTATGTTGCTCTAGACTTCG; β-actin reverse, ATGCCACAGGATTCCAT-ACC; Il1β forward, GGACAGAATATCAACCAACAA; Il1β reverse, TTACACAGGACAGGTATAG-ATT; Ccl2 forward, ATGAGATCAGAACCTACAACT; Ccl2 reverse, TCCTACAGAAGTGCTTGAG; Tnfα forward, CTCAGCCTCTTCTCATTC; Tnfα reverse, GCCATAGAACTGATGAGA.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

Mouse fecal samples stored at −80 °C were collected under sterile conditions for 16S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis, which was performed by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from mouse fecal samples using a microbial genomic DNA extraction kit under sterile conditions. DNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis upon extraction. PCR amplification was performed using the extracted DNA as the template. PCR products were recovered by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, purified with a gel extraction kit, and quantified using the QuantiFluor™-ST Blue Fluorescence Quantitation System (Promega). Illumina adapter sequences were added to the outer ends of the target region via PCR, and the PCR products were recovered using a gel extraction kit, eluted, and denatured to generate single-stranded DNA fragments. Sequences of the template DNA fragments were obtained based on fluorescence signals collected by the Illumina platform. Paired-end (PE) reads generated by Illumina sequencing were assembled according to their overlapping relationships, accompanied by sequence quality control and filtering.

Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality control and filtering using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, version 0.19.6), and assembly was performed with FLASH v1.2.11 (http://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash). To eliminate variations in sequencing depth, all samples were rarefied to the minimum sequence count across samples for standardization. Subsequent bioinformatic analyses were conducted based on the normalized valid sequences. Beta diversity at the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) level was analyzed using the R vegan package (v2.7.2) and mixOmics package (v6.34.0), while alpha diversity at the OTU level was evaluated with the R vegan package. Changes in gut microbiota at the phylum and genus levels were visualized using the R ggalluvial package (v0.12.5). Microbial biomarkers with significant intergroup differences from kingdom to species levels were screened using the R microeco package (v1.16.0). The stability of the microbial community was assessed by calculating the microbial health index, and the severity of microbial dysbiosis was determined by measuring the microbial dysbiosis index.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between two groups were evaluated by Student’s t-test. Two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed for pairwise comparisons among experimental groups. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Bars represent the mean value, error bars represent the SEM, and n indicates the number of biological replicates. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Myeloid cell-specific Becn1 deficiency induces spontaneous pulmonary injury

To clarify the clinical association between myeloid cell BECN1 and ALI/ARDS, we enrolled non-ARDS controls and ARDS patients, collected their bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and performed protein extraction and western blot analysis on the cellular fraction after centrifugation (Fig. 1A). BECN1 protein expression in myeloid cells was significantly higher in ARDS patients than in non-ARDS controls (Fig. 1B). These findings were further validated by immunofluorescence co-localization in mouse lung tissues (Fig. 1C): compared with the WT + PBS group, the LPS-induced inflammatory group exhibited increased CD68 (a macrophage marker)-positive macrophage infiltration, enhanced BECN1 fluorescence in macrophages, and co-localization of the two markers (Fig. 1D).

Arrowheads indicate CD68-positive cells co-expressing BECN1. Scale bars: 50 μm (main panels) and 10 μm (magnified inset panels)

To investigate the role of myeloid cell Becn1 in maintaining pulmonary homeostasis, we established a myeloid cell-specific Becn1 knockout mouse model (Becn1 cKO), with littermate mice carrying only floxed alleles without Cre recombinase expression as controls (Ctrl) (Fig. 2A). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on mouse lung tissues to verify knockout efficiency and changes in autophagic levels. Compared with the control group, Becn1 cKO mice showed significantly decreased BECN1 fluorescence in CD68-positive cells, accompanied by downregulated expression of the autophagy marker LC3B. These data indicate the successful establishment of a myeloid cell-specific Becn1 knockout model and the inhibition of basal autophagic activity in myeloid cells (Fig. 2B). Analysis of mouse BALF revealed that under normal physiological conditions without any external stimulation, the total cell count and total protein concentration in BALF of the Becn1 cKO group were higher than those in the Ctrl group (Fig. 2C-D). Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining showed that compared with the structurally intact lung tissue of the Ctrl group, the lung tissue of Becn1 cKO mice exhibited distinct pathological changes, including mild inflammatory cell infiltration and focal alveolar hemorrhage (Fig. 2E-F).

The results of immunohistochemical staining and immunofluorescence staining showed that the numbers of CD45 (a pan-leukocyte marker)-positive cells (Fig. 2G-J), CD68-positive cells (Fig. 2K-N) and Ly6G (a neutrophil marker)-positive cells (Fig. 2O-R) in the lung tissue of the Becn1 cKO group were higher than those in the Ctrl group. These findings demonstrate that Becn1-dependent processes in myeloid cells are indispensable for sustaining basal pulmonary immune homeostasis, which may underlie the increased susceptibility of the lung to acute inflammatory injury.

Myeloid·cell-specific Becn1 deficiency drives lung transcriptomic remodeling

To investigate the impact of myeloid Becn1 deficiency on pulmonary homeostasis, RNA-sequencing was performed on lung tissues from Ctrl and Becn1 cKO mice under physiological conditions. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps revealed distinct segregation of gene expression profiles between the two groups (Fig. 3A). Differential expression analysis identified 488 upregulated and 1237 downregulated genes in the Becn1 cKO group relative to Ctrl littermates (Fig. 3B), demonstrating that myeloid Becn1 deletion drives global transcriptomic remodeling in the lung.

Nodes represent significant biological processes. Nodes are colored according to the normalized enrichment score (NES)

Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed prominent enrichment in pathways including cytokine‑cytokine receptor interaction, viral protein‑cytokine/cytokine receptor interaction, osteoclast differentiation, phagosomes, and B cell receptor signaling (Fig. 3C). Gene ontology (GO) terms showed significant enrichment for immune effector process regulation, leukocyte migration, antigen processing and presentation, and cell killing (Fig. 3D), indicating broad activation of inflammatory, immune activation, and cell death‑related pathways in Becn1‑deficient lungs. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further supported these findings: gene sets linked to linoleic acid metabolism and cornified envelope formation were negatively enriched in Becn1 cKO mice, whereas lysosome‑associated gene sets were positively enriched (Fig. 3E). These data suggest that myeloid‑specific Becn1 deletion elicits lung transcriptomic reprogramming at baseline, marked by widespread activation of inflammatory and immune response networks.

Myeloid Becn1 deficiency aggravates LPS-induced acute lung injury

To examine the role of myeloid Becn1 in ALI progression, we induced acute lung injury in Becn1 cKO and control mice via intratracheal LPS instillation (Fig. 4A). BALF analysis showed significantly higher total cell counts and total protein concentration in Becn1 cKO + LPS mice than in Ctrl + LPS mice (Fig. 4B-C), indicating worsened inflammatory cell infiltration and alveolar-capillary barrier leakage. H&E staining revealed more severe alveolar destruction, septal thickening, hemorrhage, edema, and inflammatory infiltration in the Becn1 cKO + LPS group, consistent with exacerbated pathological injury (Fig. 4D-E). Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining further demonstrated markedly increased pulmonary positivity for CD45 (leukocytes), CD68 (macrophages), and Ly6G (neutrophils) in Becn1 cKO + LPS mice (Fig. 4F-Q). These data confirm that myeloid-specific Becn1 deficiency exacerbates LPS-induced pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage, highlighting a critical negative regulatory function of Becn1 in myeloid cells that restrains excessive inflammatory responses during ALI.

Myeloid cell-specific Becn1 deficiency drives immune and metabolic transcriptome dyshomeostasis in acute lung injury

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying exacerbated LPS-induced lung injury by myeloid Becn1 deficiency, RNA-sequencing was performed on lung tissues from Ctrl + LPS and Becn1 cKO + LPS mice. Pronounced divergence in gene expression profiles was observed between the two groups (Fig. 5A), with 255 upregulated and 439 downregulated genes in the cKO group (Fig. 5B), indicating enhanced transcriptomic dysregulation following myeloid Becn1 deletion under LPS challenge. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses revealed that differentially expressed genes were concentrated in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, lymphocyte-mediated immune killing, adaptive immune responses, and leukocyte migration (Fig. 5C-D). GSEA further identified marked alterations in gene sets related to PPAR signaling, ribosome biogenesis, and cornified envelope formation (Fig. 5E), suggesting that Becn1 also modulates metabolic adaptation, protein synthesis, and epithelial repair under inflammatory stress. To validate the inflammatory cytokine alterations identified by transcriptomic sequencing, we quantified mRNA levels of key proinflammatory cytokines via qPCR. Genotype, LPS challenge, and their interaction all significantly modulated proinflammatory factor transcription. Following LPS stimulation, Becn1 cKO mice showed pronounced upregulation of pulmonary Il1β, Tnfα, and Ccl2 mRNA than control mice (Fig. 5F-H). These results reveal that myeloid Becn1 deficiency exacerbates ALI by hyperactivating inflammatory immune pathways and disrupting metabolic and tissue-repair programs, thereby defining a key protective role of Becn1 in restraining pathological lung inflammation and injury.

Myeloid cell-specific Becn1 deficiency impairs intestinal barrier structure and homeostasis

Since myeloid Becn1 deficiency elicits spontaneous pulmonary inflammation and the gut-lung axis governs systemic inflammatory homeostasis, we further examined whether this deficiency directly disrupts intestinal homeostasis. H&E staining revealed that Becn1 cKO mice exhibited shortened intestinal villi and deepened crypts under physiological conditions (Fig. 6A-D), indicative of inherent intestinal pathological alterations. We next assessed intestinal barrier integrity using key structural and functional markers. AB-PAS staining showed a marked reduction in goblet cells in Becn1 cKO mice (Fig. 6E-F). TBO staining further uncovered a significant increase in mast cells, which serve as critical regulators of intestinal barrier function, in cKO mice compared with control mice (Fig. 6G-H). In parallel, immunofluorescent analyses demonstrated decreased expression of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin in Becn1 cKO mice (Fig. 6I-L), reflecting impaired mucus secretion and compromised epithelial physical barrier function. Together, myeloid-specific Becn1 deficiency directly drives intestinal injury under basal conditions, manifesting as global intestinal barrier dysfunction characterized by aberrant villus structure, reduced goblet cell counts, downregulated tight junction proteins, and excessive mast cell accumulation. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for understanding gut-lung axis crosstalk in our subsequent ALI model.

Myeloid cell-specific Becn1 deficiency induces gut microbiota structural dysbiosis

As intestinal injury is frequently accompanied by gut microbial dysbiosis, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis on fecal samples from Ctrl and Becn1 cKO mice. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance and partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) both revealed distinct segregation of gut microbial community structure between Ctrl and Becn1 cKO mice (Fig. 7A-B), without significant alterations in microbial α-diversity (Fig. 7C-D). Taxonomic analysis at the phylum and genus levels showed marked reductions in beneficial bacteria including Bifidobacterium and Actinobacteria, alongside increased abundance of the potentially pathogenic Alistipes in cKO mice (Fig. 7E-F). LEfSe further identified discriminative signature taxa between the two groups (Fig. 7G). Correspondingly, cKO mice exhibited a significantly decreased gut microbiome health index (GMHI) and increased microbial dysbiosis index (MDI) (Fig. 7H-I), corroborating robust microbial dysbiosis.

H&E staining showed that LPS alone caused intestinal morphological changes, while Becn1 cKO mice developed more severe intestinal injury with prominent villus elongation and crypt deepening (Fig. 8A-D), indicating synergistic amplification of intestinal structural remodeling by Becn1 deficiency and LPS. We further assessed intestinal barrier integrity under LPS challenge. The Becn1 cKO + LPS group exhibited fewer goblet cells (Fig. 8E-F), more intestinal mast cells (Fig. 8G-H), and reduced expression of ZO-1 and Occludin (Fig. 8I-L) compared with the Ctrl + LPS group. These results confirm that myeloid Becn1 deficiency aggravates LPS-induced impairment of intestinal barrier structure and function.

Discussion

Our study addresses a critical gap in defining the contribution of myeloid autophagy regulators to pulmonary homeostasis and ALI, using a myeloid cell-specific Becn1 knockout model. Myeloid Becn1 deficiency alone induced spontaneous pulmonary perturbations under basal conditions, confirming that Becn1 is indispensable for sustaining pulmonary immune homeostasis. These baseline abnormalities were markedly exacerbated following LPS challenge, indicating that loss of myeloid Becn1 primes the lung for heightened susceptibility to LPS-induced ALI. These findings build upon prior work linking Becn1-mediated autophagy to inflammatory regulation, and establish that myeloid Becn1 maintains basal pulmonary stability while restraining pathological inflammation during ALI [12, 13].

We posit that BECN1 upregulation during ALI/ARDS acts as an adaptive compensatory protective response to stress. Our recent work has verified that autophagy is elevated via a TLR4-dependent pathway in both murine ALI models and clinical ARDS cases. This cascade enables the clearance of damaged mitochondria, restrains aberrant inflammatory activation, and ultimately attenuates lung tissue damage, functioning as an endogenous defensive mechanism for lung homeostasis [33]. This interpretation aligns with prior evidence from Sun Y, Shi M and Deng Z [34–36], whose studies collectively validate that increased BECN1 expression confers consistent protective benefits across cardiac dysfunction, renal injury and other pathological conditions. Nonetheless, the non-autophagy-dependent regulatory mechanisms of BECN1 remain an important avenue for further mechanistic exploration in future research.

While our data associate myeloid Becn1 deficiency with enhanced pulmonary inflammatory infiltration and enriched pathways governing cytokine production and leukocyte migration, the precise downstream mechanisms driving these phenotypes remain incompletely defined. A salient example is the enrichment of lysosome-associated gene signatures observed in basal transcriptomic profiling. This pattern aligns with the well-characterized role of Becn1 in autophagosome-lysosome fusion and intracellular homeostasis, yet must be interpreted with rigorous caution [37, 38]. Specifically, this lysosomal enrichment most likely represents a compensatory adaptation to disturbed intracellular homeostasis in Becn1-deficient myeloid cells, rather than direct evidence for a discrete mechanistic pathway underlying inflammatory dysregulation. Our study thus validates a link between myeloid Becn1 deficiency and disordered pulmonary inflammation, but stops short of defining the full molecular cascade. Given the multifunctional nature of Becn1, even though our data confirm that Becn1 deletion reduces autophagic activity in myeloid cells [39], we cannot exclude autophagy-independent regulatory functions of Becn1 [40–42]. These possibilities warrant formal validation in future mechanistic studies.

An additional key observation from this work is the concurrent characterization of pulmonary phenotypes, intestinal barrier integrity, and gut microbiota composition in the myeloid Becn1-deficient model. At baseline, Becn1 conditional knockout mice displayed morphological intestinal alterations, reduced goblet cell density, diminished expression of ZO-1 and occludin, and increased mast cell infiltration [43]. These structural changes were accompanied by compositional shifts in the gut microbiota, including reduced abundance of Actinomycetes and Bifidobacteriota, and elevated levels of Alistipes. Intestinal injury and microbial dysbiosis were further aggravated after LPS exposure. On this basis, we conclude that myeloid Becn1 deficiency is linked not only to pulmonary inflammation but also to compromised intestinal homeostasis.

Given that intestinal barrier disruption and microbial dysbiosis coincide with pulmonary inflammation at baseline and are amplified by LPS challenge, the gut may contribute to the systemic inflammatory milieu observed in this model [20, 44]. Nevertheless, our experiments do not provide direct proof that intestinal alterations drive the pulmonary phenotype, nor can we exclude the possibility that myeloid Becn1 deletion triggers concurrent pulmonary and intestinal dysfunction. Our data thus demonstrate synchronized disruption of the gut and lung. In recently published work from our group [26, 45], we identified that selective gut microbiota populations confer protective effects against acute lung injury. Combined with the present findings, this underscores the urgency of mechanistic dissection of the gut-lung axis to advance understanding of acute lung injury pathogenesis [46].

Several limitations of this study merit explicit consideration. First, the Lyz2-Cre system targets a broad spectrum of myeloid subsets, preventing discrimination of the relative contributions of macrophages, neutrophils, and other myeloid populations to the observed phenotypes. More refined, cell subset-specific knockout models will be necessary to resolve this question [47]. Second, while paired intestinal and pulmonary phenotypes implicate the gut-lung axis, formal causal evidence is still lacking. Experiments including fecal microbiota transplantation, microbiota depletion, or targeted interventions to restore intestinal homeostasis will be critical to determine whether altered microbiota and barrier function actively promote lung injury in this setting [48]. Third, and of central importance, we did not perform rescue or reconstitution experiments for Becn1 deficiency, such as ectopic Becn1 expression or wild-type myeloid cell transplantation [49]. Accordingly, we cannot ascertain whether restoring Becn1 in myeloid cells would reverse baseline tissue abnormalities or mitigate LPS-induced injury. Finally, Becn1 mediates diverse biological processes beyond canonical autophagy [50], and our current data cannot distinguish autophagy-dependent from autophagy-independent effects.

Collectively, these findings establish that myeloid Becn1 acts as a central regulator orchestrating pulmonary immune homeostasis and intestinal barrier integrity. Myeloid Becn1 depletion triggers spontaneous pulmonary and intestinal inflammatory perturbations alongside microbial dysbiosis at baseline, hypersensitizes pulmonary inflammatory responses to LPS, and worsens intestinal barrier damage and microbial dysregulation, ultimately culminating in exacerbated acute lung injury. These observations define an irreplaceable role for myeloid Becn1 in coordinating homeostatic crosstalk across the gut-lung axis.
