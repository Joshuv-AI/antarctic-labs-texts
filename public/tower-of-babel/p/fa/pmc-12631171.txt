The Effect of Diabetes Medication on the Scores of the GNRI and CONUT Nutritional Indices: Cross‐Sectional and Longitudinal Retrospective Studies
Authors: Shunsuke Fujita, Kenichiro Nagata, Kano Kinjo, Haruka Mimata, Taiga Sonoda, Keisuke Mine, Takehiro Kawashiri, Takeshi Hirota, Mayako Uchida, Daisuke Kobayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMalnutrition implies a decline in the systemic status and organ function, which is closely related to sarcopenia, frailty, osteoporosis, and prognosis. Diabetes medications work in a multifaceted manner on various tissues, such as the pancreas, muscle, liver, and adipose tissue; these medications affect metabolism, which in turn affects nutritional status. This study aimed to determine the effect of diabetes medications on the scores of the Geriatric Nutrition Risk Index (GNRI) and Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) nutritional indices, both cross‐sectionally and longitudinally. This cross‐sectional study included 2146 individuals who were prescribed diabetes medications. Multivariate analysis showed that both GNRI and CONUT scores tended to be improved in patients using sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4i), or biguanide (BG). Additionally, propensity score matching of nutrition‐related laboratory values was performed to assess the variation in nutritional indices over time, which resulted in less deterioration of the GNRI in the SGLT2i and BG groups. In conclusion, this study suggests that SGLT2i and BG prevent the progression of malnutrition and may help in selecting drugs that consider the nutritional status of patients.Keywords: CONUT score, diabetes mellitus, GNRI, malnutrition, metformin, SGLT2 inhibitor

Introduction

According to the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN), malnutrition is defined as “a state resulting from lack of intake or uptake of nutrition that leads to altered body composition (decreased fat free mass) and body cell mass leading to diminished physical and mental function and impaired clinical outcome from disease” [1]. Malnutrition reduces the quality of life (QOL); increases morbidity, the incidence of infections, and poor wound healing; causes functional changes in the immune system; reduces overall muscle strength; and increases mortality and length of stay in the hospital [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Various indices are used to assess nutritional status; the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) is a simple clinical assessment tool as it uses only serum albumin (Alb) levels and body mass index (BMI), which is calculated from height and weight [7]. The GNRI is reportedly a useful predictor of prognosis in conditions including patients with cancer, renal events, and osteoporosis‐related fractures [8, 9, 10]. The Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score was calculated based on the serum albumin level (Alb), total cholesterol value (tCHO), and lymphocyte count (LYMP) (Table S1) [11]. Low nutritional status, such as a low GNRI and high CONUT score, is reported to be associated with the worsening of systemic functions, such as sarcopenia, frailty, and cognitive function [12, 13, 14, 15]. Therefore, it is important to understand the factors that influence nutritional indicators and take appropriate actions.

Diabetes mellitus is a condition wherein abnormal hyperglycemia occurs and persists due to reduced insulin secretion or increased insulin resistance. As diabetes progresses, complications such as renal damage, neuropathy, and retinopathy occur, significantly reducing the QOL of patients. Furthermore, patients with diabetes are known to be more prone to sarcopenia and frailty than patients without diabetes [16, 17]. It has been suggested that the high incidence of sarcopenia complications in patients with diabetes is due to reduced insulin action, insufficient energy supply to cells, chronic inflammation, increased energy consumption, and reduced muscle mass; however, many aspects remain unknown [18, 19, 20]. Several diabetes drugs with a wide range of mechanisms are currently in use, including drugs that directly stimulate insulin secretion, drugs that cause insulin secretion via gastrointestinal hormones, drugs that improve insulin resistance, and drugs that reduce blood glucose levels by promoting blood glucose excretion. Some of these drugs are known to cause weight fluctuations. Sulfonylureas (SU) increase body weight by promoting anabolism through enhanced insulin secretion, and thiazolidinediones (TZD) increase body weight by inducing edema through tubular reabsorption of Na and water. On the other hand, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 analogs (GLP‐1) and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have been reported to be more likely to cause weight loss. The use of drugs known to cause weight loss should be discouraged in patients at high risk of sarcopenia [21]. However, there are also reports of GLP‐1 and SGLT2i maintaining muscle mass while causing weight loss [22, 23], so further investigation is needed to establish an optimal treatment strategy. Furthermore, several diabetes drugs are reported to improve various diseases that are strongly associated with undernutrition (sarcopenia, reduced cardiac and renal function, poor general health, and poor prognosis) [24, 25]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has analyzed in detail the direct impact of diabetes drugs on changes in nutritional status. Identification of the impact of diabetes medicines on nutritional indices would enable the selection of medications that consider the nutritional status of patients and help avoid the risk of various diseases, including sarcopenia and frailty, that can result from malnutrition.

Against this background, the aim of this study was to examine the impact of the use of various antidiabetic drugs on the nutritional indicators, GNRI, and CONUT score.

Methods

Ethical Declarations

This study conformed to the “Declaration of Helsinki,” “Ethical Guidelines for Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects,” and “Act on the Protection of Personal Information.” This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards for Clinical Research of Kyushu University Hospital and Medical Institution (license number: 24071–00).

As this study was aimed at patients who had already been admitted to the hospital, it was impracticable to obtain informed consent. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the need to obtain informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Boards for Clinical Research of Kyushu University Hospital and Medical Institution. Therefore, detailed information about the research aims, procedures, and data handling was made publicly available on the website to ensure that people were aware of the trial and had the opportunity to opt‐out of participation.

Study Design and Population

This was a retrospective, single‐center, observational study of patients prescribed diabetes medications at Kyushu University Hospital from April 1, 2021, to March 31, 2024. We compared diabetic patients using and not using specific antidiabetic medications with regard to nutritional indices, GNRI, and CONUT scores, both cross‐sectionally and longitudinally. Data obtained from the medical records of patients aged 45–85 years were included in the study to consider the favorable timing of the onset of malnutrition. Patients with cancer were excluded from the analysis because cancer causes severe cachexia and malnutrition. In addition, patients who were expected to have an infection owing to antimicrobial prescriptions were excluded. The screening process is illustrated in Figure 1.

The flowchart of inclusion and exclusion of patients.

Data Collection

We collected the following data: basic characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI) calculated with height and weight, use of diabetes medications, blood test value, albumin (Alb), white blood cells (WBC), LYMP, Hb, PLT, tCHO, LDL, HDL, and triglyceride (TG). The most recent data were used in the cross‐sectional study when data from the same patient were available at more than one time point. For the longitudinal study, the oldest and most recent medical records were collected. All data were recorded between April 2021 and March 2024, and no information prior to this period was traced. Accordingly, data regarding the duration of diabetes or the history of antidiabetic medication use before the study period were not collected.

Medication Exposure

Patients were classified according to whether they had been prescribed the following diabetes medications: insulin, GLP‐1, SGLT2i, DPP4i, BG, SU, αGI, TZD and glinide. In this longitudinal study, the medication use group was defined as those who continued to take medication for the entire period. Patients taking GLP‐1/GIP analogs and glimin, a new type of diabetes drug, were excluded due to the small number of cases.

Outcome

In this study, nutritional status was assessed using the GNRI and CONUT scores. The GNRI was calculated as 14.89 × albumin (g/dL) + 41.7 × (weight/ideal weight). Ideal weight was defined using the following formula: height (m) × height (m) × 22. We set the weight/ideal weight to one when the weight exceeded the ideal weight [7]. The CONUT score is a nutritional index based on serum albumin concentration, total cholesterol level, and lymphocyte count [11] (Table S1). The effects of diabetes medications on nutritional indices at the latest point were assessed using logistic regression analysis in a cross‐sectional study. In this longitudinal study, changes in nutritional indices and laboratory values were assessed by comparing the most recent records with the earliest records during the survey period.

Statistical Analysis

Patient backgrounds were compared between groups with and without the concomitant use of diabetes medications. The comparison parameters included age, sex, BMI, observation period, GNRI, CONUT score, use of diabetes medication, Alb, WBC, LYMP, Hb, Plt, tCHO, LDL, HDL, and TG levels. Among the parameters, sex and use of diabetes medications were nominal data and the χ2 test was used. Age, BMI, observation period, GNRI, CONUT score, Alb, WBC, LYMP, Hb, Plt, tCHO, LDL, HDL, and TG were continuous data and were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

In this cross‐sectional study, multiple regression analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of diabetes medication on nutritional indices. The model used in the analysis was adjusted for age and sex.

In the longitudinal study, to minimize background bias, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) for some parameters. In PSM, we used a nearest neighbor matching algorithm to match the SGLT2i, BG, or DPP4i groups and each free group with a caliper width of 0.20. Propensity scores were calculated to estimate the likelihood of the patient being treated based on multivariable logistic regression models. Baseline parameters, including age, sex, BMI, observation period, GNRI, CONUT score, use of antidiabetic drugs (insulin, GLP‐1, SGLT2i, DPP4i, BG, SU, GI, TZD, and glinide), and laboratory data (Alb, WBC, LYMP, Hb, PLT, tCHO, HDL, LDL, TG, and HbA1c), were included as matching factors. After PSM, univariate analysis was performed to assess the long‐term effects of diabetes medications on changes in nutritional index values and parameters. Statistical significance was set at a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using the JMP Pro 18 software (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC., NC, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the Research Population in Cross‐Sectional Surveys

This study included 10,214 patients prescribed diabetes medications. 8068 of them were excluded based on the criteria, as shown in Figure 1. A total of 2146 patients were included in the cross‐sectional study and 1264 were included in the longitudinal study. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the 2146 patients (1258 men and 888 women) included in the study. The median patient age was 68 years, and 58.6% of the patients were men. Men had a higher BMI, better nutritional status, higher GNRI, and lower CONUT scores. Regarding diabetes medications, men had lower insulin levels and higher SGLT2i use rates than women. Men had higher Alb and hemoglobin (Hb) levels, whereas women had higher LYMP, PLT, and cholesterol levels (total cholesterol (tCHO), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), and low‐density lipoprotein (LDL)).

Association Between Diabetes Medication and Changes in Nutritional Indices Before Propensity Score Matching (PSM)

We examined the effects of the diabetes medications, SGLT2i, DPP4i, and BG on changes in nutritional indices. The characteristics of all the patients in this longitudinal survey are shown in Table 3. A total of 1264 patients with medical records of at least 30 days were included in the study. The median patient age was 66 years, and 57.8% of the patients were men. Men were slightly older than women and had a higher BMI. Although the latest GNRI score was lower in women than in men, the GNRI change values were not different (p = 0.1219). In contrast, the CONUT scores were higher in men at both time points, and the amount of change was similar regardless of sex. Regarding the use of diabetes medications and laboratory data, patient backgrounds in the longitudinal study were similar to those in the cross‐sectional study.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the effects of various diabetes medications on nutritional indices, GNRI, and CONUT scores. Multivariate analysis was performed in patients using antidiabetic medications, and three diabetic medications, SGLT2i, DPP‐4i, and BG, were identified as factors that improved both nutritional indices. To further investigate this effect, changes in the GNRI and CONUT scores were examined in patients with medical records of more than 30 days. The results showed that the use of SGLT2i and BG reduced the worsening of the GNRI. Simultaneously, nutritional laboratory data showed that SGLT2i and BG may reduce the decline in albumin and Hb levels. Although it is necessary to consider the existence of unidentified variables and confounders, these results indicate that compared with other diabetes medications, SGLT2i and BG have the potential to maintain and improve the nutritional status of patients.

Many diabetic drugs exert their hypoglycemic effects by affecting insulin secretion and resistance, thereby increasing intracellular glucose uptake. Previous studies reported that SU, glinides, and TZD increase body weight [26, 27, 28]. GLP‐1 analogs reduce appetite and body weight [29]. In addition, SGLT2i has a hypoglycemic effect based on the direct urinary excretion of glucose [30, 31], making it less likely to be used in patients with weight loss and a high risk of sarcopenia. In this cross‐sectional study, the impact of diabetes medications on nutritional indices, GNRI, and CONUT score was assessed. The results showed that SGLT2i, DPP4i, BG, and TZD had a positive impact on GNRI, while SGLT2i, DPP4i, and BG improved the CONUT score. As BMI and Alb were used to calculate the GNRI, SU, and TZD, candidates known as weight‐increasing drugs [26] may have contributed to the increase in the GNRI.

In contrast, drugs that are not considered to increase body weight, such as SGLT2i, DPP4i, and BG, are expected to improve nutritional status by maintaining or improving the ability to synthesize proteins. The results showed that SGLT2i, DPP4i, and BG improved the CONUT score calculated based on Alb, tCHO, and LYMP, suggesting that these three diabetic drugs may maintain nutritional status by improving protein synthesis, immunity, and lipid metabolism in the body rather than by changing body weight.

Nutritional status generally deteriorates with age. This change is more pronounced in patients with diabetes and age‐related declines in mobility and physical function occur frequently [32]. Therefore, it is important to maintain good nutritional status and physical function in people with diabetes, comparable to that of healthy adults, to improve patient prognosis. Diabetes medications are essential for good disease control and the maintenance of a patient's overall health. In recent years, it has become clear that some diabetic medications have protective effects on various organs through actions other than hypoglycemia [25, 33, 34].

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has directly investigated the long‐term effects of diabetes medication on nutritional indices. In this study, we examined the change in nutritional indices after long‐term treatment with different diabetes medications by comparing the changes in the GNRI and CONUT scores in patients whose medical records were available for > 30 days, focusing on three types of diabetes medications: SGLT2i, DPP4i, and BG. The results showed that only SGLT2i reduced the GNRI. However, because the baseline age, GNRI, CONUT score, and nutrition‐related blood test values were different between the diabetes medication groups and the observation period was also different, it was necessary to verify the effect of each drug in more detail by aligning the patient background. After PSM, the results showed a reduction in the change in GNRI values with the use of SGLT2i and BG.

One possible mechanism that diabetes medications affect GNRI is by improving glycemic control during the observation period. In this study, HbA1c was incorporated as a factor in PSM to adjust for differences in diabetes management between groups at baseline. Furthermore, there was no difference in the change in HbA1c levels between patients with and without diabetes medications after PSM. These results suggest that the improvement in GNRI observed in the longitudinal study is due to mechanisms other than diabetes management indicated by HbA1c.

SGLT2i is believed to cause weight loss and sarcopenia owing to its mechanism of direct glucose excretion into the urine; however, it has been reported in animal studies to inhibit cellular aging and decline in muscle function [35, 36]. In this study, the Alb and Hb levels were higher in the SGLT2i group than in the no‐treatment group. SGLT2i exerts nephroprotective effects and is expected to decrease proteinuria in patients with renal dysfunction [30]. The decrease in urinary protein levels owing to its long‐term nephroprotective effect may have partially contributed to the maintenance of Alb. The Hb level is also a laboratory value used to diagnose anemia and is an indicator of nutritional status. SGLT2i has been reported to increase Hb and erythropoietin levels and red blood cell count [37, 38], which may have been the same as the effects of SGLT2i in this study.

DPP‐4i is known to increase incretin hormones, such as GLP‐1 and gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP), but does not cause weight loss, as seen in GLP‐1 analog users [26]. In addition, DPP‐4i may improve cellular nutritional status by promoting glucose‐dependent insulin secretion and enhancing cellular glucose uptake. In a cross‐sectional study, a DPP‐4i was selected as a covariate to improve nutritional indices. However, DPP‐4i had no effect on the change in either GNRI or CONUT scores in the longitudinal study. DPP‐4i is a diabetes medication with a high retention rate owing to its safety profile, which includes a low incidence of hypoglycemia and serious adverse events. Therefore, DPP‐4i is the most frequently selected drug as the first choice for patients with diabetes in Japan [39]. The inclusion of many patients with early diabetes may have resulted in superior cross‐sectional nutritional indices, but limited the impact on nutritional indices with subsequent continued use of the drug.

BG is a hypoglycemic drug that improves insulin resistance and inhibits glycogenesis. Adequate glucose uptake by muscle cells allows for the proper utilization of amino acids for protein synthesis. This helps to maintain muscle mass and strength. BG has also attracted considerable attention as an anti‐aging agent. Suppression of aging is expected to maintain organ function and improve the general condition of the body.

In the BG group, the Alb level was higher than that in the non‐BG group, suggesting the maintenance or improvement of the protein synthesis capacity of BG. As Alb is mainly synthesized in the liver, the progression of common liver diseases in patients with diabetes and obesity, such as fatty liver disease and NAFLD, decreases Alb levels. Metformin is known to have an inhibitory effect on these liver diseases [25], and the improvement in liver function may have led to the maintenance of Alb values.

In the longitudinal study, only GNRI was affected by diabetes medications. The CONUT score is based on laboratory data of Alb, LYMP, and tCHO and thus reflects nutritional status, including protein synthesis, immunity, and lipid metabolism. However, since this nutritional index was originally established for elderly inpatients [11], it may not have adequately assessed the nutritional status of the outpatients in this study. In contrast, the GNRI includes BMI, which is calculated based on height and weight, as an evaluation component. Weight change is a major indicator of nutritional status, and indicators using weight for assessment, GNRI, may reflect the nutritional status more strongly. In addition, the GNRI has been reported to be superior to the CONUT score for predicting patient prognosis from malnutrition [40, 41]. Furthermore, GNRI scores defined as continuous variables have been reported as predictors of mortality and heart failure [42, 43]. Although changes in GNRI appear to have a small impact due to the limited observation period of this study, diabetes treatment is generally lifelong. Therefore, long‐term use of SGLT1i and BG could significantly impact patient prognosis. Nevertheless, whether the improvement in nutritional indices observed here would be enhanced by chronic use of these medications remains uncertain, and this issue should be clarified through longer term observational studies.

The results of this study suggest that a specific diabetes medication may have a modest but beneficial effect on the nutritional indices GNRI. Given the importance of regularly assessing nutritional indices and intervening early to improve them, novel insights into the relationship between therapeutic agents and nutritional indices are of clinical significance. However, given the inherent limitations of retrospective studies, it remains uncertain whether this medication directly reduces the risk of sarcopenia or other conditions associated with poor nutritional status. GNRI itself has been linked to clinically important outcomes, including renal impairment, mortality, and diabetic sarcopenia, in numerous recent studies [44, 45, 46]. However, most existing studies have focused on baseline GNRI values, and ΔGNRI, longitudinal changes in GNRI, have not been sufficiently evaluated. Currently, there is a lack of evidence quantifying the relationship between GNRI improvement and reductions in disease risk. For example, no published studies have established that a specific percentage increase in GNRI corresponds to a defined reduction in mortality or incidence of sarcopenia. This absence of quantitative benchmarks limits the ability to interpret observed GNRI changes in terms of therapeutic benefit. This limitation should be acknowledged, and future prospective studies are warranted to determine whether improvements in GNRI translate into reduced incidence of sarcopenia and related adverse outcomes.

Moreover, it remains unclear whether GNRI is superior to other nutritional indices in reflecting overall health status. Nevertheless, GNRI offers practical advantages: it is simple to calculate using serum albumin and body weight, and its consistent association with mortality and disease progression in older and chronically ill populations supports its clinical utility [7, 8, 9, 10, 47]. Future prospective studies should aim to establish disease‐specific thresholds for ΔGNRI and evaluate its comparative performance against other nutritional and functional measures to determine its role as a reliable marker of therapeutic benefit.

In this study, we surveyed patients who visited Kyushu University Hospital. Most patients were already diagnosed with diabetes at the time of their first visit to Kyushu University Hospital and continued taking diabetes medication. Therefore, information regarding the duration since the diagnosis of diabetes and the duration of time since the initiation of various therapeutic medications is lacking. In addition, the number of patients taking medications such as TZD and Glinide was small, and detailed studies were not possible. Moreover, new classes of diabetes medications, such as GLP‐1/GIP and Glimin, have recently been introduced to the market. A larger survey involving more facilities is required to collect detailed information on a wider range of medications.

In conclusion, the diabetes medications SGLT2i and BG were effective in preventing the worsening of nutritional status. These agents have the potential to prevent or improve the deterioration of general condition and various malnutrition‐related symptoms over time. The selection of diabetes medications based on the results of this study may help improve patient prognosis.
