The East Asian climate-warfare nexus underwent a paradigm shift across pre-industrial and industrial eras
Authors: Huan Chang, Miao Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
East Asia, marked by exceptional climatic diversity, high population density and rapid development, faces severe transboundary climate-security risks under anthropogenic climate extremes. Understanding the climate-warfare nexus—especially by comparing causal pathways under natural versus anthropogenic forcing—is critical for conflict prevention, yet quantitative comparisons across these regimes remain lacking. This study examines the climate-warfare relationship in East Asia from 1400 to 1980 CE using correlation, regression and causal analysis of climate, population, land use and warfare data. Results uncover a paradigm shift: from a slow, feedback-driven Malthusian system in the pre-industrial era to a rapid, socioeconomically-mediated resilient system in the industrial period. Crucially, temperature’s causal role shifted from a linear driver of warfare to a non-linear “threat multiplier”, indicating that climate-warfare pathways are not static but transform over time. This evolution—from a vulnerable system trapped in crisis feedback loops to a resilient one capable of absorbing shocks—implies that under modern anthropogenic warming, climate anomalies may interact with social systems in non-linear and potentially abrupt ways, necessitating advanced strategies to mitigate future climate-driven conflict risks. By integrating causal analysis, this work addresses key research gaps and offers new insights into the evolving nature of climate-warfare nexus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47182-6.

Introduction

Warfare represents a prominent manifestation of civilizational collision, occurring alongside rapid global warming that is increasing extreme climate events1. Understanding the climate-warfare nexus therefore informs both climate adaptation policies—such as food security enhancement and conflict early-warning systems—and provides a scientific basis for addressing future security challenges under ongoing climate crisis. The relationship between climatic factors and warfare outbreak has attracted substantial scientific interest2 and has been examined across multiple regions and timescales3–12. These studies, employing historical reconstructions or instrumental observations alongside qualitative and quantitative methods, have significantly advanced knowledge—revealing climate-war frequency linkages3, identifying climate as an armed-conflict risk factor9, and demonstrating that pre-industrial cooling amplified disasters and conflicts in China and Europe5,6, while recent warming increased civil-war risk in sub-Saharan Africa4. Nevertheless, the pathways through which climate change influences warfare remain complex and difficult to define13,14, involving multifaceted interactions between direct climatic stressors (e.g., climate extremes) and indirect socioeconomic mediators (e.g., resource scarcity, agricultural failure, demographic pressure, and social resilience)15,16. These analytical challenges are compounded by uncertainties in climate data, particularly reconstructions from natural proxies and historical archives associated with low signal-to-noise. Collectively, these issues sustain critical knowledge gaps concerning the precise mechanisms of the climate-warfare nexus12,17,18.

East Asia exhibits distinctive characteristics—exceptional geoclimatic diversity (see Fig. S1), high-density populations, rapid economic growth, frequent monsoon-related climate disasters19 and complex geopolitical tensions20. Historically, climate-induced stressors such as natural disasters, agricultural disruption, water scarcity, and displacement have catalyzed warfare in the region2,3,8,10,11,21,22. Under current and projected anthropogenic climate extremes23,24, East Asia faces unparalleled transboundary environmental challenges that elevate climate-related regional security risks25,26. A thorough understanding of the historical and modern climate-warfare nexus in this region is thus imperative for developing climate-resilient governance and sustainable policies that incorporate evolving warfare risks. Although several studies have explored this nexus in East Asian sub-regions2,3,10,11,21,22, most focus exclusively either on the pre-industrial period (dominated by natural climate variability) or the recent decades (with predominant anthropogenic forcing). A systematic comparison under a unified data and methodological framework is lacking—a methodological gap that extends beyond East Asia. Consequently, key questions remain unresolved18: (1) Do conclusions from contemporary climate-warfare studies align with historical findings? (2) Is the nexus time-varying or invariant across temporal scales? (3) Can historical studies provide generalizable guidance for future climate-induced warfare?

Addressing these questions is critical to advancing academic understanding and policy-relevant knowledge18. This study therefore investigates East Asia’s climate-warfare nexus using temperature, precipitation, land use, population and war frequency data from 1400 to 1980 CE (The selection of the 1400–1980 CE as our study timeframe is primarily because the warfare catalog data for this region can only be reliably extended back to 1400 CE27). In addition to applying conventional correlation and regression analysis methods, we introduce causal analysis to identify causal linkages. We further compare the climate-warfare nexus during the pre-1850 CE cooling period (natural forcing dominance) and the post-1850 CE warming phase (anthropogenic influence emergence). This comparative approach reveals how the climate-warfare nexus transforms across distinct climatic contexts. This study aims to: (1) disentangle natural and anthropogenic climate effects on warfare, and (2) test whether the climate-warfare nexus exhibits temporal universality or context-dependency, thereby bridging critical knowledge gaps in current research.

The onset of the industrial era marks a fundamental transformation in the trajectory of all variables. Climatically, the temperature series shifted from warming-cooling fluctuations to a sustained, rapid and unidirectional warming trend. Social dynamics also changed, as the pattern of warfare (Fig. 1g) moved from periodic outbreaks to a concentrated cluster of massive wars of unprecedented scale. In the latter half of the nineteenth century, war frequency reached a peak unmatched in the entire study period. A striking visual correlation is the precise alignment of this warfare peak with a sudden slowdown or stagnation in the population growth curve (Fig. 1c). Following this event, the population and land use trajectories changed dramatically. The population curve (Fig. 1c) shifted from its previous gentle slope to a nearly vertical, exponential ascent. In parallel, land use saw an acceleration in the expansion of agricultural land (Fig. 1d), while the curve for built-up land transformed from a flat baseline to an equally steep exponential rise, its pattern synchronous with the population explosion. These trends and temporal correlations across different historical eras imply complex and evolving relationships among the variables, which warrant further quantitative investigation.

Correlations between climate, land use, population and warfare

First, we performed Pearson correlation analysis for each era. During the pre-industrial era (Fig. 2a), the frequency of warfare is significantly correlated with precipitation and social factors. Specifically, it exhibits a significant positive correlation with precipitation anomalies (r = 0.1621, p-value < 0.05), suggesting that increased precipitation is a potential factor exacerbating warfare. Concurrently, the significant negative correlations between warfare frequency and cropland (r = − 0.4441, p-value < 0.05), grazing land (r = − 0.3710, p-value < 0.05), built-up area (r = − 0.3858, p-value < 0.05), and population size (r = − 0.3860, p-value < 0.05) reveal that land use and population expansions are inversely related to warfare frequency during pre-industrial era. Notably, the correlation between the core climate variable (i.e., temperature anomaly) and war frequency is not statistically significant (p-value > 0.05). The correlations between warfare and the factors have shifted markedly during the industrial era (Fig. 2b). In this warmer period, temperature anomaly itself become significantly and negatively correlated with warfare frequency (r = − 0.1860, p-value < 0.05), while the previously significant role of precipitation anomalies diminished, showing no statistical association with warfare. The inverse relationship between other factors and warfare largely persisted, with warfare frequency remaining significantly and negatively correlated with cropland (r = − 0.3217, p-value < 0.05), built-up area (r = − 0.3733, p-value < 0.05) and population size (r = − 0.3675, p-value < 0.05), although its link to grazing land weakened (shifted from − 0.3710 to − 0.1288). The partial correlation analysis refines and extends standard Pearson correlation analysis by isolating the direct relationship between two variables, thereby allowing for more accurate and interpretable results. Unlike Pearson correlation, which captures the total association (including indirect effects from other variables), partial correlation reveals the independent association by controlling for the linear influence of all other variables in the model. The partial correlation analysis conducted in this study aims to reveal the pure association between warfare and key climatic and social factors after controlling for the linear influence of all other variables in the model. In the pre-industrial era, this analysis indicates a significant negative correlation between temperature and warfare frequency (r = − 0.1005, p-value < 0.01) (Fig. 3a). This finding clarifies the non-significant result from the simple Pearson correlation analysis, suggesting that after controlling for confounding precipitation and social variables, the independent effect of a colder climate promoting warfare becomes evident. Cropland is significantly and negatively correlated with warfare (r = − 0.2205, p-value < 0.01). In contrast, built-up area is significantly and positively correlated with it (r = 0.1570, p-value < 0.01). The sign reversal for built-up area compared to its Pearson correlation analysis indicates that once the overarching effects of population and agricultural factors are removed, higher urban density itself is associated with more warfare. The correlations for grazing land (r = − 0.0780) and precipitation (r = 0.0574) are not statistically significant (i.e., p-value > 0.05). Upon the industrial era, we observe a fundamental shift in this pattern of association (Fig. 3b). Grazing land is now significantly and positively correlated with the frequency of war (r = 0.3766, p-value < 0.01), and cropland shows a similar, though less strong, positive correlation (r = 0.1891, p-value < 0.05). This shift to a positive association for both agricultural land types is a key finding that emerges after controlling for other variables. Concurrently, population is significantly and negatively correlated with warfare (r = − 0.2793, p-value < 0.01). Independent effects of climate, land use, population on warfare

To further quantify the independent effects of various factors on warfare, and on the premise that the conditions for (partial) correlation are met, we performed Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression analysis for the two eras (Fig. 4). The results are highly consistent with the partial correlation analysis and reveal significant differences in the key drivers of warfare and the magnitude of their effects across the two periods. During the pre-industrial era (Fig. 4a), the OLS regression shows that cropland has a significant warfare-suppressing effect on warfare; specifically, an increase of one million hectares in its area is associated with a decrease of approximately 0.2325 wars per year (p-value < 0.01). Conversely, built-up area is a significant warfare-promoting factor, where an increase of 10,000 hectares is associated with an increase of approximately 0.2944 wars per year (p-value < 0.01). Crucially, the OLS regression confirms and quantifies the independent effect of climate: the regression coefficient for temperature is significantly negative, where a 1 °C decrease in temperature associated with an increase of approximately 0.8570 wars per year (p-value < 0.05). Moreover, OLS regression analysis indicates that other factors are not statistically significant influencing factors for the outbreak of the warfare (i.e., p-value > 0.05).Fig. 4Results of OLS analysis. In remarkably contrast to the pre-industrial era, the factors driving warfare underwent a fundamental shift upon entering the industrial era (Fig. 4b), a point reflected in both the partial correlation and OLS analysis. Both analysis show that the direct relationship between temperature and warfare become non-significant (p-value > 0.05), implying its role might be mediated by other factors. New internal social dynamics take precedence: consistent with the key shift revealed by the partial correlation analysis, the OLS regression shows that the regression coefficient for grazing land becomes significantly positive, with its contribution being an increase of approximately 0.0632 wars per year for every one million-hectare increase in its area (p-value < 0.01). Simultaneously, both analysis confirm that the contribution of population manifests as a significant negative effect; specifically, an increase of one million people is associated with a decrease of approximately 0.0184 wars per year (p-value < 0.01). It is noteworthy that the negative coefficient for population in the OLS regression analysis is consistent with previous finding that social calamities lead to population decline28.

Causal pathways of climate, land use, population and warfare

To move beyond correlation and regression and further unveil the causal pathways and temporal lags influencing the outbreak of warfare, we performed a Granger causality analysis (Fig. 5), dynamically selecting the optimal lag for each potential driver. During the pre-industrial era (Fig. 5a), the climate-warfare nexus is a interconnected system characterized by multi-year lags. The analysis of factors causing warfare shows that variations in temperature have a linear causal effect on war frequency with a 2-years lag (lag order = 2). This finding is consistent with the OLS regression analysis, which also identifies temperature as a significant linear predictor (Fig. 4a). Concurrently, precipitation (Bayesian Information Criterion, BIC improvement: 79.73%) and population (BIC improvement: 70.58%) influenced warfare through more complex, non-linear pathways, also with a 2-years lag (lag order = 2). Land use factors, including cropland (BIC improvement: 166.44%), grazing land (BIC improvement: 100.68%) and built-up area (BIC improvement: 105.44%), also emerged as significant non-linear drivers, but with a longer 4-year lag (lag order = 4). This clarifies that while factors like cropland and built-up area are significant in the linear OLS model, their true influence operates through a complex, delayed, non-linear mechanism. More importantly, the analysis reveals a powerful feedback loop, where warfare itself is a significant non-linear cause of changes in other variables—temperature, precipitation, population, cropland, grazing land and built-up area—each with a consistent 2-year lag (lag order = 2). The impact of warfare is particularly pronounced on grazing land (BIC improvement: 421.29%) and built-up area (BIC improvement: 441.61%), indicating that warfare profoundly reshapes the social environment. Entering the industrial era (Fig. 5b), the causal network driving warfare becomes markedly simpler and faster, and the strong bidirectional feedback of the previous era largely disappears. The drivers of warfare shift entirely to a non-linear mode, with the primary factors showing a more rapid response. Specifically, temperature (BIC improvement: 214.64%), grazing land (BIC improvement: 281.31%), and built-up area (BIC improvement: 255.07%) become the sole significant causal factors of warfare, each manifesting its influence after a 1-year lag (lag order = 1). Critically, the factors that are key drivers in the pre-industrial era—cropland, population, and precipitation—lost their direct causal influence on warfare. The only remaining causal feedback is a non-linear influence from warfare to built-up area, which also operates on a 1-year lag (lag order = 1). This structural transformation—from a multi-factor, long-lag, mixed-pathway system to a streamlined, faster-responding, purely non-linear one—profoundly reflects the evolution of societal responses to internal and external shocks following the shift in modes of production.

Discussion

Our quantitative results reveal a fundamental transformation in East Asia’s climate-warfare relationship over the past six centuries—from a pre-industrial agricultural system dominated by Malthusian pressures to an industrialized system characterized by rapid, socioeconomically-mediated responses. Here, we conduct a brief discussion into the potential mechanisms underlying both systems through integrating the quantitative analysis presented above with existing knowledge on the climate-society-warfare relationship drawn from previous researches.

The pre-industrial era: an agricultural system dominated by Malthusian pressures

Pre-industrial East Asian society constituted a classic Malthusian system29, characterized by high population pressure against limited agricultural resources, with slow technological progress maintaining most of the population at subsistence levels. This inherent vulnerability meant climate deterioration could easily trigger “regulating checks” (a concept adapted from Malthusian theory, representing mechanisms that increase the death rate to bring the population back within the limits of resource carrying capacity) like warfare and famine30. Our study provides quantitative evidence for these shock-response mechanisms.

Causality analysis reveals warfare drivers operated through a complex network with varied response times. Direct subsistence shocks—defined here as immediate threats to food security, where climatic anomalies (temperature and precipitation) act as external shocks to crop yields and high population density acts as an internal pressure that reduces per-capita resource availability—showed a 2-year causal lag. A 2-year causal lag does not measure generational population growth, but rather the collapse time of societal buffering mechanisms under stress. The first year (0–1) represents buffer absorption, where climate shocks cause agricultural failure mitigated by existing reserves, though grain prices rise and local unrest emerges31. The second year (1–2) marks system collapse: persistent crop failures exhaust buffers, causing widespread famine, social disorder, and ultimately organized warfare. This pattern manifested vividly during the mid-seventeenth century Ming-Qing transition (see Table 1), where severe cold and drought devastated agriculture, triggering rebellions and creating conditions for Manchu invasion32. This 2-year response represents the typical timeframe for a Malthusian system to trigger warfare after stability mechanisms fail. Structural land-use pressures (cropland, grazing land, built-up area) exhibited longer 4-year lags, indicating “slower-burning crises” requiring more time to reach tipping points. Here we want to note that unlike tipping points in climate dynamics, a tipping point here represents a social threshold where environmental or social pressure exceeds a society’s buffer capacity33. Below this threshold, warfare is avoided; once exceeded, the exhaustion of buffers triggers warfare. These non-linear transitions align with the framework of Negative Social Tipping Processes (NSTPs)34, where environmental shocks destabilize social order. Moreover, the identified thresholds represent a phase transition between stability and conflict, consistent with the tipping cascades35 in the climate-security nexus. Crucially, the system displayed powerful bidirectional causality—warfare itself significantly non-linearly altered population, land use and socioeconomic structure with 2-year lags, quantitatively demonstrating warfare’s role as a core “regulating checks” mechanism that actively reshaped society3,36. Within this framework, “linear driver” refers to a direct, proportional response mechanism. For instance, in pre-industrial East Asia, temperature exhibited a predictable effect where a 1℃ drop directly increased conflict risk after a 2-year lag due to immediate agricultural failure. This pattern characterizes a system that lacked sufficient mediating mechanisms to buffer climatic extremes. Among all variables, temperature uniquely exhibited direct linear causality, reinforcing its role as a fundamental, dose-dependent shock where colder conditions directly increased warfare risk. This relationship proved particularly critical in monsoon-influenced East Asia37,38. In contrast, other factors showed complex non-linear characteristics. While OLS regression suggested simple negative correlation between cropland expansion and warfare, Granger analysis revealed non-linear pathways indicating tipping points—moderate expansion may stabilize society, while excessive expansion could trigger conflicts39. Similar non-linearity characterized precipitation (both drought and flood being detrimental) and grazing land (reflecting complex agro-pastoral dynamics), indicating the pre-industrial conflict system operated through multiple threshold-dependent pressures7. This statistical feedback quantifies the historical reality of agrarian East Asia, where society was frequently trapped in a resource-population-conflict loop due to the lack of external technological buffers.

The industrial era transition: a socioeconomically mediated, rapid-response system

The industrial era restructured climate-warfare relationships, replacing Malthusian dynamics with new logic driven by technology, globalized markets and enhanced state capacity. The causal lag for all significant conflict drivers shortened from 2 years to 1 year, and all causal pathways became non-linear. Climate’s role transformed from a direct driver of subsistence crises to an indirect “threat multiplier”39. In this context, climate does not function as a singular cause of warfare, but rather amplifies pre-existing socioeconomic and political vulnerabilities, thereby increasing the probability of warfare in a non-linear fashion40. The local temperature-food supply link was buffered by technology and trade, while climate anomalies began affecting the broader industrial economy, which is consistent with established non-linear temperature-productivity relationships41. The most striking evidence of restructuring lies in the near-disappearance of the strong “war-reshapes-society” feedback loop. Warfare no longer significantly impacted agriculture or population, indicating decoupling from subsistence bases and emergence of a more resilient system. This likely reflects enhanced state capacity, market integration and technological advances preventing local conflicts from spiraling into subsistence crises42. Globalized trade and new transportation broke agriculture’s local dependence, while industrialization reduced economic reliance on farming—containing warfare’s impacts and preventing systemic collapse43. The Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901 CE) exemplifies this new dynamic. The warfare was catalyzed by a sequence of extreme events: a catastrophic Yellow River flood in 1898 CE, followed by a severe drought in 1899–1900 CE. These disasters led to mass displacement and unemployment, which then interacted with socio-political grievances against imperialist encroachment to spark the uprising44—unlike purely subsistence-driven pre-industrial conflicts.

In this era, precipitation, cropland and population lost direct causal influence, while grazing land, built-up areas and temperature became key non-linear drivers. Built-up area’s dual role—both driving and being affected by warfare—reflects modern conflict’s shift toward urban and strategic struggles. This new driver combination indicates warfare’s transformation from internal Malthusian struggles to conflicts over resources, territory and political control during state-building, imperialism and industrial competition. The First Sino-Japanese War’s drivers—trade disputes, sovereignty and hegemony rather than food shortages45—exemplify this shift, as does grazing land’s emergence reflecting strategic competition over frontier regions46.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides a diachronic, causality-oriented analysis of the climate-warfare nexus in East Asia, yielding four principal findings. First, we identify two distinct historical paradigms: a slow, bidirectional Malthusian system during the pre-industrial era, contrasted with a rapid, largely unidirectional socioeconomically-mediated system in the industrial period. Second, the causal role of temperature has evolved, shifting from a linear driver of warfare to a non-linear “threat multiplier”. Third, our results underscore the critical importance of accounting for non-linearity and temporal lags; the application of non-linear causal analysis proves essential for revealing authentic pathways and feedbacks, while the observed shift in response lags signifies fundamental changes in societal response mechanisms. Fourth, this historical transformation offers a critical precedent for contemporary challenges, suggesting that modern anthropogenic warming may interact with complex social systems in non-linear and potentially abrupt ways.

By employing non-linear Granger causality analysis, this research advances beyond correlation to identify causal pathways and their temporal dynamics, thereby addressing recognized limitations of conventional methods in the existing literature. We not only elucidate how the climate-warfare relationship manifests but also reveal how it structurally transforms over time. The two distinct systems identified here introduce a necessary layer of mechanistic and historical depth absent from prior studies confined to single eras. In offering a quantitative, comparative examination across both pre-industrial and industrial periods, and by innovatively integrating non-linear causal analysis, this work provides new insights that address its research objectives and are poised to deepen and broaden current understanding of the climate-warfare relationship.

Nevertheless, we recognize that the research presented here remains incomplete and warrants further in-depth investigation. For instance, the outbreak of warfare is in fact modulated by complex non-linear interactions among multiple factors, although social factors such as land use and population were incorporated alongside climate variables—representing an advancement over many previous studies that focused solely on climatic factors such as temperature, precipitation, drought and floods—other critical dimensions, including economic (e.g., grain prices, commodity prices), political, religious and cultural factors, had not been quantitatively analyzed in this study. During the pre-industrial era, climatic variability—particularly extreme cooling and persistent drought—significantly increased the statistical probability of large-scale wars in East Asia by undermining agricultural output and resource availability. However, it must be emphasized that this influence might be weaker than and embedded within more fundamental political and economic structural factors13–16. For instance, the decline of central authority, collapse of fiscal systems, or governance failure often served as more direct precursors to warfare outbreak. Therefore, it is essential to situate climate within a multi-dimensional view of warfare drivers18, which also helps prevent an overdeterministic or exaggerated interpretation of our findings. However, in this study, we are unable to delve more deeply into the aforementioned, more complex socio-economically related factors. This omission is primarily due to current constraints in the spatiotemporal availability, reliability, and potential confidentiality of relevant data, particularly quantitative measures of political, religious and cultural factors. Furthermore, our study also reveals a bidirectional feedback between warfare and climatic factors (as shown in Fig. 5a). However, constrained by the primary objectives of this research and the limitations of the data and methods employed, we are unable to elucidate the precise physical mechanisms through which warfare influences climate (e.g., temperature, precipitation) here, which represents another limitation of this study. Therefore, future research should integrate broader contextual factors—including political, cultural, and religious dimensions—and adopt more advanced causal-detection methods, such as non‑linear state‑space modeling and transfer‑entropy‑based inference. These techniques impose fewer functional-form assumptions, handle non‑Gaussian data effectively, and offer greater robustness than Granger causality in revealing causal pathways. Applying them can more clearly uncover the climate-warfare relationship and yield stronger conclusions about its dynamics.

Data and methods

Climate data

Temperature and precipitation exert profound influence on human society. Extreme temperature and precipitation events can directly result in food security issues by reducing agricultural and livestock production, as well as directly affect social stability and population migration, all of which can lead to social tensions and conflicts. Therefore, temperature and precipitation are the most fundamental variables used in the study of the climate-warfare relationship. In this study, temperature and precipitation data are extracted from the Last Millennium Climate Reanalysis Project v2 (LMRv2)47, which was reconstructed based on the paleoclimate data assimilation method48 through optimally fusing transient climate simulation of the last millennium with climatic proxy (e.g., tree ring). LMRv2 provides global climate reanalysis for the period 0–2000 CE on a 2° × 2° grid at an annual resolution. Previous verifications showed that LMRv2 aligns well with instrumental data in both spatial and temporal domains47,49. Due to its spatiotemporal continuity and high reliability, LMRv2 has become a key resource for studying global and regional climate changes over the last millennium49,50. In this study, we extracted the gridded 2-m air temperature and precipitation data (hereinafter T2m and Pre, respectively) from LMRv2 according to the current boundary of East Asia, and then calculated the regionally-averaged annual T2m and Pre time series during 1400–1980 CE.

Land use and population data

Cropland area directly determines food production capacity, grazing land availability governs the subsistence space of nomadic populations, built-up land expansion leads to the loss of land for farmers and herdsmen, and population density intensifies resource competition. Empirical evidences have demonstrated that declining food supply, shrinking living space and intensified resource competition are immediate triggers of rising conflict risk17,18. Including land-use and population variables alongside temperature and precipitation in this study, therefore, moves the analysis beyond simple climate-warfare correlation toward a mechanism-driven climate-resources-society coupling framework, substantially deepening our theoretical understanding of climate-warfare nexus. In this study, the land use (e.g., cropland, grazing land and built-up area in hectares) and population data of the East Asia countries during 1400–1980 CE are downloaded from Our World in Data website (https://ourworldindata.org/) (Please see the Supplementary Information for detailed sources of the land use and population data on this website). Because the land use and population data are at irregular time intervals, by referring to the previous methods3,31, we first take the logarithm of the data points, then perform linear interpolation, and finally anti-logged back, to create an annual time series. As reported by previous study31, this processing method avoids any distortions of the population growth rate resulting from the data interpolation process, and is convenient for statistical analysis with the annual continuous temperature, precipitation and warfare data.

Warfare data

We derived the East Asia warfare series from the global conflict catalogue27—the most exhaustive record of historical wars currently available and one widely employed by earlier scholars51,52. The catalogue documents belligerents, start and end years, and the duration of each conflict worldwide that resulted in at least 32 battle-related fatalities. In line with its definition, “war” in this study refers specifically to conflicts causing a minimum of 32 fatalities per year. “Armed conflict,” in contrast, represents a broader category encompassing lower-intensity hostilities, terrorism, or localized civil unrest that may not meet this fatality threshold. By focusing on “war” as defined above, we ensure that the data remain comparable and consistently traceable across the six-century scope of our analysis. For each calendar year we tally every active war within the region—counting one war as 1, two wars as 2, and so on—thereby treating multi-year conflicts such as the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895 CE) as a separate event in each year of their duration. Aggregating these annual counts yields a continuous time series that captures intra-state wars, inter-state wars among the East Asian countries, and wars between these countries and neighboring Asian states. Consistent with definition in ref. 27, the East Asia is delimited to the territories encompassed by the present-day borders of China, Mongolia, the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, the Republic of Korea and Japan.

Analytical methods

In addition to employing traditional methods such as Pearson correlation analysis and multiple linear regression to examine the climate-warfare relationship, this study further adopts partial correlation analysis to determine the degree of partial correlation between one or more independent variables and the number of warfare. Moreover, for a long time, correlation and regression analysis have predominated in studying climate-warfare relationship. However, correlation and causation are fundamentally distinct concepts. Correlation indicates a general association, whereas causation implies a directional dependency between variables53. While causally-linked variables must be correlated, correlation does not necessarily imply causation54. In this case, traditional correlation and regression analysis thus are inadequate for addressing causation-related problems55 in systems like the climate-warfare nexus in which complex mechanisms are involved7,18,56. A typical method to compensate for the limitations of traditional correlation and regression analysis in inferring causal relationships between variables is the Granger causality test57. Granger causality analysis has been widely used in many fields such as economy, physiology, computational neuroscience and Earth science to study the causal relationship between various variables, particularly, it has been applied in climate attributions to investigate the influence of one climate variable on another variables58,59. We emphasize that increasing robust evidence suggests the climate-warfare nexus rarely follows a simple linear pattern, but rather exhibits complex non-linear dynamics7,40,56. In this case, if a linear Granger causality is used to analyze the causality between climate and warfare, unrealistic conclusion may be drawn or the causality between climate and warfare can not be detected60,61. Therefore, this study further extends the conventional linear Granger causality analysis to non-linear case by adopting Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) model62 to serve as the autoregressive model of Granger causality analysis, following the approach recommended by a previous study58. Please refer to the Supplemental Information for details regarding the principle, methodology descriptions and implementation procedures of Granger causality analysis.
