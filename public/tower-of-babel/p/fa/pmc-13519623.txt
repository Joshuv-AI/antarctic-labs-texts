A murine stable thrombosis model enabling therapeutic evaluation of antithrombotic drugs
Authors: Charlotte E. Fauth, Andréa Thevenot, Andrei Garzon, Anita Eckly, Pierre H. Mangin, Alexandra Yakusheva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•An adapted FeCl3 model generates stable thrombi suitable for postonset therapeutic testing.•The thrombi display a heterogeneous composition (platelets, fibrin, red blood cells) that resembles that of human thrombi after thrombectomy.

Introduction

Arterial thrombosis refers to the formation of a thrombus, primarily composed of platelets and fibrin, which partially or completely obstructs blood flow within an artery and usually occurs following atherosclerotic plaque disruption. This leads to serious ischemic events such as stroke or myocardial infarction, which according to the World Health Organization, constitute the third leading cause of death worldwide and the leading cause of acquired disability in adults.1,2 Over the past 30 years, a large array of antithrombotic agents has been developed, including aspirin, P2Y12 antagonists, and αIIbβ3 blockers.3,4 However, because these agents favor the occurrence of bleeding events in patients, there remains a need to develop new, efficient, and safe therapeutic strategies.

Over the past decades, many different murine models of arterial thrombosis have been established to evaluate the efficacy of new candidate drugs. Classical strategies to induce thrombosis in healthy murine vessels involve damaging the vessel wall chemically,5 mechanically,6 with electricity,7 or by laser.8,9 The most widely used model consists of applying a Whatman paper soaked in FeCl3 solution to the external surface of a vessel, usually the carotid artery. This model has become popular notably because of its technical simplicity and reproducibility,10 even though the exact mechanism by which it triggers thrombosis is still not fully understood. Some studies have shown protection against thrombosis in glycoprotein VI-deficient mice, suggesting exposure of collagen within the subendothelial matrix.11,12 Nevertheless, this mechanism has been questioned in other studies, in which FeCl3-induced damage did not promote endothelial denudation.13,14 It has been suggested that the formation of a thrombus results from the complexation of Fe3+ ions with plasma proteins and red blood cell (RBC) membranes, promoting aggregation of platelets through the coagulation cascade.13,15, 16, 17 However, other groups detected no obvious RBC adhesion14-18 and proposed that thrombosis is initiated by tissue factor on spheres filled with FeCl3, present on the surface of endothelial cells.14 These different scenarios probably depend on the type of vessel to which FeCl3 is applied.19,20

All these models have enabled major advances in the field, allowing a better understanding of the mechanisms involved in arterial thrombosis. They have also enabled evaluation of the effect and efficacy of different antithrombotic agents currently used in clinical practice or under development.21,22 However, these models have mainly been used to examine the effects of antithrombotic agents on the prevention of thrombosis. This is because of the biphasic nature of thrombus formation, which is shared by these models, consisting of an initial phase of growth followed by a natural disintegration, leaving a patch covering the site of lesion.23 This biphasic evolution of the thrombus has been explained by the presence of regional heterogeneity within it. The thrombus is composed of a core and a shell, the latter being partially removed by blood flow. The core contains tightly packed activated platelets and is the region where high levels of thrombin are generated, allowing the formation of dense fibrin fibers that stabilize the thrombus. The outer shell is more permeable, containing loosely packed P-selectin–negative platelets, low thrombin concentrations, and little or no fibrin; therefore, making it more susceptible to embolization with the flow forces.23,24 The reversible nature of this process precludes investigation of the impact of pharmacological agents on an already formed thrombus.

In this study, we developed a novel arterial thrombosis model to evaluate the effects of pharmacological agents on preformed, stable thrombi and their capacity to recanalize the vessel. Thrombosis was initiated by applying 2 strips of 7.5% FeCl3-saturated Whatman paper to the adventitial surface of the murine carotid artery. Histological analysis revealed no exposure of the subendothelial matrix, which was confirmed by electron microscopy. Intravital video microscopy revealed rapid recruitment of platelets, followed by fibrin formation and the accumulation of RBCs, findings that were further validated by confocal imaging. Pharmacological characterization showed that the thrombus was sensitive to antiplatelet agents but was only partially sensitive or insensitive to anticoagulants and thrombolytic agents. Altogether, these data suggest that our model may be suitable for studying the curative effects of pharmacological agents on an already formed thrombus.

Methods

Animals and treatment

C57BL6-J mice were provided by Charles River Laboratories and were maintained in the animal facilities of the Établissement Français du Sang Grand-Est.

FeCl3 injury thrombosis model

Mice (aged 7-10 weeks) were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Lidocaine (10 mg/kg) was injected subcutaneously at the site of surgery. DiOC6 (3,3′-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide; 400μM), DiD (1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine, 4-chlorobenzenesulfonate salt; 10μM), and an anti-fibrin monoclonal antibody (59D8, 0.34 mg/mL) were injected via IV (2.5 μL/g) to label platelets, RBCs, and fibrin, respectively. The common carotid artery was exposed, and vascular injury was caused by applying 2 strips of 7.5% FeCl3-saturated Whatman filter paper (1 mm × 3 mm) to both sides of the carotid artery for 2.5 minutes. All pharmacological agents were administered through IV injection 15 minutes after the initiation of thrombosis. The thrombus was visualized in real time using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems SA, Wetzlar, Germany) coupled to a charge-coupled device camera (ORCA-fusion C14440, Hamamatsu). Data were acquired using MetaMorph software for 45 minutes and analyzed using ImageJ Fiji software. To determine the time of occlusion, a Doppler probe (moorVascular Monitoring System-LDF2) was placed on the surface of the carotid artery, and blood flow was recorded using Vascular Monitoring System software. At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized by dislocation of the cervical vertebrae.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPrism software (version 10). Values are reported as the mean ± the standard error of the mean. A normality test was performed for all comparisons. To compare 2 groups with data after a normal distribution, the difference in variances was assessed. When the variances were not significantly different, an unpaired t test was applied, and when the variances were different, a Welch t test was applied. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, a Mann-Whitney U test was performed. To compare >2 groups, if the data followed a normal distribution, the difference in variances was determined. When the variances were equal, a 1-way analysis of variance test was used, and when the variances were different, a Welch analysis of variance test was used. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, a Kruskal-Wallis test was performed.

Detailed methods are described in the supplemental Methods.

Ethical approval for the experiments was obtained from the French Ministry of Research (animal facility authorization, G-67-482-10 and l'Autorisation de Projets utilisant des Animaux à des Fins Scientifiques 50849-2024080817029838).

Results

Kinetics of thrombus formation in the stable thrombosis model

To obtain a stable thrombus, we adapted the classical FeCl3 injury model of the carotid artery14 by placing 2 strips of Whatman paper on both sides of the vessel (Figure 1A). Given that we expected to observe the formation of a large, stable thrombus whose composition would be close to that of thrombi found in patients, we decided to stain not only platelets (green) but also fibrin (cyan) and RBCs (red). Intravital microscopy revealed a strong platelet signal that appeared soon after injury (2.8 ± 0.8 minutes) and increased over time to occupy almost all of the vessel lumen (9.3 ± 3.2 minutes), before decreasing to 50 ± 9% of its maximum value at the end of the experiment (45 minutes; Figure 1B-C; supplemental Figure 1A-C). Interestingly, signals for fibrin and RBCs were detected 7 ± 5 and 4 ± 3 minutes after injury, respectively, and continuously increased throughout the experiment (Figure 1B-C; supplemental Figure 1A-C). Doppler probe measurements detected the arrest of blood flow at 14.3 ± 3.2 minutes, indicating the formation of an occlusive thrombus (Figure 1D). Combining all 3 signals for platelets, fibrin, and RBCs, which together define the thrombus, we observed a plateau-type curve of thrombus formation (Figure 1E), with the thrombus area remaining within 14% ± 8% of its maximum value from 16.6 ± 9.9 minutes after injury. This stability of the thrombus differed from the typical dynamics observed after application of only 1 strip of 7.5% FeCl3-saturated Whatman paper, where thrombus formation was seen to be biphasic when combining platelet, fibrin, and RBC signals (Figure 1F-H; supplemental Figure 1D-F). In this case, Doppler probe measurements did not detect the arrest of blood flow, indicating the formation of a nonocclusive thrombus (Figure 1I). Although prolonged application of only 1 strip of Whatman paper saturated with 20% (but not 10%) FeCl3 induced occlusive thrombus formation, this type of injury resulted in massive burns on the vessel wall, which prevented ideal imaging of the thrombus. This precluded the use of such experimental conditions to develop a model of stable thrombosis (supplemental Figure 1G-K). Histological characterization of the level of vessel injury and thrombus composition

To determine the level of endothelial injury in our stable thrombosis model, we performed a detailed histological analysis of the vessel wall 45 minutes after application of FeCl3. The natural undulation of a healthy endothelium was seen to be lost in sections of the vessel exposed to FeCl3 (Figure 2A-B). In addition, the injured endothelium appeared blue, certainly because of the presence of iron molecules (Figure 2B). These results are perfectly in line with a previous characterization of the FeCl3-injury model based on 1 patch.14 To confirm the absence of exposure of subendothelial matrix proteins, which could contribute to the initiation of thrombus formation, we performed transmission electron microscopy analysis, which showed a preserved endothelial layer and the presence of spherical bodies, consistent with previous observations14 (Figure 2C). Hence, the application of 2 strips of 7.5% FeCl3-saturated filter paper did not induce endothelial denudation. We next characterized thrombus composition at 15 and 45 minutes after injury (Figure 2D-E). Histological analysis revealed that the thrombi exhibited marked flow-directed heterogeneity, reflecting the spatial variability described in occlusive arterial thrombi from patients25,26 (Figure 2F). As the downstream region was predominantly erythrocyte-rich, consistent with flow cessation, and its composition remained unchanged between 15 and 45 minutes (Figure 2G), our analyses focused on the upstream region in direct contact with flowing blood. We observed that 15 minutes after injury, 82% ± 9% of the thrombus was occupied by platelets, 45% ± 24% by fibrin, and 14% ± 11% by RBCs (the percentages exceed 100% because of spatial overlap between the components; Figure 2H; supplemental Figure 2A). Interestingly, although the total thrombus area remained constant throughout the 45 minutes of data acquisition, the ratio of its components varied. There was a significant reduction in platelet area to 55% ± 28% (Figure 2H; supplemental Figure 2A), whereas fibrin and RBC areas did not change significantly (60% ± 22% and 32% ± 27%, respectively), resulting in a relative enrichment of the thrombus in RBCs (Figure 2H; supplemental Figure 2A). Effects of antiplatelet agents in the stable thrombosis model

To evaluate the effects of postonset antiplatelet treatment, an anti-αIIbβ3 agent (GR144053 trihydrochloride), an anti-P2Y12 agent (cangrelor), or aspirin was injected 15 minutes after initiation of thrombus formation (Figure 3A-D). The time of drug administration was chosen to correlate with the maximum thrombus area and occurred within the first 5 minutes after blood flow arrest, as detected by the Doppler probe (Figure 1D-E). Intravital microscopy showed that GR 144053 trihydrochloride and cangrelor induced disaggregation of the central intraluminal platelet-rich portion of the thrombus, leading to recanalization of the vessel at the end of the experiment (supplemental Videos 1-3), with a profound decrease in the final thrombus area (area under the curve [AUC]: GR 144053, [5 ± 2] × 107 μm2; Cangrelor, [4 ± 1] × 107 μm2; saline, [12 ± 3] × 107 μm2; Figure 3B-C,E-F). In contrast, no recanalization was obtained after injection of aspirin, and there was no significant difference in thrombus area between the aspirin-treated and control groups (AUC: aspirin, [9 ± 3] × 107 μm2; saline, [12 ± 3] × 107 μm2; Figure 3D-F; supplemental Video 4). At the end of the experiment, aspirin-treated thrombi displayed a reduced fibrin area as compared with controls ([65 ± 23] × 103 μm2 vs [152 ± 52] × 103 μm2), whereas platelet and RBC areas were preserved (Figure 3G; supplemental Figure 3A). Interestingly, no significant rearrangement of thrombus components was detected after injection of GR 144053 trihydrochloride or cangrelor (Figure 3G; supplemental Figure 3B-C). Effects of anticoagulants in the stable thrombosis model

The large amount of fibrin present in the thrombus (Figure 1B-C) is a sign of the implication of coagulation, which led us to evaluate the effects of postonset therapy using anticoagulant agents such as hirudin, unfractionated heparin (UFH), and heparin (Choay). Intravital microscopy showed that none of these agents could induce recanalization of the vessel by the end of the experiment (Figure 4A-D; supplemental Videos 5-7). Injection of hirudin, UFH, or heparin did not cause thrombus disaggregation, and there were no significant differences in thrombus area between treated and control groups (AUC: hirudin, [4 ± 2] × 108 μm2; UFH, [5 ± 1] × 108 μm2; heparin, [3 ± 1] × 108 μm2; saline, [4 ± 1] × 108 μm2; Figure 4E-F). Moreover, anticoagulant treatment did not alter the composition of the final thrombus compared with the controls, and platelets were observed in the lumen of the vessel (Figure 4G; supplemental Figure 4A-C). Nevertheless, injection of heparin induced destabilization of the thrombus, with an increased number of reopening events during the experiment than hirudin, UFH, and control groups (number of events: hirudin, 2.4 ± 1.5; UFH, 3.75 ± 1.5; heparin, 4.3 ± 1.4; saline, 1.8 ± 1.3) (Figure 4H). Effects of thrombolytic agents in the stable thrombosis model

Because the thrombi formed in our stable thrombosis model were particularly rich in fibrin, we looked at the impact of thrombolytic agents, which are clinically used to promote vessel recanalization through fibrin degradation. Intravital microscopy showed that recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) and tenecteplase (TNK) significantly reduced the fibrin area compared with the control (rtPA, [8 ± 7] × 104 μm2; TNK, [6 ± 3] × 104 μm2; saline, [19 ± 6] × 104 μm2; Figure 5A-D; supplemental Videos 8 and 9). However, neither treatment induced complete thrombus disaggregation and vessel recanalization during the experiment, because there was no significant difference in total area of the thrombus between treated and control groups (Figure 5E-F). Interestingly, rtPA treatment induced thrombus destabilization with an increase in the number of reopening events (number of events: rtPA, 3.3 ± 1; TNK, 2.3 ± 1.3; saline, 1 ± 0; Figure 5G). Altogether, these findings indicate that rtPA and TNK exhibited fibrinolytic effect in the stable thrombosis model but did not lead to total recanalization after 30 minutes. Discussion

In this study, we developed and thoroughly characterized an innovative arterial thrombosis model that enables evaluation of the effects of antithrombotic agents on preformed, stabilized thrombi. Unlike conventional thrombosis models, which typically exhibit a biphasic pattern of thrombus formation, our system ensures sustained thrombus stability over an extended period after vascular injury. This feature provides a valuable experimental platform to investigate the curative value of therapeutic agents for recanalization of the thrombus rather than to prevent its initial formation.

To monitor the dynamics of thrombus formation by intravital microscopy, we used combined staining of fibrin, platelets, and RBCs. This allowed us to monitor the stability of the thrombus throughout the experiment and analyze changes in its composition in response to different pharmacological agents. Therefore, our model represents a very useful tool to study the roles of fibrin, platelets, and RBCs in these processes in vivo. Notably, the thrombus displays core-shell dynamics for up to 15 minutes after vessel injury, which were then lost as the thrombus became stable and occlusive, at later time points. Interestingly, even after formation of an occlusive thrombus, as detected by Doppler probe measurements, the platelet signal exhibited a maximum value with a 50% decrease at the end of the experiment. This decrease in platelet area could be explained by shrinkage of the thrombus.35, 36, 37 Platelet fragmentation and apoptosis could also occur as a consequence of overstimulation by the thrombin generated within the thrombus.38,39 In addition, triple staining revealed that the thrombus was richer in fibrin and RBCs at 45 minutes than at 15 minutes after vessel injury. The evolution toward a fibrin-rich thrombus at later stages is reminiscent of thrombi collected from patients by thrombectomy, particularly those of cardioembolic and large artery atherosclerosis origin, which have been reported to contain large amounts of RBCs and/or fibrin.39,40

In classical FeCl3 models, a single patch is applied to the top of the vessel, leading to rapid occlusive thrombus formation but preventing real-time analysis by intravital fluorescence microscopy because of optical interference.16,18 A previous side-application model enabled such imaging but resulted in nonocclusive thrombi, probably because of reduced exposure of the endothelium to FeCl3, potentially through gravitational effects.14 In our current experiments, intravital microscopy indicated that the first cells adhering to the injured vessel wall were platelets, which arrived before RBCs. This was confirmed by histology and is in line with previous reports suggesting that the accumulation of RBCs within the thrombus is most likely the result of trapping.18,14 In addition, we showed that applying 2 strips of Whatman paper soaked with FeCl3 instead of just 1 did not induce the denudation of endothelial cells, as reported earlier by our group.14 These factors collectively distinguish our stable thrombosis model from other FeCl3-induced models that are likewise capable of detecting occlusive thrombus formation.12,41 The combination of a low FeCl3 concentration and 2 spots of injury instead of 1 resulted in a superficial vessel lesion with a low concentration of RBCs,16 allowing slower occlusion of the lumen with an optimal time interval to test postonset treatment.

According to the structure of the thrombus at different time points, the time of drug injection was set to 15 minutes after the initiation of thrombosis. Indeed, histological analysis showed that at this time point the thrombi presented microchannels that were expected to allow penetration of the drugs. Our evaluation of anti-P2Y12 and anti-αIIbβ3 agents confirmed the predictive validity of the model. Aspirin did not have any effect on preformed thrombi, consistent with a recent study by Lee et al, which showed no effect of aspirin on thrombus formation, whereas clopidogrel disrupted the thrombus shell.42 These results confirmed that antiplatelet drugs could reach the interior of the thrombus and disaggregate or weaken a platelet-rich thrombus, depending on their potency. In contrast, later injections (≥20 minutes) of antiplatelet agents had no influence on the composition of the thrombus (data not shown), in line with a more compact, fibrin-rich structure, called a white thrombus by pathologists, which is known to be more resistant to lysis. Clinical reports have described these thrombi to be resistant to thrombolysis, which correlates with our findings.43,44

To overcome this limitation, mechanical recanalization techniques such as thrombectomy have been developed, leading to higher recanalization rates and significantly improved clinical outcomes, including reduced mortality in patients presenting with acute ischemic stroke.45,46 An ambitious perspective of this work, may be to use this stable thrombosis model as a starting point to develop a mouse model of mechanical recanalization, enabling the testing of innovative miniaturized stent retrievers. However, it should be noted that mechanical recanalization also presents some limitations. Indeed, all centers do not have the equipment to perform this type of procedure, and this approach cannot be applied to small arteries. Currently, the only pharmacological agents approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of stroke are rtPA and TNK. In our stable thrombosis model, injection of rtPA or TNK led to a reduction in the fibrin area, suggesting that both agents have a fibrinolytic effect in this model. Therefore, our system represents a unique approach allowing one to study the effect of postonset treatment on subocclusive thrombus formation, which could open the way to test new agents targeting alternative pathways leading to a stable thrombus.

Other experimental models have been used to evaluate the effects of antithrombotic agents on already formed thrombi. For example, stroke models induced by intravascular injection of murine thrombin or by application of FeCl3 to the middle cerebral artery allow postocclusion therapeutic intervention,47, 48, 49 but these approaches are technically demanding and less accessible for routine pharmacological screening. Another example is the electrolytic (Folts) model, which permits drug administration after thrombus formation.7 However, its limitations include the predominantly platelet-rich composition of the thrombi and the absence of sustained thrombus stability. In contrast, our model combines technical simplicity with prolonged stability and a heterogeneous composition of the thrombus, providing a practical and reproducible platform to evaluate postonset therapeutic strategies.

Analysis of our postonset model in comparison with approaches to test preventive therapies indicates that the same drugs may display different effects (Table 1). In the classical FeCl3 model, injection of the direct thrombin inhibitor hirudin before thrombus growth fully blocked thrombus formation.14 By contrast, in the stable thrombosis model, injection of hirudin after thrombus growth had no influence on thrombus recanalization or destabilization (Table 1). Moreover, UFH and heparin Choay, both of which are used in clinical practice and catalyze the antithrombin-mediated inhibition of thrombosis, had no recanalization effect, although heparin significantly destabilized the thrombus and UFH showed a tendency toward destabilization.31 These results could explain how heparins improve patient outcomes by preventing secondary myocardial infarction. Our work indicates that the same molecules do not have the same effects on the formation and stability of a thrombus, whereas protection against thrombus formation does not necessarily imply a capacity for recanalization. In addition, as postonset and preventive forms of antithrombotic treatment resulted in different outcomes, our model provides a tool to evaluate therapeutic candidates that may not prevent thrombus formation but instead promote thrombus destabilization or recanalization, including agents targeting pathways involved in the stabilization of a thrombus.

Several pathways implicated in thrombus stability represent promising directions for future investigation, including von Willebrand factor (VWF), neutrophil activity, and fibrinolysis regulation. In classical FeCl3 models, neutrophil-derived proteases such as elastase and cathepsin G enhance thrombus formation by promoting the activation of coagulation and subsequent fibrin generation.50,51 Concomitantly, plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 limits fibrinolysis by inhibiting tissue plasminogen activator, thereby contributing to the resistance of platelet-rich thrombi to lysis.52, 53, 54 In contrast, VWF plays a central role in platelet adhesion and aggregation under high shear conditions, and targeting its interaction with collagen or platelet receptors has been shown to markedly reduce thrombus formation.55 Nevertheless, these pathways have been evaluated almost exclusively in preventive settings. Testing agents such as neutrophil elastase inhibitors, plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 inhibitors, or VWF-targeting compounds in our postonset model could allow one to determine whether these mechanisms also regulate the stability and recanalization of preformed thrombi.

At the same time, the model described in this study presents some limitations, which restrict the type of treatment it can be used to evaluate. First, interspecies differences between mice and humans in platelet biology and coagulation may influence the translational relevance of our findings. Second, histology and transmission electron microscopy showed an absence of exposure of subendothelial matrix proteins, which nonetheless play an important role in thrombus formation. Therefore, our approach may underestimate mechanisms primarily driven by vascular wall injury and matrix exposure. Third, the thrombin-dependent nature14 of the model could bias responses toward therapies targeting thrombin generation or activity. However, because our model was set up to evaluate the impact of pharmacological agents on preformed stable thrombi, we do not consider this to be a major limitation. A more important restriction of the study is related to the relatively short observation period after drug administration (30 minutes in our model). Although this time frame enables one to study acute effects on thrombus stability, it does not allow the investigation of long-term processes (over hours to days) involved in thrombus remodeling, such as matrix metalloproteinase-mediated extracellular matrix degradation56 or inflammatory cell recruitment.57 Finally, we did not look at the impact of embolization resulting from vessel recanalization and whether this could lead to ischemia in the downstream regions. Work of this kind may be of interest and could represent the topic of an upcoming study.

In conclusion, we describe a new experimental model of thrombosis that allows one to study the effects of postonset therapy on thrombus stability and disaggregation. Compared with the classical model of FeCl3-induced thrombosis, the composition of the thrombus in our model is much closer to that of human thrombi collected after thrombectomy. We showed that the thrombus is also sensitive to antiplatelet and anticoagulant agents with differential effects depending on the timing. Our approach represents a useful tool for testing the effects of novel agents on the stability of already formed thrombi, thereby enabling the identification of new strategies to ultimately improve clinical outcomes.
