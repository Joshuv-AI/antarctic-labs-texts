A Response to: Letter to the Editor Regarding Association Between Diabetes Management Behaviors and State-Level Social Vulnerability: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Debra Winberg, Nicolas Marchi, Tiange Tang, Lizheng Shi
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We thank Dr. Rattanapitoon and colleagues for their thoughtful commentary on our study examining associations between state-level Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) and diabetes management behavior [1]. Their critique raises important methodological considerations that advance the scholarly discourse on social determinants of health (SDoH) in diabetes care. We appreciate this opportunity to address their concerns and further contextualize our findings within the broader literature.

First, we do acknowledge the limitations highlighted by the commentators. The ecological fallacy concern regarding the state-level aggregation is well-founded. The authors of the letter correctly note that census tract-level analyses, such as those by Spielman et al. [2], can reveal important variations that are masked by broader geographies, such as the state. Therefore, the within-state heterogeneity did limit our ability to capture local-level disparities to the extent in granularity that is crucial to combat through targeted interventions. Similarly, we agree that the relatively small absolute differences in behaviors such as self-monitoring warrant cautious interpretation. Using the number needed to treat or population attributable fractions to interpret the result would benefit policy-makers or clinicians reading this study and should be incorporated into future analyses. Additionally, the methodological concern about causality is also valid and noted in our initial study. Given the data available for this study, a cross-sectional analysis was conducted, excluding biomarkers and focusing solely on behaviors. Accordingly, our study identifies associations rather than causal effects, and results should be interpreted within that framework. We would welcome research conducting natural experiments on Medicaid expansion or other policies to further assess causality.

The commentators raise especially important questions about our choice of SVI over Area Deprivation Index (ADI), particularly regarding SVI’s inclusion of race and minority status as vulnerability metrics. We chose to use SVI because of several theoretically grounded reasons. First, SVI’s inclusion of race and minority status is based on extensive research demonstrating that race serves as a proxy for structural racism and historical disadvantage [3]. As the American Diabetes Standards of Care explicitly recognizes the importance of focusing on these factors instead of biological differences, we decided to use SVI [4]. Additionally, recent work argues that excluding race from vulnerability indices may obscure the impacts of structural racism. Second, as discussed in the article, diabetes exhibits racial disparities. For example, non-Hispanic Black adults have higher rates of uncontrolled diabetes than non-Hispanic White adults, even after controlling for other SDoH [5], highlighting that structural racism is important and this would not have been captured by ADI. Third, while Rollings et al. demonstrate that index selection affects vulnerability mapping [6, 7], this variability itself supports our approach. Different indices capture different dimensions of disadvantage, and SVI’s multidimensional framework provides a more comprehensive assessment of social vulnerability than purely economic measures. We acknowledge that future comparative analyses using both SVI and ADI would strengthen the evidence base, as the commentators suggest and invite research in this space. We are doing work comparing indices in other diabetes work.

While we agree with the commentators about limitations and future directions this research can take, we wanted to highlight the important contributions of the original research on policy and clinical care. Primarily, our counterintuitive findings challenge assumptions about vulnerability–care relationships. For example, we found that moderate vulnerability states showed higher diabetes education rates and high vulnerability states demonstrated increased physician visits, although the opposite is expected. While these patterns diverge from studies like Kurani et al. [8], they reveal the complex relationship between social vulnerability and healthcare behaviors that oversimplified models may miss, and create the need for further research as the commentators discuss. Several mechanisms may explain these patterns. First, it may be explained by programmatic targeting. States with moderate vulnerability may have administrative capacity and funding to implement education programs compared to the most vulnerable who may channel limited resources towards clinical access. Second, differential uptake of Medicaid and state diabetes initiatives can improve physician visit rates in high-vulnerability settings [9]. Lastly, higher disease burden (and higher rates of uncontrolled diabetes) in vulnerable states may increase clinical encounters [10, 11]. Additionally, although state-level analyses have limitations and may obscure local variation, they do provide actionable insights for state health departments, state-level Medicaid programs, and other statewide programs, and federal-level resource allocation to the states.

We agree that our study provides evidence of associations between state-level SVI and diabetes management behaviors, not causal relationship. We also agree that further research, including comparing indices, using causal methodologies, integrating biological measures, and evaluating education programs, will be beneficial. The unexpected patterns we observed may not be limitations, but represent genuine signals about community resilience, programmatic targeting, and healthcare adaptation that warrant further investigation through the suggested research. Our work advances understanding by challenging assumptions, providing population health evidence for policy planning, and demonstrating that social vulnerability–health relationships are nuanced. These contributions, combined with the future research directions, position this work as a meaningful addition to the diabetes care literature.
