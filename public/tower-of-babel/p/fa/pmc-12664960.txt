Freeze-thaw cycles and biodegradable microplastics alter the microbial degradation of atrazine in mollisols
Authors: Yanjie Xie, Huihui Cao, Qicheng Bei, Shi Yao, Li Xu, Yongrong Bian, Xin Jiang, Yang Song, Irmina Ćwieląg-Piasecka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Global warming results in more field soil suffering freeze-thaw cycles (FTCs). The environmental risk of microplastics—recognized as a global emerging contaminant—in soils undergoing FTCs remains unclear. In this study, the combined effects of FTCs and poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) microplastics on microbial degradation of atrazine in Mollisols were investigated. Freeze-thaw cycles, rather than microplastics, significantly inhibited the biodegradation of atrazine in soil, with average inhibition ratios of 33.69% and 4.99% for FTCs and microplastics, respectively. Thawing temperature was the main factor driving the changes in soil microbial community structures and the degradation of atrazine. The degradable microplastics with an amendment level of 0.2% had different and limited effects on the dissipation of atrazine under different modes of FTCs. Among the four modes, microplastics only showed a trend toward promoting atrazine degradation under high-frequency and high-thawing-temperature FTCs. Across all modes, microplastics altered microbial interactions and ecological niches that included affecting specific bacterial abundance, module keystone species, microbial network complexity, and functional genes in soil. There's no synergistic effect between microplastics and FTCs on the degradation of atrazine in soil within a short-term period. This study provides critical insights into the ecological effects of the new biodegradable mulch film-derived microplastics in soil under FTCs.

Introduction

Soil freeze-thaw cycles (FTCs) are a common physical process, specifically referring to the repeated freezing and thawing of soil due to daily and seasonal temperature fluctuations [1]. Global climate warming would result in the expansion of ranges, increased frequency, and intensity of FTCs [2]. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report emphasized that for every 1 ​°C increase in global surface temperature, the volume of permafrost will decrease by approximately 25%, which will affect the FTCs [3]. Nowadays, about 55% of the Northern Hemisphere regions are undergoing the FTC process [4,5]. Freeze-thaw cycles can disrupt soil physicochemical properties and structure [6]; concurrently, freezing-induced ice formation lyses microbial cells, while thawing releases bioavailable nutrients from aggregates and dead bacteria [[7], [8], [9]]. Critically, FTCs can suppress organic contaminant degradation—exemplified by inhibited mineralization of petroleum hydrocarbons in seasonally frozen soils through impaired enzymatic activity and microbial metabolic capacity [10]. Therefore, the effect of FTCs on soil ecology and health is of great worldwide concern.

Plastic mulch is widely used in the freeze-thaw regions, aiming to increase soil temperature and preserve moisture during the farming period [11]. However, inefficient recycling of mulch has led to serious global plastic pollution in farmland [12]. For example, microplastics (<5 ​mm) derived from the abiotic or biotic fragmentation of the residual mulch film pose serious ecological risks to soil health [13,14], including disruption of soil structure [15], release of toxic additives (plasticizers, heavy metals) [16], inhibition of soil biota growth [17,18], and disruption of carbon and nitrogen cycles [19], etc. To solve this problem, biodegradable mulch film was proposed as a substitute for conventional mulch film [20]. It has been estimated that the global bioplastics production will reach approximately 7.43 million tons by 2028 [21]. Paradoxically, emerging evidence reveals that degradable-mulch-derived microplastics exhibit amplified ecotoxicological impacts compared to conventional microplastics during early degradation phases [22,23]. Microplastics can modulate the environmental degradation of co-occurring organic contaminants by acting as competitive sorption sites, thereby sequestering contaminants and reducing their bioavailability to degrading microbes or chemical reactants [24]. By adsorbing herbicides and impairing microbial degraders, microplastics may reduce herbicide bioavailability while impeding natural attenuation [25]. Thus, the environmental risk of degradable microplastics in soil needs to be estimated, especially under FTC conditions, which may affect the behavior of degradable plastics.

The excessive application of herbicides in agriculture has resulted in their high residue levels in farmland soils [26]. Microbial degradation serves as an efficient mechanism for removing these herbicides from soil; however, it would be affected by the factors of FTCs and microplastics in the fields suffering from FTCs and plastic mulch, as mentioned above. Moreover, FTCs may alter the surface morphology and amorphous regions of microplastics in soil, thereby modifying their interactions with both herbicides and soil microbial communities [27,28]. However, the combined effects of FTCs and degradable microplastics on herbicide biodegradation mechanisms remain poorly understood. Thus, this study bridges this gap by quantifying the interactions between biodegradable microplastics and FTCs modes in governing herbicide degradation kinetics and microbial resilience.

Therefore, the aim of this work was to investigate the combined effects of FTCs and degradable microplastics on the decay of herbicides, soil microbial community structures, and functions. To this end, atrazine, the widely used herbicide in the world, was selected as a model organic contaminant. The long-term use of atrazine has caused widespread soil pollution, including many areas experiencing FTCs such as northeast China [29]. The emerging degradable mulch, poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) [30], was selected as the origin of microplastics. The FTCs modes with different freeze-thaw frequencies or thawing temperatures were set to investigate the degradation of atrazine in soil as affected by microplastics under various scenarios. Field in-situ experiments could capture natural complexity but lack control over FTCs variables (e.g., frequency, thawing temperature); in contrast, laboratory simulations enable precise manipulation of FTCs parameters critical for mechanistic insights [31]. The high-throughput sequencing and metagenomic sequencing were performed to further reveal the shifts of soil microbial community structures and functions as affected by microplastics and FTCs. This study will not only elucidate the mechanism by which FTCs and microplastics affect the herbicide degradation, but also provide a theoretical basis for optimizing plastic mulch management and formulating herbicide application strategies in cold regions, thereby helping to promote sustainable farming practices.

Materials and methods

Soil and reagents

The Mollisol was sampled from the forest area in Hailun, Heilongjiang Province, China, to minimize any contamination of atrazine or microplastics. Hailun City is located in the transitional zone from the Xiaoxing'anling Mountains to the Songnen Plain, characterized by a continental monsoon climate. The mean annual temperature ranges from 1 to 2 ​°C, the frost-free period is approximately 130 days, and the annual precipitation ranges between 500 and 600 ​mm. The region experiences seasonal FTCs, with freezing commencing in late October and thawing occurring in June of the following year [1]. Soil samples were collected from a depth of 0–20 ​cm after the surface litter and other impurities had been removed. After removing large plant residues, litter, or roots, the soil was air-dried and passed through a 2 ​mm mesh sieve prior to incubation experiments. Detailed soil physicochemical properties were compiled in Text S1 and Table S1. PBAT mulch (Shanghai Hongrui Company) was cryogenically milled and sieved to isolate 250–600 ​μm microplastics. Atrazine (purity ≥ 99%) was purchased from Ehrenstorfer GmbH (Germany). Diffusive Gradients in Thin-films (DGT) device was purchased from Nanjing Wishen Environmental Protection Technology Company. All other reagents were of analytical grade.

Freeze-thaw cycles experimental design

Prior to the freeze-thaw cycles experiment, soil samples spiked with atrazine and microplastics were prepared (The details are shown in Text S2). Microplastics were incorporated at an environmentally relevant concentration of 0.2% (w:w) with particle sizes within the documented range for agricultural soils [[32], [33], [34]], which is also commonly employed in related experimental studies [35,36]. Atrazine was applied at 5 ​mg/kg, a concentration reflecting its significantly elevated levels in soils resulting from widespread agricultural over-application [37,38]. Four distinct FTC regimes, designed at environmentally relevant thawing temperatures (5 ​°C and 22 ​°C) and frequencies (high and low) to encompass current conditions while incorporating projected increases under global warming scenarios, were established based on observational trends and climate impact studies (Table S2) [39,40]. Briefly, Mode 1 included 1, 3, 6, 12, and 20 FTCs, each FTC consisted of freezing at −20 ​°C for 24 ​h followed by thawing at 5 ​°C for 24 ​h. Mode 2 was the same as Mode 1 except for the thawing temperature (22 ​°C). Mode 3 set freezing and thawing temperatures at −20 ​°C and 5 ​°C, respectively, and samples were taken after freezing for 2 days and thawing for 2 days, freezing for 7 days and thawing for 7 days, freezing for 15 days and thawing for 15 days, and freezing for 30 days and thawing for 30 days. Mode 4 was the same as Mode 3 except for the thawing temperature (22 ​°C). Each mode included four treatments: (1) soil incubated at a constant temperature of 5 ​°C or 22 ​°C as controls (CK); (2) soil spiked with 0.2% PBAT microplastic (w:w) and incubated at constant temperature (MP); (3) soil subjected to freeze-thaw cycles (FTC); and (4) soil spiked with 0.2% PBAT microplastic (w:w) and subjected to freeze-thaw cycles (FTC ​+ ​MP). Soil moisture content was set at 25% and ultrapure water was added every two days to maintain soil moisture content except during the freezing period. The freezing process was conducted in a refrigerator (Haier, BCD-521WSGKU1), and the thawing process was conducted in an incubator (Shanghai Senxin Experimental Instrument Co., LTD. SHP-250). Three replicates were conducted for each treatment. At the sampling time point, a portion of the sampled soil was used to determine the total and bioavailable concentrations of atrazine in soil, and a portion of the soil was frozen at −80 ​°C prior to microbial analysis.

Characterization of microplastics

To observe the changes of microplastics' properties after FTCs, the pure PBAT microplastic was subjected to different freeze-thaw regimes: one cycle (freezing at −20 ​°C for 1 day followed by thawing at 22 ​°C for 1 day), twenty repeated cycles (same conditions), and a prolonged cycle consisting of 30 days of freezing at −20 ​°C and 30 days of thawing at 22 ​°C. The surface morphology of microplastics was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS EVO18). The internal polymerization structure of microplastics was measured by a 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (NMR, JNM-ECZ600R) with solvent of CDCl3 and analyzed with the software MestReNova (Mestrelab, Spain). The crystallinity was determined by a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, NETZSCH DSC 200F3). Changes in functional groups on the surface of the microplastics were determined by fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nexus 870 FT-IR), and the carbonyl index of the microplastics was calculated. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Escalab 250XI) was used to characterize the surface chemical composition of microplastic. The detailed parameters of these measurements above are shown in Text S3.

Bioavailability of atrazine in soil

Atrazine bioavailability in soil was determined using the DGT technique [41]. The DGT device was placed in a Petri dish containing contaminated soil, and the extraction process was set at 25 ​°C for 24 ​h. Then, the atrazine on the adsorbent membrane in the device was eluted and concentrated. Detailed procedures and calculations of the DGT technique are shown in Text S4, and the extraction and measurement of the total concentration of atrazine in soil are described in Text S5.

Characterization of soil microbial community structure

Soil microbial DNA was extracted using FastDNA™ SPIN kit (MP Bio, USA), and the V4–V5 region of the microbial 16S rRNA universal gene (containing bacteria and archaea) was amplified using primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG-3′) and 907R (5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′) for high-throughput sequencing analysis of soil bacterial communities (Detailed procedures are shown in Text S6) [42].

Metagenomic sequencing and data analysis

The extracted DNA was examined by Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) for library construction. The library was sequenced by PE150 using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 high-throughput sequencing platform. Clean data after quality control was de novo spliced using MEGAHIT (k-mer selected kmin 35, k-max95, k-step20) and were put together and mixed for assembly [43]. Then, the open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted by MetaGeneMark using the scaftigs (length > 500 bp) from the single samples and the mixed assemblies. The Linclust software was used for gene clustering and redundancy to obtain the non-redundant gene catalogue [44]. The BBMap software was used to compare the clean reads to the gene catalogue, and the relative abundance information of each gene in each sample was calculated. Functional annotation of non-redundant gene sequences was performed using Diamond software (The detailed procedures are shown in Text S7).

Data analysis

The t-test was performed on different treatments using SPSS 26.0 to assess the significant differences between treatments, with the significance of p-value < 0.05. Calculation of alpha diversity index of bacterial communities in soil samples and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) were carried out using R software with the “vegan” package. To ensure network credibility and reliability, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with sample relative abundance greater than 0.01% were only included in the analysis, and network connections were constructed using the “Hmisc” and “igraph” packages in R. Network plots with correlation coefficients > 0.7 and p-values < 0.001 were constructed and visualized using Gephi software (v 0.9.2). In addition, according to previous studies, the within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi) values were calculated using the “igraph”, and the nodes with high connectivity were classified, i.e., module hubs (Zi ​≥ ​2.5, Pi ​< ​0.62), connectors (Zi ​< ​2.5, Pi ​≥ ​0.62), and network hubs (Zi ​≥ ​2.5, Pi ​≥ ​0.62) [45]. Z-score normalized heatmaps of gene abundance compositions were produced using the “pheatmap” package in R. A partial least squares path model (PLS-PM) was established using the “plspm” package in R.

Results and discussion

Effects of FTCs and microplastics on the degradation of atrazine

The average degradation ratios of atrazine in treatments incubated under constant temperature were 47.3% higher than those in treatments involving FTCs (p ​< ​0.05) (Fig. S1). The degradation ratios of atrazine were relatively low under low-thawing-temperature or high-frequency FTCs compared to those under other modes (Fig. 1a). These results suggest that the risk of considerable herbicide residues is high in fields that undergo FTCs, which depend on the thawing temperature and frequency. Freeze-thaw cycles may inhibit the degradation of atrazine through multiple mechanisms, including altering the microbial habitat, disrupting cellular structure, and reducing both bacterial metabolic and soil enzymatic activities. As the frequency of FTCs increases, soil porosity and the maximum effective pore size both show an upward trend, with more severe damage to soil physical structure [46]. High-frequency FTCs cause soil water expansion, disrupting cellular osmotic pressure balance, thereby inhibiting physiological functions and leading to reduced bacterial activity or even death [47]. Additionally, it was found that the inhibitory effect on carbon- and nitrogen-hydrolyzing enzymes in soil becomes more pronounced with increased FTC frequency [48]. In fact, the optimal temperature range for the growth of atrazine-degrading bacteria is 25–35 ​°C [49,50]; thus, low thawing temperatures are unfavorable for the growth and metabolic activities of these bacteria.Fig. 1Degradation ratio (a) and bioavailability (b) of atrazine in soil under different modes of freeze-thaw cycles. FTC, freeze-thaw cycle treatment; FTC ​+ ​MP, freeze-thaw cycle ​+ ​microplastics treatment).Fig. 1

The degradation ratios of atrazine were 21.9%, 31.8%, 24.1%, 41.6% in FTC treatments and 17.5%, 32.8%, 21.6%, 39.9% in FTC ​+ ​MP treatments under the four different modes, respectively (Fig. 1a). Compared to FTC treatments, the atrazine degradation ratios were slightly reduced in FTC ​+ ​MP treatments in Modes 1, 3, and 4 (p ​> ​0.05). Studies reported that microplastics can act as vectors for sorbing contaminants in soil, increasing their persistence and inhibiting degradation [16,32,51]. Meanwhile, the release of plasticizers such as phthalates from microplastics would be accelerated during FTCs [16], which may disrupt the integrity of microbial cell membranes and community diversity. Yuan et al. [52] found that microplastics reduced the activities of atrazine degradation-related metabolic enzymes and the diversity of microbial communities in wetlands. In this study, microplastics can alter microbial community composition and functional genes, as detailed in subsequent sections.

Effects of different modes of FTCs and microplastics on the bioavailability of atrazine

In Modes 1−4, the bioavailabilities of atrazine in soils were 28.6%, 23.2%, 7.9% and 11.3% in FTC treatments and 21.3%, 26.9%, 9.6% and 12.3% in FTC ​+ ​MP treatments, respectively (Fig. 1b). Significantly higher atrazine bioavailability was observed in Modes 1 and 2 compared to Modes 3 and 4 (p ​< ​0.05) (Fig. 1b), suggesting that high-frequency FTCs could significantly enhance the bioavailability of atrazine. The frost-heaving stress generated by ice crystals during continuous freezing and thawing can create large voids in soil, destroy soil macroaggregates, and lead to a decrease in the sorption performance of soil colloids as well as desorption of soil-immobilized contaminants, which collectively enhance the bioavailability of contaminants [53,54]. For example, the total porosity of mollisols after undergoing 1 and 20 FTCs increased by approximately 32.1% and 247.8%, respectively; repeated FTCs significantly enlarged the maximum effective pore diameter in mollisols [47]. Thus, high-frequency FTCs were more damaging to the soil, leading to a significant increase in the bioavailability of atrazine.

The amendment of PBAT microplastics significantly reduced the bioavailability of atrazine in Mode 1 (p ​< ​0.05). Microplastics in soil can adsorb contaminants and reduce their bioavailability by virtue of their hydrophobic surfaces and porous structure [55,56]. Conversely, Mode 2 exhibited negligible microplastic effects, attributed to FTC-induced polymer aging under high thawing temperature [33,57]. Our results showed that high-thawing-temperature and high-frequency FTCs (Mode 2) increased the surface cracks of microplastics, disrupted the amorphous zone, and formed carboxylate groups through ester group breakage, which increased the crystallinity and polarity of PBAT microplastics (Fig. S2 and Table S3). Since hydrophobic partitioning was the main mechanism of atrazine sorption on PBAT microplastics [32,58], the increased polarity of PBAT microplastics would reduce their sorption capacity for atrazine. This aligns with reported FTC-driven increases in microplastic polarity, which reduce atrazine adsorption [33]. There was no significant difference in the bioavailability of atrazine between FTC and FTC ​+ ​MP treatments in Mode 3 or Mode 4 (Fig. 1b). Thus, high-frequency FTCs (Modes 1 and 2) can significantly increase the bioavailability of atrazine in soil (p ​< ​0.05), while microplastics under low-thawing temperature conditions reduce the bioavailability of atrazine.

The diversity of soil bacterial community

The Chao1 and Shannon indices of α-diversity can reflect the richness and diversity of soil bacterial communities, respectively. Overall, the Chao1 and Shannon indices under low-thawing-temperature FTCs of Modes 1 and 3 were slightly higher than those under high-thawing-temperature FTCs of Modes 2 and 4 (p ​> ​0.05) (Fig. S3). This may be because high-thawing-temperature incubation benefited the growth of soil-dominant bacteria, as evidenced by the total relative abundance of dominant bacteria (Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes) in Modes 2 and 4, which was more than 90%. The effect of FTCs on soil microbial community diversity is closely related to the frequency and intensity of FTCs. Microbial diversity depletion induced by elevated thawing temperatures may not be detrimental to soil atrazine degradation, while proliferation of atrazine-degrading populations appears to exert a more determinative influence on degradation efficacy. In this study, PBAT microplastics slightly reduced the bacterial α-diversity under FTCs condition (Figs. S3 and S5). This inhibition may result from additive leaching (e.g., plasticizers, stabilizers) in plastics, screening out sensitive bacterial members [16,59]. In addition, the Venn analysis revealed fewer core OTUs between the FTC and FTC ​+ ​MP treatments in the high-thawing-temperature FTCs (Fig. S6), suggesting that PBAT microplastics played a greater role in shifting the soil bacterial community under high-thawing-temperature FTCs than those under low-thawing-temperature FTCs.

The PCoA analysis based on Bray–Curtis distance of bacterial communities showed that the first two principal components explained 25.7% and 15.9% of the community variance, respectively (Fig. 2a). Significant difference in bacterial community composition between FTC modes was verified by Adonis (R2 ​= ​0.62, p ​< ​0.05), where Modes 2 and 4 clustered together while Modes 1 and 3 clustered together. This result indicates that thawing temperature, rather than FTCs frequency, is the main factor shifting soil bacterial community structures. In the same mode, there was no significant difference of the Bray–Curtis distances in FTC ​+ ​MP treatment compared with FTC treatment (Table S4), indicating that microplastics limitedly affect the whole soil bacterial community structure under FTCs in this study, in accordance with the study from Zhou et al. Community composition of soil bacteria

In Modes 1−4, the abundant soil bacterial phyla with relative abundance higher than 1% were Proteobacteria (15.0%–64.6%), Actinobacteria (14.2%–63.3%), Firmicutes (7.8%–40.5%), Acidobacteria (1.6%–24.2%), and Planctomycetes (1.2%–6.6%) (Fig. S4). The PBAT microplastics in FTCs of Modes 1 and 2 led to increases in the relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Thaumarchaeota (p ​< ​0.05), while decreases were observed for Acidobacteria, Firmicutes, Planctomycetes, and Chloroflexi (p ​< ​0.05). In Modes 3 and 4, PBAT microplastics led to increases in the relative abundance of Gemmatimonadetes and Planctomycetes (p ​> ​0.05), while decreases for Proteobacteria and Verrucomicrobia (p ​< ​0.05). PBAT microplastic also increased the relative abundance of Actinobacteria in Modes 2, 3 and 4 (p ​< ​0.05) (Fig. 2b). Among them, Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, the most active microorganisms in the permafrost [61], were reported as the main degrading bacteria of atrazine and degradable plastics [38,62]. In Mode 2, the abundances of both Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria increased, potentially playing a key role in the degradation of atrazine in soil. Microplastics increased the abundance of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria in environments with intense freeze-thaw cycles, which could be ascribed to their high adaptability to freeze-thaw environments and high resource utilization ability [63,64]. In contrast to most studies on the behavior of microplastics conducted in constant temperature environments, this study found that microplastics under different FTC modes caused changes in the abundance of various bacteria.

To compare the differences in the abundances of the abundant and rare members in response to microplastics, the taxa were categorized into three groups, i.e., high-abundance members (relative abundance >1%), medium-abundance members (1% ​> ​relative abundance >0.1%), and low-abundance members (relative abundance <0.1%) (Figs. 2c and S7). The PBAT microplastics did not significantly affect the abundance of high-abundance genera under FTCs condition. Interestingly, microplastics generally led to a decrease in the relative abundance of most low- and medium-abundance genera both in Modes 1 and 2. In contrast, in Modes 3 and 4, microplastics increased the relative abundance of low- and medium-abundance genera to some extent throughout the experimental period. In bacterial communities, relatively rare taxa exhibit higher diversity than abundant taxa, and the relative abundance of rare taxa increases with repeated disturbances, playing an important role in the stability of bacterial communities under extreme conditions [65,66]. This result indicates that the rare bacterial members could be strongly influenced by PBAT microplastic under FTC conditions and that this effect varies with FTC's mode of frequency rather than thawing temperature. Compared to low-frequency FTCs, microplastic amendment under high-frequency cycling drastically reduces the diversity of low-abundance bacterial taxa. This likely stems from lethal stress imposed by recurrent freezing-thawing on sensitive, rare biota, whereas microplastics act as competitive substrates that selectively enrich dominant populations.

Co-occurrence network and keystone species analysis

There were differences in the topological characteristics of soil bacterial community networks among different modes and treatments (Fig. 3a, Table S6). Compared to CK treatment, PBAT microplastics increased the complexity of the bacterial network (Fig. S8, Table S5). At constant temperature, degradable microplastics could increase DOC content and provide carbon sources for bacterial mutualistic symbiosis [67,68]. However, the number of connections, average degree, and network density of the bacterial networks significantly decreased in the FTC ​+ ​MP treatment compared to the FTC treatment in Mode 1 (p ​< ​0.05) (Fig. 3a, Table S6), indicating that microplastics weaken the complexity of soil bacterial community networks under this FTC mode. The number of negatively correlated connections increased in the FTC ​+ ​MP treatment of Mode 1, indicating the increased competition between microorganisms due to limited resources and ecological niches. On the contrary, microplastics enhanced soil network complexity in Modes 2, 3, and 4. The higher the network complexity, the more resilient and self-regulating the bacterial community is, helping the microbes to resist disturbances from FTCs [69]. Under high thawing temperature conditions, the activity of bacteria was relatively high, and PBAT microplastics may provide exogenous carbon to increase bacterial interactions. Modules in bacterial co-occurrence networks represent different ecological niches, and species within modules perform similar ecological functions [70]. The result showed that microplastics increase the modularity of bacterial networks under high-frequency FTC conditions, indicating that microplastics increase the redundancy of bacterial community functions and enhance the community's adaptability to environmental changes. This result is consistent with the study from Li et al. [28]. Nodes with high connectivity are often defined as key taxa, i.e., module hub, connector, and network hub [45]. In this study, only module hubs appeared in the bacterial network, and their number and types varied across modes and treatments (Table S7). The keystone species used in this study are clearly defined as nodes with significantly high connectivity (Zi ​> ​2.5) within the network module, and whose abundance changes can significantly alter the strength of functional associations between modules [71]. As can be seen from Fig. S9, only in Mode 1, microplastics reduce the number of bacterial community module hubs, suggesting that microplastics may reduce bacterial community network complexity by reducing the number of module hubs under high-frequency and low-thawing-temperature FTCs. Meanwhile, in Modes 2, 3, and 4, microplastics led to the emergence of new nodes in common. For example, Acidobacteria acted as one of the module hubs after the amendment of microplastics in these three modes (Table S7). Although Acidobacteria cannot directly degrade atrazine, PBAT microplastics may promote interactions between Acidobacteria and other bacterial genera, alter their ecological niche, and facilitate the biodegradation of atrazine. It has been reported that Acidobacteria, with strong metabolic capacity, could dominate the bacterial community in the Arctic permafrost soil [72,73]. In general, microplastics can significantly change the taxa of module hub bacteria in different FTCs, potentially affecting the community and function of the whole microorganism.

The effects of FTCs and microplastics on soil metagenomics

Using metagenomics combined with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotation, a total of 10,840 KOs (KEGG ORTHOLOGY) genes were obtained. The PCoA analysis showed significant differences in gene composition across FTCs modes (R2 ​= ​0.54, p ​< ​0.05) (Fig. S10, Table S8). Most of the differential KO genes induced by microplastics were related to protein families, including genetic information processing, metabolism, signaling, and cellular processes in KEGG secondary classification (Fig. S11). As emphasized by Typas and Sourjik [74], protein interaction networks underlie all bacterial cellular processes, enabling complex cellular functions. Microplastic-induced alterations in soil bacterial protein family genes may affect cellular structure and metabolic pathways, thereby modifying their functional outputs. Further comparison of gene abundances revealed that PBAT microplastics altered genes governing carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, membrane transport, signal transduction, translation, and lipid metabolism in FTC-treated soils (Fig. S12). Microplastics have been shown to impair cellular proliferation by compromising membrane integrity, disrupting signaling pathways, and obstructing energy transduction [75,76].

The series of changes in the soil functional genes mentioned above may influence the degradation of atrazine. Biodegradation is an important pathway for atrazine removal from the environment, and the microbial hydrolysis process of atrazine mainly consists of two stages (Fig. 4a) [38]. In this study, we matched the genes related to the entire metabolic pathway of atrazine through KEGG and eggNOG databases. As supported by metagenomic data in Fig. 4a, microplastics reduced the abundance of the degradation genes belonging to COG0402 in four modes (p ​< ​0.05), which are mainly responsible for the upstream metabolic processes of atrazine, including dechlorination, dealkylation, and cyanuric acid production, determining the parent compound amount of atrazine in soil [38]. Carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, and lipid metabolism constitute the core functional backbone of microbial survival strategies. The abundance of most metabolic genes in the freeze-thaw treated soil of Mode 2 was higher than that in other modes, including the genes related to citrate cycle, carbon fixation, pyruvate metabolism, sulfur metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, and fatty acid biosynthesis (p ​< ​0.05) (Fig. 4b). Notably, most of the important metabolic gene abundances in Mode 2 increased with microplastics amendment, but decreased in the other three modes (Fig. S13). FTCs reduced soil aggregate stability, and cell lysis released small organic molecules (e.g., amino acids, carbohydrates), which served as critical environmental factors governing soil carbon/nitrogen metabolism and associated genetic abundance [61,77,78]. The intensity parameters (temperature, frequency) of FTCs modulate soil nutrient liberation and perturbation magnitude of bacterial community [47,79]. More importantly, metagenomic analysis revealed that Mode 2 exhibited the highest abundance of PBAT metabolic genes, encompassing both upstream decomposition to 3-oxoadipyl-CoA and downstream citrate cycle pathways (Fig. S14 and S15). Therefore, high-intensity FTCs (Mode 2) select stress-tolerant bacteria with enhanced metabolic capacity while increasing nutrient release from soil and microplastics. This conclusion is consistent with the microbial network results in Fig. 3. These released nutrients further stimulated the activity of these adapted bacteria, thereby promoting atrazine degradation. As shown in Fig. 1a, it was observed that among the four FTC modes, only Mode 2 with microplastic addition showed a tendency to promote atrazine degradation.

Relationship among FTCs, microplastics, and atrazine degradation

Seventeen bacterial genera capable of degrading atrazine were identified in this study, namely Acinetobacter [80], Bacillus [81], Brevibacterium [38], Caulobacter [38], Exiguobacterium [82], Microbacterium [83], Ochrobactrum [84], Pseudomonas [85], Ralstonia [86], Rhodococcus [49], Sphingomonas [87], Stenotrophomonas [88], Streptomyces [37], Polaromonas [89], Paenibacillus [90], Paenarthrobacter [91], Staphylococcus [38]. The correlation analysis demonstrated that atrazine degradation was significantly and positively correlated with Rhodococcus, Streptomyces, and Paenarthrobacter under constant temperature and different FTC modes (Fig. S16a). Interestingly, all three genera belong to Actinobacteria, suggesting the significant role of Actinobacteria in atrazine degradation in Mollisols. The abundances of these degrading bacteria were higher under high-thawing-temperature FTCs than those under low-thawing-temperature FTCs (p ​< ​0.05), mechanistically accounting for differential atrazine degradation efficiency across thawing temperatures (Fig. S16b). The optimal growth temperature range for many atrazine-degrading bacteria is 25–35 ​°C [49,50]. Additionally, increased temperature may indirectly benefit the degradation bacteria by inhibiting competitors, allowing them to proliferate relatively faster under reduced competitive pressure [92]. The thawing temperature of 22 ​°C used in this study is closer to the optimal growth temperature for atrazine-degrading bacteria, explaining their enhanced growth and the higher degradation efficiency of atrazine.

Considering the complexity of atrazine degradation in soil, the link between microbes, microplastics, FTCs, and atrazine degradation was further elucidated using PLS-PM analysis (Fig. 5a). The R2 values for the path coefficients of the PLS-PM model range from 0.79 to 0.85 (p ​< ​0.01), and the Goodness of Fit (GoF) index is 0.86. The thawing temperature showed positive effects, while the FTCs frequency and microplastics showed negative effects on atrazine degradation (Fig. 5a). In addition, the standardized total effect result indicates that thawing temperature contributed the greatest to atrazine degradation (Fig. 5b). As mentioned above, high thawing temperature had a direct positive effect on the abundance of degrading bacteria, atrazine bioavailability and degradation. At high thawing temperatures, the surviving dominant bacteria had strong resistance to FTCs and high metabolic activity [57], and the bioavailability of the contaminants increased, making them easy to degrade by microorganisms [93]. These combined effects resulted in high atrazine degradation efficiency under high-thawing-temperature mode. High-frequency FTCs had a positive effect on atrazine bioavailability but a negative effect on degrading bacteria and atrazine degradation. Although atrazine with high bioavailability was favorable for degradation, high-frequency FTCs could severely damage microbial metabolism and enzyme activities [48], directly inhibiting atrazine degradation. Except for Mode 2, microplastics exhibited slight negative effects on the atrazine bioavailability, degrading bacteria, and atrazine degradation. Many studies have shown that microplastics have a certain sorption capacity for organic contaminants and can reduce their bioavailability, as well as interfere with microbial community structure, and ultimately inhibit contaminant degradation [94]. However, it is difficult to generalize the effect of microplastics on the degradation of contaminants. PBAT microplastics showed a tendency to promote atrazine degradation under high thawing temperature and high-frequency FTCs in this study. Environmental implications

Four FTC modes with different thawing temperatures and frequencies were set to deeply elucidate the effects of FTCs and microplastics on contaminants' degradation and soil bacterial community structures and functions. Collectively, thawing temperature constitutes the primary driver modulating soil bacterial community structure and atrazine degradation, with enhanced removal efficiency observed as temperatures approach the optimal growth range of atrazine-degrading bacteria. Secondly, the frequency of FTCs is another factor affecting atrazine degradation, with a higher frequency exhibiting a certain inhibitory effect on atrazine degradation. The low-thawing-temperature and high-frequency FTCs (Mode 1) generally increased the environmental risks of atrazine in soil compared to other scenarios, i.e., increased bioavailability and decreased degradation. Therefore, the use of herbicides in low-temperature areas and seasons suffering high-frequency FTCs requires caution.

In this laboratory, the PBAT microplastics with a 0.2% level did not significantly affect the degradation of atrazine in the short-term FTC conditions. Microplastics and FTCs have no obvious antagonistic or synergistic effect on the degradation of atrazine. This indicates that in the areas that undergo FTCs, targeting residual film reduction during agricultural practices constitutes a critical measure to mitigate mulch-derived MPs risks, given their limited influence on herbicide dynamics during FTC phases. However, PBAT microplastics indeed alter bacterial ecological networks, characterized by increased community complexity and niche specialization dominated by Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Acidobacteria. In addition, FTC-exposed microplastics may perturb rare taxa governing soil metabolic processes, suppressing critical gene expression and compromising ecosystem functionality. Long-term research priorities should systematically investigate how degradable plastic films influence soil microbial ecology/function and contaminant behavior under persistent FTC conditions, so as to provide a global scientific basis for the rational use of degradable plastic films in seasonal frozen fields.

As a key focus of this study, the effect of microplastics on soil atrazine degradation is modulated by freeze-thaw patterns. Microplastics tend to promote atrazine degradation under high-frequency and high-thawing-temperature FTCs. Global warming is accelerating the degradation process of permafrost, and the associated freeze-thaw processes will further intensify, manifested by increased frequency and higher thawing temperatures [95,96]. High-frequency and high-thawing-temperature FTCs will further enhance the bioavailability of organic contaminants such as atrazine. Higher thawing temperatures will also promote the soil microbial metabolic activities. However, global warming will also stimulate the growth of both plants and weeds. The future field application of herbicides will also be heightened to control weeds, resulting in high residues of them in the soil. Thus, considering that the long-term effect of microplastics on the degradation of herbicides remains unclear, the effect of global climate change on the environmental behavior of herbicides in soil is complicated and needs further in-depth research. Considering the complexity of microplastics, more studies with different types, doses, and diversities of microplastics should be conducted in the future. To address this challenge, reducing herbicide use and encouraging plastic mulch recycling are still the optimal measures in current agricultural management.

Conclusions

This study investigated the combined effects of FTCs and PBAT microplastics on the microbial degradation of the herbicide in Mollisols. Through controlled laboratory incubations simulating different FTC scenarios (varying frequency and thawing temperature) and employing chemical measurements, high-throughput sequencing, and metagenomic analysis, we demonstrated that FTCs significantly inhibited atrazine degradation, primarily governed by thawing temperature, while PBAT microplastics at 0.2% played a secondary role. Across all FTC treatments, microplastics modified microbial interactions and ecological niches, manifested through alterations in specific bacterial abundance, keystone species, network complexity, and functional genes. This study provides critical insights for optimizing biodegradable mulch use and herbicide application timing in seasonally frozen farmlands.
