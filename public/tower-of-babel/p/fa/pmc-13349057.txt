Comparative Evaluation of Biodentine and Bioceramic as Pulp Medicaments Following Laser and Conventional Pulpotomy in Primary Molars: A Double-blind Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Dhivyashanthi Sankar, Selvabalaji Arumugam, Vasanthakumari Ananthan, Ramesh Venkatesan, Ramanandvignesh Pandiyan, M. Nisha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim:The purpose of this randomized controlled trial was to evaluate and compare the clinical and radiographic success of Biodentine and Bioceramic putty as pulp medicaments following diode laser-assisted and conventional pulpotomy in primary molars.Materials and Methods:Forty-eight primary molars in children aged 4–9 years were randomly assigned into four groups (n = 12 each): Conventional pulpotomy with Biodentine, conventional pulpotomy with Bioceramic, laser-assisted pulpotomy with Biodentine, and laser-assisted pulpotomy with Bioceramic. Clinical and radiographic outcomes were evaluated at 1, 3, and 6 months using Zurn and Seale criteria (2008). Data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA, with significance set at P < 0.05.Results:All groups showed favorable clinical and radiographic success at 6 months. The laser–Bioceramic group demonstrated the highest overall success (83.2%), followed by laser–Biodentine (75%) and conventional Bioceramic (75%), whereas conventional Biodentine showed comparatively lower success (50%). Differences among groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05).Conclusions:Both Biodentine and Bioceramic were effective pulpotomy medicaments in primary molars, with Bioceramic showing slightly better outcomes. Laser-assisted pulpotomy provided additional clinical benefits, particularly in enhancing hemostasis and healing, although the differences were not statistically significant.

Introduction

The preservation of primary teeth until natural exfoliation is vital for maintaining function, esthetics, and arch integrity in children. Dental caries, the most common chronic childhood disease, often leads to pulpal involvement if untreated. Pulpotomy remains the preferred treatment for vital primary molars with healthy radicular pulp, and its success depends on accurate diagnosis, technique, and the medicament used.[1] Although Formocresol (FC) has been the gold standard for decades, concerns over its toxicity, systemic absorption, and potential carcinogenicity have prompted the search for safer alternatives.[2] Consequently, newer materials and techniques such as Glutaraldehyde, Ferric Sulfate, Electrosurgery, Laser therapy, mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), Biodentine, and Bioceramics have been developed to preserve pulp vitality.[345]

Biodentine (Septodont, France) is a tricalcium silicate-based material introduced in 2009. It consists of a powder–liquid system, with tricalcium silicate as the main component and a liquid containing calcium chloride (setting accelerator) and polycarboxylate (water-reducing agent).[6] Biodentine exhibits excellent biocompatibility and antibacterial activity and promotes pulpal healing. Compared to MTA, it offers a shorter setting time, stronger adhesion to dentin, higher compressive strength, lower porosity, and reduced microleakage.[7]

Advances in medical materials science have led to significant innovations in bioceramic materials, known for its excellent biocompatibility and hydroxyapatite-like properties.[8] In 2007, Canadian researchers developed a pre-mixed calcium silicate-based bioceramic material, eliminating manual mixing, available as BC RRM Putty (fast-setting), BC RRM Paste (syringe-delivered), and BC Sealer (bioceramic sealer). Composed of tricalcium silicate, dicalcium silicate, zirconium oxide, tantalum pentoxide, calcium phosphate monobasic, and various fillers, this aluminum-free, radiopaque material promotes hydroxyapatite formation, supports dentin regeneration, and exhibits minimal cytotoxicity when exposed to body fluids.[910]

Since the 1960s, lasers have been widely adopted in dentistry, with studies showing their effectiveness in primary tooth pulpotomy comparable to or superior to FC. Ebihara (1985) first demonstrated that Nd: YAG laser treatment enhanced pulp healing and dentinal bridge formation.[11]

Diode lasers offer several clinical advantages, including minimal or no intraoperative bleeding, faster healing, reduced postoperative infection, and little or no requirement for local anesthesia. The diode laser is a compact device that utilizes microscopic gallium arsenide or similar semiconductor chips to generate coherent light.[12] Laser emission is produced by energy transitions between the valence and conduction bands of electrons within these semiconductors, resulting in higher overall efficiency and greater clinical practicality. The diode laser emits infrared radiation, where the conversion of laser energy into heat produces precise and localized soft-tissue ablation. This process is commonly accompanied by limited peripheral thermal effects and tissue coagulation at the site of contact. Owing to the strong absorption of its wavelength (approximately 980 nm) by tissues with high water content, such as dental pulp, diode lasers are particularly suitable for pulpotomy procedures. When operated in contact mode, the laser affects only the soft tissues directly touching the emitting tip at a micrometer level, while adjacent tissues remain largely unaffected.[13]

Lasers are used in oral surgery, cavity preparation, and disinfection, with diode lasers (810–980 nm) particularly suited for soft-tissue procedures due to their selective absorption and minimal effect on hard tissues.[14] In pulpotomy, lasers provide aseptic, minimally traumatic tissue removal, offering advantages such as hemostasis, sterilization, stimulation of pulp cells, and greater comfort for children and parents due to the absence of vibration, contact, and odor.[15]

Despite encouraging outcomes with bioactive materials and laser techniques, limited evidence compares Biodentine and Bioceramic as pulpotomy medicaments using both conventional and laser-assisted methods. Hence, this double-blind randomized controlled trial aimed to evaluate and compare their clinical and radiographic success in pulpotomy of primary molars.

Materials and Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled parallel-group trial conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board, and the study was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/06/069218). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians of all participants.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4, Dusseldorf, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) with an effect size of 0.45, α = 0.05, power = 95%, and allocation ratio of 1:1:1:1, resulting in 48 teeth.

Randomization

Forty-eight teeth meeting the inclusion criteria were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 12 each) using simple randomization (computer-generated sequence). Allocation concealment was achieved through opaque sealed envelopes.

Sample grouping

All children were randomly assigned into four groups with 12 members each: Group A: Conventional pulpotomy + Biodentine, Group B: Conventional pulpotomy + Bioceramic putty, Group C: Laser pulpotomy + Biodentine, and Group D: Laser pulpotomy + Bioceramic putty.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, the operator was not blinded. Participants and outcome assessor were blinded to group allocation. The trial followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines [Figure 1].

Clinical procedure

Local anesthesia (2% lidocaine with epinephrine 1:100,000) was administered, and the tooth was isolated with a rubber dam. Caries was removed using a sterile spoon excavator, and an access cavity was prepared with a No. 330 bur to expose the coronal pulp. Pulpal bleeding was assessed; teeth showing bright-red bleeding (vital pulp) were included, while those with sluggish or cyanotic bleeding were excluded. The coronal pulp was excised, the chamber deroofed, and the site irrigated with sterile saline.

In the conventional group, a saline-soaked cotton pellet was placed for 1 min to control bleeding, followed by placement of Biodentine (Septodont) in Group I and Bioceramic (Bio-C Repair) in Group II, and the cavity was restored with GIC and SSC placed on the next day. In laser group, the coronal pulp ablated with a diode laser (980 nm) (Indilase) was used at 0.8 W, pulsed mode, fiber tip diameter 300 μm, applied for 2 min per site in contact mode, followed by Biodentine (Septodont) in Group III and Bioceramic (Bio-C Repair) in Group IV and the cavity was restored with GIC and SSC placed on the next day.

The children were recalled for clinical and radiographic examination postoperatively after 1, 3, and 6 months. The clinical and radiographic examinations were evaluated according to Zurn and Seale criteria (2008) [Tables 1 and 2], and the data were analyzed using the SPSS version 21 software (IBM, Armonk, New York, United States). Clinical and radiographic variables were assessed with a one-way ANOVA test, and P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 48 teeth from 45 children aged between 4 and 9 years were included in the study, with equal allocation of 12 teeth in each of the four groups: Conventional Biodentine, conventional Bioceramic, laser Biodentine, and laser Bioceramic. No patients were withdrawn from the study. The majority of participants were 5–6 years old (62.6%), followed by 7 years (16.7%), 8 years (10.4%), 9 years (6.3%), and 4 years (4%). Females constituted 56.3% of the sample, whereas males represented 43.7%. The mandibular second molars were the most frequently treated teeth, with LLE (29.2%) and RLE (18.8%) showing the highest prevalence. At baseline, all groups had identical clinical and radiographic scores (mean = 1.00, P = 1.00), indicating no pre-treatment bias.

Clinical evaluation

All groups showed favorable clinical outcomes at 1 month (mean = 1.00–1.25, P = 0.646). At 3 months, Biodentine groups – particularly the laser-assisted subgroup (mean = 0.92) – showed slight improvement, whereas Bioceramic groups remained stable (P = 0.591). At 6 months, the Laser Biodentine group recorded the lowest clinical score (0.67), followed by Conventional Biodentine (0.75), with both Bioceramic groups steady at 1.00 (P = 0.319) [Table 3].

Clinical failures were noted in two Conventional Biodentine, one Laser Biodentine, and one Laser Bioceramic case due to postoperative pain or tenderness. However, differences between conventional and laser subgroups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Radiographic evaluation

All groups showed progressive healing over time. The Laser Biodentine group demonstrated the best radiographic improvement (mean = 0.67), indicating optimal dentin bridge formation and periapical healing, while the Bioceramic groups remained stable at 1.00 [Graph 1]. Radiographic failures occurred in two Conventional Biodentine, one Conventional Bioceramic, and one Laser Bioceramic case, attributed to persistent periapical radiolucency. Intergroup differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Discussion

Dental caries is a common chronic condition in children, affecting primary teeth more rapidly due to thinner enamel and dentin, larger dentinal tubules, and a bigger pulp chamber. Pulpotomy is indicated when inflammation is limited to the coronal pulp.[516] This procedure involves removing the coronal pulp while preserving the radicular pulp, ensuring complete caries removal to enhance the restorative seal and prevent microleakage. The aim is to maintain pulp vitality, promote reparative dentin formation, and preserve the tooth as a functional unit.[1718]

Proper isolation plays a fundamental role in ensuring the long-term success of pulpotomy procedures. The rubber dam prevents contamination, improves access, and stabilizes the operative field.[1920] Eidelman (1983)[21] reported superior retention of restorative materials with rubber dam use, whereas Wang (2016)[22] demonstrated significantly lower risks of pulpal contamination when isolation was maintained throughout pulp therapy. Such findings justify its use as a standard protocol in pediatric pulp therapy.

Following pulpotomy, achieving a hermetic seal is crucial to prevent bacterial leakage, which can jeopardize pulpal healing. Bacterial ingress after restoration is more detrimental than contamination during the procedure.[23] Soxman[24] and Holan et al.[25] both highlighted the superior longevity and clinical performance of SSCs as the restoration of choice in primary molars.

Periodic follow-up assessments at 1, 3, and 6 months allowed for consistent monitoring of both clinical and radiographic parameters. These intervals were chosen in accordance with studies by El Meligy et al.[26] and Godhi et al.,[5] who observed that 3–6 months is adequate for initial evaluation of pulpotomy success.

The current study reported a 50% success rate for Biodentine at 6 months, which aligns with findings by Carti and Oznurhan[27] and Habashy et al. (2020),[28] who reported success rates of 60% and 73.3%, respectively. Biodentine’s biocompatibility, high pH, and ability to release calcium hydroxide contribute to its reparative potential. There were three failures in the Biodentine group after 6-month follow-up. This might have occurred due to poor case selection, improper manipulation, and improper coverage of the permanent restorative material.

Bioceramic materials achieved higher clinical and radiographic success (75%) in this study. These results are consistent with those of Joo et al.,[29] who reported an 82% success rate with Well-Root PT, and Abdelwahab et al.,[30] who recorded 68%–84% success with Bioceramic root repair materials. The pre-mixed form of Bioceramics eliminates manual mixing errors and ensures consistent handling, which enhances reliability. Their bioactivity, dimensional stability, and resistance to microleakage make them an attractive alternative to traditional calcium silicate materials.[31]

The incorporation of lasers in pulpotomy has further advanced the biological and clinical outcomes of vital pulp therapy. Lasers provide several benefits, including hemostasis, sterilization of the pulp chamber, and biostimulation of odontoblastic cells, all of which contribute to pulp healing.[3233] These results align with Saltzman et al.,[34] who reported a 70.8% success rate with diode laser–MTA pulpotomies in primary teeth. Similarly, Ansari et al.[35] and Pei et al.[36] reported excellent outcomes with diode laser pulpotomy, showing 100% and 92% clinical success and radiographic success rates 90% and 78.6% at 12-month follow-up.

Overall, this study demonstrated that pulpotomy remains an effective and conservative treatment for managing deep carious lesions in primary molars, with an overall success rate of 70.8%. No statistically significant difference was found between Biodentine, Bioceramic, and their laser-assisted counterparts (P > 0.05). However, the trends observed suggest that combining bioceramic-based medicaments with laser application enhances healing and reduces bacterial contamination, potentially improving long-term prognosis. Continued research with longer follow-up periods and larger sample sizes is warranted to confirm these preliminary findings and establish standardized protocols for laser-assisted pulpotomy in pediatric dentistry.

Limitations of the study

The main limitations of this study were the relatively small sample size and the short follow-up period (6 months), which may not be adequate to detect late complications such as internal resorption or periapical changes. Future research with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods (12–24 months) is necessary to confirm these outcomes and assess the long-term reliability of bioceramic medicaments and laser-assisted pulpotomy techniques.

Conclusions

Overall, both Biodentine and Bioceramic demonstrated favorable short-term success as pulpotomy medicaments in primary molars, with Bioceramic showing slightly better outcomes. The adjunctive use of diode laser provided additional clinical benefits, though not statistically significant. These findings support the growing evidence that calcium silicate-based biomaterials and diode lasers represent viable and biologically compatible alternatives for pulpotomy in pediatric dentistry.
