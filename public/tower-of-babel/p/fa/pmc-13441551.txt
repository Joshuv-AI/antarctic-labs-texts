Allergic Rhinitis and Its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) Guidelines: Moving Towards Living Guidelines With Regionally Tailored Recommendations
Authors: Bernardo Sousa-Pinto, Rafael José Vieira, Ludger Klimek, Torsten Zuberbier, Yoon-Seok Chang, Jean Bousquet
Published: 2026

The Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) guidelines had their first edition in 1999 (published in 2001),1 with subsequent revisions published in 2008,2 2010,3 2016,4 and 2020.5 Interestingly, ARIA 2016 was used as the case scenario on how to read a health Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)-based guidelines. Motivated by novel advances in the field, a new revision of the ARIA guidelines focused on the treatment of allergic rhinitis (AR) is currently being developed. This revision is endorsed by the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (ARIA-EAACI 2024–2025 guidelines) and follows the GRADE approach.6 The first 2 reports of the ARIA-EAACI 2024–2025 guidelines have been published,78 with one focusing on intranasal treatments and the other on oral and ocular treatments for AR. A third report is being prepared, and it will focus on treatment strategies, such as use of co-medication and the use of rhinitis treatment on an as-needed basis.

The ARIA-EAACI 2024–2025 guidelines are being conceived as evidence-based, person-centered, digitally enabled, and assisted by artificial intelligence (AI). They differ from previous editions of the guidelines not only in some of the questions they address (e.g., for the first time, the ARIA guidelines produce recommendations concerning individual medications within the same treatment class), but also in terms of their sources of evidence. In fact, these guidelines are being informed by systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials, data introduced by the patients in the MASK-air® app, pharmacovigilance data, planetary health data, and a survey to ARIA experts on the costs of AR treatments, among others.6

The most important aspect of ARIA from 2010 onwards has been the person-centered approach, and we need to extend the global approach of ARIA into a tailored regional or country approach that also takes into account the diversity of access to healthcare for different populations within one country. The ARIA-EAACI guidelines will be innovative in terms of adaptation diversity across the globe. In fact, the ARIA guidelines have always been developed with the purpose of being used internationally. However, it is important to note that there can be wide geographical variability in terms of exposure to allergens, availability and access to medications, treatment use patterns, and medication cost-effectiveness, among others. Therefore, producing regionally tailored recommendations is essential to render ARIA guidelines more suitable to be used in different settings or contexts.

The regional tailoring of the ARIA guidelines will encompass (i) identification of relevant patient-centered questions and production of de novo recommendations, (ii) adaptation of existing recommendations, and (iii) production of material in local languages. In terms of guideline questions, a novel AI-supported approach was used to identify patient-centered questions for the ARIA 2024–2025 guidelines.9 In particular, we used large language models to identify relevant guideline questions based on the most popular web searches on AR.9 This process is being repeated by analyzing the most common web searches in different countries or regions: such process is being done for Latin America, the Middle East and China.

As for adaptation of existing recommendations, this involves considering local evidence whenever possible. In detail, the ARIA recommendations have been produced following the Evidence-to-Decision framework.1011 This framework presupposes that evidence is collected on a set of different criteria, such as the desirable and undesirable effects (“benefits and harms”) of the interventions, values, costs and cost-effectiveness, acceptability, equity, feasibility and planetary health. Based on the information available for the different criteria, the guideline panel then formulates a strong or conditional recommendation in favor or against the intervention. For adapting ARIA recommendations to different regions, we may consider (i) local evidence for criteria such as costs (i.e., what are the costs of medications in the countries being addressed) and cost-effectiveness, and (ii) what medications are available, accessible and affordable in the countries or regions of interest. In addition, when adapting recommendations for countries where MASK-air® is available, we will consider MASK-air® data from individual countries to inform criteria such as the acceptability of interventions.

Finally, to support dissemination, it will be important not only to have ARIA recommendations translated into local languages but also that translation efforts extend to other materials such as treatment algorithms or plain language recommendations (PLRs). PLRs are documents that present information from guideline recommendations in a language accessible to the general public and with content that is particularly relevant for patients and the public.12 Considering that AR is a disease in which self-management is of particular importance, and that many patients worldwide do not speak English, having PLRs translated into multiple languages will be particularly important for guideline implementation.

All these efforts require a collaboration between the ARIA group and local experts and societies. The latter can play a key role in the identification of key contextual factors (e.g., drawing attention to practices that may be specific to one country or region), provision of local data (e.g., on treatment costs), translation of ARIA material and dissemination of the ARIA guidelines through local healthcare professionals and patients. Recommendation tailoring is essential to ensure the best delivery of the care in different regions and context and will be a key piece in the broader process of transforming ARIA into living guidelines. We then shall assess new evidence and regularly update the guidelines using a web-based approach and, if needed by a large body of evidence, make periodic updates.
