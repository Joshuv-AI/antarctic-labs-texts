Redo aortic valve replacement in twin pregnancy: navigating high-risk cardiac surgery with maternal and fetal success – A case report

Introduction

Redo aortic valve replacement, particularly in pregnant patients, represents a complex clinical challenge, requiring careful consideration of maternal cardiovascular health and fetal well-being. Aortic valve replacement (AVR), whether performed as an initial procedure or as a redo surgery, is a life-saving intervention for individuals with severe aortic valve pathology, such as stenosis, regurgitation, or prosthetic valve dysfunction. Mechanical prosthetic heart valves, commonly used in AVR due to their durability, introduce additional complexities during pregnancy, as they necessitate long-term anticoagulation therapy to prevent thromboembolic complications. This issue becomes particularly critical during pregnancy, which is a state of hypercoagulability that significantly increases the risk of prosthetic valve thrombosis [1].

Prosthetic valve stenosis, characterized by a thrombus or fibrous tissue growth (pannus) obstructing valve function, can result in catastrophic hemodynamic instability if left untreated. It is estimated to occur in 0.1 % to over 6 % of patients annually, depending on factors such as valve type, position, and anticoagulation regimen. In pregnant patients, AVR presents unique challenges due to the physiological and anatomical changes of pregnancy, including increased blood volume, altered hemodynamics, and the hypercoagulable state, which collectively heighten the risk of valve malfunction. Furthermore, the necessity of anticoagulation therapy in these patients introduces a delicate balance between reducing maternal thromboembolic risk and minimizing fetal complications, such as embryopathy, fetal loss, or preterm delivery [1].

Redo AVR, often indicated for prosthetic valve dysfunction such as thrombosis, pannus formation, or patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), is particularly high-risk in the context of pregnancy. Furthermore, redo AVR during pregnancy remains a high-stakes procedure requiring multidisciplinary collaboration.

Herein, we report a case of a 31-year-old pregnant woman with a twin gestation who was eventually diagnosed with severe prosthetic aortic valve (PrAV) stenosis and underwent a successful redo AVR. This case underscores the complexity of managing aortic valve prostheses in pregnancy, highlighting the unique challenges of surgical timing, hemodynamic optimization, and anticoagulation. It also emphasizes the importance of individualized care tailored to the patient's clinical status and gestational age, with a focus on achieving favorable outcomes for both mother and fetus.

This case report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE criteria [2].

Case presentation

A 31-year-old female in her twin pregnancy was referred to the cardiothoracic surgery clinic with complaints of progressively worsening shortness of breath over a duration of two weeks. On examination, it was revealed that her symptoms included dyspnea while supine, orthopnea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, significantly impairing her quality of life.

The patient had a history of aortic stenosis and sub valvular membrane, which had resulted in aortic valve replacement (AVR) being performed when the patient was 11 years of age. The surgery itself was performed using the Manouguian technique. She was advised lifelong warfarin therapy for anticoagulation and to prevent valve thrombosis; however, it was self-discontinued and since 2014, she was not on any anticoagulation. During her current twin pregnancy, she was prescribed enoxaparin under the guidance of a physician from her hometown. At the time of presentation, her gestational age was 14 weeks.

Upon further work up, which included an Echocardiography that revealed an ejection fraction (EF) of 60 %, the mechanical aortic prosthesis demonstrated absent disc mobility, severely increased gradients across the PrAV alongside mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, resulting in elevated transvalvular gradients of 172 mmHg peak and 94 mmHg of mean gradient. She was admitted at another hospital in emergency and thrombolysis was performed with Streptokinase as suspected valve thrombosis with partially improved disc mobility reported though her symptoms persisted, and the gradient remained high across the mechanical aortic valve. She was then referred to our institute for further management.

In view of increasing symptoms, high transvalvular gradients and increased future risk with progress of gestation the multidisciplinary team recommended surgical intervention based on the recommendation by the multidisciplinary team. The multidisciplinary team included cardiothoracic surgery, cardiology, obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, and anesthesia specialists. Preoperatively, the anesthesia team conducted a thorough assessment of maternal cardiovascular status and fetal viability, in close collaboration with obstetrics regarding gestational age and risk.

Subsequently, the high risk of maternal and fetal adverse outcomes was explained, and the patient was scheduled for an elective reoperative open heart surgery. The procedure involved a full re-do sternotomy using an oscillating saw and simultaneous exposure of right femoral vessels to be used in case of emergency. After redo sternotomy careful dissection was performed to reach the aorta and right atrium for the establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). It was run under mild hypothermic conditions, using a high-flow, high-pressure strategy to support fetal circulation (mean perfusion pressure > 70 mmHg). After cardioplegic arrest of the heart the aortotomy was performed, the explantation of previously placed mono-leaflet mechanical valve (Fig. 1) was carried out, subaortic thick fibrous membrane (pannus) resected out (Fig. 2), the aortic root was calcified more at the enlargement site, aortic re-root enlargement performed with bovine pericardium and a bi-leaflet size 21 mechanical valve (Onyx) was placed using interrupted ethibond sutures. Intraoperatively, transesophageal echocardiography was used to guide the procedure with detailed pre-procedural information and ensure procedural integrity at the end of the procedure after coming off CPB. The total CPB time was 235 min, and a cross-clamp time of 180 min. The cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time was significantly prolonged due to the technical complexity of the reoperative procedure, which involved multiple critical surgical steps. Firstly, explantation of the previously implanted mechanical valve was required, which was particularly challenging due to dense adhesions from the prior surgery and fibrotic tissue surrounding the prosthesis. Furthermore, careful dissection was necessary to avoid damage to surrounding cardiac structures, particularly the aortic annulus and left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). Secondly, a thick subaortic pannus excised carefully to relieve obstruction and restore unobstructed flow through the LVOT. This step was essential to address both functional stenosis and improve hemodynamics postoperatively. Thirdly, the aortic root, which had undergone prior enlargement using the Manouguian technique, was found to be heavily calcified. Aortic root re-enlargement was performed using a bovine pericardial patch to reconstruct the root, allowing for safe valve implantation and reducing the risk of iatrogenic annular rupture or distortion. Postoperatively, our patient did well and was extubated within the first 24 h. The maternal fetal medicine team was actively involved, and repeated fetal assessments were done, which included ultrasound and fetal heart rate monitoring, that confirmed viability and fetal well-being. In the later part of her course, she developed complete atrioventricular dissociation, necessitating temporary pacing via the epicardial wires followed by a permanent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation on the 10th day. Subsequently, the patient remained stable and was gradually mobilized under a structured cardiac rehabilitation program. At the time of discharge, she was hemodynamically and vitally stable with a viable pregnancy as determined by fetal ultrasound (Fig. 3), and she was counseled on anticoagulation management and obstetric follow-up.Fig. 3Fetal ultrasound images from the 15-week gestation scan demonstrating dichorionic diamniotic twins with ventriculomegaly and hydrocephalus in one fetus.Fig. 3

At the five- to six-month postoperative follow-up, the patient was clinically stable with improved functional capacity. She had experienced pre-term labor at 28 weeks due to premature rupture of the membrane, delivering both twins alive; however, tragically, both infants passed away during neonatal intensive care at a tertiary public healthcare facility. Further follow-up with a transthoracic echocardiography (Fig. 4) revealed moderate to severe aortic regurgitation due to a paravalvular leak adjacent to the mechanical aortic prosthesis. Given her stable hemodynamic status and the absence of infectious or embolic complications, elective transcatheter closure of the defect was recommended, with careful planning for interval imaging and anticoagulation management.

Case discussion

The management of prosthetic valve thrombosis (PVT) in pregnant patients requiring redo aortic valve replacement (AVR) presents a significant clinical challenge due to the complex interplay between maternal hemodynamics, fetal health, and the necessity of lifelong anticoagulation therapy. This case highlights the critical considerations in surgical decision-making, anticoagulation management, and multidisciplinary care required to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

The patient had previously undergone AVR at 11 years of age using the Manouguian technique, a procedure employed to enlarge a small aortic annulus and allow implantation of a larger prosthetic valve to reduce the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) [3]. PPM is a significant concern in young patients, as it increases the risk of structural valve deterioration (SVD) and stenosis [4]. Redo AVR, often indicated for prosthetic valve dysfunction such as thrombosis, pannus formation, or patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) as result of the patient over growing the prosthesis placed in childhood, is particularly high-risk in the context of pregnancy. PPM, characterized by an effective prosthetic valve area too small for the patient's body size, can exacerbate left ventricular outflow obstruction and lead to elevated transvalvular gradients [5]. When superimposed with thrombosis, PPM can critically impair valve function, necessitating urgent surgical intervention. Advances in surgical techniques, including the use of smaller prosthetic valves with superior hemodynamics and enhanced cardiopulmonary bypass protocols, techniques to enlarge aortic roots to accommodate bigger valves have improved outcomes in such cases [5].

Pregnant women with mechanical heart valves have also been shown to have greater chances of morbidity and mortality due to hemorrhagic or thrombotic episodes. Mechanical Valve thrombosis complicates 4.7 % of these pregnancies, and a total of 18.4 % of patients suffer a loss of pregnancy [6]. Additionally, redo AVR is associated with higher surgical risk, including increased adhesions, structural disruption, and greater perioperative mortality. The maternal mortality rate associated with cardiac surgery varies from 1.5 % to 4.2 %, compared with the fetal mortality rate of 9.5 % to 33 % [7]. The fact that this case was a redo AVR, which itself has a higher mortality rate compared to primary AVR, further highlights the complexity of this case.

The decision to proceed with redo AVR was guided by clinical decompensation and high transvalvular gradients, and the possibility of worsening with the progress of pregnancy. Surgical risk was stratified using the available literature, which indicated a higher predicted risk due to prior cardiac surgery, prosthetic dysfunction, and pregnancy. Given the severity of valve obstruction, high transvalvular gradients, and progressive symptoms, conservative or catheter-based options were deemed inappropriate. Redo surgical AVR was selected after a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation. Existing literature shows that while percutaneous or thrombolytic therapy is a potential alternative, its success is significantly lower in patients with mechanical aortic valves and associated pannus formation, aligning and justifying with the decision for surgical intervention in our patient [6].

Another challenge of management includes the intraoperative anesthesia management that was fundamental to successful outcomes. The application of mild hypothermic, high-flow CPB, vigilant maintenance of perfusion pressures, strict glucose and temperature control, and careful vasopressor optimization were critical to maintain the delicate control of uteroplacental circulation and prevent fetal hypoxia or loss. Multidisciplinary coordination- especially between anesthesia, obstetrics, cardiology, and surgical teams—was central to ensuring both maternal-fetal homeostasis and technical surgical success through the perioperative process.

Postoperative care required continued multidisciplinary collaboration, with coordinated efforts by the cardiology, obstetrics, and critical care teams to ensure optimal maternal and fetal outcomes. Continuous fetal monitoring was employed alongside maternal hemodynamic assessments to detect any early signs of decompensation or fetal distress. Pain control, mobilization, and nutritional support were also individualized to minimize maternal stress and support recovery. Postoperative echocardiographic evaluations were performed to assess prosthetic valve function, and maternal medications were adjusted in consultation with obstetrics to ensure fetal safety. This integrated approach was instrumental in navigating the complexities of the early recovery phase and maintaining maternal-fetal stability throughout that eventually led to delivery of both twins.

Anaesthetic management requires use of agents with minimal teratogenic effects, and vasoconstrictors are avoided in such patients due to their impact on uterine blood flow. CPB poses risks including coagulation changes, altered blood component function, vasoactive substance release, and hypothermia [8]. CPB during pregnancy has to be monitored closely as it can affect the balance between the fetus and the mother. Effective anxiolysis, analgesia, and anesthesia are crucial to prevent further decrease of cardiac function during surgery and labour [9].

During the redo procedure, a 21 mm On-X mechanical valve was implanted due to its superior hemodynamic profile and lower thrombogenicity compared to conventional mechanical valves. The On-X valve has been associated with improved long-term survival, better anticoagulation control, and lower thrombotic complications, particularly in patients who self-monitor INR levels [10]. Despite these advantages, redo cardiac surgery remains a high-risk intervention, particularly in pregnant patients, due to prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) times, increased bleeding risk, and altered coagulation physiology [7].The cardiopulmonary bypass time was prolonged due to several reasons, such as the need for careful explantation of the previous prosthesis in an smallish calcified root, subaortic membrane excision, aortic root re-enlargement in a calcified area in order to avoid left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction.

Anticoagulation in pregnancy, particularly in patients with mechanical heart valves, involves navigating a narrow therapeutic window to ensure both maternal and fetal safety. Warfarin, though the anticoagulant of choice in non-pregnant patients with mechanical valves due to its efficacy in preventing valve thrombosis, is best avoided in the first trimester in higher doses owing to its teratogenic effects and association with fetal warfarin syndrome.

Surgical intervention was performed in the second trimester, a period considered safer for both maternal and fetal outcomes. Enoxaparin continued throughout the perioperative period. Warfarin therapy was resumed postoperatively once the patient was stabilized, and fetal concerns were no longer present. This timeline reflects a cautious and guideline-informed approach, balancing the need for effective anticoagulation with fetal safety at various gestational stages [11].

The presence of a twin pregnancy further complicated decision-making, as twin gestation carries higher risks of preterm labor, gestational hypertension, and increased thrombotic events. While some clinicians advocate pregnancy termination in such high-risk cases, a multidisciplinary shared decision was made (based on patient refusal to terminate the pregnancy) to continue the pregnancy with intensive maternal-fetal monitoring.

As discussed above, our patient developed a complete AV dissociation, a recognized complication of redo AVR due to perioperative conduction system injury. This necessitated temporary pacing, followed by the implantation of a permanent dual-chamber pacemaker to ensure adequate heart rate regulation. The involvement of cardiothoracic surgery, cardiology, and obstetrics teams was critical in managing this complication while ensuring continued fetal viability.

Redo aortic valve replacement (AVR) during pregnancy is an exceptionally rare intervention, with most documented cases involving either valve-in-valve transcatheter approaches or open surgical procedures performed during early gestation or in the postpartum period. Furthermore, most of these reports involve singleton pregnancies and do not describe concurrent fetal anomalies. In contrast, our case describes a redo mechanical AVR associated with elevated maternal and fetal risks, performed at 14 weeks of multiple gestation and fetal anomalies, highlighting the profound clinical and ethical complexities involved in managing life-threatening maternal cardiac conditions during high-risk pregnancies [12,13].

This case underscores the complexity of managing mechanical valve thrombosis in pregnancy and highlights the importance of pre pregnancy counselling and a multidisciplinary approach to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes. The interplay between maternal cardiovascular health and fetal development necessitates a highly individualized, closely monitored treatment strategy, with long-term follow-up essential to monitor prosthetic valve function, anticoagulation status, and overall maternal and fetal health.

Conclusion

Redo aortic valve replacement in pregnancy is a complex, high-risk procedure requiring meticulous planning and multidisciplinary care. This case highlights the challenges of prosthetic valve thrombosis, patient-prosthesis mismatch, and anticoagulation management in pregnancy. Successful outcomes depend on individualized surgical strategies, optimized anticoagulation, and continuous maternal-fetal monitoring. Despite the risks, advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative care have improved patient outcomes. This case underscores the importance of pre pregnancy counselling provision of contraception, recognition of complications, multidisciplinary collaboration, and long-term follow-up to ensure optimal maternal and fetal health.
