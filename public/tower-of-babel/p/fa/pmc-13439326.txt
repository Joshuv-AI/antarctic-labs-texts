Comparative efficacy of intermittent hypoxic training modalities on athletes’ performance: a systematic review with pairwise and network meta-analyses
Authors: Xuyang Jiang, Yanwei Sun, Jiaxue Huang, Zhiru Zhang, Faiz Bin Baharudin, Muhammad Atiq Noviudin Pritama, Mohamed Nashrudin Bin Naharudin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA systematic review and network meta-analysis of 29 randomized controlled trials (603 trained athletes) was conducted. Pairwise and network meta-analyses were performed to compare the effects of three different type of hypoxic training: IHT, RSH, and SIH, against intensity-matched normoxic training on aerobic and anaerobic capacity. Simulated altitude was examined as an exploratory subgroup moderator. Exploratory subgroup analyses indicated that moderate and high simulated altitudes enhanced aerobic capacity, whereas anaerobic improvement occurred only at moderate altitude. Modality-specific analyses revealed a distinct outcome-dependent hierarchy: IHT (pairwise SMD = 0.55; network SMD = 0.53) and RSH (pairwise SMD = 0.43; network SMD = 0.40) significantly improved aerobic capacity, whereas SIH did not. For anaerobic capacity, only RSH showed significant improvement (pairwise SMD = 0.57; network SMD = 0.55). Probability ranking indicated IHT ranked highest for aerobic capacity (SUCRA = 84.2) and RSH for anaerobic outcomes (SUCRA = 95.6). IHT appears most suitable for aerobic development in endurance athletes, while RSH seems to offer the most consistent benefits across both aerobic and anaerobic domains. These findings provide directional guidance for hypoxic training prescription, pending confirmation from adequately powered head-to-head trials with standardized protocols and long-term follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55001-1.Keywords: Hypoxic training, Aerobic performance, Anaerobic performance, Athletes, Network meta-analysisSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

The strategic integration of hypoxic stimuli into athletic training regimens represents a well-established ergogenic aid. It is principally aimed at enhancing oxygen transport and consumption, hence improving aerobic performance1. Main types of intermittent hypoxic training have been systematically identified: interval hypoxic training (IHT), repeated-sprint training in hypoxia (RSH), and sprint interval training in hypoxia (SIH)2. These modalities are defined as follows: IHT involves medium duration (~ 30 s to 5 min), high-intensity (70% VO2max) intervals with similar duration recoveries, generally performed in an attempt to improve high-intensity exercise capacity3,4; RSH entails repeated maximal sprints (≤ 10 s) with short, incomplete recoveries (≤ 60 s) to target the development of repeated-sprint ability and metabolic resilience5–7; and SIH is defined as 20-s to 30-s “all out” efforts with 3 to 5 min of recovery in between to stress both anaerobic and aerobic energy systems8,9.

Despite their widespread application in elite sports, a significant practical dilemma persists coaches and athletes are often uncertain which modality yields optimal results for their specific performance goals10. The choice between IHT, RSH, and SIH is frequently guided by intuition or convenience rather than empirical evidence. This leads to suboptimal training prescriptions and potentially wasted training time. This uncertainty is compounded by the lack of clear guidelines regarding the ideal altitude dose for maximizing performance gains. Consequently, practitioners rely on trial‑and‑error approaches when designing hypoxic training programs11.

Despite their distinct physiological targets, a synthesis that integrates both pairwise and network meta-analytical approaches and systematically examines simulated altitude as a moderator remains absent. Moreover, while certain research indicates that IHT may enhance endurance measures, its use in sporting settings has produced variable results12,13. Likewise, direct evidence supporting RSH’s superiority in enhancing aerobic performance remains scarce and inconclusive, even though RSH has theoretical appeal for sports characterized by high-intensity bursts14,15. Similarly, the efficacy of SIH on athletic performance remains insufficiently investigated. Existing studies yield inconsistent findings regarding its potential benefits for both aerobic and anaerobic outcomes6,9. Previous meta-analytical endeavours have been confined to either conventional pairwise comparisons or network meta-analysis (NMA) exclusively, but not both16–18. Furthermore, the prospective moderating influence of simulated altitude on performance outcomes remains ambiguous, as prior syntheses have rarely analysed it systematically19.

This study advances the current literature by addressing two critical gaps. First, we employ a convergent analytical strategy. We perform both pairwise and network meta‑analyses to enable direct comparisons and generate a ranked hierarchy of interventions through the integration of direct and indirect evidence. This dual approach enhances methodological rigor and strengthens the reliability of the conclusions by cross‑validating findings across two complementary analytical frameworks. Second, simulated altitude (low, moderate, high) has rarely been systematically examined as a potential effect modifier across hypoxic training modalities in prior meta-analyses. By incorporating these advanced analytical features, this research seeks to establish a more refined and evidence‑based framework for optimizing hypoxic training prescriptions. This framework is tailored to specific athletic performance objectives.

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses NMA (Appendix S1)20. Additionally, the study protocol has been registered with PROSPERO under registration number CRD420261286882.

Data sources and searches

The following English electronic databases were searched systematically: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane from their inception date to Nov 1, 2025. The detailed search strategy is presented in Appendix S2. We manually searched all review articles related to altitude or hypoxic training and traced additional possible studies by reviewing their reference lists.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were established in accordance with the PICOS framework. The population comprised trained athletes. Eligible interventions included IHT, RSH and SIH. The comparator was defined as intensity-matched normoxic training. Primary outcomes were aerobic and anaerobic capacity. Regarding study design, only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were considered, regardless of whether blinding or allocation concealment was implemented.

Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: duplicate publications; studies with irrelevant or inconsistent research objectives; acute intervention protocols; hypoxic modalities other than IHT, RSH, or SIH; absence of a normoxic control group; and unavailability of full-text articles.

Data extraction

All manuscripts were reviewed by at least two independent reviewers. Two reviewers independently determined potentially eligible articles meeting the inclusion criteria using the titles and abstracts. Two independent reviewers then applied the eligibility criteria after obtaining the full texts and generated a final list of included articles through consensus.

A standardized data extraction form was applied to each study to extract information on the first author, publication year, participant characteristics (sample size, sex, and athlete type), intervention protocols (training duration and frequency), altitude exposure, and outcome measures. All included studies reported continuous outcome data as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Risk of bias and GRADE assessment

Two reviewers independently rated the methodological quality of the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool21. This tool assesses seven domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases. Because blinding of participants and personnel is inherently difficult in exercise intervention research, this domain was excluded from the overall ROB score. This limitation is widely acknowledged in hypoxic training meta‑analyses18.We classified each study’s overall ROB as low if no domain was rated high risk and three or fewer domains were rated unclear risk; as moderate if one domain was rated high risk, or no high risk but four or more domains were rated unclear risk; and as high in all other cases22. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology was used to evaluate the certainty of evidence for the main outcomes from the network meta-analysis23.

Data synthesis and analysis

The pre-to-post changes in performance outcomes for both hypoxic training groups and their matched-intensity normoxic control groups were pooled to estimate intervention effects. The standard deviation (SD) of the change scores was calculated according to the formula recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions24, based on baseline and post-intervention SDs and correlations where available. Since this correlation was rarely reported in the included studies, it was assumed to be r = 0.5, as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook. To assess the robustness of our findings to this assumption, we performed sensitivity analyses using alternative correlation coefficients of r = 0.3 and r = 0.7. Therefore, the primary findings are considered robust to the choice of the imputed correlation. Interventions are inherently heterogeneous. Therefore, in addition to network estimates, we also report pairwise meta-analytic estimates using Review Manager 5.3 (Nordic Cochrane, Denmark) to assess heterogeneity among studies. This heterogeneity was further examined through sensitivity analysis.

For the pairwise meta-analyses, pooled-effect estimates were calculated using a random-effects model in Review Manager 5.3 (Nordic Cochrane, Denmark), which was also used to quantify heterogeneity. The standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was selected as the effect measure for both aerobic and anaerobic capacity outcomes to facilitate comparison across studies that employed different testing protocols. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with I² > 50% or a Q test p-value ≤ 0.10 indicating substantial heterogeneity25. Potential publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots and statistical testing using Begg’s test.

A frequentist framework random-effects network meta-analysis was performed using STATA 16.0 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), assuming a common heterogeneity parameter across all treatment comparisons within the network. In cases where insufficient direct evidence existed for a given comparison, effect estimates were informed through indirect evidence via the network structure. The relationships between interventions IHT, RSH, SIH and Normoxic Control were visualized using network diagrams, in which the size of each node was proportional to the number of participants and the thickness of connecting lines represented the number of studies for each direct comparison. The contribution of each direct comparison to the network estimates was assessed via contribution graphs.

Transitivity assumptions were assessed by evaluating the inclusion criteria of individual studies, by determining whether all participants in the network could be randomly allocated to any intervention, and by using consistency models. Loop‑specific heterogeneity tests were used to evaluate consistency within each closed loop. A global inconsistency model was applied to test for overall inconsistency. Node‑splitting analysis was performed to examine local inconsistency for each comparison. The consistency model was considered appropriate when no statistically significant inconsistency was detected (p > 0.05).

The relative ranking of the interventions for each outcome was estimated using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA)26. SUCRA values range from 0% to 100%, with higher values indicating a greater probability of an intervention ranking among the most effective. It should be noted that SUCRA reflects probabilistic ranking only and does not convey information about effect magnitude or evidence certainty; rankings should therefore always be interpreted alongside the corresponding SMD estimates and GRADE assessments.

Results

Literature search and trial selection

Figure 1 shows the flow chart that explains the study selection process works. From systematic searches across four electronic databases, we found a total of 4937 records: PubMed (n = 819), Web of Science (n = 482), Embase (n = 1369), and the Cochrane Library (n = 2267). We had 3693 records left for title and abstract screening after removing 1244 duplicates. During this screening phase, 3597 records were excluded, leaving 96 articles that could be fully assessed. After a thorough review of the complete texts, 67 studies were omitted due to various reasons, including non-target hypoxic modalities, absence of a control group, unavailability of the study design, non-athlete populations, or acute interventions. As a result, this meta-analysis comprised 29 studies for the quantitative synthesis.

Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram depicting the study selection process.

Description of the included trials

The Data Extraction Form Appendix S3 lists the details of the studies that were included. There were 29 randomized controlled trials published between 2005 and 2025, and they involved a total of 603 trained athletes. The participants included male and female athletes from several sports, such as team sports (like football, rugby, and basketball), endurance sports (like running, swimming, and cycling), and other individual sports.

The interventions were classified into three principal hypoxic training modalities: Interval Hypoxia Training (IHT, n = 10 studies), Repeated Sprint Training in Hypoxia (RSH, n = 14 studies), and Sprint Interval Training in Hypoxia (SIH, n = 5 studies). All interventions were compared against an active control of intensity-matched normoxic training. The simulated altitude in the experiments varied from 2,100 to 5,000 m, with 2,500 to 3,500 m being the most prevalent range. The average length of the intervention was 4.3 weeks (SD = 2.1), with 2 to 4 sessions each week.

Standardized assessments were used to measure key performance outcomes on a regular basis. Incremental treadmill or cycle tests were the main way to measure aerobic capacity. These tests measured VO₂max or peak power production. Tests including the Wingate Anaerobic Test, Repeated Sprint Ability (RSA) protocols, and sport-specific time trials were used to measure anaerobic capacity.

The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was used to check the quality of the methods used in the studies. It showed that 17 studies (58.6%) had a low overall risk of bias. The other 12 studies (41.4%) were judged to have a moderate risk of bias, while none were judged to have a high risk. The most prominent reasons for a moderate grade were not giving enough information about how allocation concealment worked and the fact that it was hard to keep participants from knowing about the hypoxia intervention.

Methodological quality assessment

The Risk of Bias Summary (Appendix S4) provides a full description of the methodological quality evaluation for each included study. Regarding random sequence generation, 24 studies (82.8%) were rated as low risk and 5 studies (17.2%) as unclear risk; no study was rated as high risk. For allocation concealment, all 29 studies (100%) were rated as unclear risk because the included trials generally did not report adequate allocation concealment procedures. For blinding of outcome assessors, 19 studies (65.5%) were rated as low risk, 10 studies (34.5%) as unclear risk, and none as high risk. Incomplete outcome data and selective outcome reporting were adequately addressed in all 29 studies (100% low risk). In the “other bias” category, 14 studies (48.3%) were rated as low risk, 12 studies (41.4%) as high risk, and 3 studies (10.3%) as unclear risk. Using the prespecified summative criteria, 17 studies (58.6%) were judged to have a low overall risk of bias, 12 studies (41.4%) a moderate overall risk of bias, and no study a high overall risk of bias.

Pairwise meta-analysis

Primary outcome

Aerobic

A pairwise meta-analysis was conducted to integrate the effects of several hypoxia training methods against a normoxic control. Figure 2 shows the forest plot of the pooled estimates for aerobic capacity. It also shows comprehensive trial-level information for each type of intervention. For aerobic capacity, pairwise meta‑analyses were based on 28 studies involving 537 athletes. IHT showed a significant improvement in aerobic capacity compared to the control group (SMD = 0.55, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.25, 0.85], I² = 0.0%, τ = 0.00), with no observed heterogeneity among the included studies. Similarly, RSH had a substantial positive effect (SMD = 0.43, p = 0.043, 95% CI [0.01, 0.84], I² = 60.1%, τ = 0.56), but there was some variation. SIH, on the other hand, did not show a statistically significant improvement (SMD = 0.25, p = 0.220, 95% CI [–0.15, 0.66], I² = 0.0%, τ = 0.00) (Appendix S5). To evaluate the robustness of our findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding each individual study and by applying alternative correlation coefficients (r = 0.3 and r = 0.7). In all cases, the pooled effect estimates, and their 95% confidence intervals remained consistent with the primary analysis, with no material change in the direction or statistical significance of the results (Appendix S5). The use of funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test to check for publication bias showed that there was no substantial bias in the analysis (Appendix S5).

Anaerobic

A pairwise meta-analysis was conducted to integrate the effects of several hypoxia training methods on anaerobic capacity in comparison to a normoxic control. Figure 3 shows the forest plot of the pooled estimates, which includes extensive trial-level information for each type of intervention. For anaerobic capacity, pairwise meta‑analyses were based on 24 studies involving 474 athletes. RSH exhibited a notable enhancement in anaerobic capacity compared to the control group (SMD = 0.57, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.30, 0.84], I² = 23.5%, τ = 0.24), with minimal heterogeneity. On the other hand, IHT did not have a statistically significant effect (SMD = 0.13, p = 0.678, 95% CI [–0.77, 1.03], I² = 67.2%, τ = 0.76), and substantial heterogeneity was observed. Likewise, SIH did not produce a significant enhancement (SMD = 0.04, p = 0.865, 95% CI [–0.42, 0.50], I² = 21.4%, τ = 0.27) (Appendix S5). Sensitivity analyses, including sequential study exclusion and alternative correlation coefficients (r = 0.3 and r = 0.7), confirmed the robustness of the primary findings (Appendix S5). The use of funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test to check for publication bias showed that there was no substantial bias in the analysis (Appendix S6).

Subgroup outcomes

For subgroup analysis, simulated elevations were classified into three categories: low altitude (> 1500–2500 m), moderate altitude (> 2500–3500 m), and high altitude (> 3500 m)27,28. Appendix S7 shows the forest plot for aerobic capacity broken down by simulated altitude level. The paired meta-analysis demonstrated varying impacts of simulated altitude on aerobic capacity. Significant improvements were noted in both the moderate altitude (SMD = 0.43, 95% CI [0.13, 0.73]) and high altitude (SMD = 0.47, 95% CI [0.02, 0.92]) subgroups when compared to the normoxic control. However, the effect in the low altitude subgroup did not achieve statistical significance (SMD = 0.44, 95% CI [-0.01, 0.88]). The funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias in the analysis (Appendix S8).

Appendix S8 shows the forest plot for anaerobic capacity stratified by simulated altitude level. The paired meta-analysis demonstrated a unique efficacy pattern among altitude subgroups. Only the moderate-altitude category (SMD = 0.53, 95% CI [0.30, 0.77]) showed a significant improvement in anaerobic capacity. This group had minimal heterogeneity (I² = 20.1%). On the other hand, the low-altitude (SMD = 0.52, 95% CI [-0.76, 1.81]) and high-altitude (SMD = -0.38, 95% CI [-0.89, 0.13]) subgroups did not demonstrate any statistically significant effects. The assessment of publication bias by funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test indicated no substantial bias in the analysis (Appendix S9).

Discussion

The present systematic review and network meta-analysis encompassed 29 randomized controlled trials with 603 individuals to examine the impact of various hypoxia training modalities on aerobic and anaerobic capacity. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to simulated altitude levels to investigate possible sources of heterogeneity. The NMA results showed that IHT and RSH considerably increased aerobic capacity compared to the normoxic control, however SIH did not show a statistically significant effect. RSH showed a substantial improvement in anaerobic capacity, however IHT and SIH did not demonstrate any statistically significant improvements. Subgroup analysis indicated that the impact on anaerobic capacity may vary by altitude level, with intermediate altitude exhibiting the most significant advantage.

The modest attenuation of effect sizes in the NMA relative to pairwise estimates is statistically expected, as NMA integrates both direct and indirect evidence, yielding more conservative point estimates. Critically, statistical significance for IHT and RSH was preserved across both frameworks and SUCRA rankings remained stable, substantiating the robustness of the principal findings. As SUCRA rankings do not capture effect magnitude or evidence certainty, and given that several comparisons carried low GRADE ratings, they should be regarded as supportive rather than conclusive evidence of superiority.

Effect of exercise on aerobic outcomes

The comparatively superior ranking of IHT for aerobic outcomes is likely attributable to the combined stimulus of repeated hypoxic exposure and sustained aerobic training, which may promote haematological adaptations through EPO stimulation and enhance central cardiovascular function29,30. Although RSH is primarily designed to target anaerobic metabolism, its meaningful aerobic benefits may reflect concurrent peripheral adaptations, including improved mitochondrial efficiency, enhanced muscle oxygenation kinetics, and activation of oxidative molecular signalling pathways such as S100A protein signalling and Akt phosphorylation31.

Nevertheless, inter-individual variability in hypoxic response warrants consideration. Approximately 20% of athletes may not experience quantifiable performance gains from RSH, with peripheral oxygen saturation during training emerging as a key predictor of subsequent performance improvements32. Recent evidence further suggests that hypoxia-induced limitations on endurance performance may involve complex interactions between oxygen delivery, muscle fatigue mechanisms, and neural regulation, which may partly explain the variable responses observed across different training modalities and athlete populations33. This heterogeneity underscores the significance of the convergent analytical strategy utilized in this investigation, wherein pairwise and network approaches synergistically enhance the evidence base.

Improvements in aerobic capacity of the magnitude observed in this review may translate to meaningful gains in time-trial performance or sustained power output for endurance athletes, though coaches and practitioners should note that individual responses are likely to vary according to baseline fitness level and sport-specific demands. IHT appears to be a potentially advantageous hypoxia training technique for augmenting aerobic capacity in athletes. RSH may serve as a valuable alternative, particularly when concurrent anaerobic benefits are desired. The included studies showed that SIH cannot be recommended as a primary strategy for aerobic enhancement.

Effect of exercise on anaerobic outcomes

RSH, which involves repeated near-maximal efforts with limited recovery, may place substantial demands on both anaerobic and aerobic energy systems, and was associated with improvements in both domains. This dual effect may be related to peripheral adaptations, such as potentially enhanced glycolytic capacity, buffering ability, and muscle oxygen utilization, as well as possible changes in mitochondrial efficiency and oxygenation kinetics, though the precise mechanisms remain to be confirmed34,35. By contrast, IHT is typically characterised by longer-duration efforts under hypoxic conditions and may more preferentially stimulate adaptations associated with oxygen transport and cardiovascular function, which could contribute to its relatively more consistent aerobic benefits36. SIH, although involving high-intensity exercise, often features longer sprint and recovery durations that may not fully replicate the metabolic demands of repeated-sprint activity, potentially explaining its less consistent effects on anaerobic outcomes37.

It should be acknowledged that the composite anaerobic outcome in the present review encompasses heterogeneous measures, including Wingate Test and Repeated Sprint Ability Test. This represents a limitation of the present synthesis. Nevertheless, the observed benefit of RSH across these composite anaerobic outcomes is consistent with recent evidence18, which demonstrated that RSH produced significant improvements across RSA, aerobic, and anaerobic outcomes compared with normoxic repeated-sprint training, with RSA yielding the most pronounced effect. These findings suggest that while RSH exerts its strongest influence on repeated-sprint performance, meaningful benefits across both aerobic and anaerobic domains are also supported by the broader literature, reinforcing the versatility of RSH as a training modality.

The convergence of pairwise and network estimates for RSH further supports the robustness of these findings. Improvements in anaerobic capacity of the magnitude observed in this review may translate to meaningful gains in repeated-sprint maintenance for athletes, though coaches and practitioners should note that individual responses are likely to vary according to baseline fitness level and sport-specific demands. The included studies showed that neither IHT nor SIH demonstrated significant anaerobic benefits, suggesting that RSH may be the modality of choice when anaerobic adaptation is the primary goal.

Subgroup on aerobic and anaerobic outcomes

The pairwise subgroup analyses revealed a notable distinction in the modulation of simulated altitude on the effects of hypoxia training for both aerobic and anaerobic outcomes. For aerobic capacity, improvements were consistent and significant throughout low, moderate, and high altitudes, with no statistically significant subgroup differences. This pronounced tendency suggests that the physiological processes enabling aerobic adaptations (such as enhancements in oxygen transport and utilization) are effectively engaged throughout a broad range of hypoxic doses, making altitude a less critical factor for this purpose. Current evidence indicates that aerobic adaptations may be effectively stimulated across a broad range of hypoxic doses, as the physiological dose-response to hypoxia remains active at both low and high simulated altitudes38.

Altitude appeared to moderate the anaerobic effects of hypoxic training in a pattern worth noting. In these subgroup analyses, statistically significant improvements in anaerobic capacity were observed only in the moderate-altitude stratum, while low and high-altitude groups failed to reach significance, resulting in a statistically significant between-subgroup difference (p = 0.006). This pattern is physiologically plausible given that moderate hypoxia (typically 2500–3500 m) may provide sufficient metabolic stress to augment glycolytic flux and buffering capacity without compromising sprint mechanics or training volume to the degree observed at higher altitudes39,40, and recent work tentatively places approximately 3000 m as a functional threshold beyond which power output begins to decline, with a dose-response investigation similarly situating optimal anaerobic adaptation within this moderate range40,41. Nonetheless, these analyses were conducted solely within the pairwise framework without integration into the NMA and involved few studies per stratum. These findings therefore complement the primary NMA result by suggesting a possible altitude-dependent moderating effect on RSH anaerobic improvements, but should be regarded as exploratory results, and adequately powered prospective trials systematically varying altitude dose are needed before firm recommendations can be justified.

Limitation

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this meta-analysis. First, the validity of a network meta-analysis fundamentally relies on the transitivity assumption. Although our analysis focused exclusively on trained athletes and we attempted to control for effect modifiers through our inclusion criteria, the participants encompassed a wide range of sports and levels of competition. These differences in athlete populations, along with variations in baseline training status, intervention duration, and specific outcome measures, may act as effect modifiers and threaten the clinical comparability of the included studies. Second, outcome heterogeneity represents a notable methodological concern. The composite aerobic and anaerobic outcomes in this review encompass heterogeneous measurement tools, and pooling these measures under a single SMD may obscure modality-specific effects. Third, although pairwise subgroup analyses were performed, the restricted number of studies within each altitude stratum led to sparse evidence networks, preventing a comprehensive NMA within subgroups; these findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory. Fourth, while both Begg’s and Egger’s tests were performed, publication bias assessments are underpowered when few studies are available per comparison. Lastly, the GRADE assessment indicated that the relative effectiveness of the active hypoxic modalities (IHT vs. RSH, IHT vs. SIH, RSH vs. SIH) was supported by mostly low certainty evidence, largely because direct head-to-head comparisons among the three active modalities were largely absent from the primary literature, meaning that active intervention rankings rely predominantly on indirect evidence. This underscores the need for future high-quality head-to-head RCTs with standardised outcome measures.

Conclusion

This network meta-analysis provides synthesised evidence that hypoxic training modalities offer meaningful performance benefits over normoxic training, with RSH appearing to be the most versatile option given its consistent associations with both aerobic and anaerobic improvements, and IHT suggesting better suited to contexts where aerobic development is the primary objective.

The practical implications of the present findings warrant consideration in the context of athlete population and sport type. For aerobic capacity development, IHT appears most applicable to endurance-based athletes such as cyclists, runners, and swimmers, given that the aerobic demands of these sports are most closely aligned with the adaptations targeted by IHT. However, whether the magnitude of aerobic benefit is equivalent across elite and sub-elite populations remains uncertain. Elite athletes, who already operate near their physiological ceiling, may derive smaller absolute gains from hypoxic training compared with sub-elite counterparts, a phenomenon consistent with the diminishing returns hypothesis in highly trained individuals. For anaerobic capacity and repeated-sprint performance, RSH appears most directly applicable to team-sport and court-sport athletes whose competitive demands involve repeated maximal efforts interspersed with incomplete recovery. Nonetheless, the degree to which laboratory-measured anaerobic indices translate to on-field competitive performance remains an important gap. Practitioners are therefore encouraged to interpret the present findings as providing directional guidance for training modality selection rather than prescriptive recommendations, and to consider individual athlete characteristics, training history, and competitive demands when implementing hypoxic training programmes. Future well-designed, adequately powered randomized controlled trials are needed that incorporate head-to-head comparisons of hypoxic modalities with standardized altitude dose, training exposure timing relative to competition, and ecologically valid performance outcomes. Studies should stratify participants by athletic level, sport type, and baseline fitness to allow modality-specific recommendations. Additionally, long-term follow-up assessments are essential to evaluate the durability of performance gains and potential detraining effects, which remain largely unexamined in the current literature.
