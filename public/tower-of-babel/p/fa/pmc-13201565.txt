Understanding and modelling ammonia partitioning and transport across reverse osmosis membrane
Authors: Zhijie Wang, Kai Yang, Subhamoy Mahajan, Ying Li, Mohan Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reverse osmosis (RO) is increasingly applied for the reclamation of ammonia-rich wastewater. However, the mechanisms governing NH3/NH4+ transport across RO membranes remain unclear. In this study, we develop an ammonia partitioning and transport (APT) model to quantitatively describe NH3/NH4+ retention and transport, incorporating pH-dependent NH3/NH4+ partitioning and membrane charge variation. The theoretical model is validated through laboratory-scale RO experiments under varying pressures, feed concentrations, and pH conditions. In contrast to stable water permeability, the permeability coefficient of total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) is sensitive to pH value. TAN retention is optimal (89.1 ± 3.2%) near neutral pH, decreasing at higher pH due to increased NH3 fraction and at lower pH due to reduced membrane charge. By decoupling diffusion, advection, and electromigration, the APT model shows distinct NH4⁺ and NH3 flux contributions, revealing pH-dependent transport mechanisms governed by speciation and membrane charge. Molecular dynamics simulations further differentiate the transport pathways of NH3 and NH4⁺, showing that NH4+ experiences a significantly higher energy barrier. Validations with both synthetic and real manure streams demonstrate good predictive accuracy for advancing RO applications in ammonia-rich streams.

Introduction

Ammonia is essential to securing the food supply and supporting global population growth. Synthesized by the Haber–Bosch process, ammonia has sustained about half of humanity over the past century1. Currently, the Haber–Bosch process accounts for 2% of global energy consumption and up to 5% of global greenhouse gas emissions2–4. This ammonia is consumed and cycled within the food chain and ends up in the excreta. In the U.S., 60% of ammonia emissions are attributed to the livestock manure wastewater produced in intensive animal feeding operations5. Excess manure nutrients lead to overapplication, increasing nutrient losses and reducing nutrient use efficiency. The high water content and low nutrient density increase costs to transport manure nutrients to cropland, with agronomic needs restricting the ability to use manure effectively.

Ammonia in manure can be directly reused through a shortcut resource recovery process6,7. To achieve clean water that meets discharge or even drinking standards, anaerobic treatment is applied to livestock manure, followed by a series of membrane treatment processes8, to recover energy, nutrients, and clean water out of waste streams. As the dominant technology for water purification with high energy efficiency, reverse osmosis (RO) is increasingly used for wastewater reclamation9, particularly for clean water recovery and ammonia concentration10,11. Compared with treating the wastewater by nitrification-denitrification and resynthesizing ammonia from the Haber–Bosch process, this shortcut was estimated to save energy as high as 16.2–19.7 kWh per kg-N11, making it a more sustainable solution12–14.

In practice, it has been found that total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) is difficult to fully retain in a single-pass RO, and thus, a two-pass RO system is often employed to minimize the presence of TAN in the final clean water. Recent studies reported experimental observation of significant variability in ammonia retention efficiency by RO membranes, ranging from 0 to 98%10,11,15,16. These results are particularly sensitive to the operating pH11. It is commonly believed that this variability results from the NH3/NH4+ speciation under varying pH, and NH3 and NH4+ have their respective permeabilities, which are different from each other. RO membrane is effective for only rejecting the charged ions, so a high permeability of TAN was observed at alkaline pH, where TAN is mainly in the form of NH310,17. However, even at acidic pH where all the TAN exists in the form of NH4+, its permeability is not simply a constant due to its interaction with the membrane charge11. Current understandings are limited to experimental observations and qualitative descriptions. A fundamental understanding of TAN transport through RO membranes remains limited; thus, a quantitative model is needed to establish the relation between TAN transport and pH variation.

Researchers have made significant efforts to develop models to describe the transport of water and salts through RO membranes18,19 (Supplementary Table 1). Recent experimental and computational studies demonstrated the presence of advective transport of solutes within the RO membrane9,20,21. Correspondingly, advanced models conceptualizing water and solute transport through porous RO membranes were developed, incorporating more detailed mechanisms, including Donnan steric partitioning and advective solution friction22,23. They have shown improved performance in simulating and fitting data for inert salt ions24,25. By introducing the fast acid–base reaction assumption, this modeling framework allows the inclusion of amphoteric ions. Purely simulated results of pH variation in RO showed the possible feasibility of this modeling framework26. By adjusting multiple fitting parameters, reasonable agreement with experiments was achieved when only inert ions and pH were considered27. Once additional amphoteric ions (e.g., NH4⁺) are included, the model’s generalizability declines markedly, and its predictions of permeate pH, amphoteric ions, and bivalent ions become less reliable even under a single feed pH28. The highly coupled and complex equations render the transport of amphoteric ions strongly dependent on the local membrane pH. For instance, NH3 transport is governed by the coupled transport of NH4⁺ and H⁺28.

In this study, we propose that the transport of amphoteric ions is governed by uncoupled transport equations of charged and neutral species, with the solution pH subsequently corrected through equilibrium recalculation. We analyzed and elucidated the separation and transport of TAN in RO membranes, as well as the complex changes induced by pH. Based on solute transport theory that considers friction between substances, we developed a theoretical model, named ammonia partitioning and transport (APT) model, to describe NH3/NH4+ transport in RO membranes. This model takes into account diffusion, advection, and electro-migration of solutes, while also considering the interactions between water, solutes, and the membrane matrix. RO experiments were conducted under different feed concentrations, pH levels, and operating pressures to validate the model. Our theoretical and experimental observations reveal the changes in TAN removal performance resulting from the pH-dependent shifts NH3/NH4+ speciation and RO membrane surface charge, as well as the differences in NH3/NH4+ transmembrane transport behavior, and extend the applicability of the solution-friction model in describing TAN transport in RO membranes.

We observed stable water permeability independent of feed pH variations. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, both experimental results (0.539 ± 0.019 L m−2 h−1 bar−1) and model calculations (0.554 L m−2 h−1 bar−1) demonstrate a stable water transport coefficient across the tested feed pH range (5.5–11). This is probably because water transport primarily depends on the frictional interaction between water molecules and the membrane24. Although the water transport is theoretically coupled with ion transport, the ion-associated term in Eq. 3 is negligible in dilute solutions. Therefore, observable changes in water permeability only occur when the membrane properties are significantly altered, such as by swelling under extreme pH conditions34 or compaction under excessively high pressure35.

TAN permeability coefficient initially decreases slightly as feed pH increases, followed by a sharp increase when the pH exceeds 8 (Fig. 2b). The model results indicate that the lowest permeability coefficient occurs at pH 7.3, with a value of 0.656 L m−2 h−1. Correspondingly, TAN rejection exhibits a parabolic trend (Fig. 2c), with the highest experimental rejection of 89.1 ± 3.2% observed near pH 7 and a model-predicted maximum of 88.7% at pH 7.3. Both high and low pH levels in the feed solution led to a reduced TAN rejection efficiency. This arch-shaped behavior has also been reported in previous studies11,36. To better understand the arch-shape, we use the APT model to separate the pH-dependent transport behavior of TAN into individual transport characteristics of NH3 and NH4⁺.

NH3 permeability remains constant across the feed pH range of 5.5–11, with a value of 24.75 L m−2 h−1 (Fig. 2b). As a result, its rejection remains consistently low at 17.2% (Fig. 2b). This is because NH3 is uncharged and therefore does not interact with the membrane charge. However, the NH3 transport flux increased from 0.00851 to 44.7 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹ when the feed pH value varied from 5.5 to 10.4 (Fig. 2d). This results from the pH-dependent distribution of NH3 and NH4⁺. Given that the permeability of NH3 is approximately 30 times higher than that of NH4⁺, an increase in NH3 fraction at high feed pH significantly boosts overall TAN permeability and reduces rejection efficiency.

NH4⁺ permeability slightly decreases from 1.18 to 0.21 L m⁻² h⁻¹ as feed pH increases from 5.5 to 11, respectively. As shown in the inset of Fig. 2b, there are two distinct drops for NH4+ permeability, one at a feed pH value of 5.5 and the other at 8.1. Both drops could be attributed to the increased negative charge of the membrane at elevated feed pH (Fig. 1b), which enhances the electrostatic repulsion of co-ions and reduces their partitioning into the membrane, thus reducing their transport. The counter-ions (i.e., NH4+) have to match the charge neutrality with co-ions in the permeate, thus reducing their transport and increasing NH4+ rejection (Fig. 2b, c)37. For example, the flux contributed solely by NH4⁺ is slightly higher at pH 6.2 (7.24 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹) than at pH 7.0 (6.34 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹), which can be attributed to the lower membrane surface charge at more acidic pH. At pH values below 8, NH4⁺ is the dominant TAN species, and thus its transport behavior governs the behavior of TAN. Although the second drop for NH4+ permeability is also due to membrane charge, at feed pH values above 8, NH3 becomes the dominant species, and the drop of NH4+ permeability contributes less significantly to the TAN permeability variation. A similar trend is also observed for Na⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 5), supporting that the transport of charged ions is mainly governed by electrostatic interaction.

In addition to total TAN concentration and the NH3/NH4⁺ speciation (Fig. 2e), the APT model also accurately predicts permeate pH values, showing good agreement with experimental results (R2 = 0.973, Fig. 2f). Notably, the permeate pH is constantly higher than the feed pH. For instance, with a feed pH of 8.45, the permeate pH increases to 9.6 (Fig. 2f). This pH elevation results from the re-establishment of equilibrium on the permeate side after the individual transport of NH3 and NH4⁺. Specifically, while NH3 and NH4⁺ exist in equilibrium on the feed side, NH3 transports across the RO membrane more readily than NH4⁺. As a result, NH3 temporarily reaches a higher concentration on the permeate side before re-equilibration occurs, driving the equilibrium toward NH4⁺ formation, thereby increasing the permeate pH. Despite being based on different theoretical assumptions, our pH prediction is consistent with outcomes derived from hydrated proton transport theory26.

Decoupling diffusion, advection, and electro-migration

The APT model also provides further insight into the flux contributions of NH4+ and NH3 during their transport across the membrane. The charged species NH4+ is transported by advection, diffusion, and electro-migration24, whereas the transport of uncharged species NH3 is governed by advection and diffusion. As shown in Eqs. 2.1 and 2.2, advection flux is the product of solute concentration, fluid velocity, and a hindrance factor for transport in narrow pore, while diffusion flux is driven by concentration gradients and electromigration by potential gradients along the transport direction. Both diffusion and electromigration are also influenced by the diffusion coefficient, membrane porosity, and hindrance factor.

For NH4+, the highest total transmembrane flux of 13.81 mmol m−2 h−1 occurs at pH 5.3. As pH gradually increases to around 6, the significant increase in membrane charge causes a notable decrease in total flux to 7.727 mmol m−2 h−1. Further increasing pH to 9 results in a second notable decrease in total flux to 1.616 mmol m−2 h−1, eventually reducing it to nearly zero as pH further increases due to the combined effect of increased membrane charge and decreased NH4+ proportion.

Although the total NH4+ flux exhibits a decreasing trend with increasing feed pH value, the individual flux contributions from diffusion, advection, and electromigration show distinct behaviors (Fig. 3a). At acidic feed pH conditions, diffusion of NH4+ ions is negligible, whereas advection and electromigration fluxes occur in opposite directions. Advection transports NH4+ towards the permeate side along with water molecules, while electro-migration contributes to a negative flux, thereby acting as a hindrance to NH4+ transport. For example, at pH 6, where the membrane charge is 68.60 mM, the fluxes from diffusion, advection, and electromigration are 2.03, 481.14, and −475.44 mmol m−2 h−1, respectively, resulting in a total flux of 7.73 mmol m−2 h−1.Fig. 3Transport flux contributions from advection, diffusion, and electro-migration with varying feed pH.a Flux of NH4+, where the left y-axis stands for the fluxes from advection, diffusion, and electro-migration, and right y-axis stands for the total NH4+ flux; b flux of NH3, where the left y-axis stands for the diffusion flux as well as the total flux of NH3, and the right y-axis stands for the advection flux. As the feed pH value increases to around 8.4, electro-migration flux becomes more negative to −740.8 mmol m−2 h−1 due to increased membrane charge to −156.2 mM, until the NH4+ concentration decreases rapidly at pH 9 as the dissociation equilibrium shifts towards NH3. Similarly, as counter-ions, more NH4+ ions partition into the membrane with the increasing membrane charge at pH larger than 8, resulting in a higher in-membrane NH4+ concentration, and therefore a high advection flux of 1001 mmol m−2 h−1 at pH 8.8. However, we note that the increased advection does not lead to a higher total transport flux due to the high electromigration hindrance of −665.1 mmol m−2 h−1 at pH 8.8. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation also reveals the strong electrostatic adsorption when NH4+ ions transport out of the membrane, as discussed in the next section. Further increasing feed pH to 10 significantly reduces NH4+ concentration. Combined with the effect of membrane charge and potential changes, it drastically decreases the electromigration flux. Given that electro-migration is more sensitive to NH4+ concentration, the reduction in electro-migration is more significant than that of advection for the feed pH range of 8–10. These substantial changes in both electromigration and advection result in a reverse concentration gradient within the membrane, driving a reverse diffusion flux that balances the advection flux. The reverse diffusion flux reaches a maximum of –477.94 mmol m−2 h−1 at pH 9.3, where the electromigration flux decreases to −327.09 mmol m−2 h−1 while the advection flux is still as high as 806.08 mmol m−2 h−1.

For NH3, the advection and diffusion fluxes increase from both almost 0 to 0.5645 and 46.46 mmol m−2 h−1, respectively, as the feed pH value increases from 5.3 to 11, while electromigration is absent due to its neutrality. Therefore, we observe an increasing total NH3 flux from almost 0 to 47.02 mmol m−2 h−1, of which the diffusion flux is the major contributor, with advection contributing only marginally about 1.2% (Fig. 3b). Such a trend is attributed to the NH3 speciation, as the sharp increase begins at pH 8, where the NH4+/NH3 equilibrium starts to shift towards NH3 formation.

Notably, although the total NH3 flux, of which the highest is 47.02 mmol m−2 h−1, is significantly higher than that of NH4⁺, with a highest of 13.81 mmol m−2 h−1, the component fluxes differ greatly in magnitude. For example, the advective flux of NH4+ in the range of 256.9–1001 mmol m−2 h−1 is approximately 3–4 orders higher than that of NH3 in the range of 0–0.5645 mmol m−2 h−1. This is because of their distinct partitioning mechanisms at the membrane–water interface (Fig. 1c). The Donnan effect drives cations (NH4⁺) to partition at higher concentrations within the membrane to balance its negative charge, resulting in a higher concentration inside the membrane and thus a larger advection flux for NH4⁺. However, its total NH4⁺ flux remains relatively low due to the negative electromigration flux. In contrast, NH3 is unaffected by membrane charge or the Donnan effect and is subject only to steric exclusion at the interface, leading to a lower concentration inside the membrane and thus a smaller advection flux. However, free from electrostatic interactions, NH3 maintains a sustained concentration gradient across the membrane from the feed side to the permeate side, resulting in a high diffusion flux which dominates NH3 overall transport.

MD simulation reveals the different transport mechanism

The potential of mean force (PMF) of moving one NH3 or NH4+ across the polyamide RO membranes is evaluated to determine the energetic barriers. The PMFs determine the intrinsic energy barriers at low driving force (concentration gradient and/or pressure) and low concentration, where molecular transport occurs through a quasi-static process (no irreversibility). The commercial polyamide membrane is modeled as a crosslinked membrane composed of m-phenylenediamine (MPD) and trimesoyl chloride (TMC) with a 90% degree of crosslinking38. The experimental charge densities of the membrane associated with the pH of 5.5 (−48 mM) and 10 (−402 mM) are enforced (Eq. 10), which produce a surface charge on the polyamide membrane (Supplementary Fig. 10). The MD simulations provide a good qualitative comparison with the experimental results and validate our numerical models. However, several limitations arise in capturing the experimental conditions. As experimental rejection of molecules approaches 100%, standard nonequilibrium simulations become ineffective at identifying the rejection mechanisms. In such cases, enhanced sampling simulations under equilibrium conditions40, as used in this work, are used to determine intrinsic energy barriers for molecular transport. Such equilibrium simulations cannot capture the inherent nonequilibrium effects41, such as concentration or pressure gradients, concentration polarization, membrane compaction, and energy dissipation (e.g., friction). Therefore, the simulated energy barriers shown in this work describe a quasi-static transport process under low driving force (pressure or concentration).

Model validation using complex feed matrices

The APT model effectively describes the transport of water and NH3/NH4+ across the RO membrane and accurately captures the impacts of operating parameters, including pressure, feed concentration, and feed pH. Notably, all these model curves are generated using a single set of physicochemical parameters, with fitting achieved only through the solute partitioning coefficient, Φi, and the water-membrane friction coefficient, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{{{{\rm{f}}}}-{{{\rm{m}}}}}$$\end{document}ff−m. The consistency between model predictions and experimental results highlights the reliability and applicability of the APT model.

To further validate its robustness, we evaluated its performance with solutions containing more competing ions. When NaCl was added (Supplementary Fig. 6), the model prediction remained stable and accurate in the presence of 10.7 and 21.4 mM additional Na+ ions. For the real samples collected from the manure treatment plant, the 1st pass RO permeate was relatively clean, containing moderate levels of TAN (176.7 mg L−1), and small amounts of Na+, K+, and SO42− (7.3, 78.6, and 38.8 mg L−1, respectively) (Supplementary Table 3). Since the TAN concentration exceeds the discharge standard, a second RO pass is typically required. The simple composition and absence of organic matter in it allow us to validate the model directly using this real wastewater. Experimental results with this real wastewater are consistent with our observations using synthetic wastewater. Similarly, the RO membrane exhibits optimal TAN rejection around pH 7, with rejection decreasing at both higher and lower pH values (Fig. 5b), consistent with our previous observations and discussion. The predicted optimal point is at pH 7.6 with the highest TAN rejection of 88.3%. The presence of K+ and SO42− at relatively low concentration levels does not significantly affect TAN transport. Similarly, the transport flux from NH4+ gradually decreases with increasing pH, while NH3 flux increases sharply above pH 8, leading to an increase in TAN flux (Fig. 5c). The flux from NH4+ at pH 6.2 was 9.01 mmol m−2 h−1, while that from NH3 was 0.0503 mmol m−2 h−1. These flux contributions are reversed at pH 9.7 as that from NH4+ decreases to 0.427 mmol m−2 h−1 while NH3 increases to 41.7 mmol m−2 h−1. Model predictions generally matched the experimental results, with only a slight underestimation of TAN rejection under mildly acidic conditions, which is likely due to minor unmeasured cations present in authentic wastewater that were not included in the model, leading to a slight overestimation of Cl⁻ partitioning (Supplementary Fig. 12). Implications and outlook

We developed the APT model based on Maxwell–Stefan theory and the extended Nernst–Planck equation to describe the transport behaviors of NH3/NH4+ across the RO membrane. The APT model considers solute partitioning at the membrane–water interface, along with advection, diffusion, and electro-migration transport within the membrane. Furthermore, we included NH3/NH4+ partitioning changes and membrane charge variations due to pH changes within the model framework, which is crucial for addressing the limitations of the traditional solution-diffusion model in adequately describing the transport of amphoteric ions.

Our APT model was validated by experimental results and demonstrated good applicability with high competing-ion concentration solution and real wastewater. TAN permeability was found to be associated with operating pressure, feed concentration, and pH, with the strongest dependency on pH. By evaluating the PMF for transporting a single NH3 or NH4⁺ molecule across RO, we further confirm that the distinct transport behaviors of NH3 and NH4⁺ in RO membranes primarily arise from the electrostatic attraction experienced by NH4⁺, resulting in a higher energy barrier. The theoretical model indicates that lowering the pH below 8 to ensure TAN exists as NH4+ rather than NH3 is an effective way to enhance TAN retention. However, it is important to avoid excessive acidity, which could reduce membrane charge. From this perspective, an RO membrane with a lower isoelectric point could achieve effective TAN retention at a lower pH. In our study, considering the trade-off between membrane charge and NH3 dissociation equilibrium, pH 7 emerged as the optimal point (with 89.1 ± 3.2% rejection), maximizing permeate water quality, though multi-pass treatment is still needed for complete TAN removal. The concentrated RO retentate with higher TAN levels can more economically recover ammonia for reuse, effectively helping to reduce energy consumption from manure treatment and the Haber–Bosch process.

The APT model presents an effective framework for describing the transport and rejection of reactive amphoteric ions in RO processes and could be potentially incorporated into design software for RO-based water treatment facilities in the future. Due to the complexity of the RO membrane’s microscopic structure, current modeling approaches often treat it as a black box. The APT model essentially opens a window into this black box, describing the physical migration of water and solutes. A few parameters in the model were determined by fitting experimental data, as it remains challenging to precisely determine solute dimensions and membrane structure, particularly in terms of ion hydration and partial dehydration during partitioning. We note that they may not be globally optimal or unique. A key challenge is the strong interdependence among the parameters in our coupled equations, particularly between counter-ion NH4⁺ and co-ion Cl⁻ (Supplementary Fig. 11). While the combined effect of them is well constrained by the model, their individual contributions are hard to uniquely deconvolute. We also note that the experimental dataset is limited relative to the number of fitting parameters, and parameter, such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varPhi }_{{{{{\rm{NH}}}}}_{3}}$$\end{document}ΦNH3 becomes influential only at high pH values. More densely collected experimental data in the future would help to provide sufficient constraints to simultaneously lock in the precise values of all parameters.

Currently, the model was developed and validated using relatively clean streams, and it demonstrates strong robustness under high concentrations of competing mono- and even di-valent ions. However, it remains unable to account for other complex constituents that cannot be described by the Nernst–Planck framework. In real-world applications, the ammonia-rich streams often contain complex components, such as organic matter (Supplementary Table 3). Even UF-treated water may still contain diverse organic species for which it is impossible to formulate individual transport equations. For specific feeds, appropriate simplifications need to be carefully considered to ensure they do not compromise predictive performance. Complex feeds also present long-term operational challenges, including membrane fouling, scaling, compaction, and mechanical damage. With sufficient observational data, such effects might be incorporated indirectly by adjusting model parameters, such as membrane porosity (ε) or membrane charge density (X). However, the focus of the APT model in this work is to reveal the fundamental microscopic transport mechanisms, and not to address complex macroscopic engineering challenges. Future work may build on the insights provided by the APT framework to develop higher-level process models capable of describing the engineering-scale impacts of such operational factors. Advances in molecular simulations and artificial intelligence may, in the future, help us better understand and address complex interactions, thereby validating the applicability of ATP towards high-strength feed streams.

Methods

Model development

The commercial RO membranes typically use the thin-film composite polyamide membranes produced through interfacial polymerization42. The model in this study describes the membrane layer as a polymer network with continuous, water-filled sub-nanometer channels through which clusters of water molecules and solutes can move24. The support layer also has a similar polymer network, but with much larger voids, and thus, its effect on water and solute transport can be neglected during model development, allowing us to focus only on the active layer of the membrane. In the polymer matrix of the membrane, water exists in a liquid phase, while solutes are dispersed in the water and transported across the membrane through the aqueous phase.

Previous modeling strategies for the transmembrane transport of amphoteric ions were commonly based on the assumption of infinitely fast acid–base equilibrations43, treating amphoteric ions as a group and establishing their relationship through dissociation equilibrium at every local point inside the membrane28. However, such method cannot describe the transport mechanism of NH3 as its concentration was calculated based on the transport of NH4+ and local pH instead of its own governing equation. To address this, we established individual transport equations for both NH4+ and NH3 according to their respective transport mechanisms (Eqs. 1.1–2.2). For simplification, we did not set up transport equations for the proton or hydroxyl ion since both of them are in very low concentrations within the pH range we care about43, otherwise their involvement would introduce more equations than unknowns in the coupled system, leading to conflicts of solving. The permeate pH is determined by the re-established dissociation equilibrium between the transported NH3 and NH4+. In studies modeling ion exchange membranes, membrane thickness (100 μm) was commonly discretized into tens of grids43,45, and theoretically, equilibrium can be reestablished after each grid to improve accuracy. However, the active layer of the RO membrane is already extremely thin (100–150 nm)37, much thinner than a single discretization layer used in ion-exchange membrane models. So, we reasonably assume the membrane active layer is sufficiently thin and that solute transport is infinitely fast, to reduce the computational complexity.

Due to the limited pore size of the membrane matrix network (average 0.76 nm)46 and its inherent charge, solutes experience steric hindrance and electrochemical potential effects. During transmembrane transport, solutes must first undergo concentration partitioning at the feed-side membrane–water interface before entering the membrane and then partition again at the permeate-side interface before entering the permeate (Eqs. 1.1 and 1.2). Equation solving and parameter fitting procedure

The model is solved analytically following a structured two-loop procedure. The outer loop iterates over experimental conditions investigated in this study, including feed pH, solute concentration, or permeate flux. Under each fixed condition, an inner loop determines whether the concentration polarization condition (Eq. 6) is satisfied. At the beginning of each iteration, the solute concentration at the membrane surface is assumed equal to that in the feed (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{{{{\rm{s}}}}}={c}_{{{{\rm{f}}}}}$$\end{document}cs=cf, no polarization).

At the feed-side membrane–water interface, the Donnan-steric partition equations (Eqs. 1.1 and 1.2), together with the electroneutrality condition inside the membrane (Eqs. 8 and 10), form a solvable system that yields the solute concentrations and the dimensionless potential difference (∆φ) at the interface. Within the membrane, transport equations (Eqs. 2.1 and 2.2) are constructed, which, together with Eqs. 7, 8, and 10, form another solvable system. This system yields the concentrations and potential difference at the permeate-side interior of the membrane. At the permeate-side membrane–water interface, the Donnan-steric equilibrium conditions (Eqs. 1.1 and 1.2), coupled with Eqs. 8–10, provide analytical solutions for the permeate concentrations of all solutes.

The resulting permeate concentrations (cp) are then substituted back into Eq. 6 to compute the membrane surface concentrations that satisfy the concentration polarization condition. If the updated values differ from the initial guess, the membrane-surface concentrations are updated by averaging the new and old values; the process iterates until concentrations at the feed, membrane surface, and permeate simultaneously satisfy Eq. 6. At convergence, the solution set satisfies all coupled relationships, including concentration polarization, Donnan and steric partitioning, and Nernst–Planck transport. Because NH3 and NH4⁺ exhibit different permeabilities, they must reestablish chemical equilibrium via Eq. 5, which determines the permeate pH. After obtaining solute concentrations and fluxes, the pressure drop across the membrane is determined through Eqs. 3, 13, and 14.

Parameter fitting was performed by minimizing the discrepancies between measured and calculated values. The partitioning coefficients were fitted by minimizing the sum of squared errors between measured and predicted TAN permeability coefficient and the rejection, with a weighing factor to adjust their magnitude to be the same level, while the fluid–membrane friction parameter was fitted using water permeability measurements. Optimization was conducted in MATLAB using the ‘fminsearch’ function (Nelder–Mead algorithm). To enhance convergence and reduce the likelihood of local minima, the partitioning coefficients were constrained to the literature-based range of 0–0.224,28, and the friction parameter was searched within a logarithmic range of 1013–1017. A multi-start strategy (n = 4) was applied to further reduce the risk of local minima. Minor manual adjustments were applied to ensure a consistent parameter set across multiple operating conditions (e.g., varying feed pH, operating pressures, and feed concentrations; Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). A complete list of parameter values and their origins (literature-based, experimentally determined, or fitted) is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Chemicals and materials

Lab-scale RO experiments were conducted using both synthetic NH4Cl solution and real anaerobic effluent from a dairy farm. The synthetic NH4Cl solution was prepared by dissolving reagent-grade ammonium chloride (Laboratory Grade, Ward’s Science, Rochester, NY) in deionized water. A 1 M NaOH solution and a 1 M HCl solution were used to adjust the pH of the RO feed. The real wastewater was collected from a dairy farm located in northern Wisconsin (44°39’37.6”N, 87°35’15.1”W), where the manure wastewater had been anaerobically digested and processed by a series of membrane treatments. The wastewater was from the effluent of UF and 1st pass RO, respectively, which served as the feed for the following 1st pass and 2nd pass RO, respectively. Detailed chemical information about the water is provided in Supplementary Table 3. The membrane used in the RO setup was a commercial polyamide thin-film composite RO membrane (BW30, Dow Filmtec), purchased from Sterlitech Corporation (Auburn, WA), designed for brackish water desalination with low pressure requirements. The received membranes were stored in the dark at 4 °C.

RO setup and experiments

A bench-scale crossflow RO system (Innovator CF016, Sterlitech Corporation, Auburn, WA) was employed to carry out the RO experiment. The system featured a rectangular plate-and-frame feed flow channel with dimensions of 5.3 cm in length, 3.9 cm in width, and 0.23 cm in depth, giving it an active membrane area of 20.6 cm2. A porous stainless-steel frit provided mechanical support on the permeate side.

All experiments were conducted at 25 ± 1 °C, with a crossflow velocity of 6.2 mm s−1 at the membrane surface. The retentate was recirculated back to the feed tank, and experiments were conducted across a range of operating pressures from 5 to 20 bar, feed TAN concentrations from 50 to 1270 mg N/L, and feed pH values from 5.5 to 10.5 to determine TAN rejection rates and permeate flux. The feed volume for each experimental condition was 2 L. Newly installed membranes in the RO system were first equilibrated with deionized water for 5 h at the experimental operating pressure. Subsequently, the membranes were equilibrated with the feed solution under experimental conditions for 30 min before permeate samples were collected. The permeate reservoir was placed on an electronic balance (Scout SPX2201, OHAUS, Pine Brook, NJ) to continuously monitor permeate flux by tracking weight changes. The volume of permeate collected during the experiment did not exceed 2% of the total feed volume, allowing us to assume that the composition of the solution in the feed tank remained unchanged. The TAN, chemical oxygen demand, and total nitrogen were measured using the Nitrogen-Ammonia Reagent Set HR, the COD Digestion Vials HR, and the Total Nitrogen Reagent Set HR (Hach, Loveland, CO), respectively, with a spectrophotometer (DR900, Hach, Loveland, CO). The individual concentrations of NH3 and NH4+ were calculated using Eq. 1. The cation composition of the real wastewater was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (5800 VDV ICP-OES, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), while the anion composition was measured using ion chromatography (IC, Thermo Dionex 2100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Repeated experiments were conducted with different membrane samples cut from the same sheet under identical experimental conditions to minimize errors due to membrane variability.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Membrane preparation

Reverse-osmosis polyamide membrane with dimensions of 4.7 × 4.7 × 9 nm3 was prepared with MPD and TMC as described in our previous work53. Monomers MPD and TMC were placed randomly inside a box, maintaining a target density of 1.3 g cm–3 and MPD:TMC ratio of 1.5. The initial system was energy-minimized using the steepest-descent method, followed by crosslinking reactions at 300 K using a canonical ensemble. During crosslinking, reactive sites within a cutoff distance of 0.45 nm were pulled towards each other using virtual elastic springs. As the distance between reactive sites decreased below 0.15 nm, the topologies were updated by adding amide bonds and removing appropriate H and Cl atoms. The crosslinking steps were stopped after the degree of crosslinking reached 90%. The unreacted monomers were removed, and TMC residues were deprotonated randomly54 to achieve a charge density of –48 mM (8 TMCs) and –402 mM (66 TMCs) for pH 5.5 and 10, respectively. It was ensured that an equal number of TMCs were charged on either side of the membrane. Additionally, TMCs with one bound MPD were deprotonated before deprotonating TMCs with two bound MPDs.

Potential of mean force

The simulations were performed following the protocol below54. The polyamide membrane was placed at the center of a simulation box of 4.7 × 4.7 × 18 nm3, and NH3 or NH4+ was placed at (2.35, 2.35, and 0) nm. Thereafter, the system was filled with TIP3P water, and sodium ions were added to neutralize the system. The SwissParam forcefield55 was applied to the polyamide membrane, whereas water, ions, NH3, and NH4+ were modeled using the CHARMM36 force field. The accuracy of the SwissParam forcefield has been validated for neutral MPD-TMC membranes with TIP3P water in previous work38. The system was equilibrated sequentially using steepest-descent, canonical ensemble (NVT) for 50 ps, and isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) for 20 ns. The temperature was maintained at 300 K using stochastic velocity rescaling56. The pressure was maintained at 1 bar using the Berendsen barostat57 with compressibility of 4.5 × 10–5 bar–1 along the z-axis. The box dimensions along the x and y directions were kept fixed. The initial configurations for umbrella sampling were generated by pulling NH3 or NH4+ across the membrane using a force constant of 100 kJ mol–1 nm–2 and a pulling rate of 10−3 nm ps–1. The displacement vector between the center of mass of the membrane and NH3 or NH4+ along the z-axis was chosen as the reaction coordinate. A total of 141 umbrella sampling windows were chosen for the reaction coordinate, ranging between ±7 nm. Each umbrella sampling simulation underwent simulated annealing (20 ns), NPT equilibration (5 ns), and NPT production of 80 ns. The production simulations were performed with an umbrella force constant of 200 kJ mol–1 nm–2, Nose-Hoover thermostat58, and Parrinello–Rahman59 barostat. The PMF profiles were calculated with the weighted histogram analysis method using the periodic (along z-axis) assumption59. All simulations were performed using GROMACS 202260.
