Comparative transcriptome analysis to reveal key drought stress-responsive genes in sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench)
Authors: Dona Vihara Sugandhika Kaluthanthri, Suriya Arachchige Chandrika Nishanthi Perera, Pinumkarage Nilanthie Dasanayaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlowering pathways are accelerated for the rapid production of flowers and seeds in response to drought in certain varieties of sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench). The objective of the present study was to identify potential drought-responsive genes that affect flowering time in sorghum under drought stress. Sorghum germplasm accessions representing early-, intermediate-, and late-flowering groups were selected, and drought stress was applied to 25-day-old seedlings in the drought-stressed (DS) group by withdrawing water, while those in the control (WW) group were well watered. At anthesis stage, flag leaf tissues were harvested, and total RNA was separately isolated from the samples. Transcription profiles consisting of 60 base pairs and paired-end reads from the total RNA of each sample were explored via the Illumina Genome Analyser deep sequencing method. An average of 66,059,932 clean reads were mapped. Among the 10,468 DEGs, 126 genes were upregulated, and 61 genes were downregulated in all comparisons. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the de novo purine biosynthesis and lipoate biosynthesis pathways and the Wnt signalling pathway affect sorghum genes that are differentially expressed in response to drought. Transcriptome level differences among the early-, intermediate- and late-flowering groups of sorghum under WW and DS conditions were explored efficiently in the present study via RNA sequence analysis tools. The functional characterization of the DEGs and pathways identified here could reveal a sophisticated and highly coordinated survival strategy for sorghum under drought. Candidate genes and pathways that might be used to improve drought tolerance in sorghum were identified. The findings of the present study could lead to new targets for enhancing drought stress tolerance in sorghum and other crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13145-4.Keywords: Abiotic stress, Drought tolerance, Gene expression, Germplasm, Sorghum, Transcriptome analysis

Background

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench) is the fifth most important grain crop worldwide in terms of production and cultivated area. Sorghum genetic resources display a gradient of phenotypes varying from susceptibility to tolerance in response to drought. Drought stress is unpredictable and may occur during different stages of sorghum growth and development, including the early seedling vegetative stage, panicle development (preflowering stage), postflowering stage, period of grain filling and physiologically mature stage [1]. According to Kebede et al. [2], drought at the preflowering (GS2) and postflowering (GS3) stages is more severe and leads to greater undesirable effects on yield than drought at earlier stages of growth (GS1). The acceleration of flowering pathways for the rapid production of flowers and seeds has been reported in response to drought stress in certain crop species and cultivars/accessions. Understanding the relationship between drought and flowering in sorghum is vitally important, especially in view of the significant variation in flowering time among different germplasm accessions of sorghum. Moreover, identifying the mechanisms affecting flowering time in response to drought is crucial for improving the understanding of the relationships between drought and yield. Advances in novel technologies and bioinformatic tools facilitate the analysis of transcriptomes, characterization of drought-responsive transcripts, etc., which are important research gaps that should be addressed in revealing responsive mechanisms to drought. Owing to the high genetic variability among sorghum genotypes and relatively small genome size, sorghum is an appropriate model for the identification of drought-related genes and genomic regions [3, 4]. Drought stress tolerance strategies in drought-sensitive and drought-tolerant sorghum genotypes have been revealed via RNA sequencing by Fracasso et al. [5]. A study on the use of microarrays to reveal transcriptome variations in sorghum in response to combined drought and heat stresses was reported by Johnson et al. [6]. Moreover, several attempts have been made to map quantitative trait loci (QTLs) via recombinant inbred lines (RILs) to increase sorghum varietal development for production under drought stress [7–9]. In addition to drought, attempts have been made to reveal transcriptional level changes under salt stress conditions in sorghum plants [10].

In the present study, three sorghum germplasm accessions with different flowering patterns (i.e., early, intermediate and late flowering) were selected. There was a slight acceleration of flowering pathways for rapid production of flowers under drought-stressed conditions in the plants of the late-flowering group, whereas the plants of the other two flowering groups showed no such acceleration. The objective of this study was to identify potential drought-responsive genes in different sorghum varieties with distinct flowering patterns. In this study, RNA sequencing technology was used to reveal transcription level variations in different sorghum germplasm accessions under drought-stressed and well-watered conditions. The findings of the present study will help elucidate the drought tolerance mechanisms and mechanisms that affect flowering time in sorghum in response to drought stress. Therefore, screening available sorghum varieties for features of drought-induced flowering enhancement and the development of novel varieties with increased flowering production under drought conditions could be facilitated.

Methods

Sorghum plats representing early-, intermediate-, and late-flowering groups (‘FRANCE 6005’, ‘KURUNEGALA 421’ and ‘MONARAGALA 93’) (Fig. 1) were subjected to two treatments: well-watered (WW) and drought-stressed (DS) conditions. Drought stress was applied to 25-day-old seedlings from the DS group by withdrawing water, while the control plants were well watered. The WW pots were maintained at a water potential > − 0.2 MPa (15–20% soil water content) with irrigation, whereas the DS pots were maintained at < − 1.5 MPa (5–8% soil water content). A total of 180 biological replicates (30 plants per group) were maintained in a net house under a controlled environment following a completely randomized block design. Each group consisted of three blocks, with ten plants per block. A comprehensive set of morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits was evaluated for each group. Flag leaf tissues were harvested from three different plants in each group at the pollen shedding stage. Due to drought-induced variation in flowering time, sample collection occurred on different days for different groups. All harvested leaves were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Studied groups of sorghum germplasm accessions in the present study (A - early [‘FRANCE 6005’], B - intermediate [‘KURUNEGALA 421’], and C - late [‘MONARAGALA 93’] flowering groups)

Total RNA was separately isolated from frozen flag leaf samples via the NucleoSpin® RNA Plant Kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL). RNA quality and integrity were assessed via a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega) prior to RNA sequencing. Library preparation was performed via a TrueSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit. The RNA was sequenced via a HiSeq 2500 (Illumina) sequencer following HCS 2.2.70 sequencing control software.

Transcription profiles consisting of 60 base pairs and paired-end reads from total RNA of early, intermediate and late flowering groups under well-watered (WW) and drought-stressed (DS) conditions were explored with the Illumina Genome Analyser deep sequencing platform. Quality control of the sequenced raw reads was performed via FastQC, and the Trimmomatic program was used to remove adapter sequences and bases with a base quality lower than three from the ends. Bases of reads that did not qualify for a window size of 4 and a mean quality of 15 were trimmed via the sliding window method. Reads with lengths shorter than 36 bp were dropped to produce trimmed data. Trimmed reads were mapped to the reference genome (GCF_000003195.3) with HISAT2. The software Stringtie was used for transcript assembly. The expression profile was calculated for each sample, and transcripts/genes were used as read counts, FPKMs (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) and TPMs (transcripts per kilobase million). Differential expression analysis was performed via Ballgown and edgeR. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analysed in 3 tests vs. control comparisons (late DS-WW, intermediate DS-WW and early DS-WW). If more than one count value was 0, such samples were not included in the analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified at the thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and log2 of fold change [FC] ≥ 2 in the comparison of sorghum germplasm accessions under well-watered and drought-stressed conditions.

To characterize the drought-responsive genes, the genes identified in the comparison of sorghum germplasm accessions under well-watered and drought-stressed conditions were further analysed for gene ontology (GO) enrichment and pathway enrichment using KEGG pathway analysis and the PANTHER classification system [11].

Total RNA isolated from three different biological replicates of the well-watered (WW) and drought-stressed (DS) groups of different flowering groups was validated using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) (Supplementary document 1). Relative changes in gene expression were calculated using the comparative CT (ΔΔCT) method, with sorghum genes; Eukaryotic initiation factor 4 A/ EIF4α (XM_002451491), Peptidylprolyl isomerase/ CYP (XM_002453800.2), and Serine/threonine-protein phosphatase/ PP2A (XM_002453490) serving as endogenous reference genes for normalization [12]. The expression levels of the selected genes were analysed under both WW and DS conditions, with three independent biological replicates per sample. The CT values of target genes were normalized against the geometric mean of multiple reference genes.

Results

Morphological, physiological and biochemical assays

Six different groups of sorghum plants were studied to characterize the drought-responsive transcripts in the present study. The mean and standard deviation values of the studied parameters are presented in Table 1 for each flowering group under both control and drought-stressed conditions. Flowering time increased progressively from early to late germplasm, with significant drought delaying flowering in intermediate groups (+ 10.5 d). interestingly, drought reduced height in early and late groups but unexpectedly increased it in the intermediate group (to 175.9 cm). Root growth showed strong drought-induced plasticity, with root length increasing most dramatically in late-flowering plants under drought, while root width expanded under drought in early and late groups but narrowed in the intermediate group. Leaf traits varied significantly, as flag leaf dimensions were largest in early well-watered and intermediate drought-stressed plants but severely reduced in early drought-stressed plants. Regarding reproductive architecture, early-flowering plants produced substantially more panicles and flowering stems compared to other groups, and inflorescence length increased with earlier flowering and under drought, whereas inflorescence width was maximal in early well-watered plants and minimal under early drought. Zinc partitioning exhibited distinct tissue-specific patterns: root Zn was highest in early and late well-watered plants and severely reduced by drought; leaf Zn peaked in late drought-stressed plants; pollen Zn was highest in late-flowering plants regardless of water status and lowest in early drought plants; and stem Zn was exceptionally high in early well-watered plants, but was almost entirely abolished by drought.

In here, flowering time governs sorghum’s drought responses. Early-flowering plants maximize tillering and stem zinc storage but suffer severe pollen zinc loss and stem zinc depletion under drought. Intermediate-flowering plants maintain pollen zinc and flag leaf size, showing effective stress compensation. Late-flowering plants prioritize survival extreme root elongation, higher stomatal density, and leaf zinc allocation, but have low tillering. Early drought eliminates grain zinc reserves; late drought protects reproductive zinc at the expense of root uptake.

Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the loading plot of the first two principal components grouped the studied traits into four distinct clusters (Fig. 2). The scatter plot based on the first two principal components clearly separated samples according to WW and DS conditions within early, intermediate, and late-flowering groups (Fig. 3). Together, the PCA loading and scatter plots demonstrated that early and late-flowering groups exhibited clear differences in the expression of the studied traits under WW and DS conditions. The intermediate flowering group also showed some variation between the WW and DS conditions, although the differences were less pronounced than those in the early and late groups.

Reference mapping, assembly results and analysis of differentially expressed genes

After high-quality filtered reads were mapped onto the sorghum reference genome (GCF_000003195.3), an average of 66,059,932 clean reads were mapped (Table 2). The number of mapped reads per sample ranged between 55,194,377 and 72,689,068. The reported average values of the percentage of GCs, percentage of bases with phred quality scores greater than 20 and percentage of bases with phred quality scores greater than 30 were 52.55%, 98.84% and 96.03%, respectively.

Transcripts were additionally assembled from the results of the mapped reads to predict novel transcripts and novel alternative splicing transcripts without the StringTie-e option. Intergenic transcripts were predicted by reads that were not mapped to known exons or genes but rather to the intergenic region, and a total of 3120 novel transcripts were recorded across all groups, with varying representation among them. Novel alternative splicing transcripts are transcripts that are mapped to new exons or have different assembled structures from known transcripts. A total of 40 975 transcripts were identified as novel alternatively spliced transcripts across the groups, with each group contributing a unique set.

Differential expression analysis identified a total of 10,468 genes that were differentially expressed (DEGs) in response to drought stress across the three flowering groups. Among these, a core set of 187 genes showed a conserved response, with 126 consistently upregulated and 61 consistently downregulated in all group comparisons. These likely represent fundamental components of the universal sorghum drought stress response. The vast majority of DEGs, however, were unique and comparison-specific, highlighting divergent transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 4).

Counts of up- and downregulated DEGs and unique DEGs in test (DS) vs. control (WW) comparisons (fold change ≥ 2 and p value ≤ 0.05)

Furthermore, differential expression analysis revealed statistically significant expressions of putative genes related to variation in flowering time in different flowering groups of sorghum under drought-stressed conditions. Cryptochrome-1-like was upregulated in the late-flowering group (cry1 - Gene ID: 110435145) but downregulated in the intermediate-flowering group (cry 2 - Gene ID: 110436666). In the early-flowering group, the DELLA protein Dwarf8 (Gene ID: 8062929) was downregulated. This differential regulation provides a molecular basis for the observed divergence in flowering phenology under drought. Further analysis revealed group-specific expression patterns for genes associated with the contrasting physiological traits measured (Table 4).

GO enrichment of drought-responsive genes in different experimental groups

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed to categorize the differentially expressed genes. DEG enrichment analyses revealed significant overrepresentation in several functional categories (Table 5). In the Cellular Component domain, DEGs were highly enriched in membrane-related structures, including the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, endomembrane system, and organelle membranes (fold enrichment up to 232.3). For Molecular Function, genes associated with glycosyltransferase, monooxygenase, and oxidoreductase activities showed strong enrichment (fold enrichment 8.3–116.2), highlighting their roles in enzymatic and metabolic processes. In the Biological Process category, DEGs were predominantly involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis, particularly cyanogenic glycoside and glycoside metabolic processes, as well as general biosynthetic and metabolic pathways (fold enrichment up to 232.3). These results indicate that the differentially expressed genes are largely associated with membrane functions, enzymatic activities, and secondary metabolism.

The network of upregulated DEGs (Fig. 5) was dominated by biosynthetic processes, with general terms such as “biosynthetic process” and “organic substance biosynthetic process” encompassing overall cellular molecule production, and more specific terms like “cellular nitrogen compound biosynthetic process” highlighting protein, nucleic acid, and amino acid synthesis. These processes are supported by catalytic and metal ion binding activities, indicating enzyme-driven and cofactor-dependent regulation. The genes were associated with membrane-bound organelles, including the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, nucleus, mitochondria, and chloroplasts, reflecting compartmentalized biosynthesis. Overall, the network emphasized the integration of nitrogenous compound production with broader metabolic pathways, such as carbohydrate-derived nucleotide synthesis.

The network of downregulated genes under drought stress (Fig. 6) revealed a coordinated cellular response aimed at conserving energy by suppressing growth, development, and major metabolic activities. Biological processes such as auxin transport, transcription factor mediated development, pyruvate metabolism, GPI-anchor and carbohydrate biosynthesis, and transmembrane transport were downregulated, indicating reduced growth, respiration, and biosynthetic activity. At the molecular level, catalytic and binding activities including glycosyl transferase, peptidase, calcium ion, protein, and DNA binding as well as transporter functions were decreased. The cellular components affected included the chloroplast, Golgi apparatus, endosomes, and membranes, reflecting a widespread slowdown in photosynthesis, protein processing, and vesicular trafficking. Overall, these results suggest that drought stress triggers a coordinated transcriptional shutdown, minimizing energy expenditure while maintaining essential survival functions.

Furthermore, Panther classification revealed that drought-responsive DEGs in sorghum affected two biosynthetic pathways: de novo purine biosynthesis and lipoate biosynthesis as well as the Wnt signalling pathway, indicating targeted disruptions in nucleotide metabolism, cofactor synthesis, and key regulatory signalling processes under stress.

Identification of functional gene products related to drought resistance

Protein domain analysis of DEGs in the test (DS) vs. control (WW) comparisons revealed several functionally relevant domain enrichments across the flowering groups (Fig. 7).

Protein domains of differentially expressed genes in test (DS) vs. control (WW) comparisons

The late-flowering group was enriched for domains associated with protein turnover and transport regulation, including RING-type zinc fingers, mitochondrial carrier proteins, BAR domains, and MFS transporters, indicating active protein recycling, metabolic adjustment, and membrane remodelling under drought. The intermediate-flowering group showed a distinctive enrichment of pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins, reflecting a strong emphasis on post-transcriptional regulation and organellar RNA processing. The presence of RING zinc fingers alongside PPRs suggests a strategy that prioritizes fine-tuning organellar gene expression rather than broad transcriptional reprogramming. The early-flowering group was enriched for domains linked to photosynthetic adjustment and stress signaling, including chlorophyll a and b binding proteins, Rieske [2Fe–2 S] components, kinases, and hydrolases. These enrichments indicate targeted modulation of photosynthesis and active signaling reconfiguration, which is consistent with an early, proactive drought-response strategy.

Discussion

The development of high-throughput sequencing technologies has revolutionized the study of plant stress biology, providing powerful tools to uncover molecular mechanisms and map genome-wide expression patterns across crops, including sorghum [5, 6, 13–18). Exposure to drought triggers a complex hierarchy of responses governed by a multitude of genes and pathways [5]. In this study, we employed RNA-Seq analysis to evaluate these adaptive mechanisms in three sorghum germplasm accessions subjected to a uniform drought stress. In here, RNA was extracted from flag leaves collected when pollen shedding began. The leaf is both a photosynthetic and signaling hub, where the whole-plant decision to flower, especially under stress like drought is calculated and communicated. In sorghum, stressed plants may not progress from the pre-anthesis stage to full flowering. Pollen shedding is a definitive marker of reproductive maturity. Sampling at this precise stage, rather than by chronological age ensures plants are compared at equivalent development. Because plants reached this stage on different days, comparing their gene expression at their respective “pollen shed day” reveals the genetic basis for their differing flowering speeds.

Days to flowering was identified as the primary trait influencing the grouping of germplasm accessions for evaluating drought responses in the present study. Drought stress affected flowering behavior across all groups, suggesting that this trait is highly sensitive to water availability. While a drought escape (DE) response accelerated flowering to complete the life cycle, is indeed a common and well-documented strategy, it is not universal. The literature also supports the existence of a “drought avoidance” or “stress tolerance” strategy [19], where flowering is delayed allowing for continued resource accumulation, root growth, and physiological acclimation until conditions improve. Our data reveal that sorghum germplasm harbors natural variation for these contrasting strategies, which are underpinned by distinct molecular programs, as evidenced by our transcriptome data.

Notably, plants in the late-flowering group exhibited a non-significant slight acceleration of flowering under drought conditions, possibly as an adaptive mechanism to ensure reproductive success before the onset of severe water limitation. The late group executes classical drought escape. Its transcriptome is reprogrammed to promote flowering. This is evidenced by the downregulation of key flowering repressors (Phytochrome B-like, DOF4.6, PHD-finger repressor) and the upregulation of floral integrators/promoters (bZIP44-like, SPL4, LAF1, NF-YC6). This molecular shift drives the observed maintenance of flowering time despite severe stress. In contrast, the early- and intermediate-flowering groups did not exhibit such acceleration, indicating group-specific variation in the adaptive strategies. Contrary to an escape strategy, their transcriptomes under drought are dominated by the upregulation of abiotic stress-responsive genes (e.g., peroxidases, heat shock proteins, LEA proteins) and do not show the coordinated downregulation of floral repressors seen in the Late group. The molecular “brakes” on flowering remain engaged. These genotypes prioritize survival and acclimation over a rushed reproductive cycle. They invest in structures for long-term stress endurance (e.g., the massively enlarged flag leaf in the Intermediate group) at the cost of a delayed reproductive transition.

At the molecular level, these strategies are reflected in opposing regulation of key flowering pathway genes. The late group’s escape response is coordinated by downregulating floral repressors (PhyB, DOF4.6) and upregulating promoters (Cry1, SPL4), effectively accelerating development. Conversely, the intermediate group’s delay is associated with the downregulation of a different cryptochrome (Cry2) and sustained expression of repressors, maintaining the vegetative state. Cryptochromes 1 like genes (cry1 and cry2) in sorghum are blue/UV-A light receptors that regulate photomorphogenesis, flowering, and stress responses [20]. In sorghum, a short-day crop, cryptochromes play a central role in photoperiodic flowering control, influencing biomass and environmental adaptation. cry1 promotes flowering and shows circadian-regulated expression in leaves whereas cry2, can rescue late-flowering, highlighting its strong regulatory role. The early group’s strategy appears enabled by releasing growth repression (DELLA downregulation). The DELLA protein dwarf8 gene encodes a DELLA protein that acts as a negative regulator of gibberellin (GA) signaling, thereby reducing stem elongation and slowing the transition to flowering [21]. This demonstrates that variation in drought response is orchestrated.

The observed reduction in plant height and leaf area in early- and late-flowering groups aligns with classic growth inhibition under turgor loss [22], whereas the increased height and enlarged flag leaf in the intermediate group suggest a compensatory, avoidance-oriented investment in photosynthetic capacity [23, 24]. This resembles the “stay-green” strategy but occurs pre-florally, indicating that stress timing relative to developmental transition shapes resource allocation. Root elongation across all groups [25] and increased stomatal density in early and late groups reflect common and group-specific adjustments in water foraging and gas exchange. The intermediate group’s reduced root width, however, may indicate a trade-off favoring aboveground investment. A notable finding is the reconfiguration of zinc partitioning under drought. The increased mobilization of Zn to leaves and pollen, particularly in the late group, suggests an active nutrient management strategy to support photosynthetic and reproductive functions during resource-limited reproduction. This goes beyond passive ion movement and points to a role for Zn homeostasis in drought resilience, potentially as a cofactor for antioxidant enzymes or stress-signaling proteins [26–28]. Collectively, these findings highlight that studied Sorghum bicolor germplasm accessions exhibit diverse adaptive responses to drought, with flowering time strongly influencing physiological and morphological adjustments. Variations in growth, leaf dynamics, reproductive development, root architecture, and micronutrient regulation across different groups emphasize the complexity of drought adaptation strategies in sorghum.

In this study, a total of 3120 transcripts were identified as novel transcripts. Another transcriptome analysis of drought-resistant and drought-sensitive sorghum genotypes in response to PEG-induced drought stress carried out by Abdel-Ghany et al. [29]. revealed 70 novel genes with no known function. Interestingly, Cui et al. [18]. reported 5,647 differentially expressed genes in two varieties of sorghum in response to salt stress. In the present study, we identified 10,468 DEGs. The identification of key drought-responsive genes provides a dual resource: it elucidates critical molecular mechanisms and supplies valuable markers for breeding. This enables the targeted development of sorghum varieties with improved drought tolerance and optimized flowering timing, which is essential for maintaining productivity in water-limited environments.

Varoquaux et al. [30] demonstrated that preflowering drought induces more complex transcriptional dynamics in sorghum than postflowering drought, highlighting the critical window of reproductive transition. Understanding the regulation of flowering-time genes under this stress is therefore essential. It reveals the molecular basis of a key adaptive trait optimizing flowering for yield stability under water scarcity. Identifying the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved allows us to dissect how drought signaling integrates with developmental pathways. This knowledge is fundamental for developing drought-resilient sorghum varieties through targeted breeding, directly supporting agricultural sustainability in a changing climate.

Pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across all comparisons revealed a sophisticated and coordinated survival strategy for sorghum under drought stress, summarized in Fig. 9. This two-part strategy involves the broad suppression of growth-related processes (e.g., cell expansion, photosynthesis) and the targeted activation of protective systems (e.g., LEA proteins, zinc redistribution), effectively shifting the plant into a state of defensive austerity. This coordinated shift to a low-activity, resilient state supports findings by Cui et al. [18], whose functional annotation of sorghum under salt stress revealed similar enriched GO categories for molecular function and biological processes.

Metabolic and signalling shifts in sorghum under drought stress as revealed in the present study

Panther analysis identified three key pathways modulated by drought: de novo purine biosynthesis, lipoate biosynthesis, and Wnt signaling. The purine biosynthesis pathway was highly active, marked by strong upregulation of the chloroplastic gene adenylosuccinate synthetase 1. Nakagawa et al. [31]. reported that delayed growth, significant reductions in seedling biomass and cell viability following drought shock are attributable to deficiencies in purine metabolites such as uric acid. Accordingly, to acquire tolerance to drought, the biosynthesis of purines is crucial in plants. Similarly, the lipoate biosynthesis pathway was induced, notably through the mitochondrial lipoyl synthase gene. Lipoic acid is known to function in abiotic stress tolerance [32]. Sezgina et al. [33]. reported that exogenous application of alpha lipoic acid can stimulate photosystem II activity and the gene expression of carbon fixation and chlorophyll metabolism enzymes in maize seedlings under drought stress. Notably, the analysis also implicated the Wnt signaling pathway, a pathway primarily studied in animal development. The Wnt signalling pathway is highly regulated and plays a decisive role during embryonic patterning and cell fate determination [34]. Kashyap et al. [35]. using transcriptome profiling and de novo assembly of the wild tomato Solanum chilense, introduced the Wnt signalling pathway as a novel pathway in response to salinity stress in plants to understand the salt tolerance mechanism. The presence of Wnt or Wnt-like signalling pathways in plant systems is still unknown.

Overall, the qRT-PCR results were consistent with the RNA-seq data, confirming the reliability of the transcriptome analysis. The genes exhibited distinct expression trends among flowering groups, with some showing upregulation. Minor differences in fold-change magnitudes between the two methods were observed, likely reflecting inherent differences in sensitivity and dynamic range between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq. These results support the differential expression patterns identified by RNA-seq.

This study provides a comprehensive molecular framework for sorghum’s drought response and flowering time regulation by integrating transcriptomic and physiological data. Its key strength lies in identifying the conserved survival strategy, suppressing growth and activating protection modulated by flowering time. The novel implication is that flowering time is the primary determinant of drought behavior, creating a clear dichotomy: late-flowering genotypes use drought escape, while early-flowering ones rely on constitutive tolerance mechanisms. A major practical significance is the identification of high-priority candidate pathways for breeding, particularly lipoate biosynthesis and specific protective metabolite pathways, offering targeted genetic levers for improving drought resilience.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Drought stress was imposed under controlled greenhouse conditions, which may not fully replicate field environments. Flowering time grouping was based on a single set of environmental parameters; different photoperiods or temperature regimes might alter the observed patterns. Transcriptome analysis measures steady-state mRNA levels and does not capture post-transcriptional regulation or protein activity. The novel transcripts were identified using the current sorghum genome annotation, and future improved references may alter their coordinates or functional assignments. Functional characterization of candidate genes was beyond this study’s scope, so claims about mechanistic roles in drought tolerance remain preliminary. Future work should include transgenic functional studies and field-based validation across multiple seasons and locations.

Conclusions

The current study attempted to elucidate the relationship between flowering groups of sorghum (early, intermediate, and late flowering) and drought stress. A slight acceleration of flowering pathways was observed in the late-flowering group under drought-stressed conditions, whereas plants in the other two flowering groups showed no such acceleration. Differences in transcriptome levels among the three flowering groups under well-watered and drought-stressed conditions were explored via RNA sequence analysis and validated by qRT-PCR. The novel transcripts identified in this study may contribute to future improvements in sorghum genome annotation. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed differentially expressed genes related to cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. The findings suggest several candidate genes that could be further investigated for their potential role in drought tolerance. Functional characterization of the identified DEGs and pathways may help reveal aspects of sorghum’s response strategy under drought. Overall, these results may offer potential targets for future studies aimed at enhancing drought stress tolerance in sorghum and other crops.
