Endovascular repair of a common hepatic artery aneurysm using overlapping self-expanding covered stent grafts
Authors: Hiroki Nakabori, Hideyasu Ueda, Kenji Iino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatic artery aneurysms are uncommon but clinically important because rupture is associated with high mortality. A 69-year-old asymptomatic man was referred after medical screening ultrasound study suggested a pancreatic head mass. Computed tomography demonstrated a 43-mm true common hepatic artery aneurysm. Endovascular repair with self-expanding covered stent grafts was performed after coil embolization of the gastroduodenal artery. Intraoperative angiography revealed a type III endoleak at the overlap zone, which resolved after balloon molding with an angioplasty balloon catheter. The postoperative course was uneventful. At the 3-year follow-up, computed tomography showed no aneurysm enlargement or endoleak, with preserved hepatic arterial flow.

Hepatic artery aneurysms (HAAs) are rare but clinically important because rupture is associated with high mortality.1,2 Treatment options include open repair and endovascular treatment with coil embolization or stent grafting.3 Stent graft placement can exclude the aneurysm while preserving antegrade hepatic arterial flow and may be particularly useful for common hepatic artery aneurysms (CHAAs). However, experience with stent graft repair of CHAAs remains limited, particularly with long-term imaging follow-up. We report a case of a 43-mm CHAA successfully treated with overlapping VIABAHN stent grafts (W. L. Gore & Associates). The patient provided written consent for publication of the case details and accompanying images.

Case report

A 69-year-old asymptomatic man was referred to our department after medical screening abdominal ultrasound study suggested a mass lesion in the pancreatic head region, and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) at the referring institution suggested a CHAA. His medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hepatitis A. He was receiving two antihypertensive agents and one oral hypoglycemic agent. He had smoked 40 cigarettes/day from 20 to 45 years of age. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a 43 × 43-mm CHAA with calcification of the aneurysmal wall and mural thrombus (Fig 1). There was no history of abdominal trauma, pancreatitis, hepatobiliary surgery, or systemic infection. Based on the presence of wall calcification and mural thrombus without findings suggestive of infection or pseudoaneurysm, the lesion was diagnosed as a true CHAA. Because the anatomy appeared suitable for aneurysm exclusion while preserving antegrade hepatic arterial flow, endovascular repair with covered stent grafts was planned. The proximal and distal landing zone diameters were 7.2 and 6.5 mm, respectively. The selected stent grafts provided approximately 10% oversizing. A 6F short sheath was placed via the right femoral artery. Celiac angiography using a 4F Shepherd hook catheter confirmed the CHAA (Fig 2, A). The gastroduodenal artery was selectively cannulated with a 1.9F microcatheter and a 0.016-inch guidewire. Because the gastroduodenal artery originated adjacent to the aneurysm sac and was considered a potential source of retrograde sac perfusion, it was embolized with coils to prevent type II endoleak before stent graft deployment (Fig 2, B). Access to the hepatic artery was secured with a 0.035-inch guidewire, and the sheath was exchanged for a 7F guiding sheath, the tip of which was advanced into the common hepatic artery. The right hepatic artery was selectively cannulated using a 1.9F microcatheter and a 0.016-inch guidewire, and the guidewire was exchanged for a 0.018-inch guidewire to secure a stable working rail. A VIABAHN stent graft (7 mm × 2.5 cm) was introduced and deployed with its distal end positioned in the proper hepatic artery (Fig 2, C). A second VIABAHN stent graft (8 mm × 5 cm) was then deployed with approximately 1 cm of overlap, with its proximal end positioned proximal to the CHAA. Angiography after deployment showed contrast filling at the overlap zone, consistent with a type III endoleak (Fig 2, D). This resolved after balloon molding with a 7 × 20-mm percutaneous transluminal angioplasty balloon catheter centered on the junction (Fig 2, E). Completion angiography confirmed complete aneurysm exclusion and preserved hepatic arterial flow (Fig 2, F). There were no postoperative complications. Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel was started on postoperative day 1, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 2. One year after the procedure, antiplatelet therapy was reduced to aspirin monotherapy. Follow-up CT was performed at 3 months after the procedure, followed by another scan at 6 months and annual surveillance thereafter. Discussion

Visceral artery aneurysms are uncommon, with a reported incidence of 0.1% to 2%, and HAAs are often detected incidentally on CT performed for other conditions.1,2 The Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines recommend treatment for all symptomatic HAAs and for asymptomatic true HAAs larger than 2 cm or those enlarging by more than 0.5 cm within 6 months.3 The present 43-mm CHAA therefore clearly met the criteria for repair. Open repair, including ligation or bypass, remains an important option; however, endovascular treatment is favored when the anatomy is suitable, and hepatic arterial flow can be preserved.3,4

Published reports of HAA repair with covered stents or stent grafts remain limited. Balloon-expandable covered stents,5 the FLUENCY plus stent graft (Bard Peripheral Vascular),6 and the VIABAHN stent graft7,8 have been reported. Because visceral arteries are often tortuous, self-expanding stent grafts may be advantageous compared with balloon-expandable devices in selected anatomy.9,10 In the present case, overlapping self-expanding covered stent grafts were selected to accommodate the vessel course and the diameter mismatch between the proper and common hepatic arteries while maintaining hepatic arterial inflow.

Several technical points were important in this case. First, the gastroduodenal artery was embolized before stent graft deployment to reduce the risk of type II endoleak. Second, two stent grafts were used to achieve adequate proximal and distal sealing while preserving antegrade hepatic arterial flow. Third, an intraprocedural type III endoleak at the overlap zone was identified and successfully treated with balloon molding. These steps resulted in complete aneurysm exclusion on completion angiography.

Potential complications after covered stent repair include endoleak, in-stent thrombosis, branch vessel occlusion, and late occlusion. Zhakubayev et al6 reported intraprocedural in-stent thrombosis requiring additional treatment. If late stent graft occlusion occurs, hepatic ischemia may develop when collateral hepatic arterial supply is insufficient. Although previous reports suggest that many hepatic artery stent graft occlusions are asymptomatic because of collateral development, this patient underwent gastroduodenal artery embolization, which could limit one potential collateral pathway.11 Therefore, postoperative imaging surveillance is essential to confirm durable aneurysm exclusion and preservation of hepatic arterial flow. In the present case, CT at 3 years showed no endoleak or aneurysm enlargement and confirmed continued hepatic arterial patency. This case illustrates a practical branch-preserving strategy for anatomically suitable CHAAs: selective embolization of a potential collateral inflow, overlapping self-expanding covered stent grafts to accommodate vessel diameter mismatch, and careful treatment of overlap-related endoleak.

Conclusions

We report successful endovascular repair of a 43-mm common hepatic artery aneurysm using overlapping VIABAHN stent grafts, with preserved hepatic arterial flow and no endoleak or aneurysm enlargement at 3 years.
