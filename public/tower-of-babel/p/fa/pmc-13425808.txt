Engineering adaptive self-healing biomaterials from jammed microfluidic elastomeric particles
Authors: Jennifer Kieda, Kaitlyn Ramsay, Richard Jiang, Shira Landau, Ramak Khosravi, Chuan Liu, Amid Shakeri, Karl T Wagner, Dhana Abdo, Anna Maria Popovic, Sean Farley, Katherine S Elvira, Milica Radisic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomaterials designed for implantation must accommodate the dynamic mechanical and structural environment of the human body. Yet, current strategies for creating materials capable of adapting their shape post-implantation remain limited. In this work, we introduced a remoldable, biodegradable, and biocompatible granular scaffold composed of monodisperse poly(octamethylene maleate (anhydride) citrate) (POMaC) particles. Monodisperse POMaC droplets with controlled diameters were generated using a droplet microfluidic platform, then subsequently jammed and UV-crosslinked to form interconnected, porous, and self-healing elastomeric scaffolds. The scaffold chemical composition and crosslinking parameters directly influenced mechanical properties, stability, and permeability, enabling tunability across a range of tissue engineering applications. The granular scaffold exhibited autonomous self-healing, enhanced molecular diffusivity, and robust cell infiltration, while remaining moldable into prescribed geometries under both in vitro and in vivo conditions. Notably, following implantation, the granular scaffolds could be remolded in vivo 24 h post-implantation, allowing adaptation to a desired geometry while preserving structural integrity, promoting the recruitment of pro-reparative macrophages, and facilitating vascular ingrowth. Collectively, this work established a versatile strategy for designing adaptive implantable biomaterials capable of responding to the body's dynamic environment, with broad potential applications in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering.

Introduction

Living tissues can adapt and heal in ways that synthetic materials have long struggled to replicate. Human cells and extracellular matrix can mold to their environment, transport molecules through complex microarchitectures, and restore their integrity after injury [1]. Most synthetic scaffolds, however, are relatively static: they often limit mass transport, their high stiffness prevents conformal shaping, and, most notably, they cannot self-heal or remould at the treatment site [2,3]. Here, we envision a paradigm in which implanted materials can be reshaped in situ, enabling dynamic adaptation to patient-specific anatomy and healing responses after implantation.

A promising route towards this goal is to construct macroscopic scaffolds from microscopic units [4]. Materials such as alginate, polyethylene glycol (PEG), and gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) have been used to generate monodisperse cell-compatible microgels that can be jammed to result in sophisticated, mechanically stable, and porous structures [[5], [6], [7]]. For these applications, droplet microfluidic platforms enable tight control of particle size and monodispersity, facilitating their bottom-up assembly into porous scaffold architectures [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. When droplets are tightly packed, they undergo “particle jamming”, a transition from fluid-like to solid-like behavior once the critical packing fraction is reached [12]. Below the critical packing fraction, granular scaffolds exhibit fluid-like behavior, where particles are unbounded by mechanical constraints. In these cases, scaffolds can undergo relative rearrangement of the particle assemblage. Above the critical packing fraction, a jamming transition occurs. Jammed scaffolds prohibit relative particle rearrangement, forming cohesive load-bearing structures that start to exhibit mechanically stable, solid-like behavior [13]. Despite this mechanical transition, jammed scaffolds maintain interparticle porosity, which can continue to facilitate movement of nutrients, cells, and tissues into and through the scaffold [[14], [15], [16]]. Previous studies have demonstrated that jammed microgels can function as printable inks with tunable porosity [17]. Similarly, microporous annealed particle (MAP) scaffolds, assembled from hydrogel microparticle building blocks that are cross-linked or annealed to form a continuous porous architecture, have been shown to accelerate wound healing and promote in vivo tissue integration [18]. Such granular systems enable nutrients and cells to pass freely through scaffolds, facilitating efficient exchange and tissue integration [16,19], outperforming uniform gels and bulk materials while permitting geometric reconfiguration.

A distinct advantage of granular scaffolds lies in their ability to self-heal [20,21]. Since damage in a jammed scaffold only disrupts local particle contacts, new interfaces can readily form as microgels re-pack. Early attempts to design self-healing tissue scaffold systems achieved delayed crack formation but did not effectively initiate repair after damage. While first-generation systems patched damage imperfectly, second-generation materials facilitated restoration of both scaffold morphology and function [22]. However, achieving this degree of repair under physiological conditions remains challenging. While dynamic covalent bonds (e.g. imines, disulfides, Diels–Alder adducts) and supramolecular interactions (hydrogen bonding, host–guest complexes, hydrophobic domains) have shown promise for self-healing of constructs [[23], [24], [25]], most require non-physiological triggers such as heat, UV light, or low pH [22,25]. Hydrophobic interactions are particularly influential in aqueous systems since freshly formed hydrophobic interfaces spontaneously reassociate, expelling water and restoring cohesion [26,27]. Granular scaffolds, with their large surface area, amplify these interactions, making them especially suited to autonomous repair [28].

Yet, hydrogels poorly resist cell tractional forces, compacting with time in culture to distort their original shape, a phenomenon termed cell-gel compaction [29]. Additionally, they suffer from insufficient mechanical stability that often limits the choice of implantation sites and prevents direct fixation by suturing. Biodegradable elastomeric polymers, such as poly(octamethylene maleate (anhydride) citrate) (POMaC) [30], have emerged as promising options to address these limitations. POMaC is photopolymerizable, biocompatible, and exhibits tuneable elasticity. It has already been used to fabricate devices such as the AngioChip [31] and shape memory scaffolds [32], which can support vascularization and perfusion of engineered cardiac tissue patches. Importantly, bulk POMaC hinders diffusion of molecules larger than ∼70 kDa, requiring the use of porogens [31,33] and thus limiting both cytokine transport and cell infiltration into scaffolds. This difficulty in balancing structural integrity and permeability constrains the suitability of bulk POMaC and similar elastomers in applications involving personalized tissue reconstruction.

Conventional bulk hydrogels and elastomeric scaffolds frequently encounter a compromise between mechanical strength and mass transport, as higher crosslinking may restrict pore interconnectivity and cellular infiltration. Microgel-based granular systems mitigate this constraint by creating microporous networks that facilitate cell movement and transport. Sheikhi et al. [34] showed that microfluidically produced annealable microbeads can be constructed into injectable microporous scaffolds that improve cellular penetration. Nevertheless, these systems are predominantly hydrogel-based and lack inherent elastomeric properties, remoldability, and autonomous self-repair capabilities. This study merges a granular design with a biodegradable POMaC elastomer, incorporating microporosity, injectability, self-healing, and elastomeric mechanical properties into a unified platform.

In principle, by integrating the benefits of granular materials and bulk elastomers via bottom-up engineering, granular elastomeric scaffolds can be designed to overcome the limitations inherent to each class of material. However, it remains difficult to produce defined monodisperse droplets from pre-polymers such as POMaC due to their high viscosity and slow UV-crosslinking. Bulk dripping methods can achieve high throughput but yield poor droplet uniformity, while conventional microfluidic methods produce monodisperse droplets at low throughput. New plug-and-play devices resolve this trade-off without the high costs or technical expertise required to create complex microfluidic platforms [35]. Using this approach, we efficiently generated monodisperse POMaC droplets (∼100–1000 μm), creating consistent building blocks for granular, self-healing scaffold assembly.

Here, we test the hypothesis that assembling a biodegradable elastomer into a jammed granular architecture enables creation of a scaffold that is simultaneously permeable, mechanically stable, and capable of autonomous self-healing and remolding under physiological conditions. We introduce a granular POMaC scaffold made from monodisperse microfluidic droplets generated using a plug-and-play microcapillary platform (Fig. 1). The microcapillary platform allows for the generation of polymer droplets in an aqueous solution, eliminating the need for cumbersome device surface treatment [36]. These droplets can be tightly packed to create elastic, remoldable, and self-healing scaffolds with improved molecular diffusivity. The scaffolds are also able to adapt to high-resolution geometries, support widespread cell infiltration, and exhibit self-healing properties at physiological conditions. In vivo, the scaffolds remodel to conform to host tissue shape whilst maintaining their integrity and promoting cell infiltration. (Fig. 1).

Results

Controlled microdroplet fabrication for tunable scaffold architecture

Monodisperse POMaC microparticles were reproducibly generated using a plug-and-play microfluidic platform, enabling precise control over scaffold architecture. These monodisperse particles were then jammed to form porous, elastomeric structures that could be molded into desired shapes (Fig. 1). Here, we outline the fabrication workflow, starting from droplet formation to scaffold assembly. The resulting scaffolds exhibited self-healing behavior, supported cellular vascularization, and demonstrated potential for reversible scaffold remodeling following subcutaneous implantation.

Producing monodisperse POMaC microparticles is technically challenging because the polymer's high viscosity and adhesiveness impede standard microfluidic and emulsion-based fabrication approaches. Here, POMaC particles were fabricated by combining the POMaC pre-polymer with polyethylene glycol dimethacrylate 500 (PEGDM), using either bulk suspension methods (Fig. 2a) or a plug-and-play microfluidic approach (Fig. 2d). Pre-polymer synthesis was performed based on extensive optimization and characterization published previously, which included purification using 1,4-dioxane to remove unreacted monomer and oligomer alongside H NMR and FTIR to verify successful synthesis and purity of pre-polymer preparations [31,[37], [38], [39]].

In the bulk process, suspension polymerization was performed by introducing the POMaC mixture into a continuously stirred solution of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA). This led to the spontaneous formation of particles, as the PVA solution encapsulated the pre-polymer droplets during mixing. Due to the absence of precise control over the droplet formation process, this method produced particles with a wide size distribution (Fig. 2b–c), an observation that was further exacerbated at suboptimal stirring speeds and not appreciably improved by modifying injection flow rate.

In contrast, a “plug-and-play” microfluidic device inspired by prior work [26] provided a more controlled approach for producing POMaC particles. This device employed a junction box fabricated from a soft polymer to securely position tubing and needles in the configuration required for coaxial droplet formation. The dispersed (droplet) phase consisted of a 3:2 mixture of POMaC and PEGDM, while the continuous (surrounding) phase comprised 1% PVA in Milli-Q water. POMaC-based droplets formed at the interface between the two phases and were collected in a Petri dish (Video S1), followed by brief UV crosslinking to impart sufficient stability for subsequent handling and jamming. The continuous phase was controlled at 60 mL/h, while the dispersed phase flow rate was systematically varied to control droplet size, with modulation of the flow from 1.0 to 5.0 mL/h yielding monodisperse particles ranging from approximately 50 to 600 μm in diameter (Fig. 2f i–v). Notably, the plug-and-play design enabled droplet generation directly in an aqueous environment, eliminating the need for surface treatment of the device itself. Brightfield microscopy confirmed uniform droplet morphology across all conditions, and size distribution analyses revealed a direct, flow-rate-dependent relationship with particle diameter (Fig. 2f i–v). Collectively, this approach enabled precise and reproducible control over droplet size and uniformity (Fig. 2h).

pH and ionic conditions govern scaffold assembly in concert with UV-crosslinking

Standardized rectangular granular POMaC scaffolds (1 cm × 0.5 cm) were fabricated using Vytaflex molds (Fig. 3a). POMaC particles were pipetted into the mold and washed with Dulbecco's Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS), allowing them to be compacted into a jammed assembly with uniform geometry followed by a second UV-crosslinking step. The resulting granular material exhibited consistent visual and structural characteristics across replicates. To assess the effects of solution conditions on scaffold assembly, POMaC particles were washed and UV-treated in solutions spanning a range of pH values and ionic strengths (Fig. 3b). Among the tested conditions (PVA, deionized (DI) water, titrated DI water, Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), saline, and DPBS), only DPBS (pH 7.32, 157 mM) consistently supported the formation of a stable, jammed granular scaffold (Fig. 3b.).

(c) FTIR spectra of bulk and granular POMaC scaffolds. (d) Schematic illustrating the scaffold permeability assay, in which transwell inserts containing either bulk or granular POMaC scaffolds were loaded with FITC-dextran in the upper chamber and permeability was assessed by quantifying tracer transport to the lower chamber. (e) Representative images of bulk and granular scaffold permeability assays before and after the addition of FITC-dextran. Fig. 3c shows the FTIR spectra of the granular POMaC + PVA system compared with bulk-cured POMaC. Accordingly, both samples exhibited the characteristic bonds of POMaC, including ∼1730-1750 cm−1 (ester carbonyl, C=O), ∼3584-3700 cm−1 (hydroxyl, O-H), ∼1150-1300 cm−1 (ester network and citrate-derived backbone, C-O-C/C-O), and two peaks between ∼2850 and 2950 cm−1 (aliphatic stretching, C-H2). In turn, the absence of substantial new peaks (e.g., in the ester carbonyl region) in the granular material suggests that the self-healing behavior arises primarily from reversible intermolecular interactions as opposed to differences in covalent crosslinking. The observed spectral differences were subtle and concentrated in regions associated with hydrogen bonding, hydration, and chain organization, consistent with self-healing materials. Most notably, the granular sample exhibited greater absorbance in the O-H stretching region, consistent with residual PVA and enhanced hydrogen bonding.

To evaluate permeability of granular scaffolds compared to bulk POMaC, three dyes of different molecular weights—trypan blue (∼1 kDa), 70 kDa Fluorescein-isothiocyanate (FITC)-Dextran, and 2000 kDa FITC-Dextran—were added to the top chamber of separate transwell inserts containing either granular material or bulk POMaC, and transfer into the lower chamber was measured over time (Fig. 3d and e). The initial phase of dye transport across both bulk and granular materials was markedly slow for all three dyes, with minimal dye detected in the lower chamber 1 h after addition (Fig. 3f). However, the small molecule trypan blue was able to permeate through both the bulk and granular material at similar rates after 24 and 48 h (Fig. 3f(i)). Importantly, after 1 and 2 days, substantial dye transport of both 70 kDa and 2000 kDa FITC-Dextran was observed through granular scaffolds, while bulk POMaC remained largely impermeable (Fig. 3e). Quantitative fluorescence analysis confirmed significantly greater dye accumulation in the lower chamber for the granular condition at both 24 and 48 h (Fig. 3f). Moreover, the 70 kDa FITC-Dextran diffused through the granular material more easily, with increased accumulation in the lower chamber compared to the 2000 kDa FITC-Dextran (Fig. 3f). Together, these findings demonstrated that the porous architecture formed by jammed POMaC particles facilitated molecular transport of both small and larger molecules, in contrast to nonporous bulk material, which only permitted diffusion of smaller particles.

Next, we combined qualitative morphological assessment of scaffolds stored in DPBS over an extended period with quantitative image-based analysis of surface area changes to evaluate scaffold dimensional stability and infer the extent of surface erosion over time. During long-term storage in DPBS, granular scaffolds visually exhibited less cracking compared to bulk POMaC scaffolds (Fig. 3g). Qualitative assessments of scaffold integrity on days 0, 14, and 28 (Fig. 3g), along with corresponding scaffold surface area measurements (Fig. 3h), confirmed the dimensional stability of the granular group in comparison to bulk material. Specifically, bulk POMaC scaffolds exhibited a trend towards decreased surface area with time, consistent with surface erosion. Granular scaffolds maintained unchanged scaffold area over time, albeit with visible granule detachment at longer time points which would be consistent with bulk erosion. Contact angle measurements further indicated enhanced hydrophobicity of granular versus bulk scaffolds, with significantly higher contact angles observed for granular scaffolds in PVA, Milli-Q water, and HBSS (p < 0.05; Fig. 3i), suggesting hydrophobic interactions as a primary mechanism of scaffold stabilization.

Granular scaffolds exhibit self-healing behavior

Self-healing is a functional property of granular biomaterial scaffolds that allows for the restoration of structural integrity following mechanical deformation. We explored whether POMaC-based granular scaffolds could recover continuity after injury under physiological conditions. To assess this self-healing capacity, scaffolds were cleanly cut in half followed by an attempt to manually re-adhere the cut ends at 37 °C. We defined successful healing of scaffolds using both qualitative and quantitative criteria. Qualitatively, we assessed whether re-adhered scaffold halves successfully joined together to create a continuous structure that could be manipulated without breaking, additionally employing scanning electron microscopy to assess visual continuity at the re-adhered interface. Quantitatively, we performed mechanical testing to compare the Young's moduli and hysteresis loops of uninjured versus re-adhered scaffolds, ideally looking for no significant changes in these mechanical properties after injury/re-adherence. Whereas the cut bulk scaffold pieces remained separated and exhibited no signs of self-healing (Fig. 4a; Video S2), granular scaffolds healed completely into a single continuous structure when manually re-adhered (Fig. 4b; Video S3). Notably, repeating the same injury and re-adherence procedure with granular scaffolds assembled from alginate particles instead of POMaC did not lead to successful healing, highlighting this as a unique property of our elastomer-based system (Fig. S4). Prior to incision, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) demonstrated a smooth, continuous topography in bulk POMaC control scaffolds, whereas uninjured granular samples displayed the expected appearance typical of jammed microgels, with uneven particle interfaces and few smooth areas (Fig. 4c). Immediately after healing of granular scaffolds, SEM revealed partially fused particles and reduced interparticle voids at the cut interface, consistent with successful reintegration. By two weeks post-healing, SEM revealed coalescence of particles and re-establishment of inter-droplet continuity (Fig. 4d).

POMaC-based granular scaffolds exhibit self-healing properties, displaying gradual interfacial remodeling while maintaining consistent mechanical profiles before and after injury/re-adherence. a) Stepwise demonstration of cutting and attempted healing of bulk scaffolds and b) granular scaffolds maintained at 37 °C. c) Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of bulk and granular materials prior to incision. d) SEM images of granular material immediately after healing (depicting droplet interface reformation) and 2 weeks post-healing (depicting progressive coalescence and surface remodeling). e) Compressive mechanical testing indicated reduced elastic modulus in the granular material compared to the bulk POMaC controls, with consistent moduli measured for injured and healed scaffolds (n = 3 per group; ∗∗p < 0.01; One-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison test). f) Stress–strain curves also revealed significantly lower g) energy dissipation (hysteresis area) in the granular groups relative to controls, with no significant differences between uninjured and healed granular scaffolds (n = 3 per group; ∗p < 0.05; One-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison test). (h–i) Tables summarizing the mean Young's modulus (h) and area of hysteresis (i) of uninjured and healed scaffolds, together with the corresponding calculated self-healing efficiency of mechanical properties (%) for each group.

Importantly, mechanical testing demonstrated that uninjured and healed granular scaffolds exhibited identical apparent elasticity and hysteresis loops, indicating recovery of mechanical stability post-healing (Fig. 4e–g). As expected, both uninjured and healed granular scaffolds exhibited lower apparent elasticity than bulk POMaC controls (Fig. 4e). Remarkably, the healed granular scaffolds retained elasticity comparable to the uninjured condition, demonstrating effective self-healing without compromising mechanical integrity. Stress-strain analysis revealed a smaller hysteresis loop in granular samples when compared to bulk POMaC (Fig. 4f–g), indicating improved energy recovery, but once again revealed no significant difference between uninjured and healed granular scaffolds. Comparing the properties of the healed granular scaffolds directly to uninjured granular controls quantitatively underscored the effectiveness of self-healing from a mechanical standpoint. Healed granular scaffolds exhibited an average Young's modulus of 0.9 kPa, compared to 1.1 kPa for uninjured scaffolds, representing an 82% efficiency in recovery to baseline elasticity after healing (Fig. 4h). The area of the hysteresis loop also displayed a small and non-significant decrease after healing, changing from 23.4 Pa before injury to 22.3 Pa after, a nearly 95% efficiency in recovery (Fig. 4i).

Enhanced cell adhesion and vascular integration on granular scaffolds in vitro

Maintaining cellular compatibility and promoting vascularization of tissue constructs are both essential for the translational success of engineered scaffolds. Consequently, we assessed the behavior of endothelial and stromal cells within granular and bulk POMaC scaffolds. To evaluate cellular compatibility, GFP-expressing endothelial cells (GFP-ECs) were co-cultured with dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) on both granular and bulk scaffolds. DPSCs were selected as a stromal cell recognized for their highly effective potential to support the formation of stable self-assembling vasculature in vitro [[40], [41], [42]]. When seeded together on our scaffolds, these cells provided a physiologically relevant option for investigating adhesion and vascularization in vitro, [42] two essential properties for applications in both regenerative medicine and tissue engineering. Fluorescence microscopy at days 3 and 6 post-seeding showed greater cellular coverage on granular scaffolds compared to the bulk controls (Fig. 5a). Confocal imaging confirmed deeper cell penetration and more uniform distribution in the granular group (Fig. 5b). Immunostaining for CD31 (endothelial marker), GFP (HUVEC cell label), and DAPI (nuclei) revealed more extensive vascular-like structures on granular scaffolds compared to bulk controls (Fig. 5c). Additionally, αSMA-positive stromal cells were observed wrapping around the vessels within the granular scaffold, indicating vascular integrity (Fig. 5d). Quantification of GFP area showed significantly higher cell density in granular scaffolds than bulk scaffolds (∗p < 0.05; Fig. 5e), suggesting improved support for vascular network formation.

Granular POMaC scaffolds facilitate enhanced endothelial cell infiltration and vascular-like network development in comparison to bulk scaffolds. a) Representative fluorescence images of GFP-labeled endothelial cells (GFP-ECs) co-cultured with dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) seeded on bulk and granular scaffolds taken at Days 3 and 6 of culture. b) Confocal microscopy images captured on Day 6 of culture showing GFP-EC cell distribution and network morphology on bulk and granular scaffolds. c) Immunofluorescence staining and imaging of GFP (green), CD31 (red), and DAPI (blue) highlighted endothelial cell distribution and vascular-like network formation on bulk and granular scaffolds. d) Representative staining for αSMA (red) illustrated perivascular-like organization, with enhanced αSMA+ coverage on granular scaffolds. e) Quantification of cell density (% of GFP + area) revealed significantly greater cellular infiltration and distribution in granular scaffolds compared to bulk (n = 8 per group; ∗p < 0.05; nonparametric unpaired t-test with Mann-Whitney test).

Granular scaffolds adapt to Complex Geometries In Vitro and In Vivo

To assess shape adaptation and remodeling potential, granular scaffolds were molded into rectangular shapes and either remolded into triangles in vitro (Video S4) or implanted subcutaneously in mice then reshaped into circular forms in vivo (Fig. 6a). We first assessed the feasibility of in vitro manual remolding, observing stable maintenance of the remolded scaffold configuration (Fig. 6b). Confocal imaging and quantitative analysis of the scaffold pore microstructure revealed appreciable overlap in the overall pore size distributions before and after remolding. We observed what appeared to be a lattice comprised of three or four particles packed throughout the scaffold structure. Both distributions exhibited modal interparticle distances centered close to 20 μm, with similar average interparticle distances of approximately 40 and 35 μm before and after remolding, respectively (Fig. 6c–f). Scaffolds that were implanted in vivo then manually reshaped were explanted from mice after 24 h for visual assessment. Whereas bulk POMaC retained its original rectangular shape despite attempts at manual remolding, granular scaffolds preserved the newly imposed circular geometry (Fig. 6g). These findings demonstrate the potential of granular scaffolds to undergo dynamic, on-demand, surgeon-guided remodeling post-implantation, evolving their geometry as desired.

Granular scaffolds enhance reparative macrophage recruitment

To investigate long-term integrity and functionality of the granular scaffolds, we conducted a four-week subcutaneous in vivo implantation study in mice. Representative Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson's Trichrome (MT) images displayed an increased amount of cellular infiltration into the granular scaffold compared to the bulk scaffolds, where cells largely remained at the scaffold's periphery (Fig. 7a). This suggests that the granular scaffold's topography and permeability may facilitate improved cell migration and tissue integration.

Additionally, we investigated macrophage and endothelial infiltration through immunofluorescence staining for: CD68, a marker for total macrophages (Fig. 7b; Fig. 7d(i)) [43]; CD206, a marker of pro-regenerative, pro-angiogenic macrophages (Fig. 7b; Fig. 7d(ii)) [44]; and CD31, a marker of endothelial cells [45] (Fig. 7c; Fig. 7d(iv)). In all cases, improved infiltration of macrophages and endothelial cells was noted in the interior of granular scaffolds compared to the bulk controls, where cells largely remained at the periphery. Importantly, the CD206/CD68 ratio (Fig. 7d(iii)) was significantly higher in granular scaffolds compared to the bulk controls, indicating an enrichment of pro-regenerative M2 macrophages. In addition, the potential for a trend in increased blood vessel density warrants further investigations of scaffold vascularization in vivo, especially given the prior observation of vascular endothelial network penetration within granular scaffolds in vitro [46]. The overall infiltration distance of cells into explanted scaffolds, expressed as a percentage of the total scaffold thickness, was significantly higher for the granular material compared to bulk POMaC, where infiltration was limited or nonexistent (Fig. 7d(v); Fig. S5). These findings underscore the importance of the jammed particle topography and microenvironment in enabling pro-regenerative macrophage infiltration and tissue integration.

Discussion

Synthetic biomaterials have long struggled to match the adaptive resilience of living tissues, which can remodel in response to their surroundings, permit efficient molecular diffusion through the matrix, and heal after injury. Many scaffolds are brittle, lacking the permeability for mass transport, the elasticity for conformal shaping, and most critically, the capacity for self-repair and dynamic on-demand remolding after implantation [2,3]. The ability to reshape an implanted scaffold or a medical device in situ to accommodate patient-specific healing responses would represent a transformative advance in surgical therapy. The present work addresses these challenges by combining plug-and-play microfluidic fabrication with a biodegradable elastomer to create a scaffold that conforms to a pre-defined shape, supports molecular diffusion, and remolds and self-heals with tissue-like agility. The POMaC granular scaffold is composed of uniform droplets that jam into a cohesive, remoldable material uniquely at physiological pH and ionic strength conditions (i.e., in DPBS), aided by a secondary UV-crosslinking step. We reason that the ionic composition and osmotic environment in which scaffolds are initially jammed are critical towards achieving stable particle-particle interactions for effective scaffold consolidation. This was supported by the failed consolidation of particles jammed in DI water, saline (pH 5.37), or dilute PVA solutions that may have provided insufficient ionic strength or interfacial stabilization to maintain scaffold integrity following particle assembly compared to DPBS. These observations underline the utility of further studies of the microscale chemistry mediating differential particle consolidation in jammed granular scaffolds. Overall, alignment of the optimal cross-linking environment with physiological conditions is key towards minimizing cell and tissue toxicity to expand the range of potential applications for this scaffold. The plug-and-play droplet approach allows for precise size control, overcoming the processing barriers that viscous polymers like POMaC pose in conventional microfluidics, such as clogging and low throughput [47]. By leveraging a modular microcapillary device [23], we generated monodisperse POMaC droplets without costly equipment or harsh processing. The result is an architectural innovation: an elastomeric macroscale scaffold made of microscale particles, bringing inherent permeability and uniformity to the assembly.

The utilization of microfluidically generated hydrogel microparticles as a fundamental component of tissue engineering scaffolds has been increasingly investigated owing to their capacity to form interconnected porous structures while preserving adjustable mechanical characteristics. Anderson et al. [48] revealed that microfluidically produced microgels may be organized into MAP scaffolds, wherein scaffold porosity functions as a crucial regulator of cellular behavior and tissue integration. In a similar vein, Sheikhi et al. [34] developed a microfluidic-enabled bottom-up hydrogel platform that assembled annealable protein microbeads into microporous scaffolds, emphasizing the benefits of microfluidic fabrication for generating uniform building blocks and controllable scaffold architectures. Furthermore, Di Caprio et al. [49] reported injectable granular composites including mesenchymal stromal cell spheroids and hydrogel microgels for tissue engineering applications, illustrating the capacity of granular microgel scaffolds to facilitate tissue formation while preserving injectability. In line with these investigations, the microfluidically produced POMaC particles created a porous granular scaffold that facilitated cellular infiltration and tissue remodeling. The POMaC platform, alongside the advantages of granular scaffold architecture, offers biodegradability, elastomeric mechanical properties, self-healing capabilities, and remoldability, potentially enhancing its applicability in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

The granular architecture of these engineered scaffolds imparts several key advantages. First, it dramatically improves molecular transport. Bulk POMaC slabs provide tissue-like elasticity, but often impede molecular diffusion [31,33]. In contrast, our jammed-microgel scaffold features an interconnected network of voids, allowing solutes to diffuse freely. This enhanced permeability is crucial for cell infiltration, survival and function in tissue constructs, enabling the exchange of cytokines and the removal of waste throughout the scaffold. Notably, this enhancement did not come at the expense of scaffold stability, supported by the fact that the granular scaffold preserved its shape and integrity in DPBS over several weeks. The preservation of scaffold morphology and surface area over time, alongside the appearance of individually detaching POMaC particles in DPBS, may indicate a potential bulk degradation mechanism and warrants further application-specific investigations. Second, the granular scaffold's permeability greatly aids cell infiltration and vascular integration. The modal and average interparticle distances measured in both native and remolded scaffolds, ranging from 20 to 40 μm, suggested pore sizes sufficient for cellular infiltration. With the addition of co-cultured cells, endothelial cells and dental pulp stem cells penetrated into the jammed particle scaffold and formed extensive vessel-like networks, whereas bulk POMaC supported only superficial cell networks. Such cell-permissive behavior aligns with prior reports for microporous particle scaffolds that promoted deep cellular ingrowth and accelerated tissue integration [17,18]. By providing pre-formed pores and a large surface area, the granular microenvironment circumvents diffusion and cell-migration limitations, echoing the design philosophy of MAP scaffolds, which achieved superior in vivo integration and wound healing [18].

Equally compelling is the scaffold's ability to heal under physiological conditions, where we observed no significant differences in granular scaffold elasticity or hysteresis area after healing when compared to uninjured controls. Though self-healing represents an increasingly desirable characteristic in biomaterial design [20,21] realization in situ without external triggers has remained elusive [22,25]. Early self-healing polymers could slow crack growth or partially repair damage, but they often required embedded adhesives or did not fully restore functionality. Newer strategies have employed dynamic covalent bonds and supramolecular motifs to enable repeatable healing [23,25]. In contrast to many previously studied MAP scaffold systems [18,50,51] that rely on secondary annealing mechanisms involving photochemical, enzymatic, or click-chemistry-mediated crosslinking to form interparticle connections, the POMaC granular scaffold is able to self-heal at 37 °C, neutral pH, and physiological osmotic pressure without the need for chemical additives or external stimuli. This property was also unique to our elastomeric system when compared to comparable granular scaffold structures assembled from alginate particles, which may be able to recreate the advantageous porosity of our material but cannot self-heal after injury.

Although the full mechanism of the healing behavior remains to be elucidated, the surface chemistry in bulk and granular materials is largely similar, suggesting that the self-healing behavior arises primarily from reversible intermolecular interactions as opposed to differences in covalent crosslinking. The granular POMaC + PVA system contains a much larger interfacial area than the bulk material, which could concentrate hydrogen bonds, contain dangling chains, and possess unreacted functional groups. When fractured or cut, these groups can then reconnect more readily than a fully cured, homogeneous bulk POMaC network.

The presence of 1,8-octanediol in the POMaC backbone introduces hydrophobicity to the overall amphiphilic behavior of POMaC, with hydrophilicity driven by the citrate-derived groups, ester carbonyls and maleate groups. The presence of highly hydrophilic PVA in the system, in turn, introduces more -OH groups, thereby increasing the system's hydrogen-bonding capacity. When the granular network is washed with PBS (water-based), the network becomes “sticky”, i.e., exhibiting interfacial cohesion. Water absorption, intensified by the presence of PVA, may plasticize the network, increasing chain mobility, and facilitating hydrogen-bond exchange, collectively allowing the polymer chains to rearrange and diffuse more easily, enabling broken interfaces to re-establish contact and hydrogen bonds to break and reform dynamically. In turn, this is why the self-healing behavior of the granular POMaC + PVA system is observed only under humid conditions or in DPBS, which is beneficial for in vivo applications.

In summary, the similar surface chemistry and increased surface area of the granular scaffolds supports our reasoning that reversible intermolecular interactions contribute to the self-healing properties of the granular POMaC scaffold. Severed scaffolds rapidly re-adhered upon contact, restoring handling strength and original compressive modulus, and tolerating cyclic strain with minimal energy loss. These results demonstrated full structural and mechanical recovery, consistent with second-generation self-healing materials [22]. It is important to note that no external trigger was needed beyond the physiological temperature, pH, and ionic strength, conditions found in culture media and physiological fluids. This underscored the uniqueness of hydrophobic interaction-driven healing in a fully aqueous, cell-compatible environment. Unlike most physical hydrogels, which require prolonged or specialized conditions to heal [24], the jammed microstructure and hydrophobic chemistry enabled rapid, spontaneous repair, addressing a key limitation in biomaterials design. While hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions likely contribute to healing, further mechanistic studies will be required to determine their relative contributions.

Representing another unique advantage of the granular POMaC scaffolds, their potential for adaptable reshaping is reminiscent of a shape-memory scaffold's behavior [32], yet here we propose it can arise from physical rearrangement of particles rather than programmed polymer phase transitions. Such intrinsic malleability has significant translational potential, possibly enabling a surgeon to shape the material to fit a complex defect. Moreover, the granular nature may help distribute mechanical stresses across many contact points, which likely contributes to its damage tolerance. MAP scaffolds have been recognized as a strong biomaterial platform for regenerative medicine, attributed to their injectable characteristics, interconnected microporosity, and shown capacity to enhance cell infiltration and tissue healing in vivo. In contrast to granular POMaC scaffolds, the remodeling potential of comparable MAP scaffold systems implanted in vivo is inherently limited by conditions required for rearrangement of the bonds formed between their constituent microspheres, which tends to require non-physiological external stimuli as outlined previously [18]. Thus, the passive physiological in vivo remodeling potential of the granular POMaC scaffolds demonstrated here further illustrates their novel advantages for applications involving in vivo biomaterial implantation.

Situating our work in the landscape of granular biomaterials and self-healing hydrogels highlights its novelty. Previous granular hydrogel platforms demonstrated injectability and promoted regeneration by providing a microporous niche for cells [15]. Others showed that hydrogels can be engineered to self-heal via reversible bonds [24,25,44,52] However, integrating a physiological self-healing mechanism into an elastomeric granular scaffold has not been reported previously. A recent comprehensive review of self-healing hydrogels underscored the difficulty of achieving autonomous repair in biomedical contexts and called for strategies that work with, rather than against, the body's environment [28]. Unlike scaffold systems based on bulk hydrogels or MAP scaffolds assembled from microspheres [18], our granular scaffold (based on the amphiphilic POMaC) possessed innate self-healing capacity without requiring any additional chemistry, thermal annealing, external stimuli, or supplemental bonding agents. The granular POMaC scaffold formation process is carried out under cytocompatible conditions [26,27], aligning with the practical demands of tissue engineering. Additionally, the elastomeric character of POMaC enabled retention of interparticle porosity alongside sufficient structural integrity when jammed and permitted in vivo remolding without collapse. By uniquely combining biodegradability, elastomeric mechanics, and permeability alongside innate self-healing and in vivo remolding potential, granular POMaC scaffolds provide versatile utility in a broad range of applications in tissue engineering, minimally invasive delivery, or smart defect filling compared to previous materials like MAP or bulk hydrogel scaffolds that present more targeted or application-specific advantages.

Additionally, the results of the four-week in vivo implantation study underscored the importance of the scaffold microenvironment. Granular scaffolds enabled improved cellular penetration into constructs compared to the bulk condition, likely due to their permeability facilitating cell migration. This observed permeability and cellular infiltration underscore the functional advantage of the granular architecture. This finding is consistent with other studies that have concluded that scaffold architecture modulates cell infiltration and tissue integration in vivo [53,54]. In particular, granular scaffolds enhanced macrophage response when compared to bulk scaffolds, increasing the CD206 (M2-like macrophages)/CD68 (total macrophages) ratio. M2-like macrophages support anti-inflammatory, tissue remodeling, and pro-angiogenic functions, suggesting that the granular topography may be favourable for regenerative healing applications [55].

Limitations and future work

While our study focused on the comparative differences between granular scaffolds and conventional bulk materials implanted in vivo, we note that the in vivo studies served as a preliminary proof-of-concept evaluation with a small sample size (n = 3 per group) and therefore require validation in larger, suitably powered animal studies. In addition, we note that future translational applications support in-depth benchmarking of our scaffolds against "no scaffold" controls to fully characterize their baseline impact on the body's own reparative, macrophage recruitment, and angiogenic processes beyond the scope of this work. Additionally, long-term in vivo studies characterizing the kinetics and chemistry of scaffold degradation, alongside corresponding cell- and tissue-level responses, represent an important next step towards realizing translational applications of these materials. Building on the present work establishing the granular POMaC scaffold platform, future in-depth microscale investigations are warranted to fully define the molecular and interfacial mechanisms that govern their self-healing and remodeling behavior. Although the scope of our study focused specifically on POMaC, the insights described above would not only inform broader application of these scaffolds but may also yield generalizable design principles for other polymer-based granular systems tailored to specific clinical indications.

Beyond the application in subcutaneous tissue engineering demonstrated here, our versatile remoldable granular scaffold presents broader potential utility in applications including minimally invasive delivery of injectable cells and tissues, filling of non-uniform tissue defects, reconstruction of soft tissues, or in advanced wound healing therapeutics. The scaffold's permeability, biodegradability, and self-healing behavior may be particularly useful when adapted towards injectable delivery into dynamic and high-remodeling tissue environments, where static pre-formed scaffolds and hydrogels may fail to adapt to changing in vivo conditions. These potential use cases harnessing the advantageous properties of the granular POMaC scaffold underline the rationale for future studies tailored to support the translation of this material for a specific disease indication.

Conclusions

The integration of a plug-and-play microfluidic platform, jammed granular assembly, and POMaC chemistry produced a multifunctional scaffold designed to emulate key aspects of living tissue dynamics. POMaC, a photocrosslinkable citrate-based elastomer, provided a biodegradable and tunable matrix that supported cell growth. Processing POMaC into uniform, UV-stabilized microparticles introduced permeability and modularity without compromising elasticity. Microfluidic fabrication ensured granule uniformity, resulting in predictable scaffold architecture and mechanics, representing a marked improvement over conventional porous scaffolds. The jammed granular structure imparted moldability and adaptability, enabling casting into predefined shapes and post-fabrication remodeling in vitro. This behavior was preserved in vivo, where the scaffold remained intact and manually remoldable 24 h after implantation. By engineering a scaffold that heals under physiological conditions, we underscore a broader standard: that biomaterials can be designed to work with the body's own complex healing processes rather than merely resist failure. The developed granular POMaC platform exemplifies adaptability, biocompatibility and self-healing, providing considerable promise for future applications in tissue engineering and regenerative therapeutics. Overall, these findings position granular POMaC scaffolds as a practical platform for adaptive, self-healing biomaterials that can be reshaped after implantation.

Materials and methods

POMaC prepolymer synthesis

POMaC pre-polymer was synthesized via the polycondensation of citric acid, maleic anhydride, and 1,8-octanediol in a molar ratio of 5:1:4 based on previous published work [56,57]. A 100 mL two-neck round-bottom flask was clamped to a stand and placed in a 160 °C oil bath on a hot plate, stirring using a magnetic stir bar. Nitrogen was connected to the reaction vessel, which was passed through a water condenser to collect any by-products. The three monomers were precisely weighed (6 g: 12.25 g: 22.83 g, for molar ratio of 5:1:4) and added to the two-necked flask along with a magnetic stir bar with rubber stoppers closing the exposed necks. The nitrogen line was then attached to the outlet. The monomers were then subsequently melted at 160 °C in a nitrogen environment with constant stirring at 200 rpm. The temperature was subsequently reduced to 140 °C, and the reaction was left to run for 3 h under a continuous nitrogen flow, protecting it from light. Once the reaction had completed, the reaction vessel was removed from the oil bath, detached from the outlet glassware (i.e. condenser and collection vessel), and the pre-polymer was left to cool for 5 min. The cooled POMaC pre-polymer was purified by dissolving it in 1,4-dioxane to remove oligomers and unreacted monomers. The purified pre-polymers were then added dropwise into a 1L beaker of deionized water. The resulting pre-polymer was collected at the bottom of the beaker. After precipitation, deionized water was decanted, and the polymer was transferred to a 20 mL scintillation vial. The precipitate was air-dried under constant air flow for 72 h to remove any remaining solvent. The end-product was a transparent, viscous pre-polymer, which was stored in the dark at 4 °C until used.

Before use, the POMaC pre-polymer was combined with polyethylene glycol dimethacrylate 500 (PEGDM) at a 3:2 ratio in a 20 mL glass scintillation vial. Next, 5 w/v % of 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenenone photoinitiator was added. Using a stir bar, the POMaC mixture was stirred on a hot plate at 100 °C for 30 min, shielded from light with aluminum foil, until the mixture appeared homogeneous. Lastly, the polymer was transferred into a 5 mL syringe connected to a 20G needle and protected from light until used.

Fabrication of spherical POMaC and alginate particles via bulk shearing

Following previous studies, such as Landau et al. [58] POMaC particles generated using bulk shearing were fabricated by emulsifying the polymer into a 1 w/v % polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution. First, the PVA solution was prepared by dissolving PVA powder (87-90% hydrolysis degree, Sigma-Aldrich) in Milli-Q water (1 w/v %) and stirring at room temperature for 40 min. The PVA solution was then heated to 100 °C and stirred for an additional 40 min. Next, the temperature was reduced to 65 °C, and stirring was continued overnight. Once dissolved, PVA solution was added to a 200 mL glass reagent bottle and placed on a hot plate to stir at 400 rpm. A 5 mL syringe was filled with a mixture containing POMaC pre-polymer, PEGDM, and 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenenone photoinitiator, as described above. The syringes were secured in place into an Aladdin syringe pump (Aladdin Single-Syringe infusion Pump AL-300, WPI). The pump was then rotated 90° and placed on a lab jack stand, with the syringe opening pointing downward toward the PVA bath. A 23G needle was fitted to the syringe. Lastly, the syringe pump was set to a flow rate of 16 mL/h, and the hot plate was set to 1000 rpm. POMaC was dispersed dropwise into the stirred PVA solution for 40 min, or until the desired number of droplets has been achieved. For comparison of the effect of variable injection flow rates on droplet size distribution, the above protocol was performed at a stirring speed of 1000 rpm for flow rates of 8 mL/h, 16 mL/h, and 20 mL/h. For assessment of the effect of stirring speed on droplet size distribution, the protocol described above was performed at an injection flow rate of 16 mL/h and stirring speeds of 500, 1000, and 1200 rpm. Alginate particles were also generated using bulk shearing methods. Alginate was first prepared using a 1 w/v% alginic acid sodium salt solution (Sigma-Aldrich) in DI water. Using a 23G needle, alginate was then dropwise added to a 10 mL mineral oil bath containing 1% Tween 20 in a 20 mL beaker with a stir bar at 500 rpm. A total volume of 100 μ L of alginate was added to the mineral oil bath, followed by 100 μ L of 100 mM CaCl2 (calcium chloride dihydrate, Molecular Biology Grade, >99.5%). The solution was stirred for 10 min to allow crosslinking.

Microfluidic device fabrication

Building on the approach from Farley et al. [35] our modular microfluidic platform featured a single junction box cast from two 3D printed molds using polylactic acid (PLA). The molds were designed in Fusion 360 (Autodesk; Fig. S1) and 3D printed on an Ender-3 printer (Creality, Shenzhen, China) (Fig. S1). The design included a mold replicated twice: one for the inlet half and one for the outlet half of the junction box. Once the molds had been 3D-printed, polyurethane resin (Vytaflex 30, Smooth-On) was used to cast the junction boxes. This resin was selected for its low Shore hardness (30A), which enables it to seal blunt needles or tubing without requiring gaskets or adhesives. The Vytaflex 30 polymer and crosslinker were mixed in a 1:1 ratio, following the manufacturer's instructions. The 3D-printed molds were then sprayed with mold release (Smooth-On), and the mixed polyurethane resin was poured in. Next, the two molds were aligned and held together with rubber bands. The resin was left to cure overnight at room temperature. The junction box was then removed from the molds. To complete the assembly of the device, a 20G blunt needle was inserted into polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, Avantor) tubing (ID: 0.79 mm, OD: 2.38 mm) for each of the two inlets, and the outlet. The final configuration included one needle inserted into the inlet (POMaC phase), one into the top opening (PVA phase), and one into the outlet (droplet collection) (Fig. S2).

Microfluidic setup

Uniform POMaC microdroplets were generated using two syringe pumps. Controlled flow rates were used to independently deliver the dispersed POMaC phase at 0.5 mL/h and the continuous PVA phase at 15 mL/h into the microfluidic junction box. A pre-mixed 5 mL POMaC polymer syringe (as described above, containing pre-POMaC, PEGDM, and 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenenone photoinitiator) was secured in place into the syringe pump, where it was introduced into the droplet generator using PTFE tubing connected to the inlet on the side of the junction box. A 50 mL syringe containing 1 w/v % PVA in the continuous phase was connected via PTFE tubing to the top inlet (Video S1). Droplets formed via coaxial flow at the T-junction within the junction box, ensuring consistent droplet size. The resulting POMaC droplets were collected directly into an empty 100 mm petri dish for subsequent processing. Droplet size analysis was performed using Fiji (Version 2.3.0), where the maximum two-dimensional (2D) diameter of each droplet was measured to approximate particle size. Size distribution graphs for particles generated via bulk mixing and plug-and-play microfluidics were created using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.4.1).

Formation of the scaffold mold

Granular material was molded into defined shapes for experimental consistency. To fabricate rectangular scaffolds, a 1 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.7 cm mold was prepared by cutting a flat polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, SYLGARD 184 Silicone Encapsulant, Ellsworth Adhesives) sheet. Briefly, PDMS silicone elastomer base and curing agent were manually mixed in a 10:1 (w/w) ratio with a spatula until homogeneous, degassed in a vacuum desiccator (∼30 min until bubble-free), poured into a square petri dish, and cured in a convection oven at 60 °C for 2 h. The PDMS cut-out was placed flat in a 35 mm petri dish and filled until the top edge of the PDMS mold with polyurethane resin (Vytaflex 30, Smooth-On), then cured overnight at room temperature. The next day, the PDMS cut-out was removed and the Vytaflex mold was ready to use.

Formation of granular scaffolds

Once POMaC droplets had been formed and collected, they were transferred to a 35 mm petri dish and crosslinked under UV light (365 nm; 80,000 μW cm2 at 38 cm; Labino UVG3 2.0 UV LED Flashlight, spotlight) for 20 min under constant stirring within PVA solution. Then, 400 μL of POMaC droplets (∼82,000) were pipetted into the mold and left to settle for 1 min. Once settled, the PVA solution (1% w/v in MilliQ water) was removed using a pipette, and the droplets were washed twice with Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS (without calcium and magnesium); pH 7.3). After the second wash, DPBS was removed with a pipette, leaving behind a jammed, semi-dry granular assembly. The appearance of the material at this stage served as a quality-control indicator: a glossy or wet-like surface suggested under-crosslinking, whereas loose or unjammed particles indicated excessive crosslinking time. The granular scaffold was then crosslinked under UV light (365 nm; 80,000 μW cm2 at 38 cm) for 1 min at a distance of 9.5 cm from the flashlight. Following crosslinking, the mold was gently removed to yield a stable, shape-retaining granular scaffold that was ∼1 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.3 mm in size (Fig. S3).

Bulk scaffold formation

Using the rectangular-shaped mold described above, the mold was placed in a 35 mm petri dish and 100 μL of pre-mixed POMaC (as described above, containing pre-POMaC, PEGDM, and 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenenone photoinitiator) was applied. The mold was then placed under UV light (365 nm; 80,000 μW cm2 at 38 cm) at a distance of 9.5 cm for 18 min. The mold was then removed, producing a ∼1 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.12 cm rectangular bulk scaffold.

Granular scaffold conditional development test

To assess the influence of pH and ionic strength on scaffold assembly, a similar scaffold fabrication procedure was used. First, POMaC droplets were collected and exposed to UV light (365 nm; μW cm2 at 38 cm) under constant stirring at a distance of 8.5 cm for 20 min. Then, 400 μL of POMaC droplets (∼82,000) were pipetted into the mold and left to settle for 1 min. Once settled, PVA solution (1% w/v in Milli-Q water) was removed with a pipette, and the droplets were washed twice with 400 μL of one of the following washing solutions: PVA (pH 4.87, 0 mM ionic strength), deionized (DI) water (pH 5.89, 0 mM ionic strength), DI water (titrated, pH 7.30, 0 mM ionic strength), Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; pH 8.26, 80 mM ionic strength), saline (pH 5.37, 154 mM ionic strength), or Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; pH 7.32, 157 mM ionic strength). Then, the scaffolds were exposed to UV light (365 nm; 80,000 μW cm2 at 38 cm) at a distance of 9.5 cm for 1 min on a VWR Lab Disc Magnetic Stirrer (∼100-200 rpm). The stability of scaffold formation was visually assessed immediately; a successful scaffold appeared as a fully jammed, self-supporting structure that maintained its shape after liquid removal. For alginate granular scaffolds, ∼400 μL of bulk sheared alginate droplets were pipetted into the mold and left to settle for 1 min. Once settled, the mold was removed.

Permeability test of monodisperse granular material

To evaluate permeability, 24-well transwell (Greiner Bio-One, ThinCert, TC Insert for 24W Plate, PET, 1.0 μm Pore Size) inserts were used to compare granular POMaC scaffolds with bulk POMaC polymer (as described above, containing pre-POMaC, PEGDM, and 2-Hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenenone photoinitiator). For the bulk condition, we added 100 μL of POMaC polymer into the transwell using a syringe, and UV crosslinked the material (365 nm; 80,000 μW cm2 at 38 cm; Labino UVG3 2.0 UV LED Flashlight, spotlight, Sweden) in the transwell for 14-17 min. For the granular condition, we made POMaC particles as described above and UV crosslinked (365 nm, 80,000 μW cm2 at 38 cm) them in a 35 mm petri dish with a stir bar for 20 min. Then 100 μL (∼20,500 particles) were added to the transwell, excess PVA was removed, and the particles were washed twice with DPBS. Lastly, the transwell containing particles was UV illuminated (365 nm; 80,000 μW cm2 at 38 cm) for 1 min. A 24-well plate was filled with 800 μL of PBS per well. The transwell inserts containing the scaffolds were then placed inside each well. 80 μL of a 1 mg/mL solution, diluted in PBS, of either trypan blue (Trypan Blue Solution, 0.4%; Gibco), 70 kDa Fluorescein-isothiocyanate–Dextran (FITC-Dextran; Sigma-Aldrich; average mol wt 65,000-85,000 g/mol), or 2000 kDa FITC-Dextran (Sigma-Aldrich; average mol wt 2,000,000 g/mol) was then added to the top of each scaffold. Permeability was assessed by measuring the fluorescence intensity of the dye for the two FITC-Dextran solutions, or absorbance for the trypan blue solution using a plate reader (SpectraMax i3, Molecular Devices) as the dye accumulated in the lower chamber of the 24-well plate after 1 h, 24 h and 48 h.

Stability in DPBS

To assess stability over time, bulk POMaC scaffolds and granular POMaC scaffolds were prepared using the methods described above. Samples were distributed into separate wells of a 24-well plate containing 2 mL of DPBS. The scaffolds were imaged on Days 0, 14, and 28 to identify notable morphological changes such as cracking, fragmentation, or particle detachment. Stability was evaluated based on the retention of the original shape and the absence of visible structural defects, rather than weighing the scaffolds since both bulk and granular scaffolds tended to break apart after repeated manipulation for weighing. Image analysis was performed in ImageJ (Fiji), with area quantified manually.

Contact angle

Contact angles were measured using the sessile drop method. Droplet images were acquired using a goniometer (Holmarc, Model HO-IAD-CAM-01B) and images were analyzed using the OpenDrop droplet image analysis software [59]. Each value is the average of the left and right contact angle for n = 3-5 independently placed droplets.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The chemical structure of the crosslinked granular POMaC + PVA system was compared to bulk cured POMaC using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker ALPHA system) to further investigate the material's self-healing behavior. To minimize spectral interference from PBS in the granular samples, a 1X PBS background spectrum was acquired for background correction. Granular POMaC + PVA slabs were prepared as previously described, while bulk POMaC control samples were cast from POMaC prepolymer and UV-cured for approximately 15 min under identical conditions. The resulting FTIR spectra were subsequently baseline-corrected and vector-normalized to ensure that observed spectral differences reflected changes in chemical composition and intermolecular interactions rather than variations in sample mass or thickness.

Self-healing assessment

Individual POMaC bulk or granular scaffolds were immersed in DPBS, then removed from the solution and placed on a glass slide, which was positioned on a 37 °C hot plate to maintain physiological temperature during handling. A clean, centralized cut was made through each scaffold using a scalpel to simulate mechanical injury. The two severed halves were carefully realigned, and gentle pressure was applied for 1 min using tweezers to ensure full contact along the cut interface. Similarly, for alginate scaffolds, the mold was removed and moved to a glass slide that was positioned on a 37 °C hot plate to maintain physiological temperature during handling. A clean cut was made and an attempt at adherence was made using tweezers for 1 min.

SEM

Surface morphology of the granular POMaC scaffolds was examined in four conditions: control (uncut), uninjured, immediately post-injury, and two weeks post-healing. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) characterization was performed using a Hitachi SU 3500 VPSEM, under high vacuum conditions, with a 1.5 kV beam energy, using a large working distance of between ∼27 mm and 31 mm, with a spot intensity of 30. Images were acquired using charge suppression scan (CSS) image capture conditions.

Mechanical testing

Compressive mechanical testing was performed within 1-2 days of formation using a MicroTester G2 system (CellScale) to evaluate the mechanical behavior of granular POMaC scaffolds and bulk POMaC slabs. A tungsten microbeam (outer diameter: 0.5588 mm) equipped with a flexible 2 × 2 cm plate (51 μm thick) was mounted on the instrument according to the manufacturer's protocols. The testing plate was carefully lowered until it made light contact with the sample surface. Cyclic compression tests involved applying 10–20% strain over three consecutive cycles. Each cycle included a 30-s loading phase, a 10-s hold, a 30-s unloading phase, and a final 10-s rest. Force and displacement were recorded at 1-s intervals during the test. Compressive modulus was calculated by dividing the force by the beam's cross-sectional area and estimating strain from the observed displacement. Hysteresis curves were also examined to assess energy dissipation and recovery across different material groups.

In vitro cell culture and imaging

GFP-expressing human umbilical vein endothelial cells (GFP-HUVECs; cAP-0001GFP, Angioproteomie) were cultured in Endothelial Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2, ScienCell). Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs; PT-5025, Lonza) were maintained in low-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA), 1% GlutaMAX™, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin [58]. Media changes were performed every 48 h. GFP-HUVECs and DPSCs were suspended at a 1:1 ratio to a final concentration of 1.6 × 106 cells/mL and were mixed with bovine fibrinogen (40 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich). Thrombin (4 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the cell-fibrinogen mixture at a 1:1 ratio to initiate gelation. A total volume of 20 μL of the resulting cell-laden gel was seeded into non-adherent 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow gel stabilization, followed by EGM-2 addition. Constructs were cultured for up to 6 days and evaluated by fluorescence microscopy, confocal imaging, and immunostaining for endothelial (CD31), mural (αSMA), and nuclear (DAPI) markers to assess vascular organization and scaffold integration. On day 6 of culture, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 1 h at room temperature, washed 3 times in PBS (5 min each), permeabilized with 0.3% Triton for 20 min, and blocked in 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h. Primary antibodies CD31 (1:100; Agilent Technologies; M082301-2) or αSMA (1:500; Invitrogen; 14-9760-80) were applied for 24 h at 4 °C in the dark. After 3 PBS washes (5 min each), Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-Mouse IgG (1:500; Invitrogen; A-21422) was incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, followed by 3 PBS washes. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:1000, Invitrogen, D1306) for 15 min. The tissues were imaged with a Leica Stellaris 5 Lightsheet Confocal Microscope at 20x magnification.

In vitro remolding of granular scaffolds

Similarly to rectangular molds, a triangular mold was prepared by cutting a flat PDMS sheet (1.1 mm × 0.9 mm × 0.3 mm). The PDMS cut-out was placed flat in a 35 mm petri dish and filled until the top edge of the PDMS mold with polyurethane resin (Vytaflex 30, Smooth-On), then cured overnight. The next day, the PDMS cut-out was removed and the Vytaflex mold was ready to use. Next, using forceps, a pre-made rectangular granular POMaC scaffold was manually remolded into the triangular mold, washed once with DPBS (no calcium or magnesium). The mold was then removed leaving a triangle-shaped POMaC scaffold (Video S4). Confocal imaging was performed on scaffolds before and after remolding using a 405 nm laser excitation wavelength. Z-stack images were collected with 25 slices per sample, with a step size of 10 μm between slices, resulting in an overall imaging depth of approximately 250 μm per stack. For each slice, the maximum intensity projection was used to generate a Z-project stack of all slices. Brightness and contrast were adjusted uniformly across images to enhance visualization while maintaining consistency between samples. All images were exported and saved in RGB TIFF format for further analysis. Pore interconnectivity was assessed by measuring the interstitial void spaces formed between adjacent particles using ImageJ. For pores formed by the formation of three particles, three measurements were taken from one edge of the particle, making a perpendicular extension towards the opposing edge of the void space. For pores formed by the formation of four particles, 2 measurements were obtained from one edge of the particle, making a perpendicular extension towards the opposing particle. At least 300 pores were analyzed per sample to determine the average interstitial pore size.

In Vivo implantation and remodeling

To assess how granular scaffolds can be remolded in a physiological environment, subcutaneous implantation studies were conducted using adult female (n = 4) athymic nude mice (7–8 weeks old, Charles River Laboratories). All animal procedures were approved by the University of Toronto Animal Care Committee (Protocol #20012969). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and two 1 cm dorsal incisions were made to create subcutaneous pockets. Rectangular scaffolds (granular and bulk POMaC) were implanted into each site. Twenty-four hours post-implantation, a manual deformation was applied to each implant to induce a transition from a rectangular to a circular shape. Following the 24-h implantation period, animals were euthanized, and the scaffolds were retrieved by careful dissection from the surrounding tissue. The explanted materials were imaged under an upright microscope and evaluated for morphological changes to determine shape retention and remodeling capacity.

To assess the long-term cell integration, separate scaffolds were implanted as described above and maintained for 4 weeks. After 4 weeks, mice were euthanized, and the scaffolds were gently dissected from the subcutaneous tissue. Samples were then removed for imaging and histological/immunohistochemical studies.

Histology and immunohistochemistry for In Vivo samples

Scaffolds explanted at 4 weeks were placed in a 10% neutral-buffered formalin solution overnight. Samples were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned serially (4 μm thickness), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunofluorescence staining, 4 μm thick sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated prior to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer for 25 min. Following blocking with Protein Block (Agilent Dako) to reduce nonspecific background staining, sections were incubated overnight with antibodies for CD68 (Abcam ab125212, 1:500) or CD206 (Abcam ab64693, 1:500). Primary antibody binding was visualized using Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen A21245, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000, Thermo Fischer Scientific) and mounted using Fluorescence Mounting Medium (Agilent Dako). For immunohistochemical staining, the slides were deparaffinized by passing slides through five changes of xylene, followed by rehydration through a graded series of ethanol to distilled water. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was then performed. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched via incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Following blocking with 2.5% normal horse serum, sections were incubated overnight at room temperature with primary antibodies for CD 31 (Novus NB-100-2284; 1:300; rabbit polyclonal). Slides were developed using the ImmPRESS® HRP Horse Anti-Rabbit IgG Polymer Detection Kit (Vector Laboratories MP-7401) which provides an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, following the manufacturer's protocol, followed by colour development with DAB (Dako). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted using MM23 mounting media (Leica). Fluorescence images were digitally acquired with a Zeiss AxioObserver microscope attached to a Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash 4.0 camera under a 20X magnification objective. Brightfield images were acquired using the Aperio AT2 brightfield scanner (Leica Biosystems) at 20X magnification. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ (CD31 images) or a custom MATLAB script (CD68 and CD206 images).

Cell infiltration into the scaffold was quantified using ImageJ. Six measurements were taken along different axes through each implanted scaffold, where the measurements were always performed along the axis perpendicular to the scaffold edge. The maximum infiltration depth was determined and expressed as a percentage of the total scaffold thickness.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 10. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation unless stated differently. The Shapiro–Wilk test was utilized to evaluate normality for all datasets subjected to statistical comparisons. Considering that the majority of experimental groups had small sample numbers (n = 3–4), normality testing was approached with caution due to its limited statistical power at low n.

Unpaired Welch's t-tests were performed for pairwise comparisons where the data conformed to a normal distribution. In instances of non-normally distributed data, unpaired nonparametric Mann–Whitney tests were utilized.

For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used when the data were normally distributed.

Two-way ANOVA was conducted for trials with two independent variables, utilizing the Geisser–Greenhouse correction when applicable, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test to address multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 and depicted in figures as ∗ p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
