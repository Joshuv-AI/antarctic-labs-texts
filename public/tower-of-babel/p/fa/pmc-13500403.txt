A Proposed Standardized Scoring Equation for Murine Models of Colitis
Authors: Lauren Mayer, Maddy Frech, Nneze Ekowa, David L Boone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWithin the inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) research community, histological scoring of colitis is an important grading system to determine colitis severity in pre-clinical mouse models of IBD. However, there is currently no set standard for grading the severity of colitis in mouse models. There are currently 30 types of scoring systems within the IBD research community.PurposeIn this paper, we propose a simple, 4-characteristic scoring equation based on one of the most popular scoring systems in the community to streamline and advance the research on pre-clinical models of colitis.MethodsUsing multiple linear regression, we found that 4 of 8 canonical characteristics of colitis are particularly significant to produce a simplified model equation with a fit of R2 = 0.908.ResultsUpon testing, this model appears to fare well within both non-ulcerative and ulcerative mouse models of colitis as a severity scoring system of colitis.ConclusionThe Boone–Mayer Scoring System appears to be a sound proposal for standardizing scoring severity of murine models of IBD across pre-clinical IBD laboratories. Introduction

Standardized histological scoring systems are widely used clinically, for example in grading tumor characteristics or other diseases. In IBD, standardized clinical scoring for ulcerative colitis includes the Geboes Score, the Nancy Index, or Robarts Histopathology Index, which are used in clinical trials and for patient monitoring and determination of histological remission [1]. Pre-clinical mouse models of IBD have been essential for subsequent development of human IBD therapies, but there is no set standard system for grading the severity of colitis in mouse models. Instead, it is frequently the case that individual labs set histopathology criteria to determine colitis severity in their mouse models [2]. This lack of a standard scoring system leads to incongruity within the IBD research community and hinders effective pre-clinical mouse models of IBD.

The Koelink et al. scoring system is well cited and serves as a severity scoring system for inflammatory diseases of the colon, especially in mouse models of disease [3]. Moreover, it fares well with immunocompetent and innate immune-mediated forms of colitis [4, 5]. Despite its efficacy, versatility, and high validity, Koelink’s 4-characteristic mouse colitis histology index (MCHI) is not an agreed-upon standard among all IBD laboratories [3]. The original scoring system was utilized over the MHCI which omits abscess number in score calculation due to preliminary evidence of correlation between abscess number and colitis severity within RAG1−/− innate colitis models. In this paper, we create a scoring system based on 4 key characteristics of the original 8 Koelink et al. scoring system and house it freely on a website to aid in streamlining the IBD community on a simplified model for colitis severity scoring.

Methods

Generation of Scoring Equation: Training Data

~ 300 pathology scores generated previously from H&E-stained histological sections of the cecum, proximal colon, and distal colon of colitis and healthy mouse lines were compiled into a singular.csv file consisting of columns with each of the 8 characteristics from the Koelink et al. scoring system plus a column for overall score and number of abscesses counted. Using RStudio version 2023.12.1 + 402 and R version 4.4.0—“Puppy Cup,” a multiple linear regression model was generated using the lm() function from this training data.csv file.

Model Fit

A model of best fit utilizing only the number of abscesses to generate overall score was initially generated using lm(). Hierarchical ANOVA was then performed to determine when residuals and number of colitis characteristics were both minimized while maintaining a high R2 for general goodness of fit. The maximum number of characteristics permitted was 4 out of the original 8 from Koelink et al. to produce a simplified multiple linear regression model of the original scoring system.

Testing on Innate Colitis Mouse Model

We have developed a robust, early-onset innate immune-mediated model of colitis involving mice expressing TNFAIP3 under the villin promoter (villin-TNFAIP3) crossed to mice lacking adaptive immunity (RAG-1−/−). We have also shown that these mice develop colitis in the absence of type 3 innate lymphoid cells (RORC−/− × villin-TNFAIP3 × RAG-1−/−) [4]. Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) stained slides of villin-TNFAIP3 × RAG-1−/− background mice and RORc−/− × villin-TNFAIP3+/0 × RAG1−/− mice with(out) antibiotic treatment were scored blindly by 2 researchers utilizing the Koelink et al. method and 1 researcher utilizing the Boone–Mayer scoring system to determine overall colitis severity score. Interobserver variability was determined by performing (non)parametric t-tests for difference in average score as well as percentage of re-scores that were issued to researchers utilizing the Boone–Mayer Score by dividing the number of sections scored outside of range by Boone–Mayer score by the total number of sections scored.

Testing on IL-10−/− Colitis Mouse Model

Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) stained slides of IL-10−/− B6 mice were scored blindly using the Boone–Mayer Score and compared to scores generated by Koelink et al. methodology. Interobserver variability was determined by performing Student t-tests for differences in average severity scores utilizing the Boone–Mayer methodology and Koelink et al. methodology. This process was repeated for two additional experiments (“Experiment 4” and “Experiment 5”). (Experiment names were arbitrary to the scorers as to remain blinded to treatments of the mice being scored.)

This equation uses half of the canonical markers of colitis proposed by Koelink et al. and produced an R2 value of 0.908 with a “re-score” rate of 9.64% against the original Koelink et al. system when the standard deviation was set to ≥ 1.5.

Equation 1 describes Boone–Mayer Scoring Equation for Colitis Severity.

Historically, when scores vary by 3 or more points between scorers, the standard deviation of average scores is greater than or equal to 1.8 standard deviations. When scores vary by 2.3 or more points between scorers, the standard deviation of average scores among the group is greater than or equal to 1.5 standard deviations. Boone–Mayer Score resulted in a theoretical (optimal) re-score rate of ~2% when the desired spread is less than or equal to 1.8 standard deviations from the mean (Table 3).

Testing Model on Non-ulcerative Colitis Scoring

Training data for the Boone–Mayer Score was performed at random on various mice from mouse lines of the c57Bl/6 RAG1−/− background (healthy immunocompromised mice) and RAG1−/− × villin-TNFAIP3+/0 background mice[4, 5]. Testing data for validity of the Boone–Mayer Score on innate immune-mediated models of colitis were performed scoring 181 colonic sections of new mice on vilin-TNFAIP3+/0 × RAG1−/− C57Bl/6 backgrounds not previously utilized in the training dataset. Compared to scorers utilizing the Koelink et al. method, the Boone–Mayer Score produced a re-score error rate of 16.0% at a standard deviation of > = 1.8 and 19.9% when the standard deviation was required to be < = 1.5 (Table 4). Figure 1 shows the types of mouse models and experiments in which the Boone–Mayer Score was tested against the Koelink et al. scoring system for non-ulcerative models of colitis. No statistical significance was found between the average Boone–Mayer Score and the average score of all 3 scorers combined at an alpha level of 0.05. (Fig. 1).

Interobserver Versus Intraobserver Variability Results

Intraobserver variability has also been a subject of interest regarding the severity scoring of colitis in murine models. While scoring is inherently qualitative and subjective observation, intraobserver variability between scorers can never be eliminated or negligible. A main objective of this study was to essentially eliminate interobserver variability between the Boone–Mayer scoring system and the Koelink et al. system. To test if this was successfully accomplished, an ANOVA was run for each of these studies to determine if the mean score produced by the Boone–Mayer scoring equation significantly differed from that using the Koelink et al. methodology. (After average scoring of each project was completed, to eliminate any bias, the individual subscores for each of the 8 characteristics was obtained by the scorer for further analysis.) Fig. 2 demonstrates how the same subscores for the 4 characteristics of interest in the Boone–Mayer scoring system fared against the 8 characteristic scoring system by Koelink et al. to evaluate intraobserver variability. Using the same sub-characteristic scores for crypt hyperplasia, crypt density, abcess count, and inflammatory infiltrate yielded statistically significant different scores between the Boone–Mayer and Koelink et al. methods only once.

Testing Model on Ulcerative Colitis Scoring

Innate immune-mediated mouse models of colitis lack the adaptive branch of their immune systems. These immunodeficient mice rarely develop ulcers as a result, leading to a non-ulcerative model of colitis. As this scoring system was trained on RAG1−/− mice only, we aimed to see if our scoring system could be expanded to ulcerative/immunocompetent mouse models of colitis.

Figure 3 and Table 5 show, respectively, the testing results and re-score rates of scoring IL10−/− × WT B6 and untreated WT B6 mouse colonic sections. In total, 258 colonic sections (cecums, proximal colons, and distal colons) were scored by one lab member using the original Koelink et al. method and another using the Boone–Mayer scoring system. To prevent potential bias, scores between scorers were kept confidential until all 258 sections were completed. Experimental treatments/procedures were also kept blind to the scorers for Koelink methodology and Boone–Mayer scoring. Mouse groups were composed of male & female IL10−/− mice treated with various levels of atorvastatin followed by DSS to induce IBD. (These results have not yet been published.) There appeared to be no statistically significant difference between the scoring systems on these immunocompetent, ulcerative mice at the alpha level = 0.01 (Fig. 3).

Testing Model on Chemically Induced Mouse Models of Colitis

To validate the Boone–Mayer Scoring System across various etiologies of colitis mouse models, we aimed to see if our scoring system could be expanded to chemically induced mouse models of colitis, specifically dextran sulfate sodium-induced (DSS-induced) and 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS-induced) mouse models of colitis (Fig. 4). Moreover, to look at feasibility of first-time histological scorers adapting the Boone–Mayer Scoring system, we onboarded a novice Boone–Mayer scorer with approximately one month of exposure to Boone–Mayer Scoring System as well as colitis histopathology in general. Overall, we saw no difference between the expert Koelink et al. scorer and expert Boone–Mayer scorer at the alpha level = 0.05. Finally, with few trainings, we saw a trend toward no statistical significance in novice Boone–Mayer scorer compared to the Koelink et al. scorer as there was no statistical significance between levels or scoring systems in the TNBS mice.

Discussion

In 2017, Mosli et al. described 30 different scoring methods for human patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) [1]—exemplifying the issue of a lack of standards within the IBD research community at the pre-clinical and clinical levels. Mosli et al.’s objectives were to identify the most validated scoring models currently available. They concluded that out of the 30 scoring systems available for clinical research in UC patients, only 11 have had a form of formal systematic validation [1]. Of those 11 with some validation, only 2 (the Nancy Index and Robarts Histopathology Index) were rigorously validated on the criteria of inter/intra-rater reliability, validity in measuring colitis accurately, responsiveness to changes in colitis severity, and ease of use [1].

Similarly, 18 well-cited scoring systems of colitis in mouse models were identified, including 8 different indices for DSS-induced colitis [6]. Each of these scoring methods used different combinations of canonical characteristics of colitis with different subscoring formats, including percentages (e.g., ulceration percentage), binary (1-present or 0-absent), and integer scaling (e.g., 0–3) [6]. These differences in scoring systems contribute to variability among laboratories and are a barrier to meta-analyses, comparisons of colitis severity, and assessment of effectiveness of pre-clinical therapies.

Erben et al. proposed a streamlined scoring system for the broader GI research community centered on inflammation research in mouse models which highlighted 3 canonical categories of intestinal inflammation within mice: inflammatory cell infiltrate, epithelial changes in the gut structure, and mucosal architecture [7]. The latter two were further sub-characterized by identifying crypt hyperplasia; goblet cell numbers; cryptitis; neutrophil location; erosion of the top layer of the crypts; ulceration; granulation of tissue; crypt density; and villi structure [7]. Erben et al. caveated that the most accurate way to score mouse models may depend on the mechanism of inducing colitis in the mouse (i.e., chemically induced through DSS/TNBS, genetic mouse models, T cell transfer models, IL10−/− mice, etc.), emphasizing the obstacles with creating a standardized scoring methodology of inflammation in the GI/IBD research community [7].

In 2024, Remke et al. expanded the efforts of standardizing colitis scoring in mouse models to include models with the tendency to manifest into colon cancer [8]. Their scoring system included immune cell infiltration, tissue damage, and tissue structure, with each category further subdivided into characteristics including locational leukocyte infiltration, abscesses/ulcers, and hyperplasia/carcinomas [8]. Total score was calculated by summing each sub-characteristic grade to produce an overall colitis severity score out of 12 [8]. Remke et al.’s model suggested strong resistance against interobserver variability with high reliability between blind scores generated by varying expertise (a PhD student, experienced pathologist, and veteran pathologist). Remke et al. state that subjective observations may still prove to be an issue during scoring as one scorer may dictate inflammatory infiltration where another scores lower by concluding the abundance of leukocytes is due to a Peyer’s patch, for example [8].

In comparison, the Boone–Mayer Scoring System returned intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) between 0.473 and 0.927, suggesting on average the Boone–Mayer Scoring System accounts for 47.3% to 92.7% of true variation in histological scores not due to random chance variations. This suggests that there is statistically “moderate to good reliability” agreement between the Boone–Mayer Scoring System and the Koelink et al. Scoring System. In regard to therapeutic effects (type of colitis mouse model induced), the innate systems comparisons produced ICCs of 0.705, 0.714, and 0.793. In the immunocompetent mouse models, IL10−/− × WT mice experiments returned ICCs of 0.51, 0.788, and 0.927, and chemically induced models of colitis experiments returned ICCs of 0.682 and 0.473. These scores further highlight the trend in good reliability utilizing the Boone–Mayer Scoring System as a simplified version of the Koelink et al. method.

Many published articles creating novel scoring systems for colitis cite DSS-induced models of colitis as their source for generating their methodology [2, 6, 9]. However, these methods exclude immunodeficient models of colitis as well as other ulcerative models such as IL10−/− mice. Promising new models approach the problem of a wide gamut of IBD and colorectal cancer [8] scoring, but more researchers are needed to cite their work. These authors’ works are valued and important to the field, but the transition from novelty to streamlined scoring consistency is essential moving forward in the use of mouse models of colitis.

Conclusions

We propose this scoring equation as a standard for scoring mouse models of colitis in the IBD community. The similar average scores between scores generated with the Koelink et al. system and the Boone–Mayer equation in mouse models with the presence of abscess/ulcerative-like pathology suggest it is a simplified adaptation of this well-cited scoring system with half of the initial 8 subscores. This, along with its web-based calculator (https://sites.nd.edu/lauren-mayer/), will streamline the process of scoring and allow for mobile scoring outside of the laboratory. We are optimistic that this proposed scoring system may help unify the IBD community to advance therapeutic research for colitis treatments.

With the rise of AI and image analysis software, scientists have recently turned to automation of image severity scoring using computer programs. The Definiens Developer platform was able to produce an 88% similarity score compared to pathologists’ scoring the same H&E sections [10] in DSS-induced models of colitis. Despite the promising results from Kozolowski et al., their work as of this print has been cited 15 times in over a decade since its publication. Moreover, Klopfleisch et al. were inspired by Kozolowski et al.’s work to expand scoring systems in the colitis research community but discovered that Definiens Tissue Studio could not accurately segment H&E-stained images, highlighting the limitations for predefined, open-source workflows which serve as an enticing option for pathologists and biologists whose time is limited to become well-versed in programming [6]. These types of image analysis software appeared to correlate well with pathologists’ scores when the type of staining is color dependent (IHC-based staining) instead of H&E morphologically based [6]. However, H&E-staining tends to be the default way to interpret disease severity as H&E-staining illuminates morphology of the subject and often singular antibodies serving as holistic disease markers are unknown or unavailable.

With the rapid advancement of machine learning and AI over the past couple of years, we believe the challenges of H&E analysis in image analysis software challenges brought on due to their morphological incongruities during cutting or staining [6] will be resolved in the near future. Specifically, the results from Ohara et al. regarding high accuracy in neutrophil identification in H&E-stained slides as well as the generation of another scoring system from neutrophil counts [11] within patient biopsy H&E-stained slides augment our confidence in the projected future of a standardized AI automation to score pre-clinical trials in mouse models of colitis. We are currently in contact with collaborators at the University of Notre Dame regarding the performance of image analysis using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on our H&E mouse colons with the goal to incorporate this machine learning into a web app linked to the site included in this paper where users may upload an image and automatically receive a score for that section. Ideally, the idea of a web-based automated image analysis for a wide gamut of applications including various IBD and colorectal cancers would be the aim of the future, and eventually, a new standard for even more GI pre-clinical research.
