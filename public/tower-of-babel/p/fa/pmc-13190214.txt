Learning curve, safety and diagnostic yield of the GalaxyTM, Noah Medical Group robotic bronchoscopy platform
Authors: Lamia Aljundi, Ara Vartanyan, Tingyu Yang, Ching-Fei Chang, Shiqian Li, Monika Kakol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNoah Medical Group (Reseda, CA) obtained Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for the robotic-assisted bronchoscopy (RAB) GalaxyTM System in 2023. The platform utilizes electromagnetic navigation (EMN) and integrated digital tomosynthesis (DT) for precise nodule targeting. Real-world data on its performance remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic yield and safety of the GalaxyTM system in the first year of its integration in an academic center.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-center study at an academic interventional pulmonary program, reviewing RAB procedures performed with the GalaxyTM system between February 2024 and February 2025. The primary outcomes were strict diagnostic yield and safety. Secondary analyses evaluated the impact of operator learning curve, biopsy modality [1.1-mm cryoprobe, 21-gauge fine-needle aspiration (FNA), and forceps], and nodule characteristics.ResultsNinety-one patients with 94 nodules (median size 2.1 cm) were included. Overall diagnostic yield was 68%. Yield improved from 61% in the first six months to 75% in the latter half of the study [P=0.44, odds ratio (OR) =1.289]. The 1.1-mm cryoprobe achieved the highest yield (80.6%), outperforming FNA (42.9%, P=0.01, OR =5.543) and forceps (51.2%, P=0.046, OR =3.867). Pneumothorax occurred in 13% of cases (all requiring chest tubes), and one case of self-limited massive hemoptysis was reported.ConclusionsIn this real-world descriptive study, the GalaxyTM system demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to the other EMN RAB. Outcomes were influenced by a learning curve and trainee participation. Routine use of the 1.1-mm cryoprobe may optimize yield.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This real-world, non-sponsored study evaluates the performance of the GalaxyTM robotic-assisted bronchoscopy (RAB) platform during its first year of implementation at an academic center. Ninety-one patients (94 nodules) were included resulting in a strict diagnostic yield of 68%. Complications included pneumothorax in 13% of patients and one case of self-limited massive hemoptysis. The observed pneumothorax rate is comparable to that reported in the FRONTIER study (11%) and may reflect the procedural learning curve; in contrast to FRONTIER, this study uniquely involved substantial participation of trainees, which may have further contributed to the observed complication rate.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior to this study, data on the GalaxyTM platform were limited to two highly controlled trials (FRONTIER and MATCH2), which involved experienced operators without trainee participation and excluded ground-glass nodules.

• Our study reflects a pragmatic clinical setting with active trainee involvement. With these differences, our diagnostic yield was comparable to that reported in the large multicenter TARGET trial evaluating the other electromagnetic navigation-guided RAB platform (MONARCH), which demonstrated a diagnostic yield of 61.6%.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Adoption strategies for RAB should be structured to account for the learning curve—initially concentrating procedural experience with a single faculty operator before gradually incorporating trainees under experienced supervision. Additionally, our study highlights the significance of the 1.1-mm cryoprobe as a primary biopsy tool given its superior diagnostic yield.

Introduction

Lung cancer screening reduces mortality by 16% to 24% through early detection in high-risk individuals (1,2), yet uptake remains low, with roughly 6% adherence reported in 2020 (3). However, with rising awareness and expansion of screening programs, lung cancer screening adherence has increased, with adherence up to 18% in a more recent 2025 report (4). With the increased detection of pulmonary nodules, there has been a corresponding rise in referrals for tissue sampling (5). Traditionally, transthoracic needle aspiration (TTNA) has demonstrated higher diagnostic yields compared to bronchoscopic approach, albeit with a greater risk of complications (6-9). The VERITAS trial, however, has demonstrated that electromagnetic navigational (EMN) bronchoscopy with the aid of digital tomosynthesis (DT) is non-inferior to computed tomography (CT)-guided TTNA with diagnostic accuracy of 79% and significantly less risk for pneumothorax requiring chest tube in the navigation bronchoscopy group (0.8% versus 11.5 %) (10). Recently, the emergence of robotic-assisted bronchoscopy (RAB) has expanded the diagnostic capabilities for pulmonary nodules by offering enhanced maneuverability and improved sampling stability (11). A meta-analysis of 27 studies—predominantly evaluating only two RAB platforms, EMN-guided and shape-sensing systems—reported a pooled diagnostic yield of 69.6% (12).

In 2023, Noah Medical Group (Reseda, CA, USA) obtained Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for the GalaxyTM System. The system utilizes a single use bronchoscope with a 4.0-mm outer diameter and 2.1-mm working channel. This platform relies on EMN and provides integrated DT by incorporating readily available C-arm fluoroscopy while maintaining vision capabilities. DT similar in principle to CT imaging, reconstructs a 3D image by capturing a series of X-rays from multiple angles. However, unlike conventional CT, which uses a full 360° rotation, DT typically acquires images over a narrower arc (15°–60°), allowing for the use of a standard fluoroscopy C-arm rather than a dedicated CT scanner (13,14). DT is a helpful tool in overcoming CT to body divergence, an issue that is a major hurdle in RAB at a lower cost than fixed and mobile cone beam CT (15,16).

Compared to other robotic EMN platforms, data on the diagnostic yield and safety of the GalaxyTM System remains limited, with only two peer reviewed publication addressing these outcomes. The FRONTIER study was the first-in-human study by Saghaie et al. that evaluated 18 patients and sampled 19 pulmonary nodules, reporting a strict diagnostic yield of 89.5%. Two pneumothoraces were observed, one of which required chest tube placement (17). More recently, the MATCH 2 study described the experience with the GalaxyTM platform in a nonrandomized trial conducted at two high-volume centers, reporting a diagnostic yield of 96.7% and a pneumothorax rate of 3% (18). Notably, ground-glass nodules were explicitly excluded from this study.

In this manuscript, we describe our experience and learning curve with the GalaxyTM system over the first year of usage in an academic interventional pulmonary (IP) training program. To our knowledge, this is the first real-world, non-sponsored account of this platform utilization. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2700/rc).

Methods

We reviewed the diagnostic yield and complications of all RAB performed at Keck Hospital of University of Southern California (USC), Los Angeles, California, USA using the GalaxyTM robotic system since its acquisition in February 2024 until February 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The Keck Medicine of USC Institutional Review Board (IRB) exemption was granted given the observational and retrospective nature of the project.

Peri-procedural CT chest scan images as well as positron emission tomography (PET) scans were reviewed to obtain data regarding nodule features, location, presence of a bronchus sign and PET avidity of nodules as well as mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes. Location of nodules was classified as inner, middle and outer third of the hemithorax per hilar circles strategy (19,20).

Patients who underwent RAB for the evaluation of pulmonary nodules were included in the study. Those undergoing RAB solely for fiducial marker placement were excluded. Diagnostic classification followed strict criteria consistent with the Delphi consensus definition of diagnostic yield by the American Thoracic Society and American College of Chest Physicians Research Statement (21). Most of the patients with non-diagnostic samples were reviewed at the multidisciplinary tumor board. Subsequent management decisions included CT-guided biopsy, surgical intervention, or interval imaging, based on clinical context and imaging findings.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic yield and safety profile of RAB utilizing the GalaxyTM platform. Secondary endpoints included assessment of factors influencing diagnostic yield and safety, such as learning curve, use of different diagnostic tools, and characteristics of the target nodules, including size, location, presence of bronchus sign and radiographic features.

Procedure description

The GalaxyTM system requires a preprocedural CT chest scan with 0.8 to 1 mm thickness and 0–50% overlap. The chest CT scan is then uploaded to the navigational software that creates a 3D virtual plan to the target nodule. Subsequent registration takes place during navigation and EMN guides the system within 2 cm of the target lesion. Following this, proprietary DT, which is referred to as Tool-in-Lesion Technology (TiLT+TM) is utilized via a standard fluoroscopy C-arm to confirm the tool’s precise positioning relative to the lesion.

RAB utilizing the GalaxyTM robot was performed in the bronchoscopy suite under general anesthesia (GA). Patients were placed on an EMN-compatible bed where the EMN board was positioned underneath the patient. Patients were intubated with a size 8.5-mm endotracheal (ET) tube as per the institution protocol to facilitate mediastinal staging by curvilinear endobronchial ultrasound after the RAB portion of the procedure is completed. Bronchoscopy and therapeutic aspiration of airway secretions was performed with Olympus therapeutic bronchoscope (BF-1TH190, Olympus America, Inc., Cypress, CA, USA). Ventilator settings were set as follows in order to reduce atelectasis in accordance with the VESPA trial (22), albeit with some modifications: tidal volume of 8 mg/kg of ideal body weight, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 8 to 12 cmH2O and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) less than 60%. No recruitment maneuvers were performed after intubation. Next, the robotic bronchoscope was docked, and registration was performed. Once the distal tip of the robotic bronchoscope was within 2 to 3 cm of the target lesion we attempted to isocenter the C-arm at the site of the lesion in three planes: antero-posterior (AP), 30 degrees right anterior oblique (RAO) and 30 degrees left anterior oblique (LAO). Once we were satisfied with the position of the C-arm, an inspiratory hold using the “vital capacity” feature on the piston ventilator was utilized with an inspiratory pressure of 20 cmH2O. Next, the C-arm was manually rotated from 30 degrees LAO to 30 degrees RAO to obtain a reconstructed 3D image. The reconstructed image was then reviewed by the operator who would identify the target lesion and allow the software to update the target for biopsy. In most of the cases, radial endobronchial ultrasound (r-EBUS) was also utilized to confirm target lesion location. DT was not routinely used to demonstrate tool in lesion as this required the patient to be apneic for prolonged period and hence was not deemed necessary as part of RAB at our institution. After biopsies were obtained with fluoroscopy guidance using different tools: 21-gauge PeriView FLEX transbronchial fine-needle aspiration (FNA) needle (Olympus, Olympus America), flexible single use 1.1-mm cryoprobe (ERBECRYO® 2, Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany), Super DimensionTM (Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA) biopsy forceps or cytology brush, rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) by cytotechnologist determined the adequacy of the samples. Biopsy samples were obtained either with or without a breath-hold, based on the operator’s discretion and the degree of divergence. When a breath-hold was used, it was achieved either by activating the “vital capacity” feature on the piston ventilator or by having the anesthesiologist perform a manual breath-hold while maintaining a PEEP of 20 to 30 cmH2O through manual occlusion of the ventilator rebreathing bag.

During the initial six months of implementing the platform, RAB was performed by three IP faculty members in conjunction with an IP fellow. In the subsequent six-month period, following the departure of one faculty, the remaining two continued to perform RAB procedures alongside a newly appointed IP fellow. A representative member of the GalaxyTM team was usually present to assist bronchoscopy suite respiratory therapists set up for the procedure as well as during the procedure to help trouble-shoot if needed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by a trained statistician using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and significance was set to P value <0.05. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) model was used to evaluate the relationship between diagnostic yield and learning curve, diagnostic tools, presence of bronchus signs and nodule characteristics including size, location and radiographic features while accounting for repeated measures within each nodule. In addition, generalized estimating equations and logistic regression models were utilized to assess relationships between pneumothorax rate and learning curve, nodule and patient characteristics.

The mean procedure time, defined as the interval from robotic docking to undocking, was 58 minutes (range, 24–227 minutes). Mean fluoroscopy time was 2 minutes, and the mean radiation dose (cumulative air kerma) was 16.7 milligray (mGy).

Discussion

Our one-year experience with the GalaxyTM system represents a real-world learning curve for a team of interventional pulmonologists and their fellow at an academic center adopting a new RAB platform. There is limited data in the literature describing the GalaxyTM platform diagnostic yield and safety. To our knowledge, this is the first publication looking at the real-world performance characteristics of the GalaxyTM system. Unlike the FRONTIER and MATCH 2 studies, our experience yielded a lower diagnostic yield. However, while both studies were prospective trials in which patients were selected specifically for the trial, our encounter is more representative of real-world experience.

When comparing our experience with the two published studies using the Galaxy platform, the FRONTIER study involved four proceduralists who assisted one another during RAB, a factor which may have contributed to a higher diagnostic yield given the cooperative efforts of experienced operators. In addition, 20% (N=19) of the nodules we sampled were non-solid, and although nodule characteristics did not have a statistical significance in determining diagnostic yield (Table 3), nodule density does impact image quality of the DT (23,24). On the other hand, the FRONTIER study did not describe the density of the nodules sampled, and hence if the nodules chosen for the study were strictly solid, this may have assisted in better image quality and as a result better diagnostic yield. In contrast, the more recent MATCH 2 study described the experience with the GalaxyTM platform in a nonrandomized trial conducted at two high-volume centers and explicitly excluded ground-glass nodules. Furthermore, the sample size in MATCH 2 trial was relatively small, and no trainees participated in the procedures, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to real-world clinical practice in academic centers. In addition, the operators had prior experience with the platform, whereas the present study reflects outcomes during the initial adoption phase.

Our diagnostic yield of 68% aligns with findings from a recent large multicenter study that included 715 patients by Murgu et al., which reported a 61.6% yield using the MONARCH system (Johnson & Johnson MedTech), another RAB platform that similarly utilizes EMN (25). In the TARGET trial, 14.3% of nodules sampled were pure ground glass, a category of nodules that was excluded from the MATCH 2 trial.

We anticipate continued improvement in our yield, particularly following the integration of the 1.1-mm cryoprobe which occurred in the last 3 to 4 months of the year. This tool has shown superior performance compared to forceps, achieving an 80% diagnostic yield versus 41.2% with the forceps (P=0.046) and 42.9% with 21-gauge FNA (P=0.01). Moreover, the 1.1-mm cryoprobe provides larger biopsy specimens, facilitating molecular analysis (25-28). Our findings are consistent with the results from Trujillo et al. where authors demonstrated superior yield with the 1.1-mm cryobiopsy opposed to FNA, albeit with shape sensing RAB (29). Notably, the cryoprobe was not used in the study by Murgu et al., which may have contributed to the lower yield despite the longer experience with the MONARCH platform.

The pneumothorax rate observed in our cohort was higher than expected but comparable to that reported in the FRONTIER study, where 2 of 18 patients (11%) experienced pneumothorax. However, a key distinction lies in management: only one patient in the FRONTIER study required chest tube placement, whereas all pneumothoraces in our series were treated with chest tube insertion. This discrepancy may reflect institutional practice patterns, as our center typically proceeds with chest tube placement even for relatively small pneumothoraces. Additionally, the FRONTIER study did not explicitly describe the ventilator settings utilized; hence, perhaps the ventilator settings used in our institution played a role in the development of post-procedural pneumothorax, particularly the manual inspiratory breath hold performed by the anesthesia team. Variability in the pressure applied to the rebreathing bag could have inadvertently caused advancement of the biopsy instrument beyond the intended depth.

Operator experience and procedural training may have also contributed to the observed complication rate. Throughout the study period, procedures were primarily performed by an IP fellow under direct faculty supervision. In the first half of the year, the fellow had approximately six months of prior experience using a different RAB platform before transitioning to the GalaxyTM system. In the second half of the year, a new fellow began training. While IP fellows generally possess advanced procedural skills, typically entering their fellowship at a seventh post-graduate year (PGY), trainee involvement has been associated with higher complication rates (30,31).

Additionally, existing literature indicates that the learning curve for RAB extends from 15 to 43 cases (32-34). With each of the IP faculty in our cohort performing 23 to 37 procedures within the first year, a substantial proportion of cases likely occurred during this learning phase, potentially accounting for the lower diagnostic yield and higher complication rate. Although our IP faculty had prior RAB experience on a different platform and completed Galaxy-specific training on porcine models prior to clinical use, the IP fellows were relatively novice. As such, it is possible that outcomes and complication rate may have differed with less fellow involvement during the early adoption phase, as faculty progressed along their learning curves. An alternative strategy could have involved allowing a single faculty member to accrue greater procedural experience before increasing fellow participation, with the fellow primarily paired with that operator, while other faculty continued to develop proficiency independently.

Furthermore, the GalaxyTM robotic system does not incorporate pleural border detection capabilities and therefore cannot inform the operator of the target’s proximity to the pleura or adjacent fissures. While no statistically significant association was found between nodule location and pneumothorax risk, several procedural factors may have contributed to its occurrence. Notably, the outer diameter of the GalaxyTM robotic bronchoscope is 4 mm, which may pose challenges when navigating and intubating smaller, more distal airways. This mechanical limitation could increase the risk of airway trauma and subsequent pneumothorax.

Conclusions

Here, we present the first real-world report of using the GalaxyTM system by Noah Medical for RAB biopsies of pulmonary nodules. Based on our initial diagnostic yield of 61%, which improved to 75% after 6 months of use, this system appears to be adaptable and provides comparable results to its EMN-platform competitor. The use of integrated DT can be a cost-effective alternative to cone beam CT and may contribute to increasing interest in this platform.

Nevertheless, this study is limited by its single-center design at an academic institution, which may not be representative of broader operator experiences with the GalaxyTM platform. Additionally, the relatively short duration of platform use may not fully capture its potential. Further studies from multiple centers with varying levels of experience are necessary to more accurately evaluate the effectiveness and clinical utility of the GalaxyTM platform.
