Soil Microbiome for Climate Smart Agriculture in Legumes: A Review
Authors: Kusum Raj Tamang, Sakina Mahdi, Prabesh Koirala, Joshua Yeboah Asiamah, Swastika Sharma, Shreesha Padyana, Christian B Carson, Babu Valliyodan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate-smart agriculture is a multidisciplinary farm model approach that helps reduce the impact of climate change not only on soil but on entire agricultural systems. One of the critical approaches of climate-smart agriculture is using the soil microbiome to help the crop adapt to the changing climate and increase crop production. The microorganisms that are present in the soil play a crucial role in ecosystem services, crop protection and performance, and productivity. Legumes are essential for their nutritional value and interaction with soil microbes, particularly in fixing atmospheric nitrogen and improving soil health through plant-microbe association. Beyond Rhizobium, a wide range of microorganisms and fungi assist legumes in adapting to climate stresses such as drought stress and salinity, which ultimately enhances biomass and legume yield. However, the molecular mechanisms behind the interactions between microbiomes and plant growth, ecosystem services, and carbon sequestration remain unclear in legumes. This review aims to explain the key roles of the soil microbiome, legume rhizobiome, and the microbial genes associated with functional plant traits that help plants adapt to a changing climate. Understanding how microbial populations shift due to climate variations and how these changes affect legume yields is crucial, given the ever-changing nature of ecosystems due to climatic shifts. Therefore, more research is warranted to explore plant microbial interactions, understand the dynamics of climatic shifts, and develop microbiome engineering tools for the improved health of crop and rhizobiome ecosystems. Furthermore, a parallel study is required to examine the harmful microbiome that causes crop destruction and antagonizes important bacteria.

Introduction to Climate Smart Agriculture (CSA)

Food demand has grown dramatically due to the global rise in population, and the issue is quickly becoming more critical than ever. These critical needs have intensified the agricultural output, but the problem remains [1–3]. Agriculture, including both livestock and crop cultivation, accounts for 10–12% of global anthropogenic emissions of greenhouse gases (GHGs), with non-CO2 emissions (comprising GHGs other than CO2) totalling 5.2–5.8 Carbon dioxide equivalent (GTCO2) per year [4]. Thus, there is a need to go for a more sustainable agriculture approach. The concept of Climate Smart Agriculture (CSA), an integrative approach that aims to reduce the impact of a changing climate on soils and agro-ecosystems, is vital to supporting sustainable agriculture worldwide [5, 6]. The unified framework of the CSA method intends to disseminate agricultural practices and innovations that boost crop output while enhancing climate change mitigation and reducing GHGs emissions [6, 7, 8]. The CSA can decrease GHGs emissions compared to farmers’ current practices by 7 to 23% of the global warming potential while achieving economic benefits of 42–129% [9]. The multi-objective approach of CSA also offers a helpful framework for decision-making at all levels, from the farm to the policy [10]. To evaluate the effectiveness of CSA, the World Bank and other organizations assess CSA indicators, which are divided into three major categories: (a) policy, (b) technology, and (c) results [11]. Each category tracks whether CSA objectives have been met and whether an intervention is required. Policy indicators evaluate how institutional and governance are supporting the policy for CSA; technology indicators look at the adoption and redevelopment of the CSA technologies promoted; and results indicators measure outcomes such as yield gains, resilience to climate change, and greenhouse gas reductions [12]. Under the technology section of the CSA approach, the microbiome affiliated technologies can be utilized to achieve CSA goal. Microbiome-associated technologies, such as microbial inoculants, mycorrhizal formulations, complementary microbial groups, and microbiome-informed management practices, can help scale up climate-smart agriculture technologies that have proven roles in improving productivity, enhancing climate resilience, and reducing the environmental pressures on agricultural systems [13].

Microbiomes refer to microbial organisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and their genetic materials and collective population [9]. Microbiomes, particularly the soil microbiome, are a significant variety that play a crucial role in the agricultural system. Despite being understudied, they hold significant ecological significance [14]. The most prevalent microorganisms in the soil, bacteria and fungi, have 102–104 times more biomass than the other major constituents of the soil microbiome - protists, archaea, and viruses [9]. These soil microbiomes enhance soil quality and serve as indicators to assess degradation trends, thereby aiding growers in managing their soil health efficiently [14].

Legumes are a climate-smart commodity and a significant plant-based and dietary protein producer [15]. Legumes are essential for climate-smart agriculture due to their multi-dimensional functionalities such as biological atmospheric nitrogen fixation, improvement in soil health, enhancemof ent system resilience, and overall contribution to sustainable and nutrition-secure agricultural systems [16]. In the face of climatic variability and increasing biotic and abiotic stress, legumes are predicted to experience critical reductions in growth and productivity [17]. Therefore, building a pr tical knowledge base on the association between legumes, microbiomes, and various biotic and abiotic stressors is necessary [18, 19, 20]. Thus this review aims to (i) summarize the functions of microorganisms that provide essential ecosystem services and promote key legume traits, (ii) examine how plant–microbiome associations could be fortified for better crop resilience, productivity, and soil health, (iii) highlight the molecular and physiological mechanisms driving key interactions, and (iv) discuss potential for microbiome engineering could be applied for better crop production and climate change adaptation. By addressing these objectives, we provide a comprehensive perspective on how legume–microbiome associations can be exploited to enhance sustainable and climate-resilient agricultural systems.

Legumes as a Nutritional and Climate Smart Crop

Legumes along with other crops are one of the most important crops for human and animal nutrition [21, 22, 23]. Legumes offer various dietary fibers, vitamins, and minerals and have significant health effects [15]. Furthermore, they consist of amino acids (histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine), fatty acids (linoleic acid and linolenic acid), eight macro elements (Na, N, K, Ca, Mg, S, P, and Cl), microelements (Fe, I, F, B, Se, Mo, Ni, Cr, V, Si, As, Sn, and Co), and vitamins (A, D, E, K, C, B1, B2, B3, niacin, B6, folate, biotin, and B12) [15]. All these elements are vital for good health. In addition, legumes contain a class of phytochemicals called isoflavones, particularly abundant in soybeans, which may lower the risk of cancer, heart disease, and osteoporosis, and help during menopause [24]. Additionally, they are a source of resistant starch, which bacteria in the large intestine break down to create short-chain fatty acids (like butyrate) used by intestinal cells as a dietary energy source [24]. In addition to the mature seeds, legumes are also cultivated for fresh consumption as pods or immature seeds [19]. These vegetable legumes are also high in protein, dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals, and might mitigate the effects of climate change while ensuring global food security [25, 26].

Legumes are an important crop in developing countries due to their nutritional value and role in addressing food insecurity. They can be cultivated with fewer resources, making them a more accessible protein source than animal-based protein [22]. Legumes constitute a significant portion of the crops consumed in the Sub-Saharan region. Although legumes such as Glycine max (soybeans) play a substantial role in the economy, native landraces and indigenous legumes, like cowpea, which are well-suited to the Sub-Saharan environment, hold great potential for ensuring food security [8, 27]. Cowpea is particularly promising in Sub-Saharan Africa due to its geographical adaptability. It can be consumed as tender leaves, fresh pods, and grains [27]. Furthermore, cowpeas are nutritionally remarkable, offering significant protein and carbohydrate content while being low in fat [28]. Considering this, we know that the enhancement of legume nutritional composition presents an appealing target for addressing the “hidden hunger” [15, 25]. In addition to their nutritional values, legumes also play an important ecological role in reducing the impact of agriculture on GHGs [29, 30]. Legume planting does this through symbiotic Nitrogen (N2) fixation. Legumes can add a significant amount of nitrogen to the soil through intercropping or crop rotation, increasing crop yields in low-input farming systems [31, 32, 33]. In a previous study, the total inputs of nitrogen in the soil after one growing season are 25–30 kg per hectare for groundnut monocropping and sorghum-cowpea intercropping and 120 kg per hectare for velvet bean-crotalaria intercropping [31]. Similarly, soybeans can fix atmospheric nitrogen at a rate of 130 kg per hectare [24, 25].

Legumes are widely used as cover crops to improve soil health and water use efficiency [26]. In addition to this, cover cropping with legumes can improve soil quality by reducing weed infestations, improving soil structure and hydraulic properties, increasing organic matter and nitrogen content, and limiting the use of pesticides and mineral fertilizers [34, 35]. As an example, studies in sugarcane showed that the sucrose yield increased by an average of 7% over nine sugarcane crops by growing soybeans, which were incorporated as green manure [29, 35]. Soybean cultivation in Brazil is highly profitable due to the use of inoculants, which supply the plant’s nitrogen requirements and enable the full expression of its genetic yield potential [36, 37].

Similarly, legumes are essential for crop rotation, nutrient availability, and promoting climate-smart agriculture to improve crop yields. For instance, when legumes were included in rotation with wheat and canola, the protein content in both wheat and canola also improved. In contrast, the total seed protein in wheat declined over time when legumes were excluded from the rotation [28]. Legumes also significantly improve water use efficiency in agricultural systems due to their beneficial traits, such as nitrogen fixation, deep root systems, and their ability to enhance soil organic carbon [28, 38, 39]. For example, legumes like peanuts improve water use efficiency when rotated with rice. Peanuts are highly efficient in utilizing water resources to produce yields compared to other crops, thereby reducing water demand and helping to maintain soil texture and carbon content [39].

Soil Microbiome Influencing Plant Performance Traits and Ecosystem Services in Legumes for Improved Climate Adaptability

Soil microbes, primarily bacteria and fungi (1–100 μm body size), are the most diverse and ubiquitous organisms [40, 41]. Microorganisms colonize plant surfaces, inner tissues, and soil, significantly impacting our planet by influencing everything from natural vegetation to intensive agricultural production systems [42, 43]. The soil microbiome is crucial for helping the legumes adapt to the changing climate [2, 44]. The functional attributes that microbes provide in legumes, which help combat the challenges posed by climate change, are explained in the subsequent paragraphs (Fig. 1).

Soil microbiotas are fundamental to climate-smart agriculture since they have an immense role in ecosystem functions, nutrient solubilization, land restoration, soil enzyme production, and improving the soil physical properties, which are all essential parts of soil health and climate resilience. Additionally, these microbes help make plants more resistant to biotic and abiotic stress and directly influence their functional plant traits. Thus, by improving soil and plant health, these organisms play significant roles in mitigating the consequences of climate change. There are beneficial organisms, such as mycorrhizal fungi, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Rhizobium, etc., that enhance plant nutrition, stimulate growth, regulate hormones, and help plants cope with abiotic and biotic stress. At the same time, microorganisms are not the ultimate heroes. Some soil microorganisms are pathogenic, with genera like Ralstonia, Rhizoctonia, Fusarium, Phytophthora, and Gaeumannomyces responsible for major plant diseases. Additionally, these soil-borne pathogens can cause serious human and animal diseases, including nocardiosis, anthrax, malignant edema, and blackleg

Nutrient Solubilization to Improve Nutrient Availability for Plants

Nutrient solubilization is the process by which certain soil microorganisms release nutrients from insoluble compounds, making them available for plant uptake. This process is crucial for plant nutrition and growth [45]. This is especially important in climate-smart agriculture because one of the aims of CSA is to reduce the usage of chemical fertilizers that cause environmental degradation and increase the production of greenhouse gases [6, 46]. Soil microorganisms are essential for improving soil nutrient availability by facilitating the decomposition of organic matter, initiating its mineralization into inorganic nutrients readily available to plants, and improving root associated traits [40, 47]. For this process, a broad and varied set of microorganisms cooperate to degrade complex and refractory polymers into simpler and more labile monomers [48]. Some examples include phosphate-solubilizing bacteria, such as Micrococcus sp. Sfcm-14-01, Agrobacterium sp. Sfl-043-09, and Enterobacter sp. Sfcm-014-02 and Sfcm-054-06, Rhizobia (Ensifer triangle R1-012-02 and Bradyrhizobium glycines Rcm-025-01), which increase foliar phosphorus concentration (ppm) in legumes, thereby increasing crop yields [48]. In addition, microorganisms can influence plant iron (Fe) dynamics, which can support overall plant growth. When corn is intercropped with legumes, certain bacteria such as Microbacterium, Sphingomonas, Shinella, and Rhizobium tend to dominate the rhizosphere. These bacteria can produce siderophores, iron-chelating compounds under conditions of limited iron availability, such as in alkaline soils [49]. While siderophores primarily help bacteria acquire iron for their own growth, under some circumstances, plants may access some of this bound iron, although the exact mechanism is not well known. However, this effect is not guaranteed and depends on the plant–microbe interaction and soil conditions [50]. Bradyrhizobium elkanii strains SEMIA 587 and 5019, Bradyrhizobium japonicum strain 5079, and Bradyrhizobium diazoefficiens strain 5080 are widely recommended for soybean inoculation in Brazil and other countries [37, 38].

Fixing Degraded Lands to Increase Production Acreage

Land degradation is one of the major issues of the 21st century, causing a profound impact on global food security. This leads to desertification, soil erosion, and a decline in soil fertility [51]. Microorganisms, including beneficial fungi, can improve the soil ecosystem as a result of natural or human-induced degradation. These days, microbial inoculation, also known as bio-fertilizer, has become a novel technique for sustainable agriculture to restore degraded land and enhance crop productivity and profitability [43]. For example, when desert land is inoculated with native arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and rhizobial nitrogen-fixing bacteria, they increase the bacterial population, such as Nitrobacter, Glycomyces, Pimelobacter, Terrabacter, Catenulispora, and Rhizobium, which helps improve soil fertility, soil organic matter, and aggregation, thereby improving the soil quality [51]. In addition to these, plant-growth-promoting bacteria can also produce essential phytohormones that help repair disturbed soil aggregates [52, 53]. In Northeastern Brazil, the scientist studied the composition of microbial communities and how the degradation process altered their function. They discovered that areas restored from desertification had more microbial communities, highlighting the significant role of microbes in mitigating land degradation [54]. While these microorganisms play a significant role in fixing degraded land, further research is needed to understand their roles in soil dynamics and to explore potential bacterial consortia in various agricultural systems to fully comprehend how soil microbial communities are essential for mitigating these processes [52, 54].

Enhancing Plant Performance

One of the significant challenges climate change has on the legumes is the drastic reduction of the yield due to various abiotic factors associated with the changing climate. In legumes, microorganisms enhance plant performance and growth by exerting diverse beneficial effects on soil and plants, ultimately helping them adapt to climate change and improve yield parameters [55, 56]. Microorganisms help plants in various ways, from seed germination through maturation. In germinating seeds of legumes, there is a higher abundance of bacteria with pathogen-inhibiting abilities and cellulose-degrading bacteria than in mature plants and ungerminated seeds that promote seed germination and help maintain seed vigor [57]. In addition, the rhizosphere microbial richness is higher in the seedling stage than the mature stage in spermosphere, hinting that the host microbe relationship is more robust during the early stages than in the later stages [57]. This is particularly important because plants are more susceptible in the early seedling stage than in later stages, as they have not yet developed a robust defense system [41]. At the phylum level, legumes were cultivated, Actinobacteria were abundant in the rhizosphere region across all growth stages, followed by Alphaproteobacteria and Acidobacteria [58, 59]. These bacteria have a significant role in the plant-soil ecosystem and influence plant growth [58, 59]. Similarly, in many legumes, there is a positive association between the host plant root and microorganisms to provide beneficial effects. For example, in Bambara groundnuts (Vigna subterranean), there were beneficial interactions between their roots and Bradyrhizobium, Rhizobium, Bacillus, and Actinomycetes to provide beneficial effects [45, 57]. Similarly, co-inoculation of soybean with Bradyrhizobium spp. and Azospirillum brasilense strains Ab-V5 and Ab-V6 at the farmers’ level in Brazil increased root nodulation by 35% and grain yield by 8%, bringing an economic return of US$111.5 ha−1, with additional benefits in reducing GHG emissions [36]. Likewise, two symbiotic Bradyrhizobium strains (B. Arachidis, B. Liaoningense) were tested for their effects on the growth and the rhizobacterial community of peanuts (Arachis hypogaea). Both strains promoted plant growth and influenced the structure of the rhizobacterial community in the rhizosphere [60, 61].

Increasing Disease Resistance to Reduce Pesticide Use

Pests and diseases and their associated crop loss, including legumes, significantly hinder agriculture’s sustainability. Tons of chemical pesticides are applied yearly for pest and disease control, leading to substantial environmental threats [62]. An alternative to chemical pesticides, microbiomes could be used to increase the disease resistance capacity of plants. One way to do this is by protecting the crops from pests and diseases by enhancing the plant nutrition, thus making them robust against biotic stressors [45, 40]. Other mechanisms by which disease suppression occurs include parasitism by beneficial microbes against pathogens, competition for nutrients, and the induction of local resistance in plants. Similarly, soil microorganisms promote plant growth through multiple mechanisms, including ACC deaminase–mediated ethylene regulation, siderophore production, and the induction of induced systemic resistance (ISR). These parallel processes activate the plant’s immune system in response to a pathogen attack, thus promoting plant growth [45]. Likewise, some beneficial bacteria synthesize phenazine, a compound with antibiotic properties, which has been shown to protect crops from disease [62, 63]. In sorghum, Pseudomonas strain VK46 suppresses haustorium formation by degrading syringic acid, while Arthrobacter strain VK49 strengthens endodermal suberization, reducing the impact of striga infection. These strains could potentially combat striga infections in legumes as well, but additional research is necessary [64]. While there are beneficial bacteria in the soil that help plants combat pests and diseases, there are also bacteria that cause disease and act as pathogens to plants. The bacteria, particularly Rhizoctonia, Fusarium, and Ralstonia, cause devastating plant diseases. These diseases lead to more use of chemical pesticides, thereby causing environmental harm [64, 65]. Thus, the goal of the CSA should be to enhance the growth-promoting bacteria that improve the yield and carbon sequestration potential of the plant and reduce or suppress the harmful pathogenic bacteria [65].

Endophytes as a Vital Microbial Consortia

Similarly, microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi can live inside plant tissues without causing harm to the legume; these microorganisms are known as endophytes. Many endophytes enhance seedling emergence, plant growth, and nutrient acquisition, e.g., nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilization, and iron chelation. Endophytes suppress pathogens through antimicrobial activity or siderophore production and induce systemic resistance [64]. In addition, they produce secondary metabolites in plants having anticancer properties, which could be used in the pharmaceutical industry [65]. Their population density varies with microbial species, host genotype, developmental stage, and environmental conditions, with several reports highlighting genotype- and cultivar-specific associations [64, 65]. Endophytic nodules are most commonly colonized by the genera Bacillus and Pseudomonas, followed by Paenibacillus, Enterobacter, Pantoea, Agrobacterium, and Microbacterium. They are primarily hosted by Glycine max, followed by Vigna radiata, Phaseolus vulgaris, and Lens culinaris [66]. Inoculating diazotrophs into soils with native microbiota significantly enhances various traits, such as plant height, stem weight, root volume, and root weight in legumes. This might be due to a potential beneficial interaction between bacterial endophytes and the legumes under stress conditions [66, 67]. Similarly, non-rhizobial endophytes such as Phyllobacterium salinisoli and Xanthomonas translucens enhance Mesorhizobium symbiotic performance and chickpea growth under salinity, suggesting their potential as candidates to improve the productivity of legumes under salt-stress conditions [67, 68]. Although there is clear evidence that endophytes enhance plant performance traits and add nutritional values to plants, the molecular mechanisms underlying host-microbe associations are not yet well understood. Similarly, it does not effectively characterize the complete list of microbes that have a symbiotic relationship with plants. Much of the work has been done in Bacillus and Pseudomonas. Thus, further research is needed to explore more of the microbiome and potentially distangle this genetic phenomenon [64–67].

Soil Enzyme Production and Ecosystem Function

Natural soil enzymes help the soil to adapt to climate change factors such as drought and salinity by enhancing carbon sequestration, reducing GHGs, and overall improving soil health. These enzymes are produced by microorganisms present in the plant roots in the soil fauna [69]. Cover cropping with legumes improves microbial diversity, thus enhancing soil health. Cover cropping with leguminous crops enhances the soil enzymes, enhancing both plant and soil health. After 6 years of vegetable production with cover crops [legume-rye, mustard-rye], a positive correlation was found between microbial biomass and activities of enzymes ß-glucosidase, ß-glucosaminidase, alkaline phosphatase, dehydrogenase, and L-asparaginase, except aspartase [35]. Similarly, legumes are also great in enhancing microbial activity and improving soil enzymes. During the tomato growing season, there was higher microbial biomass carbon in the organic farming system (cover cropping with lana vetch) than that in the conventional system. Similarly, there was an increase in the levels of soil enzymes such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), Urease (URE), Catalase (CAT), Sucrase (SUC), and β-Glucosidase (β-GC) when the crop was rotated with legumes [69, 70]. They also found enhanced microbial activities and the production of important metabolites essential for nutrient cycling, which ultimately aid in crop production. These combined effect of soil enzymes and soil ecosystem restoration helps plants achieve food security and climate resilience [71]. While these enzymes improve soil quality, further studies are required to understand how these soil enzymes lead to plants’ response to oxidative stress, ultimately enhancing their ability to adapt to the changing climate [72].

Legume Microbiome for Water and Salinity Stress for Better Crop Adaptation and Performance

Abiotic stress is one of the primary concerns in crop production globally, and legumes are no exception [70]. The abiotic stress, such as drought stress, is particularly important as it causes a lot of economic loss worldwide [71–72]. Plants have different responsive mechanisms for combating abiotic stresses, such as salinity, and other plant stresses [72, 73]. Study and manipulation of the legume microbiome could be an efficient way of regulating the effect of water stress [67]. The mechanisms of how the microbiome causes crop resilience are important and have been studied recently. Generating phytohormones, 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC), deaminase, volatile chemicals, and antioxidants, soil rhizobacteria colonize the endo-rhizosphere/rhizosphere to increase drought tolerance. Additionally, they reduce the variation in root shape that occurs during drought development and the regulation of stress-responsive genes [74].

In addition, inoculating plants with beneficial bacteria, such as Caulobacter and A. brasilense and Bradyrhizobium spp. can enhance root development and nodulation, leading to increased grain yield and improved quality [53, 75]. This makes plants more efficient in absorbing water from the soil, particularly in low soil moisture conditions [75]. Similarly, Azospirillum is also one of the most used PGPR in the world, but little is known about its interaction with rhizobia when both are simultaneously applied to the legume seeds. Hungria and team evaluated the co-inoculation of Bradyrhizobia and Azospirilla on soybean seeds under different soil and climate conditions in Brazil. They found co-inoculation was efficient and increased yield without adding any chemical N fertilizers [39]. Likewise, in corn, the inoculation with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi influenced the expression of SWEET1 genes that are associated with better sugar transport and water use efficiency. The sugar transporter families in the legumes include 7 MtSUT and 7 PsSUT members, 72 MtMST and 59 PsMST members, and 26 MtSWEET and 22 PsSWEET members, respectively. However, there is less research on the inoculation of bacteria and fungi and the expression of these genes in relation to drought resistance in legumes [76, 77]. High microbial diversity in the soil allowed plants to produce more and recover quickly from drought. When there were improved soil microbial diversity at the time of sowing, yield losses were reduced by 15%. This was possible through improvement in soil aggregate and nitrogen availability, thereby enhancing the nitrogen uptake and making the plant vigorous against the stress [77, 78].

Like water stress, the global productivity of grain legume crops is also severely challenged by salinity stress, particularly with the injudicious use of irrigation water and improper agricultural land management leading towards salt deposition [79]. For salt stress, the soil microbiome could play an active role in nullifying this stressor [80]. Soil microbes could aid in the production of antioxidant enzymes, phenolic acids, and flavonoids, contributing to stress defense mechanisms such as cytoplasm detoxification and cellular turgor maintenance for healthy plant establishment in legumes [80]. Similarly, a native legume’s bacteria in the rhizosphere can increase growth and yield while promoting salinity stress tolerance. The Rhizobium sp. SL42, Hydrogenophaga sp. SL48 and bASV652 (Novosphingobium) are some of the promising strains to have the largest positive effects on soybean growth and salinity tolerance [81]. Likewise, conicoculation with Bradyrhizobium japonicum 532 C (Bj) was found effective against salt stress. Compared to the control, the treatment of Bradyrhizobium japonicum532C (Bj) showed increased shoot biomass at all growth stages. Additionally, the legumes that had this bacterial co-inoculation also had better nitrogen assimilation and higher K+/Na+ ratios [81]. Similar to this study, Anand and team also found the remediating effect of the microbiome on salt stress. They discovered that Vigna radiata’s rhizospheric microbiome can be modified to lessen salinity stress [82]. While these experiments prove that soil microbes could be used effectively against salinity stress, however, the effectiveness of nodule bacteria’s protection is influenced by various other characteristics, including the type and concentration of salts, partner compatibility, virulence, adaptability, and the bacteria’s ability to fix nitrogen (N2). Therefore, other factors contributing to plant resistance should also be considered when developing microbiome-plant stressor relations [80]. In addition, native strains are often better adapted to the local environment. Thus, to outcompete resident soil populations, inoculants should contain high rhizobial densities or highly competitive strains, with concentrations 1,000 times greater than the native Rhizobial population, to achieve 50% nodule occupancy [82, 83].

Nitrogen Fixation

Nitrogen fixation is one of the most important biological phenomena that provides nitrogen, an essential nutrient, available to plants. Root nodulation is a crucial step for nitrogen fixation, and it depends upon the signal molecules microorganisms produce, which are controlled by small bacterial RNA fragments [84]. Symbiotic nitrogen fixation by the legume-Rhizobium symbiosis is a biologically regulated mechanism requiring energy and carbon metabolism [74]. The rhizobium–legume interaction is very host-specific, and the host-rhizobium compatibility is established during the early stages of root infection through molecular signaling. The process of root nodulation begins with the secretion of flavonoids by the host plants, which induces the nod genes of rhizobia [16]. The common nod genes (nodABC) of rhizobia produce a Nod factor, a signaling molecule needed to start nodule formation. Host-specific genes (hsn) of the rhizobia then modify the Nod factor so it can trigger root hair changes only in compatible host plants [16]. This leads to the secretion of lipochitin and oligosaccharides, initiating a complex pathway involving calcium spiking in root hairs. As a result, the root hairs curl and trap the rhizobia, forming an infection thread that ultimately grows and develops into a root nodule [85]. Because the legume-rhizobium interaction is highly strain-specific, if the signaling is incompatible, infection and nodule formation do not occur, even if rhizobia are present in the soil [86] (Fig. 2).

Microbial genes and their interactions with soil and plants form the foundation for ecosystem services and beneficial plant traits. Multiple plant–microbe interactions, such as disease defense signaling, root nodulation, and nitrification, denitrification, co-occur in the soil rhizobiome. These functional processes help plants become vigorous and contribute to improved soil and plant health, ultimately creating a healthy ecosystem

Most rhizobia cannot fix atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) when living freely in soil, except for Bradyrhizobium japonicum, which can fix nitrogen under free-living conditions. Nitrogen fixation occurs only after they enter the plant root, form nodules, and differentiate into bacteroids. The bacteria rely on the plants for energy; therefore, if the bacteria are outside the host, they cannot fix nitrogen. Hence, nitrogen fixation by rhizobia is mostly host-dependent rather than bacteria-dependent [87]. In addition to rhizobium, a member of the genus Caulobacter belonging to the family Caulobacteraceae can also form root nodules in legumes when inoculated [88]. However, the molecular mechanism by which this bacterium aids root nodulation and the underlying genetic mechanisms still need to be further explored. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for utilizing this microbe as a bio-inoculant to enhance soil nutrients and overall soil health [86].

Likewise, in addition to Rhizobium, recent observations suggest that Pseudomonas can also contribute to root nodulation in legumes. Furthermore, it has been observed that Pseudomonas interacts synergistically with nitrogen-fixing rhizobia while exhibiting antagonism toward parasitic ones. These microbes can also promote plant growth and protect plants against pathogens [86].

Denitrification

It is an important biological phenomenon in nitrogen cycling where Nitrate (NO3-) is reduced to gaseous forms of nitrogen, such as N2O (Nitrous oxide) or N2 (Nitrogen gas), which is released into the atmosphere [88]. During the process, NO3- is reduced to NO2- with the help of Nitrate reductase (narGHIJ, napA genes). Then NO2- is converted into Nitric oxide in the presence of Nitrite reductase. This mechanism is facilitated by the expression of the nirK and nirS genes, which are related to Nitrite reductase required for the reaction [89]. This mechanism is also associated with the relative abundance of some genes that depend on soil temperature [42]. Generally, nirK and nirS genes found in denitrifying bacteria such as Pseudomonas dentrificans, Bacillus denitrificans, and Thiobacillus denitrificans are involved in denitrification alongside the genes stated in Table 1 [19, 42, 58]. Waghmode and team observed that the abundance of the nirK and nirS genes was higher in the control plots than in the higher temperature-treated plots, thereby inferring that the denitrification process might be influenced by the soil temperature, indicating that higher temperature reduced the abundance of these genes [20]. Some methanotrophic bacteria and ammonia-oxidizing archaea can produce nitrite from hydroxylamine (the intermediary product during the conversion of Ammonia to N2O even though they lack a homolog of the HAO enzyme (that causes oxidation of hydroxylamine to nitric oxide). However, the specific enzymes responsible for this reaction are still unknown and require further research [89].

Phosphate Solubilization

Phosphorus is the second most important macronutrient after nitrogen required for metabolism, growth, and plant development. Despite phosphorus being present in abundance in the soil, it is tough for plants to uptake these macronutrients because of the complexities they form in the soil [90]. The use of agrochemicals to supply the phosphorus demand has caused the deterioration of the ecosystem and soil health. Hence, there is a need for an eco-friendly strategy for supply. One such method is the use of phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms [91]. These microorganisms break down this insoluble phosphate in soil into the soluble form through their enzymatic action [90]. For this, microorganisms secrete organic acids such as gluconic acid, citric acid, oxalic acid, and lactic acid, which chelate metal cations (Ca²⁺, Fe³⁺, Al³⁺) that bind phosphate. This releases soluble phosphate into the soil solution in the form of Po43− [90, 91]. There are two genes: function-Acidolysis related genes and enzymolysis related genes, which facilitate the process. The acidolysis genes. During acidolysis, microbes secrete organic acids (gluconic acid, citric acid, oxalic acid, lactic acid) into the soil. These acids lower the pH and chelate metal ions (Ca²⁺, Fe³⁺, Al³⁺) that bind phosphate [91]. While in enzymolysis, microbes produce phosphatases or phytases that hydrolyze organic phosphorus compounds. Organic P in soil (like nucleic acids, phytate, phospholipids) is converted to inorganic phosphate. These microorganisms also promote plant growth and crop yield, in addition to phosphate solubilization to make them resistant against biotic and abiotic threats. Microbial genes, such as pqq genes (Pyrroloquinoline quinine), and glucose dehydrogenase (gcd), contribute to phosphate solubilization in plants, through acidolysis [92]. Likewise, genes such as phoA – acid phosphatase, appA – alkaline phosphatase, and phn phosphonate degradation enzymes help through enzymolysis phosphate solubilization [107].

Genes encoded by Microbes that Enhance Important Plant Performance Traits

In addition to nutrient acquisition and uptake, numerous microbial genes contribute to important plant functional attributes, such as the production of plant growth-promoting hormones, the support of plants against abiotic and biotic stress effects, and the maintenance of soil structure and texture [62]. The genes such as iaaM and ipdC present in microorganisms such as Pseudomonas and Azospirillim help in root growth and elongation, which helps the plants absorb water more efficiently and fight against drought conditions [62, 86]. Similarly, one important plant-protective mechanism facilitated by microbial genes is the synthesis of hydrogen cyanide (HCN). This is a chemical secreted by microorganisms that is toxic to many organisms, including pests and pathogens. The hcnA/B/C genes present in microorganisms contribute to hydrogen cyanide (HCN) synthesis, making plants resistant to many pests and diseases [18]. Similarly, 2,4-Diacetylphloroglucinol is a chemical that safeguards plants from pathogens. The phlD/C genes contribute to the synthesis of 2,4-Diacetylphloroglucinol (2,4-DAPG), which has potent antimicrobial activity against a wide range of plant pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, and oomycetes [62]. Likewise, legume nodules also contain Cys cluster proteins (CCPs), and some of these proteins have antimicrobial activity and may have a role in protecting plants from pathogens [93]. In addition, some microorganisms facilitate the plant defense mechanism through phenazine biosynthesis. The core genes for phenazine biosynthesis in microorganisms are phzA/BCDEFG. These genes are present in all known phenazine producers and exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties [62]. In legumes, the microbes are associated with genes K11912 (ppkA), involved in bacterial secretion systems, K13308 (esi), linked to multiple secondary metabolite pathways, and K12645 (CYP170A), also tied to secondary metabolite production to support plant microbiota and health [93]. The genes in legumes that control their interactions with beneficial soil microbes (BSM) should be viewed as one connected genetic system. Since the plant genome is more stable than that of microbes, crops should be bred to regulate the most beneficial microbes and disregard the harmful ones. To support this, focused legume breeding should be done for better performance under sustainable conditions, reducing the need for chemical inputs [94]. In addition, to understand how dependent plants are on these microbes, scientists need to identify the core microbiota at different ecological levels and use pan-microbiome analyses to study their functions [13].

Response of Micro-organism to Changing Climate

The changing climate influences the aboveground biomass and the below-ground biota. Aboveground impacts are visible and quantifiable, while below-ground impacts are often ignored and difficult to assess and quantify [20]. The resulting impact of the microbiome could be startling because of the anomalous behavior of the microbiome to a changing climate and the multitude of parameters governing the population of microbes [96]. Warming soil temperatures and elevated carbon dioxide (eCO2) are some of the dire consequences of climate change, and a large amount of research including genetics and physiology, needs to be done on microbial response to these impacts. This is a collective endeavour, as natural experiments could be influenced by numerous confounding factors impeding the validity of the research [17]. Although there was no effect on the bacterial community in soil warmed for 5–8 years, there were significant differences in the bacterial community in the organic soil, mostly because the organic soils behave as mineral soil after this long time [82]. Similarly, in a study by Wagmode and team, it was seen that warmer plots, both under irrigated and non-irrigated conditions, had altered bacterial diversity compared to control plots. They found significant differences in the Acidobacteria, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Gemmatimonadetes in warmed and control plots. Furthermore, they observed that soil warming decreased the abundance of ammonia-oxidizing archaea and denitrifiers but increased the abundance of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria [20]. However, another study did not observe any influence of soil warming on the bacterial community on the organic horizon of the soil, although they observed the change in microbial diversity with the seasons [96].

Additionally, many studies have discovered altered microbiomes and functional genes in response to elevated CO2 and warming in grassland ecosystems. They reported that elevated carbon dioxide (eCO2) stimulated key functional genes involved in carbon degradation and fixation, methane metabolism, nitrogen fixation, denitrification, and nitrogen mineralization. These discoveries underscore the complexity and variability of microbial communities’ behaviour and their population, opening up new avenues for research and understanding in legumes [19].

Genetic Engineering to Identify and Apply Elite Microbial Inoculants

The complex relationship between microbiomes and ecosystems, with their direct impact on plant health and indirect influence on ecosystem services, has sparked a continuous pursuit of understanding and optimizing these dynamics for improved crop-microbiome interactions [97]. The growing interest in microbiome research and technological advancement has opened new avenues for developing superior microbial inoculants, called microbiome engineering. Microbiome engineering is a powerful approach that provides valuable insights and practical tools for constructing microbial communities [2, 97]. This approach to developing microbial inoculants involves two key steps: first, shaping the microbial community, and second, transferring the microbiome from hosts with desirable traits to microbe-free hosts. Studies have demonstrated the potential influence of plant traits such as flowering and biomass production through successive microbiome transfers [98]. Microbiome engineering relies on effective host control mechanisms that play a significant role in shaping microbial communities [98]. Legumes are highly suitable for this approach due to their ability to induce substantial modifications in microbial communities inside and outside the plant system [2]. The primary emphasis in microbiome engineering has been on constructing advantageous microbiomes for plant hosts. However, this methodology has also been proposed to identify alterations in the abundance of microbial species and changes in allele frequencies within microbial populations present in microbiomes [2, 70]. The Rhizobial compatibility pathway (RCP) involves key processes such as changes in the abundance of microbial species and shifts in allele frequencies within microbial populations. These factors are fundamental to the four stages of rhizobia transmission: infecting the roots, persisting within the host tissue, being released from the host roots, and surviving in the soil [99].

The combination of microbiome engineering and the cost-effectiveness of next-generation sequencing offers an opportunity to develop approaches that can rapidly detect changes occurring at each of the four stages of rhizobia transmission. Additionally, biotechnological methods utilizing potential genes expressed in transgenic plants may be crucial in developing climate-resistant chickpea plants. Promising preliminary results were obtained using CAP2, galactinol synthase genes, proteomic methods, RNA sequencing data, stay green traits, and -OMICS [2, 70].

Soil Microbial Inoculants as Tools for Climate-Smart Agriculture in Legumes

A healthy environment, high yield, and economic return are the global goals of CSA and microbiota can help achieve all these objectives. However, for the successful implementation of the climate-smart agriculture (CSA) goal, the strategies must be translated into agronomic outcomes [4]. The global CSA goal can be achieved through reduced fertilizer use, improved product shelf-life, and increased postharvest value of the legume, which would help farmers become successful [27]. Both symbiotic and non-symbiotic plant–microbe interactions can help ameliorate climate smart agriculture challenges. While the symbiotic association directly contributes to the plant’s health and performance, the non-symbiotic associations aid rhizosphere microbes by facilitating endophytes. Some endophytes are vertically transmitted to subsequent generations via seeds. Thus, there is a continuous fight against climate change rather than a single-shot approach, which ultimately helps sustain agriculture [4]. Importantly, legumes help in the selective reshaping of the composition of the rhizosphere microbiome. By releasing root exudates, signaling molecules, and secondary metabolites, they can recruit beneficial microorganisms and modulate the microbial community to combat adverse environmental stresses [93]. Additionally, biological fertilizers, specifically microbial inoculants, can enhance soil aggregation and alleviate water repellency, thereby offering greater resilience against environmental stresses. However the successful CSA strategies should consider not only microbial traits but also plant genotype and how it interacts with the microbiota. Integrating knowledge of plant–microbe interactions with inoculant design and soil management practices can improve the use of microbiome-based approaches in climate-smart legume production systems [33]. However, achieving consistent field results is challenging as inoculated microbes must compete with native soil microbiota, and other physical and chemical factors (soil texture, pH, organic matter, moisture, and temperature) that affect their survival and activity. Therefore, we first need to develop and test stable microbial consortia and integrate advanced tools such as microbiome engineering, weather data analysis, predictive modelling, and genome editing. Furthermore, there should be complementary supportive policies and regulations to enhance these interventions’ sustainable agricultural production further and achieve the Climate Smart goal [33, 34].

Future Perspectives

The exceptional nutritional values and their unique adaptability for efficient water use, efficiency, and suitability of microbiome engineering make legumes a suitable crop for mitigating the challenges posed by climate change. However, the sustainability of legume production under increasing these stressors requires a thorough understanding of plant responses to the abiotic stressors and the integration of efficient breeding techniques. Additionally, engineering legume crops to enhance beneficial host–microbe associations, along with microbiome engineering approaches such as targeted microbial inoculants or complementary microbial consortia are promising approach to improve yield-related traits. We should develop the legume to accept a wide range of bacteria, improve energy dynamics in the host-symbiont relationship, and assist in conserving the selective microorganism. Likewise, we should further engineer the organism to operate across broader environmental conditions and expand the functional range of rhizobia and other nitrogen-fixing microbes, with significant agronomic implications, potentially reducing the need for nitrogen fertilizers. This could lead to a more sustainable and environmentally friendly agricultural industry. Although soil microbiomes significantly impact ecosystem services, soil, plant, and human health, it remains necessary to quantify the ecosystem services these microbiome communities provide within legumes. Future research must delve into the genetic contributions of beneficial microorganisms to the rhizosphere mechanics and dynamics to better understand their role in ecosystem services. There is a critical need for sequencing microbial genomes to uncover the genetic architecture of these microbes, identifying genes that contribute to plant nutrition, growth, disease suppression, and carbon sequestration. The World Microbiome Catalogue represents regional and national agri-ecosystems, aiding in the development of legume-specific microbiomes for future advancements in crop improvement and food security [100]. In addition to soil analysis, a Global Microbiome Atlas or Global Soil Microbiome Atlas is needed to enhance our understanding of soil microbial diversity and its role in sustainable agriculture. Changes in microbial populations due to climatic conditions may result from variations in available carbon and soil carbon sequestration. Therefore, genomic approaches are essential for studying the functional dynamics of contrasting microbial populations. More soil biological studies are needed, and markers for soil health should be developed. Research should also explore why microbes associate with each other and the synergistic effects of the microbiome, as well as how to manipulate these interactions for improved soil health and plant production. Current research predominantly focuses on bacteria, but future studies should also address the roles of viruses, fungi, and other eukaryotic microbes in soil health and plant growth. Furthermore, future research should include other climate-smart crops to evaluate their collective performance within legume based integrated cropping systems under diverse environmental conditions to provide sustainable solution.

Conclusion

Legumes are the major cost-efficient plant protein crops for human food and animal feed. They produce low GHGs and water footprints and enrich the soil through nitrogen fixation, soil microbial activity, and maintaining soil microbial biodiversity. In this review, we explored the strong potential of microbiomes to increase crop nutritional value and highlighted the benefits of adopting a multidisciplinary approach to the biofortification of legumes, ultimately allowing the development of environmentally resilient and nutritious crops. Their presence in the legumes’ root system colonizes the rhizosphere’s outer layer, forming phytohormones that increase drought resistance and stress control; therefore, microbiomes assist in developing resistant against these environmental stressors. Moreover, a higher microbiome community diversity is associated with improved drought resistance compared to plants with lower microbiome diversity. However, regarding microorganisms, not just their bright side but also their dark side should also be considered. While there are plenty of microbes that assist in plant and soil health, there are also pathogenic bacteria, parasitic fungi, and nematodes that cause significant crop loss in legumes. Thus, future research should aim to better characterize both the beneficial and detrimental aspects of the microbiome for designing climate-resilient crops. Although numerous studies have been conducted to find the benefits of microorganisms, there is still a lot of work required to characterize them. Warming and increased atmospheric CO2 do not always hamper the organism; the simulated experiments show fascinating results. While warming and increased CO2 decrease the abundance of some beneficial bacteria, they are also found to increase the abundance of other important bacteria. Therefore, more studies should be conducted to explore these complex dynamics. Likewise, although numerous genes contributing to ecosystem services and plant growth have been identified in microorganisms in model crops, studying their interactions and expressions in the future is essential for legumes for their sustainable production.
