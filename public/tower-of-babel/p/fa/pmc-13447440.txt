Imaging Evaluation to Determine the Timing of Early Rehabilitation in Pediatric Non-Traumatic Acquired Brain Injury: A Case Report
Authors: Shimpei Yamamoto, Taichi Maruyama, Ittetsu Takeya, Yasuko Yoshida, Tatsuro Oshikawa, Takahito Inoue, Atsushi Ogawa, Teruaki Izaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Determining the optimal timing for the initiation of early rehabilitation in pediatric non-traumatic acquired brain injury (ABI) remains challenging, largely because the onset of injury is often difficult to identify, particularly in infancy. We report the case of a 1-year-old boy with non-traumatic ABI of uncertain onset, in whom mobilization was initiated more than 24 hours after an imaging-based diagnosis. Neurorehabilitation, consisting primarily of active practice, was implemented safely, with no adverse events or neurological deterioration during the intervention. This case suggests that imaging-based assessment may serve as an adjunctive objective reference for determining the timing of early rehabilitation in pediatric non-traumatic ABI.

INTRODUCTION

Acquired brain injury (ABI) is brain damage that occurs after birth due to traumatic (e.g., head injury, motor vehicle accident, sports injury) or non-traumatic (e.g., stroke, encephalopathy, etc.) events. A survey conducted in Japan showed that pediatric ABI is distributed almost equally between traumatic and non-traumatic etiologies, both of which carry a risk of long-term motor and cognitive dysfunction [1]. These sequelae have been reported to contribute to broader social problems, such as increasing the risk of bullying victimization and suicide among children, and increasing the burden of caregiving and medical costs [2]. Therefore, neurorehabilitation aimed at functional recovery, particularly through active practice, which is based on the theory of neuroplasticity and promotes self-initiated sensorimotor activity, is crucial following pediatric ABI.

Early initiation of active practice after ABI is desirable, as this is supported by findings that delayed initiation of rehabilitation may reduce its therapeutic benefits [3]. However, excessive neural activity in the hyperacute period may exacerbate excitotoxic processes [45]. These considerations highlight the question of when active practice should be initiated. Particularly in pediatric non-traumatic ABI, the exact time of injury onset is often unclear, and this difficulty in identifying onset may itself constitute a barrier to determining the appropriate timing of early rehabilitation. Therefore, an objective indicator is needed.

In this case report, we describe our clinical experience of using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings to help guide the timing of early rehabilitation initiation in pediatric non-traumatic ABI and discuss its implications.

CASE DESCRIPTION

Clinical course

A 1-year-0-month-old boy was brought by his parents to a local pediatrician with a chief complaint of fever and poor oral intake. Multiplex-Polymerase Chain Reaction detected Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), and he was referred to our hospital with a diagnosis of RSV bronchiolitis accompanied by poor oral intake and was admitted.

His medical and family histories were unremarkable. Perinatal information included gestational age of 39 weeks and 4 days, birth weight of 3,900 g, and birth length of 53.2 cm. No developmental delays or congenital diseases had been noted during standardized national health screenings. According to information obtained through a parent interview, before the illness, he was able to crawl on hands and knees and pull to stand but had not yet acquired cruising. Based on both the health screening records and the parent interview, he was considered to have shown typical motor and cognitive development before the illness.

At admission, the vital signs were body temperature: 37.0°C, heart rate: 154/minutes, blood pressure: 115/82 mmHg, respiratory rate: 62/minutes, peripheral capillary oxygen saturation: 93% (delivered via a nonrebreather mask at 10 L/minutes). He responded to stimuli but soon became somnolent. Chest retractions were observed, and diminished lung sounds and slight coarse crackles were auscultated during inspiration. There was no peripheral coldness or edema, and Capillary Refill Time < 2 seconds.

Fig. 1 shows the progress after admission. On day 2 of admission, High-Flow Nasal Cannula (HFNC) was started because oxygen therapy did not improve oxygenation sufficiently. On day 3 of admission, ventilator management (PC mode, PEEP 5.0, above PEEP 25.0, IE ratio 1:2, frequency 30/minutes) was implemented in the intensive care unit (ICU) with sedation to achieve the State Behavioral Scale of −2. At this point, he was judged to have moderate acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) according to the definition of ARDS [6], because he had respiratory failure within a week of developing RSV pneumonia. X-ray and Computed Tomography (CT) scan of the chest showed infiltrative shadows in the bilateral lung. Furthermore, electrocardiography and physical examination revealed no signs of heart failure, and PaO2 /FiO2 (P/F) ratio was 200 mmHg (PaO2: 120 mmHg) in the ventilator setting under intubation at PEEP 5 cm H2O. On day 7 of admission, a brain CT scan was performed to evaluate possible acute encephalopathy, as spontaneous breathing remained insufficient during sedation withdrawal; however, no obvious abnormality was noted. On day 8 of admission, he was weaned from the ventilator and HFNC was resumed.

On day 10 of admission, his level of consciousness was improving, but there were regression signs such as difficulty in controlling his head and maintaining sitting posture. Furthermore, electroencephalogram showed low voltage and slow waves. As his consciousness had only partially recovered and the observed regression was initially attributed to disuse syndrome, he was kept under observation. On day 13 of admission, pyramidal signs and paresis of both lower extremities were observed. The brain MRI scan showed symmetrical high-signal-intensity areas in the mammillary bodies and hippocampi on Diffusion Weighted Image (DWI), while the corresponding Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) maps appeared isointense (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, the diagnosis of hypoxic brain injury secondary to ARDS was made after Wernicke encephalopathy was ruled out as a differential diagnosis, because he was not undernourished and did not have ocular abnormalities especially nystagmus. On the same day, HFNC was replaced to oxygen therapy, and he was transferred out of the ICU.

Oxygen therapy was terminated on day 15 of admission (day 3 of imaging-based diagnosis). The brain MRI scan on day 30 of admission (day 18 of imaging-based diagnosis) showed that the DWI high-signal-intensity areas seen in the previous scan were generally obscured, but the ventricles and subarachnoid space were enlarged as in the previous scan, suggesting mild progression of supratentorial atrophy (Fig. 2). He was discharged from our hospital on day 32 of admission (day 20 of imaging-based diagnosis), then returned to nursery school and periodic outpatient follow-up was initiated.

Progress of rehabilitation

On day 7 of admission, the P/F ratio was still in the 200s, and he still had respiratory failure, with excessive respiratory secretions and diminished breath sounds. Respiratory physiotherapy was started at this point, with the primary goals of preventing atelectasis and promotion of sputum expectoration. Interventions included postural drainage and squeezing. In addition, efforts were made to alleviate the infant’s stress by encouraging the parents to promote parent-infant bonding through physical contact and verbal interaction (Fig. 3).

In the physical therapy evaluation on day 14 of admission (day 2 of imaging-based diagnosis), he showed obvious pyramidal signs such as hypertonia in both upper extremities, hyperreflexia in both Achilles tendons, and positive foot clonus, as well as paresis with weakness in both lower extremities. After informed consent was obtained, mobilization was initiated more than 24 hours after the imaging-based diagnosis, following confirmation of respiratory stabilization and with careful clinical monitoring. He had difficulty controlling his head, and although he attempted to control his postural sway when sitting, he could not hold it without assistance, and had difficulty performing hands-knees crawling.

In this case, functional recovery was longitudinally assessed using the Enjoji Scale of Infant Analytical Development [7], which provides Developmental Quotient (DQ) scores across six subscales: gross motor skills, fine motor skills, basic habits, social relationships, expressive language, and receptive language. On this scale, he showed a DQ of 21 in gross motor skills on day 14 of admission (day 2 of imaging-based diagnosis). The DQs for basic habits, social relationships, expressive language, and receptive language, which cover the cognitive function domain, showed 21, 13, 4, and 4, respectively (Fig. 3). After discussions with his parents, the rehabilitation goal after the ABI diagnosis was determined as “to reacquire locomotion skills and return to previous activities at home and in nursery school.” On the same day, occupational and speech therapy were initiated. Furthermore, social work intervention was introduced on day 16 of admission (day 4 after the imaging-based diagnosis).

During the 18-day period from day 14 of admission (day 2 after the imaging-based diagnosis) to day 31 of admission (day 19 after the imaging-based diagnosis), neurorehabilitation centered on active practice was provided for 1–2 hours per day, 5 days per week (Fig. 3). Before the introduction of active practice, the rehabilitation team provided face-to-face education to the parents. The education covered possible neurological sequelae associated with ABI, neuroplasticity during infancy, and the importance of repeated practice. These points were explained in plain language at the bedside and during family meetings, including the role of parental involvement in the rehabilitation process. The parents were encouraged to ask questions, and their understanding and willingness to proceed were confirmed through discussion. In the first few days following the ABI, the content and total minutes of active practice were kept at low intensity to accommodate his endurance and ensure safety. Both the total minutes and variety of active practice gradually increased over time. Specifically, static postural skills that had been acquired before the disease, such as lying on the belly, sitting, getting on hands-knees, kneeling, and standing, were practiced with assistance according to his physical function level. Additionally, dynamic transitional skills [8] such as rotating into sitting and pulling to stand using furniture, were practiced repeatedly with minimal assistance. Furthermore, the use of the lower limbs was actively encouraged because of suspected paresis of the lower limbs. Since he was one year old and could not understand verbal instructions or gestures, we adjusted the environment so that the active practice became “task-specific” [4] by using affordances, such as toys, to give purpose and motivation to his actions and to induce trial-and-error learning and movement exploration based on his daily life situations. In this way, we attempted to restore his overall gross motor skills. During his occupational therapy, practices for fine motor function such as reaching and manipulation were also conducted as active practice.

In addition, information was shared with parents and nurses to facilitate the continuation of rehabilitation outside of scheduled intervention hours. We shared information about daily changes in the infant’s physical functions, newly acquired abilities, and ongoing challenges, encouraging parents to support the infant’s motor tasks at the limit level of his abilities without therapist guidance.

We observed no adverse events during the 18-day period, including worsening neurological symptoms or hemodynamic instability. On day 31 of admission (day 19 of imaging-based diagnosis), the day before discharge, he showed capability of hands-knees crawling, which was his pre-morbid locomotion skill, for 3 consecutive cycles, and DQ of gross motor skills had recovered to 58. The DQs for the cognitive domains of basic habits, social relationships, expressive language, and receptive language were 73, 100, 73, and 89, respectively, indicating a trend toward recovery (Fig. 3). He was discharged on day 32 of admission (day 20 of imaging-based diagnosis).

Follow-up

After discharge, home-visit rehabilitation was started twice a week. Three months after discharge (corresponding to day 114 after admission), at the reassessment, cruising was possible, and gross motor skills had recovered to 70. Basic habits, social relationships, expressive language, and receptive language were 77, 87, 87, and 77, respectively, indicating that these domains were largely maintained compared with the previous assessment. No new neurological deficits or clinical deterioration were noted at follow-up (Fig. 3).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

No identifiable information was disclosed in writing this case report; therefore, further ethics committee approval was waived in accordance with local regulations. The purpose and content of this report were explained to the parents, and their written informed consent was obtained.

DISCUSSION

This case, involving a 1-year-old boy with non-traumatic ABI of uncertain onset date, demonstrated that active practice, initiated early on the day after diagnosis based on imaging findings and conducted in collaboration with the family, was feasible and could be implemented safely, with no adverse events or neurological deterioration during the intervention.

Secondary lung-mediated acute brain injury resulting from ARDS is known to commonly involve the hippocampus [9], and the findings in this case are consistent with this pattern, indicating non-traumatic brain injury with hypoxic insults as the primary pathology.

In the present case, mobilization was initiated more than 24 hours after the imaging-based diagnosis, and early rehabilitation was implemented. A previous study of pediatric ABI, including non-traumatic cases, reported that early rehabilitation was associated with shorter length of stay and positive patient outcomes [10]. Early rehabilitation may be particularly important in infants because motor development has a limited window [1112], and the immature brain is both more vulnerable [1314] and more plastic [15].

However, caution is warranted in interpreting the assumption that earlier initiation of rehabilitation following ABI invariably leads to better outcomes. Animal studies have raised concerns regarding potential excitotoxic processes associated with early excessive activity after brain injury [45]. In the present case, rehabilitation was initiated more than 24 hours after the imaging-based diagnosis. Considering that detection on imaging necessarily occurs after the actual onset of ABI, this timing does not contradict the recommended window for early mobilization in adults with acute ischemic stroke [16], nor the suggested criteria for early mobilization in critically ill pediatric patients which is within 48–72 hours after ICU admission or upon fulfillment of clinical safety criteria [17]. Furthermore, previous studies in adults have shown that infarcts up to five days old typically present as hyperintense on DWI and hypointense on ADC maps, whereas this pattern is generally absent beyond ten days [18]. In this case, although the exact onset of ABI could not be definitively established, the normal brain CT findings on day 7 of admission together with the subsequent DWI/ADC findings suggested that the injury may have occurred between days 7 and 8 of admission. These imaging findings were cautiously interpreted as supportive evidence that the hyperacute period had likely passed.

Previous studies have often described the beginning of ABI uniformly as the “date of injury” or “event date,” without clearly distinguishing between traumatic and non-traumatic etiologies [1920]. Meanwhile, it is evident that determining the onset date of non-traumatic ABI in infants and young children is clinically challenging, partly because early neurological abnormalities are difficult to detect before language acquisition, and because assessing consciousness during critical care is inherently limited. Nevertheless, no comprehensive definition has been established for determining the beginning of pediatric non-traumatic ABI.

In the present case, we encountered similar challenges since the ABI developed during hospitalization and the exact onset date remained unknown. Under these circumstances, early mobilization and active practice were initiated based on imaging findings, and no apparent adverse events were observed thereafter. Therefore, this case suggests that neuroimaging, particularly the combined use of DWI and ADC maps, may play an important role in informing clinical guidance regarding the timing of rehabilitation initiation.

In addition, families in the acute to early recovery phase after pediatric ABI are often in a state of psychological crisis [21], which may make active engagement in rehabilitation difficult. Therefore, throughout the rehabilitation, clinicians should actively promote adaptive adjustment by helping families balance the maintenance of hope with the gradual acceptance of ABI as a potentially lifelong condition [22]. In the present case, from the early phase after ABI, we explained both the potential for long-term sequelae and the child’s remarkable capacity for recovery (neuroplasticity), which helped the parents understand the importance of rehabilitation. Furthermore, in addition to the social worker’s intervention, which was consistent with recent emphasis on family-centered and psychosocial support in pediatric ABI rehabilitation [23], active information sharing between therapists and parents throughout the hospitalization may also have facilitated the parents’ early proactive engagement in rehabilitation and their adaptation. Importantly, in the present case, no neurological deterioration was observed during the early rehabilitation period, including parent-delivered activities added to the usual therapy sessions. This finding is consistent with the clinical feasibility of early rehabilitation in this case.

Symmetrical high-signal-intensity areas were observed in the mammillary bodies and hippocampi on MRI. Clinically, the infant presented with paresis of both lower extremities, although no abnormal signal intensity was identified in motor-related regions. Because of the patient’s age and clinical condition, detailed neurological examination was limited, making it difficult to determine the relationship between the lesion sites and the observed symptoms. Therefore, the present report cannot establish a direct mechanistic relationship between these imaging findings and the focal neurological deficits, and the role of active practice should be interpreted cautiously. No conclusion can be drawn regarding its direct influence on neuroplastic changes in peri-lesional or functionally related brain regions. However, cognitive function, which was presumed to be related to the affected lesions, showed recovery over time, although long-term follow-up remains necessary. Taken together, these findings suggest that early rehabilitation, centered on active practice, was implemented safely in this case.

Although this case suggested the potential utility of neuroimaging assessment in pediatric non-traumatic ABI, the findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be overgeneralized, as this is a single case report. In addition, no corresponding coronal ADC image of the hippocampi had been acquired, although an axial ADC image of the mammillary bodies was available, limiting the completeness of the imaging assessment. Furthermore, neuroimaging does not always identify mild or subtle cases of ABI [18], and therefore some early pathological changes may remain undetected. Accordingly, the use of imaging findings to guide decisions regarding the initiation of early rehabilitation may not be suitable in mild cases. Further evidence is needed before such imaging findings can be systematically used to guide rehabilitation timing. Despite these limitations, neuroimaging may help guide rehabilitation timing in pediatric non-traumatic ABI, particularly when the onset of injury is difficult to determine.
