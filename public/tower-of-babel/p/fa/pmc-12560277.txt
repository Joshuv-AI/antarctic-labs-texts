Relationship between empathy and quality of work life in oncology nurses: the mediating role of resilience
Authors: Lijuan Wang, Qingwei Pang, Jinhong Yang, Tingting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore the relationship between empathy and quality of work life (QWL) in oncology nurses, with a focus on the mediating role of resilience.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in a tertiary grade hospital on a sample of 321 oncology nurses in Weifang, China. Participants completed measures of the general information questionnaire, Interpersonal reactivity index for China, Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, and Work-related Quality of Life Scale-2. The data were analyzed by using SPSS 26.0 and Amos 20.ResultsOncology nurses’ QWL score was 3.05 ± 0.27. Their resilience, empathy, and QWL were positively correlated with each other, and resilience partially mediated the relationship between empathy and QWL (β = 0.419, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe QWL of oncology nurses should be further improved. The data revealed significant associations among empathy, resilience, and QWL, with resilience demonstrating a mediating effect in the empathy-QWL linkage. Nursing decision-makers should consider fostering organizational climates that support resilience and empathy development, as these factors show significant associations with QWL.Keywords: Oncology nurse, Empathy, Resilience, Quality of work life

Introduction

According to the statistics in 2022, there are approximately 4.82 million new cancer cases and 3.21 million cancer deaths in China [1], accounting for approximately 23.7% of new cancer cases and 30% of cancer deaths worldwide [2]. Cancer has become a public health issue that endangers public health and raises healthcare costs [3]. The growing number of oncology patients has increased demands on oncology nurses. Nurses in clinical oncology settings generally face great challenges, such as establishing close and continuous contact with patients in severe pain, suffering, and even the edge of death, facing potential risks the preparation and administration of antineoplastic agents, and prolonged exposure to distressing and traumatic events of oncology patients and families [4, 5]. Additionally, the shortage of nursing professionals and the complexity of cancer treatment and management have brought with excessive workload on oncology nurses [6]. These challenges may lead to job dissatisfaction, stress, and burnout, which in turn affects their work and life [7, 8].

In recent years, the concept Quality of Work Life (QWL) has gained increasing attention, especially in nurses [9, 10]. The QWL refers to an employees’ satisfaction with work and life elements, such as job security, job satisfaction, work environment, career prospects, nature of job, and work-life balance [11]. In the context of nursing, QWL was defined as the degree to which nurses can satisfy important personal needs through their experiences in work organization while achieving the organization’s goals [12]. Previous studies have shown that QWL of oncology nurses was unsatisfactory and lower QWL can lead to serious consequences, such as poor performance, decreased retention rate, reduced job satisfaction, eventually exacerbating the shortage of nurses and the decline of quality of care [5, 9]. Since there is still limited research on QWL and its predictors among oncology nurses, attention to oncology nurses’ QWL should be highly regarded.

Empathy: the Dual-Aspect cornerstone of nursing practice

Empathy, defined as the capacity to recognize and differentiate others’ emotional states, enables individuals to adopt others’ perspectives, comprehend their needs, and generate congruent affective responses—a foundational competency for effective nursing communication and trust-building [13]. This capacity equips nurses to meaningfully engage with patients, ultimately advancing therapeutic outcomes, as substantiated by nursing experts [14]. Within nurse-patient relationships, empathy manifests as authentic understanding of patients’ experiences and concerns. However, chronic exposure to high-stress clinical environments may impair nurses’ empathic capacity. Crucially, technical proficiency alone proves insufficient without complementary interpersonal skills to recognize patient contexts and respond empathically [15]. Empirical evidence confirms that empathic engagement enhances patient trust, perceived support, and decision-making satisfaction [15].

Empirical and theoretical links to professional Well-Being

Conceptual models further illuminate empathy’s role in professional well-being: Shi et al.‘s framework for hospice nurses identifies empathy within the “nurse environment” domain, interacting with external factors to shape professional quality of life [16]. Consistent with this, cross-population studies—particularly among healthcare workers—demonstrate significant empathy-QWL associations [17–20]. Wang et al. observed positive links between empathy and compassion satisfaction (and inverse relationships with burnout) in hospice nurses [17], while Gong et al. established empathy’s indirect effect on turnover intention via QWL among new graduates [19]. Notwithstanding these advances, empathy-QWL dynamics remain underexplored specifically in oncology nursing contexts.

Resilience: the protective nexus in emotional labor

Notably, empathy’s dualistic nature warrants attention, While essential for therapeutic connections, the cognitive-emotional labor of imaginatively experiencing patients’ suffering predisposes nurses to compassion fatigue vulnerability [21], with 40% of clinicians reporting its deleterious physical, psychological, and social sequelae [21]. This risk underscores the critical buffering role of resilience—a protective mechanism preserving functional integrity amid adversity [22]. Resilience not only mitigates empathy fatigue but enhances empathy satisfaction [23], with emerging evidence suggesting bidirectional reinforcement: Empathy cultivation fosters stress resilience through strengthened relational competence and adaptive coping [20, 24], while resilient dispositions conversely optimize QWL outcomes [25, 26].

Conceptual integration and hypothesized model

Although pairwise relationships among these constructs are documented [20, 27, 28], their tripartite integration remains theoretically nascent. To address this gap, we propose the mediation model in Fig. 1, hypothesizing that: (1) Empathy positively associates with QWL; (2) Empathy positively associates with resilience; (3) Resilience positively associates with QWL; (4) Resilience mediates the empathy-QWL relationship.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The present study, a cross-sectional online survey with a convenience sampling method, was conducted among oncology nurses employed by a tertiary hospital in Weifang City, Shandong Province, China, from October 2022 to December 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) had registered nurse licenses; (2) to be in front-line clinical nursing practice roles in oncology wards; (3) had at least two years of work experience in oncology wards; (4) aware of the study and consent to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria included: (1) to be in training and advanced practice; (2) off-duty due to illness, childbirth, personal leave, etc.; (3) severe stress or unable to properly interpret the questionnaire.

Wen Juan Xing (www.wjx.cn), an online questionnaire platform, was utilized to collect data. The researchers contacted the directors of nursing department to obtain the investigation permission and invite them to advertise this study among oncology nurses. Then two well-trained researchers from our research group were responsible for collecting data in oncology wards. Before the questionnaires were distributed, the unified instructions were sent to eligible participants through WeChat (a popular social application in China) to explain the study’s purpose, significance, questionnaire filling methods and precautions, as well as the anonymity and confidentiality principle of the questionnaires. Then the questionnaires were delivered through Wen Juan Xing to the respondents after they provided informed consent.

The sample size for the study was calculated using MedPower, a power calculator developed by Kenny [29]. We hypothesized the effect sizes for the three paths running from empathy to resilience, resilience to WRQoL, and empathy to WRQoL all 0.25. Considering an expected dropout rate of 10%, the sample size should be at least 172. A total of 330 questionnaires were distributed, and 321 valid questionnaires were included, with an effective recovery rate of 97.3%. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Weifang People’s Hospital in Shandong Province (wfwsjk-2019-181). All methods performed in the study involving human participants were in accordance with ethical standards, guidelines, and regulations. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cross-sectional study was used in reporting this research.

Measures

General information questionnaire

A self-designed general information questionnaire includes age, gender, marital status, presence or absence of children, educational background, working years in the oncology wards, and professional title.

Interpersonal reactivity index for China (IRI-C)

The empathy abilities of respondents were assessed by the IRI-C which was developed by Davis et al. [30] in 1980 and translated and revised into Chinese by Zhan [31]. It included 22 items across 4 dimensions: fantasy (FS), personal distress (PD), perspective-taking (PT), and empathic concern (EC). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 to 4, and the total score ranges from 0 to 88. The ability to empathize is more powerful the higher the score. It is frequently employed in the test of individual’s empathy abilities and has been proven to have good reliability and validity [31]. The Cronbach’s alpha of this scale in this study was 0.805.

Connor-Davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC)

The CD-RISC was developed by Connor and Davidson [32] to measure population’s resilience. The original scale included 25 questions and five factors: ability, instinct, acceptance of change, control, and mental influence. Yu and Zhang [33] translated and revised it into Chinese, and three aspects have emerged from exploratory factor analysis: resilience (13 items), optimism (4 items), and strength (8 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The total scores range from 0 to 100, with a higher score suggesting greater psychological resilience. The Cronbach’s alpha of this scale in this study was 0.961.

Work-related quality of life Scale-2 (WRQoL-2)

Van Laar developed the the Work-related Quality of Life Scale (WRQoL) for evaluating individuals’ QWL in 2007 [34], and then further revised it into form Work-related Quality of Life Scale-2 (WRQoL-2) in 2013 [35]. The Chinese version of the WRQoL-2 was revised by Shao et al. [36] and demonstrated good reliability and validity. It has 7 factors and 34 items: Job and Career Satisfaction (JCS), General Well-Being (GWB), Home-Work Interface (HWI), Stress at Work (SAW), Control at Work (CAW), Working Conditions (WCS), and Employee evaluation (EEN). The 34th item was independent used by respondents for overall evaluation of QWL. The WRQoL-2 used a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Each factor score is the sum of the score of the factor divided by the number of factor entries. The total scale score is the sum of the factors scores divided by the number of factors. The higher the total score, the better the QWL. The scale’s Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.844.

Statistical analysis

The electronic questionnaire was downloaded and transcribed, and the data was analyzed using SPSS26.0. Descriptive statistics were employed for general demographic data and the research variables. The measuring data had a normal distribution and was reported by means and standard deviation; the counting data was represented by frequency and frequencies and percentages. Pearson correlation was used to examine the relationships between empathy, resilience, and QWL, while ANOVA and t-tests were utilized to examine demographic differences in QWL. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to investigate the impact of demographic factors and empathy and resilience on QWL. Finally, mediation analysis was performed using AMOS software, which examined the mediation role of resilience between empathy and QWL. The Bootstrap method of deviation correction was utilized in PROCESS for parameter estimation to report the 95% confidence interval.

Results

Test for common method bias

Since the data was collected by the same self-reported questionnaires, this study may exhibit common method bias. In addition to anonymous reverse and scoring different points scoring, we also used Harman’s single factor test to conduct non-rotating factor analysis for all items. The results revealed 6 common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 29% of the variance, less than 40%, thus there is no serious common method bias in this sample.

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 321 oncology nurses participated in this study, with males representing 92.21% and females representing 7.79%. The majority of the respondents was between 26 and 45 years old, accounting for 74.45%; marital status was mostly married, accounting for 69.16%. The largest proportion of working years was 30.22% for 6–10 years, followed by 24.3% for 10–20 years. In terms of professional titles, 71.96% are nurse practitioners and nurse in charge. Additionally, the majority of nurses had a bachelor’s degree (87.85) and a child (64.5%). The specific situation is shown in Table 1.

Discussion

The present cross-sectional study investigated the status of QWL among oncology nurses, explored the relationships of empathy, resilience and QWL and tested the mediating role of resilience among the relationship between empathy and QWL. Our results showed that the QWL score of oncology nurses was 3.05 ± 0.27 (1 ~ 5). Empathy, resilience, and QWL were positively associated with each other, and resilience played a significant mediating impact on the correlation among empathy and QWL.

In this study, Chinese oncology nurses showed moderate level of QWL, which was similar to the Arıkan Dönmez A’s [4] reports among oncology nurses. Among the scores of QWL dimensions, JCS and HWI scored the lowest, which was lower than the a study on Chinese nurses surveyed in 2013 [37] and a recent survey on nuclear magnetic resonance technology experts [38]. The possible reason was the increase in cancer patients and shortage of nurses due to Chinese aging population. Busy work schedules of nurses have led to low job satisfaction among oncology nurses and imbalance between family and work.

According to the findings, empathy was favorably correlated with the oncology nurses’ QWL, which was in line with the findings of Wang et al.’s study [17]. Their study of 725 hospice nurses revealed that greater empathy was associated with higher QWL. According to another study in 315 newly graduated nurses, empathy was significantly associated with QWL and affected turnover intention via the mediating role of QWL [28]. One of the main themes in a qualitative study that described the communication difficulties that oncology nurses encountered was their inability to demonstrate empathy, this provided insights for oncology nurses to receive communication training [28]. In order to better equip nurses for the emotional demands of clinical work, Curtis et al. [39] recommended that they first learn about compassion and be taught self-management skills. This would allow nurses to continue to offer compassionate care. The present study in conjunction with previous studies demonstrated that the favourable impact of empathy on QWL, which emphasized the development of prevention programs and intervention strategies to improve oncology nurses’ empathy abilities.

Resilience endows individuals with the ability to effectively respond to and adapt to adverse circumstances. When oncology nurses engage with patients and the department environment, they may pick up on negative or good information that is brought about by the environment. In the realm of nursing, resilience has been extensively researched [20, 22, 25], and has been shown to guard nurses against fatigue, support their mental health, enhance the quality of their sleep, and foster job satisfaction. In this study, we found that resilience was positively associated with QWL among oncology nurses. The favorable impact of resilience on oncology nurses’ QWL was further supported [20, 26]. Therefore, strategies aiming to enhance resilience may potentially contribute to better QWL outcomes, though causal inferences require longitudinal validation. A suggested and implemented evidence-based study to measure resilience among oncology healthcare professionals led to improvements in resilience ratings that persisted for six months [40]. Another study [41] highlighted the significance of organizational elements in enhancing resilience. They contended that it is challenging for employees to be flexible if the workplace fundamentally lacks enough resources, communication, collaboration, shared decision-making, support mechanisms, and recognition. According to a study on developing nurses’ capacity to handle emergencies, promoting interpersonal interactions is considered to be a fundamental step to strengthen the positive culture of individual units and organizations since individual flexibility training cannot address these organizational problems, and they believed building a resilient nursing team begins with strong social ties [37].

In this study, resilience played a partial mediating role in the relationship between empathy and QWL. Therefore, the research findings supported our research hypothesis. Rees et al. [42] proposed the International Collaboration on Workforce Resilience−1 (ICWR−1) model which has a number of crucial psychological factors (such as neuroticism, mindfulness and self-efficacy) that are anticipated to significantly affect psychological adjustment via the mediating role of resilience. Together with this study, empathy is an emotional characteristic of biopsychosocial, where empathy reflects the emotional response of warmth and sympathy to others’ suffering, which arouses the desire to assist patients. Nurses with cognitive empathy may manage patients’ situations logically and consider solutions to issues rather than becoming overwhelmed by patients’ circumstances [43]. Oncology nurses have higher levels of empathy may develop resilience by practicing their own empathic capabilities frequently and informally [30]. Then, higher levels of resilience perceived by oncology nurses further foster their QWL by their confidence in overcoming difficulties, positive coping strategies, and strong mutual interaction with supportive social resource [30]. Hence, we need to improve oncology nurses’ QWL by increasing empathy abilities and the level of resilience body and mind discomfort reactions among clinical nurses.

Overall, this study offers the first empirical evidence that the oncology nurses’ empathy positively predict QWL via the mediation role of resilience, which provides new insights into QWL enhancement and strategies to improve it in clinical settings. Our findings highlight associations that could inform theoretical frameworks for nursing management, pending causal verification. In addition to prioritize the quality of cancer patients care, it is also important to focus on the psychological health and the overall QWL of oncology nurses. Strategies targeting empathy enhancement warrant exploration in intervention studies to assess their possible effects on QWL.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study need to be acknowledged. Firstly, as a cross-sectional study, our findings reflect associations rather than causal relationships. The mediation model should be interpreted as describing potential pathways requiring longitudinal validation. Secondly, this study only investigated one third-class oncology hospital, which affected the representativeness of the samples. It remains to be investigated in hospitals of different regions and levels in the future to verify the results and conclusions of the study. Thirdly, the questionnaire collected using self-report tests that may be less precise and accurate than an evaluation conducted by an experienced clinician. These scales have some sub-dimensions, and the relationships between them has not been thoroughly studied, which may limit the accuracy and depth of the research results.

Practical implications

To explore potential applications of these associations in clinical practice, we propose targeted interventions for distinct stakeholders. For healthcare administrators, institutional initiatives should focus on: optimizing resource allocation to ensure appropriate nurse-to-patient ratios and specialized equipment; establishing trauma-informed support frameworks through evidence-based resilience programs; and implementing quarterly skill reinforcement workshops. Concurrently, oncology nurses should adopt personalized strategies including: emotional boundary management using self-monitoring tools; integration of micro-resilience practices such as brief mindfulness exercises during shift transitions and peer-support mechanisms; and active participation in unit-based advocacy committees to articulate resource needs and engage in scheduling decisions. Collectively, these multi-tiered initiatives address systemic and individual determinants of professional well-being. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish causal pathways, develop oncology-specific empathy metrics with behavioral anchors, and empirically test these interventions across varied resource settings.

Conclusions

This study is the first to explore the mediating role of resilience in the relationship between empathy and QWL among Chinese oncology nurses, which may provide theoretical basis for improving QWL among clinical nurses. Nursing managers should attach importance to effective training and organizational support for cultivating empathy abilities and developing resilience, thereby fostering QWL of oncology nurses.
