Incorporating Individual‐Level Treatment Effects and Outcome Preferences Into Personalized Blood Pressure Target Recommendations
Authors: Simon B. Ascher, Richard L. Kravitz, Rebecca Scherzer, Jarett D. Berry, James A. de Lemos, Michelle M. Estrella, Daniel J. Tancredi, Anthony A. Killeen, Joachim H. Ix, Michael G. Shlipak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are no shared decision‐making frameworks for selecting blood pressure (BP) targets for individuals with hypertension. This study addressed whether results from the SPRINT (Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial) could be tailored to individuals using predicted risks and simulated preferences.Methods and ResultsAmong 8202 SPRINT participants, Cox models were developed and internally validated to predict each individual's absolute difference in risk from intensive versus standard BP lowering for cardiovascular events, cognitive impairment, death, and serious adverse events (AEs). Individual treatment effects were combined using simulated preference weights into a net benefit, which represents a weighted sum of risk differences across outcomes. Net benefits were compared among those above versus below the median AE risk. In simulations for which cardiovascular, cognitive, and death events had much greater weight than the AEs of BP lowering, the median net benefit was 3.3 percentage points (interquartile range [IQR], 2.0–5.7), and 100% of participants had a net benefit favoring intensive BP lowering. When simulating benefits and harms to have similar weights, the median net benefit was 0.8 percentage points (IQR, 0.2–2.2), and 87% had a positive net benefit. Compared with participants at lower risk of AEs from BP lowering, those at higher risk had a greater net benefit from intensive BP lowering despite experiencing more AEs (P<0.001 in both simulations).ConclusionsMost SPRINT participants had a predicted net benefit that favored intensive BP lowering, but the degree of net benefit varied considerably. Tailoring BP targets using each patient's risks and preferences may provide more refined BP target recommendations.

Hypertension affects nearly half of all US adults and is the leading modifiable contributor to cardiovascular disease (CVD) and early death. 1 , 2 Although evidence from the SPRINT (Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial) and elsewhere supports treating blood pressure (BP) below a goal of 140/90 mm Hg, ongoing controversies over optimal BP targets complicate hypertension treatment decision‐making and represent a major challenge to address the worsening trends in BP control. 3 , 4 Hypertension guidelines recommend shared decision‐making to individualize BP targets, but no framework exists for tailoring the evidence about BP targets to individuals or for incorporating patient preferences into these decisions. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Several studies demonstrate that the expected benefits and harms from intensive BP lowering vary widely across individuals. 9 , 10 , 11 However, benefits and harms have largely been evaluated separately, rather than combined into a single construct such as net benefit (difference between benefits and harms), which might better facilitate treatment decision‐making. Considering patient preferences on the relative importance of different end points would allow for a direct comparison of benefit–harm trade‐offs according to an individual's personal values. 12 Previous work emerging from SPRINT showed that preferences can impact the benefit–harm trade‐offs of lower BP targets, but these efforts either applied patient preferences to population‐level treatment effects or used physician preferences, which may differ from those of patients. 13 , 14 , 15 In addition, these studies did not incorporate the cognitive benefits of intensive BP lowering recently demonstrated in SPRINT MIND (Memory and Cognition in Decreased Hypertension). 16 It is also unknown whether the benefit–harm balance of lower BP targets varies according to an individual's risk of experiencing adverse events (AEs) from BP lowering.

In the current study, we used SPRINT data to (1) develop and validate parsimonious models that predict multiple potential outcomes under conditions of intensive versus standard BP treatment for each SPRINT participant and (2) combine treatment effects together into an individualized net benefit using a plausible range of patient preferences that weigh the relative importance of each outcome. We hypothesized that (1) parsimonious prediction models would have adequate discrimination and calibration for each benefit and harm; (2) the magnitude of the individualized net benefits of intensive BP lowering would vary substantially across a range of predicted risks and outcome preferences; and (3) intensive BP lowering would be the preferred strategy for a large proportion of SPRINT participants, including those at high baseline risk of AEs.

METHODS

Study Design

The design and conduct of SPRINT have been reported previously. 3 , 17 In brief, SPRINT was a National Institutes of Health‐funded, open‐label clinical trial that randomized participants with hypertension and high CVD risk to an intensive systolic BP target (<120 mm Hg) versus a standard systolic BP target (<140 mm Hg). Key inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Data S1. A total of 9361 participants were enrolled between November 2010 and March 2013 across 102 sites in the United States and Puerto Rico. The SPRINT study was approved by the institutional review board at each participating study site, and all participants provided written informed consent. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Biologic Specimen and Data Repositories and the corresponding author upon request.

Predictors

Candidate baseline variables included 34 clinical variables identified in previous work to be related to hypertension and to be commonly available in the electronic health record. 11 Measurement and ascertainment of predictors are described in Data S1.

Outcomes

We evaluated outcomes that were adjudicated in SPRINT and grouped them based upon whether risk of the outcomes was lowered or increased by intensive BP treatment, and whether the outcomes would be expected to yield similar patient preferences. Benefit outcomes included (1) all‐cause death, (2) acute decompensated heart failure, (3) stroke, (4) myocardial infarction or acute coronary syndrome not resulting in myocardial infarction, (5) probable dementia or mild cognitive impairment. Harm outcomes included the following prespecified serious AEs in SPRINT: (6) acute kidney injury (AKI) or serious electrolyte abnormality; (7) syncope, hypotension, or bradycardia; and (8) injurious falls. The definition, ascertainment, and formal adjudication of these events have been previously described in detail. 3 , 16 , 17 , 18

Statistical Analysis

Development and Validation of Prediction Models

Cox proportional hazards models were fit to estimate the probability of experiencing the first occurrence of each outcome and were administratively censored at 4 years of follow‐up. SPRINT participants were also censored at death or the last available follow‐up when the trial stopped in August 2015. Because cognitive outcomes were also ascertained at an extended follow‐up visit between October 2017 and July 2018, the model for the composite of probable dementia or mild cognitive impairment was administratively censored at 5 years of follow‐up. Bayesian model averaging was then applied to guide the selection of a parsimonious set of predictors to be used across all 8 outcome models and is described in Data S1. 19 , 20 We selected the same set of predictors across models to facilitate clinical application. The relative treatment effect of BP lowering on each outcome was assumed to be constant across levels of baseline risk. 21

Model performance was assessed using measures of discrimination and calibration that were internally validated using 1000 bootstrap resamples to correct for optimism. 22 , 23 Discrimination was evaluated using the Harrell C statistic. Calibration was evaluated graphically with smoothed calibration curves and estimation of corresponding numerical summary measures. 24 These include the integrated calibration index, E 50, and E 90, which are the mean, median, and 90th percentiles of the absolute difference between the observed and predicted probabilities, and the calibration slope, which is a measure of spread of the predicted probabilities. Percentile‐based 95% CIs were calculated from 1000 bootstrap resamples of internally validated measures.

Estimation of Individualized Treatment Effects

For each individual, the probability of experiencing each outcome within 4 years (or 5 years for the composite cognitive outcome) given their baseline covariates was calculated using the parsimonious Cox models as: 1−S 0(t=4)e(X×β), where S 0(t) refers to the probability of not experiencing the outcome by time t, X is the set of baseline covariate values, and β is the set of model coefficients. The individualized treatment effect for each outcome was calculated as the predicted difference in absolute risk between intensive versus standard systolic BP lowering using an individual's baseline characteristics, treatment assignment, and counterfactual treatment assignment. 25 , 26

Individualized Net Benefits and Patient Preference Simulations

The individualized net benefit was calculated as the weighted sum of risk differences across the benefit and harm outcomes, with preference weights corresponding to the relative importance of each outcome for an individual. 12 , 27 Simulated preference weights were based on weights ascertained in previous studies 13 , 14 , 15 and consensus agreement (S.B.A., R.L.K., R.S., J.H.I., and M.G.S.). We simulated the following scenarios: (1) unknown preferences describes the scenario when preferences are not elicited and all outcomes are assumed to have equal weight; (2) harm tolerant describes preferences for a person who views the benefit outcomes (CVD events, cognitive impairment, and death) as much more important to avoid than the AEs from BP lowering; and (3) harm averse describes preferences for a person who views the benefit and harm outcomes as having similar intermediate importance. Death was assumed to have a preference weight of 1 in all simulations; thus, net benefit can be described as the summative effect of intensive BP lowering across all outcomes that have been scaled to their relative importance compared with death. A positive net benefit (>0) indicates the benefits of intensive BP lowering outweigh the harms, and a net benefit of 2 percentage points can be interpreted as intensive BP lowering leading to a cumulative 2 percentage points lower absolute risk of the weighted composite of events, with all events scaled relative to death. In sensitivity analyses, we evaluated whether prediction model coefficients were similar when using Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard regression models that account for the competing risk of death. 28

In subgroup analyses, we compared results among participants at higher versus lower risk of the AEs from BP lowering using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Baseline AE risk was determined by summing the predicted risks for each harm outcome (AKI, serious electrolyte abnormality, syncope, hypotension, bradycardia, and injurious falls). Individuals were then categorized as above or below the median AE risk.

All analyses were conducted using Stata (Stata Statistical Software, release 17; StataCorp, College Station, TX) and the R statistical computing language (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Model Development and Validation

From 34 candidate predictors, 7 were selected for inclusion in the parsimonious prediction model that was used across all outcomes: age, sex, current smoking, history of CVD, total number of medications, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and albuminuria (Table S1 and Table S2). In sensitivity analyses, prediction model coefficients were similar when accounting for the competing risk of death using Fine‐Gray models for each outcome (data not shown). After bootstrap internal validation, the models for each outcome had adequate discrimination and calibration (Table S3). The optimism‐corrected C statistics (95% CI) ranged from a low of 0.66 (0.64–0.69) for the syncope, hypotension, or bradycardia composite outcome, to a high of 0.80 (0.76–0.84) for heart failure. The optimism‐corrected, smoothed calibration curves and corresponding numerical summary measures (integrated calibration index, E50, E90, and calibration slope) indicated good agreement between the observed and predicted event probabilities (Figure S1).

Individualized Treatment Effects

The predicted difference in absolute risk associated with randomization to intensive versus standard BP lowering on each outcome varied across individual SPRINT participants (Figure 1). The absolute benefits of intensive BP lowering were generally larger for death and cognitive impairment than they were for stroke, heart failure, and myocardial infarction or acute coronary syndrome, and the absolute harms of intensive BP lowering were generally larger for AKI or electrolyte abnormality, and syncope, hypotension, or bradycardia than they were for injurious falls.

Individualized Net Benefits

The individualized net benefit combining the predicted risk differences for all outcomes substantially varied among SPRINT participants. Without weighing the relative importance of each outcome, only 47% of patients were predicted to experience a net benefit >0. However, after preference weights were added to each outcome, the benefit‐harm balance substantially shifted in favor of intensive BP lowering. When simulating a harm tolerant outcome preference profile, with CVD outcomes, cognitive outcomes, and death having much greater relative importance compared with the AEs from BP lowering, 100% of SPRINT participants had a predicted net benefit >0; 24% had a net benefit of 0 to 2 percentage points, 36% had a net benefit from 2 to 4 percentage points, and 40% experienced net benefits >4 percentage points (Figure 2A). When simulating a harm averse outcome preference profile, with each outcome having a similar, intermediate importance relative to death, 87% of SPRINT participants had a predicted net benefit >0. Among those with a positive net benefit, 69% had a net benefit of 0 to 2 percentage points, 18% had a net benefit from 2 to 4 percentage points, and 13% had a net benefit >4 percentage points (Figure 2B).

Among SPRINT participants at higher risk of AEs from BP lowering, intensive BP lowering increased the absolute risk of these AE events approximately two‐fold greater than in participants at lower AE risk. DISCUSSION

In this analysis of SPRINT, we derived prediction models for multiple benefit and harm outcomes related to BP lowering and used simulated patient preferences to weigh the relative importance of each outcome. We demonstrated that the majority of SPRINT participants had an individualized net benefit that favored intensive BP lowering (ie, benefits>harms). However, the magnitude of the individualized net benefit was sensitive to both predicted outcome risks and preference weighting. Importantly, although the SPRINT participants at highest risk for treatment‐related harms did experience more AEs from intensive BP lowering, they also derived substantial CVD, cognitive, and mortality benefits that resulted in greater predicted net benefits from intensive BP lowering in both harm tolerant and harm averse preference scenarios. These findings suggest that use of prediction models to determine probabilities for both benefits and harms, together with patient outcome preferences, may be a strategy to deliver evidence‐based, patient‐centered BP management in the context of shared decision‐making.

Hypertension guidelines emphasize using shared decision‐making to achieve better treatment adherence and BP control, but no framework exists for tailoring the evidence about BP targets to individuals. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Our findings suggest that incorporating a patient's estimated risks and preferences can inform a personalized BP target recommendation. Intuitively, clinicians recognize that treatment effects can vary from patient to patient, and that each patient has unique preferences and values; this makes it challenging to apply population‐level evidence in clinical practice. Although most SPRINT‐eligible individuals would be expected to have a personalized BP target recommendation in line with current hypertension guidelines, the wide variation in the magnitude of the predicted net benefits suggests that the strength of the recommendation should vary for each individual. Our findings also indicate that in some cases, an individual's estimated risk and preferences would suggest uncertain benefits or potential harm from intensive BP lowering.

Similar to our findings, previous studies have demonstrated that the predicted benefits and harms from intensive BP lowering vary widely among SPRINT participants. 9 , 10 , 11 However, these studies largely evaluated each outcome separately. In SPRINT, the average CVD, cognitive outcome, and mortality absolute risk reduction from intensive BP lowering is smaller in magnitude than the absolute increase in risk for serious AEs, in part because the AEs were more common. Because preventing a CVD event, cognitive impairment, or death is likely much more important to most individuals than, for example, an AKI event, we incorporated patient outcome preferences to place benefits and harms on a common scale, which allows patients to prioritize BP targets according to their personal values. This approach follows from prior studies that have used quantified preferences to translate the evidence of BP target trials to individuals. 13 , 15

The present study provides several novel contributions to the literature. First, whereas previous efforts evaluating the benefit–harm trade‐offs of lower BP targets either focused on physician preferences or used population‐level risk estimates, this analysis evaluated the impact of plausible patient preferences applied to individual‐level treatment effects, representing an important step toward personalized decision‐making. 13 , 15 Our finding that the individualized net benefit of intensive BP lowering was sensitive to the simulated weighting of each outcome reinforces the importance of incorporating patient preferences into BP target decisions. However, the prevalence and stability of preferences for BP lowering outcomes are unknown, and they may vary across different populations and settings. Additional studies are warranted to develop tools that reliably ascertain patient preferences for BP lowering outcomes, and to determine the degree to which preferences may vary over time.

Second, to our knowledge, this is one of the first studies evaluating the benefit–harm balance of different BP targets that incorporates the results from SPRINT MIND, which showed that intensive BP lowering led to a significant reduction in the composite of probable dementia or mild cognitive impairment. 16 Hypertension is a leading modifiable contributor to dementia and cognitive decline, and the prevalence of people living with dementia is expected to increase nearly 3‐fold by 2050. 29 However, current hypertension guideline recommendations primarily focus on the CVD and mortality benefits of lower BP targets. Given the high incidence of adverse cognitive outcomes in SPRINT, many SPRINT participants likely had large absolute cognitive benefits from intensive BP lowering that contributed to a positive net benefit. These findings illustrate the importance of considering the potential cognitive benefits of lower BP targets in tandem with the more widely recognized CVD and mortality benefits when making BP target decisions.

Third, although clinicians may be inclined to recommend a more conservative BP target for patients at increased risk of BP treatment‐related harms, we found that SPRINT participants at higher risk for harms had greater predicted net benefits from intensive BP lowering than those at lower risk for harms. This illustrates the hypertension treatment‐risk paradox observed in prior studies; individuals at higher risk for AEs from BP lowering may be less likely to be treated intensively by providers, although they would derive the most CVD, cognitive, and mortality benefits. 11 , 30 , 31 Consistent with this finding, prior studies from SPRINT have demonstrated that intensive BP lowering among the oldest and frailest participants leads to significant CVD and mortality benefits. 32 , 33 Meanwhile, for lower risk individuals who are also harm averse, it may be reasonable to be less aggressive with BP treatment. Additional work is needed to determine the risk thresholds at which BP target decisions become beneficial irrespective of an individual's outcome preferences, and to evaluate whether explicitly incorporating patient preferences into BP target decisions can help to overcome therapeutic inertia due to concerns about BP treatment‐related harms in clinical practice. 34

In the future, individualized BP target decisions could be operationalized using an interface within the electronic health record that allows for extracting clinical characteristics and for entering patient outcome preferences. A recommendation could be automatically applied to all hypertensive adults with systolic BP >140 mm Hg to prompt providers to engage with patients in setting BP targets. Such an approach to translating the evidence about BP targets to individuals would deliver a personalized approach to shared decision‐making in hypertension, but it would require time and effective risk communication, and it serves as only the first step toward achieving BP control. We also envision a similar framework of applying individual‐level treatment effects from clinical trials in other settings, for example anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation, antiplatelet duration following percutaneous coronary intervention, and chemotherapy for cancer, where patient outcome preferences could be used to personalize treatment decisions.

Our study has several important limitations. Although the derived prediction models had adequate performance within SPRINT, widespread application of our findings to the clinical setting requires external validation. Second, our study may not have incorporated the full spectrum of outcome preferences relevant to BP target decision‐making, such as medication burden, financial costs, and additional antihypertensive side effects (eg, cough from angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or lower extremity edema from dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers). Third, although our analyses may have had less statistical power by using individual events instead of a composite CVD end point or a composite of treatment‐related harms, intensive BP lowering impacts each outcome differently, and patients may prioritize the avoidance of each specific outcome differently. Fourth, we applied a single baseline assessment of risk and preferences; both may change over time in some patients. Finally, a common set of variables was selected across all models for ease of application, but future studies may consider developing unique models for each outcome.

In summary, we demonstrated in SPRINT that combining the effects of intensive BP lowering on multiple benefit and harm outcomes with simulated preferences for each outcome led to most SPRINT participants having an individualized net benefit that favored intensive BP lowering. However, the degree of net benefit varied with baseline risk of these outcomes and across a range of simulated outcome preferences. Contrary to what clinicians may perceive in clinical practice, we found that individuals at higher risk of treatment‐related harms had greater predicted net benefits from intensive BP lowering. Our study shows that evidence from SPRINT can be translated to SPRINT‐eligible individuals within a shared decision‐making framework that directly incorporates individual‐level risks and preferences. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether a new paradigm of applying personalized BP targets to clinical practice can lead to improved BP control.
