Identification of anoikis-related tumor microenvironment characteristics and prognostic signature in ovarian cancer at bulk and single-cell levels
Authors: Zhenye Tang, Chenfei Zhou, Yinyin Xu, Zhizai Chen, Shuting Huang, Jing Chen, Ping Jin, Jiemei Hu, Shanyang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer (OC) remains among the deadliest cancers in women worldwide. Anoikis, a critical process that prevents the establishment of detached cells in non-native sites, is closely associated with cancer cell aggressiveness and poor patient outcomes. Despite its significance, research into the prognostic impact of anoikis-related genes (ARGs) in OC remains scant.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was adopted to analyze anoikis activity. We used 41 ARGs across distinct cell types for this analysis. The genomic and clinicopathological data was sourced from GSE26712 project (training cohort) and TCGA-OV project (independent validation set), respectively. Cox regression and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) technique were utilized to develop an anoikis-related risk score (ANRS) for prognosis evaluation. Additionally, the correlation between ANRS and tumor microenvironment (TME) characteristics was explored. Functional experiments were conducted to elucidate the molecular role of the key gene AP1S2 in OC.ResultsSurvival analyses in both cohorts categorized OC patients into two groups based on the median ANRS. The high-ANRS category exhibited evidently worse survival outcomes. Our findings highlighted a strong link between ARGs and TME characteristics, particularly the stromal components, at both bulk and single-cell transcriptomic levels. This underscores the complex interplay between cancer progression and the tumor-promoting stroma. Additionally, AP1S2 knockdown markedly reduced the proliferative and aggressive capabilities of OC cells.ConclusionThe ANRS-derived prognostic tool offers substantial promise for advancing our understanding of OC progression and assisting gynecologists in developing effective treatment strategies for women suffering from this malignancy.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is still acknowledged as the most lethal gynecological malignancy worldwide [1, 2]. The challenges of limited surgical options, reduced responsiveness to chemotherapeutic agents, and metastatic spread are significant barriers to developing new therapeutic strategies and improving survival outcomes for women suffering from OC [3]. One major issue is that most OC cases are not detected until they are in an advanced stage because there are no effective screening methods or early signs [4]. This leads to a high death rate [5, 6]. Consequently, the 5-year survival rate for many women afflicted by this disease remains alarmingly low (less than 48%) [7]. Therefore, the derivation and validation of a useful and reliable prognostic tool is imperative to elucidate the pathological mechanisms of ovarian malignancy and to identify more effective treatments.

Over recent decades, anti-cancer strategies have evolved from conventional broad-spectrum cytotoxic chemotherapy toward precision medicine approaches. These not only exploit intrinsic vulnerabilities of malignant cells but also strategically modulate the tumor microecosystem, encompassing stromal and immune constituents [8, 9]. Accumulating evidence highlights that such a dual-pronged strategy, simultaneously targeting tumor cell-autonomous signaling and remodeling the tumor microenvironment (TME), offers superior efficacy in mitigating tumor progression and metastatic dissemination [10, 11]. OC, with its high heterogeneity and aggressive nature, underscores the urgent need for integrated therapeutic approaches to improve patient outcomes. Anoikis, a cell death mechanism activated by cell-matrix dissociation, serves as a critical biological process interconnecting malignant cells with their intricate microecosystem by preventing disseminated cells from colonizing unsuitable locations, thereby maintaining physiological homeostasis [12, 13]. Recent studies suggest that anoikis resistance exerts crucial influence in the proliferative migration of OC. For example, Cai et al. demonstrated that the activation of the Src/Akt/Erk axis and metabolic rewiring facilitates OC cell evasion of anoikis, thereby accelerating cancer progression [14]. Similarly, Cheng et al. found that a kind of oncogenic RAB protein, RAB25, disrupts anoikis regulation and increases OC invasiveness by triggering the PI3K/AKT axis and suppressing pro-death proteins [15]. Furthermore, the invasiveness of terminal and relapsed OC in bowel and omentum has been attributed to resistance to anoikis-mediated apoptosis [16]. This is exemplified by the interaction between integrin-dependent networks and extracellular matrix (ECM) molecules, leading to the elevated activity of focal adhesion molecules, abnormal OC cell adherence to collagens, and extensive settlement within the peritoneal cavity [17]. Consequently, anoikis dysregulation is emerging as a defining characteristic of malignant tumors, particularly OC [18].

Here, we aim to establish an anoikis-related prognostic proxy to uncover the relation between anoikis-related genes (ARGs) and their clinical implications in OC. By examining the link between ARGs and TME characteristics at both single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bulk RNA-seq transcriptome levels, and experimentally investigating the role of the key gene AP1S2, we hope to uncover therapeutic avenues with significant potential for women afflicted with ovarian malignancy.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The genomics data (transcripts per million) including 153 tumor samples from GSE26712 dataset and 374 cancer specimens from TCGA-OV dataset with corresponding clinicopathological data were accessed from the UCSC Xena project (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) and GEO project (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) respectively [19]. Only cases with incomplete pathologic parameters were removed. The list of 41 ARGs was accessed from MSigDB (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) and GeneCard websites (https://www.genecards.org/) .

Analysis of scRNA-seq data

We retrieved the scRNA-seq dataset from a previously published study titled “High-grade serous tubo-ovarian cancer refined with single-cell RNA sequencing: specific cell subtypes influence survival and determine molecular subtype classification”. This dataset consists of 18,611 cells from 12 clinical samples, including two primary high-grade serous tubo-ovarian carcinomas (HGSTOC), one normal ovary tissue, one metastasis from HGSTOC to the omentum, one normal omentum tissue, six metastases from HGSTOC to the peritoneum, and one normal peritoneum tissue sample. These samples were derived from seven treatment-naive ovarian cancer (OC) patients [20]. For our analysis, we selected all the cancer samples, comprising 13,809 cells from two primary lesions and seven metastatic lesions. Data processing was performed using the ‘Seurat’ R package. Cells were filtered by retaining those expressing more than 200 genes but fewer than 10,000 genes, with mitochondrial gene content less than 15% and hemoglobin gene content less than 5%. To address batch effects, we applied the canonical correlation analysis (CCA) algorithm implemented in Seurat. The count matrix was normalized using the ‘NormalizeData’ function. We identified 2,000 highly variable genes using the ‘FindVariableFeatures’ function. Subsequently, we performed data scaling and principal component analysis (PCA) using the ‘ScaleData’ and ‘RunPCA’ functions, respectively. For cluster identification, the ‘FindNeighbors’ and ‘FindClusters’ methods were employed. Dimensionality reduction and cluster visualization were performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Each cluster was then annotated based on classical gene markers. Anoikis scores (ANSs) were calculated using the “AddModuleScore” function to quantify ARG activity by comparing their average expression to control genes, enabling the identification of clusters with distinct anoikis-related activity. Finally, we used the ‘CellChat’ R package to analyze cell–cell communication patterns, inferring ligand–receptor interactions and cancer-associated signaling pathways in ovarian cancer.

Derivation of anoikis-related prognostic predictive tool

Univariate Cox regression algorithm was applied to demonstrate the potential impact of ARGs on survival outcomes of GSE26712 cases. And we chose prognostic ARGs with p < 0.05 as the criterion for further least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method by utilizing the R “glmnet” package. The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined through 10-fold cross-validation based on the minimum criteria, identifying 7 genes significantly associated with clinical outcomes in OC cases, each assigned a corresponding coefficient. N denotes the number of prognosis-related ARGs, expi indicates the expression level of each ARG, i refers to an individual ARG, and coefi corresponds to its respective regression coefficient obtained from the analysis [21, 22]. Then we selected Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival tool derived from R “survival” and “survminer” packages to visualize the clinical results of OC cases classified into two populations exhibiting different ANRS in GSE26712 training project and TCGA-OV validation project. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve originated in R “timeROC” package was executed to demonstrate the ANRS-derived predictive tool with good performance. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression algorithms were chosen to confirm the role of ANRS in independently forecasting survival outcomes of OC cases.Moreover, R ‘rms’ package was devoted to creating a predictive nomogram integrating ANRS and pathological variables for survival evaluation of patients. We utilized calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) at 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year to assess the predictive performance of the nomogram.

Association of ANRS and TME characteristics

A comprehensive single-cell atlas of the multicellular ecosystem comprising seven cell types in OC was established. We classified cell types according to literature-derived marker genes. Each cluster with varying anoikis scores (ANSs) was identified using the AddModuleScore algorithm in Seurat, which quantified cell-type-specific activity by scoring expression levels of predefined ARGs. We employed CellChat function to infer interactions between cells. At the bulk level, R ‘ESTIMATE’ tool of ‘IOBR’ package was devoted to deriving the TME scores including stromal score, immune score and ESTIMATE score for OC patients. Furthermore, we investigated the relationship of ANRS and TME scores. ‘EPIC’ tool of ‘IOBR’ R package was subsequently applied to assess the correlation of the abundance of tumor infiltration cells and ANRS [23]. And ‘ssGSEA’ tool was adopted to assess immunocyte abundance and immune function of OC [24].

Relationship between ANRS and chemosensitivity strategies

We utilized R “oncoPredict” package to compute the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of chemotherapeutic agents for cancer cases [25].

Cell culture and transfection

The OC cell lines CAOV3 and SKOV3 and noncancerous ovarian epithelial cell line IOSE-80 were obtained commercially from Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. And the cell lines were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A(SKOV3), DMEM (CAOV3) and 1640 (IOSE-80), added with 10% fetal bovine serum. si-AP1S2 and negative control (si-NC) were obtained commercially from Thermo-Fisher (USA). And Lipo3000 (Invitrogen, USA) was adopted to transfect the SKOV3 and CAOV3 cells.

Quantitative real‑time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‑PCR)

Total RNA was harvested from cell lines by TRIzol Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). And we acquired cDNA using the reverse transcription kit (Mei5 Biotechnology, China). Relative mRNA levels of AP1S2 were normalized employing 18 S Ribosomal RNA. The primers were: AP1S2, F: 5′- TTCAGACCGTTTTAGCACGGA-3′ and R: 5′- TGTCCTGATCCTCAATAGCACA-3′; 18 S Ribosomal RNA, F: 5′- ATCACCATTATGCAGAATCCACG-3′ and R: 5′- GACCTGGCTGTATTTTCCATCC-3′.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The CCK-8 assay was employed to assess cell proliferation due to its simplicity, low cytotoxicity, and superior accuracy and reproducibility compared to other cell viability assays such as MTT and WST-1 [26, 27]. Cells were maintained at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates firstly. Then 10µL of CCK8 solution (GLPBIO, USA) was added to each well at specified time points. Incubation was completed for 2 h at 37 °C before optical density values at 450 nm (OD450) in 0 h, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h were detected by employing a spectrophotometer.

Colony formation analysis

Given its ability to reflect long-term proliferation and clonogenic potential, the colony formation assay was preferred over the EdU incorporation assay, which mainly captured short-term DNA synthesis [27, 28]. 2000 transfected SKOV3 and CAOV3 cells were inoculated in a 6-well plate and cultured for two weeks, respectively. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The cell colonies were further quantitatively analyzed after being dyed with crystalline violet.

Wound healing

Cells were maintained in 6-well plates and incubated until 90–95% confluence. Then wounds were obtained by utilizing a 200μL pipette tip before cells were resuspended with serum-free medium. Images were captured to assess the migration rate as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Migration\:Rate=\frac{Initial\:wound\:area-Remaining\:wound\:area\:at\:time\:T}{Initial\:wound\:area}$$\end{document}

The initial wound area was measured at 0 h, and the remaining wound area was quantified at the designated time points (24, 48 h) using ImageJ [27].

Transwell invasion and migration assay

5 × 104 transfected cells were plated into transwell upper chambers (JETBIOFIL, China), to which 200 µL of serum-free medium was added. The upper of chamber was precoated with Matrigel. 600µL medium added with 10% FBS was provided in the lower culture plate. Following a 12–48 h incubation, the upper surface of the membrane was scraped off. And the invaded cells on the underside of the membrane were dyed with crystal violet for 30 min after being fixed in methanol. The invasion capacities of invaded cells were evaluated by microscope. Five visual fields were captured per plate, and the cell counting was performed utilizing Image J. Additionally, we evaluated cell migration by performing Transwell migration assay. The cells underwent the identical treatment as in Transwell invasion assay, excluding Matrigel-precoated chambers. The invaded cells were fixed, stained, and counted in the same manner as the migration assay [27]. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

R 4.3.1 version and GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 were utilized for data processing and accessing significant distinctions between various categories. KM survival curves contributed to derive the clinical results. p < 0.05 denoted statistical significance. (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns: not significant)

Results

Anoikis characteristics of OC in single-cell transcriptomics

Initially, we examined the involvement of 41 ARGs within the TME of OC, utilizing scRNA-seq data. This dataset comprised 13,809 cells derived from seven untreated OC patients. Through initial cell clustering, we classified the cells into seven distinct subpopulations (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, we annotated these cells as epithelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells, T cells, B cells, macrophages, and ovarian stromal cells, based on previously established marker genes [20]. The principal marker genes for each subpopulation were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 1b). To quantify anoikis resistance, we calculated ANSs for individual cells using the 41 ARGs, employing the AddModuleScore algorithm within the Seurat R package, alongside a Wilcoxon rank-sum test with CAFs serving as the reference group. Boxplots and t-SNE plots revealed differential ANSs among cell subtypes, with CAFs displaying generally higher ANSs compared to other cells (Fig. 1c, d). Subsequently, we segregated the CAFs into groups with high/low ANSs according to median values, identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between these groups employing the FindAllMarkers algorithm in Seurat, employing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for statistical significance (adjusted p-value < 0.05). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these DEGs highlighted their enrichment in biological processes and signaling pathways pertinent to immune responses and tumor advancement (Fig. 1e). Literature suggests that CAFs enhance tumor cell proliferation and migration, marking them as adverse prognostic indicators in OC [29, 30]. Additionally, we conducted cell communication analysis via the CellChat package to explore the interactions between CAFs of varying ANSs and malignant epithelial cells within the TME. Results demonstrated that CAFs with elevated ANSs engaged in more frequent communication with malignant epithelial cells compared to their low-ANSs counterparts (Fig. 2a). These high-ANSs CAFs predominantly influenced collagen, fibronectin 1 (FN1), heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG) and insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling pathways, which are integral to the adhesion, anoikis-resistant invasion and proliferative growth of cancer cells (Fig. 2b).

Development of an anoikis-related prognostic model for OC patients

Preliminary single-cell transcriptome analysis highlighted the potential of ARGs as key drivers in OC progression. Building on this foundation, we extended our investigation to examine ARGs within the context of bulk RNA-seq transcriptomics. Employing univariate Cox regression analysis on 41 ARGs within the GSE26712 dataset, we identified eight genes—IGFBP4, WTAP, AP1S2, ISG20, CCT5, GPM6B, SPINT2, and AP1M2—as significantly prognostic for OC outcomes (p < 0.05, Fig. 3a). Excluding AP1M2, seven genes were refined through LASSO regression analysis as a prognostic gene set for OC, based on optimality criteria (Fig. 3b). Subsequently, anoikis-related risk scores (ANRS) for 153 patients were calculated using the coefficients of these seven prognostic ARGs (Fig. 3c). Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, illustrating the area under the curve (AUC) for one, three, and five years, evidenced AUC values of 0.666, 0.720, and 0.788, respectively, affirming the prognostic signature’s reliability using the ‘timeROC’ R package (Fig. 3d). This prognostic model effectively stratified the 153 patients into high-risk (n = 76) and low-risk (n = 77) subgroups, based on the median ANRS. KM survival analysis revealed significantly better outcomes for patients in the low-ANRS category (Fig. 3e). Validation in TCGA-OV project, employing the identical methodology, categorized 374 cases into comparable high-risk (n = 187) and low-risk (n = 187) sets. Consistently, mortality rates were notably reduced in the low-risk subtype versus cases with high-ANRS, corroborating the GSE26712-derived prognostic signature (Fig. 3f).

Independent prognostic significance of ANRS signature

The distribution of the ANRS and life status scatter plots, along with the heatmap of the ANRS signature gene expression levels, were depicted in the GSE26712 and TCGA-OV cohorts (Fig. 4a, b). Leveraging the extensive clinical data of the TCGA-OV cohort, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted on overall survival (OS) within the TCGA-OV set to ascertain the prognostic efficacy of ANRS. And these results affirmed the ANRS as an available and valuable prognostic proxy for OC cases, with HR greater than 1 and p-value less than 0.05 (Fig. 4c). Consequently, we established the ANRS as a formidable, independent prognostic factor for OC. In furtherance of elucidating the clinical utility of the ANRS, a nomogram incorporating three clinicopathological parameters was developed (Fig. 4d). Calibration plots of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS demonstrated the nomogram’s capacity to predict prognosis with commendable precision and dependability (Fig. 4e). Additionally, the DCA for the ANRS and other clinical variables at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals highlighted the proficiency of ANRS signature in accurately forecasting the OS for OC patients (Fig. 4f).

TME characterization in two OC subgroups

To elucidate the association between tumor infiltration status in OC and the bulk transcriptome, the ‘ESTIMATE’ method, implemented via the ‘IOBR’ R package, was initially employed to derive the TME scores for patients across two subtypes. Analysis revealed that, in comparison to the low-ANRS subset, the high-ANRS subset exhibited elevated stromal and ESTIMATE scores within the GSE26712 dataset, albeit without significant disparities in immune scores between the two risk subtypes (Fig. 5a). Further, a positive statistical correlation was observed between ANRS and both stromal and ESTIMATE scores (Fig. 5b). The ‘EPIC’ function of the ‘IOBR’ R package was subsequently adopted to compute the proportion of tumor-infiltrating cells. Notably, the distribution of stromal cells differed between subtypes, with increased infiltration of CAFs and endothelial cells observed in the high-risk subgroup of the GSE26712 cohort (Fig. 5c). Employing the ssGSEA technique enabled the quantification of diverse immunocyte infiltrations and the assessment of biological pathway activities for both groups. It was found that activated and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (aDCs and pDCs, respectively) were prevalent in the low-risk set, whereas the abundance of neutrophils displayed the inverse trend (Fig. 6a). Additionally, specific immune-related pathways, including those promoting inflammation, para-inflammation, MHC class I, and Type I IFN response, were predominantly active in the low-risk subtype, with the Type II IFN response being more pronounced in the group exhibiting higher ANRS (Fig. 6a). Correspondingly, increases in stromal scores aligned with rising ANRS within the TCGA-OV dataset, alongside greater abundance of CAFs and endothelial cells in the higher ANRS group (Fig. 7a-c). In the TCGA-OV set, immunocytes, such as natural killer (NK) cells, aDCs, Th1 cells, and various immune pathways including APC co-inhibition, checkpoint, cytolytic activity, HLA, inflammation promotion, MHC class I, para-inflammation, T cell co-inhibition, and Type I IFN response, were more pronounced in the low-ANRS subtype. Conversely, mast cell levels and Type II IFN response were elevated in the high-ANRS subgroup (Fig. 6b). Thus, these integrated findings from both datasets affirm the ANRS signature’s correlation with OC’s immune status.

Correlation analysis of ANRS and chemotherapeutic application

The utility of the ANRS in gauging the efficacy of immunotherapy and pinpointing potential therapeutic targets has been substantiated. Analysis integrating data from two independent cohorts (GSE26712 and TCGA-OV) revealed differential chemosensitivity associated with ANRS levels. Specifically, GSK1904529A, an inhibitor of the insulin-like growth factor − 1 receptor (IGF1R), demonstrated enhanced sensitivity in patients exhibiting high ANRS values (Fig. 8a). Conversely, AZD2014, a dual PI3K/MTOR inhibitor, showed a heightened therapeutic response in OC patients characterized by low ANRS (Fig. 8b).

Elucidation of the functional phenotype of AP1S2 in OC cells

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified AP1S2 as a pivotal prognostic ARG, exhibiting the highest HR (HR = 1.549, 95% CI: 1.052–2.281, P = 0.027), indicative of its potential role as a driving gene in OC progression (Fig. 3a). KM survival analysis further revealed that upregulated AP1S2 expression was linked to adverse survival results of OC patients (P = 0.017, Fig. 9a). Immunohistochemistry data sourced from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) online project (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) demonstrated significantly higher AP1S2 expression in OC tissues compared to non-cancerous ovarian tissues (Fig. 9b). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that AP1S2 mRNA levels were significantly upregulated in SKOV3 and CAOV3 cells. Specifically, AP1S2 expression was 1.63-fold higher in CAOV3 cells and 7.49-fold higher in SKOV3 cells compared to noncancerous ovarian epithelial cells (IOSE80) (Fig. 9c). Both SKOV3 and CAOV3 cell lines were selected for in vivo experimentation to further validate AP1S2’s functionality, given the pronounced difference in AP1S2 expression compared to IOSE80 cells (P < 0.0001). Post-siRNA-mediated knockdown, AP1S2 expression levels in SKOV3 and CAOV3 cells were significantly reduced (Figs. 9d and 10a). Colony formation assays unveiled that AP1S2 silencing markedly downregulated the proliferative growth of OC cells (Figs. 9e and 10b), a finding corroborated by CCK-8 assays (Figs. 9f and 10c). The wound healing assay demonstrated that AP1S2 knockdown significantly attenuated the migratory potential of SKOV3 and CAOV3 cells. (Figures 9g and 10d). Subsequent Transwell assays provided insight into AP1S2’s influence on cell invasion and migration, revealing that AP1S2 inhibition significantly reduced the invasive and migratory capabilities of OC cells (Figs. 9h-i and 10e-f). Collectively, these findings suggest that AP1S2 facilitates the malignant progression of OC, positioning it as a viable therapeutic target.

Discussion

OC remains the foremost cause of death from gynecological malignancies worldwide, despite significant advancements in clinical management strategies, including surgery and chemotherapy and targeted therapies [31–33]. A substantial proportion of cases are diagnosed at a late stage, marked by high rates of recurrence and chemotherapy resistance [34]. Traditionally, pathological factors such as age and stage are utilized to predict OC prognosis and inform treatment decisions. Yet, individuals with the same FIGO cancer stage often present markedly disparate prognoses and responses to treatment, underscoring the limitations of clinicopathological parameters in predicting outcomes for OC. The development of a robust predictive signature is crucial for enhancing preclinical diagnosis, prognostication, and the customization of therapy for women afflicted with ovarian malignancies[35]. Anoikis, a specific form of programmed cell death, prevents ectopic colonization by detaching dysplastic cells, thereby inhibiting their settlement in unsuitable locations. Dysregulation of anoikis is a hallmark of cancer, facilitating metastatic spread and contributing to resistance against anticancer therapies, garnering significant research interest [36]. Consequently, targeting genes associated with anoikis presents a promising therapeutic strategy to curb metastasis and enhance the prognosis of OC cases.

In this research, a highly sensitive ANRS signature was developed, derived from seven specific ARGs: IGFBP4, WTAP, AP1S2, ISG20, CCT5, GPM6B, and SPINT2. This signature demonstrated exceptional predictive accuracy for the survival probabilities within the GSE26712 and TCGA-OV cohorts. Specifically, it enabled the stratification of patients into groups with distinct OS outcomes. Notably, a significant survival time disparity was demonstrated between the high- and low-ANRS subtypes. Furthermore, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses affirmed the ANRS as an independent predictor of clinical outcomes such as age and stage in OC patients. Additionally, a nomogram incorporating the ANRS alongside pathological factors containing age and stage has emerged as a potentially valuable tool for predicting OC prognosis and aiding in the formulation of clinical management strategies. Collectively, these results underscore the significant clinical and translational potential of the ANRS in OC.

We expanded beyond the scope of previous studies by employing bulk and single-cell transcriptome analyses to ascertain the roles of ARGs. Our approach led to the development of an ANRS signature based on seven specific ARGs. This signature notably outperformed existing prognostic indicators in predicting survival outcomes within the GSE26712 and TCGA-OV cohorts. Initially, we quantified ANSs at a single-cell level, revealing that CAFs exhibited the highest ANSs, underscoring their significant role in anoikis mechanisms. Subsequently, CAFs were classified into two distinct groups based on their ANSs, which facilitated a detailed examination of their interactions with malignant cells in the TME. Our findings showed that higher-scoring CAFs engaged more intensively with OC cells, suggesting a direct correlation between ANS levels and the extent of cell-cell communication.

Additionally, our analysis uncovered significant dynamics in intercellular communication, particularly through signaling pathways mediated by adhesion-related molecules like collagen, HSPGs, FN1 and IGF. These molecules play crucial roles in facilitating cancer progression. Our results align with existing literature highlighting dysregulated collagen production, the influence of IGF on cell-matrix adhesion and ECM remodeling, the involvement of HSPGs in distant spread and anoikis-resistant phenotype, and the role of FN1 in the survival of CTCs during metastasis in TME [37–41].

At the transcriptome-wide level, the prognostic relevance of the ANRS was further validated through applications of ESTIMATE, EPIC, and ssGSEA analyses, confirming patterns of immune infiltration. Notably, a correlation was established between elevated ANRS and increased stromal and ESTIMATE scores across both cohorts analyzed. This suggests a significant association between tumor stromal interactions and anoikis resistance patterns, consistent with findings from our single-cell analysis.

Our study also highlighted differences in chemotherapy responses between groups with high and low ANRS in GSE26712 and TCGA-OV cohorts, with high-ANRS subtypes demonstrating sensitivity to IGF1R inhibitors, and low-ANRS groups responding more favorably to PI3K/MTOR inhibitors. This underscores the potential of targeting specific cancer-promoting signaling pathways for improved therapeutic outcomes.

Moreover, we studied the relation between prognostic stratification and immune infiltration, utilizing ssGSEA to assess immune function. Our findings indicated that low-ANRS groups exhibited heightened activity in anti-cancer immune cycle steps, suggesting more robust anti-tumor immune activity [42, 43].

Collectively, our comprehensive analysis emphasizes the critical role of the ANRS in understanding the complex dynamics of the TME and its implications for OC progression and treatment. This work advocates for the integration of the ANRS in developing personalized therapeutic approaches and highlights the potential of anti-stromal therapies as a promising strategy for disrupting the TME [44].

Regarding previously published ARG prognostic models in OC, we noted that most studies characterized the relationship between ARGs and the TME only at the bulk RNA transcriptome level, which limited the resolution of cellular heterogeneity and had limited prognostic value (e.g., GSE30161 validation, P > 0.05) [45, 46]. Bulk sequencing captured average gene expression efficiently but missed cellular heterogeneity, while single-cell sequencing revealed diverse cell populations despite higher complexity; integrating both understanding of cancer biology [47, 48]. Integrative analysis of bulk and scRNA transcriptomic data had substantially advanced our understanding of TME heterogeneity and prognostic relevance [49]. Uniquely, concordant bulk and single-cell findings linked increased CAF infiltration and associated communication networks involving collagen, FN1, and IGF signaling and with higher ANRS and poorer prognosis, reinforcing the model’s biological and clinical relevance. Besides, ANRS signature demonstrated significant prognostic value validated across independent cohorts, highlighting its strong clinical potential. As such, ANRS model might serve as a robust risk stratification tool and personalized treatment decision support system that optimized therapeutic strategies and improved clinical outcomes for OC patients.

AP1S2, an important prognostic factor associated with anoikis, emerged as a tumor promoter in our study. We noted a marked upregulation of AP1S2 protein expression in ovarian malignancies compared to normal ovarian tissue, as validated through immunohistochemistry. This expression pattern was further confirmed in OC cells via in vitro qRT-PCR. Functional assays conducted on OC cells revealed that downregulation of AP1S2 significantly inhibited cell proliferation, motility, and invasiveness, suggesting an oncogenic role for AP1S2 in OC. This is in line with findings by Luan et al., who demonstrated that LICN00518 enhances melanoma metastatic growth by modulating AP1S2, and Gu et al., who observed reduced proliferation and invasion in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) cells following AP1S2 knockdown, mirroring our results [50, 51]. Therefore, aberrant activation of AP1S2 may serve as a novel biomarker for OC. As conventional biomarker-targeted chemotherapeutics tended to cause side effects and resistance, combining them with traditional medicine-derived compounds might lead anti-anoikis approaches toward a more favorable therapeutic profile [52].

However, our study has several limitations. First, the validation of the prognostic model for ARGs was limited to retrospective public datasets, which may introduce batch effects and platform-related variability due to differences in sample collection, processing and sequencing technologies. Moreover, the majority of these datasets consist of patients from specific ethnic backgrounds, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings across diverse populations. Future studies involving multi-center, prospective cohorts with standardized protocols and more ethnically diverse populations are essential to further validate and generalize the ANRS model. Additionally, in-depth exploration of the biological functions and translational relevance of AP1S2 and other pivotal ARGs in OC progression is crucial, with validation efforts encompassing mechanistic studies in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mouse models and biomarker-driven monitoring strategies, including liquid biopsy, to assess their detectability and clinical applicability. Finally, clinical implementation of the ANRS model faces challenges, including the high cost and limited accessibility of high-throughput transcriptome sequencing and the need for standardized and reproducible bioinformatics pipelines for score calculation. Overcoming these is essential for clinical application.

Conclusions

In summary, our study elucidated the relationship between anoikis mechanisms and the TME through comprehensive analysis of scRNA-seq and bulk RNA sequencing data. Moreover, we introduced an anoikis-related risk model derived from bulk RNA-seq datasets, incorporating seven prognostic genes. This model demonstrates a high degree of accuracy in forecasting the OS of OC cases and provides insights into the TME characteristics. As such, this prognostic assessment tool holds significant potential for enhancing our understanding of OC progression and aiding clinicians in devising robust and effective treatment strategies for women afflicted with OC.
