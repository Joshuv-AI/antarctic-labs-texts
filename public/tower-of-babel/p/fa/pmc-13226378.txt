Anesthesia during cesarean section in women with congenital heart disease
Authors: Susanne Greve, Nathalie Weigt, Christiane E. Beck, Anna Fischbach, Tim Heusinger von Waldegg, Sebastian Heiderich, Vanessa Rigterink, Steffen B. Wiegand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen with congenital heart disease (CHD) often reach childbearing age and may become pregnant. In this study we examine the anesthetic management of cesarean section (C-section) in a university hospital certified for CHD.MethodsThe anesthesiological care for women with CHD who presented at our certified university hospital between January 2013 and December 2023 was descriptively analyzed. We restricted analysis to women who underwent C‑section (n = 38) and the women were classified according to the complexity of the CHD based on the WHO classification of CHD into I mild (A; n = 6), II moderate (B; n = 13) or III severe (C; n = 19). This study is mainly descriptive and serves as a preliminary study for a larger prospective multicenter study.ResultsMost of the C‑sections were planned and scheduled (A: n = 3; 50%; B: n = 11; 85% and C: n = 16; 84%) for the dayshift. Anesthesia was performed in more than two thirds of cases by two anesthesiologists. Neuraxial anesthesia was most often performed to achieve adequate surgical tolerance. The frequency of arterial catheter and central venous catheter placements were similar among the groups. Although sometimes introducer sheaths were placed to enable faster introduction of a cannula for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), no use of ECMO was necessary. Postpartum women with severe CHD were more likely to be admitted to the ICU. None of the mothers or newborns died.ConclusionThis study aimed to describe the anesthesiological management in women with different severity of CHD undergoing C‑section. This management requires a multidisciplinary approach within a pregnancy heart team. Due to the overall low frequencies and heterogeneity of the CHDs larger multicenter studies are necessary to develop recommendations for obstetric anesthesia in pregnant women with CHD.Graphic abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version of this article (10.1007/s00101-026-01670-6) contains further material. Introduction and background

Adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) are increasingly common due to medical and surgical advances that have enabled individuals with congenital heart disease (CHD) to survive into adulthood [18]. Consequently, women with CHD often reach childbearing age and can become pregnant. The prevalence of CHD worldwide is estimated to be approximately 8.2–9.1 per 1000 newborns, with an estimated 1.2 million adults living with CHD in the European Union alone [14, 15]. Recently published data from a Norwegian registry showed that the proportion of pregnant women with congenital heart defects in all pregnancies is 0.39% in the period between 1994 and 2014. The number of pregnant women with CHD increased by 3–4% annually during this period [30]. In recent years the formation of specialized cardiac care centers and the implementation of guidelines for the management of ACHD have contributed to the improved survival and quality of life of individuals with CHD [4, 26].

The ACHDs have an increased risk of morbidity and mortality compared to the general population, with a reported 5‑year survival rate of 92% compared to 98% in the general population [1, 20]. Especially pregnant women with CHD are at higher risk for complications during pregnancy and delivery, which includes cardiac arrhythmia, heart failure and maternal mortality [8]. The risk of complications is particularly high in women with complex CHD, such as those with single-ventricle physiology or significant left-to-right shunting [7, 16]. Additionally, the presence of CHD can also increase the risk of preterm labor, placental abruption and other obstetric complications [2].

Despite the growing number of women with CHD who reach childbearing age, there is an unmet need for systematic analysis on the anesthesiological management of pregnant women with CHD. Unlike the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), which has published guidelines about the management of patients with CHD, including specific guidelines on pregnancy [4, 26], there are no current guidelines from anesthesiology societies on the anesthetic management of pregnant women with CHD. Although ESC guidelines recommend vaginal birth for most CHD women, a cesarean section (C-section) is currently performed in real life in about two thirds of cases in women with more severe CHD, mostly due to obstetric or cardiac reasons [12, 22, 28].

This study presents a single-center analysis of anesthetic management in women with CHD undergoing C‑section and serves as a preliminary study for a larger prospective multicenter observational study. Due to the limited number of cases a descriptive analysis was performed, no primary or secondary hypotheses were tested.

Study design and investigation methods

Ethical approval

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, the principles of Good Clinical Practice and the standards of the local ethics committee. Data were analyzed anonymously. The local ethics committee of Hannover Medical School approved this retrospective analysis of patient data (11163_BO_K_2023). This study was registered online in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00033197).

Study population

In this retrospective, single center study, women (> 18 years old) with CHD who gave birth between January 2013 and December 2023 were included for descriptive analysis. Severity of CHD was assessed according to WHO score [4].

Analyzed parameters and definitions

Basic demographics and pregnancy history were analyzed. Anesthesia-related data such as the qualification of anesthesiologists, the anesthesia technique used, invasive monitoring, administration of different catecholamine and relevant cardiopulmonary complications during anesthesia were assessed. Anesthesia providers were categorized as follows: fellow in anesthesia training, senior (board-certified anesthetist with at least 5 years of training), Consultant (8 years of training with additional training in obstetric anesthesia and cardiac anesthesia), specialized cardiac anesthetist (additional 2 years training in cardiac anesthesia). The time point of delivery was determined and assigned to the work shifts (daytime 7:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m., late shift 4:30 p.m.–12 p.m. and night shift 12 a.m.–07:30 a.m.).

Maternal and fetal morbidity parameters included frequency of (neonatal) intensive care unit (ICU, NICU) admission, frequency of peripartum hemorrhage, atony and blood loss. Fetal stress parameters included umbilical cord pH and Apgar scores.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.8.4.3.; San Diego, CA, USA). Normal distribution was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Student’s t‑test and ordinary ANOVA were used for analysis of parametric data. For nonparametric data the Mann-Whitney‑U test with two-tailed p-value was used to compare two groups and the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction was used for multiple comparisons. The χ2-test was used for the comparison of discrete variables if all cells have expected frequencies ≥ 5, otherwise Fisher’s exact test was used. Data are expressed as medians (interquartile range, IQR), to attenuate the impact of outliers. Other measures of central tendency and variability or reliability are available in the supplements (Additional file 1). A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).

Results

Patient characteristics

Using the ICD 10 codes Q20.1–Q26.4 (congenital malformations of the circulatory system) n = 6051 cases were identified (Fig. 1). Of those n = 91 underwent obstetric procedures and were older than 18 years. We excluded n = 31 cases, due to incorrect classification (acquired heart disease, n = 3; healthy mothers with children diagnosed with congenital heart disease, n = 24 or patent foramen ovale [PFO], n = 4). Cases of curettage (n = 13) and vaginal birth with epidural anesthesia (EA) (n = 5) or without consultation of anesthesia (n = 4) were excluded. We restricted analysis to women who underwent C‑section (n = 38). Congenital heart disease may lead to impaired cardiac function. To check systolic function of the heart, the ejection fraction (EF) is measured by the use of transthoracic echography (TTE). Almost all women underwent a TTE during pregnancy (A: n = 5; 80%; B: n = 13; 100%; C: n = 19; 100%). The EF differed significantly between the groups (p = 0.040) (Fig. 2a). Ejection fraction was preserved in the group A and was significantly higher than in group C (A: 62.5% ± 6.5% vs. C: 54.5% ± 9%; p = 0.040). EF was not obtained in every patient (missing results: A: 2/6; B: 2/13; C: 7/19).

The NT-pro BNP as a serum marker for cardiac insufficiency was compared. Concentration of NT-pro BNP differed between the groups (p = 0.020) (Fig. 2b). The highest concentrations were measured in group C. Almost two thirds of the women in this group presented with elevated NT-pro BNP, whereas only one quarter in the group B had elevated NT-pro BNP levels. The women in group C had significantly higher NT-pro BNP concentrations compared to group B (C: 207 pg/mL ± 183 pg/mL vs. B: 44.5 pg/mL ± 123.75 pg/mL; p = 0.020). NT-pro BNP was not measured in all patients (missing results A: 2/6; B: 1/13; C: 2/19).

The metabolic equivalent of task is used to evaluate cardiopulmonary resilience and quantifies a person’s functional capacity by measuring the energy cost of physical activities. The MET score was recorded during the premedication consultation as part of the medical history. A MET score under 4 suggests that an individual may struggle with activities requiring more than minimal physical effort. Overall MET score was lowest in the severe group with n = 8 women having less than 4 points (Fig. 2c). Women in group A had median MET score of 5 ± 2, while women in the moderate group (B) had a MET score of 6 ± 4. The median MET score in the severe group (C) was 4 ± 3.

Institutional guided technique for SA and EA

As a local anesthetic for the SA a mixture of sufentanil 5 µg/mL and bupivacaine 5 mg/mL (0.5%) is prepared. To prepare the mixture 2 mL (10 µg) sufentanil is mixed with 2 mL (10 mg) of bupivacaine 0.5%. Of this mixture 2.4–2.6 mL is administered to ensure surgical tolerance. In every SA in this study 2.4–2.6 mL of the described mixture was used.

To enable surgical tolerance for C‑section under EA a mixture of sufentanil 5 µg/mL and ropivacaine 7.5 mg (0.75%) is prepared. It contains 2 mL (10 µg) sufentanil mixed with 18 mL (135 mg) ropivacaine 0.75%. Fractional administration to the extent of sensory spread up to thoracic level 4–6 is recommended. Usually the administration of 12–20 mL of the mixture is necessary to enable surgical tolerance. In order to minimize the risk of hemodynamic instability a longer period of time is used to administer the mixture. In this study between 12–22 mL of the mixture was administered.

Prolonged preparation time for epidural anesthesia in women with severe CHD

Most of the C‑sections were planned and scheduled (A: n = 3; 50%; B: n = 11; 85% and C: n = 16; 84%) for the dayshift (Table 3). Anesthesia was performed in more than two thirds of cases by two anesthesiologists (A: n = 5; 83%; B: n = 9; 69%; C: n = 14; 74%; p > 0.999). In all cases, at least one anesthesiologist was a consultant, except for 8% (n = 3), where the highest qualification was a senior anesthesiologist. Impaired oxygen delivery to the fetus is a potential complication in pregnancies of women with CHD. To detect fetal hypoxia and stress, pH levels are measured in both arterial and venous umbilical cord blood. There was no overall difference between arterial or venous umbilical cord pH levels among the groups (A: 7.34 ± 0.07; B: 7.30 ± 0.08; C: 7.31 ± 0.05; p = 0.320) (Fig. 4b) and no significant difference in venous umbilical cord pH between the groups (A: 7.37 ± 0.10; B: 7.33 ± 0.08; C: 7.36 ± 0.07; p = 0.530) (Fig. 4c). In each group one individual had an arterial umbilical cord pH below the lower limit of norm (LLN: 7.2). An acidotic venous umbilical cord pH level below the LLN was observed in one individual from group B (LLN: 7.25).

Apgar scores are routinely assessed for every newborn. An Apgar score ≥ 7 at 10 min indicates good neonatal adaption. While all newborns in the study achieved Apgar 10 score ≥ 7 the mean (range) Apgar 10 score differed between the three groups (A: 9, 7–10; B: 10, 9–10; C: 10, 7–10; p = 0.020) (Fig. 4d).

The frequency of newborns requiring admission to NICU did not differ between the groups (A: 83%; n = 5; B: 15%; n = 2; C: 53%; n = 10; p = 0.515), nor did the length of stay in the NICU (A: 14 days ± 76; B: 13 days ± 1; C: 21 days ± 19; p = 0.660).

Birth weight is dependent on pregnancy duration. To check for discrepancies in each group correlation between birth weight and pregnancy duration was analyzed. Birth weight and pregnancy duration correlated moderately in group (B) (r = 0.635; p = 0.022) and strongly in groups (A) (r = 0.849; p = 0.032) and (C) (r = 0.881; p ≤ 0.001) (Figure S2b).

The documented birthweight in dependence of gestational weeks was 9 times outside of the 97th or 3rd percentile (group A: n = 3, 50%; B: n = 1, 8%; C: n = 5, 26%) (Figure S2c).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to describe the anesthesiologic considerations and perioperative management of pregnant women with CHD who underwent C‑section. Several classifications exist for the risk stratification of cardiovascular complications in pregnant women with CHD (mWHO, CARPREG, CARPREG II, ZAHARA, Harris) [7, 29, 32], from which the mWHO classification demonstrated to have the highest predictive value for the development of cardiovascular complications [3, 5]. The mWHO stratifies patients into five groups (I, II, II-III, III, IV). Due to the limited number of patients in our cohort we based our analysis on the 2020 ESC classification. This approach is used in recent registry studies [12, 30].

Pregnant women with CHD have an increased risk of maternal complications and mortality compared to pregnant women without CHD. The overall frequencies of complications correlate with the severity of CHD [8, 16]. In our cohort the vast majority of women presented with some form of cardiovascular comorbidity. Current ESC guidelines recommend that women classified as moderate or high-risk undergo a comprehensive preconception evaluation, receive multidisciplinary care in a specialized center for high-risk pregnancies, and have an individualized, structured delivery plan in place [26]. In our cohort approximately 85% of the women with moderate to severe CHD underwent planned C‑section during regular daytime hours. This included 7 out of 8 procedures involving placement of introducer sheaths for quicker ECMO cannulation. Only 5% of C‑sections (1 mild and 1 severe with ECMO back up) were performed during night shifts. Both cases had been thoroughly discussed and planned in advance by the multidisciplinary pregnancy heart team. All cases have been reviewed in advance by an interdisciplinary team consisting of an ACHD cardiologist, gynecologist, cardiac surgeon, anesthesiologist, neonatologist and perfusionist. The protocols outlining the planned delivery procedure are uploaded to a shared file within the patient’s record. It ensures that, in the event of sudden maternal or neonatal deterioration, a team with the appropriate composition and expertise can be mobilized immediately according to the predefined plan.

In most of the cases in this study, anesthesia care was provided with a double medical staffing, and in nearly all cases a senior anesthesiologist was in charge. Double staffing is not routinely required for most medical procedures and is generally not standard practice in obstetric anesthesia; however, in complex clinical scenarios double staffing within the anesthesiology team can be beneficial to meet the increased demands and ensure patient safety [24]. The presence of highly experienced obstetric anesthesia providers is essential to reduce the risk for complications and minimize regional anesthesia failures. Specialized expertise in cardiac anesthesia is particularly critical for procedures performed with ECMO back-up and should be represented within the multidisciplinary team.

The highest rate of dual medical staffing (83%) was observed in group A in which only 50% of C‑sections were scheduled during daytime hours. This finding suggests a higher incidence of unscheduled emergency C‑sections occurring outside of regular working hours, which may also explain the increased use of GA in this group. Limited opportunities for advance planning may have been compensated for by increased staffing to manage perioperative risk. In this low-risk group, the overall number of hospitalized women was low, with 71% undergoing C‑section on the same day as hospital admission. This likely reduced the time available for anesthetic evaluation and planning unless such planning occurred in advance.

Overall, EA was the most frequently employed technique for C‑section. From an anesthesiologist’s perspective, epidural catheter placement is technically more demanding than SA, involving higher drug doses and greater risk of block failure [23]. The primary advantage of an EA lies in its slower onset of sympathetic blockade, allowing the maternal cardiovascular system more time to adapt. Compared to SA, EA is associated with a lower risk of abrupt hemodynamic instability such as profound hypotension and impaired tissue perfusion; however, the development of adequate surgical anesthesia requires more time, which should be taken into account during time-critical situations [27].

Neuraxial anesthesia was preferred over GA and is our institutional protocol guided technique selection; however, neuraxial anesthesia could not be performed in all cases due to various reasons, e.g., emergency C‑section (n = 4), patient’s decision (n = 3), known hemophilia (n = 1), failed SA (n = 1). In one case of a women with idiopathic severe pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) GA was the preferred method. In a Chinese study involving pregnant women with PAH, GA was avoided in more than 90% of cases [17]. Overall mortality was 5.5% in this cohort but no further information about the anesthesia technique used in these cases was available. A Danish single center study found that GA was predominantly performed in WHO class III (severe) (41%) and SA was the most used procedure in classes I and II (mild, moderate) [12]. The rate of GA in the high-risk group in our cohort was lower with 27% but cohorts are not entirely comparable, as our center also had a higher rate of patients from the high-risk group compared to the Danish cohort. Although current ESC guidelines recommend vaginal delivery in most cases, C‑section was performed in more than two thirds of all cases primarily for cardiac or obstetric reasons [22]. Exceptions, in which cesarean delivery is recommended, include severe pulmonary hypertension (e.g. Eisenmenger syndrome), severe aortopathy and severe aortic valve stenosis [26]. A retrospective study from China analyzing the management and outcomes of pregnant women with PAH reported C‑section rate of 90.5%. Maternal mortality was 5.5% with neonatal mortality of 3.3%, highlighting the significant risk associated with pregnancy in these women [17]. According to data from the registry of pregnancy and cardiac disease, elective C‑sections do not result in improved maternal outcomes but are associated with an increased risk of preterm birth and a lower neonatal weight [29]. We hypothesize that the management of pregnant women with CHD places a considerable burden on obstetricians and that cesarean delivery may offer greater sense of control in high-risk setting. A scheduled C‑section allows the care team to be selected in advance based on the clinical needs of the patient. This continuity of care may contribute to improved patient safety, particularly in complex scenarios where institutional experience with such cases is limited [10].

To prevent maternal exhaustion, reduce cardiac stress associated with increased catecholamine release from pain and anxiety, early and prophylactic initiation of EA at the onset of labor is recommended. This approach is beneficial in the context of maternal anticoagulation, as it allows the epidural to be placed within a safe time frame. In the nationwide Danish cohort, EA was used in 71% of all vaginal deliveries [12]. An existing epidural catheter can also be readily used for C‑section if necessary.

Anticoagulation is an essential topic, which requires discussion within the interdisciplinary team. Women with conduits, arrhythmia or a history of thrombosis may require therapeutic anticoagulation during pregnancy. From an anesthetic perspective, close coordination and appropriate bridging, typically with intravenous heparin, are critical to preserve the option of regional anesthesia.

Impaired cardiac function may necessitate the placement of arterial catheters for continuous hemodynamic monitoring. The placement of arterial lines in women with CHD should be reserved to severe cases with expected hemodynamic instability [13]; however, severe complications are rare and comparable to the general population. The CHD is relatively uncommon, and the limited availability of evidence-based guidelines contributes to considerable clinical uncertainty. Physicians responsible for anesthesiological management possess the requisite expertise to establish invasive arterial access, enabling continuous, beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring, which is both readily achievable and clinically advantageous. In our cohort, women in the high-risk group exhibited more pronounced cardiac impairment as indicated by lower ejection fraction and elevated NT-proBNP levels (Additional File 3). Arterial catheter placement was part of the monitoring strategy in 79%. The decision to insert a CVC for differentiated catecholamine therapy was based on patient-specific clinical considerations and was made in 37%. All women who received a CVC, also had an arterial line placed. In all cases involving planned introducer sheath placement (n = 8), both the arterial line and the CVC were inserted under local anesthesia. Once the introducer sheaths for ECMO cannula were placed correctly, EA was carefully adjusted by fractional injections. To prevent hypotension and support cardiac function in these patients, a cautious fluid preloading with a balanced isotonic electrolyte solution was performed. Norepinephrine and epinephrine were administered via syringe pumps (norepinephrine 0.02–0.27 µg/kg per min and epinephrine 0.02–0.05 µg/kg per min). The prolonged interval between anesthetic initiation and surgical incision was due to mandatory waiting period following epidural catheter placement before systemic heparinization could be safely initiated [33]. Despite this delay, surgical duration was comparable.

Other catecholamines which were used to prevent or attenuate vasoplegia included administration of boli of phenylephrine and ephedrine (ephedrine after cord clamping). Phenylephrine and ephedrine are the preferred catecholamines during C‑section based on the current guidelines [6]. We did not use the combination preparation of theoadrenaline and cafedrine, which is frequently used in Germany during obstetric anesthesia.

The decision to place the introducer sheaths is based on the clinical experience of the cardiac surgeons who are in charge of the ECMO team in our clinic and the cardiologists who are specialized in congenital heart disease. While there are no randomized clinical trials or prospective studies to guide utilization of ECMO during labor, ECMO may be initiated in patients with cardiogenic shock as a bridge to recovery, durable support, or heart transplantation. Overall, ECMO use in the peripartum period is a rare event. Veno-venous-ECMO is more often used than veno-arterial ECMO. Limited data are available for women with CHD, but severe mitral valve insufficiency was listed as a cause for implantation of veno-arterial ECMO [19].

Although placement of introducer sheaths for faster ECMO cannulation was recommended in selected cases, none of the women required ECMO support or intra-aortic balloon pump therapy. The use of ECMO during delivery is rare [9] and in recently published studies on obstetric anesthesia in pregnant women with CHD neither ECMO nor intra-aortic balloon pumps were used [12, 21, 30, 31]. Future studies should aim to establish evidence-based criteria to support rational and standardized decision-making regarding placement of introducer in high-risk pregnancies.

Postoperative ICU admission was more common among mothers in group C. Nevertheless, two larger studies have shown that overall maternal mortality, even in the most severe CHD cases, remains low (n = 372; 0.4%; or n = 172; 0%) [11, 25]. Postpartum changes, particularly relative hypovolemia may lead to arrhythmias and acute heart failure in women with CHD [26]. In addition, these patients may require transfusion thresholds that differ from current guideline recommendations for healthy women, as well as close cardiac monitoring during the postpartum period. These needs are best addressed in an ICU or in an intermediate care setting. Similar admission rates to the ICU in the high-risk groups were observed in the Danish cohort [12].

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and the overall small sample size did not allow us to draw recommendations regarding the anesthetic management. The study population was derived from a single-center setting, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations or healthcare systems. In addition, limited documentation reliability represents a common challenge in retrospective study designs. The considerable heterogeneity of CHD, along with the overall low prevalence of certain CHD subtypes, further complicates the development of a standardized approach to anesthetic management in this population. In this study, we wanted to descriptively analyze the anesthesiological care provided at our clinic to get a better impression of the current care situation. The findings serve as preparation for a multicenter prospective observational study that will begin shortly.

Conclusion

Anesthetic management of pregnant women with CHD often requires a multidisciplinary approach coordinated within a specialized pregnancy heart team. This team typically involves close collaboration between anesthesiologists, obstetricians, cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, perfusionists, neonatologists, and other healthcare professionals [8, 26]. The primary goal of anesthetic care in this setting is to ensure safety of both mother and fetus, while minimizing the risk for complications and optimizing clinical outcomes. Based on this cohort, no anaesthetic practice was found to be superior; however, neuraxial techniques were well-tolerated across groups and EA is recommended for most situations. The most challenging CHD lesions included Eisenmenger syndrome and patients with Fontan circulation. In the near future a larger prospective multicenter study in Germany, Austria and Switzerland will start recruiting and we hope to be able to draw up evidence-based recommendations for the anesthetic management of this complex patient population.
