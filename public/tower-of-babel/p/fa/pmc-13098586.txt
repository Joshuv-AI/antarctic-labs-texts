Arthritis and High Alcohol Intake at Diagnosis Increase Future Risk of Hepatocellular Carcinoma in HFE Hemochromatosis
Authors: Richard H. Goodheart, Louise E. Ramm, Grant A. Ramm, John K. Olynyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsHFE hemochromatosis (HH) may cause arthritis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Cirrhosis is the main risk factor for HCC in HH. Arthritis is common in HH, strongly associated with cirrhosis and readily diagnosed. Thus, we evaluated whether arthritis might be of clinical utility in predicting the future risk of HCC in HH.MethodsTwo hundred four clinically well-characterized patients with HH were recruited between 1974 and 2013 and attended for regular follow-up at a single center over a median duration of 15.2 years. Clinical, biochemical, and cirrhosis status were recorded at diagnosis. The occurrence of HCC was defined as a documented diagnosis in the medical record.ResultsTen of the 204 patients with HH developed HCC during a follow-up; 9 of these had recorded arthritis status. Seven of 88 patients with arthritis at diagnosis of HH subsequently developed HCC compared with 2 of 115 patients without arthritis (relative risk = 4.57, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.11–19.07, P = .042). The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive values were 78% (95% CI 45%–96%), 58% (95% CI 51%–64%), 8% (95% CI 4%–15%), and 98% (95% CI 93%–100%). Patients with arthritis and cirrhosis at diagnosis who went on to develop HCC had significantly higher alcohol consumption at baseline compared with those individuals who had arthritis and cirrhosis but did not progress to HCC (median 100 g/d vs 45 g/d, P = .0312).ConclusionArthritis combined with higher alcohol consumption at diagnosis of HH is associated with an increased risk of future development of HCC.

HFE hemochromatosis (HH) is a genetic disorder characterized by excessive iron (Fe) absorption leading to Fe overload in some patients. Most often, it is caused by homozygosity for the p.C282Y variant in the human homeostatic iron regulator (HFE) gene.1 HH affects approximately 1 in 200 persons of Northern European descent; however, the penetrance is variable, with less than 30% of men and 1.2% of women developing Fe-overload disease.1,2 HH has well-known associations with mortality and adverse liver outcomes, most consequential being liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).3, 4, 5, 6 Patients with HH have a 6.8-fold higher risk of cirrhosis and a 15-fold higher risk of HCC compared with the general community, supporting the need for a high level of clinical vigilance for early detection and surveillance of at-risk patients.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 HCC accounts for up to 45% of deaths in HH.6 Identifying those most at risk of HCC is paramount as surveillance leads to earlier detection, a higher chance of curative treatment and improved survival. Whilst the primary risk factor for the development of HCC in HH is cirrhosis, clinical ascertainment of cirrhosis in the community is often variable and challenging for clinicians.11,12

Extra-hepatic complications of HH are common, with arthritis being the most common cause of morbidity, affecting at least 24% of HH patients.13,14 It impacts on quality of life and is often one of the first symptoms that prompts initial diagnosis and further investigation. Typically, HH arthritis involves the metacarpophalangeal joints as well as hip, ankle, elbow, shoulders, and knees.13,15,16 Arthritis is associated with an increased likelihood of cirrhosis in HH.13,17,18 Given the relative simplicity of detecting arthritis compared with the often-indolent nature of cirrhosis in primary care, we aimed to determine if arthritis was associated with an increased risk of HCC in HH.

Methods

We studied the relationship between arthritis at diagnosis and subsequent development of HCC in 204 clinically well-characterized patients with HH, originally diagnosed at the Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital between 1974 and 2013. All patients were untreated at the time of their original diagnosis. The exclusion criteria included age less than 16 years or other forms of chronic liver disease, including chronic viral hepatitis, immune-mediated liver disease, and known metabolic liver diseases. Arthritis was diagnosed based on clinical assessment of symptoms, signs, and radiography using the criteria described by Carroll et al.13 The diagnosis of HCC was based on standard clinical imaging and/or histological diagnosis contained within the medical record.

Alcohol consumption was assessed in grams of ethanol equivalent per day as defined by the National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia.19 In addition to clinical details, the following data were also available for all patients at diagnosis: serum transferrin saturation (TSat) and ferritin levels, presence or absence of cirrhosis, hepatic iron concentration (HIC), and hepatic iron index (HII) (HII = HIC/age). Liver biopsy–based diagnosis of cirrhosis was conducted according to the Scheuer classification.20

Following diagnosis, all patients underwent phlebotomy therapy. Mobilizable Fe stores were calculated by multiplying the number of units of blood removed by weekly phlebotomy to reduce the serum ferritin to 50–100 μg/L by 250 mg (the quantity of Fe removed with each unit of blood). Study patients were followed-up regularly in a specialized hospital single-center clinic by specialists with expertise in management of HH.

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committees of the Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital and the Queensland Institute of Medical Research Berghofer Medical Research Institute, Brisbane, Australia, and informed written consent was obtained at the time of entry into the study.

Statistical Analysis

All results are presented as the median and interquartile range unless otherwise stated. Continuous data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Sensitivity (the fraction of those who developed HCC correctly identified as positive by the presence of arthritis), specificity (the fraction of those who did not develop HCC correctly identified as negative by the absence of arthritis), positive predictive value (the fraction of people with arthritis who developed HCC), negative predictive value (the fraction of people without arthritis who did not develop HCC), relative risk and likelihood ratios were calculated. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 (GraphPad Software). A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Liver cirrhosis and arthritis are the principal clinical sequelae which determine morbidity and mortality in HH.1,4,15 Unfortunately, patients with HH may have cirrhosis for many years before any clinical features arise and may remain unrecognized.21,22 Arthritis, however, is often symptomatic in HH, and therefore much more likely to be detected by both patients and clinicians.1,13,14 Previous studies have established that the presence of arthritis is not only associated with a 7.5-fold higher risk of having cirrhosis, but the absence of arthritis is a strong negative predictor for cirrhosis in HH.23 We now extend the clinical utility of arthritis in HH by demonstrating a novel association with the risk of future development of HCC. Long-term clinical follow-up of patients with HH demonstrated that the risk of developing HCC is 4.57-times higher in patients with HH with arthritis at diagnosis compared with those who do not have arthritis (8.0% vs 1.7%, P = .042). For comparison purposes with cirrhosis over the same period, 34% of cirrhosis patients develop HCC compared with 0% of noncirrhotic patients, highlighting the critical requirement for cirrhosis in the pathogenesis of HCC in HH.12

In our study, all patients who developed HCC had cirrhosis, with 7 of 9 also having arthritis (Figure, Table 2). We also had 18 patients who had cirrhosis and arthritis but did not progress to HCC. When we compared these groups, those who had cirrhosis and arthritis who went on to develop HCC had higher baseline alcohol consumption compared with those who had cirrhosis and arthritis but did not develop HCC (median 100 g/d vs 45 g/d, P = .0312). Baseline age, BMI, ferritin level, TSat, mobilizable Fe, HIC, and HII were similar in these groups. Whilst higher alcohol combined with excessive Fe deposition leading to more aggressive liver disease, cirrhosis, and HCC could underlie this observation,1,7,14 it is important to note that arthritis can occur at any point of a subject’s journey with HH, even after therapeutic phlebotomy.13 It is routine standard-of-care to advise individuals with cirrhosis to abstain from alcohol following the diagnosis, but we do not know whether the individuals with cirrhosis and arthritis who went on to develop HCC altered their baseline alcohol consumption. The mechanisms whereby alcohol may interact with Fe in the liver to enhance the progression to liver disease and carcinogenesis are complex. Long-term coadministration of Fe and alcohol in animal models results in increased hepatocellular necrosis and inflammation.24 A recent review by Teschke25 indicates that both Fe and alcohol can contribute to ferroptosis, production of reactive oxygen species, increasing the proinflammatory cytokine environment within the liver and accelerating liver injury. Furthermore, treatment of Fe overload with phlebotomy therapy may result in lower intestinal Fe concentrations, due to greater Fe absorption, potentially altering the microbiome to a more injurious type.25 Regardless of the causative pathway, this is an important elucidation as arthritis, especially when combined with higher levels of alcohol consumption, may assist risk stratification and guide requirements for diagnosis of cirrhosis in HH and surveillance for HCC, compatible with The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases 2023 HCC guidelines which recommend surveillance should ideally be individualized rather than applied uniformly to an entire population.26

Strengths of our study include it being the first study to report the association between arthritis and risk of HCC. Arthritis is a simple condition to screen for with a clinical history and examination. The cohort clinically, biochemically, and histologically is well-characterized, with robust data and a long follow-up period. We also acknowledge limitations. Whilst statistically significant, the primary outcome of HCC occurred in 10 patients and 8% of those with arthritis at diagnosis. Our observations in a relatively small number of HCC cases should ideally be replicated larger HH cohorts that have undergone long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report a novel association between arthritis and higher levels of alcohol consumption at diagnosis and future HCC risk in patients with HH. Arthritis has the benefit of being an easy to diagnose clinical feature of HH which can be potentially utilized in the clinic setting to identify patients at higher risk of progressing to cirrhosis or HCC.
