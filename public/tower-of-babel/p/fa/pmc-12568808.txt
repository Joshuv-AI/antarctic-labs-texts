Low skeletal muscle index combined with reduced bone density increases mortality rate in older adults with heart failure
Authors: Qinyue Wang, Yixiang Ye, Shuxin Zhang, Junchao Yang, Huilin Che, Xinkai Qu, Lin Qi, Mingxuan Li, Kailei Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) is associated with high mortality and poor prognosis in older adults. Reduced skeletal muscle mass and low bone mineral density are prevalent comorbidities in this population; however, their combined impact on clinical outcomes remains underexplored.AmisThis study aimed to investigate the prognostic impact of reduced skeletal muscle mass and bone mineral density in older adults with HF.Methods and resultsIn this retrospective, single-center cohort study, 412 older HF patients (aged ≥ 60 years) were classified into four groups based on thoracic CT-derived vertebral bone density (VBD) and skeletal muscle index (SMI): normal VBD and SMI, low SMI alone, low VBD alone, and combined low VBD and SMI. All-cause mortality was assessed over a median follow-up of 729 days (IQR: 686–764). Patients with combined low SMI and VBD had the highest mortality (33.3% vs. 15.3% in the normal group; p = 0.032). Multivariable Cox regression showed that combined low SMI and VBD independently predicted mortality (adjusted HR = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.12–4.24; p = 0.021), whereas isolated deficits were not statistically significant.ConclusionsThe coexistence of reduced skeletal muscle mass and bone density synergistically increases mortality risk in older adults with HF. Early identification of musculoskeletal deficits through CT-based assessments may facilitate targeted interventions to reduce adverse outcomes. Integrated strategies addressing both bone and muscle loss are essential for improving long-term prognosis in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03213-2.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a major global health challenge characterized by high prevalence, poor prognosis, and elevated mortality [1]. Despite advancements in management, more than 30% of patients die within one year of diagnosis, and over 60% within five years [2].

Sarcopenia, defined as the progressive decline in muscle mass and function, is common in older individuals with HF. A meta-analysis of 68,556 HF patients found that sarcopenia significantly increased mortality risk (pooled HR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.20–5.25) [3]. Osteoporosis, which affects approximately 20% of women and 4% of men over 50 years of age, is also prevalent in elderly HF patients [4]. Low bone mineral density has been linked to increased mortality in this population (HR = 0.069; 95% CI: 0.011–0.444; p = 0.005) [5].

Reduced skeletal muscle mass and bone density share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms. Prior studies have shown that their coexistence substantially increases the risk of all-cause mortality (OR = 3.18; 95% CI: 1.54–6.57) and disability (OR = 2.11; 95% CI: 1.19–3.74) compared with either condition alone in older adults undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement [6]. However, the prognostic significance of concurrent musculoskeletal deficits in HF patients remains insufficiently defined.

Computed tomography (CT) has recently emerged as a valuable tool for body composition analysis, including the assessment of skeletal muscle mass and bone mineral density [7]. Chest CT scans, routinely performed in HF patients for cardiopulmonary evaluation, provide a unique opportunity for simultaneous assessment of musculoskeletal health without additional radiation exposure [8]. Quantitative analysis of muscle cross-sectional area and bone density at thoracic vertebral levels on CT has been validated as reliable and reproducible, correlating well with gold-standard modalities such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [9, 10]. Using chest CT for musculoskeletal evaluation enables a comprehensive assessment of multisystem involvement in HF and facilitates early identification of high-risk individuals.

Given the rising prevalence of multimorbidity among the older HF population.

, this study investigated the prognostic impact of coexisting low bone density and reduced skeletal muscle mass, assessed using chest CT, on long-term survival outcomes.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huadong Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. 20250093; Protocol No. 2025K252; approval date: June 17, 2025). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical regulations.

Study population and design

Older patients (aged ≥ 60 years) admitted to Huadong Hospital, Fudan University, with a diagnosis of HF between April 2020 and December 2023 were included. All participants underwent noncontrast chest computed tomography (CT) during hospitalization or within one week before admission. HF was diagnosed according to the 2018 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Heart Failure. The maximum follow-up duration was 1,200 days.

Among 556 patients who met the initial screening criteria, 103 were excluded (81 with CT image issues, 15 with malignancies, 5 who had major surgeries, and 2 with autoimmune diseases), and an additional 41 were excluded due to in-hospital mortality. A total of 412 patients were enrolled for comparative analysis of post-discharge outcomes based on skeletal muscle mass and bone density parameters.

Skeletal muscle index assessment

Skeletal muscle mass was quantified using the skeletal muscle index (SMI) at the 12th thoracic vertebra (T12) [11]. SMI was calculated as skeletal muscle area (SMA, cm²) divided by height squared (m²), in accordance with consensus guidelines [12]. Skeletal muscles at T12 were automatically segmented using Slice-O-Matic software (v5.0, Tomovision, Quebec, Canada) by two trained investigators, applying a Hounsfield unit threshold of − 29 to + 150 HU to identify the muscle tissue [13]. All measurements were independently conducted by two well-trained investigators in a blinded manner. Inter-observer reliability was evaluated using 20 randomly selected images, resulting in an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.972 (95% CI: 0.930–0.989), which indicates excellent reproducibility. A representative segmented CT image is shown in Figure S1.

Bone density measurement

Vertebral bone density (VBD) was assessed by measuring trabecular attenuation on axial CT images at the T12 vertebral level. CT images were extracted and analyzed using Radiant DICOM and Communications in Medicine Viewer software. Under the supervision of a senior radiologist, two trained observers manually delineated the region of interest (ROI) within the central trabecular area of the vertebral body (Figure S2). Regions with bone abnormalities, artifacts, or lesions were excluded. Vertebral segments for which reliable trabecular measurements could not be obtained were excluded. Two well-trained investigators independently performed all measurements in a blinded manner. Inter-observer reproducibility demonstrated excellent agreement, with an ICC of 0.992 (95% CI: 0.978–0.997).

Data synthesis

Based on a previous study, a CT attenuation threshold of ≤ 117 Hounsfield units (HU) at T12 defined low VBD [14]. Low SMI was defined as T12-SMI < 31.1 cm²/m² for males and < 26.3 cm²/m² for females [15].

Patients were classified into four groups based on their VBD and SMI: (1) normal VBD and SMI, (2) low VBD alone, (3) low SMI alone, and (4) combined low VBD and SMI.

Study endpoints and Follow-up

Clinical outcomes were obtained from electronic health records, linked databases, and telephone follow-ups with patients or their families. Follow-up continued until August 2024, with a median duration of 729 days (IQR, 686–764 days). The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality. Survival time was calculated from the date of enrollment to the date of death or last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as counts (%). Group differences were analyzed using Welch’s t-test/ANOVA or the Wilcoxon/Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Cox regression, adjusted for sex, age, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), BNP level, and HF type, evaluated the associations of low SMI alone, low VBD alone, and their combination with outcomes. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests. Subgroup effects were examined using Cox regression. The predictive values of T12-SMI and T12-VBD were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (v25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and RStudio (v4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Hypertension and coronary artery disease were the most common comorbidities, present in 66.1% and 54.0% of patients, respectively. Atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease were present in 39.1%, 37.7%, and 31.7% of patients, respectively. The median left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 47.0% (IQR: 38.0–60.0%). Most patients had heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF, 44.9%), followed by heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, 34.5%) and heart failure with mid-range ejection fraction (HFmrEF, 20.6%).

Compared with patients with normal bone density and skeletal muscle mass, those with combined low VBD and SMI were older, with the highest mean age (81.0 ± 8.5 years). The proportion of female patients was significantly higher among those with low VBD alone (56.1%) and combined low VBD and SMI (55.2%). Patients with low SMI alone and those with combined low VBD and SMI had significantly lower BMI than those with low VBD alone (25.05 ± 3.85 kg/m²) or normal bone density and muscle mass (24.71 ± 3.05 kg/m²). There were no significant differences among the four groups in HF-specific pharmacotherapy or comorbidity management (p > 0.05).

Impact of bone density and muscle mass on In-Hospital outcomes

A total of 41 patients (9.1%) died during hospitalization. Unadjusted analyses revealed no statistically significant differences in in-hospital mortality or median hospital length of stay (LOS) among the four groups. Among survivors and non-survivors, LOS was similar across the groups (Table S1).

Postdischarge outcomes based on bone density and skeletal muscle mass

During a median follow-up of 729 days (IQR: 686–764), 85 patients (20.6%) died. Mortality rates varied across the groups: 15.3% (17/111) in patients with normal SMI and VBD, 15.6% (12/77) in those with low SMI alone, 20.0% (23/115) in those with low VBD alone, and 33.3% (33/109) in those with both low SMI and VBD. The highest mortality was observed in the group with concurrent reductions in both VBD and SMI (p = 0.032) (Fig. 2).

Subgroups analysis based on bone density and skeletal muscle mass

As shown in Fig. 4, subgroup analysis by sex indicated a stronger association in females (HR = 7.79; 95% CI: 1.05–57.96; p = 0.045), although the interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.125). Age stratification revealed a significantly increased mortality risk in patients < 75 years with combined low SMI and VBD (HR = 4.27; 95% CI: 1.87–9.75; p = 0.001). Among patients with serum albumin < 35 g/L, the combined low SMI and VBD group had a markedly higher risk of mortality (HR = 4.49; 95% CI: 1.54–13.10; p = 0.006), with a statistically significant interaction (p for interaction = 0.036). Other subgroups, including BMI, smoking history, hypertension, T2DM, cerebrovascular disease (CBD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), atrial fibrillation (AF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary artery disease (CAD), and LDL levels did not demonstrate significant interactions (all p for interaction > 0.05).

Evaluation of predictive performance

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for SMI and VBD to predict all-cause mortality in the overall cohort (Figure S3a), males (Figure S3b), and females (Figure S3c). Compared with either variable alone, the combination of SMI and VBD yielded higher areas under the ROC curve (AUC) across all groups, demonstrating improved predictive performance. To enhance clinical applicability, the optimal cutoff values for VBD and SMI were determined from ROC analyses as the points maximizing both sensitivity and specificity (Table S3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study to evaluate the combined effect of objectively measured reduced skeletal muscle mass and low bone mineral density on clinical outcomes in hospitalized older adults with HF. Our findings indicate that although the coexistence of reduced muscle mass and low bone density did not significantly affect in-hospital mortality, it was associated with an increased risk of post-discharge mortality.

Sarcopenia, a common comorbidity older adults, has received increasing attention in recent years [16]. The decline in skeletal muscle mass may contribute to cardiovascular injury through systemic metabolic disturbances [17] and neuroendocrine dysregulation [18], thereby influencing cardiovascular prognosis. A multicenter prospective cohort study reported that sarcopenia was an independent predictor of one-year mortality in patients with both HFpEF and HFrEF [19]. However, a meta-analysis found no significant association between sarcopenia and adverse outcomes in patients with HFpEF [3]. In the present study, we observed a trend toward increased mortality during follow-up among patients with low SMI. However, this association did not reach statistical significance during hospitalization or long-term follow-up, suggesting that reduced skeletal muscle mass may have limited value as an independent predictor of mortality in patients with HF.

Previous studies have independently examined the impact of osteoporosis on both the incidence [20] and mortality [21, 22] of HF, suggesting that this association may be mediated by multiple mechanisms along the bone–cardiac axis [23]. These mechanisms include impaired bone metabolism, dysregulated secretion of bone-derived regulatory factors, and systemic inflammation [23, 24]. Accordingly, osteoporosis has been proposed as an independent risk factor for the development and progression of HF beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors. In our study, we found no significant association between low bone mineral density and in-hospital mortality in patients with HF. Although a trend toward increased mortality was observed over a median follow-up of two years, the difference was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the adverse impact of low bone density on HF prognosis may emerge in the long term and may have limited value in predicting short-term outcomes.

A novel finding of this study is that patients with HF who exhibited both reduced skeletal muscle mass and low bone density had a higher mortality rate during follow-up than those without this combination. Previous studies investigating the synergistic impact of concomitant muscle and bone loss—commonly referred to as osteosarcopenia—have primarily focused on patients with coronary artery disease or valvular heart disease, and have reported similar associations [6]. In contrast, neither low SMI nor low VBD alone was significantly associated with adverse outcomes in our cohort. This finding suggests that osteosarcopenia represents a distinct pathophysiological condition with implications beyond the additive effects of sarcopenia or osteoporosis alone. The adverse impact of this syndrome may stem from the complex interplay between muscle and bone tissues, resulting in dual metabolic dysfunction that amplifies systemic frailty [25, 26].

The findings of this study suggest that hospitalized patients with HF who exhibit low VBD or low SMI should be identified early and targeted for timely intervention. Special attention should be paid to patients with concurrent reductions in both VBD and SMI, as these patients appear to be at particularly high risk for adverse outcomes. Although the pathophysiological mechanisms linking low VBD and reduced SMI in patients have not been fully elucidated, potential contributing factors include aging, impaired renal function, infections, malnutrition, and reduced physical activity.

Targeted strategies such as nutritional supplementation, resistance training, and pharmacological therapies aimed at enhancing musculoskeletal health have shown promise in improving prognosis [27]. Specifically, nutritional supplementation—including increased intake of calcium, vitamin D, and other essential nutrients—may help promote bone density and slow the progression of osteoporosis [28]. Resistance training (e.g., weight lifting or squatting) is effective in increasing muscle mass, mitigating muscle atrophy, and enhancing physical performance. When combined, these interventions may act synergistically to produce substantial clinical benefits. Pharmacological treatments, including bisphosphonates or denosumab for bone health and agents that promote muscle growth, such as testosterone replacement therapy, may counteract the deterioration of bone and muscle, thereby improving overall health status [29–31].

However, interventions targeting bone and muscle loss are likely to play a more significant role in the long-term management of patients with HF, as improvements in VBD and SMI are unlikely to occur during brief hospitalizations. Future research should evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of these strategies and assess their impact on reducing the burden of musculoskeletal decline in patients with HF.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design introduced potential selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, the use of thoracic CT–derived SMI and VBD measurements, while practical, may not fully align with the gold-standard diagnostic methods for osteoporosis (e.g., dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) or sarcopenia (e.g., gait speed and grip strength assessments). Variations in CT protocols or segmentation thresholds could also affect reproducibility. Third, unmeasured confounders such as nutritional status, physical activity, and socioeconomic factors, which may influence both musculoskeletal health and HF outcomes, were not accounted for. Fourth, the median follow-up duration of two years may have been insufficient to capture the long-term effects of osteosarcopenia, particularly in patients with chronic HF. Finally, as an observational study, causality could not be established, nor could the efficacy of interventions targeting bone and muscle loss be evaluated. Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized protocols and longer follow-up periods are needed to validate these findings and to explore therapeutic strategies for mitigating musculoskeletal decline in patients with HF.
