Measurement of inkjet droplet speed using interference fringe by diffracted light
Authors: Dong Yeol Shin, Yoon Jae Moon, Byeong-Kwon Ju, Kyung-Tae Kang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Inkjet printers are key technologies in manufacturing organic light-emitting diodes and quantum dot light-emitting diode panels, but precise measurement and control of inkjet droplets remains challenging. The international standard, IEC 62899-302-1, uses shadow image-based measurement with high magnification microscopes to observe picoliter-sized droplets. However, high magnification lens results in a shallow depth of field or narrow optimal measurement area, causing the blurring image if the droplet does not pass through the optimal measurement area. To solve this, we propose using the interference image-based measurement with interference fringe patterns by inkjet droplets as a tool to measure the flight speed of droplets. The interference fringe patterns can be obtained simply passing the droplet through within the light beam path, providing approximately 1000× wider measurement area compared to the shadow image-based measurement, making it practical to use in the industry. The flight speed of droplets analyzed with the interference image-based measurement at various frequencies and amplitudes of the inkjet driving voltage were compared with the shadow image-based measurement. The interference image-based measurement showed a coefficient of variation of less than 3%, showing higher repeatability than the shadow image-based measurements.

Introduction

In the display industry, inkjet printing is a manufacturing process that is used to produce the OLED (Organic Light-Emitting Diode) and QLED (Quantum Light-Emitting Diode)1–3. Inkjet printing offers high precision and resolution, in the deposition process of very fine features and patterns, which makes it suitable for producing high-quality displays with sharp images, fine details, and small pixel sizes4,5. Additionally, it is a versatile process that can be used with various substrates, including flexible materials, enabling the production of flexible and bendable displays6–9. These types of displays are in high demand for applications such as curved screens10, wearable devices7–9, and rollable displays11. Moreover, inkjet printing is a relatively efficient manufacturing process in terms of material usage as it enables precise control over the amount of ink deposited, thereby reducing material waste and costs. Lastly, inkjet printing’s compatibility with a wide range of functional materials, including organic compounds and conductive inks12, makes it suitable for producing displays with advanced features, such as flexible OLED displays or printed sensors7,13–15.

However, in the inkjet printing manufacturing process, controlling the uniform droplet size and the uniform flight speed is very important. The flight speed, at which inkjet droplets are propelled from the printhead and reach the printing substrate, directly affects the print quality. Even if the same amplitude of the driving voltage is applied to the piezoelectric elements of the inkjet nozzles, the flight trend of the inkjet droplet from each nozzle may not be the same due to the crosstalk between adjacent nozzles16. This, in turn, can have a detrimental impact on the reliability of the manufactured display panel due to variations in the thickness of the thin films formed pixel by pixel. The droplet flight speed also impacts the dot size and spacing in the printed image, which in turn affects the resolution17. Measuring the flight speed of the droplet allows for the optimization and fine-tuning of various printing parameters. By understanding the relationship between the flight speed of the droplet and print quality, manufacturers can adjust settings such as printhead voltage, ink viscosity, and drop volume. For example, if the droplet flight speed is too slow, increasing the amplitude of the driving voltage or reducing ink viscosity is needed to improve the speed and accuracy of droplet deposition. Fine-tuning these parameters based on the flight speed of the droplet measurements leads to optimized printing performance and enhanced print quality.

To measure the flight speed of the droplet, different techniques can be employed, depending on the specific requirements and constraints of the printing system. High-speed cameras can capture the movement of droplets in flight, allowing for precise measurement of their size and velocity18. The laser-based systems utilize laser light scattering to determine the size and flight speed of the droplet19,20. Currently, most widely used droplet observation system in inkjet printing manufacturing processes is the shadow image-based measurement to utilizes strobe light and the charge coupled device (CCD) camera, the international standard IEC 62899-302-121, as shown in Fig. 122–24.Fig. 1A schematic diagram of a commercialized shadow image-based measurement method for observing inkjet droplets.

A schematic diagram of a commercialized shadow image-based measurement method for observing inkjet droplets.

By synchronizing the strobe lighting with the inkjet printing system, high-speed moving inkjet droplets can be easily tracked. This enables real-time monitoring of the behavior of inkjet droplets, allowing for immediate actions to be taken in case of any issues with the inkjet printer’s operation. Additionally, the shadow image-based measurement allows for the measurement of parameters such as the speed, trajectory, and size of inkjet droplets. Furthermore, it provides a convenient means to diagnose problems such as nozzle clogging, fluctuations in inkjet droplet velocity, and defective spraying. However, the shadow image-based measurement is susceptible to environmental influences due to its reliance on strobe lighting and a CCD camera. Proper lighting and placement conditions are required, particularly considering that the size of observed droplets captured by the camera may vary with the brightness of the lighting, and vibrations can impact the accuracy of measurements. Moreover, a limitation of the system is that when multiple droplets are observed with a single camera, the edges of the observed droplets may not be clearly defined because of the relatively small droplet size to the distance between nozzles. This introduces a disadvantage in precisely observing inkjet droplets using the shadow image-based measurement, as errors can arise depending on the threshold of droplet edge definition and the pixel size of the CCD camera.

In this study, we proposed a new method to measure the flight speed of inkjet droplet using the interference image-based measurement that interferes with diffraction signals between two or more successive droplets and compared with the measured values by IEC 62899-302-121, the shadow image-based measurement.

To compare the results of measuring the droplet flight speed with interference image-based measurement, the flight trend of inkjet droplets for each experimental condition was collected from the shadow image-based measurement using strobe lighting and CCD camera (BASLER, acA640-90). The average of flight speed measured by the shadow image-based measurement was calculated through droplets ranging from 500 to 2500 μm from the inlet of the nozzle. The flight speed of the inkjet droplet, driven by an input waveform with an amplitude of 19 V across frequencies ranging from 4 to 10 kHz, ranged from 1.55 to 1.72 m/s. Increasing the amplitude to 21 V resulted in a flight speed of 2.61–2.88 m/s, and at an amplitude of 23 V, the flight speed increased to 3.82–3.98 m/s. For every obtained result, the coefficient of variation, calculated by dividing the standard deviation by the average, consistently remained at approximately 3% or less. This indicates a high level of result repeatability in interference image-based measurements, with similar outcomes consistently achieved in each measurement iteration.

In Table 1, the average speed is non-uniform for different jetting frequency operating at the fixed amplitude. In the inkjet printing, resonance inside the nozzle is necessary for ink to be jetted. The resonance inside the nozzle is affected by the input voltage waveform, jetting frequency, and viscosity of the ink. Even if the amplitude of the input voltage waveform is the same, varying the jetting frequency may change the resonance tendency inside the nozzle. As the resonance tendency changes, the speed of the droplet changes26. The change in resonance tendency is due to unstable acoustic waves remaining inside the nozzle. Even after the inkjet droplets are jetted, acoustic waves remain inside the nozzle. These remaining acoustic waves continue to move along the ink path until the acoustic waves are completely dissipated by energy dissipation. The acoustic waves remaining at high frequencies interferes with the newly formed pulse wave by the input voltage waveform, and subsequent droplet formation becomes unstable. Therefore, the non-uniform behavior of the average speed in Table 1 is due to remained acoustic waves inside the nozzle at high jetting frequency.

In Fig. 3, the results of two methods show that there are differences in standard deviation. In the shadow image-based measurement of Fig. 3, the average and standard deviation were calculated by analyzing the average 4× measurement data for each condition. Shadow image-based measurements had deviations of approximately 0.18–0.37 m/s, but interference image-based measurements had deviations of approximately 0.02–0.1 m/s. As for the coefficient of variation for each measurement, it was calculated as average of 8.9% for the shadow image-based measurement and average of 1.8% for the interference image-based measurement. The interference image-based measurement demonstrated the repeatability of results that is 5× higher than the shadow image-based measurement.

The shadow image-based measurement requires the high-magnification microscope lens to be precisely focused on the inkjet droplet location to capture clear images. If the inkjet droplet is out of focus, the image will appear blurry due to defocusing, which significantly impacts the measurement accuracy of the droplet’s size and speed. Typical high-magnification lenses have a depth of field of several tens of micrometer. In contrast, the interference image-based measurement can consistently obtain accurate results when there are at least two inkjet droplets in the laser. Additionally, it is sufficient to capture only the central part of the diffraction signal to measure the speed of the inkjet droplets. To verify the measurement area, we used a metal wire instead of inkjet droplets in the existing experimental setup to analyze the diffraction images (see Figure S2). The position of the metal wire was adjusted using a precision stage, and its diffraction signal is shown in Figure S3. The diffraction signal generated by the metal wire is similar to that of a single-slit diffraction pattern. Since the metal wire is positioned vertically, the diffraction signal forms horizontally. As the distance between the metal wire and the lens changes, the number of fringes in the diffraction signal from the center varies. The position of fringe #1 did not change at the various distance between lens and metal wire from 40 to 110 mm. In this diffraction measurement study, the presence of diffraction patterns near the center, such as fringe #1, is important. The presence of diffraction pattern near the center means that the interference fringe pattern can be obtained to measure the flight speed. Therefore, the interference image-based measurement provides the measuring area at the several tens of millimeter, approximately 1000× wider than the shadow image-based measurement.

The shadow image-based measurement’s limitations of extracting overlapped images, i.e., motion blur, can lead to errors in the speed measured. On the other hand, the interference image-based measurement has the advantage that the deviation is measured below 0.1 m/s shown in Table 1. This has the advantage of being able to compensate for the disadvantage of not being able to distinguish the exact boundary of droplets due to the blurring of the shadow image-based measurement frame, which is one of the problems of the shadow image-based measurement. With a smaller laser diameter and a very high-frequency environment of more than 10 kHz, sufficient droplets can reach the laser path. This means that it can be a useful tool for measuring the flight speed of the droplet jetted at tens of kilohertz as required by the inkjet printing industry.

Discussion

This study aimed to measure the flight speed of high-speed-moving inkjet droplets through the analysis of interference image-based measurement, which uses interference fringe patterns in diffraction signals. The flight speed measured by interference image-based measurement was compared with shadow image-based measurement, which is the international standard to observe the inkjet droplets. Different amplitudes of voltages (19 V, 21 V, and 23 V) were applied at frequencies ranging from 4 to 10 kHz in the experiment. Interference image-based measurements showed a high coefficient of variation about 3% or less in more than 400 measurements. The shadow image-based measurements showed approximately 8.9% coefficient of variation in about 4 measurements. The shadow image-based measurements use high-magnification microscopes to observe picoliter-sized droplets. However, this high magnification results in a shallow depth of field, causing the image to blur if the droplet does not pass precisely through this depth. However, our proposed tool provides a clear image if the inkjet droplets just pass within the parallel light region. Compared to shadow image-based measurements, interference image-based measurement provides approximately 1000× wider measurement area, making it practical for use in the industry. The interference image-based measurement technology provides the inkjet printing industry, which operates at frequencies of several tens of kilohertz for mass production, with the opportunity to simultaneously measure the flight speed of droplets during printing. Furthermore, our proposed measurement technology can enable real-time monitoring of the consistent jetting speed of tin droplets in lithography equipment utilizing extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light. Additionally, it can be applied to measure solder ball velocities during the semiconductor packaging process. The suggested measurement method in this study holds the potential and promise to contribute to addressing technical challenges not only in the inkjet printing industry but also in the semiconductor field.

Fraunhofer diffraction equation uses the distance between an object (inkjet droplets in this study) and a screen (camera sensor in this study). However, the distance between the object and the screen may not always be constant. Therefore, many Fraunhofer diffraction studies use the traditional method of “Fourier lens arrangement” setup, which convex lens is placed between the object and the screen. This setup has the significant advantage of always sensing diffraction patterns at the same location, even if the diffraction occurs at different positions. Therefore, in the Fraunhofer diffraction equation, the parameter representing the distance between the inkjet droplet and the screen can be replaced by the focal length of the convex lens. For this reason, we used the focal length F of the convex lens at Eq. (3).

Figure 5 shows the relationship between the jetting of droplets and the digital pulse signal in Fig. 1. In the inkjet printing process, digital pulse signals are sent to the waveform generator in response to the computer’s control signal. The ink inside the inkjet head is pushed out to form inkjet droplets when this waveform supplies voltage to the inkjet head through the inkjet head driver. One digital pulse signal produces one inkjet droplet, and the supply cycle of the digital pulse signal matches with the inkjet droplet’s formation cycle. When the distance between inkjet droplets is d, the time it takes for the inkjet droplet to travel the distance d is equal to the time interval of the digital pulse signals, which is equal to the reciprocal of the jetting frequency. Thus, the distance between inkjet droplets and the computer-controllable jetting frequency can be used to calculate the flight speed of the inkjet droplet.Fig. 5Schematic of flight speed measurement using jetting frequency and distance between the inkjet droplets.

Experimental set-up

To obtain the diffraction signal of inkjet droplets, various optical instruments, including lasers, were combined to set up the experimental device25. Figure 6 is a schematic diagram of the experimental device used in this study. The laser (Thorlabs, CPS532) with a 532 nm wavelength, the opening of the pinhole was 2 mm, the focal length of the plano-convex lens (Thorlabs, LA1509-A) was 10 cm, and a laser line filter (Thorlabs, FL532-10) was used to pass the 532 nm wavelength. A high-speed camera (Kron Technologies Inc., Chronos 1.4) with a sensor of 6.6 μm × 6.6 μm per unit was used with a microscopic lens (HAYEAR, HY-180XA) with a working distance of 95 mm. Here, the mask was used to protect the camera sensor from the high intense light of the laser. The mask deposited silver into a circular shape with a diameter of 2 mm using vacuum deposition. The deposition rate was 1 Å per second and formed about 150 nm thickness. A neutral density (ND) filter (Thorlabs, NE15A-A) was used to reduce the intensity of laser light. The range of measurable speeds varies depending on the specifications of the optical components, which are shown in Table S2. The inkjet droplets were formed by the DMC-11610 cartridge and the inkjet printing system. The inkjet droplets used ink (Advanced Nano Products, DGP-40LT-15C) with silver nanoparticles dissolved. The inkjet head is positioned between the ND filter and the plano-convex lens, and the height was adjusted to allow the laser to pass approximately 500 μm below the nozzle inlet of the inkjet cartridge.Fig. 6Schematic diagram of the interference image-based measurement system to measure the flight speed of inkjet droplets.
