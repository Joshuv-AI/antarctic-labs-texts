Analysis of the comprehension and familiarity among the Saudi Arabian population regarding multiple sclerosis
Authors: Norah A. Alhamdan, Razan A. A. Lasloom, Abdulrahman M. T. Alharbi, Faisal A. I. Alaboodi, Amjad M. Al. Majrashi, Fatimah M. D. Alqahtani, Ragad S. Y. Alwadie, Shooq M. Al-Shahrani, Twfiq A. A. Alghamdi, Waad R. S. Alghamdi, Khames T. Alzahrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease affecting the central nervous system, characterized by immune-mediated demyelination and axonal degeneration. Its development is influenced by genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, and environmental factors such as viral infections, vitamin D deficiency, and smoking. MS affects over 2.3 million people globally, with prevalence varying by region, including an increasing trend in Saudi Arabia. Despite the growing burden, public awareness of MS in Saudi Arabia remains limited, contributing to delayed diagnosis and poorer patient outcomes. Assessing MS knowledge across regions is essential to guide targeted educational interventions, improve early symptom recognition, and enhance disease management.Methodology:The study was conducted across the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia among randomly selected adults. A minimum sample size of 384 individuals was calculated using the Qualtrics calculator at a 95% confidence level. Data were collected using a validated Multiple Sclerosis Knowledge Questionnaire consisting of 27 items assessing MS-related symptoms, risk factors, diagnostic beliefs, and general awareness. Scores ranged from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating better knowledge. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics.Results:Among 607 participants, significant knowledge gaps were identified. The mean age was 30.5 years, and the majority were female (69.9%). Only 11% recognized that MS affects multiple body systems, while 60.6% correctly identified its primary impact on the central nervous system. Additionally, 66.1% were unaware of MS epidemiology in Saudi Arabia, and 55.4% had misconceptions about its immunological basis. Overall, 57.7% demonstrated poor MS-related knowledge, with significant associations found between knowledge levels and gender, education, and personal acquaintance with MS.Conclusion:The findings highlight substantial deficiencies in MS awareness, symptom recognition, understanding of risk factors, and general health literacy among the Saudi population. Targeted educational programs and public health initiatives are urgently needed to improve early recognition, timely diagnosis, and better disease management.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, inflammatory, autoimmune disease that affects the central nervous system, primarily involving the brain, spinal cord, and optic pathways.[1] The disease commonly impacts the spinal cord, brainstem, optic nerve, and periventricular white matter.[2] Its pathophysiology is characterized by immune-mediated demyelination and axonal degeneration influenced by genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, and environmental triggers such as viral infections, vitamin D deficiency, and smoking.[3] Additional evidence highlights the role of environmental exposures in contributing to MS development.[4] These mechanisms contribute to the unpredictable course of MS and lead to progressive neurological impairment.[5]

Globally, MS affects more than 2.3 million people, with prevalence rates ranging from 50 to 300 per 100,000 population.[6] Higher-latitude regions report increased prevalence, while Middle Eastern countries—including Saudi Arabia—have shown an upward trend in MS incidence and prevalence over the past decade.[7] Regional studies across Saudi Arabia demonstrate considerable variation in MS awareness and prevalence between the Western, Eastern, Qassim, and Al-Madinah regions,[89101112] further emphasizing its significance as a growing public health concern.

Despite the increased burden of MS, previous research in Saudi Arabia consistently reveals substantial gaps in public knowledge regarding MS symptoms, risk factors, diagnosis, and available treatments.[89101113] These deficits contribute to delays in early symptom recognition, delayed diagnosis, and poorer health outcomes among patients.[14] Quality-of-life assessments in Saudi MS patients also show the negative impact of unrecognized symptoms and delayed care.[15]

Improving MS awareness carries important implications for both healthcare practitioners and patients. For practitioners, understanding public awareness levels supports the design of targeted educational interventions, encourages earlier case identification, and enhances clinical decision-making related to diagnosis and management.[1617] For patients and their families, increased MS literacy promotes earlier recognition of symptoms, supports timely diagnosis, encourages adherence to treatment, and ultimately improves quality of life.[1415]

While several studies have examined MS awareness in individual regions of Saudi Arabia, nationwide data remain limited, and significant regional knowledge disparities persist.[89101113] This highlights a clear research gap. Therefore, the present study aims to assess the level of MS-related knowledge and awareness among adults across different regions of Saudi Arabia. By identifying areas of inadequate understanding, this study contributes valuable insights to guide public health strategies aimed at improving early recognition and management of MS.

Objectives

Our study aims to evaluate the population’s level of knowledge and comprehension of the symptoms of multiple sclerosis.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This is an observational cross-sectional study. The study was conducted in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. A convenience sample of adults residing in Saudi Arabia was recruited through online distribution. This study was conducted between July 2024 and October 2024.

Sample size

With Calculator.net, the sample size was determined. 50% prevalence of awareness, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence interval were employed. A total of 384 people were the expected sample size.

Sample method

A convenience sampling technique was used to recruit participants. The questionnaire was distributed electronically through social media platforms across different regions of Saudi Arabia to reach adults aged 18 years and above.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Residents of Saudi Arabia aged 18 and more.

Exclusion Criteria: Residents of Saudi Arabia aged less than 18 and residents of Saudi Arabia who cannot read Arabic or English.

These inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to ensure that participants were capable of understanding the questionnaire and providing accurate and reliable responses.

Method for data collection, instrument, and score system

The data collection tool was a validated self-administered survey distributed across the regions of Saudi Arabia. It was constructed after reviewing relevant studies conducted and modified from a literature study published by Eyas K Farran et al.[10] The MS Knowledge Questionnaire was utilized. When its validity was examined in a prior study, both the content validity and internal consistency were found to be satisfactory. The Cronbach’s alpha for the reliability of our questionnaire was 0.79. The questionnaire used in this study had been previously validated in published research, and its content validity was reviewed by experts in neurology and public health. The questionnaire has 27 items in total, split into two main sections: the actual section, which consists of 20 items to gauge knowledge of MS, and the sociodemographic data section. Every item is a multiple-choice question with three to five alternative responses. One point was awarded for the right response. As a result, the lowest possible score after finishing the survey is zero, and the most probable score is 20.

Scoring system

Knowledge was assessed by using a 20-item questionnaire; each correct answer gets a point. The total knowledge score ranged from 0 to 20. The overall level of knowledge was assessed using Bloom’s cutoff point. Based on this cutoff point, a score between 20 and 13 points was good knowledge, a score between 12 and 9 points was moderate knowledge, and 8 or less was scored as poor knowledge.

Pilot test

The survey was distributed to 20 persons, who were asked to complete it. This was done to assess the feasibility of the study and how simple the questionnaire was. The final analysis’s findings did not include data from the pilot research. The pilot test aimed to assess clarity, comprehension, and feasibility of the questionnaire, and the feedback was used to refine the final version.

Analyses and entry method

Data entry was performed using Microsoft Office Excel 2016 for Windows. Subsequently, the data were transferred to IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, and Chi-square tests were performed to evaluate associations between knowledge levels and demographic variables, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

The present study aimed to analyze the comprehension and familiarity of the Saudi Arabian population regarding MS, revealing significant gaps in knowledge and understanding of this complex neurological condition. Previous research from diverse parts of Saudi Arabia indicates that a large segment of the populace has no idea about MS’s side effects, diagnosis, and treatment. For example, Dahlawi et al.[11] noted low awareness about MS of the community in the Western region of Saudi Arabia, presenting the same low awareness about the disease implications and management strategies. In addition, it was found by Al-Hamdan et al.[9] that most participants in the Qassim region lacked knowledge about MS, which corroborated the fact that this lack of awareness has affected all regions of the Kingdom.

The present findings are consistent with prior research from Saudi Arabia indicating poor public awareness of MS symptoms, diagnosis, and management (Dahlawi et al., 2022;[11] Al-Hamdan et al., 2021;[9] Alnefaie et al., 2023).[8] However, this study differs by using a larger sample across broader regions and providing an updated demographic breakdown, demonstrating unique gender and educational disparities.

As our study analyzed the demographic characteristics, we observed a mean age of participants of 30.5 years and strong gender discrimination as 69.9% of respondents are female. This is in keeping with what Al Jumah et al.[12] observed in Saudi Arabia when they studied the epidemiology of MS and identified the prevalence of female patients was higher than the prevalence of male patients. Our sample of 56.7% hold a bachelor’s degree or higher and yet even an educated population still has substantial knowledge gaps. This is concerning as studies of previous work have indicated that higher education levels are tied to better health literacy.[7]

We found that only 20.4% of participants responded correctly and 34.9% of participants believed that a repeat MRI was needed for confirmation of first attack. This lack of understanding of diagnostic criteria poses alarm given the other studies that document the importance of early diagnosis of MS that is necessary for adequate management of MS.[16] For instance, Makkawi pointed to the crucial role of advanced imaging techniques in diagnosing MS and the importance of public education on the procces of diagnosis for this disease.[17]

Furthermore, the study showed that 66.1% of subjects were unsure as to the causes and incidences of MS in Saudi Arabia, as well as 55.4% were unaware of the role of the immune system in the disease. The findings of this study echo the work of Al Wutayd et al.,[4] who found misconceptions about MS common in the Saudi population, impeding timely diagnosis and treatment. Of concern is this lack of awareness of the immune-mediated nature of MS and thereby the potential to confuse the pathophysiology of the disease and the need for immunomodulatory therapies.[5]

While 59.5% of participants already recognized muscle weakness and spasms as MS symptoms, a great number remained ignorant of other essential symptoms, such as fatigue and features of relapsing-remitting MS. The results correlate with what Alzeer et al.[14] observed in Saudi Arabia, that most MS patients cannot precisely describe their symptoms, which can lead to a delay in receiving the needed medical care. Such gaps in knowledge have important implications for morbidity and reduced quality of life for those affected by the disease.[15]

Statistical analysis of our study demonstrated significant correlations between participants’ knowledge levels versus factors, including gender, educational level, and participant characteristics. This is consistent with previous research in which demographic factors have been the focus in discussing their impact on health literacy and disease awareness.[18] For example, Alharbi et al.[19] found that educational attainment significantly affects the comprehension of MS among patients in Riyadh, and if this were targeted by educational intervention, the researchers could improve knowledge and awareness.

It is noteworthy that over 57% of participants exhibited poor MS knowledge despite 56.7% holding a Bachelor’s degree or higher. This suggests that educational attainment alone does not guarantee health literacy for complex neurological diseases. The observed knowledge gaps may reflect inadequate dissemination of MS information within academic and public health channels.

Contrasting literature, such as Alharbi et al. (2022), suggests higher awareness in urban populations, while our data reveal pervasive gaps even in well-educated subsets. Gender analysis indicates females had better MS knowledge than males, aligning with AlJumah et al. (2021) and highlighting possible differences in information-seeking behavior. Alarmingly, over 49% were uncertain about core MS symptoms like vision loss and fatigue, underscoring symptom awareness issues and delays in diagnosis, as described by Makkawi (2024) and Nazish et al. (2018).

However, though important, it is still necessary to acknowledge several limitations of this study. The problem with this cross-sectional design, though, is it cannot conclude what causes knowledge levels and demographic factors. Furthermore, there may be a bias from self-reported data because participants may overestimate their knowledge of MS. Besides, the present study was limited to a specific geographic area, which might not have implications for the various geographic areas of Saudi Arabia. Further research should expand these knowledge gaps, asking about these constructs in a more diverse population and assessing these awareness changes over time using longitudinal designs.

Our thematic analysis revealed three major issues: demographic disparities, systemic knowledge gaps, and uncertainties in symptom identification. These deficiencies contribute to delayed diagnosis and suboptimal MS management. As such, targeted educational initiatives are necessary to bridge the gender, educational, and regional divides, thereby improving early recognition, treatment outcomes, and patient support in Saudi Arabia.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for establishing causal relationships between demographic characteristics and MS knowledge. Second, data were self-reported, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Third, the use of convenience sampling limits the generalizability of the findings to the entire Saudi population. Finally, distributing the survey online may have excluded individuals with limited Internet access or lower digital literacy.

Future directions

Future research should include larger and more diverse samples across all regions of Saudi Arabia using probability sampling. Longitudinal studies are recommended to assess changes in MS awareness over time. Further studies should also evaluate the effectiveness of targeted educational interventions and explore how cultural, educational, and healthcare system factors influence public MS knowledge.

Conclusion

The results of this study stress the immediate importance of the establishment of educational programs that will ensure the spread of awareness and a broader understanding of multiple sclerosis in the Saudi Arabian population. Addressing the major knowledge gaps identified will help healthcare providers improve health outcomes and support for people who are affected by this complex condition. This result is consistent with the previous studies which mentioned the same deficiencies of MS awareness across different areas in Saudi Arabia indicating the ongoing need to educate the society against MS.
