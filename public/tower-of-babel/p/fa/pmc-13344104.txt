Contemporary achievement of guideline recommendations for blood pressure management after stroke in the CONVINCE trial

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for stroke. Studies between 1996 and 2013 reported high rates of non-achievement of guideline recommended blood pressure (BP) targets following stroke/TIA. Few recent large studies of long-term post-stroke BP management exist, and no studies have reported findings since the introduction of new guideline recommendations (systolic blood pressure [SBP] < 130 mmHg) in 2022.Patients and methodsWe analysed BP control in the CONVINCE trial, which randomised 3154 recent ischaemic stroke/high-risk TIA patients to colchicine 0.5 mg daily or usual care. Clinic BP was measured at 6-monthly trial visits from 2017 to 2024. We calculated mean follow-up BP using all available follow-up BP measurements, and examined factors associated with non-achievement of pre-2022 guideline BP targets by multivariable logistic regression.ResultsOf 3144 randomised consenting patients, at least 1 follow-up BP reading was available for 2920. A total of 36% of participants had mean SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and failed to meet pre-2022 guideline targets, while 70% had mean SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, not achieving current guideline targets. New approaches are needed to improve guideline implementation in practice.Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02898610).Keywords: hypertension, recurrent stroke, secondary prevention

Introduction

Hypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for stroke and vascular events.1 Following ischaemic stroke or TIA, elevated blood pressure (BP) is associated with an increased risk of recurrent events.2,3 Pharmacological BP reduction has been demonstrated in randomised trials to reduce the risk of recurrent events after stroke, with a linear association between the degree of BP lowering and the reduction in recurrence risk.4 In the general population, a 5-mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure (SBP) reduces the risk of a major adverse cardiovascular event by about 10%, and stroke by 13%.5 Following ischaemic stroke, pharmacological SBP reduction reduces recurrent stroke risk by about 20% compared to placebo.6

There has been gradual adoption of lower SBP targets following stroke in international guidelines. European Stroke Organisation (ESO) 2008 guidelines recommended BP lowering, recognising the benefit of a BP lowering of 10/5 mmHg, but did not recommend lowering to a specific BP target.7 International hypertension guidelines from the European Society of Hypertension (ESH) and European Society of Cardiology (ESC), the American Heart Association (AHA) and American Stroke Association (ASA) Stroke guidelines, adopted targets of < 140/90 mmHg in 2013/2014.8–10 In 2022, ESO recommended BP targets of < 130/80 mmHg for long-term secondary prevention.6 Current ASA guidelines also recommend this target.11

Despite randomised trial evidence of the benefits of BP reduction, implementation was suboptimal in older observational studies of real-world practice. In Europe, the ASPIRE-S study and EUROASPIRE III survey from the 2000s noted that only 37% and 38% of participants achieved SBP targets of < 140 mmHg for secondary stroke prevention, with Australian data reporting that 20% of patients had discontinued antihypertensive medication 1 year following stroke or TIA.12–14 However, contemporary real-world practice for late post-stroke BP management in Europe is unclear, as few recent data exist and almost no studies have reported since guidelines were updated in 2022.

Using data from a large contemporary randomised trial (CONVINCE) with prospective follow-up and multiple sequential BP readings, we aimed to examine achievement of guideline blood-pressure targets after ischaemic stroke, and factors associated with sub-optimal BP control.15 We first examined long-term achievement of the pre-2022 guideline targets (which applied for most of the follow-up period), and then explored achievement of the post-2022 stricter guideline targets (<130/80).

Patients and methods

Study population

The CONVINCE trial randomised 3154 recent ischaemic stroke/high-risk TIA patients to colchicine 0.5 mg daily, or usual care, at 144 hospital sites in 13 European countries and Canada. The interval between the qualifying event and randomisation was between 72 h and 28 days. The full inclusion/exclusion criteria have been previously described. In brief, the trial included clinically stable patients aged at least 40 years, with non-severe ischaemic stroke caused by large artery atherosclerosis of an ipsilateral carotid, vertebral or intracranial artery, lacunar disease or cryptogenic embolism or high-risk TIA, defined as transient focal motor or speech symptoms with an ABCD2 score of 4 or greater, large artery lumen stenosis of at least 50% on imaging, or hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted MRI consistent with symptoms. Patients were ineligible if the qualifying event was caused by atrial fibrillation, other cardioembolic source or other defined cause (eg, dissection), or if they had moderate to severe renal or liver impairment or other contraindications to colchicine use. Trial follow-up visits were conducted from 2017 to 2024. Median follow-up duration was 33.6 months.

Blood pressure measurement

All randomised patients in CONVINCE with at least one BP reading after the baseline visit were included in the analysis. Clinic BP was measured at 6-monthly follow-up trial visits from 2017 to 2024. Blood pressure readings were taken following a 5-min rest.

Endpoint

We defined achievement of guideline BP recommendations as mean BP during follow-up of < 140/90 mmHg, consistent with ESH/ESC and AHA hypertension guidelines, and ASA stroke guidelines which applied throughout most of the follow-up period. We also assessed control against 2022 ESO guideline targets of BP < 130/80 mmHg.

Statistics

Continuous variables were reported as means and SDs, or medians and IQRs, and categorical variables were reported as numbers (n) and percentages (%). Mean follow-up BP for an individual was calculated using all available follow-up BP readings for the individual, without imputation of missing values. We calculated percentages of participants achieving BP thresholds during follow-up at individual SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) thresholds; SBP < 140 mmHg, SBP < 130 mmHg, DBP < 90 mmHg, DBP < 80 mmHg, as well as guideline-based combined SBP and DBP thresholds of < 140/90 and < 130/80 mmHg. We performed univariate and multivariate forward logistic regression for factors associated with pre-2022 guideline non-adherence (mean systolic BP ≥ 140). We used a threshold P value < .05 on univariate analysis for inclusion of a variable in the multivariate model. Results from the multivariate logistic regression are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. A P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant.

In a sensitivity analysis, to investigate the influence of the updated 2022 ESO guideline targets on clinical management of BP in the CONVINCE cohort, we compared the mean SBP in the pre-2022 follow-up visits, with the mean SBP at corresponding trial visits for later-recruited patients after the guideline change (eg, mean SBP at the pre-2022 6-month visit for earlier-recruited patients was compared with the mean SBP at the post-2022 6-month visit for later-recruited patients).

All statistical analysis was conducted using R (version 4.5.0).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 3144 randomised consenting patients, at least 1 follow-up BP reading was available for 2920. The baseline characteristics according to achieved mean systolic BP are described in Table 1. There were high rates of modern secondary prevention medication use, with 97.6% of participants taking antiplatelet medications, and 94.1% taking statins at randomisation. At baseline, 65% of participants had a history of hypertension, and 70.2% were taking at least one antihypertensive medication. The most frequently prescribed antihypertensive classes were angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (36.2%), followed by calcium channel blockers (34.6%), beta blockers (18.7%), diuretics (15.7%) and angiotensin-receptor blockers (15.5%).

Blood pressure guideline adherence

At the baseline randomisation visit, the mean SBP was 137.6 mmHg, and mean DBP 79.0 mmHg. At baseline, 52% of participants had BP readings which met pre-2022 guideline recommendations (BP < 140/90 mmHg) and 25% met post-2022 recommendations (<130/80 mmHg). There were 11,422 follow-up BP readings available, 7001 (61.3%) pre-2022 guideline, and 4421 (38.7%) post-2022 recommendations.

Over the follow-up period, mean SBP was 136.6 mmHg (SD 12.8), and mean DBP was 79.3 mmHg (SD 7.8). During follow-up, 36% of participants had a mean SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, while 9% had a mean DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. Compared to the post-2022 guideline targets, 70% of participants had mean SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, and 47% had mean DBP ≥ 80 mmHg.

When analysed according to individual visit BP measures, 50% of participants had BP values exceeding the 140/90 mmHg pre-2022 guideline threshold, and greater than 75% of participants exceeded the 130/80 mmHg post-2022 guideline threshold (Figures 1 and 2). At each time-point, control of DBP was achieved in a higher proportion of participants than for control of SBP.

Factors associated with non-achievement of the SBP 140 mmHg threshold

On univariate logistic regression analysis, age (P = < .001), history of hypertension (P = < .001), diabetes mellitus (P = .002) and lacunar stroke subtype (P = .018) were associated with non-achievement of the pre-2022 SBP target of 140 mmHg. No associations were observed with sex, race, body mass index, baseline disability, treatment arm, smoking history, history of stroke prior to index event, stroke as qualifying event, ischaemic heart disease, impaired renal function or carotid stenosis.

On multivariable logistic regression, increased age (OR: 1.026; 95% CI, 1.018–1.034, per year increase), history of hypertension (OR: 1.47; 95% CI, 1.24–1.74), and lacunar stroke subtype (OR: 1.26; 95% CI, 1.06–1.49), were independently associated with non-achievement of the 140 mmHg target (Table 2), while there was a strong statistical trend for diabetes mellitus (OR: 1.20; 95% CI, 1–1.45).

Sensitivity analyses, including guideline target achievement following introduction of ESO target < 130/80 mmHg

We did a sensitivity analysis of the BP measures restricted to those after 6 May 2022, the date of publication of the updated target of < 130/80 mmHg in the revised ESO guideline.6 There were 4421 BP measurements available for this trial period, representing 38.7% of total recorded BP readings. When the mean SBP measured at each trial visit prior to guideline changes was compared with the mean SBP at the corresponding trial visit in the post-2022 guideline era, no differences were observed between achieved SBP in the pre- and post-2022 eras (Figure 3). Almost all BP measurements (98.9%) at final follow-up (end-of-trial) visits were taken following the ESO guideline change. A sensitivity analysis limited to participants with ≥ 3 BP readings (n = 2383) found similar mean BP and rates of BP control to the primary analysis. A further multivariate analysis model, incorporating baseline number of antihypertensive medications in place of hypertension history, had similar findings to the primary analysis (Table 2; Figure 4).

Discussion

In this analysis of CONVINCE trial participants, less than one-third (30%) of participants met current ESO SBP guideline targets of < 130 mmHg, and over one-third of participants did not meet older less-stringent targets of SBP < 140 mmHg, based on average follow-up BP measures. At 1 year or more after the publication of the updated ESO guidelines in 2022, 65% exceeded recommendations for SBP targets and 51% for DBP targets at the end-of-trial visit.

These findings are similar to previously published observational and secondary prevention studies. The EuroASPIRE study conducted from 2006 to 2008 found only 37.6% of patients with stroke achieved target SBP < 140 mmHg at the time of interview.13 At 3-year follow-up, the Norwegian Cognitive Impairment After Stroke prospective study (41.6% controlled), and the usual care arm of the INSPiRE-TMS trial (44% controlled), found similar rates of BP control.16,17 There has been some evidence of improved BP control. The SANO cluster-randomised study in Germany found 68.9% of usual care participants had controlled BP 1 year post-stroke.18 A retrospective cross-sectional analysis of the 2003–2014 National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES) found 64.5% of stroke survivors using antihypertensive medications were at BP goal, while an Irish primary-care study found 63.1% of patients with a history of stroke/TIA were at BP target.19,20

Our results highlight the challenge to achieve compliance to BP guidelines in a clinical trial setting, with selected patients and protocol-guided visit schedules and risk factor control. Achieving compliance to guideline targets is likely to be more challenging in unselected patients in routine clinical practice, particularly since the adoption of more-stringent targets of < 130/80 mmHg since 2022, and suggestions of further target reductions in some patient groups if tolerated.21 System-wide adoption of updated guidelines can take time to become clinically evident. However, we do not believe that the recent updating of guidelines was a major factor in our results, as a 2018 European survey reported that one-third of European countries (14/41) had national guideline targets of < 130/80 mmHg, prior to the ESO guideline update.22 The recent INTERASPIRE study highlighted similar challenges in achieving contemporary guideline BP targets for secondary prevention in patients with coronary heart disease.23

Several factors may contribute to poor BP guideline compliance after stroke: First, a general under-development of organised late secondary prevention services for stroke may be an important contributor. In Europe, BP control is predominantly carried out in general practice primary care settings following an ischaemic stroke or TIA.22 The location of antihypertensive prescription may play a role in achieving BP guideline targets, with evidence supporting in-hospital prescribing leading to increased rates of antihypertensive usage.24 Second, the under-utilisation of home blood pressure measurement (HBPM) may be a contributor. This is supported by data from the 2018 European Stroke survey, where BP was predominantly assessed using office-based readings, with only a third of countries performing out-of-office measurements routinely.22 Many patients now have home devices, but some evidence exists to suggest frequent incorrect measurement technique after stroke.25 Third, sub-optimal patient adherence to BP-lowering medication may contribute to our findings.26 This is supported by a meta-analysis of observational studies which found only 64.1% of stroke patients had high medication adherence to secondary prevention medications.27 Adherence to antihypertensives following stroke reduces the risk of recurrent vascular events, but effectively emphasising these benefits to complex, multimorbid patients following a stroke is challenging, particularly given elevated BP is often asymptomatic.28 Fourth, therapeutic inertia may partially explain our findings, which may relate to clinician perceptions of post-stroke disability, frailty, multi-morbidity and concern about adverse effects of medication. One study estimated that a third of treatment intensification opportunities may be missed by clinicians in stroke patients.29 The challenge of engaging both patients and clinicians to achieve BP targets should not be underestimated.

Identification of the relative contributions of these factors is important in future studies to prioritise interventions to improve BP control in stroke survivors. Several potential solutions exist, including improved communication with primary care,30 and greater organisation of late secondary prevention services which has been shown to improve BP control after stroke.31,32 Greater use of HBPM is acceptable to stroke patients,33,34 but individual participant data meta-analysis suggests that high-intensity co-interventions may be also required with HBPM to improve BP control after stroke.33 Remote BP monitoring combined with frequent medication titration may be particularly effective, as evidenced in the COMMIT study, which demonstrated lower SBP at 1 month and reduced risk of recurrent stroke, as part of a prospective population-based sequential comparison study. Initiatives to improve medication adherence have been suggested, such as virtual visits and telehealth, but these require further evaluation in larger studies to determine the best strategies for specific groups after stroke. Finally, simplification, convergence and effective education regarding BP guidelines may assist clinicians in primary and secondary care to overcome therapeutic hesitancy leading to higher rates of successful achievement of BP targets after stroke.35

Factors associated with guideline non-achievement identified in this study included increasing age, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus and lacunar stroke. These may be useful clinical factors to identify patients who require more intensive intervention to achieve targets, while also identifying patients at higher risk who may have the most to gain from intensive BP lowering.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this analysis include the large sample size, inclusion of 93% of CONVINCE participants and the high proportion of participants receiving guideline-based secondary prevention therapy representing contemporary practice. We acknowledge some limitations. Objective measures of antihypertensive medication adherence and dosage during follow-up were not recorded. Patients with severely disabling stroke were not included. However, those with non-severe stroke and TIA included in our study represent the population most intensively targeted for risk factor management in clinical practice. Our study enrolled a predominantly White European population which may limit generalisability. The CONVINCE trial was conducted from 2017 to 2024, and fewer (38.7%) BP readings were taken following the introduction of the 2022 guidelines. The COVID-19 pandemic may have impacted achievement of guideline targets.

Conclusions

In a large contemporary secondary stroke prevention cohort, over one-third of participants did not achieve older BP guideline targets, and 70% did not achieve current ESO guideline recommendations. These findings highlight ongoing challenges for management of hypertension in the setting of lowering BP targets.
