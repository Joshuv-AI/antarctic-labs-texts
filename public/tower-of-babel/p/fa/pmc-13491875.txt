Circulating leptin level associates with emergence time from general anesthesia in obese patients: a prospective study
Authors: Xin Liu, Tongtong Pei, Ranxi Wang, Jierui Shi, Yanjing Tian, Haidong Wei, Pengbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral anesthesia administration in obese patients presents significant challenges. In addition to the complexity of dosage and parameter management, delayed recovery from anesthesia substantially increases the risk of postoperative complications. Thus, promoting rapid recovery in this population is crucial. However, factors associated with delayed recovery in obese patients remain poorly understood. Given the influence of metabolism on brain activity and connectivity, this exploratory study aimed to identify metabolic factors associated with delayed recovery in obese individuals.MethodsThis study included 102 obese patients undergoing minor or moderate surgery under total intravenous anesthesia, with depth of anesthesia guided by the bispectral index. Peripheral circulating levels of leptin, orexin A, adiponectin, and lipid profiles were measured. Emergence time was defined as the duration from discontinuation of anesthetics to eye opening on command.ResultsThe median emergence time was 8 min (IQR 6–12). Univariate analysis indicated that patient height, sex, and leptin concentration were correlated with emergence time. Multivariate analysis revealed that circulating leptin was an independent factor associated with emergence time (β = −0.91, 95% CI: −1.42 to − 0.39, adjusted R² of 0.18).ConclusionsCirculating leptin level is associated with emergence time from general anesthesia in obese patients. However, it explains only a limited portion of the variability in emergence time given the low R2. The findings support an association rather than a ready‑to‑use predictive marker, and external validation is required before any clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-04086-2.Keywords: Leptin, Obesity, Metabolism, Anesthesia recovery

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a major global public health concern [1]. With its increasing prevalence worldwide, the incidence of obesity-related surgical conditions is also rising [2]. Perioperative care for obese patients presents considerable challenges due to alterations in pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics, as well as compromised physiological functions [3]. Of particular concern is the elevated risk of postoperative acute upper airway events [4]. Reduced functional residual capacity, diminished pulmonary reserves, and frequent comorbidities such as obstructive lung disease or obstructive sleep apnea predispose obese patients to hypoxia in the immediate postoperative period [3]. Therefore, promoting rapid recovery from general anesthesia in this population is critically important [5]. However, aside from the use of inhaled anesthetics like desflurane and strategies to increase ventilation and cardiac output to enhance drug clearance, few effective methods are available to accelerate anesthetic recovery in obese patients [4].

Given that redistribution of anesthetics plays a limited role in recovery [4], it is plausible that intrinsic factors—such as obesity-related metabolic changes—may contribute to delayed emergence. Indeed, hormones involved in energy balance and metabolic regulation in obesity, including leptin, orexin, and ghrelin, are also implicated in sleep regulation [6–9], which shares notable similarities with general anesthesia [10]. Furthermore, energy metabolism itself significantly influences sleep-wake cycles [11, 12]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear which specific obesity-associated metabolic factors may be linked to delayed recovery from anesthesia.

In this study, we hypothesized that the peri-induction obesity-related metabolic factors in peripheral blood were associated with emergence time from general anesthesia in obese surgical patients.

Subjects and Methods

Subjects

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University on November 1, 2022 (approval No. 2022246), and was institutionally registered via the National Medical Research Registration and Record Information System (www.medicalresearch.org.cn) on December 2, 2022, under record number MR-61-22-020645. Written consents were obtained from all patients or legal surrogates. A total of 148 obese patients from December 2022 to November 2023 were enrolled. The inclusion criteria include: (1) consent to the study, (2) scheduled for elective minor or moderate surgery under general anesthesia; (3) age between 18 and 70 years; (4) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II; (5) body mass index (BMI) > 28 kg/m² based on Chinese criteria [13] ; and (6) anticipated surgery duration within 4 h. Exclusion criteria included: (1) systolic blood pressure > 180 mmHg or < 90 mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure > 110 mmHg or < 60 mmHg; (2) severe cardiac, cerebral, hepatic, renal, pulmonary, or endocrine disease, or active severe infection; (3) preoperative use of narcotics or sedatives; (4) diagnosed sleep disorders; (5) consumption of alcohol or stimulant beverages within 3 days before surgery; and (6) unstable mental status.

Anesthesia protocol

Prior to surgery, all patients underwent an 8-hour fasting period. Upon entering the operating room, intravenous access was established and standard monitors were applied, including continuous measurement of heart rate, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry (SpO₂), electrocardiography (ECG), body temperature, bispectral index (BIS), and end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂). No premedication was administered. Anesthesia was induced with midazolam (2 mg), sufentanil (0.4 µg/kg), rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg), and etomidate (0.25–0.3 mg/kg). Maintenance was achieved using a continuous infusion of propofol (2–8 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil [0.15–0.3 µg/(kg·min)], titrated to maintain a BIS value between 40 and 60. Muscle relaxation was maintained with intermittent boluses of rocuronium (0.15 mg/kg) until surgical closure. Hemodynamic stability was maintained within 15% of baseline values using atropine, ephedrine, norepinephrine, or nitroglycerin as needed. Thirty minutes before the anticipated end of surgery, ketorolac (30 mg) was administered intravenously for analgesia. All anesthetic infusions were discontinued at the completion of surgery. Neuromuscular blockade was reversed with neostigmine (2.0 mg) and atropine (0.5 mg) upon the return of spontaneous breathing. Tracheal extubation was performed according to the Difficult Airway Society guidelines [14], after patients regained consciousness, achieved a respiratory rate > 8 breaths/min, and maintained an ETCO₂ < 45 mmHg. Patients were continuously monitored in the post‑anesthesia care unit before being discharged to the ward.

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded. Preoperative laboratory results—including blood lipid profiles (triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC], high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL‑c], low‑density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL‑c]) and cystatin C—were retrieved from the electronic medical record system. The following time intervals were defined and documented. Surgery duration: from skin incision to completion of skin closure. Anesthesia duration: from initiation of anesthesia induction to discontinuation of all anesthetic infusions.

The primary outcome was emergence time, defined as from cessation of anesthetic administration to eye‑opening on verbal command as assessed by an observer in the post-anesthesia care unit, using a consistent tone of voice at one-minute intervals. Secondary outcomes included extubation time (the interval from discontinuation of anesthetics to tracheal extubation), as well as the length of stay in the post‑anesthesia care unit (PACU).

Blood sample collection and analysis

Following anesthesia induction, 5 ml of peripheral blood was collected from each patient 10 to 15 min later to avoid pain and stress caused by blood drawing. Serum was separated and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Circulating levels of leptin (LEP), orexin‑A, and adiponectin (ADPQ) were measured using commercially available enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (JL11979, JL46192, and JL12255, respectively; JONLNBIO, Shanghai, China). All assays were performed in duplicate according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All laboratory assessments were performed blinded to emergence time.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) or frequency. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Variables were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using the chi‑square test. Univariate associations with emergence time were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Factors identified as potentially relevant were subsequently included in a multiple linear regression model to identify independent factors of emergence time through Prism10 (Graphpad software, LLC. Boston, MA, USA). Following model diagnostics, a robust linear regression analysis was performed on the original data using Matlab R2021b (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) to mitigate the influence of outliers and avoid distributional assumptions. All tests were two‑tailed, and a P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sample size was estimated using PASS 2021 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA) based on Spearman’s rank correlation test. A two-tailed alpha level of 0.05 and statistical power of 0.8 were set, and the correlation coefficient (r-value) was set to 0.3 in accordance with Cohen’s standards. The calculated minimum sample size was 87, and a 15% dropout rate was considered, leading to a final planned sample size of 102 patients.

Results

Patient characteristic and baseline data

Of the screened 148 patients, 37 were excluded due to a surgical duration exceeding 4 h, and a further 9 were excluded because of the administration of propofol for sedation prior to the return of consciousness in the post‑anesthesia care unit (PACU). Consequently, data from 102 patients were included in the final analysis. The baseline characteristics of the included patients are summarized in Table 1. The median emergence time was 8 min (IQR 6–12). For patient grouping, a cutoff value of 8 min was adopted, based on a previous study that reported the 95% confidence interval (CI) of emergence time as 331 to 508 s after total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) in obese patients [15].

Univariate association of factors with emergence time

Univariate analysis identified patient height, gender, serum leptin concentration and extubation time as variables potentially associated with emergence time. These variables except for extubation time were subsequently entered into a multiple linear regression model. The analysis demonstrated that only leptin concentration remained an independent factor of emergence time, as seen in Table 3, the adjusted R² was 0.24. Despite the absence of significant multicollinearity (all VIFs < 2.5), the residuals violated the normality assumption (D’Agostino-Pearson test, P < 0.01) and three outliers were detected (> 2.5 SD). Given these findings, a robust linear regression was employed. The results, presented in Table 4, indicated that leptin concentration remained an independent factor of emergence time in the robust model, with an adjusted R² of 0.18. Discussion

In this study, we found that patient height, sex, and leptin concentration were correlated with emergence time after general anesthesia in obese patients by univariate analysis, while multivariate analysis indicated that circulating leptin was an independent factor associated with emergence time in obese patients. Nevertheless, the final robust regression model explained only 18% of the variance in emergence time (adjusted R² = 0.18), meaning that the majority of variability remains unexplained. Thus, although the association between leptin and emergence time is statistically robust, leptin in its current form should not be considered a clinically useful predictive biomarker.

Obesity may cause delayed recovery from anesthesia, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative complications, particularly pulmonary adverse events [16, 17]. Strategies to promote faster emergence in obese patients include the use of volatile anesthetics with low blood/gas partition coefficients or the adoption TIVA [18, 19]. Additionally, accelerating the pulmonary clearance of inhaled anesthetics through increased ventilation may also shorten recovery time [20]. In the present study, we employed TIVA to minimize the influence of pulmonary elimination kinetics. Nevertheless, we still observed delayed recovery in obese patients, suggesting that factors beyond pharmacokinetic alterations contribute to prolonged emergence. For instance, adiposity-related conditions such as fatty liver have been linked to delayed recovery [21]. However, whether other obesity-associated metabolic factors are associated with emergence from general anesthesia remains unclear.

Obesity fundamentally results from an imbalance between energy intake and expenditure. In this study, we examined two adipokines involved in energy homeostasis—leptin and adiponectin [22], as well as the neuropeptide orexin A, which participates in both energy regulation and arousal in animal studies [23]. Notably, we found that leptin was significantly associated with emergence time from general anesthesia in obese patients, a result consistent across both univariable and multivariable analyses. Leptin is predominantly secreted by adipose tissue, and its circulating levels generally correlate with body fat mass [24]. However, in obesity, leptin concentrations can vary widely, ranging from relatively low to markedly elevated [25], a pattern also evident in our cohort. Low leptin levels in obese individuals may stem from genetic or transcriptional alterations, whereas high levels often indicate leptin resistance [25]. It is also important to note that leptin levels are influenced by additional factors such as sleep patterns and dietary habits [26, 27]. Leptin exerts potent effects in both peripheral tissues and the central nervous system in animal studies [25]. In the brain, its most well‑studied roles pertain to energy homeostasis and satiety. Specific hypothalamic nuclei involved in these processes are known targets of leptin. For example, leptin‑responsive neurons in the arcuate nucleus modulate orexin in the lateral hypothalamus [28], yet whether leptin has a role in anesthetic emergence is not defined. Beyond energy balance, leptin also modulates neural connectivity and activity in selected brain regions [29]. Leptin excites neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), increasing sympathetic outflow [30]. Furthermore, leptin promotes dopamine release in the dorsal striatum and nucleus accumbens [31]. Although insights from experimental animal studies shed light on the potential role leptin-sensitive pathways in the regulation of brain connectivity and activity, our observational study can only conclude that leptin may serve as a potential biomarker requiring validation for emergence time in obese surgical patients. Given the small R2 value of 0.18, leptin explains only a limited fraction of variability. Besides, external validation is required before any clinical translation.

Given the shared similarities between sleep and anesthetic states, it is noteworthy that sleep disorders are prevalent among obese patients [9]. Sleep deficiency not only contributes to obesity but may also delay recovery from general anesthesia [32, 33]. While sleep habits were not formally recorded in this study, preoperative sleep deprivation could partially explain the observed association between low leptin levels and delayed emergence, as sleep loss is known to reduce circulating leptin concentrations [9, 34]. Obese patients are often accompanied with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which manifested with delayed recovery than the non-OSA patients [35]. Patients with OSA exhibit high levels of leptin, contradicting to the negative association of leptin levels with emergence time from general anesthesia. A possible explanation might be that OSA patients are concurrently resistant to leptin [36]. Simultaneously, the sleep restriction imposed by OSA which complicates the recovery profile may also serve as a major confounding factor for the relationship between leptin and emergence time [37], representing a significant limitation in this study. Given the complex interaction between sleep, leptin, and OSA, the observed association may partly reflect unmeasured sleep-related physiology rather than a direct mechanistic effect of leptin itself. Future investigations into the correlation between emergence time and leptin in OSA patients may shed light on these issues.

Return of consciousness is one of the important endpoints for reversal of anesthesia state. The latency to eye opening is one of the commonly used parameters. Other behavior endpoints include return of the gagging reflex, extubation time or return of spontaneous respiration, and responsiveness to verbal commands [38]. In the present study, emergence time, defined as latency to eye opening, was selected as the primary outcome, as it serves as a reliable efficiency indicator in resource-intensive settings such as operating rooms and PACUs, where even minute-by-minute improvements contribute to substantial cost savings [38]. In obese patients, emergence time also correlates well with other anesthesia reversal endpoints including extubation time and length of PACU stay [16, 17, 39]. Consistent with this, our study observed a strong correlation between emergence time and extubation time (Table 2), a finding of clinical significance given that prolonged emergence may act as a risk factor for postoperative respiratory complications in obese patients [16].

It’s evident that male patients have a longer recovery time than female after general anesthesia [40]. Our findings corroborate this sex-specific disparity in the obese cohort. The multifactorial mechanisms underlying faster recovery in women have been summarized elsewhere [40], including physiological differences, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic variations. For instance, lipid-soluble anesthetics exhibit a larger volume of distribution and higher clearance in women, while water-soluble agents have a smaller volume of distribution, requiring lower doses. Additionally, women have reduced plasma protein binding of anesthetics, are less sensitive to general anesthetics (conferring a higher risk of intraoperative awareness), and female hormones may modulate anesthesia states via central nervous system pathways. Women also display greater sensitivity to µ-receptor agonist morphine and κ-receptor agonists [40]. Sexually dimorphic brain changes and interactions between brain physiology and anesthetic agents may further elucidate this sex-specific effect [41]. Thus, sex imbalance in the study cohort could constitute an important confounder; however, multivariate analysis revealed no significant sex-specific effect on outcomes (Table 3).

Several limitations of this study merit consideration. First, as discussed above, the final model explained only 18% of the variance in emergence time, indicating that leptin is not a clinically useful predictive biomarker at this stage. Second, the potential influence of sleep‑related physiology (sleep quality, duration, and undiagnosed OSA) remains a major source of residual confounding; the observed association may partly reflect these unmeasured variables rather than a direct leptin effect. Third, the study is observational and cannot establish causality; leptin could only be framed in terms of association rather than direct mechanism. We also acknowledge that samples were obtained after induction rather than preoperatively; therefore, the measured concentrations may not fully reflect true baseline physiological conditions. The potential influence of anesthetic induction on circulating biomarkers cannot be completely excluded. As for the multivariable model, the conventional method is to include all factors with a univariate P value less than 0.1. However, clinically relevant factors such age, BMI, anesthesia duration, and anesthetic drug exposure should be evaluated. In our initial multivariable analysis, we include these factors such age, BMI, anesthesia duration, and anesthetic drug exposure, with the exception of extubation time due to its overlap with emergence time, although their univariate P values were all above 0.1. The results consistently identified leptin as the only independent factor. We adopted current conservative modeling strategy based on univariate screening to avoid overfitting.

Future research should focus on prospective validation of the leptin‑emergence association in independent cohorts, incorporating objective sleep assessments and characterization of obstructive sleep apnea status. Additionally, mechanistic studies in relevant animal models or human neuroimaging paradigms evaluating leptin‑sensitive arousal pathways and neurocircuits that control metabolism and food intake are needed to determine whether the observed association reflects a causal biological effect.
