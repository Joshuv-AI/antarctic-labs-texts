MAPK Inhibitor‐Tolerant Persister Cells in Melanoma: Mechanisms and Therapeutic Vulnerabilities
Authors: Bhoomi Shah, Mackenzie M Mayhew, Jacob Sellers, Russell G Witt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThough major advancements have been made within the realm of targeted and immune‐based therapies, metastatic melanoma still remains one of the most notoriously incurable malignancies due to early drug tolerance and eventual resistance. Increasing evidence has started to show drug‐tolerant persister cells as a critical nongenetic mechanism that allows survival under MAPK pathway inhibition. While genetically resistant clones support stable mutations, persister cells enter reversible, slow‐cycling states which are triggered by stressful conditions. This review synthesizes current research on the various molecular and cellular mechanisms which support melanoma persister cell formation, focusing specifically on phenotype plasticity, selective translational control, metabolic rewiring, redox buffering, and compensatory signaling pathways. We highlight how dynamic transitions among MITF‐low, SOX10‐low, and KDM5B‐high states enable persistence under therapeutic pressure, and how epitranscriptomic regulation and metabolic shifts toward oxidative/lipid‐dependent processes help decouple and focus on survival rather than proliferation. Furthermore, we examine microenvironment‐driven activation of RhoA‐FAK‐AKT signaling and redox‐adaptive sulfur metabolism as unique adaptive resistance mechanisms. Finally, our paper emphasizes persister states serving as evolutionary intermediates from which stable resistance can eventually emerge, and outline therapeutic strategies which exploit the transient persister vulnerability and help prevent melanoma drug‐resistance.Keywords: cell proliferation, drug tolerance, melanoma, proto‐oncogene proteins, signal transduction

Introduction

Despite major therapeutic advances, distantly metastatic melanoma remains a lethal disease, with more than half of patients dying within 5 years of diagnosis [1]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors produce durable responses in a subset of patients, with objective response rates of approximately 35%–50% for anti‐PD‐1 monotherapy and 58%–67% for combined anti‐CTLA‐4 and anti‐PD‐1 therapy. In contrast, patients with BRAF‐mutant melanoma experience rapid responses to combined BRAF and MEK inhibition, with response rates of 65%–80%, but relapse typically occurs within 12–15 months, highlighting the limited durability of MAPK‐directed therapy [2, 3, 4, 5].

Accumulating evidence identifies drug‐tolerant persister cells as a central driver of early relapse following MAPK pathway inhibition. Persister cells survive drug concentrations lethal to most tumor cells, producing apparent clinical responses followed by delayed relapse or outgrowth at other sites. Persister survival is mediated by nongenetic mechanisms, including epigenetic remodeling, selective mRNA translation, and metabolic adaptation, rather than stable resistance mutations [6, 7].

Unlike genetically resistant clones, which acquire heritable alterations that restore proliferation under continued therapy, persister cells enter a reversible, slow‐cycling state characterized by suppression of global protein synthesis with selective translation of stress‐adaptive transcripts [8]. This adaptive state is highly plastic and heterogeneous, encompassing multiple phenotypes such as KDM5B‐high slow‐cycling cells and SOX10‐low invasive cells, each shaped by therapeutic and microenvironmental stress (Table 1) [13, 18, 19].

In this review, we synthesize current insights into the major stress‐adaptive programs that enable melanoma persister cells to survive MAPK pathway inhibition, focusing on phenotypic plasticity, selective translation, metabolic rewiring, redox buffering, and activation of alternative survival signaling pathways. Together, these adaptations represent both fundamental barriers to durable response and promising targets for rational combination strategies aimed at preventing resistance.

Genetic and Signaling Basis

Approximately 50% of cutaneous melanomas harbor activating BRAF V600E mutations that drive constitutive MEK–ERK signaling and create a strong dependence on the MAPK pathway for proliferation and survival [9]. An additional 15%–25% of melanomas carry NRAS mutations, which activate MAPK signaling upstream through RAS [27, 28, 29]. Despite distinct upstream drivers, both genetic contexts converge on sustained ERK activity as a central oncogenic requirement in melanoma.

Selective BRAF inhibitors produce rapid clinical responses in BRAF‐mutant melanoma, but monotherapy responses are short‐lived due to adaptive reactivation of ERK signaling through loss of negative feedback and engagement of bypass pathways [10]. Combined BRAF and MEK inhibition more effectively suppresses MAPK signaling and delays relapse, yet disease progression remains inevitable, implicating nongenetic adaptive mechanisms in early drug tolerance.

Single‐cell studies have shown that MAPK pathway inhibition does not uniformly suppress ERK signaling in melanoma cells [10]. Rather, a subset of cells displays brief ERK reactivation after drug exposure, a phenomenon that is not readily captured by bulk assays and has been linked to persistence and outgrowth under therapy [9, 10, 30]. Intravital imaging studies further support a role for stromal niches in early drug tolerance during BRAF inhibition [31]. Mechanistically, BRAF inhibition suppresses the dominant MAPK signaling axis but can permit adaptive rebound through receptor tyrosine kinase‐driven upstream signaling and RAF dimer‐dependent ERK reactivation [10, 32, 33, 34, 35]. In a subset of melanoma cells, these transient ERK signals support persistence and escape from complete cell‐cycle arrest under continued drug pressure [10, 30].

Cell fate is determined by the amplitude and duration of ERK signaling. Excessive or prolonged ERK activation induces apoptosis, whereas precisely tuned transient signaling enables survival. This balance is further shaped by disruption of MAPK negative feedback, as BRAF inhibition rapidly depletes dual‐specificity phosphatases, rendering cells hypersensitive to upstream inputs while increasing reliance on residual buffering mechanisms to prevent toxic ERK hyperactivation [10, 36].

The adaptive microenvironment is further shaped by inflammatory cues from the tumor microenvironment. During MAPK pathway inhibition, melanoma inflammatory niches consisting of tumor‐associated macrophages and fibroblasts contribute to treatment tolerance through a cytokine‐signaling network [37]. Macrophage‐derived IL‐1β stimulates fibroblasts to produce CXCR2 ligands, and this signaling is amplified in the presence of MAPK inhibitors. While tumor‐derived factors can induce IL‐1β production in macrophages, the precise signals that generate IL‐1β‐producing macrophages in the context of MAPK inhibitor treatment remain incompletely defined [38, 39, 40].

Phenotypic Heterogeneity and Plasticity

Drug‐tolerant persister cells are distinguished from genetically resistant melanoma cells not by acquisition of secondary mutations, but by their capacity for phenotypic plasticity, defined as the reversible transition between alternative transcriptional and metabolic states in response to drug stress. Transcriptomic profiling in a melanoma cell line model (KDM5B‐high cells) suggests that persister cells may occupy multiple distinct survival states during MAPK pathway inhibition, supporting phenotypic heterogeneity rather than a single uniform persister population [20].

A central regulator of melanoma phenotypic plasticity is the microphthalmia‐associated transcription factor MITF, which governs lineage survival while coordinating proliferation, differentiation, and invasion programs [41]. The MITF rheostat model proposes that graded changes in MITF expression drive distinct phenotypic outcomes, with very low levels associated with senescence, intermediate‐low levels promoting invasive and migratory behavior, and moderately high levels supporting proliferation with reduced invasiveness [42]. Low MITF states are frequently coupled to receptor tyrosine kinase activation, particularly AXL, and a low MITF‐to‐AXL ratio has been linked in preclinical studies to reduced sensitivity to MAPK pathway inhibition [43]. Recent work identifies Transglutaminase Type 2 as a regulator of this axis, with loss of Transglutaminase Type 2 shifting cells toward a MITF‐low and AXL‐high, therapy‐tolerant state [41].

At the opposite end of the differentiation spectrum, loss of the neural crest transcription factor SOX10 defines a distinct persister program characterized by invasive, mesenchymal‐like features, extracellular matrix remodeling, and increased focal adhesion kinase signaling. SOX10‐low persister cells exhibit enhanced tolerance to MAPK pathway inhibition, but this reprogramming introduces a specific vulnerability. Reduced expression of the inhibitor of apoptosis protein cIAP2 renders these cells selectively sensitive to IAP antagonists such as birinapant, and combining IAP antagonism with MAPK pathway inhibition delays relapse and prolongs survival in preclinical models [11, 13].

A third persister phenotype is defined by high expression of the histone demethylase KDM5B. KDM5B‐high melanoma cells adopt a slow‐cycling, melanocyte‐like state marked by impaired cytokinesis and reversible growth arrest, enabling survival during MAPK pathway inhibition [20]. Sustained induction of KDM5B traps cells in G1 phase, suppresses tumor growth, and preserves therapeutic sensitivity, highlighting how enforced stabilization of selected persister states may be exploited therapeutically.

Together, these studies illustrate that melanoma persister cells span a continuum of phenotypic states rather than a single adaptive endpoint. Resistance emerges through dynamic interconversion among MITF‐low and AXL‐high, SOX10‐low, and KDM5B‐high programs in response to therapeutic and microenvironmental cues, underscoring the need for state‐aware therapeutic strategies. Translational and Epitranscriptomic Rewiring

Translational remodeling is a central adaptive strategy that enables melanoma persister cells to survive MAPK pathway inhibition. BRAF and MEK inhibitors impose acute metabolic and proteotoxic stress, activating the integrated stress response, a conserved pathway that suppresses global protein synthesis while enabling selective translation of stress‐adaptive transcripts. In melanoma cells, MAPK pathway inhibition rapidly induces phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 alpha, inhibiting eIF2B activity and broadly reducing translation initiation [8, 24, 44, 45].

Despite this global suppression, the integrated stress response promotes selective translation of transcripts that support lineage plasticity and survival. Under conditions of glutamine deprivation or sustained eIF2 alpha phosphorylation, melanoma cells preferentially induce activating transcription factor 4, a key stress response mediator. ATF4 represses MITF transcription while upregulating AXL, driving a dedifferentiated, invasive, and drug‐tolerant persister phenotype [8]. Genetic studies indicate that eIF2α phosphorylation is a key driver of this adaptive program, supporting a causal role for integrated stress response activation in melanoma cell plasticity rather than it being simply a passive consequence of drug exposure [8, 24, 46, 47].

Selective translation in persister cells is further reinforced by epitranscriptomic regulation through N6‐methyladenosine modification. Ribosome profiling of MAPK inhibitor‐treated melanoma cells revealed marked suppression of global protein synthesis, yet efficient translation of a restricted subset of mRNAs encoding epigenetic regulators and components of the PI3K‐mTORC2 survival pathway, including CREBBP, ARID1A, CHD6, RICTOR, and PIK3CA [48, 49]. These transcripts are enriched for m6A modifications within their 5′ prime untranslated regions, enabling cap‐independent translation initiation through the RNA helicase eIF4A. Disruption of m6A methyltransferase components, including METTL3 and WTAP, impairs selective translation and restores sensitivity to MAPK pathway inhibition, identifying m6A‐dependent translation as a critical survival mechanism in persister cells [48].

Consistent with this mechanism, pharmacologic inhibition of eIF4A with silvestrol selectively targets melanoma persister cells by suppressing translation of m6A‐enriched survival transcripts. In preclinical models, silvestrol limits the emergence of persisters and reduces viability of established persister populations when combined with MAPK pathway inhibition, highlighting translational machinery as a therapeutically exploitable vulnerability [48].

Selective translational control also directly shapes metabolic plasticity in persister cells. The RNA‐processing kinase UHMK1 promotes translation of mRNAs encoding mitochondrial and metabolic proteins, enabling a shift toward oxidative phosphorylation during MAPK pathway inhibition. Loss of UHMK1 impairs mitochondrial respiration, reduces persister cell survival, and delays resistance emergence in vivo, linking translational rewiring to the metabolic adaptations required for persistence [50].

These findings demonstrate that translational remodeling enables melanoma cells to decouple survival from proliferation under therapeutic stress by selectively sustaining stress‐adaptive, metabolic, and survival programs, positioning the translational machinery as a central node in nongenetic drug tolerance.

Metabolic Reprogramming

Glycolytic Suppression and the MITF–PGC‐1α Oxidative Rebound

Melanoma cells preferentially rely on aerobic glycolysis, a phenotype driven by constitutive MAPK signaling and reflected clinically by high 18F‐FDG uptake [51, 52]. ERK‐dependent induction of HIF‐1α and c‐MYC sustains this glycolytic state, and BRAF inhibition rapidly suppresses these programs, leading to collapse of glycolytic flux [53]. In response, a subset of melanoma cells undergoes a rapid, reversible metabolic transition toward mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, defining a key persister adaptation (Figure 3).

In pretreatment state, melanoma cells exhibit a glycolysis dominant (Warburg‐like) state driven by MAPK/ERK signaling, with increased glucose uptake and lactate production. When exposed to therapy, persister cells undergo metabolic reprogramming characterized by suppression of glycolysis and a shift toward mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation/fatty acid oxidation. This transition is associated with reduced MITF expression, activation of PGC‐1α, increased fatty acid uptake through CD36, and engagement of ACOX1‐mediated peroxisomal FAO. In NRAS‐mutant persister cells, chronic stress leads to reduced glycolysis and therefore redox buffering capacity as mitochondrial reactive oxygen species accumulate.

Loss of ERK activity stabilizes MITF and enhances transcription of PGC‐1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism. Upregulation of PGC‐1α drives a marked increase in oxygen consumption within days of MAPK pathway inhibition, consistent with a nongenetic metabolic adaptation rather than clonal selection [54, 55]. Tumor biopsies from patients with BRAF‐mutant melanoma progressing on BRAF inhibitor with or without MEK inhibitor showed elevated PGC‐1α expression in approximately half of resistant tumors, implicating PGC‐1α–driven OXPHOS as a major adaptive program that limits the efficacy of MAPK pathway inhibition [56]. In contrast, other resistant melanomas epigenetically suppress PGC‐1α and adopt alternative metabolic programs, highlighting the heterogeneity of resistance [56, 57].

This metabolic rebound is therapeutically targetable. Dual inhibition of mTORC1 and mTORC2 promotes phosphorylation and cytoplasmic sequestration of MITF, suppresses PGC‐1α expression, and collapses oxidative phosphorylation. In preclinical models, combined mTORC1/2 and MEK inhibition selectively resensitizes MITF‐PGC‐1α‐high tumors while having minimal impact on OXPHOS‐low melanomas [56]. These findings position MITF localization and PGC‐1α expression as pragmatic biomarkers for identifying patients likely to benefit from MEK plus mTORC1/2 combination strategies.

Peroxisomal Lipid Metabolism in BRAF‐Mutant Persisters

Analysis of single‐cell transcriptomic data from residual persister cells isolated from patient‐derived melanoma xenografts treated with BRAF/MEK inhibition identified a persister population with features of nutrient deprivation and lipid dependence, termed starved‐like melanoma cells [6]. These cells upregulate the fatty acid transporter CD36 and activate fatty acid oxidation programs; however, survival depends specifically on peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation mediated by the rate‐limiting enzyme acyl‐CoA oxidase 1. CD36 induction is rapid and reversible, consistent with metabolic plasticity rather than clonal selection [25].

Genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of acyl‐CoA oxidase 1 selectively impairs oxygen consumption, induces toxic lipid accumulation, and produces durable tumor control when combined with BRAF and MEK inhibition [6, 26]. In contrast, blockade of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation alone does not replicate this effect, highlighting peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation as a nonredundant metabolic dependency in this persister state [6, 25]. In this context, melanoma persister cells shift from glycolysis to oxidative respiration supported in part by a PPARα‐regulated peroxisomal fatty acid β‐oxidation program.

Beyond fatty acid oxidation, peroxisome biogenesis itself is essential for survival of CD36‐positive persisters. Disruption of peroxisome assembly induces cytotoxic ceramide accumulation, which persister cells buffer through UDP‐glucose ceramide glucosyltransferase‐mediated detoxification. Combined inhibition of peroxisome biogenesis and ceramide clearance selectively eradicates CD36‐positive persisters and prolongs tumor control in BRAF inhibitor‐relapsed xenograft models [26].

ROS‐Biased Quiescence in NRAS‐Mutant Persisters

NRAS‐mutant melanomas represent a distinct metabolic entity compared with BRAF‐mutant disease, signaling through CRAF and PI3K pathways and exhibiting reduced baseline oxidative and glycolytic capacity [58]. This configuration limits metabolic flexibility under acute stress. During transient nutrient deprivation, NRAS‐mutant cells adapt by rerouting signaling toward NRAS‐BRAF to sustain glycolytic flux, exposing a vulnerability to glycolytic inhibition under glucose‐limited conditions.

In contrast, chronic MAPK pathway inhibition drives a quiescent persister state characterized by suppression of both glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway. This adaptation reduces NADPH and glutathione production, compromises redox buffering, and creates heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress [59]. Thus, NRAS‐mutant persisters rely on a ROS‐biased survival strategy that is distinct from the oxidative or lipid‐dependent programs observed in BRAF‐mutant melanoma, revealing genotype‐specific metabolic liabilities that may be therapeutically exploited.

Redox and Sulfur Metabolism

MAPK pathway inhibition induces a rapid metabolic shift toward mitochondrial respiration, increasing electron transport chain activity and generating a burst of reactive oxygen species. Within hours of BRAF and MEK inhibitor treatment, melanoma cells accumulate superoxide and peroxide, triggering oxidative stress that must be actively buffered for survival [60]. Drug‐tolerant persister cells engage multiple, partially redundant redox‐adaptive programs that collectively limit oxidative damage while preserving mitochondrial function.

One prominent adaptation involves rewiring sulfur metabolism to generate reactive sulfur species. Drug exposure induces selective upregulation of cystathionine gamma lyase with concurrent repression of cystathionine beta synthase, redirecting cystine toward production of hydrogen sulfide and low‐molecular‐weight persulfides [61]. These sulfur species promote protein persulfidation, a reversible modification that shields catalytic cysteine residues from irreversible oxidation while maintaining enzymatic activity [61, 62, 63]. In addition, oxidation of hydrogen sulfide and persulfides supports electron flow through the respiratory chain, sustaining ATP production under oxidative stress [61, 64, 65, 66]. In vivo, inhibition of cystathionine gamma lyase destabilizes this redox shield and delays resistance onset when combined with MAPK pathway inhibition, establishing sulfur metabolism as a functional dependency of persister cells [61].

Persister cells also activate a cysteine backfilling program driven by ATF4 and NRF2. When cystine import through the xCT antiporter or glutathione synthesis is compromised, ATF4 induces transsulfuration pathway enzymes to restore intracellular cysteine pools while NRF2 reinforces antioxidant defenses during prolonged stress [67]. This program not only preserves redox balance but also influences melanoma lineage state, as cysteine‐derived metabolites support histone acetylation at the MITF promoter and maintain differentiation programs. Disruption of xCT reduces MITF expression, impairs lysosome biogenesis, and sensitizes melanoma cells to lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [68, 69, 70]. Ferroptosis inducers such as erastin retain cytotoxic activity against BRAF inhibitor‐resistant melanoma cells, selectively eliminating drug‐tolerant populations [71].

NF‐κB signaling provides an additional redox‐buffering layer in persister cells. Therapy‐induced ROS activates NF‐κB‐dependent transcriptional programs, including induction of mitochondrial superoxide dismutase 2, which converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide for downstream detoxification [60, 72]. NF‐κB‐driven antioxidant signaling supports survival under sustained oxidative stress and intersects with invasive, dedifferentiated melanoma cell states, reinforcing the link between redox control and phenotypic plasticity [73].

Finally, persister cells engage a metabolic brake on oxidative metabolism through regulation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Oxidative stress activates pyruvate dehydrogenase kinases, leading to phosphorylation of the PDH E1 alpha subunit and reduced flux of pyruvate into the tricarboxylic acid cycle. This limits further mitochondrial ROS generation while preserving viability [74]. Pharmacologic inhibition of PDK activity reverses this adaptation, increases mitochondrial ROS, and sensitizes melanoma cells to MAPK pathway inhibition, highlighting the PDH‐PDK axis as a complementary target to disrupt persister redox homeostasis.

These layered redox‐buffering programs enable melanoma persister cells to tolerate therapy‐induced oxidative stress while maintaining metabolic function. Although mechanistically distinct, each pathway converges on limiting ROS‐mediated damage, revealing multiple, potentially synergistic vulnerabilities that may be exploited to destabilize persister cell survival under MAPK pathway inhibition.

Compensatory RhoA‐FAK‐AKT Signaling

FAK as a Central Node in Microenvironment‐Driven Resistance

In BRAF V600E mutant melanoma, nongenetic resistance to MAPK pathway inhibition is strongly shaped by the tumor microenvironment. Intravital imaging studies revealed that although ERK signaling is rapidly suppressed following BRAF inhibitor administration, subsets of melanoma cells undergo early pathway reactivation in regions enriched with melanoma‐associated fibroblasts, whereas cells in low‐stromal‐density regions maintain sustained ERK suppression [31].

Mechanistically, BRAF inhibition stimulates fibroblast‐mediated extracellular matrix remodeling, including increased fibronectin deposition. Engagement of fibronectin with integrin beta 1 promotes focal adhesion assembly and activation of focal adhesion kinase and Src signaling, enabling adaptive ERK reactivation despite continued MAPK pathway inhibition [31]. Pharmacologic inhibition of FAK alone has minimal impact on drug‐naive melanoma cells [31], but combined inhibition of FAK and BRAF prevents ERK reactivation and induces cell death in melanoma‐fibroblast coculture systems, underscoring the context‐dependent role of focal adhesion signaling [22, 31, 75].

Transcriptomic analyses of posttreatment melanoma biopsies further support focal adhesion signaling as a compensatory pathway, with focal adhesion gene sets consistently enriched in resistant tumors [75]. Treatment with RAF and MEK inhibitors increases phosphorylation of FAK, indicating adaptive engagement of this pathway under drug pressure [75].

Upstream of FAK, Rho family GTPases regulate actin cytoskeletal dynamics and focal adhesion assembly. BRAFi‐resistant melanoma cells frequently exhibit enhanced actin polymerization and elevated RhoA activity, and paired tumor analyses reveal increased RhoA or RhoC signaling in a substantial fraction of resistant cases [76]. Although direct targeting of RhoA is challenging, inhibition of downstream Rho‐associated kinases disrupts actin stress fibers and selectively sensitizes RhoA‐high resistant melanoma cells, providing a tractable entry point into this adaptive pathway.

FAK functions downstream of RhoA and upstream of AKT activation. Inhibition of RhoA signaling attenuates FAK phosphorylation and downstream AKT signaling, whereas enforced AKT activation bypasses FAK inhibition and sustains survival under MAPK pathway inhibition. In vivo, FAK inhibition suppresses AKT activation and enhances sensitivity to MAPK pathway inhibitors, while constitutive AKT signaling confers resistance even in the presence of FAK‐targeted therapy [75].

Nuclear FAK‐Mediated Suppression of Apoptosis

In addition to its cytoplasmic signaling functions, FAK promotes melanoma persister cell survival through a distinct nuclear mechanism. Inhibition of FAK induces apoptosis more effectively than blockade of downstream effectors such as PDK1, AKT, or SRC, underscoring its role as a central regulatory node in nongenetic resistance [77, 78].

Under therapeutic stress, FAK translocates to the nucleus where it directly binds wild‐type p53 and suppresses its transcriptional activity. This interaction prevents induction of pro‐apoptotic target genes including BAK, BAX, and PUMA, promoting an apoptosis‐resistant state that supports persister cell survival [77]. Genetic knockdown or pharmacologic inhibition of FAK restores p53 activity, reverses invasive phenotypic switching, and re‐sensitizes melanoma cells to apoptotic stimuli.

These findings establish focal adhesion kinase as a central integrator of nongenetic resistance in melanoma persister cells. Through cytoplasmic activation of the RhoA‐FAK‐AKT axis, FAK enables adaptation to microenvironmental cues and sustains survival under MAPK pathway inhibition. In parallel, nuclear FAK suppresses p53‐dependent apoptosis, providing an additional, growth factor‐independent layer of protection. By bridging extracellular matrix sensing, cytoskeletal remodeling, and intrinsic apoptosis resistance, FAK occupies a unique position among compensatory survival pathways and represents a compelling target for combination strategies aimed at eliminating melanoma persister cells.

Persister‐to‐Resistance Evolution

Although genetically resistant melanoma cells are phenotypically distinct from drug‐tolerant persisters, accumulating evidence indicates that resistance frequently emerges from persister populations under continued MAPK pathway inhibition. Spatiotemporal profiling of patient‐derived xenograft models supports a reproducible trajectory in which melanoma cells transition from a proliferative, glycolytic state to slow‐cycling persisters characterized by metabolic rewiring and invasiveness, followed by re‐emergence of proliferative resistant populations with distinct molecular features (Figure 4) [79].

Initial MAPK‐dependent, highly proliferative cells respond to MAPK inhibition. Slow‐cycling persister cells slowly emerge from these drug sensitive cells, characterized by metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic remodeling, and activation of survival pathways. Over time, resistant populations arise through either the stabilization of the nongenetic adaptive state or the genetically altered state, allowing proliferation under therapy.

Persister cells therefore represent a critical evolutionary reservoir rather than a transient byproduct of therapy. Targeting persister subpopulations, such as neural crest stem cell‐like states that depend on FAK‐AKT signaling, delays relapse and prolongs progression‐free survival in preclinical models. Notably, suppression of nongenetic persister programs ultimately forces tumors toward genetically fixed resistance mechanisms, including MAPK pathway reactivation [22]. These findings position persister cells as the gateway through which resistance evolves and underscore the importance of early intervention to limit adaptive trajectories under targeted therapy. Limitations and Future Directions

Despite these advances, several important limitations remain. Much of the current understanding of melanoma persister cell biology is derived from in vitro systems and murine or xenograft models, with relatively limited validation in clinical patient samples. This stems from a difficulty in finding and isolating these cells, as they are not clinically apparent after appropriate disease remission. As a result, the timing, prevalence, spatial organization, and reversibility of persister states in human tumors remain incompletely defined. A key challenge for clinical translation will be determining when persister states arise, how they can be reliably identified in patient samples, and which adaptive dependencies dominate within individual tumors. It also remains unclear how frequently distinct persister programs coexist within the same lesion, whether these programs are conserved across melanoma subtypes, and which biomarkers will be most useful for guiding patient‐directed therapeutic strategies. Addressing these questions will require longitudinal patient sampling, single‐cell and spatial profiling, and clinically relevant model systems that more faithfully capture residual disease under therapy.

Conclusion

Drug‐tolerant persister cells constitute a major barrier to durable therapeutic responses in melanoma. Rather than arising from fixed genetic alterations, these cells emerge through nongenetic adaptive programs that enable survival under MAPK pathway inhibition. As reviewed here, persister cells comprise a heterogeneous and dynamic spectrum of states unified by phenotypic plasticity, allowing transient tolerance of lethal therapies while retaining the capacity for resensitization or progression to stable resistance. This framework helps explain why suppression of MAPK signaling alone has failed to deliver sustained clinical benefit. Persister cell survival is supported by adaptive programs that decouple viability from proliferation, including integrated stress response‐mediated selective translation, metabolic rewiring toward mitochondrial or peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation, sulfur‐based redox buffering, and compensatory RhoA‐FAK‐AKT signaling. These pathways persist despite effective MAPK pathway inhibition and provide alternative routes for tumor maintenance.

Accordingly, effective therapeutic strategies will need to move beyond oncogene‐centric approaches and instead target the stress‐adaptive states that sustain persister cell survival. Rational combination therapies that exploit these transient vulnerabilities before resistance becomes genetically fixed may offer a path toward more durable disease control. A key challenge for clinical translation will be defining when persister states arise, how they can be reliably identified, and which dependencies dominate within individual tumors, particularly given that current understanding of persister biology is derived largely from BRAF‐mutant cutaneous melanoma treated with MAPK pathway inhibition. Whether similar adaptive programs operate across other melanoma subtypes, such as acral and mucosal, remains an important unanswered question.
