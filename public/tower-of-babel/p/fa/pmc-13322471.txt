Recognizing and managing linear morphea in a pediatric patient with skin of color
Authors: Alexia S. Collins, Tristi M. Edwards, Laura N. Uwakwe, Mary F. Lee-Wong, Jessica Perfetto, Sharon A. Glick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Linear morphea is the most prevalent subtype of morphea in pediatric patients and poses a significant clinical concern because of its potential to involve deeper tissues, including muscle, fascia, and bone. If not identified and treated promptly, this can result in long-term complications such as joint contractures, limb length discrepancies, and functional impairment. Diagnosing and managing morphea in children with skin of color presents unique challenges, as hallmark inflammatory signs like erythema may be less visible or absent altogether, leading to delays in diagnosis and difficulties in monitoring disease activity. In darker skin tones, active lesions may instead present as hyperpigmented or hypopigmented patches, which can mimic other common pediatric dermatoses. These diagnostic limitations underscore the need for heightened clinical suspicion and tailored diagnostic strategies in this population. While consensus-based treatment algorithms exist for pediatric morphea, these guidelines are largely derived from studies in lighter skin types and do not address diagnostic and monitoring challenges unique to patients with skin of color. Additional research is needed to develop inclusive guidelines that address these gaps and improve long-term disease control. Herein, we present the case of linear morphea in a 5-year-old African American child, highlighting the diagnostic, therapeutic, and monitoring challenges specific to pediatric patients with skin of color.

Introduction

Morphea is a rare inflammatory skin disorder with global incidence estimates of 3.4 to 27 per 100,000, occurring more frequently in women than men and typically presenting in the fourth decade of life.1 Pediatric cases most often arise between 7 and 11 years of age, while incidence in the United States is lower at approximately 2 to 3 per 100,000 and is reported more frequently in White individuals than in African Americans. Linear morphea with involvement of deeper structures, including muscle and bone, has been reported in patients with skin of color.1 Magnetic resonance imaging is the preferred imaging modality for evaluating deep tissue involvement, particularly in lesions crossing joints or demonstrating rapid progression.1

The exact cause of morphea is unclear, but autoimmune dysregulation, abnormal cytokine activity, and vascular dysfunction play key roles. Excess collagen buildup in the dermis is a hallmark feature, often triggered by trauma, environmental factors, or radiation. Skin biopsies reveal inflammatory infiltrates composed of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and eosinophils, accompanied by laboratory evidence of immune dysregulation, including elevated interleukin 4 levels and autoantibodies such as antinuclear antibody.1 Increased expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 facilitates T lymphocyte recruitment in early disease stages. CD4+ T helper cells drive disease progression by releasing interleukin 4, which stimulates transforming growth factor-beta, leading to excessive fibroblast activity and collagen production.

Inflammatory lesions make up the primary lesions in linear morphea.2 However, erythema in darker skin tones is harder to appreciate, which can lead to delays in diagnosis and difficulties in monitoring disease activity.2 In addition, keloidal morphea, a rare variant characterized by keloid-like plaques arising in the absence of trauma, is reported more frequently in individuals with darker skin tones, requiring careful consideration in diagnosis and disease management.2 For moderate-to-severe morphea, the preferred treatment approach involves methotrexate alongside systemic corticosteroids as an initial bridging strategy.2 If the condition does not respond or worsens despite therapy, alternative options include mycophenolate mofetil, cyclosporine, or hydroxychloroquine.2 Herein, we present the case of a 5-year-old African American patient with linear morphea, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges specific to pediatric patients with skin of color.

Case presentation

A 5-year-old African American girl (Fitzpatrick V) presented for evaluation of a hyperpigmented, mildly atrophic linear patch first noted on her left shoulder in 2023. The original presentation was consistent with lichen striatus. Over the following year, additional lesions developed, affecting the left arm, left flank, left abdomen, and left chest, prompting further evaluation. The patient was lost to follow-up for 1 year before returning for assessment. Punch biopsies obtained from the left posterior arm and right abdomen were consistent with linear morphea. While awaiting biopsy results, topical calcipotriene and tacrolimus were initiated. Histopathology subsequently demonstrated dermal sclerosis with thickened collagen bundles, reduced adnexal structures, and a perivascular lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, consistent with active morphea; no features specific to skin of color were identified. Because of continued progression, systemic therapy was initiated with oral methotrexate 7.5 mg weekly and weekly pulsed intravenous methylprednisolone. She received methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg per dose (maximum 1 g) once weekly, initiated on November 1, 2024, with a planned 12-week course and taper based on clinical response. She later transitioned to methotrexate 18.8 mg administered subcutaneously once weekly for improved efficacy.

At follow-up, previously involved areas softened without further extension; however, focal enlargement of hyperpigmented areas on the left trunk was noted. These areas lacked evidence of activity such as induration or erythema, but there was concern for active morphea versus postinflammatory changes. Laboratory evaluation, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, creatine kinase, aldolase, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and platelet count, was within normal limits. Given her history of rapid progression and linear involvement crossing joint lines, magnetic resonance imaging was obtained to assess for deeper tissue involvement, which was not identified.

Discussion

Diagnosing morphea in skin of color presents unique challenges, as lesions may lack the classic erythematous or violaceous borders observed in lighter skin tones.3 Instead, they often appear hyperpigmented, which can mimic several other dermatologic conditions, including lichen striatus, postinflammatory hyperpigmentation, atopic dermatitis, and even early vitiligo.3 This case highlights the diagnosis and management challenges of linear morphea in skin-of-color patients. Given the overlapping presentation, a delay in diagnosing morphea is common in children with darker skin tones, potentially leading to disease progression before appropriate intervention is initiated.4 While erythema is a key indicator of active morphea in lighter skin, in darker skin tones, hyperpigmentation may persist long after active inflammation has resolved, making it difficult to distinguish ongoing disease from residual postinflammatory changes.5

Despite the established efficacy of methotrexate and corticosteroids in morphea, treatment response can also be more difficult to assess in skin of color.5 In patients with skin of color, pigmentation may remain unchanged despite improvement in underlying sclerosis, complicating assessment of treatment response.5 Therefore, clinicians must rely on improvement in skin texture, pliability, and patient-reported symptoms rather than changes in pigmentation alone when evaluating treatment efficacy.5

In addition, when morphea extends across joint lines, there is an increased risk of functional impairment due to progressive fibrosis and deep tissue involvement.4 In rare cases, keloidal morphea may occur, a variant that clinically resembles keloids or hypertrophic scars and is most often reported in Black women but can also occur in children with skin of color.6 Keloids are fibroproliferative dermal growths arising after cutaneous injury in genetically predisposed individuals, whereas keloidal morphea presents as keloid-like plaques without prior trauma and reflects underlying dermal sclerosis rather than disorganized collagen deposition.7,8 Keloidal morphea may be mistaken for more common scar-related conditions, further delaying diagnosis and appropriate treatment.6

While pediatric patients with morphea generally respond well to therapy, systemic treatments appear to offer more durable disease control with fewer recurrences.8 This supports prior findings that early, aggressive intervention can lead to rapid improvement in disease activity.8However, the observed higher recurrence rate in children underscores a critical need for long-term surveillance, even in patients who initially respond well to treatment.8 A recent case report documented the successful use of baricitinib, a Janus kinase inhibitor, in a child with linear morphea.9 The patient received oral baricitinib at a dose of 2 mg daily for 1 year, resulting in significant softening of lesions, no further disease progression, and no reported adverse events.9This report underscores the potential utility of targeted immunomodulators in refractory cases of morphea, particularly when conventional treatments such as methotrexate and corticosteroids are insufficient.9 Increased awareness and earlier recognition of morphea in skin of color could lead to more timely interventions and improved patient outcomes. In addition, serial clinical photography is valuable for monitoring subtle disease progression in patients with skin of color.

Although complete disease control had not yet been achieved because of expanded focal hyperpigmentation, previously active areas improved 4 weeks after initiation of systemic therapy.10 Improvement in sclerosis often requires 8 to 12 weeks, and prolonged treatment reduces relapse risk.10 In linear morphea, systemic therapy is typically continued for at least 12 months after inflammatory activity subsides.10

Conclusion

This case underscores the complexity of managing linear morphea in pediatric patients with skin of color, highlighting the need for early recognition, timely intervention, and individualized treatment approaches. The use of systemic immunosuppressants necessitates careful monitoring, and further research is essential to refine treatment protocols and optimize imaging modalities for long-term disease control in pediatric morphea. Early aggressive treatment is particularly important when lesions cross joint lines, given the risk of long-term functional impairment.
