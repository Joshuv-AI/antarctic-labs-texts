Effect of chitooligosaccharides on the growth and immune capability of Crassostrea gigas larvae
Authors: Wentong Hao, Bo Wang, Junyan Zhao, Ao Xu, Ning Kong, Lingling Wang, Linsheng Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlight•Chitooligosaccharides (COS) promoted the growth of Crassostrea gigas larvae.•COS enhanced the immune capability of C. gigas and increased its survival rate.•COS is expected to be a sustainable alternative to antibiotics in the oyster farming industry.Keywords: Crassostrea gigas, Oyster larvae, Chitooligosaccharides, Growth, Innate immunity

Introduction

Bivalves, including oysters, mussels and scallops, serve as economically and ecologically critical species in aquaculture. Most bivalves go through a planktonic larval stage during their development, which is conducive to the proliferation of their populations. However, the high larval mortality rate, largely attributed to bacterial infection, has seriously constrained the sustainable development of shellfish farming industry [1,2]. Therefore, developing antibiotic-free strategies to bolster larval immunity is imperative for ecological health and the green, high-quality development of the oyster industry. Among them, chitooligosaccharides (COS), as a water-soluble oligosaccharide, have received widespread attention in recent years for their effects on growth and development, immunity enhancement, and microbiota regulation [3,4].

Accumulating evidence highlights COS as potent growth enhancers in plants and animals, primarily through promoting nutrient accumulation and activating anabolic pathways [5,6]. For example, the fresh mass, dry mass, length of both shoots and roots and the contents of soluble sugar, soluble protein and chlorophyll of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) in the COS-treated group were significantly increased compared to the control group [7,8]. Addition of COS to the soaking water enhanced barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) seed germination and promoted radicle and shoot growth [9]. Adding COS is also proved to have a growth-promoting effect on aquatic animals. For instance, COS supplementation significantly enhanced growth performance in tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), demonstrating marked improvements in final body weight, weight gain rate, specific growth rate (SGR), feed efficiency rate and protein efficiency ratio compared to control diet [10]. Treatment of COS also improved the SGR of loach (Paramisgurnus dabryanus) [11], juvenile largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) [12] and sea cucumber (Apostichopus japonicas) [13].

In addition to improving growth performance, COS also exhibit immune-enhancing activity. COS were observed to enhance the phagocytic activity of macrophages, induce the production of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin 6 (IL-6), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), nitric oxide (NO) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in mice [14]. Dietary COS increased the SOD activity, the IgG levels and decreased the malondialdehyde (MDA) content in the duodenum of broilers [15]. Treatment of COS triggered the humoral immunity in grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) by driving the potent expansion of IgMlo plasmablasts and the secretion of natural IgM [16].

As an eco-friendly prebiotic, COS was proved to regulate the intestinal microbiota by increasing probiotic abundance and inhibiting pathogens, which in turn promote growth and enhance immunity. For example, COS supplementation reduced the Escherichia coli population while increased the count of Lactobacillus in the intestine of pigs [17,18]. Dietary COS could change the composition of the gut microbiota in turtles (Pelodiscus sinensis) through enriching specific bacteria, such as Cellulosilyticum, Helicobacter and Solibacillus [19]. COS treatment also improved the gut health in tilapia (O. niloticus), potentially altering the gut microbial community by increasing beneficial bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus spp.) and decreasing harmful bacteria (e.g., Mycoplasma sp.), which improves its resistance to Aeromonas hydrophila [20].

The Pacific oyster, Crassostrea gigas, is one of the most economically important aquaculture shellfish in the world. Recently, disease outbreaks of oyster larvae which lack the fully developed the immune system and are sensitive to the pathogens have become one of the major bottlenecks to the sustainable development of the oyster farming industry [21]. Although COS have been shown to promote growth, enhance immunity and regulate the composition of microbiota in vertebrates, whether COS confer similar benefits to bivalve larvae remains unexplored. In the present study, the effect of COS on growth and innate immunity in C. gigas larvae were assessed by (1) testing larval developmental characters (shell height, shell length and mRNA expression of growth-related genes) under COS immersion, (2) measuring the antimicrobial activity and the survival rate against V. splendidus challenge after COS treatment, and (3) characterizing the larvae associated microbial communities after COS treatment. These findings aim to provide a sustainable strategy for bivalve hatchery management through the development of non-antibiotic larval immunopotentiator.

Materials and methods

Animals and bacteria

The umbo larvae (6 d post fertilization, dpf) were cultured at Zhangzidao Group Co., Ltd (Dalian, China). V. splendidus were cultured in 2216E medium at 16°C for 24 h, and harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min [22]. After being washed with PBS, the precipitate was resuspended in filtered seawater. All experiments were approved by the Academic and Ethics Committee of Dalian Ocean University.

COS treatment, V. splendidus challenge and sample collection

The COS was provided by Professor Kecheng Li, Institute of Oceanology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Qingdao, China) with 2–6 degree of polymerization and 12 % degree of acetylation. The experimental design was shown in Fig. 1, the umbo larvae were immersed in seawater containing 0.1, 1, 10 mg/L COS at a density of 8–10 larvae/mL for 2 d At the end of the immersion, the larvae were sampled and then temporarily reared for one week in filtered seawater at a temperature of 22–24 °C and salinity of 31. Almost 30 larvae were sampled from each group every 3 d and observed under microscopy to measure their shell height and shell length. The challenge experiment was carried out in 5 L seawater with the final concentration of V. splendidus of 2 × 107 CFU/L. All treatments and assays were performed with three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

The experimental design.

Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from larvae using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and quantified with Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 μg of total RNA with the Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Kit (AG11706, Accurate Biotechnology, Hunan, China). The qPCR amplification was performed with SYBR Green qPCR Master MixⅡ kit using gene-specific primers (Table 1). The relative mRNA expressions of target genes (CgRunx, CgGATA, Cglysozyme, CgC3, and CgBigdefensin) were normalized to CgEF and calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Assays of antioxidant enzyme activities and antimicrobial activities of larval protein extracts

The antioxidant enzyme activities and antimicrobial activity was measured based on a previous report [23]. The larvae from each group were homogenized in 200 μL PBS. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and the protein concentration was quantified by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). The activity of SOD, CAT and T-AOC were detected following the manufacturers’ instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Antimicrobial activity was determined by co-incubating 50 μL larval protein extracts (0.8 mg/mL in PBS) with 50 μL V. splendidus, Pseudoalteromonas aliena and S. aureus (OD600=0.3 in 2216E medium), respectively, in triplicate independent experiments. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 h. Bacterial growth kinetics were monitored every 30 min. At the time point of half the time to reach the plateau period, the OD600 value of the sample was used for statistical analysis.

Survival rate

After COS treatment, larvae were temporarily reared in filtered seawater and the V. splendidus challenge experiment were conducted 7 d later. Equal number of larvae were placed in 5 L of seawater containing V. splendidus at 2 × 107 CFU/L for 48 h to test the pathogen resistance of larvae. The survival rate was measured by neutral red method as reported [24]. A total of 100 mL larvae were transferred into a beaker and stained with 70 µL of 0.33 % neutral red solution for 3 h. After fixed by 4 % paraformaldehyde, the dye uptake of larvae was microscopically examined to represent its survival rate.

The determination of microbial burden in haemolymph by absolute quantitative PCR

The DNA of larvae was extracted to determine its microbial burden at 24 h post V. splendidus stimulation according to a previous study [25]. The 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) gene of total bacteria and Vibrio were amplified by Ex Taq DNA polymerase (TaKaRa, Japan) with primers of 341F/534R and V. spF/V. spR, respectively [26]. Products were cloned into pMD-19T and transformed into E. coli Trans5α to generate the standard plasmid. The standard curve was generated using quantitative real-time PCR with gradient-diluted plasmid templates and primers 341F/534R and V. spF/V. spR, respectively. Results were expressed as the copies of total bacteria/V. spp. per ngDNA.

DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

At the end of the immersion, larvae were collected and rinsed three times with sterile seawater. The genomic DNA of larvae samples was extracted using the E.Z.N. A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 515F/806R and sequenced on the Illumina PE250 platform (Novogene, China). Data were analyzed using QIIME 2 as reported [27], and taxonomic relative abundances at the phylum and genus levels were visualized as a stacked bar plot.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± SD. The differences among groups were analyzed using t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. Statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS 26.0. Figures were drawn by GraphPad Prism 8.

Results

Promotion of larval growth by COS treatment

The shell height and shell length of larvae showed significant difference at 9 d post incubation of different concentration of COS (Fig. 2). The shell height (1.17- and 1.14-fold) and shell length (1.19- and 1.15-fold) of larvae treated with 1 and 10 mg/L COS were significantly increased compared to the control group, respectively, with no significant difference observed between the two concentrations. Meanwhile, oyster larvae treated with 10 mg/L COS exhibited the highest absolute growth rate (AGR) among all tested concentrations between 6 d to 9 d The mean AGR values for shell height (17.61 μm/d) and shell length (15.38 μm/d) in 10 mg/L COS group were 1.45-fold and 1.71-fold higher, respectively, compared to the control group. The mRNA expression level of CgRunx significantly increased after 0.1, 1 and 10 mg/L COS treatments (p < 0.05), which was 2.35-, 2.68- and 1.77-fold higher than that of the control group, respectively. The mRNA expression level of CgGATA significantly increased (p < 0.05) after the 1 mg/L COS treatment (Fig. 3), while no significant difference was observed in 10 mg/L COS-treated group.

Shell height and shell length of larvae in different groups.

The mRNA expression levels of CgRunx and CgGATA in different groups.

Improvement of larval survival rate by COS treatment

Equal number of larvae from each group were placed in 5 L of seawater and stimulated by V. splendidus (2 × 107 CFU/L). At 48 h after V. splendidus stimulation, larval survival rates differed significantly among groups: 78 % in the blank group (untreated), 21.2 % in the control group, and 36.1 %, 37.3 % and 64.2 % in 0.1, 1 and 10 mg/L COS-treated groups, respectively (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, the 16S rRNA gene copies in larvae from the 0.1 and 10 mg/L COS-treated groups were 5.67 × 106 and 4.23 × 106 copies/ngDNA, respectively, which were 0.09- and 0.07-fold of that in control group (6.01 × 106 copies/ngDNA). The rRNA gene copies of Vibrio in the larvae exhibited a concentration-dependent decrease with COS treatment, showing reductions to 0.88-, 0.72- and 0.24-fold of the control group for 0.1, 1, and 10 mg/L COS groups, respectively (Fig. 4B-C, p < 0.05).

The survival rate and microbial burden of larvae after Vibrio splendidus challenge. A: Survival rate at 48 h. B-C: Total bacterial and Vibrio copy numbers at 24 h.

Enhancement of larval immunity by COS treatment

The total protein was extracted from each group to detect the immune capability of larvae. The CAT activity of larvae was significantly increased after 0.1, 1 and 10 mg/L COS treatments (Fig. 5, p < 0.05), which was 2.99-, 3.08- and 3.59-fold higher than that of the control group, respectively. The SOD activity of larvae was significantly increased (p < 0.05) in 10 mg/L COS-treated group (22.24 U/mg prot), which was 1.25-fold higher than that of the control group (17.73 U/mg prot), whereas no significant changes were observed at lower concentrations. The T-AOC of larval proteins was 1.13-, 1.43- and 1.72-fold higher than that of the control group in 0.1, 1 and 10 mg/L COS-treated groups, respectively. In addition, the mRNA expression levels of Cglysozyme, CgC3, and CgBigdefensin were increased after COS treatments (Fig S1), also indicating the activation of larval innate immunity.

The antioxidant activity of larvae before the stimulation of Vibrio splendidus. A: CAT activity. B: SOD activity. C: T-AOC.

The antimicrobial activity of larval proteins extracted from COS-treated oysters showed pathogen-specific responses (Fig. 6). Against V. splendidus, a dose-dependent suppression pattern emerged, with the OD600 values at 5 h corresponding to 92 % (0.1 mg/L), 96 % (1 mg/L) and 84 % (10 mg/L) of control group. The larval protein extracted from 10 mg/L COS-treated group achieved 16 % greater growth inhibition than the control group. Notably, the larval protein of 10 mg/L COS-treated group displayed unique efficacy against S. aureus, being the only group to significantly inhibit its growth (5 h OD600 = 0.93-fold of control, p < 0.05).

The antimicrobial activity of larvae before the stimulation of Vibrio splendidus. A-C: Growth curves of Vibrio splendidus, Pseudoalteromonas aliena and Staphylococcus aureus, respectively. d-F: OD600 at 5 h of co-incubation in different groups.

Changes in the associated microbial composition of larvae induced by COS treatment

The microbial composition of larvae from the control group and the most effective 10 mg/L COS-treated group was compared in Fig. 7. Proteobacteria (41.82 %–53.25 %), Tenericutes (35.30 %–50.51 %) and Bacteroidetes (3.25 %–5.08 %) were the dominant phyla in the oyster umbo larvae. The relative abundance of Proteobacteria in the COS group (41.82 %) was lower than that in the control group (53.25 %). In contrast, the relative abundance of Tenericutes in the COS-treated group was significantly higher than that in the control group (p < 0.05). At the genus level, the larvae associated microbiota was dominated by Mycoplasma, Acinetobacter and Algibacter. The relative abundance of Mycoplasma was 50.51 % in the COS-treated group, which was significantly higher than that in the control group of 35.30 %. The relative abundance of Vibrio and Acinetobacter in the COS-treated group were lower than those in the control group.

Composition of the microbiota among control group and COS group. A-B: relative abundances of the dominant phyla and genera in each group. C-G: relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Tenericutes, Mycoplasma, Vibrio and Acinetobacter in each group.

Discussion

Recently, disease outbreaks caused by bacterial pathogens in the hatching and larvae stages have constrained the bivalve aquaculture industry [28,29]. Current strategies, such as water treatment and antibiotics, face limitations due to over-sterilization and antibiotics resistance [[30], [31], [32]]. The development of non-antibiotic immunopotentiator, such as COS, is becoming an urgent issue to be addressed. In the present study, the growth-promoting and immune-enhancing effects of the COS on the umbo larvae of C. gigas were studied, and the associated microbiota of larvae were analyzed.

COS, recognized as a beneficial prebiotic additive, has been extensively studied to promote the growth performance in various aquatic species, underpinning its widespread application in aquaculture. In the present study, 10 mg/L COS immersion significantly accelerated larval growth, with shell height and shell length increasing by 14.2 % and 14.8 % at 9 d post-immersion, respectively, compared to the control group (p < 0.05). This growth-promoting effect aligns with previous studies documenting COS-enhanced developmental rates in pompano (Trachinotus ovatus) [33], cobia (Rachycentron canadum) [34] and tilapia (O. niloticus) [35]. Previous studies attribute the growth-promoting activity of COS to its ability to ferment in the gut, enhancing digestive enzyme activity and the abundance of gut probiotics [35,36]. In this study, the mRNA expression of several genes associated with growth and development were increased in COS-treated larvae. As a hematopoiesis transcription factors, CgRunx was involved in immune response and larvae hematopoiesis and its mRNA expression in 10 mg/L COS-treated oyster was 1.77-fold higher than that of the control group [37]. At the same time, CgGATA, a downstream gene of the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) pathway linked to shell formation, showed increased mRNA expression in COS-treated larvae, which may directly explain their increased shell height and shell length [38].

Beyond growth promotion, COS has been proved to increase the pathogenic resistance of aquatic animals. For instance, dietary COS can improve the resistance to A. hydrophila of loach (P. dabryanus) [39], the resistance to Aeromonas veronii of Koi (Cyprinus carpio koi) and increased their survival rates [40]. In this study, 10 mg/L COS treatment significantly increased the survival rate of oyster larvae challenged by V. splendidus, with the survival rate 3.03-fold higher than that of the control group. This improvement may be attributed to enhanced innate immunity induced by COS. On one hand, COS treatment has been shown to significantly enhance the antibody titer, the activities of CAT and SOD, and total serum protein in pearl gentian grouper (♀Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × ♂Epinephelus lanceolatus), thereby improving its resistance to Vibrio harveyi [41]. As key antioxidant enzymes, SOD and CAT are thought to contribute to innate immunity by mitigating oxidative stress. In the present study, treatment of 10 mg/L COS also significantly enhanced the antioxidant defense system in oyster larvae, as demonstrated by marked increases in SOD activity (25.4 %), CAT activity (259.1 %) and T-AOC (71.6 %) compared to the control group. On the other hand, COS treatment also improved antimicrobial activity in the larvae. Previous study reported that C. gigas larvae stimulated with inactivated V. splendidus exhibited higher survival rate against V. splendidus, and the proteins extracted from these larvae had significant agglutinating activity and inhibitory effect on the growth of V. splendidus [23]. While in this study, the proteins extracted from larvae in 10 mg/L COS-treated group showed inhibitory effect on the growth of V. splendidus, P. aliena and S. aureus, proving that COS treatment may enable oyster larvae to have a broader inhibitory effect on a variety of pathogenic bacteria and enhance its innate immunity.

Interestingly, the results in this study indicate a potential dose-threshold effect of COS. The promotion of larval growth peaked at 1 mg/L, while the enhancement of antioxidant activity and survival rate showed a clear positive correlation with COS concentration, with the most robust effects observed at 10 mg/L. These findings suggest that the metabolic pathways governing development may be more sensitive to COS than those orchestrating immune responses. Consequently, 10 mg/L was identified as the optimal concentration to concurrently improve both growth and disease resistance in C. gigas larvae.

According to previous studies, the antioxidant and antimicrobial activities of COS may be through its prebiotic regulation of microbial communities. Notably, in this study, 16S rRNA sequencing analysis revealed a significant reduction in Proteobacteria abundance within the COS-treated group, particularly demonstrating marked decreases in Acinetobacter and Vibrio. While the Acinetobacter and Vibrio in Proteobacteria were potential pathogens to fish and shellfish [27,42]. This microbial shift coincided with improved innate immune parameters, implying that the selective suppression of opportunistic pathogens may synergistically potentiate the innate immunity of larvae. Interestingly, the abundance of Mycoplasma, which is usually recognized as a sign of infection, significantly increased in the COS-treated group [43]. However, Mycoplasma emerged as the dominant gut microbiota in aquatic animals such as Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) and small abalone (Haliotis diversicolor) with no harmful or pathogenic effects [44,45]. And it has been suggested that the increase of Mycoplasma may activate the immune defense responses of Yesso scallop (Patinopecten yessoensis) [46]. Therefore, further studies on the specific effects of Mycoplasma on the C. gigas larvae are warranted.

Conclusion

In the present study, the growth-promoting and immune-enhancing effects of COS on the C. gigas larvae were investigated. These results showed that COS promoted the growth of umbo larvae and improved their immune capability. Meanwhile, these activities may be related to the remodeling of microbiota in larvae treated by COS. These findings provide a sustainable strategy for larvae hatchery management through the development of COS-based larval immunopotentiator.
