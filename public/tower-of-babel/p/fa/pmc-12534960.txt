Morphological comparison between artificial intelligence-driven and manual CAD design in single tooth restoration: a preliminary study
Authors: Bing-ying Xie, Xiao He, Li Hu, Shan-lin Guo, Jun-lan Chen, Jing Zhang, Xiao-qing Shen, Yuan-ming Geng, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into CAD/CAM workflows has revolutionized dental prosthetics manufacturing, yet its morphological trueness compared to manual design remains underexplored.Materials and methodsThis study evaluated 30 single-tooth restoration cases from 30 patients. For each case, the original clinically-approved designs were used as reference. AI designs (3Shape Automate) were compared to manual designs created by a technician (3Shape Dental System™). Morphological trueness was evaluated through 3D deviation analysis. Global surface deviations (RMSE) were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and maximum discrepancies were compared with a paired Student’s t-test, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsWhile AI demonstrated batch-processing efficiency, 6.7% of cases (2/30) with suboptimal preparation geometries required manual intervention. No significant difference was found in global surface deviation between AI (median = 79.8 μm) and manual designs (median = 68.6 μm; p = 0.1056). However, AI designs produced significantly greater maximum discrepancies (mean = 225.0 μm) compared to manual designs (mean = 184.4 μm; p = 0.0243).ConclusionThese findings validate AI’s viability for routine restoration design but emphasize the necessity of case selection protocols and algorithm improvements for dynamic occlusion modeling to ensure comprehensive clinical adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07004-z.

Introduction

The integration of Computer-Aided Design and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies has transformed contemporary protocols for various dental restorations. Modern digital workflows demonstrate superior efficiency and reproducibility compared to conventional techniques, minimizing human error while maintaining clinically acceptable marginal adaptation thresholds for diverse fixed dental prostheses (FDP), including inlays, onlays, and complete crowns [1].

However, traditional CAD design methodologies remain inherently dependent on technician proficiency, introducing variability through subjective interpretation of anatomical features—whether reconstructing cusp morphology for crowns or optimizing proximal contacts for inlays [2]. Although skilled technicians can produce clinically satisfactory outcomes, the manual design process requires substantial time investment and demonstrates significant inter-operator variability, particularly in complex cases such as deep margin subgingival preparations or minimally invasive inlay designs [3].

The recent incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) into dental CAD systems introduces paradigm-shifting capabilities in automated restoration design. These systems utilize machine learning algorithms to analyze occlusal relationships and adjacent tooth morphology, generating restorations within minutes while theoretically preserving anatomical accuracy [4]. While preliminary investigations suggest AI can reduce design time by 40–75%, its ability to replicate critical morphological details remains a concern, with recent evidence indicating lower AI accuracy on complex occlusal and proximal surfaces compared to those designed by experienced technicians [5]. Despite increasing clinical adoption of AI-driven systems, a significant knowledge gap persists in direct comparative analyses between AI-generated and technician-designed restorations [6]. Existing literature lacks comprehensive evaluation of morphological parameters that critically influence clinical performance across different restoration types [7].

This study evaluates the morphological trueness of AI-driven designs for single-tooth restorations by comparing them against clinically-approved, manually created CAD designs. The null hypothesis is that there is no significant difference in morphological trueness between the two methods. The findings aim to provide evidence to guide clinicians on the appropriate use of AI in dental design and to identify specific limitations of the current technology, thereby highlighting key directions for future algorithmic development.

Materials and methods

Data collecting

This comparative study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Protocol No: 2023-KY-164-01), with written informed consent obtained from all participants. Patients requiring single tooth restorations were consecutively enrolled between February 1 and February 28, 2024, meeting the following criteria: presence of intact adjacent teeth, absence of active periodontal disease or carious lesions in adjacent dentition.

Standardized tooth preparations were performed by a single prosthodontist with 10 years’ clinical experience (Wei Li). Digital impressions were acquired using a CEREC Omnicam intraoral scanner (Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA) following manufacturer protocols, capturing the prepared tooth surface, adjacent dentition, and opposing arch in maximum intercuspation. CAD designs were created using 3Shape Dental System (v2021.1, 3Shape A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark) by an experienced technician with 7 years’ clinical experience (Bing-ying Xie), incorporating manual margin delineation and occlusal adjustments based on software-guided dynamic analysis.

The selection of cases for this study was governed by stringent success criteria, evaluated at both the delivery appointment and a subsequent 6-month follow-up. At the time of delivery, a restoration was required to demonstrate continuous marginal adaptation, with no palpable catch or step when traced with a dental explorer; appropriate proximal contacts, verified to allow dental floss to pass with tactile resistance; and harmonious occlusion, confirmed with articulating paper (< 8 μm) to have well-distributed contacts requiring no adjustment. These clinical assessments were supplemented by the patient’s report of immediate functional comfort. To be included in the final sample, these restorations also had to maintain complete structural integrity over the 6-month follow-up period, showing no evidence of fracture, debonding, or secondary caries. A total of 30 cases fulfilled all these criteria, and their original digital design files were archived for the subsequent morphological analysis.

Studying groups

A paired study design was employed, using the same 30 cases for each of the three experimental groups.

Reference group

The original clinically-approved designs were used as reference.

Manual group

Manual designs created by the same reference technician (Bing-ying Xie) using 3Shape Dental System. The technician imported an initial restoration morphology from the system’s built-in database, then performed comprehensive manual adjustments to the crown’s shape, contacts, and occlusion, based on her clinical experience. These designs employed identical margin tracing and contour adjustment protocols as the original workflow but were executed following completion of the 6-month clinical follow-up period.

AI group

The designs for the AI group were generated using 3Shape Automate (automate.3shape.com), a platform recognized for employing artificial intelligence in automated dental restoration design [8]. All designs were created using the system’s default clinical parameters without any manual intervention. For the purposes of this study, “manual design” refers to a conventional, operator-led workflow where an experienced technician utilizes the software’s standard semi-automated proposals (e.g., for margin line and insertion path) and digital sculpting tools to achieve the final design. “AI-driven design” utilized a fully autonomous generative module (3Shape Automate) that produces a complete proposal in a single step, without intermediate operator input.

Morphological comparison

Morphological evaluation was conducted using Geomagic Control X (2018.1.1; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC), to quantify the trueness of the AI- and manual- designs against the original, clinically approved design, which served as the reference standard. The AI design file and the Manual Design file were each independently superimposed onto the Reference Design file. To ensure a precise and objective alignment, the superimposition was based on the identical, unchanged surfaces of the adjacent teeth present in all three scan files. This provided a stable and consistent reference area, isolating the restoration itself for comparison. Once aligned, the software performed a 3D surface comparison, calculating the point-to-point distances between the surface of the reference restoration and the surfaces of the AI and manual designs. The overall trueness was quantified by calculating root mean square error (RMSE). Statistical analysis

Quantitative analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v8.3.0, GraphPad Software). A paired analytical approach was employed, with all comparisons structured by the individual patient case. To guide the selection of the appropriate statistical test, the normality of data distribution was first evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). Based on these results, normally distributed data were analyzed using paired Student’s t-test, while datasets that did not follow a normal distribution were compared using the two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All morphological trueness metrics are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR).

Results

Among the 30 enrolled cases, two specimens (6.7%) failed AI system validation due to preprocessing errors. One case (tooth #25) triggered a system notification stating “Undetectable margin line: Use Dental System for manual margin identification,” while another (tooth #27) generated an alert indicating “Unable to compute the die interface. Morphological trueness assessment

Clinically approved designs served as reference standards for comparing AI-driven and manual CAD workflows (Fig. 2). Normality assessment of paired RMSE differences via Shapiro-Wilk test revealed non-parametric distributions (p < 0.001), requiring use of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for analysis (Fig. 3A). Comparative evaluation demonstrated no statistically significant difference in global surface deviations between AI-generated restorations (median RMSE = 79.8 μm, IQR = 61.6–91.0 μm) and manually designed 3Shape counterparts (median RMSE = 68.6 μm, IQR = 44.0–91.9 μm, p = 0.1056) (Fig. 3B, Table 1).

Maximum discrepancy measurements

Normality assessment of paired maximum discrepancy differences via Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed parametric distribution (p = 0.8657), justifying implementation of paired t-test analysis (Fig. 3C). Comparative evaluation revealed statistically significant elevation in maximum discrepancy for AI-designed restorations (median = 220.1 μm, IQR = 169.1–250.2 μm) compared to manual designs (median = 184.8 μm, IQR = 116.2–236.5 μm; p = 0.0243) (Fig. 3D, Table 1).

Discussion

This comparative analysis reveals three key performance characteristics of AI-driven single-tooth restoration design: (1) a global trueness, measured by Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), that was statistically equivalent to manual workflows (median, 79.8 vs. 68.6 μm; p = 0.1056 > 0.05); (2) significantly elevated peak deviations in AI outputs (median, 220.1 vs. 184.8 μm; p = 0.0243 < 0.05); and (3) a 6.7% technical failure rate requiring manual intervention for margin/die interface detection. The data collectively demonstrate that while AI systems achieve non-significant overall morphological deviation, they exhibit specific vulnerabilities in handling geometrically complex preparations and optimizing localized anatomical features.

Notably, the AI system’s batch processing capability demonstrated significant efficiency gains, successfully automating 93.3% of cases (28/30) with under one minute of active operator time per restoration for upload and system validation. This contrasts with the 30–45 min median chairside time per restoration documented for manual CAD workflows in comparable studies [9]. The parallel processing architecture eliminated per-unit time comparisons, as all 30 cases completed simultaneously during unattended operation. This efficiency profile positions AI batch processing as particularly advantageous for high-volume clinics, provided technicians remain prepared to address suboptimal geometric edge cases.

The preprocessing failure manifested specifically in two distinct error modes: margin line detection failure (tooth #25) and die interface computation error (tooth #27). The successful manual redesign of these FDP indicates that the initial failures stemmed not from insurmountable clinical challenges, but from specific algorithmic limitations—likely a combination of insufficient training data on complex geometries and margin-detection algorithms unable to resolve the ambiguity of deep subgingival preparations [10]. The successful manual resolution of these two cases underscores the AI system’s limitations with suboptimal geometries—such as deep subgingival margins or acute curvature transitions—and highlights the need for stricter case selection criteria for AI-driven workflows [11]. Given that at the current stage of development, the success of AI design is still highly dependent on a high-quality tooth preparation, its clinical viability is therefore most evident in routine cases. This reality also provides technicians with a practical reference standard for case selection: routine, clear preparations are suitable for AI processing, whereas complex cases may still require manual intervention.

A key aspect of our validation framework was the use of the same expert technician for both the clinically-approved Reference designs and the new Manual Group designs. This approach intentionally eliminated inter-operator variability, ensuring the comparison focused on the design process (AI vs. manual) rather than differing clinical philosophies. To mitigate recall bias, a temporal separation of at least six months was implemented as an effective blinding control. This interval established a realistic benchmark for manual consistency, revealing a degree of inherent intra-operator variability (median RMSE = 79.1 μm) during design recreation [12]. Notably, the AI system’s deviation from the original references (median RMSE = 79.8 μm) was not significantly different from this manual variability (p = 0.1056). This morphological equivalence suggests that for cases passing automated preprocessing, AI-generated designs achieve a level of trueness comparable to conventional, expert-led workflows.

Maximum discrepancy analysis revealed significantly greater peak deviations in AI-generated designs (mean = 225.0 μm) compared to manual ones (mean = 184.4 μm). This discrepancy pattern suggests AI systems may prioritize strict anatomical replication from reference databases, potentially overemphasizing cusp sharpness and proximal contact tightness relative to technicians’ clinically tempered adaptations. Manual designs demonstrated smoother occlusal transitions likely reflecting operator compensation for patient age-related attrition patterns and occlusal functional demands [13]. The long-term biomechanical impacts of these differences warrant investigation through fatigue testing and multi-year outcome studies [14].

However, this study’s limitations include inherent constraints from its single-center design and moderate sample size, which may affect generalizability across diverse populations. Similarly, benchmarking the AI against a single expert technician, while methodologically necessary to control for variability, restricts the findings’ generalizability to the operator’s specific design philosophy. The 6-month follow-up period, while sufficient for initial morphological evaluation, precludes assessment of long-term biomechanical behavior under functional loading [15]. Exclusion of posterior crossbite and severe attrition cases narrows clinical applicability to ideal occlusal scenarios. Restriction to lithium disilicate ceramics limits extrapolation to other restorative materials with distinct mechanical properties [16]. Furthermore, the exclusive focus on static morphological parameters omits critical functional assessments like cyclic load resistance and thermal stress performance. These methodological boundaries collectively underscore the need for expanded multicenter trials incorporating complex occlusal cases, extended observation periods, and diversified material selections. Future investigations should integrate dynamic biomechanical testing protocols to bridge the gap between digital design accuracy and clinical performance requirements.

Conclusion

AI-powered restoration design achieves comparable morphological trueness to manual workflows. However, significantly greater peak deviations and geometric limitations with suboptimal preparations necessitate case selection and increased chairside adjustment time. While our findings establish AI’s promising potential for routine crown design, further optimization requires integration of dynamic occlusal parameters and expanded clinical validation across diverse preparation geometries to enhance practical utility and clinical effectiveness across comprehensive prosthodontic applications.
