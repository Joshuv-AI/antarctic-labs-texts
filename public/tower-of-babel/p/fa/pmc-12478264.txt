Disseminated brain Nocardia abscess with bronchial and esophageal fistulas
Authors: Yunzhao Mo, Ruixin Yang, Gaici Xue, Dandan Fan, Hongmin Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Disseminated nocardiosis is a rare but life-threatening infection, with limited documented cases. Moreover, there have been no reports of Nocardia mediastinal abscess leading to bronchial or esophageal fistulae. We present a 62-year-old immunocompetent male with cerebellar and mediastinal abscesses caused by Nocardia beijingensis, complicated by both bronchial-mediastinal and esophageal fistulas. He received treatment including surgical intervention, endoscopic procedures, and antibiotic therapy. This case highlights the critical role of multidisciplinary collaboration in diagnosing and managing disseminated nocardiosis, particularly when rare complications arise. Early molecular diagnostics (next-generation sequencing, NGS) and individualized treatment adjustments were pivotal.

Case

A 62-year-old male presented with a 6-day history of dizziness and vomiting. Examination revealed disorientation to time and place. The patient tested positive for ataxia, including dysdiadochokinesia, a positive finger-to-nose test, and a positive heel-to-shin test. No dry or wet rales were heard in either lung. The remainder of the examination revealed no positive signs. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET-CT) performed at an external facility demonstrated hypermetabolic foci in the mediastinum and intracranial regions (Fig. 1A), suggestive of a primary malignancy with metastases. Admission magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a round lesion in the cerebellum with ring enhancement (Fig. 1B) and restricted diffusion (Fig. 1C), consistent with a brain abscess. The patient reported a history of fever and cough treated as pneumonia at a local hospital three months prior. His respiratory symptoms had since improved. On admission, the patient was afebrile and all vital signs were within normal limits (temperature: 36.4°C, blood pressure: 127/84 mmHg, pulse: 92 bpm). Laboratory findings indicated leukocytosis (19.6 × 10⁹/L, normal range: 3.5–9.5 × 10⁹/L; neutrophils 17.3 × 10⁹/μL, normal range: 1.8–6.3 × 10⁹/L), elevated CRP (89.11 mg/L, normal range: 0–6 mg/L), and procalcitonin (0.2 ng/mL, normal range: <0.05 ng/mL). The cerebellar abscess was suspected to be metastatic from the mediastinal focus. Due to signs of elevated intracranial pressure, surgical resection of the dominant brain abscess was performed. Intraoperatively, gray purulent material was encountered. NGS of the abscess material identified Nocardia beijingensis as the causative pathogen. Microbiological examination confirmed N. beijingensis exhibiting characteristic Gram-positive, beaded, filamentous bacilli with right-angled branching (Fig. 1D and E). A lumbar puncture was performed to obtain cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The CSF analysis showed a mildly elevated white blood cell count of 11.9/μL (normal range: 0–8/μL), with no red blood cell count. The CSF glucose level was within the normal range at 3.76 mmol/L (normal range: 2.5–4.5 mmol/L), while the protein level was elevated at 0.87 g/L (normal range: 0.15–0.45 g/L). Bacterial culture of the CSF yielded no growth. These CSF findings do not suggest significant meningitis.

The patient received initial empirical antibiotic therapy for two weeks, consisting of intravenous meropenem (2 g q8h), moxifloxacin (400 mg qd), and vancomycin (1 g q12h). This regimen was subsequently adjusted upon availability of the bacterial culture results (Table 1). Following the availability of bacterial culture results, a multidisciplinary consultation was conducted with the Department of Clinical Pharmacy and Infectious Diseases to establish the following antibiotic regimen: intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g q12h), oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX, 1.44 g q6h), and oral moxifloxacin (0.4 g q24h) for two weeks. This was followed by sequential oral therapy with TMP-SMX and moxifloxacin for an additional four weeks. Chest CT revealed a posterior mediastinal mass at the T6–7 subcarinal region, adjacent to the aorta and communicating with the esophagus (Fig. 2A). Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) demonstrated multiple esophageal fistulas covered with purulent exudate (Fig. 2B). During EGD, intermittent air bubbles were observed emanating from the esophageal fistula orifice. Notably, Irrigation of the fistula site elicited immediate coughing, suggesting a fistulous tract connecting to the bronchial tree via the mediastinal abscess. Esophagography confirmed the presence of a bronchial fistula, with contrast medium flowing from the esophageal fistula through the mediastinum into the bronchus (Fig. 2C and D). Multidisciplinary consultation (Gastroenterology, Thoracic Surgery) was obtained. Due to compromised nutritional status, a gastrostomy tube was placed for enteral nutrition support. Following clinical improvement, endoscopic closure of the esophageal fistula was performed (Fig. 2E). Thoracic surgery deemed the mediastinal abscess unsuitable for surgical intervention due to high-risk anatomy, recommending extended targeted antimicrobial therapy. The patient developed severe adverse reactions to TMP-SMX, including a generalized rash, vomiting, and pancytopenia (erythrocyte level of 2.46 ×1012/L, normal range: 4–5.5 × 1012/L; white blood cell count of 2.48 ×10⁹/L, normal range: 3.5–9.5 × 10⁹/L; and platelet count of 61 ×10⁹/L, normal range: 100–300 × 10⁹/L). These symptoms are consistent with known adverse effects of sulfamethoxazole, including skin rashes, gastrointestinal disturbances, and hematologic abnormalities such as pancytopenia. Given the severity of these adverse reactions, sulfamethoxazole was promptly discontinued. Based on the drug susceptibility testing results, oral linezolid was initiated at 600 mg twice daily. Hematopoietic support agents (including platelet, white blood cell, and red blood cell boosters) were administered. Since linezolid is also known to cause pancytopenia, the patient’s hematological parameters were closely monitored. At six-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated significant clinical improvement: (1) restored nutritional status, (2) reduced mediastinal lesion size, (3) no evidence of intracranial recurrence (Fig. 2F), and (4) complete healing of the esophageal fistula. The gastrostomy tube was removed, and the patient resumed oral intake.

The patient maintained excellent adherence to linezolid therapy. Treatment duration will be guided by serial clinical assessments, radiographic monitoring, and immune status evaluation. Linezolid therapy was planned to be continued for 12 months, with regular monitoring of hematological parameters.

Discussion

Nocardia are ubiquitous, aerobic, Gram-positive, partially acid-fast actinomycetes found in soil and decaying matter. These bacteria, known for their branching filaments and weak acid-fastness, can cause a variety of infections in humans and animals. Nocardia bacteria enter the host through inhalation or direct contact with skin wounds, leading to infection. They may lead to diverse infections, ranging from superficial skin infections to life-threatening systemic dissemination, including pulmonary and central nervous system (CNS) infections. This infection primarily affects immunocompromised individuals, such as those with HIV infection, organ transplant recipients, patients on long-term steroid therapy, and those with certain malignancies. However, there are also reports of nocardiosis in immunocompetent individuals. The Patient in this case is an immunocompetent individual.

Nocardial brain abscesses are relatively rare, accounting for about 2 % of all brain abscesses [1], but their mortality rate can be as high as 20–30 % [2]. A systematic review by Meena et al. reported an overall 22 % mortality rate in CNS nocardiosis [3]. Clinical manifestations of brain abscesses include headache, fever, neurological deficits, seizures, and altered consciousness. Frontal (41.6 %) and parietal (37.5 %) lobes are most commonly affected [2]. Treatment for nocardial brain abscess usually requires long-term antibiotic therapy and may necessitate surgical intervention.

Nocardia beijingensis, first isolated by Wang et al. from soil in China in 2001 [4], was subsequently reported in human infections [5]. Similar to numerous other Nocardia species, Nocardia beijingensis can cause human infections, including pulmonary infections, cutaneous abscesses, and disseminated nocardiosis. However, N. beijingensis also exhibits some unique characteristics. Notably, existing case reports indicate that regardless of the infection site, abscess formation is a consistent hallmark of N. beijingensis infections [6], [7]. This characteristic may serve as a key diagnostic indicator for this pathogen [8]. Previous findings do not support consistent associations between specific Nocardia species and preferential CNS infection patterns[2].

The diagnosis of nocardiosis can be challenging due to nonspecific clinical manifestations and the lengthy period required for bacterial culture. The bacterial culture process may take up to 3 weeks, though growth is often detected within 3–5 days [9]. In this case, the bacterial culture was negative after 3 days, but colonies grew by the 1st week. Therefore, timely submission of clinical samples and communication with the laboratory are crucial. Molecular methods (e.g., PCR, NGS) significantly expedite and improve identification accuracy. Additionally, for patients suspected of having nocardial brain abscess, assessing their systemic condition is necessary due to its characteristic of systemic dissemination. In this case, the mediastinal abscess and esophagomediastinal fistula might have been missed without a comprehensive systemic evaluation. Brain imaging (especially MRI) is essential, as it can reveal typical ring-enhancing lesions, aiding in diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making.

In terms of antibiotic therapy, sulfonamides are the drugs of choice for nocardiosis treatment, as they can penetrate the cerebrospinal fluid and the capsule of brain abscesses, being effective against most Nocardia strains [10], [11], [12]. However, due to the presence of drug resistance, it is sometimes necessary to combine other antibiotics, such as imipenem, meropenem, amikacin, or linezolid [10]. Susceptibility patterns of β-lactam antibiotics vary considerably among Nocardia species; therefore, testing is required to guide therapy [13]. Treatment duration is typically 6–12 months to eradicate slow-growing bacteria and prevent relapse. Patients with weakened immune function or central nervous system diseases receive treatment for at least 12 months, after which maintenance treatment may be required [14].

There is still a lack of standardized guidelines for the management and treatment of Nocardia infection. The treatment of Nocardia infection is mostly individualized according to the patient’s condition and the results of the susceptibility testing. Despite the limited literature, surgical excision is considered necessary in most cases [15], [16], enabling pathogen identification, culture guidance, and potentially improving outcomes. Moreover, nocardial abscesses can be radiologically indistinguishable from intracranial metastatic malignancies on CT or MRI [17], making histopathological examination of resected lesions imperative for definitive diagnosis. Some researchers suggest that craniotomy with complete excision of both the abscess and its wall may be more effective than aspiration and drainage alone [15].

In conclusion, this case of disseminated N. beijingensis infection highlights several key clinical messages. The pathogen demonstrates a strong tendency toward abscess formation and multifocal dissemination, underscoring the need for comprehensive screening for metastatic foci—even when infection appears localized—and mandating a multidisciplinary approach to address complex complications. Molecular diagnostic techniques, such as next-generation sequencing (NGS), are pivotal for rapid pathogen identification and facilitating early targeted treatment. Ultimately, this case exemplifies the importance of an adaptable, individualized management strategy in achieving favourable outcomes in nocardiosis.
