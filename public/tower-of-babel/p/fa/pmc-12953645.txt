Localized thermal tuning in fused silica inductive vibrating ring gyroscopes
Authors: Kai Wu, Xinyu Wang, Qingsong Li, Kuo Lu, Maobo Wang, Chengxiang Wang, Xuezhong Wu, Dingbang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inductive vibrating ring gyroscopes (IVRGs) present superior shock tolerance and reliability compared to conventional capacitive gyroscopes, making them ideal for inertial measurements in harsh environments. However, their operation in high-precision whole-angle mode requires real-time minimization of the frequency split between degenerate modes to prevent bias drift and measurement errors. Traditional electrostatic tuning methods are unsuitable for electromagnetic configurations, necessitating an alternative approach. In this paper, we propose and experimentally validate a localized thermal tuning technique to generate spatially controlled Joule heating at modal antinodes through specially patterned electrodes. This method utilizes the temperature-dependent increase of Young’s modulus in fused silica to achieve reversible and real-time frequency adjustment, with minimal thermal coupling between the degenerate modes. Finite element simulations demonstrated that optimized electrode designs reduced thermal coupling coefficient and improved split tuning efficiency. Prototypes incorporating localized thermal electrodes were fabricated and characterized, achieving efficient frequency split suppression (reducing split to 14 mHz), substantial reductions in angle-dependent bias (from 0.928°/s to 0.146°/s), significant scale factor nonlinearity improvements (from 4321 ppm to 61.3 ppm), and enhanced bias instability (from 4.8°/h to 0.67°/h), all with negligible impact on quality factor and robust temperature adaptability across −40 °C to 60 °C. These results confirm that localized thermal tuning is an available and effective strategy for inductive vibrating ring gyroscopes, paving the way for enhancing the precision in harsh environment applications.

Introduction

Ring-type micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) gyroscopes are among the most promising candidates in the field of MEMS gyroscopes, largely due to their highly symmetric structural characteristics. Indeed, the pursuit of ultra-high performance in the broader field of resonator-based MEMS sensors has led to significant breakthroughs, with recent works demonstrating high-Q pressure sensors1, high-responsivity accelerometers2 and highly sensitive temperature sensors3 by leveraging innovations in structural design, materials, and operational modes. The symmetry of the ring structure enables operation under mode-matched conditions, resulting in lower noise floors and higher sensitivity compared to other resonator types4–7. Based on their actuation and sensing mechanisms, vibrating ring gyroscopes are generally classified as capacitive7, inductive8, or piezoelectric9 devices. Among these, the capacitive scheme is the most widely used and has been extensively studied in both academia and industry.

However, the inherent design of capacitive ring gyroscopes—characterized by extremely narrow capacitive gaps between the resonant structure and fixed electrodes (usually tens of micrometers10,11), renders them highly susceptible to mechanical shock and vibration. Sudden displacements during shock events can cause the moving structure to rapidly traverse or even collapse into the fixed electrode, resulting in output deviations or, in severe cases, catastrophic pull-in failure that may permanently disable the device. In contrast, inductive gyroscopes offer greater mechanical robustness, with larger allowed displacements (usually hundreds of micrometers8) and a generally uniform magnetic field throughout the vibration envelope. These features mitigate the effects of external shocks, preventing abrupt signal anomalies and enhancing device reliability. A prominent example is the SiIMU02® inertial measurement unit (IMU) developed by Atlantic Inertia Systems (AIS), which adopts a silicon inductive gyroscope from Silicon Sensing. This device has demonstrated resilience to peak shocks as high as 20,000 g, thus highlighting the superior shock tolerance of electromagnetic gyroscope designs12.

Recently, whole-angle operation has emerged as a research focus due to its distinct advantages over conventional rate-mode gyroscopes, including exceptionally stable scale factors, theoretically unlimited measurement range, and bandwidth13–20. Inductive vibrating ring gyroscopes operating in whole-angle mode thus offer an ideal platform for high-performance inertial measurement in harsh environments. However, the whole-angle mode imposes exceptionally stringent requirements on structural symmetry, particularly in damping and stiffness19. Even minute asymmetries can induce nontrivial frequency splits and uneven damping in micro resonators, resulting in significant measurement errors and bias drift. Therefore, minimizing the frequency split in such gyroscopes is critical for unlocking their full potential.

Various methods have been developed to reduce frequency split in Coriolis vibratory gyroscopes. Common approaches include mechanical trimming and electrostatic tuning. Mechanical trimming, implemented via laser ablation21–25, chemical etching26,27, or focused ion beam (FIB) modification28–31, irreversibly alters the mass or stiffness distribution of the resonator. This approach, while effective for initial calibration, is labor-intensive, permanent, and cannot be applied post-packaging, rendering it unsuitable for real-time adjustment or in-field correction. Electrostatic tuning32–34, by contrast, provides dynamic frequency adjustment in capacitive devices through the introduction of equivalent negative stiffness at specific locations. However, it is intrinsically limited to electrostatically driven resonators and cannot be adapted to electromagnetic configurations due to their distinct electrode forms and actuation principles. Electrothermal tuning, often implemented via Joule heating, has emerged as an alternative approach for real-time frequency control in micro/nano resonators35–40. However, a significant limitation of this conventional approach is that it typically modulates the resonator’s global frequency. While effective for compensating for overall frequency drifts due to temperature or fabrication variations, it is generally incapable of addressing the frequency split between degenerate modes, as the thermal effect is not sufficiently localized or targeted to correct specific modal asymmetries.

Given these limitations, there is a pressing need for a novel, real-time, and reversible frequency tuning technique specifically tailored to electromagnetic-driven gyroscopes. In this work, we propose and experimentally validate a localized thermal tuning method to suppress frequency mismatch in fused silica inductive vibrating ring gyroscopes, in which specially patterned electrodes are used to induce spatially controlled temperature gradients on the gyroscope’s resonator. The thermal expansion and locally modified material properties, such as Young’s modulus and density, provide a means for reversible adjustment of modal frequencies. This proposed method offers several advantages as follows: First, by precisely controlling the spatial extent of the thermal influence region, frequency split can be effectively suppressed while minimizing undesired thermal coupling. Second, the thermal tuning voltage can be directly superimposed onto the existing drive signals, which simplifies circuit implementation and system integration. Third, this approach extends the practical applicability of frequency tuning to non-electrostatically driven resonators, such as electromagnetic and piezoelectric configurations, thereby significantly enhancing its universality for different actuation schemes. Crucially, this method leverages the unique thermo-elastic property of fused silica, whose Young’s modulus anomalously increases with temperature—opposite to most materials—providing an effective means to locally stiffen the resonator and raise the frequency of the lower-frequency mode. Overall, the localized thermal tuning method constitutes a significant innovation in the field of frequency tuning for MEMS gyroscopes.

The fused silica ring resonator, serving as the gyroscope’s core component, comprises an inner resonant ring, sixteen supporting beams, and an external anchor. Eight uniformly distributed and electrically isolated wire electrodes are patterned on its surface. When current flows through these electrodes, Lorentz forces drive the gyroscope’s primary mode (cos2θ mode). Under rotation, the resulting Coriolis force induces the secondary mode (sin2θ mode), generating detectable induced electromotive forces (EMFs) in corresponding electrodes8. Angular velocity data is derived by computing these EMF signals.

The IVRG can operate in both rate mode and whole-angle mode. Regardless of the operating mode, frequency split plays a critical role in determining the gyroscope’s performance. Beyond bias errors, frequency split is also a primary contributor to scale factor nonlinearity (SFNL) in closed-loop gyroscopes. In a force-to-rebalance control loop, the presence of Δω generates a quadrature error force. This parasitic force can couple into the rate-output channel, disrupting the linear relationship between the measured output and the true input rate44. Consequently, minimizing the frequency split is a foundational step for improving both bias stability and scale factor linearity.

Mechanism of decoupled thermal tuning in fused silica ring resonator

The mechanism behind frequency shifts induced by temperature variations in a ring resonator involves a complex interplay of multiple factors. The most immediate effect is thermal expansion: as the temperature increases, the material expands, altering the resonator’s physical dimensions, thereby shifting its resonant frequency. In addition, thermal stresses can cause deformations in the resonant structure, further affecting its resonant frequency. These stresses are often coupled with the geometrical changes induced by thermal expansion, complicating frequency tuning. The most significant contributor to frequency changes, however, is the variation in Young’s modulus. As the temperature increases, the Young’s modulus of fused silica increases, resulting in an increase in the stiffness of the resonator. This change in stiffness directly impacts the resonant frequency, with stiffer structures resonating at higher frequencies. The challenge of mitigating frequency splitting through thermal effects in a ring resonator primarily lies in preventing thermal coupling between the two degenerate modes. Achieving this requires precise control over the heating area. If the heating area is excessively large, thermal coupling between the modes will hinder the ability to achieve distinct frequency tuning for each mode. Furthermore, the positioning of the heat source should also be carefully optimized. The objective is selectively increasing the frequency of the lower-frequency mode while minimizing the impact on the higher-frequency mode.

According to Eq. (25), it is evident that as λ increases, the thermal coupling coefficient approaches 1, while as λ decreases, the thermal coupling coefficient approaches 0. The existence of this optimum stems from a critical trade-off: if the heat source is too concentrated (λ is too small), its region of influence is insufficient to effectively modulate the overall modal stiffness of the resonator; conversely, if the heat source is too diffuse (λ is too large), it begins to affect both vibration modes more equally, which reduces the differential tuning effect and increases thermal coupling. Practical implementations should therefore restrict heat source coverage below this angular limit to balance adequate modulation authority against undesirable thermal coupling.

Implementation of localized thermal tuning and finite element simulation

Building upon the theoretical foundation for decoupled thermal tuning, this section details the strategic redesign of the gyroscope’s wire electrodes to achieve localized Joule heating and reduce thermal coupling. While the existing eight-segment electrodes (Fig. 1b), each spanning π/4 radians, provide a mechanism for thermal tuning, their relatively broad coverage inherently results in significant thermal coupling (γ ≈ 83% at λ = π/4 according to Eq. (25)). To enhance decoupling by reducing λ, the electrode patterning was modified to concentrate resistive heating near key structural features.

Leveraging the Lorentz force drive inherent to the IVRG, Joule heating naturally arises within the wire electrodes when a drive current flows. Simultaneous driving and frequency tuning are achieved by superimposing a tuning voltage Vt onto the sinusoidal drive voltage Vd(t). This superposition enables independent control, where Vd (t) generates the Lorentz driving force, while Vt induces controlled Joule heating for frequency tuning.

To localize the heat generation near modal antinodes, the electrode’s geometry was manipulated in the regions where the 24 mass blocks attach to the ring. Crucially, the mass block locations coincide with the antinodes of the N = 2 degenerate modes, as shown in Fig. 2b, c. By narrowing the electrode width and increasing its path length specifically where it traverses these mass blocks, the electrical resistance in these discrete zones was significantly increased, as shown in Fig. 3a. The results indicate that as the resistance of the electrode layout increased, the thermal coupling decreased, which is consistent with the theoretical expectation. This is because the Joule heat generated in the wires was more concentrated in the regions near the mass blocks, thus minimizing the influence on the other mode. Furthermore, the designs of localized thermal electrodes (R2–R4) exhibited significantly higher tuning efficiency and decoupling compared to the conventional design (R1), fully demonstrating the advantages of this approach.

On the other hand, the configuration R2, which exhibited moderate resistance and localized heating, provided the highest thermal tuning efficiency. This non-monotonic behavior directly reflects the theoretical optimum (λ ≈ 0.25) discussed previously. The heat distribution of the R1 design is too diffuse (corresponding to a large effective λ), leading to high thermal coupling and lower efficiency. In contrast, the heat sources in the R3 and R4 designs become overly concentrated (corresponding to a very small effective λ), which limits their region of influence and thus reduces their ability to modulate the global modal stiffness. The R2 design, therefore, achieves the best balance between heat concentration and spatial influence, most closely approximating the optimal thermal distribution and thereby maximizing the tuning efficiency. This finding offers a critical insight for designing high-efficiency thermal tuning electrodes.

Gyroscope performance characterization

With the frequency split successfully reduced via thermal tuning, the gyroscope’s dynamics become significantly more stable, creating the necessary condition for further performance enhancement. Following this crucial step, the system’s electronic phase error was then systematically compensated for to mitigate coupled errors, following the established technique detailed in our prior work42. The subsequent performance metrics were then characterized to validate the improvements enabled by our frequency tuning method. All results presented in this section were obtained from the gyroscope system after both the frequency split and phase error were compensated.

Following the experimental validation of localized thermal tuning, the performance of the gyroscope was thoroughly characterized. Considering both the thermal coupling coefficient and the tuning efficiency, prototype R3 was selected for performance characterization. This decision was based on a critical engineering trade-off. According to the experimental data in Table 3, while prototype R2 offered the highest tuning efficiency (46.3 Hz/W), its thermal coupling coefficient was also relatively high at 37.7%. In contrast, prototype R3 provided a significantly lower thermal coupling of 28.6% (a 24% relative improvement over R2) at the cost of only a minor 8% reduction in tuning efficiency (42.6 Hz/W). This substantial improvement in mode decoupling was deemed more critical for achieving high-performance stability in a closed-loop system, making R3 the most suitable candidate for full characterization. Conclusion

This work provides an effective solution toward achieving real-time frequency split tuning in electromagnetic-driven vibrating ring gyroscopes via localized Joule heating. Through judicious electrode design, thermal coupling between degenerate modes is substantially reduced. Experimental results confirm the feasibility and superior performance of the proposed method, showing considerable improvements in gyroscope performance, including angle-dependent bias, scale factor nonlinearity, and bias instability. Furthermore, this approach exhibited good temperature adaptability across a wide operational range (−40 °C to 60 °C) with minor frequency variation. Besides, for resonators made from materials like silicon, where Young’s modulus decreases with temperature, the same frequency split reduction can be achieved by simply applying localized heat to the antinodes of the higher-frequency mode. The uniqueness of this localized thermal tuning strategy provides a promising path forward for miniaturization, precision enhancement, and wider application potential of inductive gyroscopes in harsh environment applications. In addition, it is acknowledged that the efficiency of the presented local thermal tuning method is optimal when the resonator’s principal stiffness axis is aligned with the heater electrodes. Future work could focus on developing a more universal tuning architecture, for instance, by decoupling tuning electrodes from the drive/sense electrodes and increasing their circumferential density, to handle arbitrary orientations of the frequency split axis.
