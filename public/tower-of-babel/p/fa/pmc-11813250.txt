Effect of Occupational Noise on Employee Health: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: Chin-Yin Huang, An-Ju Lai, Chun-Chieh Chen, Yu-Li Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study investigated the long-term health risks associated with occupational noise exposure. By using 9 years of health examination data from a major manufacturing company in Taiwan, this study compared the health indices of employees in noise-intensive and non-noise-intensive work environments.Methods:A retrospective analysis of 6278 health examination reports spanning 9 years was conducted to compare 20 health indices among 166 employees evenly distributed between noise-intensive and non-noise-intensive workgroups. These employees were carefully matched for sex, age, and work experience. Linear regression models were used to assess the temporal impact of noise exposure on each health index by incorporating a unique range ratio to quantify index fluctuations attributable to noise.Results:Employees exposed to occupational noise had an increase in platelet count of 16.594 K/µL, with an annual increment of 1.228 K/µL. Moreover, employees in noisy environments exhibited a significant increase in blood urea nitrogen level by 0.438 mg/dL, with an annual increase of 0.171 mg/dL. Additionally, their carcinoembryonic antigen level was significantly increased by 0.187 ng/mL on average, with the annual increase being 0.034 ng/mL. Moreover, increases in systolic blood pressure (2.015 mmHg), heart rate (4.620 beats/min), and white blood cell count (0.536 K/µL) and a decrease in red blood cell count (0.107 M/µL) were discovered to be solely attributable to noise exposure.Conclusions:Prolonged exposure to high occupational noise levels significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular, renal, and colorectal diseases. This longitudinal study underscores the detrimental health consequences of prolonged noise exposure in occupational settings.

INTRODUCTION

Noise constitutes a major environmental stressor with profound implications for public health.[12] Noise is a recognized environmental risk factor that disrupts the human body’s circadian rhythm, contributing to stress, mood alterations, and impaired worker performance. In addition to having psychological effects, noise exposure is associated with physical health issues, including hearing impairment, voice loss, fatigue, and cardiovascular abnormalities. These issues have been affected more than 30 million individuals in the United States alone.[3] Noise is among the most common occupational hazards,[4] and the World Health Organization (WHO) has projected that hearing loss will be one of the 10 main causes of disease burden in high- and middle-income countries by 2030. Furthermore, the WHO has estimated that by 2050, more than 900 million people worldwide will have disabling hearing loss.[5]

Noise is a prevalent occupational risk factor. Noise-induced hearing loss contributes to diminished attention, increasing the risks of accidents, falls, and subsequent injuries or death.[6] Furthermore, noise adversely affects higher-order cognitive functions, such as logical reasoning, mathematical calculation, and data processing.[7] Hearing loss not only hampers language comprehension but also impairs spatial perception. Moreover, given that neural pathways for auditory and cognitive processing are shared, noisy work environments can interfere with both sound reception and information comprehension. As cognitive functions decline, the risk of an occupational accident increases, highlighting the urgent need for noise mitigation in workplaces. Encouragingly, workplace health promotion and management strategies can effectively prevent hearing loss.[5]

Noise-induced health consequences extend beyond auditory impairment, affecting the cardiovascular system, mental health, and sleep quality. Noise can stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, leading to an increase in adrenaline secretion. Epidemiological studies have reported that noise exposure may increase the risks of annoyance, cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, diabetes, and atherosclerosis.[48] Research has established a correlation between greater environmental noise and higher hypertension risk.[9] However, the evidence linking occupational noise exposure to cardiovascular disease and metabolic disorders remains inconclusive.[1011]

Systematic literature reviews have established a link between occupational noise exposure and a range of adverse health effects beyond hearing loss, including circulatory, respiratory, immunological, gastrointestinal, and oncologic conditions.[612] These findings underscore the need for a comprehensive investigation of the extra-auditory health impacts of noise exposure. Despite the well-documented detrimental effects of workplace noise on employee health, long-term observational studies on specific noise-related health hazards remain limited. This study leveraged 9 years of health examination data from employees at a large manufacturing company in Taiwan. By comparing the health indices of employees exposed to noise-intensive and non-noise-intensive environments, this study sought to elucidate the potential health consequences of occupational noise exposure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort

This retrospective study examined the long-term health implications of occupational noise exposure among 1989 employees of the Taiwan Hon Chuan (THC) group (https://www.honchuan.com), a leading beverage packaging and filling manufacturer in Taiwan. By using 9 years (2011–2019) of health examination data (n = 6278), the study compared employees in noise-intensive versus non-noise-intensive production roles within THC’s Taiwanese facilities. Headquartered in Taiwan, THC has operations across China, Southeast Asia, and Africa. Foreign workers who were permitted to work in Taiwan only for a period of 2–3 years were excluded from the study. The company adheres to stringent labor health and safety regulations, including annual employee health screenings. This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board II of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (CE20343B-2).

Health management status

The company has a robust occupational safety and health program, staffed by dedicated nurses, managers, and adjunct occupational medicine physicians in accordance with the regulations of the Occupational Safety and Health Administration under the Ministry of Labor in Taiwan. This team prioritizes the safety of workers and the environment, providing on-site promotion and medical services on a monthly basis. All employees are subject to an 8-h daily noise exposure limit. Noise-intensive work environments, characterized by an average sound level of 85 dB, necessitate adherence to strict protective measures, including the following: (1) noise exposure assessments, (2) noise hazard control, (3) the wearing of soundproof protective equipment, (4) hearing protection training, and (5) health assessments and management. The company maintains meticulous records of these compliance efforts.

The company implements the following settings:

(1) Monitoring of the work environment

The company schedules biannual workplace noise assessments in April–May and October–November, which are conducted by an agency accredited by the Ministry of Labor. Fifteen days prior to each monitoring session, the company is required to submit the monitoring plan online to the Ministry of Labor. To ensure adherence to the semiannual monitoring schedule, the company must provide the most recent environmental monitoring data when applying for subsidies of occupational accident insurance or occupational health examinations from the Bureau of Labor Insurance, Ministry of Labor.

The personnel responsible for the monitoring must obtain the relevant licenses as required by law and develop a monitoring plan with sampling strategies for identifying, assessing, and controlling workplace hazards. The noise levels in the workplace are measured using sound level meters, which are employed to determine sound pressure levels. The results obtained are used to generate workplace noise maps. In this study, “noise operation” refers to work environments exceeding the 8-h daily noise dose limit of 85 dB, whereas “non-noise operation” refers to environments below this threshold.

(2) Methods for collecting exposure evidence

To assess occupational noise exposure risks, comprehensive noise measurements are essential. These measurements are divided into two categories: (1) worker exposure assessment (area sampling and personal exposure dosimetry) and (2) noise source identification (engineering control surveys). Prior to noise measurements, a site visit is conducted to understand workplace conditions, operation types, working hours, factory layout, types of noise-generating machinery, their arrangement and distribution, and the number of exposed workers. This information aids in developing an effective noise measurement strategy and establishing exposure data for each worker. The area sampling and personal exposure dosimetry procedures are described as follows. Please refer to the supplementary information on the companies and equipment for the noise measurement.

(a)Area sampling: using a Type II sound level meter, sound pressure levels are measured at various points in a noisy workplace, and the workplace is mapped with equidistant lines, with measurements taken at their intersections (spot) at 150 cm above the ground.

Area sampling: using a Type II sound level meter, sound pressure levels are measured at various points in a noisy workplace, and the workplace is mapped with equidistant lines, with measurements taken at their intersections (spot) at 150 cm above the ground.

(b)Personal exposure dosimetry: workers wear a noise dosimeter throughout their shift to measure individual noise exposure levels. The total dose is then converted into an 8-h daily average sound pressure level.

Personal exposure dosimetry: workers wear a noise dosimeter throughout their shift to measure individual noise exposure levels. The total dose is then converted into an 8-h daily average sound pressure level.

(3) Determining workers’ exposure conditions

To determine whether the sound pressure levels at the workplace and workers’ exposure levels comply with legal standards, both area sampling and personal exposure dosimetry are employed. The primary objective is to establish a comprehensive occupational noise exposure history for workers involved in noise-intensive operations, including the following: (1) workplace characteristics; (2) noise hazards; (3) worker demographics; (4) exposure group classifications based on factors such as work processes, tasks, and environmental conditions; (5) workplace noise measurements; and (6) exposure assessment data (sampling methodology). Workers are categorized into exposure groups on the basis of shared exposure factors, facilitating comparative analysis, and risk assessment.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 25.0, IBM Corporation, 1 Orchard Rd, Armonk, NY, USA), with the graphical representations in Figure 1 generated using Microsoft Office PowerPoint (2013 version, Microsoft Corporation, One Microsoft Way, Redmond, WA, USA). The dataset comprised 20 continuous health indices, with “Noise” categorized as a nominal variable and “Year” as an integer ranging from 0 to 8, corresponding to the years 2011–2019. Statistical analyses included the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U-test for comparing the medians of two groups, t-test for comparing the means of two groups, chi-square test for assessing the homogeneity between two categorical variables, and multiple linear regression for assessing the effects of noise exposure over time. All statistical tests were performed at a significance level of 0.05.

Longitudinal analysis for 9 consecutive years

Understanding the temporal effect of noise exposure could inform reassignment of employees to non-noisy environments to mitigate health risks. The data presented in Table 1 do not reflect the temporal effects of noise exposure on health indices. To elucidate the long-term effects of sustained noise exposure, the dataset of 6278 person-times spanning 9 consecutive years was restructured on the basis of individual employee history. A matched cohort of 83 employees each from noise-intensive and non-noise-intensive environments was formed. The two groups had no significant differences in sex, age, or years of service, as depicted in Table 2. Notably, all 166 participants had a consistent work environment throughout the 9-year study period. Descriptive statistics for the cohort revealed the following:

These findings underscore the importance of longitudinal studies in assessing the long-term health consequences of occupational noise exposure. For instance, although RBC count may not appear critical in short-term analyses, when exposure duration is considered, its negative association with noise becomes evident, emphasizing the need for ongoing monitoring in noise-exposed workers.

DISCUSSION

A substantial body of literature discusses the adverse impact of environmental noise on the cardiovascular system. Prolonged exposure to noise has been implicated as a risk factor for chronic stress and subsequent cardiovascular diseases such as myocardial infarction, hypertension, and stroke.[11314] Empirical evidence suggests a positive association between longer exposure to railway noise and greater susceptibility to cardiovascular disease. Excessive noise pollution from railway traffic has been linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular ailments, including hypertension, heart attacks, and stroke. The precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these relationships have not yet been fully elucidated.[15]

Our study revealed significantly higher heart rate (by 4.620 beats per minute) and systolic blood pressure (by 2.015 mmHg) in employees exposed to occupational noise after controlling for the effect of Year. These findings suggest a strong association between longer noise exposure and higher risk of hypertension. Prolonged hypertension can lead to vascular ruptures, tissue peeling that blocks blood vessels, and various complications, including stroke, myocardial infarction, deteriorating kidney function, blurred vision, carotid artery narrowing, and peripheral arterial hardening. Our study, consistent with the aforementioned research, revealed that high-noise environments could induce cardiovascular diseases such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and hypertension. Our findings corroborate existing research linking occupational noise to cardiovascular diseases and support the guidelines of Taiwan’s Occupational Safety and Health Administration regarding the placement of individuals with cardiovascular conditions in non-noise-intensive roles.

Our findings revealed a significant increase in the platelet count (by 16.594 K/µL) of employees exposed to occupational noise. An elevated platelet level is associated with increased blood viscosity, a factor contributing to stroke and myocardial infarction. Furthermore, the observed annual increment of 1.228 K/µL in platelet count underscores the importance of platelet monitoring as a crucial health indicator for individuals working in noisy environments.

This study discovered a notable increase in BUN level of 0.438 mg/dL in employees exposed to noisy environments, accompanied by an annual increment of 0.171 mg/dL. Additionally, creatinine level was found to exhibit a yearly increase of 0.008 mg/dL after adjustment for noise exposure. These findings suggest a potential association between occupational noise and impaired kidney function, characterized by elevated BUN and creatinine levels. Noise-induced renal dysfunction may ultimately progress to renal insufficiency. Monitoring changes in kidney function, particularly in middle-aged and older employees, can prevent a decline in the kidneys’ ability to excrete and metabolize waste products. This is consistent with studies suggesting that long-term exposure to noise is detrimental to human health, having cardiovascular as well as renal health implications.[1216] Given the critical role of the kidneys in erythropoietin secretion[1718] and subsequent RBC production in the bone marrow, the observed decrease in RBC count (0.107 M/µL) in noise-exposed employees may have been a secondary consequence of impaired renal function. Our study further noted a lower RBC count in the noise-exposed employees compared with those working in a nonnoisy environment, with the corresponding range ratio being 0.75 [Table 4]. A decrease in RBCs can lead to anemia and associated symptoms such as dizziness, fatigue, lack of concentration, and palpitations. These health implications not only increase the risk of workplace accidents but also pose a potential threat to life.

Research has implicated noise exposure as a potential risk factor for cancer development.[19] Our findings align with this evidence, demonstrating a significant increase in CEA level in the noise-exposed employees (0.187 ng/mL increase associated with noise and an increase of 0.034 ng/mL annually). Given the established link between elevated CEA level and colorectal cancer, these results suggest a potential association between long-term occupational noise exposure and increased colorectal cancer risk. The age-related increase in CEA level underscores the importance of regular colorectal cancer screening, particularly for older employees in noise-intensive work environments.

Table 4 reveals significantly greater variation (1.5 times) in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and WBC count among employees in noise-intensive environments compared with their non-noise-exposed counterparts over a 9-year period. The observed increase in WBC count specifically attributable to noise exposure raises concerns about heightened risk of infections, chronic inflammation, and blood cancers. To mitigate these potential risks, regular health monitoring, including body temperature checks, is recommended for noise-exposed workers. These findings align with broader research highlighting the systemic health implications of occupational noise exposure[12] beyond auditory effects.[6]

Our study revealed significant decreases in weight by 1.557 kg, BMI by 0.188 kg/m2, and waist circumference by 0.104 cm among workers exposed to occupational noise. Additionally, blood reports indicated significant decreases in levels of triglycerides by 20.141 mg/dL and total cholesterol by 4.052 mg/dL. However, due to an inability to stratify participants on the basis of physical activity level, the observed changes in weight, BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and total cholesterol cannot be definitively attributed to noise exposure alone. Therefore, these findings require further investigation to establish a causal relationship between occupational noise and metabolic outcomes.

This study underscores the detrimental impact of prolonged exposure to noise-intensive environments on employees’ health. To mitigate these adverse effects, employers operating in noisy conditions must prioritize the implementation of effective hearing conservation programs. These programs should encompass a multifaceted approach, including regular noise monitoring and exposure assessment, hazard control, environmental improvements, work scheduling, safety and health training, provision of protective gear, regular health examinations, and management for employees, and ongoing evaluation and enhancement of these measures. Additionally, workers should receive training on the appropriate selection and use of noise protection equipment and should undergo annual hearing tests.

To effectively mitigate noise pollution originating from machinery, robust noise control measures are imperative. High-noise-generating equipment should be physically isolated from general work areas to minimize noise propagation. Additionally, the integration of sound reduction, enclosure, and vibration isolation techniques into the design of machinery that produces intense noise and vibration is crucial for creating quieter and safer work environments.

Building upon the findings of this study, future research should adopt a prospective cohort design to investigate the long-term health outcomes of occupational noise exposure across multiple companies. To account for potential confounding factors, stratification based on workplace physical activity level and employees’ medication use is recommended. Additionally, incorporating regular questionnaire-based assessments of stress and sleep quality alongside health examinations would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the overall impact of noise exposure on employee well-being.

The study has various strengths. To mitigate potential biases in the health examination data, all participants underwent a standardized health assessment at the same hospital, and the same testing laboratory was employed for blood analyses. Consistency in the health-care delivery and diagnostic procedures ensured the reliability of the study findings. Notably, the dedicated team of occupational medicine physicians and occupational safety and health nurses were unchanged throughout the research process, ensuring continuity in workplace health promotion efforts. However, a limitation of the study should be acknowledged. This retrospective study spanned a 9-year period, limiting the ability to collect comprehensive data on participants’ medical treatments, medication use, and lifestyle habits. Nevertheless, the study was unable to stratify participants on the basis of their level of physical activity within the workplace.

CONCLUSIONS

This study investigated the long-term health implications of occupational noise exposure, focusing on physiological and hematological indicators. By employing linear regression, we identified significant associations between noise exposure and changes in various health indices, including body weight, BMI, waist circumference, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, WBC count, RBC count, platelet count, and levels of BUN, creatinine, uric acid, r-GT, triglycerides, total cholesterol, AFP, and CEA. Many of these factors are recognized as contributors to disease development. This study particularly emphasizes the importance of closely monitoring an employee’s platelet count, BUN level, and CEA level. These indicators were significantly positively associated with both Noise and Year in noise-exposed workers. Increments in platelet count, BUN level, and CEA level may lead to cardiovascular, renal, and colorectal diseases. Furthermore, this study identified increases in WBC count and decreases in RBC count among individuals working in a noise-intensive environment. These findings raise concerns about potential threats to the well-being of employees in such environments.

These findings strongly underscore the importance of prioritizing the mitigation of noise in working environments. To mitigate the adverse impact of noise on human health and prevent the onset of occupational diseases associated with prolonged noise exposure, ranging from hearing impairment to cardiovascular diseases and metabolic disorders, employers must prioritize the implementation of comprehensive noise control measures.
