Fruit anatomy of Tragopogon species (Asteraceae) and its taxonomic implications
Authors: Tuğçe Pelin Gençkaya, Kamil Coşkunçelebi̇, Mutlu Gültepe, Zeynep Türker, Serdar Makbul, Murat Erdem Güzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents an anatomical examination of fruits (cypselas) from 27 Tragopogon taxa, 14 of which are endemic to Türkiye. Mature outer cypselas, collected in the field or from herbarium specimens, were sectioned at the median region of the cypsela body, stained with safranin-fast green, and mounted in entellan to prepare permanent slides. The dendrogram derived from fruit anatomical data was generally congruent with the traditional classification; however, the examined taxa cannot be reliably identified solely on the basis of fruit anatomy, although these characters help to distinguish some closely related taxa. Principal component analysis based on 13 anatomical characters revealed that the widest and narrowest parts of the cypsela, including the primary ribs in the cross-section, and the pericarp width in the primary rib regions, were the most informative features for distinguishing the investigated Tragopogon taxa. Furthermore, discriminant analysis with leave-one-out cross-validation correctly classified most of the taxa into the four predefined fruit types, yielding an overall classification accuracy of 88.89%, suggesting that anatomical data generally support the informal groups initially defined by fruit characters and indicating substantial agreement between the two datasets.

Introduction

Tragopogon L. is a genus within the tribe Cichorieae Lam. & DC. (Lactuceae Cass.) of Asteraceae [1–4]. Taxonomic complexity arises from naturally occurring hybridization and varying ploidy levels (alloploidy or autopolyploidy) among the species [2–4]. Its relatively recent origin and rapid diversification have led to numerous new species with insufficient genetic and morphological differentiation [5, 6]. Consequently, species descriptions are often inadequate, complicating the delineation of species boundaries and hindering the establishment of a stable classification. Fruits (cypselas) represent one of the most important diagnostic features in Tragopogon taxonomy [2, 7]. Species delimitation in the genus is often based on cypsela morphology, particularly whether the cypsela is winged or wingless, whether the beak is longer or shorter than the cypsela body, and whether the upper portion of the beak is swollen [7, 8]. Blanca and Díaz de la Guardia (1997) [9] further demonstrated that stereomicroscopy and scanning electron microscopy can reveal additional diagnostic traits, such as cypsela size and shape, rib number, spines, and intercostal ornamentation. Despite this, relatively few studies have explored the taxonomic value of cypsela anatomy in Tragopogon [10–12]. Mukherjee and Sarkar (1995) [10] studied the cypsela anatomy of T. porrifolius L. in Australia, while Qureshi et al. (2008) [11] carried out a comparative study on T. dubius Scop. and T. gracilis D. Don. These investigations, however, were limited to a small number of species. In contrast, Sukhorukov and Nilova (2015) [12] provided the most comprehensive account to date, analyzing the cypselas of 67 Tragopogon species (nine of which are also found in Türkiye) and classifying them into eight non-taxonomic categories. These groups were not intended as formal taxonomic units but rather as a framework for comparing morphological and anatomical variation across the genus.

Tragopogon is native to temperate regions of Eurasia and North Africa, with a distribution extending from the Canary Islands and the Mediterranean basin, through much of Europe, Central Asia, Siberia, the Himalayas, and the Middle East, parts of North Africa [1, 2, 11]. Globally, it comprises an estimated ca.150 species [2, 3, 6]. For the Turkish flora, Matthews (1975) [7] provided the first comprehensive treatment of the genus in Flora of Turkey and the East Aegean Islands. Since then, additional studies have described new taxa from Türkiye and contributed molecular and morphological evidence to its taxonomy [13–22]. According to the above recent studies, a total of 27 Tragopogon taxa are distributed in Türkiye, 14 of which are endemic. However, the cypsela anatomy of these taxa, including the endemics, has not yet been studied in detail. The present study aims to fill this gap by investigating fruit anatomy in Tragopogon and providing new insights into the taxonomic classification of the Turkish representatives of the genus.

Material and methods

Plant samples from TÜBİTAK-supported projects (110T954 and 120Z001) were used in this carpological study. Specimens were gathered from natural populations during field surveys in line with local and national norms governing wild flora collection in Türkiye, and no special licenses were necessary for the researched taxa. Kamil Coşkunçelebi and Mutlu Gültepe conducted the taxonomic identification. The voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of Biology at Karadeniz Technical University (KTUB), which is not currently registered in the Index Herbariorum, as listed in Table 1. Anatomical investigations were performed on well-developed outer cypselas from field-collected material and herbarium specimens (KTUB). Cross-sections were taken from the median region of the cypsela, where diagnostic anatomical characters are most reliably expressed; therefore, sampling was restricted to well-preserved and fully mature fruits.

For the anatomical examinations, the methods of Zhu et al. (2006) [23] were followed. The cypselas were first boiled in water for 5–20 min, and then all cypselas were embedded in paraffin (melting point 65 °C) before sectioning by a rotary microtome (Shandon Finesse 325). Cross-sections were taken from the median region of the mature cypselas. The sections were sliced with thicknesses ranging from 8 to 20 μm and passed through a graded alcohol series for dehydration. The slices were stained in a safranin-fast green combination before being mounted in entellan. Lastly, permanent slides were utilized to measure, observe, and photograph the cypsela anatomical features listed in Table 2 with a light microscope (Leica DM 1000). All anatomical features were scored at least thirty times, based on permanent slides prepared from 3 to 4 cypselas collected from different individuals within the same population for each investigated taxon. The terminology of Sukhorukov and Nilova (2015) [12] was mostly adopted for describing cypsela anatomical features.

Variables used in the cluster and principal component analyses of the examined Tragopogon species

All variables were checked for consistency prior to all analyses performed in this study. For the cluster analysis (CA), a pairwise resemblance matrix was calculated using Gower’s coefficient, and a dendrogram illustrating similarity relationships among taxa was generated using the unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic averages (UPGMA) [24]. To ensure comparability among variables measured on different scales, the dataset was standardized prior to multivariate analyses. The data matrix, constructed from the average measurements of characters (Table 3), was then subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) to determine the most valuable anatomical traits among the examined taxa. For PCA, a correlation matrix was created, and three eigenvectors were extracted to give a two-dimensional plot of taxa and characters [25]. Discriminant Analysis (DA) was performed to evaluate the ability of anatomical variables to discriminate among predefined groups based on general fruit characters. The analysis included 27 taxa assigned to four predefined groups based on fruit morphological characters. Prior to analysis, assumptions of multivariate normality and homogeneity of covariance matrices were assessed [26]. Group separation was evaluated using the first two discriminant functions (DF–1 and DF–2). Classification performance was assessed based on the percentage of correctly classified cases. To obtain an unbiased estimate, a leave-one-out cross-validation procedure was applied, in which each case was classified using discriminant functions derived from all remaining cases. This approach is widely used in plant systematics and provides a robust evaluation of model performance [27]. All analyses were performed using MVSP [28] and Past 3x [29] computer programs for multivariate data analysis in ecology and systematics.

Results

Cypsela structure

The main structural characteristics of the cypselas, together with images of 14 taxa representing four distinct types, are presented and described below. The measurements used in the descriptions are based on the average values (Table 3).

In all examined species, the pericarp is composed of a single-layered exocarp, a distinct mesocarp consisting of parenchymatous and sclerenchymatous cells, and an indistinct, obliterated endocarp. The sclerenchymatous cells, together with the indefinite endocarp, form a mechanical tissue. The pericarp width in the primary rib region ranged from 90 to 1400 μm, being narrowest in T. graminifolius and widest in T. pterocarpus (Table 3). The parenchymatous tissue in this region varied between 40 μm (T. graminifolius) and 1300 μm (T. pterocarpus). The width of the ring-shaped mechanical tissue (including the endocarp) ranged from 38 μm (T. graminifolius) to 103 μm (T. artvinensis). Similarly, the width of the parenchymatous tissue ranged between 78 (T. graminifolius) and 533 μm (T. pterocarpus), while the mechanical tissue measured 34–92 μm, with T. graminifolius consistently showing the lowest values and T. artvinensis the highest in the secondary rib region (Table 3).

The seed coat consists of two horizontally arranged parenchymatous cell layers, although it is mostly indistinct. The embryo was found to be dicotyledonous, composed of several parenchymatous cells, and the endosperm was not clearly distinguishable. The maximum width of the region encircled by the seed coat ranged from 698 μm (T. graminifolius) to 1600 μm (T. anatolicus).

All the examined taxa had air cavities that can extend to the mechanical tissue (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Air cavities were detected in the primary rib regions of T. oligolepis, while all other taxa had the cavities in the secondary rib regions. The air cavities were found to be the greatest dimensions in T. pterocarpus (122 × 250 μm) and the smallest dimensions in T. graminifolius (44 × 57 μm). The air cavities were identified as angular, circular, or circular-angular (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Discussion

Numerous studies on Asteraceae have demonstrated that fruit anatomy provides valuable taxonomic information, with significance ranging from the tribal to the specific level [30–33]. Together with micro- and macro-morphological features, carpological traits have also been widely used to clarify relationships among closely related taxa [34]. Although Turkish members of Tragopogon were investigated in detail morphologically by Matthews (1975) [7] and Gültepe (2014) [35], their cypsela anatomy has not previously been studied comprehensively. Nonetheless, the fruit anatomy of nine taxa occurring in Türkiye was examined earlier using non-Turkish material [10, 12]. Based on fruit structure, Sukhorukov and Nilova (2015) [12] classified Tragopogon taxa into eight types, partially overlapping with traditional classifications. In the present study, despite similarities in general fruit structure, the examined taxa were categorized into four distinct informal types, differing in particular anatomical traits.

The cross-validated discriminant analysis correctly classified most of the examined taxa, corresponding to an accuracy of 88.89%. This supports a general correspondence between fruit morphology and cypsela anatomy. However, the assignment of three taxa to different groups suggests that anatomical traits provide supportive, but not definitive, evidence for group delimitation. Such results suggest that both general appearance and anatomical variables reflect consistent patterns of variation among taxa, thereby reinforcing the validity of the group delimitation [26, 36]. This pattern is also evident in the ordination of the first two discriminant functions (DF–1 and DF–2), where groups are clearly separated with only minor overlap (Fig. 7).

Furthermore, significant differences were detected, particularly in the dimensions of certain traits that may be useful for distinguishing closely related taxa and helping to address taxonomic problems. For example, T. buphthalmoides, T. reticulatus, and T. albinervis are quite similar in terms of their cypsela characteristics [35]. Here, T. reticulatus was assigned to Type III, whereas T. buphthalmoides and T. albinervis were included in Type II. The dendrogram based on anatomical traits further shows that all three taxa are not closely associated with each other, and this indicates a degree of differentiation among these taxa (Fig. 5, Table 3). A comparable situation was observed for T. graminifolius, T. dshimilensis, T. olympicus, and T. latifolius, which are considered as closely related species according to morphological (short beaks and wingless cypsela) and molecular evidence [35]. However, the present results suggest some anatomical differences. For instance, the relatively small cypsela diameter of T. graminifolius may help distinguish it from the other examined taxa (Table 3). Although T. latifolius and T. olympicus have been reported to be highly similar based on morphological and molecular data [19, 35], the present findings indicate that anatomical features may provide additional support for their differentiation (Fig. 5). These findings are consistent with the conclusion of Sukhorukov and Nilova (2015) [12] that cypsela diameter is an important trait for distinguishing closely related taxa, and they further emphasize the potential contribution of cypsela anatomy to the systematics of Tragopogon.

The UPGMA dendrogram and PCA analysis reveal biologically interpretable patterns of variation (Figs. 5 and 6). The first principal component (PC–1), which accounts for the majority of the total variance, is primarily associated with overall fruit size and related allometric variation, a pattern commonly observed in morphometric studies [24, 37]. Therefore, the separation observed along this axis mainly reflects size-related differences rather than independent anatomical traits, and should be interpreted cautiously in a taxonomic context.

The second principal component (PC–2) captures variation mainly related to internal tissue organization and proportional traits, as reflected by variables associated with pericarp thickness and relative tissue proportions. It should be emphasized that the UPGMA dendrogram reflects phenetic similarity based on anatomical characters and is not intended as a phylogenetic reconstruction.

Tragopogon is well known for frequent polyploidy and hybridization, both of which can influence morphological and anatomical traits, particularly those related to overall size [2]. Polyploidy is widely associated with an increase in cell and organ size, and polyploid plants often exhibit larger reproductive structures, including seeds and fruits, compared to their diploid counterparts [38]. In the present study, although most taxa are diploid, some are tetraploid, while chromosome numbers remain unknown for several endemic taxa [17]. Despite the fact that the first principal component (PC–1) primarily reflects size-related variation, the results of this study indicate that the observed anatomical differences are not directly associated with ploidy level. For example, T. reticulatus, T. latifolius, and T. buphthalmoides are tetraploid (2n = 4x = 24), whereas T. pterocarpus, T. pterodes, and T. artvinensis (unpublished data) are diploid (2n = 2x = 12). However, contrary to expectations, no positive relationship was detected between ploidy level and the clustering of taxa in the UPGMA dendrogram. Tetraploid taxa do not form a distinct or coherent group, indicating that ploidy alone does not account for the observed similarity patterns. Instead, the clustering structure is more likely shaped by the combined influence of multiple anatomical characters. The results of this study do not support a meaningful relationship between ploidy level and the observed grouping pattern. In this context, cypsela anatomy provides useful, but not sufficient on its own, taxonomic information and should be interpreted together with morphological, cytogenetic, and molecular data.

The number of cell rows and the width of mechanical and parenchymal tissues are considered taxonomically informative traits in certain genera of Asteraceae [39, 40]. According to Sukhorukov and Nilova (2015) [12], in Tragopogon, mechanical tissue generally comprises 3–6 rows, while parenchymatous tissue is wider, with 4–8 layers, and up to 30 in the primary ribs. The present study is consistent with this pattern, as all examined taxa exhibited wider parenchymatous than mechanical layers. Principal component analysis (PCA) also indicated pericarp width (X1) as the most influential variable in explaining interspecific variation (Table 3, Fig. 6), suggesting the systematic relevance of tissue widths. Among the examined traits, certain features such as the general organization of pericarp tissues and the number of tissue layers appear to be relatively conserved, whereas size-related traits show greater variability among taxa. This distinction may be particularly useful for future taxonomic and identification studies in the genus.

Seed coat characteristics are taxonomically useful in defining taxonomic boundaries within Asteraceae [41]. Previous studies reported seed coats consisting of one to three parenchymatous layers with thin or thick walls, and arranged tangentially or radially, consistent with earlier findings [10, 41]. Although minor width differences were observed (Table 3), seed coat characters appear to be of limited taxonomic value for delimiting Tragopogon taxa (Table 4, Fig. 6). Conversely, variation in nutrient tissue width was found to be notable, as also noted in other genera such as Crepis L., Vernonia Schreb., and Inula L [41, 42]. In the present dataset, nutrient tissue dimensions (X12, X13) contributed substantially to variation along the second PCA axis (7.2% of the variance). Among the taxa examined, T. graminifolius has the smallest nutrient tissue area, whereas T. pterocarpus, which has one of the largest, may be distinguished from closely related species (Table 3, Fig. 5). Molecular and morphological evidence has led to notable revisions in the taxonomy of Tragopogon in Türkiye. Although Matthews (1975) treated T. porrifolius and T. longirostris Sch. Bip., including var. longirostris and var. abbreviatus, as separate species in the Flora of Turkey and the East Aegean Islands [7], Coşkunçelebi and Gültepe (2012) combined these taxa under the three subspecies of T. porrifolius [15]. However, Coşkunçelebi and Gültepe (2012), subsequent studies raised T. porrifolius subsp. abbreviatus and T. porrifolius subsp. longirostris to species rank [22]. Coşkunçelebi et al. (2020) also further indicated that the currently accepted name for T. porrifolius subsp. longirostris is T. coelesyriacus [22]. The dendrogram derived from anatomical characters (Fig. 5) is consistent with these taxonomic revisions [18, 22]. Accordingly, T. abbreviatus is placed in Type III, whereas T. coelesyriacus (= T. porrifolius subsp. longirostris) and T. porrifolius subsp. eriospermus are grouped within Type IV.

Certain closely related taxa, including, T. pterocarpus, T. pterodes, and T. coloratus show morphological and molecular affinities [7, 19, 20]. Despite both T. pterocarpus and T. pterodes being winged, the former can be readily distinguished by its purplish-tinged pappus. Anatomically, the two species are assigned to Type I; however, they are not closely associated with each other in the dendrogram (Fig. 5). These closely related taxa also differ from each other in key features (X4: the widest part of the cypsela and X5: the narrowest part of the cypsela), which contribute to the observed variation. T. coloratus can also be distinguished from both species based on differences in anatomical dimensions. These findings again support Sukhorukov and Nilova (2015) [12], who highlighted cypsela diameter as a reliable diagnostic feature.

Another consistent character across all taxa was the presence of air cavities in the secondary ribs, previously reported in Tragopogon [10, 12]. While Sukhorukov and Nilova (2015) [12] noted a range of 60–150 μm, our study found a broader variation (44–250 μm), largely associated with cypsela size (Table 3). Importantly, T. oligolepis exhibited a distinctive anatomical pattern characterized by a rather rounded cross-section and the presence of air cavities within the primary ribs, which may be useful for distinguishing it from closely related taxa.

Sectional classifications of Tragopogon have historically relied on cypsela morphology, flower color, and phyllary length [8, 43]. Although Matthews (1975) [7] did not provide sectional divisions for Turkish taxa, later studies [18] suggested sectional placements. Interestingly, sectional assignments were not always consistent with anatomical groupings. For example, T. pterocarpus and T. coloratus were investigated in the sect. Chromopappus Boriss [8]. were placed in different fruit types (Type I and Type II), while T. latifolius and T. olympicus (sect. Sosnovskya Boriss.) clustered with T. reticulatus and T. graminifolius from other sections. On the other hand, some results showed strong congruence, such as the clustering of T. abbreviatus, T. davutii-turanii, T. dubius, and T. pichleri (Type III), which is consistent with molecular phylogenies [19, 20]. The partial concordance, together with the observed discrepancies, supports previous studies [2, 6, 12] and highlights both the diagnostic potential and the limitations of carpological traits in sectional taxonomy.

Since each taxon was represented by voucher material from a single population, intraspecific anatomical variation could not be fully assessed. This limitation is particularly relevant for quantitative traits such as cypsela size, tissue thickness, and air cavity dimensions, which may exhibit variation in response to environmental conditions or population-level differences. Nevertheless, the consistent expression of taxonomically informative characters among the examined samples suggests that cypsela anatomy provides useful information for interspecific comparisons within Tragopogon. In particular, qualitative features such as the general organization of pericarp tissues, rib structure, and the presence or absence of air cavities showed low variation within taxa and may represent relatively stable anatomical markers. The multivariate analyses conducted in this study should be considered exploratory and descriptive rather than confirmatory, and the results should be interpreted with caution in conjunction with other morphological and molecular evidence.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that fruit anatomy provides useful characters for differentiating closely related species of Tragopogon, although it is insufficient for complete sectional delimitation. Based on 13 anatomical traits, four main fruit groups can be recognized. PCA revealed that the widest and the narrowest parts of the cypselas in cross-section, including primary ribs and pericarp width in the primary rib regions, are among the most informative traits for grouping the investigated Tragopogon taxa. While fruit anatomy alone cannot fully resolve relationships, it can provide a valuable complement to morphological and molecular data. Future studies incorporating multiple populations per taxon and controlled experimental approaches (common garden conditions) will be essential to evaluate the stability and taxonomic significance of these anatomical traits. Thus, the results suggest the taxonomic relevance of cypsela anatomical traits at the species level and their supporting role in sectional systematics when integrated with other datasets.
