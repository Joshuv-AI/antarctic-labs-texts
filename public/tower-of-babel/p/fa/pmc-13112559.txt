Case Presentation

A 34-year-old man diagnosed with Marfan syndrome at age 8 years, with a strong family history (father with Marfan syndrome and a descending thoracic aortic aneurysm who died suddenly at 38 years of age, brother with Marfan syndrome and aortopathy, surgically treated at age 11 years), presented with 6 months of exertional dyspnea, fatigue, and palpitations (NYHA functional class II). His past medical history included ectopia lentis requiring lens removal during childhood. Genetic testing was not performed in the index patient or affected family members; however, the diagnosis of Marfan syndrome was established clinically in accordance with the Revised Ghent Criteria (2010), with a systemic score of 10, consistent with significant systemic involvement.Take-Home Messages•Extreme late presentation of Marfan aortopathy transforms a standardized prophylactic operation into a technically demanding procedure with significant clinical consequences, even when repair is successful.•Delayed intervention permits progression to advanced disease with preventable morbidity, underscoring the importance of timely, guideline-directed management.

Initial presentation (age 22 years)

At age 22 years, he presented with dyspnea (NYHA functional class II). Clinical examination revealed tall habitus, an increased arm-span-to-height ratio, positive wrist and thumb signs, scoliosis, and pectus carinatum.

Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a 63-mm aortic root aneurysm with chronic dissection, a partially thrombosed false lumen, moderate aortic regurgitation (AR), mild mitral regurgitation, and preserved left ventricular (LV) function. Computed tomography (CT) aortography demonstrated a well-defined saccular pseudoaneurysm arising from the anterolateral wall of the ascending aorta, immediately above the right coronary artery ostium (within 3 mm). The pseudoaneurysm measured 7.7 × 3.3 × 5.9 cm (transverse × anteroposterior × craniocaudal), with a neck measuring 3.7 × 3.9 cm (anteroposterior × craniocaudal). Partial intraluminal thrombosis was present, and the inferior extent of the pseudoaneurysm reached up to the level of the aortic annulus (Figures 1A to 1C). Re-presentation (age 34 years)

He returned with palpitations, fatigue, and dyspnea on exertion (NYHA functional class II). Blood pressure differed significantly between the upper and lower limbs (right upper limb [172/80 mm Hg] and right lower limb [213/120 mm Hg]). Cardiac examination revealed a displaced apex beat and a high-frequency early diastolic murmur in the neoaortic area. An electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation (AF) with rapid ventricular rate. Operative Course and Follow-Up

The patient underwent a modified Bentall procedure using a 27-mm TTK Chitra mechanical valve (TTK Healthcare Limited) mounted within a 28-mm AlboGraft polyester vascular graft, along with left atrial appendage exclusion (Figures 5, 6, and 7). Intraoperative assessment revealed massive aneurysmal dilatation of the aorta extending from the aortic root to the mid-ascending aorta, with a maximum diameter of approximately 100 mm. The aortic root and ascending aorta demonstrated chronic dissection with a thrombosed false lumen predominantly involving the right and noncoronary sinuses. After systemic heparinization, the distal ascending aorta, superior vena cava, and inferior vena cava were cannulated, and cardiopulmonary bypass was established. The ascending aorta was transected distal to the dilated segment. The aortic root was completely excised, and the organized thrombus within the false lumen was carefully evacuated (Figure 6). Both coronary ostia were mobilized as coronary buttons, and stay sutures were placed for controlled handling.

The native aortic valve was excised, and the annulus was sized. A total of 29 pledgeted 2-0 Ethibond sutures were placed circumferentially at the annulus. A composite valve-graft conduit was constructed using a 27-mm TTK Chitra mechanical valve within a 28-mm AlboGraft polyester graft (Figure 7). The composite conduit was seated and secured to the annulus. Reimplantation of the left and right coronary buttons was performed using continuous 5-0 polypropylene sutures.

The distal anastomosis between the composite graft and the native ascending aorta was completed using 4-0 polypropylene sutures with circumferential polytetrafluoroethylene felt reinforcement, given the friability of the Marfan aortic tissue and the presence of chronic dissection. The patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass uneventfully with low-dose noradrenaline support.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit, extubated the following day, and inotropic support was gradually tapered. Postoperative 2D TTE demonstrated a well-functioning prosthetic valve with no residual regurgitation and a mean transvalvular gradient of 6 mm Hg (Figures 8A to 8D). CT aortography demonstrated an intact surgical repair with no evidence of suture dehiscence. The graft and prosthetic valve were well positioned and functioning normally. Residual thrombus was noted along the anterolateral and posterolateral aspects of the repaired segment, with no residual dissection flap identified (Figures 9A to 9C). Discussion

Marfan syndrome is characterized by progressive aortic root dilatation with a well-established relationship between aortic dimensions and the risks of dissection, rupture, and sudden death.1 Event rates rise sharply once the aortic root exceeds 50 mm, forming the basis for European Society of Cardiology and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommendations advocating prophylactic aortic root replacement at or before this threshold.2 The presence of chronic dissection further magnifies the risk of catastrophic events and lowers operative thresholds, given the markedly increased mortality associated with medically managed chronic type A dissections.3 From a natural history perspective, a chronically dissected aortic root aneurysm exceeding 110 mm would generally be considered incompatible with long-term survival without operative repair.4

Against this background, the present case represents an extreme deviation from expected disease behavior. Survival for more than a decade with a progressively enlarging, chronically dissected Marfan aortic root aneurysm constitutes a rare survivorship outlier.5 Importantly, the absence of structured surveillance in this patient did not confer protection or stability; instead, it eliminated opportunities for timely elective intervention, allowing the disease to progress unchecked to an advanced and surgically hostile stage. Large cohort studies consistently demonstrate steep risk inflection points, with ascending aortic diameters ≥60 mm in general populations associated with rupture or dissection rates of 3% to 14% per year and substantially lower thresholds in Marfan syndrome.6

The principal educational value of this case lies not in surveillance failure but in the clinical and surgical consequences of extreme late presentation. Loss to follow-up permitted progression to severe AR, LV dilatation, systolic dysfunction, and AF—findings consistent with longstanding volume overload and adverse myocardial remodeling.7 These morbidities were not inevitable consequences of Marfan syndrome itself but predictable sequelae of advanced, untreated disease.

Advanced late presentation of Marfan aortopathy also poses significant challenges in clinical management before surgery. Chronic severe AR, ventricular dilatation, reduced systolic function, and AF complicate hemodynamic stabilization and increase perioperative risk. Clinical decision-making becomes reactive rather than preventive, focusing on balancing rhythm control, blood pressure optimization, and heart failure management while preparing for high-risk surgical intervention.8

In classical Marfan syndrome, aneurysmal disease predominantly involves the aortic root while sparing the tubular ascending aorta, producing the characteristic “onion-bulb” configuration.9 This preserved distal anatomy allows secure cannulation and distal anastomosis onto relatively normal tissue, making prophylactic root replacement technically predictable and comparatively safe. In contrast, long-standing, untreated disease in this patient resulted in diffuse aneurysmal involvement extending beyond the root into the ascending aorta, abolishing normal anatomic landmarks and eliminating a safe distal landing zone.

Marfan aortic tissue is intrinsically thin and friable due to fibrillin-1–related structural weakness, predisposing it to dissection and suture-line failure.10 These challenges are amplified in the presence of healed chronic dissection, where tissue planes are distorted and wall integrity is unpredictable. In such settings, surgical tolerance for error is minimal, and reinforcement of anastomotic suture lines with polytetrafluoroethylene felt becomes essential.

In summary, this case highlights the limitations of surgical and clinical management when Marfan-associated aortic disease presents at an extreme, advanced stage. Delayed intervention converts a technically straightforward prophylactic operation into a complex, high-risk surgical undertaking with preventable morbidity. Early, guideline-directed intervention remains essential to preserve favorable anatomy and ensure safer, more predictable surgical outcomes.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the profound surgical and clinical challenges encountered when Marfan-associated aortic disease presents at an extreme, advanced stage after prolonged absence of care. Long-standing progression resulted in loss of favorable aortic anatomy, diffuse involvement beyond the root, and extreme tissue fragility, converting an otherwise standardized prophylactic operation into a complex, high-risk surgical undertaking. Although survival to this stage was exceptional, delayed intervention led to significant and preventable morbidity, including severe AR, ventricular remodeling, and AF. This rare survivorship reinforces that timely, guideline-directed intervention remains essential to preserve operability and optimize outcomes in Marfan aortopathy.
