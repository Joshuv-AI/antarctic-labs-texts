Ultrasound-guided distal peripheral nerve blocks of the ulnar, median, and radial nerves in hand and wrist surgery: a descriptive case series
Authors: Adriana Vivas, John J Heifner, Angel P Saavedra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the increasing prevalence of surgical centers, identifying the safest and most efficient anesthetic techniques is vital. Ultrasound-guided distal peripheral nerve block (US-DPNB) is a technique that has shown promise in published literature for blocking the ulnar, median, and radial nerves at the level of the forearm. This descriptive case series examines 50 patients who underwent hand or wrist surgery with a focus on outcomes following the application of US-DPNB. The technique used in this study was performed by an anesthesiologist, Dr. Angel Saavedra, at an outpatient surgical center. The purpose of this descriptive case series is to describe beneficial clinical outcomes and the feasibility of blocking the median, ulnar, and radial nerves at the level of the forearm in the context of hand and wrist surgeries in surgical centers.MethodsThe primary outcomes included a pain scale from 1 to 5, block performance time, and postoperative complications. The secondary outcomes are tourniquet time, patient satisfaction, and return of hand movement. With IRB approval, patients were followed up for up to five days post-surgery to assess outcomes. Either a distal or proximal block was performed at the level of the forearm. Exclusion criteria included repeat surgery on the same hand, procedures expected to exceed 20 min of tourniquet time, and patients with pre-existing peripheral nerve disorders, or prior surgeries affecting nerve anatomy. The pain scores decreased in both groups over the five-day follow-up period. Notably, the only recorded complication was postoperative nausea in one patient in the PFB group.ConclusionThis descriptive case series demonstrates the feasibility of US-DPNB as a primary anesthetic technique for short-duration ambulatory hand and wrist procedures, with low complication rates and high patient satisfaction. Background

Regional anesthesia is increasingly utilized as surgical procedures transition from hospitals to outpatient centers (Patel et al. 2015). Compared with general anesthesia, regional anesthesia facilitates higher turnover rates and is particularly advantageous for patients with severe comorbidities. It can ultimately offer significant economic benefits and provides comparable, if not superior, pain relief to general anesthesia alone (Graff et al. 2023, Hagert and Rein 2024, Wong et al. 2020). Graff et al. 2023 noted that regional anesthesia is more cost-effective than general anesthesia because of shorter operating room durations, reduced time in the post anesthesia care unit (PACU), and decreased opioid use. Thus, regional anesthesia provides both clinical and economic advantages.

Among these techniques, blocks at the level of the forearm—ultrasound-guided distal peripheral nerve blocks (US-DPNB)—are especially beneficial. They avoid critical structures, such as the carotid artery and the phrenic nerve, and provide effective postoperative pain control (Sehmbi et al. 2015, Li et al. 2020), making them valuable for high-risk populations with multiple comorbidities. Ultrasound guidance enhances precision by allowing controlled anesthetic administration and direct visualization of vital structures. Another notable advantage of US-DPNBs is the faster return of motor function (Lam et al. 2014), which is essential for surgeries such as trigger finger repair, where intraoperative assessment of active finger flexion is required, and for recovery. This has been associated with high patient satisfaction (Lam et al. 2014). Despite these benefits, US-DPNBs have historically been underutilized as the sole anesthetic for hand and wrist surgeries, instead being used adjunctively with proximal blocks to manage postoperative pain (Sehmbi et al. 2015, Soberón et al. 2015). Evidence supporting the positive patient response to US-DPNBs in hand and wrist surgeries, particularly in ambulatory settings, remains limited.

The purpose of this study is to describe the efficiency and effectiveness of US-DPNB in an ambulatory surgical setting. This study reports patient experiences of a forearm-level nerve block approach targeting the median, ulnar, and radial nerves. The primary outcomes included postoperative pain, block performance time, and complications. The secondary outcomes included tourniquet time, time to extrinsic motor function return, and patient satisfaction. This study seeks to provide further evidence for the clinical utility of US-DPNBs and their role in optimizing patient outcomes in ambulatory settings.

Methods

We conducted an IRB-approved case series of 50 patients from June to November 2022 who presented to the surgical center, Miami Bone and Joint Institute, for hand or wrist surgeries qualifying for US-PNB. Eligibility criteria included procedures with a tourniquet time of less than 20 min or no tourniquet requirement to avoid anoxic pain (Association 2018, Masri et al. 2020). We discussed with the surgeon prior to obtaining consent to ensure the surgery will be less than 20 min and if the surgeon agreed with the anesthetic technique. Exclusion criteria included patients with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy or nerve disorders, prior surgeries known to affect the anatomy of the median, ulnar, or radial nerves, and patients with diagnosed neurological conditions that could confound block assessment. Patients who were deemed unfit for surgery, including patients with unstable blood pressures or heart rates, were canceled. The minimum age was 18. Table 1 summarizes the patient demographics.

The included surgeries consisted of trigger finger release, carpal tunnel release, incision and drainage (I&D) of right index finger, hardware removal, mass removal, bone spur excision (ostectomy), tenosynovectomy, and foreign body removal. The surgeries were performed by one of the three surgeons at the surgical center, and US-DPNB was administered by the same anesthesiologist. Standard preoperative monitoring was performed via 5-lead ECG, blood pressure monitoring, pulse oximetry, and peripheral intravenous access. Prior to surgery, patients were explained the purpose of the study. Verbal consent to call the patient after surgery and to record data during and after surgery was obtained preoperatively. Additionally, patient demographics were also obtained. Patients were then transferred to the operating room, where ASA standard monitoring was initiated.

The block performance time was recorded from the moment the ultrasound probe contacted the patient until the withdrawal of the needle after the final nerve block. The start and end times of the block procedure were also documented. An upper-arm tourniquet was applied and inflated upon the surgeon’s indication. An upper-arm tourniquet was used in both DFB and PFB. All procedures lasted less than 20 min, ensuring that tourniquet use was not contraindicated (Association 2018). The return of motor function was calculated from the time the block ended to when movement of all fingers started again. To assess return of motor function, the patient was asked to try to open and close their hands, assessing extrinsic hand movement. If movement was assessed intraoperatively, it was done after the tourniquet was deflated and the time was recorded.

Postoperatively, patients were transferred to the PACU, where pain scores and motor function were assessed. If motor function had not returned by the time they were discharged, patients were asked to report the time motor recovery started the following day, which was calculated from the block end time. Patients were interviewed postoperatively, on the phone, on Days 1, 3, and 5 to assess pain levels on a numerical pain score from 1 to 5, how much and if any analgesic was use, and overall satisfaction on a scale from 1 to 10. The recorded complications included postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), block re-administration intra-operatively, and opioid administration in the PACU. Patients were discharged once awake, someone was there to pick them up and hemodynamically stable. Patients were discharged with prescribed pain medication as needed for 5 days. They were told to avoid non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Acetaminophen was acceptable.

Statistical analysis was conducted using descriptive statistics only, including means, standard deviations, and medians of the results. The data are displayed in Table 3. No inferential statistical comparisons were performed between the DFB and PFB groups; the data are reported descriptively to characterize outcomes within each group.

Case description

The nerves blocked were the median, ulnar, and radial nerves. The blocks were created at the forearm with ultrasound guidance and were categorized as either distal or proximal. Distal forearm blocks (DFBs) are defined as all three-nerve blocks performed distal to the tendinous portion of the flexor tendon and two finger breaths proximal to the wrist. This block spares motor blockade and is usually performed for trigger fingers where movement is assessed intraoperatively. Proximal forearm blocks (PFBs) are defined as three-nerve blocks performed proximal to the tendinous portion of the flexor tendon and three finger breaths distal to the antecubital fossa. The PFB blocks were ideally done distal to the anterior interosseus nerve. Patients were given midazolam and fentanyl before starting the blocks per the surgeons’ preferences, unless the patient was driving themselves after the surgery. A standard dose of 2mL of midazolam, and 50-100mcg fentanyl were given to each patient.

All blocks were performed by the same anesthesiologist, Dr. Angel Saavedra, and were assisted by one of two perioperative nurses. The surgeons varied daily. The ultrasound used was a SonoSite M-Turbo with a HFL50 SonoSite Linear Probe. First, the ultrasound probe was placed perpendicular to the forearm. The median nerve is first identified and then, via an in-plane approach with a 50 mm 20-gauge Stimuplex needle, pierces the skin. Once the skin is pierced, the tip of the needle is identified and then advanced at an angle so that the final resting point of the needle is dorsal to the median nerve. The perioperative nurse aspirates the syringe, and then 2-3mL of the anesthetic is injected circumferentially until a halo is formed around the nerve. Once the halo is achieved, the needle is withdrawn, and the next nerve is targeted. The ulnar nerve is located at the medial aspect of the forearm under the fascial compartment of the flexor carpi ulnaris and is usually located medially to the ulnar artery. The superficial radial nerve is located lateral to the radial artery. The trajectory of the needle is never intended to go directly toward the nerve but instead dorsal to the nerve, after which the block begins. Contact with the nerve was prohibited. The anesthesia consisted of 2% xylocaine and 0.5% bupivacaine in a 50:50 mixture. This mixture is a preference of the anesthesiologist. For carpal tunnel release surgeries (N = 23), local anesthesia is added superficially at the wrist with the same mixture with approximately 2 mL of the mixture, a cutaneous dose, requested by the surgeons.

The patient outcomes were recorded on a worksheet. Table 3 summarizes the mean, median, and count of each outcome from immediately postoperative to Day 5.

Results

A total of 50 patients (N = 50) participated in this study, and all underwent hand or wrist surgeries that were suitable for US-DPNB. Patient demographics and surgical procedures are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The mean patient age was 67.3 years (± 11.1), and the mean BMI was 29.6 (± 5.92). A total of 29 females and 21 males participated. Of the 50 blocks performed, 17 were DFB, and 33 were PFB. The outcomes are summarized in Table 3.

Primary outcomes

Block performance time

The block performance time was recorded for all 50 patients. For DFB, the mean performance time was 95.6 s (± 31.8). In comparison, PFB had a mean performance time of 72.8 s (± 15.7).

Pain score

Pain scores were assessed using a pain scale from 1 to 5 immediately postoperatively, as well as on postoperative Days 1, 3, and 5. Both the DFB and PFB groups reported a mean pain score of 1 immediately after surgery, indicating no pain. On Day 1 postop, the mean pain score increased slightly to 2.1 (± 1.14), and 23 patients (46%) required pain medication. By Day 3, the mean pain score had decreased to 1.36 (± 0.56), and only nine patients (18%) used pain medication. On Day 5, the pain scores remained low, with a mean of 1.46 (± 0.76), and only five patients (10%) reported taking pain medication. Pain was reported when the needle pierced the skin intra-operatively, but pain score was not recorded. It should be noted that peripheral nerve blocks typically provide analgesia for up to 24 h (Hannig et al. 2023); pain scores reported on Days 3 and 5 therefore reflect the postoperative pain trajectory after block resolution rather than any ongoing block effect.

Complications

There were few complications in this study. Postoperative nausea occurred in an 85-year-old female patient after an endoscopic carpal tunnel surgery (2%) in the PFB group. No patients required block re-administration or received opioids in the PACU.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes reported were tourniquet time, return of motor function, and patient satisfaction. As noted, “return of motor function” refers specifically to the recovery of extrinsic forearm muscle activity (i.e., active finger flexion and extension), as intrinsic hand muscles innervated distally was not specifically assessed. The mean tourniquet time in DFB patients was 8.18 min (± 3.56). In the PFB group, a tourniquet was used in only 25 of the 33 patients. The mean tourniquet time in the PFB was 7.56 min (± 4.16). The mean return of movement in the DFB was 21.1 min (± 10.8). In the PFB, the mean time was 104.9 min (± 202.2). The large standard deviation is due to one patient reporting her time of return of movement to be on the following day. Finally, on Day 5 of the study, patients were asked to rate their satisfaction on a scale from 1 to 10. In both groups, the mean level of satisfaction was 9.

Discussion

As the prevalence of ambulatory surgeries continues to rise, the need for safe, effective, and patient-centered anesthetic techniques become increasingly critical (Graff et al.. 2023, Lin et al. 2013). The present study describes the feasibility and preliminary outcomes of US-DPNB in hand and wrist surgeries at a single ambulatory center. As a descriptive case series without a comparator group, it cannot establish superiority or cost-effectiveness relative to other anesthetic strategies, but it contributes observational data to an area where prospective evidence remains limited. The key parameters used to assess anesthesia techniques include metrics such as block performance time and postoperative complications, which provide valuable insights into their safety profile and clinical outcomes, with operational efficiency as a secondary consideration. This is in line with Graff et al.’s findings (Graff et al. 2023), where fast block performance time was associated with a reduction in anesthesia-related expenses. The results demonstrate the efficiency of US-DPNB, with mean block performance times for the DFB and PFB being 95.59 s and 72.85 s, respectively. The low complication rate observed is consistent with a favorable safety profile, noting that no opioids are needed, and there is a minimal incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (Mazda et al. 2021). These findings are broadly consistent with prior published studies of distal forearm nerve blocks. Nijs et al. 2023 reported comparable block performance times and low complication rates in a randomized controlled trial comparing axillary brachial plexus block to distal forearm blocks for hand and wrist surgery, providing controlled evidence that contextualizes the descriptive outcomes of the present series. Similarly, Lam et al. 2014 demonstrated high patient satisfaction with distal peripheral nerve block anesthesia for outpatient hand surgery, which aligns with the satisfaction scores observed here. Direct comparisons between the present series and these studies are limited by the absence of a control arm and the heterogeneity of surgical procedures included.

The safety profile and recovery outcomes in this study demonstrate the important clinical advantages of US-DPNB. Controlled pain management postoperatively, the effectiveness of the block intraoperatively, and high patient satisfaction are advantages of US-DPNB. Our results revealed that US-DPNB effectively blocks sensation while allowing for partial motor function, thus resulting in high self-reported patient satisfaction (Lam et al. 2014). The results show that both DFB and PFB are equally as effective, with DFB having a faster return in motor function. Furthermore, postoperative pain control is crucial when selecting an anesthetic technique. By Day 5 of this study, there was a reduction in the mean pain score from two to one, with only five of the 50 patients still requiring analgesics. The combination of xylocaine and bupivacaine allows for a fast onset of analgesia and longer duration of nerve block. Bupivacaine has higher lipid solubility and tissue permeability, allowing for longer duration of analgesia (Yano et al. 2006). Overall, a rapid return of motor function and adequate pain control is important for common hand surgeries, as it facilitates early movement, rehabilitation, faster recovery, and a quicker return to daily activities (Richman et al. 2006).

The safety and pain management outcomes observed in this series are encouraging: zero PACU opioid administrations, one case of PONV (2%), and low pain scores through Day 5 (mean pain score 1.46). This opioid-sparing profile is clinically significant, as minimizing postoperative opioid exposure reduces risks of respiratory depression, sedation, and dependence, especially in a population with a mean ASA score of 2 and comorbidities including cardiac conditions and sleep apnea (Blake et al. 2009). Though without a comparator group, cost implications cannot be formally quantified. These findings suggest a potentially favorable opioid-sparing profile that, if confirmed in controlled studies, may translate into reduced medication costs and shorter recovery unit stays (Geralemou and Gan 2016). Improved pain management supports earlier rehabilitation, faster return to daily activities, and patient independence—outcomes that are intrinsically valuable and that may additionally support cost efficiencies as bundled payment models become more prevalent (Agarwal et al. 2020).

Beyond direct patient safety outcomes, regional anesthesia has also been shown to offer operational and economic benefits by decreasing turnover times and increasing efficiency in both hospital and outpatient settings. Using strategies such as parallel or “swing” rooms in ambulatory centers and dedicated block rooms in hospitals helps streamline operations and minimize operating room usage, translating into financial savings (Head et al. 2011, Mazda et al. 2021). For example, a study by Mazda et al. 2021 revealed that the implementation of a block room before surgery significantly reduced overall operating room time and anesthesia-controlled time, leading to lower hospital costs. Similarly, adopting parallel or swing rooms has been associated with faster turnover and the ability to accommodate more cases within the same timeframe (Nijs et al. 2023). When applied to hand and wrist surgeries, combining US-DPNB with parallel operating rooms enhances patient flow, optimizes hospital efficiency, and improves financial outcomes. However, it should be acknowledged that the routine availability of dedicated ultrasound equipment in the operating room represents a meaningful upfront capital cost, ongoing maintenance expense, and logistical consideration that is not universal across practice settings. In ambulatory or resource-limited environments, the absence of ultrasound infrastructure may limit the feasibility of this technique, and any discussion of economic advantage must account for these implementation costs.

Finally, ultrasound-guided anesthesia is a minimally invasive technique that offers notable clinical benefits, especially in high-risk populations. The patient population in this study included those with significant comorbidities, as evidenced by a mean ASA score of two in both groups. Many patients were on anticoagulants, had cardiac conditions, or had sleep apnea. Direct visualization with ultrasound improves anesthetic delivery, avoiding crucial structures with direct anatomical imaging (Li et al. 2020). Precise visualization of anatomical structures and better control of anesthetic distribution also enable efficient block performance, demonstrating the technique’s operational advantage. Our results revealed minimal complications while administering the blocks and no adverse effects.

We acknowledge certain limitations in this study. First, there was inconsistency in the surgeons performing the procedures, which may have influenced both the recovery instructions provided to patients and the duration of the surgeries. Second, the assessment of movement recovery was typically conducted post-surgery, except in cases involving trigger finger procedures. This means that the actual return of movement may have occurred earlier than when it was formally evaluated. Finally, pain is inherently subjective, making its assessment and interpretation variable and challenging to standardize. The pain scale from 1 to 5 is not a standard scale and could have benefited with a more broadly used 10-point scale. More broadly, the descriptive case series design without a control or comparator group precludes any conclusions regarding the superiority or equivalence of US-DPNB relative to other anesthetic strategies. The heterogeneity of surgical procedures included limits the interpretability and generalizability of the pooled outcomes, as each procedure carries distinct anesthetic goals and functional recovery profiles. Notably, local infiltration anesthesia represents a simpler, less resource-intensive alternative for several of the included procedures and is not discussed as a comparator. Future studies should address this comparison directly. Additionally, while DFB and PFB outcomes are reported separately, no inferential statistical comparison between groups was performed, meaning observed numeric differences cannot be interpreted as statistically or clinically significant. Future studies can address whether there is a clinical difference. Future research should be conducted for further evidence of the use of US-DPNB as a valuable anesthetic technique that benefits both patients and surgical centers. Further studies should compare the outcomes of the technique reported in this paper to those of proximal blocks, such as infraclavicular blocks.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the literature and the present series support the safety, clinical efficacy, and patient-centered outcomes of US-DPNB in hand and wrist surgery. The use of US-DPNB provides clinical benefits that further support its role in patient safety. As the volume of these procedures continues to increase, US-DPNB offers a practical and safe solution that prioritizes patient wellbeing and clinical outcomes, while also supporting operational efficiency and reduced healthcare costs.
