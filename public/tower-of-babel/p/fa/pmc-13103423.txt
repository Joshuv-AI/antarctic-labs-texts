Genetic diversity and conservation implications for European Griffon Vultures (Gyps fulvus): Insights from mitochondrial D-loop HVR1
Authors: Paolo Mereu, Slobodan Davidović, Monica Pirastru, Eneko Arrondo, Antoni Margalida, Fabio Scarpa, Guillermo Blanco, Pilar Oliva-Vidal, José María Martínez, Marija Tanasković, Irena Hribšek, Torsten Günther, Pedro Morell Miranda, José Antonio Donázar, Ainara Cortés-Avizanda, Jose Antonio Sanchez Zapata, Dobromir Dobrev, Volen Arkumarev, Anton Stamenov, Stavros Xirouchakis, Yael Choresh, Ohad Hatzofe, Eleftherios Hadjisterkotis, Goran Sušić, Marco Muzzeddu, Luisa Bogliolo, Salvatore Naitana, Giovanni Giuseppe Leoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the genetic diversity of Griffon Vultures (Gyps fulvus) from two South - East European, and seven Mediterranean countries and islands, including Spain, which hosts the largest European population. Our sample, comprising 249 newly generated plus 92 public D-loop sequences, is the largest dataset so far analyzed for this species. We identified 14 novel haplotypes, with Hpt-A being predominant across populations. Exclusive haplotypes have been identified in the populations from Spain, Serbia, Israel, Croatia, and Sardinia, suggesting them as worthy of targeted conservation programs to preserve such haplotypes. Principal coordinate analysis identified a big cluster composed of populations from continental Europe and the Aegean islands that were clearly differentiated from Western Mediterranean and Middle East populations. This structure would suggest the Serbian population is potentially a good source for restocking efforts in the Eastern Mediterranean. In addition, results indicated that historical restocking actions with Spanish individuals have significantly influenced genetic composition in the insular population of Sardinia, and provided evidence consistent with possible hybridization between Griffon and Rüppell’s Vultures (Gyps rueppellii) in Spain. This raises concerns about the use of non-genotyped Spanish individuals for restocking in populations like the Eastern Mediterranean ones, where contact zones between the two species have never been reported.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43926-6.Keywords: Conservation genetics, D-loop region, European Griffon Vulture, Population genetics, Hybridization, RestockingSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Genetics

Introduction

Over the past decades, the conservation of animal species has become a major priority in biodiversity preservation. Central to these efforts is the analysis and management of genetic diversity within and among populations, which represent fundamental steps in the conservation of animal genetic resources at regional and global scales1,2. In avian species, dispersal and long-distance migration play a pivotal role in shaping genetic diversity, as they facilitate the colonization of diverse ecological niches and contribute to broad geographic distributions. The study of genetic diversity is therefore fundamental to identify evolutionarily significant units (ESUs) - reproductively isolated populations within a species - that have evolved in response to substantially different environmental conditions and should be managed separately to preserve the species’ evolutionary legacy3.

The Eurasian Griffon Vulture (Gyps fulvus, Linnaeus 1758) is an obligate scavenger species that plays a key role in ecosystem functioning by feeding mostly on large animal carcasses and thus helping to eliminate pathogenic bacteria from the environment and counteract the transmission of diseases4–6. This keystone species sustains the complex web of ecological interactions within an ecosystem and has a large influence on its natural environment. Once widespread and abundant across South-West and South-East Europe, the Middle East, Central Asia, and, at least formerly, North Africa where it is now at risk of extinction as breeding species7, the Griffon Vulture experienced a severe decline in the middle of the 20th century, bringing many populations in Europe and Asia to the brink of extinction5,6,8–12. As a result, genetic variability unique to specific Griffon Vulture populations may have been reduced or irreversibly lost. This population decline, which in turn likely affected the original genetic variability, was driven by several factors related to human activities, including illegal killing, electrocution, the use of poisoned baits to control livestock predators, exposure to lead poisoning and the reduction of natural food sources11,13–15. Due to this decline, in many countries the Griffon Vulture populations became highly threatened and, in some cases, endangered. Hence, in the last decades, the species has been the subject of conservation programs which have led to the translocation of individuals to restore the original population size of some native populations, as in the case of France, Sardinia, Central Bulgaria, and Cyprus, and to repopulate areas where the species had disappeared (i.e., Italian Peninsula) (http://www.lifepluto.it/en/adus-activities/griffon-vulture-gyps-fulvus.html;)16–19.

Restockings and reintroductions in Europe

Historically, the first Griffon Vulture population recovery program took place in France, where the species was on the brink of extinction, with only about 60 individuals remaining in the far west of the French Pyrenees. Between 1981 and 2007, nearly 350 Griffon Vultures imported from Spain were released into the wild. Today, France hosts the second-largest Griffon Vulture population in Europe with around 2,500 breeding pairs distributed from the Pyrenees to the Southern Alps and the southern part of the Massif Central (https://4vultures.org/blog/updates-on-breeding-results-from-all-four-vulture-species-in-france/)20. In Bulgaria, the species was considered extinct in the 1960s until 28 birds, along with one breeding pair were discovered in the Rhodope Mountains in 197821. To enhance the recovery of the species in former breeding areas, a total of 464 birds imported mainly from Spain and France have been reintroduced to several areas of the country between 2009 and 202422. The local population is gradually increasing andin 2019 reached 130 breeding pairs, with an estimated number of 325 individuals12(https://4vultures.org/blog/griffon-vulture-in-the-balkans-insights-from-the-2023-census/). In Sardinia, the species was once widespread across the entire region, with an estimated population of 800 to 1,200 individuals up until the late 1940s, before the drastic decline that in the 1970s restricted the species distribution range to the north-western part of the island. By 2013, a survey revealed only 30 territorial pairs and around 130 individuals remaining. To prevent the extinction of the Sardinian Griffon population, 123 birds were released from Spain and France starting in 1986. The most recent census, conducted in 2023, estimates the population size to be between 332 and 378 individuals (https://4vultures.org/blog/2023-griffon-vulture-census-in-sardinia-reveals-population-trends-breeding-success/). The Griffon Vulture was once a common and widespread species also in Cyprus, with hundreds soaring across the skies until the early 1960s. By the mid-1990s, however, the population had dropped to just 20–30 pairs, and between 2011 and 2012 only 10–12 individuals remained. To support recovery, 25 vultures from Crete were released in 2015, followed by 14 more from Spain in 2023 (www.4vultures.org/Vulture Conservation Foundation (VCF)). Despite these efforts, the population in Cyprus remains critically low, withless than 20 individuals left today. This makes Cyprus home to the smallest breeding population of Griffon Vultures in Europe7. The current population size of the main European Griffon Vulture populations is reported in Table 1.

Conservation translocations benefits

Thanks to these interventions and to the implementation of conservation measures, notably campaigns to minimize poisoning and provide safe food at vulture restaurants, today the global population of the Griffon Vulture is gradually increasing, with a current population size estimate of 80,000–900,000 mature individuals7,18. The European population is estimated at 34,800 − 44,700 breeding pairs (69,600 − 89,400 mature individuals)18, with Spain alone holding 31,000 pairs (95,930 − 122,542 mature individuals)23. The Griffon Vulture range has also expanded thanks to reintroduction projects in France, Italy and the Balkans24 and the species is listed on the IUCN Red List as Least Concern18.

Forces driving Griffon Vulture genetic diversity and impact of restocking on species conservation

The genetic variability of Griffon Vulture populations is shaped by three main factors: flight style, social behavior and conservation translocation actions such as restockings and reintroductions25,26.

Flight evolved in birds as a fast and dynamic mode of locomotion, involving three-dimensional distance units27–31. Large birds, such as vultures, rely predominantly on gliding and soaring, with wings fixed and spread30. Because of this flying style, large bodies of water constitute an important barrier to the exploration of new territories and the exchange of individuals between populations, as in the case of the Mediterranean islands. This isolation has been linked to the establishment of exclusive variants or private alleles which, over time, have contributed to genetic distinctiveness, as previously reported for the island populations of Sardinian and Cretan Griffon Vulture, and Canarian Egyptian Vulture (Neophron percnopterus)5,32.

Besides flight, social behavior is one of the key natural factors influencing genetic variability in vultures6,33,34. The Griffon Vulture is a colonial bird with high nest site fidelity driven by natal philopatry33,35 that acts as a virtual geographical barrier reducing the dispersal rates and favoring the formation of stable colonies and the fixation of private alleles and exclusive traits, as reported for the Griffon Vulture population in Serbia33,35. Such behavior appears to be advantageous in intrasexual competition for territories as familiarity with an area offers a competitive frame36,37, enhancing individual’s ability to find food, select the best nest site, and take care of offspring, all necessary aspects for breeding success38,39. This fidelity to nesting site contributes to the site-specific genetic variability detected among Griffon Vulture populations across different geographical areas33,35. Although the Griffon Vulture shows a sedentary behavior, the dispersal behavior of the immature individuals, driven by overpopulation or reduction in food availability, enables the species to successfully colonize new areas6,40.

The third factor that has undoubtedly contributed to modelling the current intra- and inter-population variability in the Eurasian Griffon Vulture is the implementation of human-mediated programs (i.e., conservation translocation), which have been carried out in the last decades, aimed at both restocking small populations and repopulating areas where native populations had become extinct. These programs have contributed to range expansion and population stabilization and increase at various sites in Europe12,22. Animal restocking is a widely used conservation tool25,26, usually applied to species with declining populations to restore relict populations and increase the abundance and distribution of species in the wild. In addition to its potential benefits, including a possible increase in intra-population diversity, restocking may also entail genetic risks if the genetic composition of both source and recipient populations is not fully characterized. In particular, the release of non-genotyped individuals may lead to the loss of locally rare alleles or to outbreeding depression41,42.

Genetic diversity and conservation programs

Genetic distance estimation can be a valuable tool for detecting genetic distinctiveness and structure at the intra-specific level43–45 and should be considered a mandatory preliminary step before starting any restocking actions in areas where the native population still exists.

Previous studies based on the use of microsatellite markers (STR) investigated the genetic variability of Griffon Vulture populations from some regions of the Mediterranean Basin. In 2008, Le Gouar et al.46 performed a study on three native Griffon Vulture colonies located in Israel, Croatia, and the French Pyrenees (Ossau), as well as on a reintroduced colony established in the southern Massif Central, France (Causses). They reported comparable levels of genetic diversity between native and reintroduced populations, with overall diversity higher in Griffon Vulture than in other European vulture species. Among native populations, genetic differentiation was generally low, except for the Croatian colony, which showed significant divergence, likely as a result of recent isolation. Importantly, no loss of genetic diversity was detected in the reintroduced Causses population, a pattern attributed to the high variability of the founding individuals and subsequent immigration from nearby populations. Indeed, substantial gene flow from Spain to Causses was documented, contributing to the maintenance of genetic diversity. Based on these findings, the authors concluded that random founder sampling can be effective for Griffon Vultures but may not be appropriate for species characterized by lower genetic diversity. One year later, Arshad et al.47 analyzed the genetic variation of four Gyps species, including G. fulvus. They detected low genetic differentiation among Griffon Vulture populations, indicating high mobility and substantial gene flow. Moreover, populations of Griffon Vultures from Spain and Israel were found to share their gene pool with other Gyps species (G. africanus, G. bengalensis, and G. indicus), possibly reflecting either hybridization or retention of ancestral polymorphisms. Based on these results, the authors emphasized that conservation strategies should prioritize the maintenance of genetic diversity and the management of gene flow to ensure the long-term survival of the species.​.

More recently, a study analyzing the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) D-loop region, carried out on three Griffon Vulture populations from the Mediterranean islands of Crete, Cyprus, and Sardinia, - the two latter were restocked - revealed a moderate level of genetic differentiation among these populations attributed to limited matrilineal gene flow, due to the reluctance of this species to cross the sea5,8. In addition, the D-loop haplotypes (Hpt) composition of the Sardinian population was found to be strongly influenced by the restocking actions with Spanish founders which significantly altered the haplotype frequencies detected in the native population8. In 2020, Davidovic et al.33 pointed out the occurrence of two genetic clusters of Griffon Vulture populations: one dominant in the Balkans, including Serbia and Croatia, and another in the Iberian Peninsula (Pyrenees and introduced populations). Two years later, a study analyzing 169 samples from Serbian Griffon Vultures revealed, using both STR markers and mtDNA sequences (D-loop and Cyt B), relatively high levels of inbreeding, with each individual having, on average, three close relatives48. Nevertheless, the population exhibited a balanced sex ratio and maintained significant genetic diversity, indicating overall genetic stability and health. The same year, Davidovic et al.35 analyzed Cyt B gene sequences from 58 Serbian Griffon Vultures to assess genetic diversity and differentiation, emphasizing their relevance for the design of effective conservation and management strategies in endangered species. Although the Serbian population displayed relatively low genetic diversity compared to other populations - harboring three haplotypes (two unique and one shared with Turkey and Sardinia) - it was nonetheless genetically stable and potentially suitable as a source for restocking and reintroduction programs within the Balkan Peninsula. The significant genetic differentiation detected between Serbian and Spanish populations raises the concern that translocations from geographically distant populations, such as Spain, could involve genetic and environmental mismatches. These findings therefore support the consideration of genetically and geographically proximate populations as candidate sources for restocking, while emphasizing that final management decisions should be based on integrated genetic assessments with the use of complementary nuclear markers, and broader ecological, demographic, and logistical considerations, to maximize the success of reintroduction efforts.​.

To date, no genetic analyses have been carried out on the entire Mediterranean and Continental Europe range of the Griffon Vultures. Importantly, this gap of sampling includes the Spanish Griffon population, which is the largest in the world and has never been extensively sampled so far, although previous studies included some samples from Spain in their analyses46,47. This lack of genetic data prevents us from drawing robust conclusions about the genetic structure and diversity of Griffon Vultures in Europe, knowledge which is also crucial for making predictions on the impact resulting from restocking actions on the genetic variability of the recipient populations. To address this knowledge gap; we sequenced and analyzed the HVR1 region of the D-loop of 249 European Griffon Vultures from nine different Mediterranean and South-East Europe populations (Table S1). Together with an additional 92 publicly available sequences, we comprise the largest genetic dataset analyzed for the Griffon Vulture so far providing us with unprecedented insights into their genetic diversity and population structure in the Mediterranean Basin and Southeastern Europe.

Results

Genetic diversity estimation

The 249 sequences produced in the present study were deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers PV539803-540051. Overall, 18 haplotypes defined by 21 polymorphic sites were found among 341 Griffon Vulture individuals (Table S2). The average nucleotide composition, one of the simplest ways of characterizing and comparing genomes across species, was 31.6% T, 24.3% C, 24.4% A, and 19.7% G.

A rarefaction–extrapolation analysis was carried out to compare haplotype richness across populations while accounting for unequal sample sizes. Sample-size-based rarefaction and extrapolation curves are shown in Figure S1, coverage-based rarefaction curves in Figure S2, and the relationship between sample size and sample coverage in Figure S3.

For details on numerical summary of coverage-standardized iNEXT estimates of haplotype richness with 95% confidence intervals see Table S3. In general, despite large differences in sample size, rarefaction curves largely overlap among several populations, indicating comparable levels of haplotype richness. More specifically, the sample-size-based curves (species diversity vs. number of individuals) revealed that most populations (i.e., Crete, Cyprus, Israel, Serbia) rapidly reached an asymptote at low haplotype richness, indicating limited diversity that was almost fully captured by the available samples (Fig. S1). Sardinia displayed intermediate richness, higher than smaller populations but clearly below Spain. The Spanish population, in contrast, exhibited the highest haplotype richness, with its curve continuing to increase even at large sample sizes, suggesting the presence of additional rare haplotypes not yet sampled. The coverage-based curves shown in Figure S2 (sample coverage vs. number of individuals) confirmed this pattern, showing a rapid convergence toward high sample coverage for all populations, indicating that most haplotype diversity has been captured even in populations with smaller sample sizes. Almost all populations achieved sample coverage values close to 1 with relatively few individuals, reflecting that their haplotypic diversity had been nearly exhausted. Sardinia similarly showed rapid saturation, implying that its diversity was adequately sampled. Conversely, Spain reached complete coverage only at much larger sample sizes, supporting the view that its haplotype pool is considerably more complex and not yet fully represented. The coverage-based diversity curves (species diversity vs. sample coverage) enabled comparison at equivalent levels of sampling completeness. At high coverage (close to 1), the smaller populations remained clustered at low haplotype richness. Sardinia appeared slightly more diverse but already saturated, whereas Spain consistently exhibited much higher values of haplotype richness, even when controlling for sample coverage (Fig. S3).

Fourteen haplotypes were newly found, while four (Hpts A, B, C, and D) had been identified previously among the Griffon Vulture populations from the Mediterranean islands of Crete, Cyprus and Sardinia5,8. Hpt-A was the most common haplotype in all nine populations analyzed, occurring at a frequency of 76.8% across the entire dataset, followed by Hpt-C (9.4%), which was shared by seven populations, and Hpt-E (3.5%) (Table 2). Except for the seven Griffon Vultures from Cyprus, which carried a single haplotype, all populations harbored at least three haplotypes, as in the case of Bulgaria, Croatia, Crete, Naxos, and Sardinia (Table 2, Fig. S4). The highest haplotypes richness (n = 11) was observed in the Spain population, with seven unshared (or exclusive) haplotypes, followed by Serbia (seven haplotypes, two exclusive) and Israel (four haplotypes, one exclusive). Two further unshared haplotypes were identified: Hpt-B, previously described in Sardinia8, and Hpt-R, newly detected in Croatia.

Analysis of molecular variance and genetic differentiation

AMOVA results obtained considering all samples (non-grouped) suggested that most of the genetic variance was within populations (93.88%), with the remaining 6.12% among populations (Table 4a). To test population structure, several grouping strategies were applied. Populations grouped by geographical distribution with two different configurations produced the best results. In the first configuration, four groups were considered: (1) Middle East (Cyprus and Israel); (2) Balkan Peninsula and Eastern Mediterranean islands (Bulgaria, Serbia, Croatia, Crete, and Naxos); (3) Iberian Peninsula (Spain); (4) Western Mediterranean Island (Sardinia) (Table 4b). The second tested configuration included five groups, considering the Aegean islands of Crete and Naxos as an independent group (Table 4c).

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA)

The result of the PCoA analysis performed on a K2P genetic distance matrix using the mean distances among the nine Griffon Vulture populations is shown in Fig. 1. The first and second axes explained 56.77% and 20.99% of the total variance, respectively. The identified genetic clusters demonstrated complete congruence with the groupings established in the aforementioned AMOVA. Specifically, the “Middle East” cluster comprised the populations from Cyprus and Israel, while the “Continental Europe” cluster aligned with the “Balkan Peninsula” group (Bulgaria, Serbia, and Croatia). Furthermore, the “Aegean Island” cluster, encompassing Crete and Naxos, mirrored the composition of the “Eastern Mediterranean islands” group. Finally, the “Iberian Peninsula” and “Western Mediterranean Island” clusters corresponded exclusively to Spain and Sardinia, respectively (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Genetic diversity and structure

This study is the first to analyze such a large dataset for this species, with 341 D-loop sequences from two South-East European, seven Mediterranean countries and islands, including Spain, which hosts the largest European Griffon Vulture population (around 90%), with 171 sampled individuals. Genetic diversity is essential for the adaptation and long-term survival of a species and, according to the IUCN’s recommendation, is one of the three forms of biodiversity that deserve conservation, along with species and ecosystem diversity49. Research on genetic diversity provides invaluable information enabling us to identify the most vulnerable, isolated, or genetically depleted populations which may require targeted conservation efforts. Without careful analysis of genetic data, misidentification of individuals can introduce bias in population estimates, hybridization events, behavioral research, and any study relying on accurate species or individual identification50.

We detected significant patterns in the genetic diversity and population structure of this species (Table 2). Overall, we found 18 haplotypes, with four (Hpts A, B, C, D) that were previously known5, while 14 were newly discovered, representing a significant contribution to the understanding of genetic variation in this species.

In order to compare diversity across the nine populations by estimating species richness and diversity at standardized levels of sample completeness, a rarefaction-extrapolation analysis was carried out. The extensive overlap among rarefaction curves suggests that differences in haplotype richness among most populations are modest once sampling effort is accounted for. Likewise, the high and overlapping sample coverage across populations further supports the conclusion that observed differences in haplotype richness are not artefacts of uneven sampling effort. The Spanish population was found to harbor the highest haplotypic diversity, with many rare haplotypes likely unsampled. Sardinia represents an intermediate case, with moderate diversity that has already been largely captured, while the remaining populations showed low diversity fully described by the current sampling effort. These patterns confirm that the striking difference observed in Spain is not merely an artefact of sample size but reflects genuine biological variation in haplotype richness.

Haplotype Hpt-A was the most prevalent (76.8%), found across all nine populations, followed by Hpt-C (9.54%) and Hpt-E (3.65%). The uneven distribution of genetic diversity across populations could be related to several factors such as geographic isolation, population size, gene flow and demographic history of each population. The prevalence of Hpt-A, the ancestral haplotype from all others evolved, as inferred from its central position in the MJN (Fig. 2), is not surprising, especially considering its high frequency (84.2%) in the Spanish population. Indeed, Spain hosts the largest remnant of the panmictic population once populating Eurasia and has also been the main source population used for conservation translocation actions in the Mediterranean Basin during the last 40 years. In this regard, along with the wandering movements of single individuals or small groups over land, today human-mediated transfers to islands may be considered a further driver of the spread of Hpt-A, which is predominant throughout the Mediterranean area.

Regarding genetic diversity estimates, θw and Tajima’s D values calculated for the whole dataset and Spain, the only two showing statistically significant values, pointed out fewer pairwise differences than expected, based on the number of segregating sites observed (Table 3). This result is consistent with a recent population expansion, as inferred by the detection of many new rare Hpts with a low degree of differentiation, and furtherly supported by the star-like topology of the MJN (Fig. 2). Accordingly, we found that Hd was moderate (0.411) when considering the entire dataset as a single group. Bulgaria (Hd = 0.833), Croatia, and the island of Naxos (Hd = 0.667 each) exhibited the highest intrapopulation variability, making them candidates as hotspots of genetic variation, whereas the Cypriot population showed no diversity (Table 3). Notably, these findings may be strongly influenced by the small number of samples from these populations present in the dataset. On the other hand, it is interesting to note that, despite being represented by the highest number of samples in the dataset, Spain showed the second lowest value for genetic diversity, preceded only by Cyprus (Table 3).

The combination of high haplotype richness and low haplotype diversity observed in Spain captures different aspects of genetic variation. While richness reflects the number of distinct haplotypes, haplotype diversity is strongly influenced by their relative frequencies. In Spain, the dominance of a single haplotype (Hpt-A; Table 2) results in a highly skewed frequency distribution, which substantially lowers Hd despite the presence of multiple rare haplotypes. This pattern is consistent with recent demographic expansion, or strong founder effects, and can explain why Hd remains low even in the presence of comparatively high haplotype richness. Anyhow, it should be noted that additional rare haplotypes may remain unsampled, but their contribution to Hd would be limited unless present at appreciable frequencies. Overall, π was low (0.00176), reflecting limited genetic differentiation, as confirmed by the analysis of AMOVA, which revealed that most of the genetic variation (93.88%) occurs within populations, with only 6.12% attributed to differences among populations (Table 4a). The combination of low π and relatively high Hd in most populations, except for Spain and Cyprus, is probably related to recent population expansion. When populations were grouped into four or five clusters (Table 4b and c), 93.63–93.74% of the genetic variation remained within populations, suggesting that conservation strategies should maintain population connectivity while also protecting small populations. Based on the pairwise FST estimates (Table S5), low levels of genetic differentiation were observed between Cyprus and Spain, as well as between Serbia and Bulgaria, suggesting the possible occurrence of natural or human-mediated connectivity by means of conservation translocations, which may have happened in the past or may still be going. However, further investigation is required to better understand the causes of such a result. Similarly, a distinct group comprising Crete, Naxos, and Croatia exhibited low divergence. In alignment with the clusters identified in the PCoA, the Croatian population also demonstrated low differentiation relative to those from Serbia and Bulgaria. Conversely, the highest values were recorded between Naxos and the populations of Spain, Sardinia, and Cyprus, likely a consequence of long-term geographic or reproductive isolation. All remaining pairwise comparisons indicated moderate differentiation, suggesting a degree of regional population structure maintained by barriers to gene flow.

The PCoA revealed genetic relationships among the nine populations, with the first two axes accounting for 56.77% and 20.99% of the total variance, confirming significant within-population diversity and moderate interpopulation differentiation. The PCoA plot (Fig. 1) identified four genetic clusters (or five, if the Aegean islands are considered separately) consistent with the grouping configurations evaluated in the AMOVA results (Table 4b and c) and the geographical distribution of populations. This pattern may reflect historical connectivity or ongoing gene flow within each genetic cluster detected in the PCoA analysis, emphasizing the importance of habitat connectivity to preserve genetic diversity. Dispersal individuals between Serbia, Croatia, and Naxos51 (documented by ring-recovery - S.D. Personal Observation) suggest connectivity between Southeastern Continental Europe and the Aegean islands, consistent with the “Continental Europe + Aegean islands” genetic cluster retrieved in the PCoA (Fig. 1). This supposed connectivity is also corroborated by the sharing of Hpt-G, detected only in individuals from Serbia and Naxos (Fig. 2; Table 2). Such a genetic affinity among groups of population is key for selecting the most suitable source population for conservation translocation, emphasizing genetic compatibility over numerical consistency. When the PCoA analysis was performed at the individual level, sequences were grouped by haplotype, and four main haplotype clusters were detected (Fig. S5).

Analysis of the sampling map and the spatial distribution of the studied populations revealed that some eastern Mediterranean populations (i.e. Bulgaria, Croatia, Israel and Naxos) show high haplotype diversity in our current dataset, but given their very small sample sizes, more extensive sampling is needed before robust geographic trends in diversity can be established. This is not surprising, given that the species range extends from Spain to India, and considering that higher genetic diversity is expected towards the center of their range52–54. The MJN (Fig. 2) highlighted Hpt-A as the most common haplotype among the nine Griffon Vulture populations, with most haplotypes differing by only one mutation, and a few by up to five mutations. The starlike genealogy suggests recent population expansion, with Hpt-A representing the ancestral haplotype. Exclusive haplotypes (haplotypes observed only in one population within the current dataset) were found in Spain, Serbia, Israel, Croatia, and Sardinia, underlining their genetic uniqueness. These populations harboring private mtDNA haplotypes may represent distinct conservation units, but formal ESU designation would require additional genomic and ecological data. Nevertheless, they deserve careful focus for managing efforts and targeted conservation programs.

The maximum pairwise genetic distance was observed between Hpt-M (exclusive to Spain) and Hpt-F (exclusive to Serbia). Overall, Hpt-M and Hpt-N were the most divergent haplotypes from all the others, as confirmed by the MJN analysis (Fig. 2, Table S4). This finding has two possible explanations. First, though less likely, they may be basal haplotypes in the species’ evolutionary radiation. However, the Bayesian phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 3) supported a different and more probable hypothesis. Hpt-M and Hpt-N cluster with the Rüppell’s Vulture outgroups, indicating that these three individuals, two harboring the Htp-M and one the Htp-N, carried mtDNA lineages typical of Rüppell’s Vulture. This is consistent with either hybridization, likely from male Griffon Vulture mating with female Rüppell’s Vulture, or misidentification, and nuclear markers would be required to distinguish between these scenarios. These three individuals were sampled from resident populations, and their morphological identification was not further re-examined.

Conservation implications

Relying exclusively on mtDNA provides a biased and incomplete framework for conservation genetics because, being a non-recombining locus reflecting only the maternal lineage, it fails to estimate overall genetic diversity as it ignores the vast majority of genetic material located in the nuclear genome and constitutes a mere quarter of the effective population size of nuclear markers, making it highly susceptible to stochastic lineage loss via genetic drift. Second, it cannot accurately detect population structure arising from male dispersal because it is blind to male-mediated gene flow, often falsely creating an appearance of population isolation when significant nuclear connectivity actually exists. Finally, basing conservation translocation decisions solely on mtDNA is inadvisable, as it overlooks adaptive nuclear variation and local adaptations, increasing the risk of either causing outbreeding depression by mixing incompatible nuclear backgrounds or unnecessarily preventing beneficial gene flow between compatible populations.

Lower levels of genetic variability are expected in edge populations as well as their genetic and phenotypic distinctiveness from populations at the range core, as demonstrated in various species53,55,56. Consequently, conservation translocation from Spain, which is situated at the westernmost edge of the species’ distribution, involves sourcing individuals from a relatively genetically uniform population. Indeed, despite having the highest number of both total (11) and exclusive (seven) haplotypes among all the populations analyzed, 84.2% of the samples harbor Hpt-A, a value much higher than the average (50–67.2.2%) observed in the remaining seven populations (excluding Cyprus). Based on this, the release of randomly selected birds from the Spanish population could increase the Hpt-A frequency, with the risk of uniforming genetic variability among regions and diluting or eliminating rare haplotypes that are a distinctive feature of some populations. This could have significant implications, with the most concerning being a potential reduction in the species’ adaptive capacity to varying environmental and climatic conditions. The restocking actions with Spanish individuals have probably had a significant impact on the genetic intrapopulation diversity of Sardinia, where Hpt-B, the most frequent haplotype in the original population, has been diluted in favor of Hpt-A. According to a study on 22 museum specimens of Griffon Vultures belonging to the native Sardinian population, Hpt-B was prevalent with an incidence of 68%, followed by Hpt-A (32%)8. As a consequence of the restocking actions with Spanish individuals, the frequencies of the haplotypes recorded in the current Sardinian population have changed, with Hpt-A being the most common (64%) and Hpt-B drastically reduced to 25%. The effect of restocking on haplotype frequencies is equally evident and even more significant in the population of Cyprus, showing the presence of only Hpt-A. Two main restockings were carried out in Cyprus during the last 10 years, one sourcing from Crete in 2015, followed by another from Spain in 2023. However, limited ethological and genetic data were available prior to the release of individuals, both for the residual native population and for the introduced birds. This may have constrained the evaluation of genetic compatibility between resident and released individuals, an aspect that is commonly recommended in reintroduction planning to reduce the risk of outbreeding depression and to help maintain population genetic integrity. While restocking contributed to population recovery in terms of abundance and viability, it may also have influenced the original genetic composition of these insular populations. It is important to point out, however, that the Cypriot samples analyzed in the present study predate the 2023 release; therefore, our results reflect the pre-2023 genetic composition (and potentially the 2015 Crete release). On the other hand, populations such as Bulgaria, Croatia and Naxos showed a higher genetic diversity deserving targeted conservation efforts. However, the small sample size of these populations represents a significant limitation that requires caution in drawing definitive conclusions. Indeed, according to a study by Goodall-Coperstake et al.57, up to six samples are considered insufficient for reliable estimates of haplotype diversity, and only from 25 samples onwards do estimates become accurate.

When genetic compatibility is not verified, there is a high risk of outbreeding depression, as mixing genetically distinct populations may lead to maladaptive traits58–60. Animals with large bodies survive better in one geographical area, and in another region, small bodies are favored, while their mixed-size offspring may struggle in both places. An explanatory example is that of the Serbian Griffon Vultures, which are larger than their West Mediterranean relatives33 and may produce offsprings with traits that are maladapted in either environment. These studies suggest that populations with reproductive isolation and genetic structuring should not undergo introductions of foreign animals to preserve local genetic variability. This is exactly the case of the Griffon Vulture populations analyzed in the present study, as inferred by the PCoA result (Fig. 1) and consistent with Davidović et al.33. Growing scientific evidence supports Serbia as a potential good source for restocking populations like Bulgaria due to its geographical proximity and genetic similarity. As previously reported33, Balkan and Iberian Peninsula populations are genetically differentiated and may have adapted to different climatic conditions. These findings suggest that the use of geographically and genetically distant source populations for conservation translocations should be carefully evaluated. Accordingly, geographically proximate populations within the Balkan region may represent candidate sources for future translocation efforts. However, the selection of donor populations should be made on a case-by-case basis and supported by comprehensive genetic screening (ideally incorporating nuclear markers) as well as demographic and ecological considerations. When a focal population is too small to support translocations, alternative populations with sufficient numbers may be considered, provided that introduced individuals are genetically assessed prior to release.

Potential hybridization between Rüppell’s and Griffon vultures

Hybridization has already been documented in Spain, and a recent study reported the first successful breeding of a mixed couple Rüppell’s Vulture - Griffon Vulture, which raised a chick in 202361. While Rüppell’s Vultures are typically non-migratory, their presence in the Iberian Peninsula has increased in recent decades62 for reasons not directly linked to climate change63. In Senegal, Griffon Vultures and Rüppell’s Vultures have been observed feeding in mixed flocks (E.A. Personal Observation). Accordingly, the most credited explanation for their increasing presence in Iberia is that Rüppell’s Vultures join Griffon Vultures during spring migration, due to social dynamics64.

The exact timing of introgression from Rüppell’s Vulture to Griffon Vulture remains uncertain. The hypervariable HVR1 D-loop can discriminate both groups of individuals within the same species and closely related species that are related by a female line of inheritance but cannot determine whether introgression is ancient or ongoing due to recent range overlap and proximity of breeding areas between the two species61,64–66. Although it is beyond the scope of this research, due to highlighted potential risks, even though the case numbers are low (three of possible hybrids out of 171 Spanish individuals analyzed), this finding should be addressed in more depth, analyzing additional nuclear markers (e.g., SSR and SNPs) for a more accurate evaluation of the possible introgression and the proportion of admixture events detected in the abovementioned Spanish Griffon Vulture individuals. The introgression of Rüppell’s Vultures’ mtDNA lineages detected in Spanish Griffon Vultures represents a reason to be cautious when using non-genotyped Spanish birds for conservation translocations in other regions, such as those in the Eastern Mediterranean area. Given the almost total absence of natural contact between Eastern Mediterranean populations and their conspecifics from the Western Mediterranean, as well as the lack of interaction with Rüppell’s Vultures, the introduction of potential hybrids into these native populations, which already exhibit signs of genetic differentiation due to natal philopatry and distinct migration patterns could prove problematic for the maintenance of their genetic variability and potential adaptations to the local environment. In such a context, the most effective strategy to solve the problem would be to genotype the individuals before their release into the wild, in order to ensure that they are genetically compatible with the residual population to which they are added and that they do not show signs of introgression from related species.

Conclusion

In this study, 18 distinct haplotypes were identified among 341 Griffon Vultures from European countries, revealing notable patterns in genetic diversity and population structure. While most genetic variation occurs within populations, the identification of exclusive haplotypes in several groups underscores genetic distinctiveness associated with geographical distribution. In addition, based on mtDNA patterns, Spain and Serbia seem suitable sources to be considered for conservation translocation programs in the West Mediterranean and East Mediterranean areas, respectively, based on the current knowledge. Particularly, despite the marked imbalance in haplotype frequencies, the Spanish population should be the primary source to be considered for conservation translocations in the Mediterranean area due to its numerical consistency of individuals and haplotypes. Indeed, when performing the PCoA and the genetic diversity analysis after removing the suspected hybrid individuals, the result were consistent with those obtained using the whole dataset, suggesting that the distinctiveness of the Spanish population is supported even with the inclusion of the suspected hybrid sequences (Fig. S6 and Table 3). However, its use should be carefully evaluated case by case, in light of the risks of homogenization and hybrid introgression. The moderate interpopulation differentiation and the observed distribution of the mtDNA haplotypes suggest historical connectivity among the studied populations, when the Griffon Vulture were spread all around the Eurasian Continent. Furthermore, phylogenetic analysis revealed the presence of a mixed clade composed of Griffon Vulture and Rüppell’s Vulture, indicating the occurrence of possible hybridization events involving these two species. These findings highlight the critical role of genetic management in the conservation of the Eurasian Griffon Vulture and emphasize the need for targeted conservation strategies to protect genetic diversity, enhance population connectivity without compromising genetic distinctiveness, and account for interspecies interactions to ensure long-term resilience and avoid the formation of “hybrid swarms”67,68.

Further studies on demographic history and based on the use of non-neutral and nuclear markers are needed to shed light on the genetic variability of the Griffon Vulture populations analyzed in the present study, both within individual colonies and in the population. Furthermore, the expansion of the dataset with additional samples from some of the underrepresented countries in this study and other populations from the Mediterranean range, such as France, Turkey and North Africa would be crucial in estimating the whole range of genetic variability of the species across the Mediterranean Basin. This would help identify the areas most in need of targeted conservation efforts, as well as the most suitable source populations for conservation translocation actions in the Mediterranean and Southeastern European areas.

Methods

Population sampling

A total of 249 samples (218 blood and 31 feather samples) of Griffon Vulture individuals from seven geographical areas of the Mediterranean region and continental Europe have been collected between 2013 and 2021 (Fig. 4). Most of the samples (n = 171) were from Spain, 47 from Serbia, 12 from Israel, seven from Croatia, seven from the island of Naxos, four from Bulgaria, and one from Sardinia (Table S1).

Sampling map with enlargement on the Iberian Peninsula showing the collection sites in the studied area.

Blood samples were collected from adult individuals and chicks directly in the nests by venipuncture using sterile equipment and with respect to animal welfare and stored in sodium citrate or QUEENS buffer69 at −20 °C. The feathers were plucked from the chest and the ventral wings area. The wearing of gloves while sampling avoided any contamination (e.g., sweat), and additionally, the researcher ensured that the feather quills were dry and free of any biological contamination (e.g., blood, feces). All samples had the same morphology (cover feathers) and a similar total length (50–100 mm). To avoid the inclusion of direct relatives in the analysis, only birds originating from different nests were used in this study.

Ethical statement

All animal work was conducted with approval from the international and national governing bodies at the institutions where samples were collected and in accordance with all relevant international guidelines and frameworks, including the ARRIVE70,71 guidelines and the EU PREPARE framework72. The procedures applied in this study were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Sassari (Prot. n°85691) and by the Ministry of Nature Protection of the Republic of Serbia and the ethical committee of the Institute for Biological Research “Siniša Stanković” Belgrade (number of the decision: 323–09135-2020-05).

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and nucleotide sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood and feather samples using the GeneJET Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat.No. K0721) and the NucleoSpin Tissue XS (Machery-Nagel, Cat.No. 740901.50), respectively. The DNA concentration and quality were checked both by a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop™ 2000 Spectrophotometer - Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat.No. ND-2000) and agarose gel electrophoresis. PCR amplification, direct sequencing of the PCR products and processing of raw sequencing data were performed following the methodology outlined by5. The marker selected for the analyses was the HVR1 domain because, in addition to being the most variable region of the D-loop, and therefore particularly suitable for intra-species analyses, GenBank contained more than 80 homologous sequences that could be added to the dataset. The HVR1 domain was amplified by using the primer pair GbCR1.L (5’ – tgtacattacactatttgccccata – 3’) - GbCR2.H (5’ – gcagggggaaagtaagatcc – 3’) in a standard 25 µL PCR mixture including 100 ng DNA template, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.20 mM each dNTP, 0.20 µM each primer, 0.02 mM BSA, 1× PCR buffer and 1.5 units Taq DNA Polymerase (Sigma-Aldrich). PCR amplifications were carried out on a GeneAmp PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems) under the following conditions: initial denaturation of 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 50 s, 56 °C for 50 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension of 72 °C for 4 min. Direct sequencing of the PCR products was outsourced to an external company and carried out by means of the cycle sequencing procedure with the dideoxy terminator method using the same PCR primers. Processed sequences were visualized using FinchTV 1.4.0 (Geospiza Inc.) and assembled into contigs, after identifying overlapping areas on BioEdit v. 7.273.

Homologous sequences retrieving and sequence analyses

BLAST queries with each of the mtDNA haplotypes detected in this study were carried out against the GenBank database, and the dataset was complemented by adding 92 homologous sequences (Table S6). The final dataset used for the analyses was composed of 341 sequences (Table S7). In order to verify the usefulness of the dataset considering the unequal sample sizes among populations, a rarefaction–extrapolation analysis of haplotype richness has been performed using Hill numbers (q = 0, corresponding to species/haplotype richness)74. Analyses were conducted in R environment v.4.3.175 by means of the package iNEXT76. In this context, it should be noted that analyses focused on diversity of order q = 0, which reflects haplotype richness and is most appropriate for assessing the completeness of haplotype sampling and the presence of rare variants. Higher-order diversities (q = 1 and q = 2), which down-weight rare haplotypes and reflect effective diversity, would be strongly influenced by the dominance of the most common haplotype (Hpt-A) and were therefore not the primary focus of this study.

We applied three complementary approaches:

Sample-size-based rarefaction/extrapolation (Type 1): haplotype richness was estimated as a function of the number of sampled individuals, with interpolation below and extrapolation beyond the observed sample size (up to twice the reference sample size).

Coverage-based rarefaction/extrapolation (Type 2): haplotype richness was estimated as a function of sample coverage (the proportion of the total haplotype pool represented in the sample), allowing comparison among populations at equal levels of completeness.

Sample coverage versus sample size (Type 3): this diagnostic curve illustrates how rapidly each population approaches complete sampling, indicating whether the observed sampling effort was sufficient to capture haplotypic diversity.

Estimates of haplotype richness were reported together with 95% confidence intervals based on bootstrap resampling as implemented in iNEXT. Rarefaction curves (solid lines) represent interpolation within the observed sample range, while extrapolation (dashed lines) projects expected richness beyond the observed sample size. Rarefaction and coverage analyses were based on the total number of individuals per population, including both newly generated sequences and sequences retrieved from GenBank (see Table S1 and Table S6 for further details).

Sequences were aligned using the Muscle algorithm implemented in MEGA v. 1177.

Genetic diversity parameters, including the number of polymorphic sites (S), the number of haplotypes (h), the haplotype diversity (Hd), the nucleotide diversity (π), the Tajima’s D test, the Watterson’s theta (θW​), and the frequency of haplotypes were estimated using DnaSP v. 678.

The transition/transversion bias (R = 6.2) was calculated with MEGA v. 11, under the Kimura two-parameter model (K2P) using a discrete gamma distribution (+ G) to model evolutionary rate differences among 5 sites categories (G, parameter = 0.15).

The estimation of the fixation indices (FST, FCT, FSC) and hierarchical analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA), were performed in ARLEQUIN v. 3.5.279.

GENALEX ver. 6.580 was used to produce a Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) to examine variation in the dataset. The population-based PCoA was performed based on a K2P genetic distance matrix using the mean distances among the nine Griffon Vulture populations. PCoA was preferred over PCA because it is a more flexible tool for large datasets that include populations with small sample sizes, and it is particularly effective for analyses involving multiple populations81.

The pairwise genetic distances corrected according to the Kimura two-parameter model (K2P) using a gamma distribution (+ G) for evolutionary rates among sites were estimated between haplotypes by means of MEGA v. 11 with 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

The Bayesian tree was calculated using MrBayes v. 3.2.482. Two independent runs, each consisting of four Metropolis-coupled reversible-jump Markov chain Monte Carlo chains were performed. A Dirichlet distribution with quasi-flat priors (1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1) was assumed for estimating the substitution frequency parameters. Analyses were carried out for 10 million generations and the trees were sampled every 10 generations. Convergence of chains was checked by ensuring that the standard deviation of split frequencies reached and stabilized at a value < 0.01 and by verifying the stationarity of the generations/log probability graph. For each run, the first 25% sampled trees were discarded. Two Rüppell’s Vulture (G. rueppelli) sequences from Gambian individuals, RUE_Hpt-10 (DQ909000) and RUE_Hpt-11 (DQ909001), were retrieved from GenBank83 and included in the dataset as outgroup. The sequence of Aegypius monachus (MON, KF682364)84 was used to root the phylogenetic tree.

The MJN of the mtDNA haplotypes was inferred by means of the software Network v. 10.2.0.0 (http://www.fluxusengineering.com/network_terms.htm). Postprocessing Maximum Parsimony (MP) calculation was performed to remove reticulations observed in the MJN, and the resulting network was manually assembled for visualization.
