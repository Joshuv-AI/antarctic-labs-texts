Evaluation of cytotoxic and cellular effects of zinc nanoparticles from Saccharum officinarum (Jaggery) extract on breast cancer cells
Authors: Muhammad Awais Farooqi, Kyeoung Cheol Kim, Ji-Hyang Kim, Dovran Shamamedov, Hafiz Muhammad Umer Farooqi, Chul Ung Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype with limited targeted treatment options, making it difficult to manage effectively. This study investigates the anticancer potential of eco-friendly zinc oxide nanoparticles (SO-ZnO NPs), synthesized using Saccharum officinarum (jaggery) extract, against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. The NPs were characterized by various techniques, confirming their spherical morphology, a primary particle size of 20–50 nm, aggregation into larger clusters (100–250 nm), and a moderately positive surface charge. The composition was predominantly zinc (96.1%), oxygen (2.3%), and carbon (1.59%), attributed to bioorganic capping agents. SO-ZnO NPs showed significant anticancer effects, with an IC50 of 125.00 ± 2.60 µg/mL, indicating potent cytotoxicity above this concentration. They also suppressed key inflammatory markers (IL-6 secretion and mRNA expression) and inhibited the proliferation of cancer stem cells. Notably, SO-ZnO NPs downregulated stemness markers (NANOG, OCT4, CD44) and disrupted mammosphere formation, suggesting a potential to target cancer stem cell self-renewal and modulate the tumor microenvironment. These findings underline the therapeutic potential of SO-ZnO NPs in treating TNBC, though further in vivo studies are necessary to evaluate their safety and clinical effectiveness.

Introduction

Breast cancer treatment has significantly advanced with the development of targeted therapies, including endocrine therapy and HER2-targeted agents, which have improved outcomes for many patients1. However, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) lacks expression of estrogen, progesterone, and Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2) receptors, making it resistant to these therapies and contributing to its aggressive behavior, high recurrence, and early metastasis2. TNBC is associated with high recurrence, early metastasis, poor prognosis, and limited treatment options3. MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, a widely used TNBC model, exhibit invasive and metastatic traits4. Current approaches of chemotherapy often face resistance, highlighting the urgent need for targeted, effective therapies5. Recent research has shifted toward nanotechnology-based strategies, including green plant-based synthesized NPs, to overcome these challenges and improve therapeutic outcomes6. The synthesis of metal oxide NPs has attracted significant attention across diverse research fields, particularly for applications in biologically synthesized therapeutic agents7–9. Among these, zinc oxide (ZnO) NPs are especially notable due to their unique physicochemical properties, i.e., high surface area, semiconducting behavior, and UV absorption capacity10. ZnO is classified by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) for specific applications, such as in sunscreens, wound healing products, and topical diagnostic tools11. While this designation applies to bulk ZnO, it does not automatically extend to engineered ZnO NPs, which are increasingly explored in biomedical applications, including oncology research, due to their biocompatibility and selective toxicity toward cancer cells12. Importantly, the use of ZnO NPs in such contexts should be supported by both in vitro and in vivo toxicity and safety studies13,14. Conventional chemical and physical synthesis methods for ZnO NPs, though effective, often involve toxic reagents and energy-intensive procedures, raising concerns about environmental safety and biocompatibility15. As a result, green synthesis approaches using plant-based materials have emerged as sustainable alternatives16. These methods are eco-friendly, cost-effective, and offer improved biocompatibility due to the natural reducing and stabilizing agents found in plant extracts17. In the present study, ZnO NPs were synthesized using Saccharum officinarum (jaggery) and evaluated for their anticancer potential against MDA-MB-231 cells18. Jaggery, derived from sugarcane, was chosen over other available green compounds due to its accessibility and widespread use in daily life. It is a traditional unrefined sugar, contains bioactive compounds such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and minerals that can effectively reduce Zn²⁺ ions and stabilize the resulting NPs19,20. Additionally, jaggery possesses known medicinal properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and detoxifying effects, which contribute functional benefits to the synthesized NPs21,22,20. The use of jaggery aqueous extract as a reducing agent aligns with the principles of green chemistry, offering a more sustainable and biocompatible approach to NP synthesis compared to traditional methods, which often involve toxic reagents and energy-intensive procedures. Furthermore, the medicinal properties of jaggery may impart functional benefits to the resulting SO-ZnO NPs, potentially enhancing their therapeutic efficacy. The synthesized SO-ZnO NPs were characterized using various analytical techniques to confirm their structural and morphological properties before evaluating their anticancer potential23.The biological activities of the SO-ZnO NPs, including cytotoxic, anticancer, and anti-cancer stem cell (anti-CSC) effects, were evaluated. Biological effects were assessed through assays measuring cell viability and cancer stemness. The expression of cancer stemness markers (NANOG, OCT4, CD44) was also evaluated24. Mammosphere formation assays were conducted to investigate stemness-related characteristics25. Additionally, the effects of S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs on cytokine IL-6 secretion and IL-6 mRNA expression were evaluated in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells26. The objective of this research is to address the clinical challenge posed by TNBC, which remains difficult to treat due to its aggressiveness and resistance to conventional therapies. This study aims to evaluate the anticancer potential of SO-ZnO NPs in MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells, with the goal of advancing sustainable and effective therapeutic strategies for TNBC treatment27.

Materials and methods

Green synthesis of SO-ZnO NPs using Saccharum officinarum (Jaggery)

The jaggery used in this study was purchased from Aram International (South Korea) via Coupang, sourced from India (Coupang product number: 7075498859–18641768828). To prepare the jaggery solution, 15 g of solid jaggery was dissolved in 60 mL of deionized (DI) water, yielding a 25% (w/v) solution. The solution was stirred at 800 rpm for 10 min to ensure thorough mixing and complete dissolution of the jaggery. Afterward, the solution was filtered through Whatman filter paper to remove any insoluble particles. Separately, 400 mg of zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(C₂H₃O₂)₂·2 H₂O) was dissolved in 4 mL of deionized (DI) water to prepare a 0.46 M (≈ 455 mM) zinc precursor solution18. The jaggery solution was then added dropwise to the zinc precursor solution at a 1:10 volume ratio under constant magnetic stirring at room temperature. The mixture was refluxed at 70 °C ± 2 °C for 4 h, as indicated by a yellowish color change. After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm (≈ 2,500 × g) for 20 min. The deposit was then washed four times with DI water and dried in hot air oven at 80 °C for 14 h to obtain dry SO-ZnO NPs as shown in Fig. 128.

Physio-chemical characterization

Zeta potential and particle size measurement

The hydrodynamic Z-Average particle size and zeta potential charge of the synthesized SO-ZnO NPs were analyzed using a Zetasizer Lab (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) at Jeju National University, Jeju-do, Republic of Korea29. First the SO-ZnO NPs were dispersed in DI water and subjected to ultrasonic treatment using a PowerSonic 520 sonicator (Hiep Phat) at a frequency of 40 kHz for 30 min at 25 °C 29. This step ensured uniform dispersion of SO-ZnO NPs and prevented the chance aggregation before analysis. After ultrasonic dispersion using a bath sonicator, the SO-ZnO NPs were loaded into a Folded Capillary Zeta Cell to measure the Z-Average particle size and surface charge by evaluating their electrophoretic mobility. Prolonged sonication was minimized to prevent potential alterations in particle size. All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD)30.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of SO-ZnO NPs

The morphological characteristics and elemental composition of SO-ZnO NPs were analyzed using a Multi-Functional Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM), TECNAI F20 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), at the Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI) Gwangju, Republic of Korea, which was equipped with an Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) detector31. TEM imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. A small volume of SO-ZnO NP suspension was dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to dry at room temperature before imaging32. Various magnifications were used to observe the shape, size, and distribution of the nanoparticles. EDS analysis was conducted for elemental mapping and composition, with the presence of zinc and oxygen confirming SO-ZnO formation; the detected carbon signal was interpreted cautiously, as the carbon-coated grid may contribute to background signals33.

Cell culture and sample treatments

MDA-MB-231 human breast carcinoma cells (passage number 5–15) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ 34. Cells were seeded into 96-well and 6-well plates for experimental treatments. S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NP samples were administered at a concentration of 250 µg/mL, which was selected based on preliminary proliferation assays35,36.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NP samples was evaluated using the EZ-Cytox Kit (DogenBio, Seoul, Korea), a water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST) assay, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. A two-fold serial dilution was used to prepare concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 µg/mL. Cells were treated with the diluted samples and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After treatment, 10 µL of WST reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Microscopic visualization

The morphological features of MDA-MB-231 cells treated with S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs (250 µg/mL) were examined using an inverted microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) at the Bio-Health Materials Core Facility, Jeju National University, Jeju-do, Republic of Korea. Representative images were captured at 10× objective magnifications to analyze the morphology in the control group and the morphological changes indicative of cell death in the treated groups (S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs)36.

Cytokine secretion and mRNA expression analysis

The secretion levels of IL-6 were assessed in the culture supernatants of MDA-MB-231 cells treated with 250 µg/mL of S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs samples, using ELISA kits (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions28. For mRNA expression analysis, total RNA was isolated from treated cells using an RNA extraction kit (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan)37. cDNA synthesis was carried out, and qPCR was performed using the TOPreal™ SYBR Green RT-qPCR Kit (Enzynomics, Seoul, Korea) with cDNA as the template to analyze the expression of cancer stem cell markers (NANOG, OCT4, and CD44)38. The specific primer sequences used are listed in Table 139. qPCR reactions were carried out in a total volume of 20 µL with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s40. Melt curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to verify the specificity of amplification, and primer efficiency was confirmed to be within 90–110%. All experiments were performed in triplicate using three independent biological samples. Gene expression data were normalized to GAPDH as a reference gene and presented as mean ± standard deviation41.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 7 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test to compare each treated group with the control group. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.0542.

Results

Characterization of SO-ZnO NPs

Particle size and zeta potential

The dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis demonstrated that the SO-ZnO NPs possessed a Z-average hydrodynamic particle size of 377 ± 42 nm which places the SO-ZnO NPs within an optimal size range for biological applications as showed in Fig. 2 (A)43. This DLS analysis indicates the formation of relatively larger SO-ZnO NPs, which indicated the presence of jaggery phytochemicals acting as natural capping and stabilizing agents44. The size distribution also suggests moderate polydispersity, as indicated by a Polydispersity Index (PDI) of 0.23, potentially due to SO-ZnO NPs agglomeration45. The zeta potential of the synthesized SO-ZnO NPs was measured to be + 16.38 ± 1.2 mV as showed in Fig. 2 (B)46. This moderately positive surface charge indicates a fair level of colloidal stability, where the electrostatic repulsion is sufficient to prevent rapid aggregation but not highly stable29. Moreover, the positive charge may enhance interactions with negatively charged cellular membranes, which is advantageous for cancer therapy47. One study reported ZnO NPs synthesized using Ocimum sanctum leaf extract exhibited a hydrodynamic size of approximately 340 nm and a moderately positive zeta potential of + 15.6 mV, indicating good colloidal stability and effective phytochemical capping48. These results corroborate the reported SO-ZnO NPs hydrodynamic size and moderately positive zeta potential synthesized using S. officinarum extract, supporting their colloidal stability and potential for antitumor therapeutic use49.Thus, the particle size and zeta potential of the SO-ZnO NPs align with typical characteristics observed in green-synthesized metal oxide NPs, confirming their potential for use in various applications such as drug delivery, antimicrobial agents, and other biological uses50.

TEM analysis

TEM images revealed that the SO-ZnO NPs exhibited a spherical morphology, confirming the successful synthesis of ZnO NPs. However, the particles showed uneven distribution, with some exhibiting signs of aggregation and agglomeration45. The primary particle size of the SO-ZnO NPs ranged from 20 to 50 nm, as seen in high-resolution images (E-G)51. These smaller particles tended to aggregate, forming larger visible clusters with sizes ranging from 100 to 250 nm52. The observed non-uniform size distribution and aggregation suggest that the bioorganic compounds from the S. officinarum extract may not have fully prevented particle clustering, despite their stabilizing potential53. In Fig. 3, (A) shows a TEM image of the aggregated SO-ZnO NPs with a size range of approximately 20–250 nm, while (B–D) reveal closer views indicating the quasi-spherical to rod-like morphology of the NPs. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images in (E–G) show agglomerates and irregular surface textures with an average primary particle size of approximately 20–50 nm. Additionally, (H–J) HRTEM images confirm the crystalline nature of the SO-ZnO NPs with visible lattice fringes, indicating a d-spacing of approximately 2.61 Å for the (002) plane, consistent with the hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO54. One study has reported ZnO NPs synthesized using thyme plant leaf extract exhibited quasi-spherical shapes ranging from 14.92 to 22.54 nm were synthesized using Cayratia pedata leaf extract55. Another study using Euphorbia dracunculoides Lam. demonstrated ZnO NPs sized between 19 and 53 nm 56. Images (H), (I), and (J) clearly show lattice fringes observing HRTEM images that confirm your SO-ZnO NPs are crystalline. Overall, the SO-ZnO NPs exhibit a crystalline structure. These findings support the typical size range, morphology, and purity of ZnO NPs produced via plant-based green synthesis methods, in alignment with our results using S. officinarum extract33.

EDS analysis

TEM-EDS analysis of the SO-ZnO NPs reveals that they are predominantly composed of zinc (Zn) and oxygen (O), the essential elements forming ZnO57. Zn accounts for a significant proportion by both weight (96.07%) and atomic composition (84.06%), confirming successful synthesis of SO-ZnO NPs. The notable presence of oxygen (2.34 wt%, 8.38 at%) corresponds to oxide formation as well as surface functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups involved in the green synthesis process as mentioned in Table 258. A substantial carbon content (1.59 wt%, 7.57 at%) reflects the contribution of the S. officinarum extract, which acts as both a reducing and stabilizing agent during synthesis. This organic extract provides a capping layer around the NPs, preventing aggregation and oxidation by forming an organic matrix that stabilizes the particles59. The high net intensity values for Zn and O indicate strong X-ray signals, while the low net error percentages confirm the accuracy and reliability of the elemental detection. The EDS elemental mapping and spectrum collectively confirm the successful green synthesis of S. officinarum-mediated ZnO NPs. The HAADF Fig. 4 (A) reveals nanoscale aggregates with uniform morphology and bright contrast due to high atomic number Zn, indicating dispersed structures without significant clustering. The carbon mapping (B) shows scattered C Kα signals, reflecting the adsorption of plant-derived phytoconstituents such as phenolics, flavonoids, or proteins that act as stabilizing agents60. Oxygen mapping (C) demonstrates even distribution, confirming the presence of O as a core ZnO component and supporting the complete oxidation of Zn²⁺ ions. Zinc mapping (D) shows strong, widespread Zn Kα signals throughout the SO-ZnO NPs sample, validating the dense and homogeneous incorporation of Zn within the SO-ZnO NPs. The overlay image (E) combining C, O, and Zn confirms surface biofunctionalization by the S. officinarum extract, with clear co-localization of all elements, supporting effective phytochemical coating. Finally, the EDS spectrum (F) exhibits sharp peaks for Zn (1, 8.6, and 9.6 keV), O (0.5 keV), and C (0.3 keV), with no extraneous signals, indicating high purity and successful biosynthesis as illustrated in Fig. 4. Overall, the EDS results confirm that the synthesized SO-ZnO NPs consist mainly of ZnO with a significant organic component derived from the S. officinarum extract, validating the effectiveness of the green synthesis approach61. The analysis validates the formation SO-ZnO NPs uniformly dispersed, biofunctionalized SO-ZnO NPs suitable for biomedical applications.

Evaluation of cytotoxicity changes induced by S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs in MDA-MB-231 cells

The cytotoxic effects of S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs on MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells were assessed using a WST assay62,63. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations (0–1000 µg/mL) of each sample33. At 250 µg/mL, S. officinarum extract reduced cell viability to 82.1 ± 3.4%, whereas SO-ZnO NPs decreased viability to 61.7 ± 2.9%, indicating significantly higher cytotoxicity by the SO-ZnO NPs (p < 0.05). At 500 µg/mL, the extract and SO-ZnO NPs further reduced viability to 54.3 ± 2.6% and 31.8 ± 2.1%, respectively. At the highest concentration (1000 µg/mL), viability dropped to 38.6 ± 2.4% for the extract and 19.7 ± 2.0% for SO-ZnO NPs. As illustrated in Fig. 5, S. officinarum extract displayed moderate cytotoxicity, with noticeable reductions from 250 µg/mL and above. In contrast, SO-ZnO NPs exhibited a substantial decline in cell viability beginning at 62.5 µg/mL (85.0 ± 2.8%), with marked reductions at 125 µg/mL (51.7 ± 2.6%) and beyond. The IC50 value for SO-ZnO NPs against MDA-MB-231 cells was approximately 125.00 ± 2.60 µg/mL, indicating significant cytotoxicity at concentrations above 125 µg/mL. In comparison, the IC50 for S. officinarum extract is expected to be higher than 250 µg/mL, as 82.1 ± 3.4% cell viability was observed at 250 µg/mL. Several studies have reported dose-dependent cytotoxicity of green-synthesized zinc oxide (ZnO) NPs against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, aligning with the findings of this study. For instance, ZnO NPs synthesized using Ceratonia siliqua extract exhibited significant cytotoxicity, reducing cell viability to approximately 60% at 250 µg/mL and 30% at 500 µg/mL64. Similarly, green ZnO NPs synthesized using Rhus coriaria extract demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability, with significant inhibition observed at concentrations ranging from 31.2 to 250 µg/mL65,66.

Attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and secretion in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells

The anti-inflammatory potential of S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs was evaluated by assessing their effects on the expression and secretion of the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells67,68. As shown in Fig. 6 (A), both treatments significantly reduced IL-6 levels, with SO-ZnO NPs exhibiting a more pronounced effect than the extract alone25. At a concentration of 250 µg/mL, treatment with S. officinarum extract reduced IL-6 secretion to approximately 1320 ± 70 pg/mL, while SO-ZnO NPs reduced it further to 850 ± 60 pg/mL (p < 0.05). Correspondingly, IL-6 mRNA expression levels decreased to 76 ± 4.5% with the S. officinarum extract and to 53 ± 3.8% with SO-ZnO NPs, relative to untreated controls (set at 100%) as depicted in Fig. 6 (B). ELISA results confirmed significantly lower IL-6 protein levels in the culture supernatant of SO-ZnO NP-treated cells compared to those treated with the S. officinarum extract. In parallel, qPCR analysis verified that IL-6 transcription was substantially downregulated following SO-ZnO NPs treatment, indicating that suppression occurs at both the transcriptional and protein levels69. One previous research also demonstrated that the green ZnO NPs derived from Catharanthus roseus leaf extract showed a notable decrease in IL-6 mRNA expression in MDA-MB-231 cells, aligning with the anti-inflammatory profile observed in this study70.

Effects of SO-ZnO NPs on cancer cell growth and stemness marker expression

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a subpopulation of tumor cells characterized by self-renewal, tumorigenicity, and resistance to conventional treatments. To assess the effects of S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs on CSC-related characteristics, we evaluated cancer cell proliferation and the expression of key stemness-associated transcription factors in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells71. As shown in Fig. 7 (A), the number of mammosphere-derived cancer cells was significantly reduced following treatment. On Day 3, the control group exhibited a cell count of (2.6 ± 0.15) × 10⁷, while treatment with Saccharum officinarum extract reduced this to (1.7 ± 0.12) × 10. The SO-ZnO NPs treatment exhibited the most pronounced inhibitory effect, decreasing the count to (1.1 ± 0.09) × 10⁷. These results demonstrate a superior anti-proliferative effect of SO-ZnO NPs on cancer stem cell growth compared to the S. officinarum extract alone. While the total number of cancer cells in the control and S. officinarum extract groups remained relatively stable over the three-day period, a significant reduction in cancer cell numbers was observed in the SO-ZnO NP-treated group by Day 3. This suggests an inhibitory effect of SO-ZnO NPs on cell proliferation, particularly in the mammosphere-derived cells. To further investigate the impact on CSC traits, we analyzed the relative mRNA expression levels of three stemness markers: NANOG, OCT4, and CD44. As presented in Fig. 7 (B), Transcriptional analysis revealed that SO-ZnO NPs markedly downregulated the expression of stemness-related markers: NANOG (52% ± 4.1%), OCT4 (55% ± 3.8%), and CD44 (48% ± 4.5%) relative to control. In comparison, S. officinarum extract treatment led to moderate suppression: NANOG (70% ± 5.2%), OCT4 (73% ± 4.9%), and CD44 (68% ± 5.1%). These findings support that SO-ZnO NPs more effectively attenuate stem cell marker expression in MDA-MB-231 cells than the S. officinarum extract alone24. The downregulation of CSC markers NANOG, OCT4, and CD44, along with reduced mammosphere formation, suggests that SO-ZnO NPs disrupt key self-renewal pathways i.e., Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and Hedgehog72,73. These effects also stem from SO-ZnO NPs-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis, enhancing CSC vulnerability to treatment74,75. Both S. officinarum extract SO-ZnO NPs treatment and groups showed downregulation of these markers, with SO-ZnO NPs producing a more pronounced effect. One study shows green ZnO NPs with Azadirachta indica (neem) and Pandanus odorifer green synthesized ZnO NPs resulted in the downregulation of key stemness markers such as NANOG, OCT4, and CD44, highlighting their potential to suppress tumor progression and recurrence by targeting the CSC population more effectively than plant extracts alone76. These findings suggest that biologically synthesized ZnO NPs, such as SO-ZnO NPs, may have potential to target CSCs and reduce tumorigenic properties in breast cancer cells, warranting further investigation77. The reduction in stemness gene expression suggests that SO-ZnO NPs impair CSC maintenance by inhibiting the transcription of critical pluripotency and survival pathways. These results collectively indicate that SO-ZnO NPs not only suppress the growth of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells but also diminish the expression of stemness-associated genes. This dual inhibitory effect highlights the potential of SO-ZnO NPs as a promising therapeutic agent for targeting CSC populations and reducing the risk of tumor recurrence and therapy resistance.

Inhibitory effects of SO-ZnO NPs on mammosphere formation in MDA-MB-231 cells

Mammosphere formation assays were conducted to evaluate the impact of S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs on cancer stem cell (CSC) characteristics in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells78. CSCs possess the ability to form mammospheres under non-adherent conditions, a trait associated with their self-renewal and tumor-initiating potential. As shown in Fig. 8, untreated control cells formed dense and numerous mammospheres, indicative of a highly active CSC population. Microscopic analysis confirmed that control MDA-MB-231 cells formed compact mammospheres, further supporting their stem-like phenotype. Treatment with S. officinarum extract (250 µg/mL) resulted in a visible reduction in mammosphere size and compactness, suggesting a partial suppression of CSC characteristics. Notably, treatment with SO-ZnO NPs at the same concentration led to a marked disruption in mammosphere architecture. These mammospheres appeared sparse, irregular, and composed of loosely associated cells, highlighting a profound impairment of CSC properties. Compared to the S. officinarum extract alone, SO-ZnO NPs demonstrated significantly greater efficacy in inhibiting both the formation and structural integrity of mammospheres. The SO-ZnO NP-treated cells exhibited fewer, smaller, and disorganized mammospheres, pointing to a potent inhibitory effect on CSC self-renewal and proliferative capacity. These findings demonstrate that SO-ZnO NPs significantly suppress mammosphere formation, by disrupting CSC maintenance and reducing proliferative potential. The data indicate that SO-ZnO NPs exert in vitro cytotoxic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-stemness effects in MDA-MB-231 cells, suggesting potential relevance for further anticancer investigation. Some research work showed green ZnO NPs effectively inhibit mammosphere formation and disrupt CSC self-renewal in breast cancer models79. For instance, ZnO NPs biosynthesized using plant extracts such as Azadirachta indica (neem) and Pandanus odorifer have been shown to significantly reduce both the size and number of mammospheres formed by MDA-MB-231 cells80. These ZnO NPs produced smaller, fewer, and morphologically altered mammospheres compared to untreated controls, indicating impaired CSC proliferation and self-renewal capacity81. The illustration Fig. 9 (A) of the CSC-enriched microenvironment showed the untreated cells, where elevated IL-6 secretion and high expression of stemness-associated transcription factors (NANOG, OCT4, CD44) support inflammatory signaling and mammosphere formation. In contrast, Fig. 9 (B) depicts the mechanistic effects of SO-ZnO NP treatment. Following cellular internalization and degradation of SO-ZnO NPs, there is a notable downregulation of stemness markers and IL-6 expression. This dual inhibition reduces the inflammation and impairs CSC self-renewal, ultimately suppressing tumor-promoting processes82. Collectively, these results support a multi-targeted mechanism by which SO-ZnO NPs disrupt CSC function and tumor progression83,84. The proposed model (Fig. 9) summarizes the inhibitory effects of SO-ZnO NPs, including the downregulation of IL-6 and stemness genes, suppression of inflammation and mammosphere formation, and inhibition of cancer cell proliferation also highlighting their promise as a nanotherapeutic strategy for breast cancer treatment.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the evaluation of the cytotoxic and cellular effects of SO-ZnO NPs on MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells26. This research encompassed green NPs synthesis, thorough characterization, and the assessment of various biological activities85. In previous research, the biosynthesis of SO-ZnO NPs was visually confirmed by a distinct color change from light white to yellowish-brown, indicating the reduction of Zn²⁺ ions. The successful formation of SO-ZnO NPs was further confirmed by UV–Vis spectrophotometry, which showed characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorbance peaks at 362, 358, and 356 nm. The total phenolic content of SO–ZnO NPs (14.28 ± 19.11 µg GAE/g-1DW) was lower than that of the S. officinarum extract (30.23 ± 8.64 µg GAE/g-1DW), indicating that the green synthesis process using jaggery (S. officinarum) extract reduces phenolic compounds, though they still contribute to the SO-ZnO NPs antioxidant and bioactivity18. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) identified functional groups i.e., hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl in the S. officinarum extract, implicating them in the reduction and stabilization of the SO-ZnO NPs. The presence of a Zn–O stretching vibration at 913 cm⁻¹ and shifts in other characteristic peaks between the S. officinarum extract and SO-ZnO NPs provided additional evidence of organic capping and successful SO-ZnO NPs synthesis. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis further confirmed the crystalline nature of the SO-ZnO NPs, showing distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to the hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO, with no impurity signals86. The average crystallite size was calculated to be 40.5 nm, suggesting uniform particle distribution and high crystallinity. Raman spectroscopy supported these findings, displaying vibrational peaks characteristic of Zn–O bonds and organic stabilizing groups from the S. officinarum extract87. DLS analysis indicated an average hydrodynamic size of 377 ± 42 nm, larger than the 20–50 nm primary particle size observed by TEM/HRTEM. This discrepancy arises from particle aggregation and surface hydration in solution, while TEM/HRTEM reflects the true individual particle size. The smaller primary particles are more relevant for cellular uptake and biological activity, whereas the larger DLS-measured aggregates are less likely to be efficiently internalized88–90. Furthermore TEM-EDS analysis confirmed (Zn) as the dominant element, along with (O) and trace amounts of (C) that evidence of successful reduction and capping facilitated by the S. officinarum extract91. A WST assay demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in MDA-MB-231 cell viability, consistent with prior findings that SO-ZnO NPs exert cytotoxicity through oxidative stress, mitochondrial disruption, and apoptosis induction18. In one recent study, anti-inflammatory effects of SO-ZnO NPs were assessed in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. At concentrations above 20 µg/mL, the SO-ZnO NPs significantly reduced nitric oxide production (13.6% inhibition at 20 µg/mL) and decreased pro-inflammatory IL-1β levels. Western blot analysis confirmed downregulation of iNOS and COX-2 expression, suggesting that SO-ZnO NPs may attenuate inflammation more effectively than the S. officinarum extract alone. Together with the observed cytotoxic effects in MDA-MB-231 cells, these findings indicate that SO-ZnO NPs could potentially enhance the biological activity of the S. officinarum extract in cancer-relevant contexts18. These results suggest that the green synthesis of SO-ZnO NPs may influence the biological activity of the S. officinarum extract, indicating potential for further investigation, while in vivo delivery and therapeutic effects remain to be evaluated34. ZnO NPs can dissolve under biological conditions, releasing Zn²⁺ ions, which may contribute to cytotoxicity and modulation of inflammatory cytokines. In our study, SO-ZnO NPs exhibited higher cytotoxicity and reduced IL-6 secretion compared to the S. officinarum extract, indicating that Zn²⁺ release could partially contribute to these effects. However, the primary biological activity appears to derive from the nanoparticulate form itself92. Quantitative assessment of Zn²⁺ release in future studies will be essential to clarify its specific role in affecting cell viability, cytokine production, and cancer stem cell characteristics93. In present research, SO-ZnO NPs also showed efficacy against CSCs, which are implicated in therapy resistance and tumor relapse along with the downregulation of stemness-associated markers NANOG, OCT4, and CD44. The downregulation of CSC markers suggests that SO-ZnO NPs disrupt CSC pathways and impair tumor-initiating capacity38. Collectively, these mechanisms may reduce CSC populations and limit tumor progression and therapy resistance. These results suggest that SO-ZnO NPs may disrupt CSC maintenance, indicating potential for further investigation as a strategy to target aggressive and drug-resistant cancer subtypes75. In addition, this study confirmed that SO-ZnO NPs possess strong anti-inflammatory activity in breast cancer cells. ELISA and RT-qPCR analyses showed significant reductions in both secretion and gene expression of IL-6 in MDA-MB-231 cells47. These effects were more pronounced than those observed with the S. officinarum extract alone, reinforcing the enhanced bioactivity of the SO-ZnO NPs formulation94. These findings are consistent with previous reports that SO-ZnO NPs can modulate signaling pathways involved in tumor-promoting inflammation95. Overall, this study presents evidence that biogenically synthesized SO-ZnO NPs exhibit anticancer, anti-pro-inflammatory, and anti-CSC properties. Their size and biocompatibility highlight their potential for further investigation as a nanotherapeutic strategy96. Future research should focus on evaluating the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects, including oxidative stress induction, DNA damage response, and modulation of apoptotic and inflammatory pathways97. In spite of the current findings, challenges remain for clinical translation. These include SO-ZnO NPs reproducibility, stability, potential toxicity, and efficient in vivo drug delivery98. Comprehensive in vivo studies are needed to assess absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) including pharmacokinetics, as well as safety, efficacy, and toxicity66. In vivo trials are warranted to further assess the safety and therapeutic efficacy of SO-ZnO NPs. In nutshell, this investigation provides insights into the biological potential of S. officinarum-mediated ZnO NPs. Their enhanced cytotoxic, anti-CSC, and anti-inflammatory effects are superior to the S. officinarum extract, supporting their potential application in targeted cancer therapy. Continued research, including preclinical studies, will further validate their efficacy. Advanced models, such as tumor-on-a-chip systems (microphysiological systems), could play a key role in optimizing SO-ZnO NPs as an effective and eco-friendly strategy for treating breast cancer and inflammation-associated diseases99.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the cytotoxic and cellular effects of SO-ZnO NPs synthesized using Saccharum officinarum (jaggery) extract on MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. Physicochemical characterization confirmed the formation of stable, biofunctional nanoparticles suitable for biomedical applications. Compared with the crude S. officinarum extract, SO-ZnO NPs exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity, reduced cell viability, suppressed IL-6 secretion, and inhibited cancer stem cell proliferation. They also downregulated stemness markers and impaired mammosphere formation, indicating disruption of cancer stem cell self-renewal. Overall, SO-ZnO NPs demonstrate in vitro anticancer activity against triple-negative breast cancer cells, suggesting their potential for further investigation as a nanotherapeutic candidate. Further in vivo studies are needed to optimize synthesis and assess safety and efficacy.
