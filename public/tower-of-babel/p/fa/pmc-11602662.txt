Patient voices on PKU care: Insights from focus groups with current and former patients
Authors: Emily Zhu, Suzanne Hollander, Stephanie Sacharow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany adults with phenylketonuria (PKU) were discharged from clinics before lifelong treatment guidelines and new therapies. As part of the PKU lost to clinical care study, moderated focus groups were conducted to understand why individuals become lost to care and how to maintain patient engagement. Focus groups involved active clinic patients and those lost to PKU care. Discussions revealed desire for enhanced communication, improved support and access, and community connections.Keywords: Phenylketonuria, PKU, Lost to care, Lost to followup, Patient engagement, Focus group

Introduction

Many adults with phenylketonuria (PKU) were discharged from clinic in childhood, prior to updated guidelines recommending treatment for life and prior to the advent of new therapies. The Boston Children's Hospital PKU team identified and attempted contact with 53 patients with PKU who had been lost to clinic follow-up for 5–50+ years [1]. In the first part of the project, we aimed to contact individuals who were lost to clinic, conduct structured interviews, share current recommendations, and offer logistical support in returning to clinical care. We identified the following reasons why individuals had not returned to clinic: discharge from clinic as a child, insurance coverage issues, financial or time constraints, lack of support from clinic providers or family members, and feelings of disconnectedness with the PKU community or treatment recommendations. This paper will share the second aim of this project, which was to conduct moderated focus groups to further understand reasons for individuals becoming lost to clinic and best practices for ongoing patient engagement.

Methods

As part of our study contacting former PKU clinic patients through the PHEFREE Consortium Pilot Program, we obtained IRB exempt status and completed discussions with our hospital compliance officers about approved modalities for searching and contacting former patients and engaging them in focus groups. Participants were identified either in the first part of the lost to PKU clinical care study or through clinic. They were invited to attend one of two focus groups, both of which were led by the study team's metabolic physician and dietitian: Focus Group 1 for active clinic patients and their families, or Focus Group 2 for individuals who had been lost to PKU clinical care. Participants were selected based on input from providers (if active clinic patients), their demonstrated willingness to participate in an additional activity, and their ability to offer of a diversity of perspectives. Participation was voluntary and without compensation. Nine individuals including patients and family members participated in a focus group discussion. Focus Group 1 for active clinic patients included five individuals: one older adolescent with PKU and their parent, both parents of young siblings with PKU, and one adult with PKU in their 50s. Focus Group 2 for individuals who had been lost to PKU clinical care included four individuals: one adult with PKU in their 60s, two adults with PKU in their 50s, and one adult with PKU in their 20s.

We developed a series of topics and questions in order to guide a discussion with the group, with the aim of ascertaining information on retention and communication for PKU patients. [See supplemental material.] Moderated focus group discussions around 90 min in duration were conducted on Zoom, and were recorded with permission. The guided discussion targeted topics including experiences with healthcare providers and PKU Clinic, access to supplies and insurance coverage, and PKU community support and engagement. The recordings of the focus groups were reviewed in order to extract both individual experience details as well as themes that presented from group discussions.

Results and discussion

The focus groups revealed several critical themes: the need for more comprehensive support from healthcare providers and PKU clinics, emphasizing the importance of a multidisciplinary team; difficulties with access to supplies and insurance; and the importance of community support and engagement. Participants were generous in their willingness to share their individual perspectives, which elucidate the impact of these areas in their lived experiences.

Experiences with healthcare providers and PKU clinic

Both focus groups highlighted the need for more support from healthcare providers, and the uniqueness of the lived PKU experience.

Participants in both focus groups described their ideal PKU care team as being multidisciplinary, including a psychologist and social worker. The family members of individuals with PKU in particular noted this need for social work connection. They felt that they were navigating a unique situation and would benefit from a provider offering resources and social support, including positive parenting support.

Individuals in the lost to PKU care group shared their previous connection to a single member of the multidisciplinary team. When that team member left, they then lost their consistent tie to clinic. Some individuals in the active patient group noted that they communicated regularly up to weekly with a member of their PKU team; however, other participants in this group noted difficulties with communicating with the clinic, including challenges with connecting directly to providers or making convenient appointments. Both groups expressed the need for ease and consistency of communication between clinic visits. They agreed that telehealth appointments were a way to significantly reduce barriers to care, eliminating the time and effort required to consistently travel to the Boston clinic location as well as eliminating for some a perceived stigma of being an adult in a children's hospital.

In addition to support and communication from PKU clinic, participants in both groups highlighted the need for their primary care providers to be informed and active members of their care team. Especially for those who had been lost to PKU clinical care, discussions with non-PKU healthcare providers had led to feelings of isolation. One individual in their mid-60s who had been lost to PKU clinic remarked, “I went to my [primary care] doctor in my mid-forties, and I mentioned I wanted to be tested for my [blood Phe] levels, and he had no clue about what it was or what to do. It fell through the cracks.” They described the frustration with attempting to follow up with their PKU care and feeling as though their primary care providers were unsure how to help them. Greater engagement from primary care providers, including appropriate metabolic center referrals and Phe monitoring through plasma amino acids, may help bridge the care gap some adults with PKU are facing. Overall, patients described a desire for more integrated support that fully leverages their healthcare network to ensure continuity of care.

Access to supplies and insurance

Affording medical products and navigating insurance coverage are significant challenges for individuals with PKU. Both focus groups described the burden of maintaining access to care and supplies and the maintenance of insurance coverage. State coverage mandates for PKU dietary products and medications vary. The majority of focus group participants were living in the state of Massachusetts, where there is mandated coverage for PKU formula and low protein food and generally with approved coverage of medications. The frustrations and burdens these patients and families have despite the relatively complete coverage of PKU supplies within the state of Massachusetts only reiterates just how challenging access to metabolic care can be. The specialized dietary products and medications essential for managing PKU are prohibitively expensive. This high cost places a considerable burden on patients and families to navigate and maintain the system's requirements, maintain adequate insurance coverage, and work with a variety of specialty pharmacies for PKU treatments.

Individuals may struggle with maintaining consistent insurance coverage, as policies and reimbursement practices can vary widely. The parents of two children with PKU actively followed in clinic described insurance as a “navigational nightmare.” Regardless of current coverage, many fear the impact that the loss of insurance coverage would have on the sustainability of expensive Phe-lowering medication management and fear having to revert to a strict diet after liberalization. The parent of an established patient stated, “We have a problem supporting [the cost of] formula and low protein food. Now the copays for [sapropterin] and [pegvaliase] are ugly. I am concerned! …I have personally been holding my daughter off from doing anything because I don't want her to go on this drug and suddenly, they don't have the funding for it.” Another parent remarked how they had difficulty accessing the funding for medical foods that were standard in the state of Massachusetts. Despite living in a state with mandated PKU insurance coverage, the complexities of dealing with insurance companies or state funding requirements can be overwhelming, requiring substantial time and effort, that can create financial and logistical barriers. Overall, patients and families needed more support to navigate insurance and to access medical food, low protein food products, and Phe-lowering medications.

Both groups noted that life transitions make these access hurdles even greater. Some individuals felt limited in where they could move, knowing that with any location change, they would need to understand the state's PKU treatment coverage system and that they would be required to find new PKU clinical care. One active clinic participant in college remarked, “It limits what I can do in the future because I won't have the same care outside of Boston. It takes a toll on you knowing you can't go anywhere in the world.” Concerns regarding access to appropriate foods and necessary medical care can impose a considerable mental burden, contributing to feelings of frustration and isolation. Individuals can feel obligated to limit their life choices, like traveling and jobs, due to the fear that their care will be negatively impacted.

The transition to adulthood for individuals with PKU is marked by increased autonomy and responsibility. One individual, who is college-aged and an active patient in our clinic, described their difficulties with transitioning into adulthood, “Coming out of college, coming off of your parents' health insurance, I have no idea what I'm doing. I won't know how to take care of myself. There needs to be a transition period for teens and young adults that teaches them how to handle insurance and to have a healthy diet with your parents less active. A young adult guide to PKU would be great!” Clinic-based support is crucial alongside family involvement. This may include a transition toolkit, group classes, and referrals to community groups, which can play a critical role in facilitating a smooth transition and development of self-management skills that include treatments themselves and how to navigate the insurance and healthcare system.

PKU community support and engagement

In terms of involvement with the PKU Community, the desires of individuals in the Focus Groups were mixed. Active clinic patients and families were very enthusiastic about ongoing connection with the PKU community. The two focus groups both emphasized their wish to connect with others who might share similar experiences. Individuals who had been lost to PKU clinic were all excited about the idea of connecting with and knowing other individuals with PKU; however, they were conflicted or ambivalent about reconnecting with the larger PKU community in a more formal capacity.

Individuals with PKU can experience feelings of isolation due to the unique challenges of managing their condition. PKU community events may provide an opportunity to meet others who face their similar challenges who are interested in engaging with a large PKU community. Additionally, opportunities for smaller group or one-on-one connections may help reach those who are hesitant to re-engage. One of the participants in their mid-60s, who had been lost to PKU clinic, remarked, “I know no one else who has this aside from the people in this Zoom right now. This focus group is for me to see who else has this and what challenges they face here.”

Within the active patient focus group, participants highlighted their desires for a support system outside of their clinical providers who could act as a guide and provide positive reinforcement. The parent of a child with PKU stated, “No one teaches you how to be a parent. No book tells you what to do. Then it's harder when it's being a parent of a child with a diagnosis like this.” The parents of two established children with PKU noted that, “I have never met any other families or kids in clinic… I have no idea who goes to Children's Hospital. That's terrifying that we have the highest standard but yet there is disconnect and lack of awareness.”

Both groups expressed a need for PKU Clinic to be actively involved as a bridge between patients and the PKU community. When asked what was the PKU Clinic's role in reaching individuals who had been lost to PKU clinic, the active patient group recommended an “all hands on deck” approach, utilizing clinic personnel alongside patient and community groups.

Conclusion

As part of our center's lost to PKU clinical care initiative through the PHEFREE Consortium, we held focus group discussions with established patients in PKU clinic and with those lost to PKU clinical care. The two focus groups discussed experiences with health care providers, access to care, and support systems. The focus groups highlighted what they desired from PKU clinic and other providers, including increased communication in between clinic visits, the option of telemedicine, support and proactive management by PKU clinic and primary care providers, and increased support from the care team through life stage and insurance transitions. There was significant concern about consistent access to PKU therapies. Nearly all described an interest in increased connection to the PKU community, either individually or in larger group environments.

The insights gained from these focus groups may inform the development of internal processes to prevent patients becoming lost to care. These efforts may include periodic re-contact, utilization of telemedicine, patient tracking strategies, and encouragement of patient engagement with local and national PKU patient and family organizations. Other strategies may include clinic sessions with age-matched peers with group classes to foster connectedness among individuals with PKU, as well as more robust efforts around supporting transition to adulthood and independence in PKU care for young adults. Lastly it would be beneficial to educate health care providers for adults that patients with PKU and many other inborn errors of metabolism should be followed by a metabolism clinic throughout adulthood, and should be referred as such. Limitations of this study included small sample size, ascertainment bias, and possible influence of having providers present as the focus group moderators. The insights gained pave the way for targeted interventions and future initiatives to enhance patient retention and care in this unique population.
