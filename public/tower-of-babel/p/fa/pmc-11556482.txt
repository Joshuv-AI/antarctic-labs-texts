Hypothyroidism in Metabolic Syndrome
Authors: Durga Prasad Verma, Shyam Chand Chaudhary, Abhishek Singh, Kamal Kumar Sawlani, Kamlesh Kumar Gupta, Kauser Usman, Himanshu Dandu Reddy, Munna Lal Patel, Sudhir Kumar Verma, Virendra Atam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Metabolic syndrome (MetS) and hypothyroidism are well-established forerunners of atherogenic cardiovascular disease (CVD). It is possible that patients suffering from both these disease entities may have a compounded risk. This study aimed at determining the prevalence of hypothyroidism in MetS.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2017 to August 2018 in the department of medicine at a tertiary care hospital in Northern India. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethical committee. The study subjects consisted of 157 patients with MetS, the diagnosis of which was based on the International Diabetes Federation criteria. After a detailed history and physical examination, relevant investigations including complete thyroid profile were done. The data were analyzed using appropriate statistical tests (P < 0.05).Results:In our study, the age of subjects ranged between 14 and 92 years, with a mean ± standard deviation of 48.1 ± 17.01 years. There were more females than males with a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.3. The prevalence of hypothyroidism was 46.5%. Hypothyroidism was more common in females (58.9%) as compared to males (41.1%). Patients with hypothyroidism had significantly higher body weight and body mass index (BMI) in comparison to euthyroid patients. The rest of the anthropometric parameters were comparable. Waist circumference and BMI of overt hypothyroid patients were found to be higher as compared to subclinical hypothyroid patients. Total cholesterol and triglyceride were significantly higher (P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively), while high-density lipoprotein levels were significantly lower in patients with hypothyroidism than the euthyroid group (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Hypothyroidism, especially subclinical hypothyroidism, is a common endocrine disorder in patients with MetS. As MetS and hypothyroidism are independent risk factors for CVD, hence there is a need for screening for hypothyroidism and the treatment of the same can be beneficial in reducing the cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in patients with MetS.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is described as a cluster of abnormalities including abdominal obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension, hyperglycemia, increased triglycerides (TGLs), and decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).[1] The prevalence of the MetS is increasing worldwide, and it affects even young adults and children. Many features of this syndrome are known to predispose men and women to premature coronary artery disease. There are several studies done in the recent years that have addressed the possible co-existence of multiple disorders and conditions that might affect cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk.

Hypothyroidism is one of the most common endocrine disorders and even subclinical hypothyroidism has been found to be associated with increased risk for atherosclerosis, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, low-grade inflammation, and hypercoagulability. Subclinical hypothyroidism is an asymptomatic condition with high serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and normal free thyroid hormone levels. It is a prevalent condition among adult population and is frequently overlooked.[2] Thyroid hormones affect metabolic parameters including HDL-C, TGL, blood pressure (BP), and plasma glucose. Previous studies have shown that higher TSH concentration is associated with increased incidence for the occurrence of MetS, especially in females. As MetS and hypothyroidism are independent risk factors for the same disease process, namely CVD, it is possible that patients suffering from both these disease entities may have a compounded risk. MetS includes a cluster of risk factors characterized by hypertension, dyslipidemia, and prothrombotic and pro-inflammatory conditions which accelerate the atherogenic process in the body.[34] Hypothyroidism is well known to cause hyperlipidemia, diastolic hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and CVD.[56] Considerable overlap occurs in pathogenic mechanism of atherosclerotic CVD by MetS and hypothyroidism.

A study on thyroid dysfunction in MetS population may help us to know the magnitude of overlap of these two groups and may highlight the importance of thyroid function test in identifying hypothyroid population in MetS. This can lead to proper planning and adequate management strategies, resulting in a significant reduction in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality due to MetS, by effective thyroid replacement therapy. This study intended to assess the prevalence of hypothyroidism in MetS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional study was done over a period of 1 year from September 2017 to August 2018 in the department of medicine at a tertiary care hospital in Northern India. Inclusion criteria: after taking written consent, a total of 157 male and female patients who fulfilled the criteria for MetS as defined by the International Diabetes Federation were included in the study. The International Diabetes Federation criteria included waist circumference which was ethnicity specific (measured midway between the uppermost border of the iliac crest and lowermost border of the costal margin; for men >90 cm and for women >80 cm) Plus any two of the below-mentioned criteria: (1) hypertriglyceridemia: TGLs more than 150 mg/dl or on specific medication (2) low HDL-C: <40 mg/ dl in men, < 50 mg/dl in women or on specific medication (3) hypertension: Blood Pressure – systolic >130 mmHg. Diastolic >85 mmHg or on specific medication (4) fasting plasma glucose >100 mg/dl or specific medication or previously diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM)/impaired fasting glucose. Exclusion criteria: patients with any of the following were excluded from the study: age <12 years, patients with type 1 diabetes, patients who are on oral contraceptive pills, patients with cardiopulmonary disease, patients with a history of pancreatitis, and patients in postoperative care.

Methodology

A detailed clinical history and physical examination was carried out for every subject. History was taken regarding symptoms of thyroid dysfunction and regarding presence of any co-morbidities like hypertension, dyslipidemia, DM or any other chronic disease. Thereafter detailed general and systemic physical examination including anthropometry (height, weight, waist circumference and body mass index [BMI]) was carried out. Investigations done in each and every patient included complete hemogram, fasting lipid profile, fasting and 2-h postprandial blood sugar, HbA1c, and thyroid profile (FT3/FT4/T3/T4/TSH). Thyroid function tests were done by chemiluminescent immunoassay method. Statistical analysis: The statistical analysis was done using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0 (software manufactured by IBM Corp. Illinois, Chicago). The values were represented in number (%) and mean ± standard deviation. Level of significance “P” mentioned in the results was considered significant if P < 0.05. Ethical clearance and funding: this study was approved by the institutional ethical committee and was not supported by any funding agency.

RESULTS

The present study included 157 patients with MetS (subjects) diagnosed as per the criteria of the International Diabetes Federation. The age of patients enrolled in the study ranged between 14 and 92 years. The mean age was 48.18 ± 17.01 years. Majority of the patients were aged >40 years (63.1%). The most common age groups were 51–60 years and 61–70 years (19.7% each), followed by 31–40 years (18.5%), while the least common age group was ≤20 years (5.7%), followed by >70 years (6.4%). Majority of the patients enrolled in the study were female (58.0%). Although the proportion of females with hypothyroid comorbidity was higher as compared to those without comorbidity (58.9% vs. 57.1%), this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.824).

DISCUSSION

Thyroid disorders are among the most common endocrine disorders, with an overall prevalence of hypothyroidism about 10.5% in general population.[7] Thyroid hormones play an important role in regulating energy balance, metabolism of glucose, and lipids. Individual components of MetS have been studied in relation to the thyroid status. Numerous studies conducted within the general population have consistently shown a direct correlation between thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and body mass index (BMI), arterial hypertension—particularly diastolic hypertension—serum cholesterol, and triglyceride (TGL) levels.[8910] MetS can be associated with endocrine and nonendocrine disorders and has widespread consequences.[11]

In our study, the prevalence of hypothyroidism was 46.5%, and subclinical hypothyroidism and overt hypothyroidism were 29.9% and 16.6%, respectively. There was a higher prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism than overt hypothyroidism. These findings are consistent with previous studies investigating thyroid function in MetS.

An Indian study conducted by Srikanth and Sumanth reported a 35.2% prevalence of hypothyroidism in MetS. Out of these, subclinical hypothyroidism was seen in 24.1% and overt hypothyroidism in 11.1% cases.[12] In the Indian population, Shantha et al. reported the prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism in 21.9% and that of overt hypothyroidism in 7.4% of MetS population.[13] Similarly, a study by Meher et al. showed a high prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism (22%) and overt hypothyroidism (4%) in MetS patients.[14] However, one more study reported by Gyawali et al. in Kavre district of Central Nepal observed thyroid dysfunction in 31.4% of MetS patients. The most common dysfunction was subclinical hypothyroidism (29.32%), followed by overt hypothyroidism (1.67%) and subclinical hyperthyroidism (0.83%).[15] In the hypothyroid group, most of the patients belong to subclinical hypothyroidism (29.9%), which is similar to our findings, i.e., subclinical hypothyroidism is more common as compared to overt hypothyroidism in the MetS group. Our finding were similar even with different population groups like studies on Hispanic population by Garduño Garcia Jde et al. and on Chennai urban population by Shantha et al.[1316]

In our study, the age of cases ranged between 14 and 92 years, with a mean of 48.1 ± 17.01 years. The maximum number of patients were seen in the age group 51–60 years (19.7%) and 61–70 years (19.7%), while the least number of patients were in the age group ≤20 years (5.7%). In a study done by Khatiwada et al., the mean age was 47 ± 12.5 years, which was similar to our study.[17] In our study, 42% were male and 58% were female. There was a female predominance which indicates that Mets is more prevalent among them. In a study done by Beigh and Jain, MetS was present both in women and men corresponding to 29% and 23% respectively. The prevalence was higher in women than in men. The contribution of several metabolic components to the MetS is different in men and women. This might contribute to gender-specific differences in the relative risk of metabolic complications such as insulin resistance.[18]

In the present study, subclinical hypothyroidism was more common in the age group of 31–40 years, while overt hypothyroidism was more common among the age group of 41–50 years. The higher prevalence of MetS in women in the present study may be due to their higher rate of obesity. In the present study, patients of MetS with hypothyroid had significantly higher body weight (86.19 ± 4.06 kg vs. 82.96 ± 6.68 kg) and BMI (90 ± 6.38 kg/m2 vs. 88.74 ± 5.10 kg/m2) as compared to MetS with euthyroid patients. A study conducted in the United States reported by Knudsen et al. has shown that even small differences in thyroid function are associated with change in BMI.[8]

In this study, the total cholesterol, TGL, and waist circumference were significantly higher and HDL level was significantly lower in patients with hypothyroidism than the euthyroid group. Hypothyroidism is associated with components of MetS such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, obesity, and often insulin resistance. It has been reported that 95% of newly diagnosed hypothyroid patients have increased level of cholesterol and 5% have hypertriglyceridemia. Hypothyroidism also leads to increase level of LDL cholesterol. All these factors directly contribute to accelerated atherosclerosis and increased CVD risk.[19] A number of studies have confirmed the associations of thyroid function with metabolic parameters in the general population.[20212223] Most of the studies conducted till date have been population-based, cross-sectional studies that performed correlation analyses of TSH and thyroid hormone levels with individual metabolic parameters. A study in Taiwan explored the serum TSH levels and MetS components and concluded that even slight increase in TSH, as in subclinical hypothyroidism, may be a MetS risk factor.[22]

The Nord-Trondelag Health (HUNT) study concluded that within the range of TSH that is considered clinically normal, increasing level of TSH is associated with less favorable lipid concentrations. The association with serum lipids was linear across the entire reference range of TSH.[24] Although our study covered groups with both normal and higher TSH (unlike the HUNT study which included only euthyroid individuals), there was a linear relation between lipid concentration and TSH levels across the entire range of TSH, which supports the role of thyroid hormones in the regulation of lipid metabolism, a dysregulation of which leads to MetS. A similar study done in the MetS group in the Hispanic population has shown a linear association of TSH with total cholesterol, LDL, TGLs, and waist circumference but no association with HDL.[16]

In a study including 2730 men and women, subclinical hypothyroidism was associated with an increased risk of congestive heart failure among older adults with a TSH level of 7.0 mlU/l or greater, but not with other cardiovascular events and mortality.[25] Another study found that there was no relationship between baseline TSH and cardiovascular mortality.[26]

In the present study, HDL-C had a significant negative linear association and FBS had a significant positive linear association with TSH level; however, other components of MetS had no statistically significant relationship. Thyroid hormones affect lipid metabolism and thus the components of MetS had a negative relation between TSH and HDL-C.[27] There are contrasting reports about the association between various MetS parameters and thyroid function. In a study in Nigeria, MetS was significantly associated with higher free T4 levels.[28] In a study in India, subclinical hypothyroidism was significantly associated with MetS and a linear association was observed between TSH levels and total cholesterol, TGLs, LDL, and HDL-C levels across the MetS group. However, in a study in Turkey, TSH was not related to any MetS parameters.[29]

The association of hypothyroidism with components of MetS was assessed by multiple logistic regression analysis in the present study. Waist circumference and HDL level showed a significant association with hypothyroidism in patients with MetS (P = 0.025 and P < 0.001, respectively), whereas there was no statistically significant association with total cholesterol, TGLs, and LDL. According to our present study, MetS patients with higher waist circumference and low HDL are more prone to having associated hypothyroidism. Knudsen et al.’s study has shown that even small difference in thyroid function are associated with change in BMI.[8] However, Kota et al. have recently demonstrated the absence of any significant relation between severity of obesity and serum TSH level.[30] In a cross-sectional study including 1333 euthyroid participants, subjects with a TSH in the upper normal range (2.5–4.5 mU/l) were more obese, had higher TGLs, and had an increased likeliness for the MetS. Therefore, a TSH below 2.5 mU/l was proposed to be associated with favorable metabolic profile.[31] Another study including 3148 subjects concluded that subclinical hypothyroidism was not associated with increased risk of MetS. Despite this, low thyroid function (even in euthyroid state) predisposed to higher cholesterol, glucose, insulin, and insulin resistance.[16] Perhaps, TSH elevation stimulates the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, which leads to an increase in the components of MetS.[22] Literature on the effect of treatment of subclinical hypothyroidism on components of MetS is scarce. As such, it was suggested that whether lowering TSH to levels below 2.5 mU/I improves metabolism needs to be investigated in interventional trials.[31]

In our study, we observed a high prevalence of both overt and subclinical hypothyroidism among individuals with Metabolic Syndrome (MetS). This leads us to suggest a direct correlation between hypothyroidism and factors such as elevated triglyceride (TGL) levels, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and high fasting blood sugar (FBS). These factors also have harmful impact on cardiovascular health. Thus, assessing thyroid function in Mets patients may help identify patients at high risk for CVD. However, it is still unclear whether patients with subclinical hypothyroidism should be treated, and the distinct benefit of prescribing levothyroxine for the cardiovascular benefits in these patients is still debatable.

CONCLUSION

As MetS and hypothyroidism are independent risk factors for the same disease process, namely CVD, it is possible that patients suffering from both disease entities may have a compounded risk. Hence, there is a need for screening for thyroid dysfunction and treatment with thyroxin in hypothyroid state even in subclinical condition; which may reduce the mortality and morbidity in patients with MetS.

Limitations

However, the present study has few limitations like small sample size. A study with large number of population with control is required to put a conclusion on the present study. However, our study is helpful for defining the correlation between hypothyroidism and associated risk factors in patients of MetS.
