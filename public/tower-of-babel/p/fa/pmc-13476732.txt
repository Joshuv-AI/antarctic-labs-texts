Tattoo blowout and pruritus
Authors: Simona A. Alomary, Rosalie Elenitsas, Michelle A. Weir
Published: 2026

A woman in her 30s presented with a 4-month history of persistent pruritus localized to a new tattoo on the medial upper arm. The tattoo had been placed approximately 4 months earlier. The patient reported that the healing process for this tattoo differed from prior tattoos (including 1 on the ipsilateral forearm), with persistent pruritus and a hazy appearance of the tattoo consistent with “tattoo blowout” or “ink drift.” These terms refer to a blurry or bruised appearance within the normal skin surrounding the tattoo, causing the borders of the tattoo to appear hazy.

Physical examination revealed a red, green, and black tattoo on the right upper medial arm with visible green-black discoloration of the nontattooed areas of surrounding skin and mild eczematous changes within some of the green pigmented areas (Fig 1). No papules, nodules, or induration were visualized within the tattoo or the surrounding skin. Given these findings, an allergic reaction to components of tattoo ink was considered. There was a low suspicion for granulomatous reactions such as atypical mycobacterium or cutaneous sarcoidosis. At the next visit, the patient presented with worsening pruritus despite the use of topical steroids and consented to 2 punch biopsies for further investigation. Biopsy specimens were obtained from tattoo skin on the distal right arm and nontattooed skin in an area of “ink drift” on the proximal right arm. Histopathologic examination of both biopsy specimens demonstrated exogenous pigment throughout the superficial and deep dermis. In the more proximal specimen (nontattooed skin), pigment was visualized extending into the subcutaneous fat (Fig 2). Mild superficial lymphocytic perivascular inflammation was seen in the distal sample. No granulomatous inflammation was present in either specimen. Periodic acid-Schiff and Fite stains were negative in both biopsies.Fig 2Histopathology of the proximal biopsy specimen showing scattered exogenous pigment deposition at the dermal/subcutaneous junction, as demonstrated by yellow arrows (H&E, ×200).

Tattoo blowout is thought to occur when ink is deposited too deeply into the skin’s dermal layers, penetrating the subcutaneous fat. It often results from improper tattoo artist techniques, such as excessive needle pressure. Dermal thickness and connective tissue density vary by body site, and areas such as the inner arm may be more prone to this phenomenon.1 Pruritus after tattoo placement is often attributed to allergic contact dermatitis, cutaneous sarcoidosis, or atypical mycobacterial infection.2,3 These conditions typically present with papules, nodules, or plaques and a corresponding granulomatous or lichenoid inflammatory reaction on histopathologic examination.3,4 While these diagnoses were considered, the absence of a papular component and lack of granulomatous reaction or significant inflammatory infiltrate made these diagnoses unlikely. Rather, the depth and distribution of exogenous pigment raise the possibility that mechanical pigment deposition beyond the superficial dermis may have contributed to the patient’s pruritus, possibly through local tissue irritation. Nonetheless, causality cannot be established. When evaluating a patient with isolated pruritus and a tattoo with a similar clinical appearance to that of this patient’s, tattoo blowout should be considered.
