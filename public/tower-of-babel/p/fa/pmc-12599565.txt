Impact of fluid creep on volume and chloride load in critically ill adults: a prospective study on its prognostic significance and association with MAKE30
Authors: Dingxin Zhou, Fengsheng Cao, Lu Zhang, Guangyu Qiu, Yusheng Chen, Yiming Li, Zhiyong Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo quantify ICU fluids for different uses, identify significant fluid sources for volume and chloride load in critically ill adults, and analyze their impact on prognosis.MethodsA prospective study was conducted in a 52-bed academic ICU. From May to December 2022, volume intake/output and fluid purpose were recorded for 5 days for all admitted patients. Fluids were categorized into five types. Patients were divided into high and low fluid creep groups by median dosage. Logistic regression was used to analyze the association with Major Adverse Kidney Events by 30 days (MAKE30).Results1,633 adult critically ill patients with a median age of 67 years and an APACHE II score of 22 were included. Fluid creep, with a median of 802 ml, accounted for 48.4% of daily fluid volume, far more than resuscitation (8.6%) or maintenance/replacement fluid (6.7%). After adjustment for confounders, high fluid creep was associated with a higher incidence of MAKE30 (OR = 1.37) and its components, including in-hospital mortality (OR = 1.49), new renal replacement therapy (OR = 1.83), and persistent renal damage (OR = 1.75).ConclusionsFluid creep is an important source of volume and chloride load in critically ill adults, and high volumes are associated with a higher incidence of MAKE30. Intravenous fluid therapy should be used with caution.

Introduction

In recent years, intravenous fluid therapy for critically ill patients has garnered significant research attention, encompassing various aspects such as timing, rate, volume, and type of therapy. In this field, NaCl 0.9%, as one of the primary resuscitation fluids, has become a significant point of debate, particularly when compared to the advantages and disadvantages of balanced crystalloids. Notably, the rationale for using saline in critically ill patients, along with its potential side effects, has attracted considerable attention from researchers [1–6]. A key reason for the criticism of saline is its chloride concentration, which is approximately 50% higher than that of plasma. Intravenous fluid therapy is the primary contributor to increased volume and chloride load in critically ill patients and can even be the sole cause in those with coexisting gastrointestinal dysfunction. While resuscitation fluids are the most extensively studied source of intravenous fluids for critically ill patients and maintenance fluids have received increasing attention in recent years [7–9]. However, fluids as drug vehicles are also an important component of fluid therapy, but often overlooked.

Fluid overload is an independent risk factor for morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients [10,11]. Previous studies of fluid therapy have focused primarily on resuscitation fluids [12]. However, in critically ill patients, the fluids that contribute most to total volume are not resuscitation fluids [13]. Instead, maintenance fluids or fluid creep play a significant role in the total volume load [8]. Once hemodynamically stable, the fluids contained in the daily medications and foods used in critically ill patients can usually reach several liters, which can meet the basic fluid needs and thus eliminating the need for regular maintenance fluids [14,15]. In contrast, fluid creep is the fluid that must be used in almost every critically ill patient, and it includes the fluids that are used as drug or electrolyte vehicles. These fluids are passively administered and may have a significant impact on the volume load. The term fluid creep means unintentional infusion of a volume of fluid that perhaps exceeds the amount of fluid needed by the patient [16].

In this study, we aim to gather detailed information on the use of fluids in the ICU, including their volumes, relative proportions, and trends over the course of a patient’s stay. We will also analyze the impact of different types of fluids, with a particular emphasis on fluid creep, on volume and chloride loading in critically ill patients, as well as on MAKE30 (major adverse kidney events within 30 days). The findings will provide valuable insights to guide the adjustment of clinical fluid management strategies.

Methods

Research population

We conducted a prospective observational study in a 52-bed general ICU in Xiangyang Central Hospital. Due to the observational nature of the study, the hospital ethics committee granted a waiver to sign the informed consent form (decision letter number 2022-048). All adult patients admitted to the ICU from May to December 2022 were included in this study.

Data collection

We recorded detailed information on fluid administration, including purpose of use, dosage and type of fluid, for each of the first 5 days of ICU stay for all patients admitted to the ICU during the study period. The sources of input fluids include intravenous drugs, oral drugs, oral diet, nasogastric feeding, intravenous irrigation lines or those used to keep the lines open,while the sources of output fluids include all types of drainage, vomit, defecation, and sputum. The first day of ICU stay starts from admission to the ICU until 8:00 am The last day of ICU stay starts from 8:00 am until the patient discharged from the ICU. At the beginning of the study we trained all recording staff, mainly nurses, on the subject.

Definitions

All fluids used daily for each patient can be divided into 5 main categories depending on the purpose of use: resuscitation fluids; maintenance/replacement fluids; fluid creep; blood products; and nutritional fluids. Each type of fluid has a clear definition.

Resuscitation fluids

usually Ringer’s lactate or saline, which is infused rapidly to maintain hemodynamic stability and does not contain other drugs or electrolytes. For patients with accompanied hypoproteinemia (<30 g/L) who concurrently require fluid resuscitation, clinicians will supplement with 20% human albumin injection as a resuscitation solution depending on the degree of albumin reduction.

Maintenance fluids

refers to providing intravenous infusion and electrolytes to patients who can’t meet the body’s needs through oral or intestinal routes, but have good performance in the balance and metabolism of fluids and electrolytes, that is, these patients basically have normal blood volume, no obvious electrolyte deficiency, no persistent abnormal fluid loss or complicated redistribution problems of the body. It is customary in our ICU not to routinely use maintenance fluids, but to consider them only if the patient’s prescribed fluid volume for the day is less than the daily fluid requirement (25–30 mL/kg/d) and to add them temporarily.

Replacement fluids

refers to intravenous fluids and electrolytes used in hemodynamically stable patients to compensate for abnormal loss or redistribution of body fluids that have existed or are occurring, usually from the gastrointestinal tract (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, vomiting, or diarrhea) or urinary tract, and redistribution of fluids due to capillary leakage in patients with severe sepsis [17]. It is important to note that replacement fluids are usually replenished after the daily maintenance needs have been met, and the purpose of fluid replacement is to provide circulatory support, prevent the loss of compensation that requires fluid resuscitation, maintain tissue perfusion, and restore electrolyte balance.

Fluid creep

refers to those dissolving or diluting solvents for intravenous, oral or enteral drugs or electrolytes, as well as keep lines open (peripheral venous access, central venous access, central venous pressure monitoring access, jejunal nutrition tubes, etc.), which are not Intentionally used but may have a significant impact on fluid volume. The intravenous drug vehicle is mainly normal saline, using intravenous drips or pumps, with occasional use of 5% dextrose injection according to drug instructions, and warm boiled water for oral and nasal medications.

Blood products

including plasma, cold precipitation, red blood cells, platelets and other kinds of component blood. Blood is managed with saline flushing before and after transfusion to avoid hemolytic reaction and waste. The flushing fluid before and after transfusion is classified as fluid creep like other flushing fluids.

Nutrition solutions

including enteral nutrition and intravenous nutrition, enteral nutrition is recorded separately in the nursing record, and the volume of intravenous nutrition is usually greater than 500 mL, containing fatty milk, amino acids, glucose, electrolytes, among others. It is usually packed in three-liter bags to control the infusion rate.

We have a strict fluid management strategy in the ICU. Resuscitation fluids are usually adjusted at any time based on inferior vena cava variability or other fluid responsiveness tests; maintenance fluids are usually not used; daily fluid balance is strictly controlled and negative fluid balance is achieved as early as possible; and enteral nutrition is started as early as possible if the patient’s intestinal condition permits.

We analyzed the effect of fluid creep dosing on MAKE30 and its various components in critically ill patients. The primary outcome was the incidence of MAKE30 after ICU admission in critically ill patients, which is a composite indicator that includes in-hospital death from any cause, new initiation of renal replacement therapy (RRT), and persistent renal damage (PRD) (last serum creatinine at discharge greater than or equal to twice the baseline serum creatinine). Data were collected until hospital discharge or 30 days after enrollment, whichever came first.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and statistically analyzed using SPASS 26.0, and data were tested for normality using Shapiro-wilk, with statistical significance at two-sided p < 0.05. Data that did not conform to a normal distribution were expressed as M (IQR), and normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages n (%). Binary logistic regression was used to explore the correlation between fluid dosage and outcomes.

Results

Fluid creep accounts for the largest proportion in fluid therapy in the ICU

A total of 1,656 patients were admitted to the ICU from May to December 2022, and 23 patients aged less than 18 years were excluded, resulting in the inclusion of 1633 patients with a cumulative total of 5,305 ICU inpatient days. The median age was 67 (55, 76) years, the median APACHE II score was 22 (16, 29), the median ICU stay was 3 (1, 7) days, and the all-cause in-hospital mortality rate was 11.7% (n = 191).

Within the category of fluid creep, drug solvents were the predominant component. These solvents were used to dissolve various medications, including antibiotics, vasoactive agents, analgesics, sedatives, and concentrated electrolytes, which were administered via intravenous drip or syringe pump. When normal saline was used as the drug solvent, it contributed an average of 116 ± 89 mmol of chloride ions per day. Additionally, 3.5% of fluid creep was allocated to maintaining line patency, while 2.8% was used for dissolving or diluting oral medications.

Resuscitation fluids accounted for 8.6% of the daily fluid usage in the ICU, with an average of 153 ± 511 mL per person per day. Crystalloid resuscitation fluids accounted for 7.9% of the total fluid. If saline were exclusively utilized as the crystalloid resuscitation fluid, it would furnish approximately 22 ± 75 mmol of chloride ions per person per day. The 20% human albumin injection for resuscitation represented 0.7% of all fluids. Maintenance and replacement fluids contributed 6.9% to the total daily fluid intake, with individual proportions of 2.6% and 4.3% respectively. Nutritional fluids emerged as the second major source of fluids for ICU patients, accounting for 28.9%.

We then examined the proportional alterations in fluid utilization for diverse purposes during the initial five days subsequent to ICU admission, and the corresponding outcomes are presented in Figure 2. As septic patients often require early fluid resuscitation, a separate analysis was performed for this subgroup. For all ICU patients, the proportion of fluid creep remained relatively high, ranging from 46% to 50% in first five days. Even as the length of stay in the ICU increased, this proportion showed only marginal variation, decreasing slightly from 50% on day 1 to 48% on day 5, remaining the predominant source of fluid for ICU patients. In contrast, the proportion of average daily fluid volume provided by resuscitation fluids fell sharply over the duration of ICU stay in all ICU patients. It fell from 19% on the first day to a meager 2% on the fifth day. The proportion of daily fluid consumption accounted for by maintenance/replacement fluids in the ICU decreased progressively over the course of the hospitalization, from 10% on day 1 to 2% by day five. Similar patterns and trends of different kinds of fluids were observed in septic patients. An increased dosage of fluid creep is associated with a higher incidence of MAKE30 in critically ill patients

To further analyze the impact of fluid creep on the prognosis of critically ill patients, patients were divided into a high fluid creep group (mean daily fluid creep dose > 802 mL/d) and a low fluid creep group (mean daily fluid creep dose ≤ 802 mL/d) based on the mean daily fluid creep dose of 802 (450, 1238) ml/d for each patient during the first 5 days of ICU stay (Table 2). Significant differences were observed between the two patient groups in terms of APACHE II scores, the prevalence of hypertension, underlying cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, daily average fluid balance, as well as the incidence of sepsis, traumatic brain injury (TBI), poisoning (primarily oral ingestion of pesticides and psychotropic drugs), severe pneumonia, and the proportion of patients requiring mechanical ventilation. Compared with the low fluid creep group, the high fluid creep group had a significantly higher incidence of MAKE30, 34.6% (n = 282) vs. 17.0% (n = 139), p < 0.001; a significantly higher mortality rate, 16.8% (n = 137) vs. 6.6% (n = 54), p < 0.001; and significantly higher new RRT utilization rate (20.9% vs. 9.4%), incidence of PRD (9.5% vs. 3.2%), length of ICU stay (4 days vs. 2 days) and total hospital costs (43,856 yuan vs. 26,550 yuan), p < 0.001.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that fluid creep constitutes the largest portion of daily fluid therapy in critically ill patients, far surpassing the amount of resuscitation fluids, with its proportion remaining relatively stable throughout the ICU stay. The amount and proportion of resuscitation fluids rapidly decreased during the first few days of ICU admission, while the volume of nutritional fluids quickly increased. Maintenance and replacement fluids comprised only a small part of the daily fluid usage in the ICU. High fluid creep was associated with an increased incidence of MAKE30 in critically ill patients, as well as its components, including in-hospital mortality, the rate of new RRT utilization, and the incidence of PRD. Our study provides a new direction for intravenous fluid therapy in critically ill patients.

In our study, as a vehicle for the dissolution of intravenous, oral and nasal medications, and as a fluid to keep lines open, fluid creep accounts for up to 48.4% of daily fluid therapy in critically ill patients far exceeding all other types of fluids, which is consistent with the findings of Van Regenmortel’s study. [8] also found that fluid creep accounted for the largest proportion in fluid therapy in the ICU. However, compared to our fluid management policy, there are some slight differences. We do not routinely use maintenance fluids. As a result, the percentage of maintenance/replacement fluids in our data is only 6.9%, much lower than 24.7%, with the main component being replacement fluids (4.3%). However, despite this difference, the overall conclusion remains consistent.

Fluid creep is not typically regarded as a factor influencing fluid balance, and critical care physicians tend to focus primarily on resuscitation fluids. Our results show that fluid creep is the source of fluid that has the greatest impact on cumulative metered fluid balance in critically ill patients. To avoid the adverse effects of fluid overload, ICU implement strict fluid management strategies, typically involving the use of diuretics. Our findings provide a novel approach for interventions, suggesting that by targeting the dosage of fluid creep, it is possible to effectively accomplish volume management in critically ill patients. On the other hand, several studies have demonstrated that low-tension fluids are excreted more rapidly than equivalent amounts of isotonic fluids [7,18]. This characteristic makes it easier to decrease the volume load, and this mechanism is likely to be even more pronounced for creep fluids, given that they constitute the largest proportion of fluids. Additionally, fluid creep that leads to a reduction in plasma chloride concentration may have a beneficial impact on the maintenance and restoration of renal function [19–21]. This, in turn, could enhance urine output and contribute to a reduction in volume load.

Due to the unavailability of detailed data regarding fluid composition, we hypothesized that if all saline were fluid creep in this study, it would supply 134 ± 95 mmol of sodium chloride daily, exceeding the 1 mmol/kg/d recommended by UK NICE guidelines [22]. In clinical practice, most intravenous drugs are dissolved in saline [13,23], and its overuse elevates plasma chloride. Hyperchloremia or chloride fluctuations potentially linked to acute kidney injury and higher mortality in critically ill patients [20,21,24–26].

Our findings suggest fluid creep as a major chloride source. Modifying its type could reduce hyperchloremia incidence. Bihari et al. showed replacing 0.9% sodium chloride with 5% glucose in fluid creep and maintenance fluids reduced chloride load and positive fluid balance without adverse effects [27]. In the absence of routine maintenance fluid use, fluid creep becomes a key intervention for reducing both chloride and volume load, highlighting the need for further optimization of fluid management in critical care.

Fluid management strategies in ICUs vary considerably across different countries and regions, and whether maintenance fluids are routinely used is one aspect of this. Most of the existing research literature involves the routine use of maintenance fluids. However, even in ICU that use maintenance fluids, their clinical practice patterns also differ greatly [28], including the dosage, rate, and component selection of maintenance fluids. Since the daily diet and intravenous medications can already meet the basic fluid needs of the majority of patients, maintenance fluids are not routinely used in our ICU. This policy avoids the increase in volume load and chloride load caused by maintenance fluids. In the future, we hope to conduct a more detailed comparison between different fluid management strategies.

Our study also has some limitations. Firstly, these data are from the ICU of a single hospital. Although fluid management strategies in ICUs in different regions may vary greatly, the classification of fluids for different usage purposes is universal. Moreover, the harmful effects of fluid overload are already widely accepted among intensivists, so the general direction of fluid management strategies is unified. Secondly, we did not precisely control and record the infusion rate of each type of fluid, especially the resuscitation fluid. Although there are records of the infusion rate for intravenous nutrition, syringe pumps, and vasoactive drugs, we could not distinguish the purpose of drug use based on the intravenous infusion rate, as done in the study by Van Regenmortel et al. [8] We distinguished the purpose of drug use based on detailed nursing records and fluid composition. Lastly, the first day of ICU stay starts from the admission to the ICU until 8:00 am, and the last day of ICU stay starts from 8:00 am until the patient is discharged from the ICU. This may result in some very short ICU stays during which patients may receive a very small amount of fluid.

Conclusions

Fluid creep, rather than resuscitation fluid, is the major source of both volume load and chloride load in critically ill adult patients. Not routinely using maintenance fluids proves to be a viable strategy for fluid management in these patients. By adjusting the dosage and composition of fluid creep, it is possible to effectively reduce both volume and chloride load. Furthermore, fluid creep is associated with the prognosis of critically ill adult patients, highlighting the urgent need for more in-depth research into its impact and management.
