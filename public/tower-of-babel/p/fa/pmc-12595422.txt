Osimertinib-induced aplastic anemia after curative surgery for EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Tomoyuki Kawamura, Kenji Minagi, Syuntaro Kawabata, Hisashi Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osimertinib, a third-generation epidermal growth factor receptor-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI), is widely used to treat EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) because of its high efficacy and favourable safety profile. However, rare but serious hematologic toxicities such as aplastic anemia may occur because of osimertinib use. Here, we report a case of severe aplastic anemia in a 75-year-old woman treated with osimertinib because of postoperative recurrence of EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinoma. The patient had previously received adjuvant oral tegafur-uracil for 2 years without any hematologic toxicity. Lymph node recurrence was diagnosed 4.5 years after surgery, and osimertinib therapy was initiated. After approximately 19 weeks, the patient developed pancytopenia including grade 3 thrombocytopenia, which necessitated drug discontinuation. Bone marrow biopsy revealed a severely hypocellular marrow without dysplastic or malignant features, confirming the diagnosis of drug-induced aplastic anemia. Treatment with cyclosporine led to gradual hematologic improvement and transfusion independence. This is a rare case of osimertinib-induced aplastic anemia occurring after curative-intent surgery for NSCLC, in contrast to previously published reports that mainly involved patients with advanced or metastatic disease. Clinicians should be aware of this rare but life-threatening complication and closely monitor peripheral blood counts during treatment, particularly within the first 2–8 months after treatment initiation.

Introduction

Osimertinib, a third-generation irreversible epidermal growth factor receptor-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI), is widely used in the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) because it selectively inhibits both EGFR–TKI sensitisation and T790M resistance mutations [1]. Although osimertinib is generally well tolerated [2], its use has been associated with aplastic anemia (AA), albeit very rarely [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Moreover, recent phase 3 data showed that adding platinum–pemetrexed to osimertinib further improved progression-free survival compared with monotherapy, although grade ≥3 adverse events increased, mainly owing to chemotherapy [8]. As osimertinib use broadens, careful attention to rare but serious toxicities is warranted.

AA is an exceptionally uncommon adverse event, but one that can lead to life-threatening outcomes if it is not promptly recognized and managed [9]. To date, only a limited number of osimertinib-induced AA cases have been reported, most of which involved patients with stage IV NSCLC and distant metastases. Here, we report a rare case of severe AA that developed during osimertinib therapy for postoperative recurrence of EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old non-smoking woman underwent left upper lobectomy with mediastinal lymph node dissection for lung adenocarcinoma. The pathological diagnosis was stage IA2 (pT1bN0M0). Although the total tumor diameter was 25 mm, the solid (invasive) component measured 16 mm according to the TNM classification, 8th edition, meeting the criteria for stage T1b. She had a history of colon cancer, for which she underwent surgical resection 3 years before surgery for lung cancer. She did not receive any adjuvant therapy, and there has been no evidence of recurrence since surgery. She had no history of other cancers or of receiving radiotherapy or immunosuppressive therapy before starting adjuvant treatment with oral tegafur-uracil (UFT), which she received for 2 years. No hematologic toxicity was observed during UFT therapy or in the 4.5 years following its completion. Four and a half years after the surgery, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed enlargement of the left hilar and pulmonary ligament lymph nodes (Fig. 1A and B). 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) showed increased uptake at both sites (maximum standardized uptake value [SUVmax]: 7.89 and 6.71, respectively) without evidence of distant metastasis (Fig. 1C and D). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with postoperative recurrence. Given the presence of the EGFR L858R mutation in the primary tumor, treatment with osimertinib 80 mg daily was initiated. After osimertinib therapy for 19 weeks, the patient developed severe pancytopenia with grade 3 thrombocytopenia (42,000 × 109 cells/L), accompanied by grade 1 anemia (hemoglobin [Hb] level: 10.8 g/dL) and grade 1 leucopenia (white blood cell [WBC] count: 2000 × 109 cells/L), prompting immediate treatment discontinuation (Fig. 2). All parameters had been within normal limits before osimertinib treatment. Despite osimertinib discontinuation, pancytopenia progressed, requiring biweekly red blood cell and platelet transfusions. Serum biochemical indices of liver and kidney function were normal. Extensive workup ruled out infectious causes and nutritional deficiencies, including iron, vitamin B12, and folate deficiencies. The initial treatment with oral metenolone was ineffective. Two months later, cyclosporine was initiated, which resulted in gradual hematologic recovery. After 4 months, transfusion independence was achieved. At 6 months, blood count improved: platelet count, 33,000 × 109 cells/L; WBC count, 3800 × 109 cells/L; and Hb level, 8.3 g/dL. The lymph nodes regressed promptly after the initiation of osimertinib. However, 11 months after its discontinuation and in the absence of systemic therapy, the previously regressed nodes showed slight re-enlargement. Nevertheless, systemic treatment was not resumed because complete hematologic recovery from AA had not yet been achieved.

Discussion

Osimertinib is widely used to treat EGFR-mutated NSCLC, because of its high efficacy and mild toxicity compared with first- or second-generation TKIs [2,10,11]. Furthermore, adjuvant osimertinib therapy has shown significant improvements in disease-free survival in patients with resected stage IB–IIIA EGFR-mutated NSCLC [12]. However, although rare, serious hematological toxicities, including AA, can be fatal. The estimated incidence of osimertinib-induced AA is approximately 0.1 % and only a few cases have been documented to date [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]].

Furthermore, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying osimertinib-induced AA remain unclear. The three major causes of acquired AA are direct toxicity, metabolite-driven toxicity, and immune-mediated mechanisms. However, the exact pathogenesis of osimertinib-associated AA remains unknown [13,14]. Any of the mechanisms may result in profound pancytopenia. In the present case, the diagnosis of AA was confirmed by a markedly hypocellular bone marrow biopsy, and infectious, autoimmune, and malignant etiologies were excluded. Thus, osimertinib was considered the most likely cause of the AA in this patient, although other causes could not be completely ruled out.

Our case findings align with those of previous reports, highlighting the rare but serious risk of AA associated with osimertinib. Notably, most previously reported cases involved patients with advanced NSCLC who had received previous chemotherapy or radiotherapy [3,5,6]. These reports mainly included patients with stage IV NSCLC and distant metastases, whereas our case involved locoregional recurrence after curative surgery. Although the possibility of a cumulative hematopoietic effect from prior UFT therapy cannot be completely excluded, this difference in clinical background suggests that osimertinib-related AA can also develop in patients without heavy prior treatment exposure. Previous reports have described cases in which patients improved with supportive measures such as transfusions or use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor alone. However, in our patient, cytopenia did not resolve with supportive care and required the introduction of cyclosporine. The use of cyclosporine ultimately led to transfusion independence, which is consistent with a previous report describing the effectiveness of immunosuppressive therapy in drug-induced AA [6]. Our findings, therefore, provide additional clinical context for the potential role of cyclosporine in the management of osimertinib-related AA.

The temporal relationship between osimertinib initiation and the onset of pancytopenia (approximately 20 weeks) was similar to that in previous reports, in which pancytopenia was reported within 10–31 weeks of treatment. Real-world pharmacovigilance led to the reporting of hematologic adverse events associated with osimertinib, including cytopenias, with a median onset of 58 days (interquartile range: 14–212) [15], encompassing both early and later presentations. Taken together, these findings indicate that AA can develop across a wide time window, underscoring the need for regular blood count monitoring, particularly within the first 2–8 months. Regarding subsequent management, Di Marino et al. reported that osimertinib could be resumed at a reduced dose after hematological recovery in select patients [3]. However, our patient did not resume osimertinib treatment because of concerns over relapse of marrow suppression. Although osimertinib-induced AA is rare, it is a potentially fatal adverse event requiring careful attention. Therefore, close monitoring of peripheral blood counts remains essential during treatment, when early diagnosis may be critical for preventing serious complications.

Conclusions

This case highlights the development of AA following osimertinib treatment for postoperative recurrence of lung cancer. Clinicians should be aware of this potential adverse event and ensure regular monitoring of peripheral blood counts during treatment.
