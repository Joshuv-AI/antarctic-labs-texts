Impact of long COVID phenotypes on quality of life following symptomatic omicron infection in Brazil: a machine learning analysis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to identify phenotypes of long COVID symptoms in adults following Omicron infection and assess their association with health-related quality of life (HRQoL).MethodsWe analyzed three prospective observational studies in Brazil, enrolling adult patients who sought care for symptomatic Omicron infection between December 2021 and March 2023. The infection was confirmed by either an antigen test or reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction. Long COVID symptoms were assessed three months after enrollment through structured interviews. Phenotypes of Long COVID-19 were identified using a machine learning-based clustering approach. Exploratory analyses were conducted to examine predisposing factors and health-related quality of life utilities, measured by EQ-5D-3 L, associated with each phenotype.ResultsA total of 2,989 patients were analyzed (39% women, median age 41 years, and 96% had completed the primary series of COVID-19 vaccination). Long COVID symptoms at three months were reported by 1,155 (38.6%) patients. Three phenotypes were identified: cluster 1 (n = 459 [39.7%]), characterized by a median of three symptoms (IQR, 2–5) with memory loss (80.4%), concentration problems (38.3%) and fatigue (35.7%) being most common; cluster 2 (n = 549 [47.5%]), characterized by a median of two symptoms (IQR, 1–4) with fatigue (43.7%), other symptoms (42.3%), and cough (20.6%) being most common; and cluster 3 (n = 147, 12.7%), characterized by a higher number of symptoms (median, 8; IQR, 7–10), with fatigue (89.9%), memory loss (88.4%), and anxiety (64.6%) as the most common. The mean EQ-5D-3 L utility at 3 months was 0.75 for cluster 1, 0.73 for cluster 2, and 0.59 for cluster 3 (p < 0.001). After adjusted regression analysis, cluster 3 was independently associated with the lowest EQ-5D-3 L utilities (mean difference, -0.21; 95%CI, -0.24 to -0.18; p < 0.001).ConclusionsDistinct phenotypic presentations of Long COVID following Omicron infection in Brazil were identified, with significant differences in quality of life.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11956-6.

Introduction

Persistent or newly emerging symptoms following SARS-CoV-2 infection, commonly referred to as post-COVID-19 condition or long COVID, remain a significant global health challenge [1–3]. While the acute phase of the disease has been well characterized, the heterogeneity of long COVID symptoms, their underlying mechanisms, and their impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) have not been fully elucidated [4]. Although recent studies have investigated different phenotypic presentations of long COVID [5–13], most have been conducted in high-income settings. This limits their generalizability, particularly to low- and middle-income countries, where healthcare infrastructure, vaccination rates, and variant prevalence may differ significantly. In these regions, data on long COVID remain scarce, further complicating the diagnosis and management of the condition. Previous analyses comparing the evolution of COVID-19 across different countries have demonstrated marked heterogeneity in long-term outcomes, likely influenced by healthcare infrastructure, vaccination rates, and social policies [14]. Accordingly, this study aimed to identify phenotypes of Long COVID symptoms following Omicron infection in Brazil using an unsupervised machine learning and assess their association with HRQoL.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a post-hoc nested analysis of three prospective observational studies (NCT05052307 – registered at November 3rd 2021, NCT05165979 – registered at November 21st 2021, NCT05197647 – registered at January 25th 2022) originally designed to assess long-term outcomes of patients with symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection in Brazil during the predominance of Omicron variant [15]. The rationale and design of these studies have been published previously [16–18]. Briefly, adult patients who sought medical care for symptomatic COVID-19 between December 2021 and March 2023 were enrolled at multiple sites in Brazil and followed for three months through structured and centralized telephone interviews. All studies included in this analysis were approved by the National Research Ethics Commission and adheres to Resolution 466/12 of the National Health Council of Brazil. Written informed consent was obtained from participants or their proxies at the time of enrollment.

Participants

This analysis included patients aged ≥ 18 years with symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection, confirmed by either an antigen test or reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), and met the eligibility criteria for one of the following studies: the Toledo BNT162b2 study (patients seeking care in the public health system with acute respiratory symptoms) [16], the Post-COVID Brazil 1 Study (patients survivors of hospitalization due to COVID-19) [17], and the Post-COVID Brazil 2 Study (patients seeking outpatient care for mild COVID-19) [18]. We excluded from analysis patients who died between the enrollment and the three-month follow-up, and follow-up losses. During the recruitment period, Omicron was the main SARS-CoV-2 variant in Brazil. Primary series of COVID-19 vaccination was defined according to the Brazilian Ministry of Health guidelines at the time of recruitment: two doses of mRNA (Pfizer-BioNTech) or viral vector vaccines (AstraZeneca), two doses of inactivated vaccines (CoronaVac), or a single dose of Janssen, later updated to two doses. Complete information on the eligibility criteria, and period of enrolment for each study that composes the present analysis is provided in the supplementary Table S1.

Long COVID symptoms

Long COVID-19 symptoms were assessed three months after enrollment. Patients were asked about COVID-19-related symptoms considering both the resolution or persistence of (I) symptoms initially associated with the COVID-19 diagnosis and (II) new symptoms emerging after the acute phase with no alternative explanation. Symptoms were self-reported and classified based on those described in the literature on “long COVID” and “post-COVID-19 condition” (Supplementary Table S2), following a previously established approach [19]. All symptoms not included in the list were classified as “others”. All three studies included in this analysis adhered to the same protocol for defining long COVID symptoms.

Quality of life

HRQoL was evaluated three months after enrollment using the utility score from the EuroQol five-dimension three-level (EQ-5D-3 L) questionnaire [20]. This instrument includes a descriptive system covering five dimensions of health-related quality of life—mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression—each classified into three levels: no problems, some problems, and extreme problems. The utility score, derived from this descriptive system for the Brazilian population, ranges from − 0.17 (with 0 representing a health state equivalent to death and negative values indicating states perceived as worse than death) to 1 (optimal health) [21]. The estimated minimal clinically important difference for the EQ-5D-3 L is 0.03, while the average utility score for the Brazilian population is 0.82 [22].

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using medians (IQR) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. To identify symptom-based phenotypes of long COVID, we applied a machine learning-based clustering approach. To determine the optimal number of clusters, we used the NbClust package, which evaluates 30 different indices. Clustering was performed with the cluster package (distance calculation) and stats (hierarchical clustering and dendrogram construction) [23]. This approach ensures that the chosen clustering structure is robust and representative of the data. After identifying the optimal number of clusters, we performed hierarchical clustering analysis - an unsupervised machine learning method that builds a hierarchy of clusters. We employed Ward’s method [24], an agglomerative technique that minimizes within-cluster variance, leading to the formation of more homogeneous groups. Because patients were assigned to each cluster according to its clinical characteristics, specific long-COVID symptoms may occur in more than one Cluster. Before clustering, we computed an appropriate dissimilarity measure for categorical data to account for the binary nature of symptom variables.

To explore factors associated with each symptom-based cluster, we performed multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for the effect of study in which the patient was enrolled. Using the asymptomatic group of patients as a reference, defined as patients who did not report any persistent or new symptoms three months after SARS-CoV-2 infection, we assessed the association of each cluster with several demographic and clinical variables, adjusting for sex, age, BMI (body mass index), healthcare worker status, vaccination regimen, WHO severity score, CCI (Charleson Comorbidity Index), diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cancer. For the comparison of EQ-5D-3 L utility scores and specific EQ-5D-3 L domain scores across clusters, we used generalized estimating equations, adjusting for study in which the patient was enrolled, sex, age, body mass index, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, and cancer), healthcare worker status, complete primary series of COVID-19 vaccination, and need for hospitalization during the index episode of COVID-19. Data visualization employed stats and factoextra R packages, while regression analyses were conducted with stats. A statistical significance level of 0.05 was used for all analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

A total of 2,989 patients with COVID-19 were screened for long-COVID symptoms across the three cohorts. Supplementary Table S3 summarizes the clinical characteristics of all three cohorts. Among the 2,989 patients screened, 1,155 (38.6%) exhibited persistent COVID-19 symptoms 3 months after the initial infection. The most common were memory loss in 575 (49.8%), fatigue in 536 (46.4%), others in 430 (37.2%), headache in 291 (25.2%) and muscle pain in 290 (25.1%). The complete list of persistent symptoms and its respective frequency is shown in Table 1. The majority of symptomatic patients were women (68.2%), with a median age of 46 years (IQR, 34–62). Most self-identified as white (65.6%), and 23.8% were employed in healthcare. Regarding body mass index (BMI), 35.2% of patients were classified as eutrophic (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m²), while 32.8% were overweight (25–29.9 kg/m²). The primary series of COVID-19 vaccination was completed by 96.0% of patients. The CCI of 0, indicating no comorbidities, was observed in most patients (66.4%). Compared to patients without persistent symptoms three months after COVID-19, those with ongoing symptoms were more likely to be female, older, White, have obesity and other comorbidities (CCI ≥ 1). No differences were observed in vaccination status, as it was highly prevalent in both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients (Supplementary Table S4).

Cluster analysis

Cluster analysis identified three distinct phenotypes. Figure 1 shows the dendrogram of symptom-based clustering. Of the 1,155 symptomatic patients, 459 (39.7%) were assigned to Cluster 1, 549 (47.5%) to Cluster 2, and 147 (12.7%) to Cluster 3. Table 2 summarizes the clinical characteristics of each cluster. Women comprised the majority in all clusters, with the highest proportion in Cluster 3. Notably, patients in Cluster 3 were older, less likely to be healthcare workers, more likely to be overweight or obese, and had more comorbidities (CCI ≥ 1).

The median number of symptoms varied across clusters. Cluster 2 exhibited the fewest symptoms, with a median of 2 (range: 1–4), followed by Cluster 1 with a median of 3 (range: 2–5) symptoms. Cluster 3 had the highest number of symptoms, with a median of 8 (range: 7–10) (Table 2). Table 2 shows the symptom prevalence in each cluster. The most common symptoms in each cluster were memory loss (80.4%), difficulty concentrating (38.3%), and fatigue (35.7%) in Cluster 1; fatigue (43.7%), other symptoms (42.3%), and cough (20.6%) in Cluster 2; and fatigue (89.9%), memory loss (88.4%), and anxiety (64.6%) in Cluster 3. Interestingly, dyspnea occurred in 42 (9.1%) in Cluster 1, in 102 (18.6%) in Cluster 2, and in 81 (55.1%) in Cluster 3 (p < 0.001).

To evaluate which symptoms were more likely to be associated with a specific cluster, a residual analysis was performed. Cluster 1 was associated with memory loss, concentration difficulties, and changes in taste and smell; Cluster 2 with other symptoms; and Cluster 3 with the broader range of symptoms, including memory loss, fatigue, headache, muscular pain, concentration difficulties, anxiety, dyspnea, cough, sleep difficulties, depression, changes in taste and smell, dizziness, gastrointestinal symptoms, ophthalmologic symptoms, tinnitus, chest pain, palpitations, and nausea.

Table 3 shows the associations between clinical variables and each cluster. Cluster 1 was associated with the presence of COPD (OR 0.54; 95% CI 0.32–0.92, p = 0.02), Cluster 2 was associated with male sex (OR 1.37; 95% CI 1.01–1.87, p = 0.044), and Cluster 3 was associated with COPD (OR 1.99; 95% CI 1.08–3.68, p = 0.028) and cardiovascular disease (OR 3.32; 95% CI 1.33–8.26, p = 0.010).

Quality-of-life

The quality of life, assessed using the EQ-5D-3 L questionnaire, revealed that Cluster 3 had the lowest quality of life (0.59), compared to Cluster 2 (0.73) and Cluster 1 (0.75), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Similar results were observed when analyzing the individual EQ-5D-3 L domains, where Cluster 3 exhibited the poorest scores in all domains (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain, anxiety, and depression) compared to Clusters 1 and 2 (p < 0.001). Discussion

COVID-19 presents with a highly heterogeneous clinical spectrum, ranging from mild symptoms to severe illness and death [1, 2, 18, 25]. In this study, we investigated the phenotypic characteristics of patients with persistent symptoms three months after the initial infection, utilizing an unsupervised machine learning analysis. Over one-third of patients reported ongoing symptoms, with fatigue and memory loss being the most prevalent. We identified three distinct clusters of symptomatic patients three months after symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection, each demonstrating unique profiles of clinical characteristics and persistent symptoms. In adjusted analyses, the cluster with the highest symptom burden was also associated with comorbidities such as COPD and cardiovascular disease, leading to a significantly impaired quality of life.

Our findings emphasize that post-COVID-19 manifestations vary according to patient characteristics, influencing both the number and severity of persistent symptoms as well as overall quality of life. Previous studies have reported a wide prevalence of persistent symptoms beyond 12 months after COVID-19 infection, ranging from 18.5% to 82% [26, 27]. This variation can be attributed to multiple factors, including differences in dominant SARS-CoV-2 variants, characteristics of the studied populations, and methods of symptom assessment (self-report vs. structured interviews). “These findings are consistent with systematic investigations showing the broad negative impact of long COVID on health-related quality of life [28, 29], as well as recent evidence suggesting that vaccination may attenuate such impairments [30].

In our cohort, 38.6% of patients reported persistent symptoms. This population was predominantly female, under 60 years of age, overweight or obese, and had a low burden of comorbidities according to the Charlson Comorbidity Index. Certain demographics appear more susceptible to persistent symptoms. Females have been shown to report symptoms more frequently than males, and middle-aged individuals are more likely to experience post-COVID-19 sequelae [31, 32]. Similarly, overweight and obesity have been associated with both more severe acute COVID-19 and an increased risk of long-term symptoms [32]. The relatively low number of comorbidities in our sample may have mitigated the prevalence of persistent symptoms. Notably, fatigue and memory loss were the most frequently reported symptoms, consistent with other studies, suggesting a possible common pattern for long-term symptomatology [10, 33, 34].

Through unsupervised machine learning analysis, we successfully differentiated three distinct patient clusters. Across clusters 1, 2, and 3, there was a progressive increase in age, overweight/obesity prevalence, and comorbidity burden. Cluster 3 had a higher proportion of female patients and was characterized by a predominance of memory loss, fatigue, and anxiety. These features have been associated with a higher risk of persistent symptoms, especially among female patients and those who experienced neurological symptoms during the acute phase of the infection [5]. Dyspnea, a hallmark of severe acute COVID-19, was also more frequently reported in the long term, especially among patients with a greater number of persistent symptoms. Beyond individual factors, contextual determinants such as prolonged home quarantine have also been associated with the control and trajectory of long COVID manifestations [35].

Other studies have similarly explored symptom clusters in post-COVID syndrome, highlighting fatigue and neurocognitive impairment as major contributors to reduced recovery and working capacity [34]. Chest symptoms, anxiety/depression, headaches, dizziness, and pain syndromes have also been noted, with variations according to sex and age [36]. These findings align with ours, particularly the association of neurocognitive impairments and dyspnea with poorer health outcomes. Clinical phenotypes resembling chronic fatigue syndrome, respiratory, chronic pain, and neurosensorial disorders were linked to significant declines in quality of life [5].

Importantly, neurocognitive impairment has been associated with lower EQ-5D index scores and reduced visual analogue scalex scores, reflecting a general decline in perceived health status [37]. In our study, quality of life worsened progressively with the increasing symptom burden across clusters, even after adjusting for baseline differences. Overall, our findings further support the notion that post-COVID syndrome is highly heterogeneous, with distinct symptom clusters exerting substantial impacts on patients’ quality of life and functional status. These results underscore the need for comprehensive, individualized management strategies tailored to the specific symptom profiles encountered in long-term COVID-19 patients.

Strengths and limitations

Our study benefits from a large, multicenter cohort of COVID-19 survivors with a wide range of disease severities assessed by 3 months post-infection. As such, it cannot provide information on whether the identified symptom clusters and quality-of-life impairments persist, resolve, or evolve beyond this period. Longer follow-up studies are needed to evaluate the durability and trajectories of long COVID manifestations. Other limitations must also be addressed. First, although the study protocols were pretty similar across the studies analyzed, the differences in case mix might have introduced heterogeneity, thus reducing statistical precision of findings. Second, male patients and individuals over 60 years of age were underrepresented. Third, although patients from distinct baseline characteristics were included, the overall low prevalence of comorbidities in the cohort may have influenced the observed rates of long-term symptoms. Fourth, the reliance on self-reported symptoms introduces potential bias related to subjective perception, which could result in underreporting of symptoms. Finally, we did not systematically collect information on prior SARS-CoV-2 reinfections. Given that reinfection has been associated with an increased risk of long COVID in previous studies, the absence of these data may have influenced our estimates of incidence and severity across clusters.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that persistent symptoms after COVID-19 are heterogeneous and can be classified into distinct clinical clusters with varying impacts on quality of life. Fatigue and memory loss were the most frequently reported symptoms, particularly among older, female, and overweight patients with comorbidities. The use of unsupervised machine learning allowed us to identify specific patient profiles that may benefit from targeted follow-up and management strategies. These findings highlight the importance of personalized approaches to address the long-term consequences of COVID-19.
