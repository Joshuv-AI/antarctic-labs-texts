Editorial on the special issue “How do intrabubble conditions influence sonochemical reactivity?”
Authors: Glyus Sharipov, Sergey I Nikitenko, Rachel Pflieger
Published: 2026

This editorial refers to the Special Issue entitled “How do intrabubble conditions influence sonochemical reactivity?” Sonochemistry is driven by the drastic conditions inside collapsing microbubbles in solutions submitted to power ultrasound. The composition of the sonicated solutions, the dissolved gases, the bulk temperature, etc. influence the intrabubble conditions. Additionally, multibubble sonochemical reactivity depends on the collective behavior of oscillating and cavitating bubble clouds, making sonochemistry a complex, multiscale phenomenon. This Special Issue spotlights recent research, advancements, and data analyses that tackle unmet needs in contemporary cavitation chemistry.

The papers presented in this special issue are listed in the Contributions section and briefly introduced here. The contribution [1] reviewed recent advances in sonoluminescence spectroscopy and sonochemical reactivity studies. Sonoluminescence spectroscopy provided evidence of non-equilibrium plasma formation inside collapsing bubbles. Such plasma is characterized by the absence of a thermal equilibrium: Te > Tv > Tr ≈ Tg, where Te is the electron temperature associated with the degree of ionization of the non-equilibrium plasma, Tv is the vibrational temperature, which characterizes the level of excitation of molecular vibrations, and Tr is the rotational temperature, often taken to be close to the gas temperature (Tg). Therefore, the intrabubble conditions cannot be described by a single gas temperature. Additionally, plasma formation indicates that vibrationally excited and ionized species significantly contribute to the overall mechanism of sonochemical reactions inside the bubbles. In aqueous solutions, the lifetime of solvated electrons is too short to contribute to the sonochemical processes in the liquid phase. Yet their lifetime in liquid ammonia is much larger than in water. This increases the probability of liquid phase reactions involving ultrasonically generated solvated electrons.

The contribution [2] focused on studying the effects of pulsed high-frequency ultrasound on sonochemical activity using calorimetry, terephthalic acid dosimetry, and capillary method. The experiments were performed at 347 kHz using a flat-plate transducer. It was found that longer on-time together with shorter off-time create more favorable conditions for sonochemical activity. The authors concluded that bubble dissolution, rather than coalescence, is the dominant factor limiting sonochemical efficiency of pulsed ultrasound. Additionally, bubble coalescence has been studied in the presence of fluorotelomer sulfonates (FtS), which exhibit surface-active properties. It has been shown that the bubble coalescence is affected even at low concentration of FtS by declustering bubble clouds. This effect is less significant with pulsed ultrasound than with continuous ultrasonic treatment.

The contribution [3] reported on the effect of ultrasonic power on sonochemical activity using a rectangular ultrasonic bath equipped with four transducers providing 154 kHz ultrasound. The study was conducted using a combination of chemical, physical, and numerical simulation techniques. It was found that, with an ultrasonic power of less than 35 W, the superposition of ultrasonic waves favored sonochemical activity. However, sonochemical efficiency decreased significantly at higher power due to several factors. These included a decrease in rectified diffusion inside the bubble, an increase in secondary Bjerknes forces leading to bubble clustering, and the movement of bubbles with traveling waves.

The contribution [4] dealt with the study of the influence of macroscopic mass transfer on the degradation of Rhodamine 6G (Rh6G) as a model system. The experiments were performed in an ultrasonic bath equipped with a 153 kHz transducer using sonochemiluminescence, laser induced fluorescence and particle image velocimetry. It was concluded that the mass transfer rate can slightly affect the sonochemical reaction rate due to the nonuniform Rh6G concentration in the early stage of reaction. The effect of macroscopic mass transfer on the reaction rate was quantified by the Damköhler number, which is the ratio of characteristic time of convection to characteristic time of chemical reaction. It was concluded that in the studied system the convective mass transfer was dominant compared with the diffusive mass transfer. The authors suggested that the Damköhler number can be applied to other ultrasonic baths with different conditions.

The contribution [5] focused on the effect of alcohol on the sonochemical degradation of CCl4 in alcohol-water mixtures under the effect of 200 kHz ultrasound. The enhancement of CCl4 degradation in the presence of alcohol was attributed to its increased solubility in the alcohol/water mixtures. The rate of degradation of CCl4 was found to vary in the following order: Ethylene glycol-CCl4 < MeOH-CCl4 < EtOH-CCl4 < PrOH-CCl4, which was the same order as alcohol hydrophobicity. It was considered that hydrophobic alcohol molecules accumulated at the bubble/solution interface leading to the scavenging of •OH radicals produced by the sonolysis of water, resulting in the production of H• atoms. The H• atoms and their secondary products, such as H2, can lead to the reductive degradation of CCl4.

The contribution [6] reported on the study of various geometric effects in 20 kHz probe systems on the oxidation of bisphenol A in aqueous solutions. A much higher sonochemical activity was obtained when the ultrasonic probe was placed close to the bottom of the circular and rectangular vessels. The effect of wall thickness was found to be more significant for the rectangular reactor than for the circular reactor. The sonochemiluminescence images demonstrated variations in the sonochemically active zones depending on the probe position, vessel shape, and vessel wall thickness. The sonochemiluminescence trends matched those of the sonochemical KI oxidation. However, the trends of bisphenol A degradation did not match those of KI oxidation in rectangular vessels.

The contribution [7] reported on a hybrid method for degrading ciprofloxacin (CIP) that combines hydrodynamic cavitation (HC), UV light, and H2O2. If they were used separately, all three factors exhibited low efficiency. On the other hand, CIP degradation accelerated under the joint action of UV and H2O2. In addition, the authors found that HC/UV/H2O2 system exhibited outstanding performance, achieving a synergetic effect of 8.174 compared to UV/H2O2 and HC systems. This synergetic enhancement suggests that degradation pathways beyond simple additive effects are active, probably involving pyrolytic processes induced by collapsing bubbles. Pyrolysis in the liquid shell surrounding the bubble may involve the degradation of CIP and the decomposition of H2O2, which leads to the generation of highly reactive radical species.

The contribution [8] dealt with a numerical simulation of the spatial and temporal evolution of vortex coherent structures generated by a Helmholtz nozzle under acoustic excitation. The numerical simulation was performed using Large Eddy Simulation and Proper Orthogonal Decomposition methods. It was concluded that external vibrational excitation significantly enhanced the generation ability of shear vortices at the nozzle outlet. When the resonant frequency excitation was applied at 2.029 kHz, the peak intensity of spanwise vortices increased by 30 %, and the periodic synchronization between large-scale vortices and cavitation was strengthened. In contrast, under ultrasonic excitation at 25 kHz, long-distance cavitation bubbles transport was facilitated. However, the correlation between vortex structures and cavitation weakened. Ultrasonic cavitation became dominant through the collapse of microbubbles, leading to vortex fragmentation and accelerated energy dissipation. High-frequency ultrasonic excitation enhanced shear effects, giving rise to disordered small-scale vortices in the second to fourth vorticity modes and promoting broader dispersion of flow energy. The first 200 modes captured only 50 % of the total energy, reflecting a transition toward turbulence-dominated cavitation.

The contribution [9] presented the experimental study and molecular simulation of the ultrasonically assisted preparation of curcumin-encapsulated oil-to-water nanoemulsion. The stable and concentrated nanoemulsions were prepared using 20 kHz pulsed ultrasound at 260 W electric power. The nanoemulsions maintained droplet sizes within 171–318 nm, with only a slight shift in the size distribution at higher oil loadings after prolonged storage. The encapsulation efficiency remained consistently high in the range of 89.5–91.4 %. Complementing the experimental work, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to elucidate the interactions of curcumin with fatty acids in the oil phase and with emulsifiers, thereby providing molecular-level insights into the nanoemulsion system’s structural organization and dynamic behavior. The simulations confirmed strong molecular interactions between curcumin, oil components, and surfactants, validating the complete encapsulation of curcumin within the oil phase.

Concluding remarks

The papers published in this Special Issue highlight some trends in modern sonochemistry. Regarding the processes occurring within collapsing bubbles, the works reviewed in contribution [1] provided evidence of non-equilibrium plasma formation during bubble collapse. Therefore, the intrabubble processes cannot be described as merely thermal splitting of molecules. Instead, ionization should also be considered, which implies a significant contribution of electron tunneling and electron impact to the overall sonochemical mechanisms. Several contributions [2], [3], [4], [5] focus on optimizing environmental remediation processes using the effects of high-frequency ultrasound (>100 kHz). These works clearly demonstrate the increasing interest in using high-frequency ultrasound for degradation of environmentally persistent pollutants. For a long period of time, it was believed that low-frequency ultrasound (around 20 kHz) was better suited for large scale applications than high-frequency ultrasound. Now it seems not to be the case for some specific applications. One example is PFAS removal from wastewater. The contribution [6] highlights the importance of reactor geometry on the efficiency of sonochemical reactions: reactor shape and wall thickness influence the behavior of cavitation bubble clouds. The contributions [7] and [8] report on hybrid systems with hydrodynamic cavitation. It should be noted that technologies based on hydrodynamic cavitation are gaining significant interest due to their potential for large-scale application and cost-effective operation. Today, however, we know much less about the mechanisms of chemical reactions produced by hydrodynamic cavitation than we do about those triggered by ultrasound. The synergistic enhancement of the degradation of organic pollutants using hydrodynamic cavitation coupled with UV light and H2O2 as an oxidizing reagent, as studied in contribution [7], is promising for the rapid removal of organic pollutants. This combined approach significantly accelerates the degradation of pollutants and enhances the cost-effectiveness of the process. It is suggested that pyrolytic decomposition of pollutants at bubble/solution interface strongly contributes to the reaction mechanism along with oxidation by highly reactive OH• radicals formed within collapsing bubbles. Contribution [8] reports on the evolution of vortex-cavitation coherent structures in self-excited cavitation waterjets under acoustic excitation. External vibrational excitation was applied via a vibrating boundary within the Helmholtz nozzle chamber. The results showed that synchronization between vortex shedding and acoustic vibrations was enhanced at a resonant frequency excitation of 2.029 kHz. In contrast, long-distance cavitation bubble transport increased, and dominant microbubble collapse caused vortex structure fragmentation under 25 kHz ultrasonic excitation. These results provide a theoretical foundation for the active optimization of cavitation waterjet performance. Ultrasonically assisted preparation of oil-in-water nanoemulsions have emerged as a promising delivery platform for biologically active compounds. In the contribution [9], 20 kHz power ultrasound was used to prepare stable highly concentrated emulsions of curcumin-loaded nanodroplets, stabilized by the non-ionic surfactants. This study's particular approach is its thorough molecular dynamics simulation, which elucidates curcumin's interactions with fatty acids in the oil phase and emulsifiers. This provides molecular-level insights into nanoemulsion structural organization and dynamic behavior. Overall, the formulated emulsions demonstrated outstanding stability and bioactive retention, positioning them as highly effective carriers for curcumin delivery.

Biographies

Professor Glyus Sharipov is a renowned physical chemist. For over 50 years, he has worked at the Academic Research Center in Ufa, Russia, where he rose to the position of Deputy Director and currently heads the High Energy Chemistry and Catalysis Research Laboratory. His research focuses on fundamental issues and aspects of the analytical application of radio-, chemi-, tribo-, and sonoluminescence. With his participation and leadership, a number of original liquid scintillators for detecting α-active nuclides and neutrinos were developed, and stable 1,2-dioxetanes for highly effective chemiluminescent diagnostics were synthesized and studied. Recently, technologies have been developed for the application of triboluminescence spectroscopy to the analysis of polyaromatic compounds in petroleum products and sonoluminescence spectroscopy to the analysis of metals in aqueous media, largely due to intrabubble sonochemical reactivity. Based on the results of this research, two monographs and two book chapters, more than 250 articles in scientific journals were published, 14 patents were received, and 9 PhD and 2 doctors of science were trained. Awarded the title “Honored Worker of Science and High Technologies of the Russian Federation” (2021).

Dr. Sergey I. Nikitenko is a Research Director Emeritus at the French CNRS and an Associate Professor at the Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières in Canada. He earned his Ph.D. in chemistry from Moscow State University in Russia. He joined the CNRS in 2005. He served as the head of the Laboratory of Sonochemistry in Complex Fluids at the Institut de Chimie Séparative de Marcoule in France for 17 years. His research focuses on the mechanisms of sonochemical reactions and developing technologies for low-carbon energy production. Specifically, he has studied sonoluminescence, the synthesis of functional nanomaterials, nuclear chemistry, and photo- and sonocatalysis. Nikitenko has published over 150 peer-reviewed papers and four book chapters, made over 100 conference contributions, and obtained five patents. He is an editorial board member of Ultrasonics Sonochemistry and Frontiers of Chemistry. He is also a board member of the European Society of Sonochemistry (ESS).

Dr. Rachel Pflieger is a senior research scientist from the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission (CEA) working at Marcoule Institute for Separation Chemistry (ICSM, France). She did her PhD in high-temperature thermodynamics applied to nuclear materials at the Institute for Transuranium Elements (JRC Karlsruhe, Germany). Her fields of research focus on fundamental sonochemistry, sonoluminescence and plasma emission spectrometry, and ultrasound-assisted decontamination of soils and solids. She published 57 papers and a book chapter, and presented her work at 35 conferences and seminars. She has been Executive Editor for Ultrasonics Sonochemistry since 2022.
