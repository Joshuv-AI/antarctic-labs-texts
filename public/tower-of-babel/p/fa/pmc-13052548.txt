Measuring subjective well-being for people racialised as Black in the USA: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Tiffany N Ford, Rosie Hanneke, Theresa Ezeani, Uchechi A Mitchell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSubjective well-being, a measurement of quality of life, varies systematically across racialised groups. However, the use of inconsistent subjective well-being measures across academic disciplines results in conflicting subjective well-being findings for people racialised as Black or African American in the USA. The aim of this scoping review is to provide a comprehensive overview of how subjective well-being is measured for people racialised as Black or African American in the USA and to examine how subjective well-being reports differ between people racialised as Black and white in the nation.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will use the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and report findings according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines. A predefined search strategy was used on 17 April 2024 to search PubMed (includes MEDLINE), APA PsycINFO (ProQuest), Sociological Abstracts (ProQuest), EconLit (EBSCOhost) and Scopus for studies published in English. Articles will be included if they specify Black or African American population, subjective well-being, and include populations within the USA. Data will be extracted and presented quantitatively and qualitatively in tabular and/or figure form to describe the characteristics and conclusions of included studies, grouped by subjective well-being component and measure. Analysis will identify methodological inconsistencies and research gaps.Ethics and disseminationThis study does not require review by our institution’s review board as primary data will not be collected in this study. The findings of this scoping review will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations, and condensed summaries for key partners in the field.Trial registration number10.17605/OSF.IO/M9GA8.

Introduction

Subjective well-being (SWB), or self-reported quality of life, is an important metric for assessing overall population health being considered by governments around the world.1 2 In addition to its usefulness as a general population health metric, SWB has also been linked to healthy ageing and longevity.3 4 SWB is a multidimensional umbrella term that requires multiple indicators to capture its complexity.5 6 Research on SWB is conducted across academic disciplines with varying terminology.5 Across disciplines, SWB is referred to as emotional well-being, psychological well-being and psychosocial well-being; these terms are understood to be conceptually similar.5 The diversity of the SWB research landscape leaves room for miscommunication as scholarship grapples with differences in conceptual definitions and valid measures of SWB.1 5

In a project sponsored by the National Institute on Aging and the UK Economic and Social Research Council, a large group of interdisciplinary SWB researchers came together to review the state of SWB research and offer guidance on measures.7 They concluded that there are three distinct components of SWB: evaluative, eudaimonic and hedonic.7 Evaluative well-being captures an individual’s overall assessment of their life. Eudaimonic well-being is focused on meaning of life and purpose.7 Hedonic well-being measures positive and negative affective indicators, or the feelings and emotions that people experience in day-to-day life.6 8 9 However, even with this clarity on how to understand the components of SWB, different approaches to measurement in each component could have a significant impact on the results.2

SWB can be measured objectively (via facial measures, observer reports, etc), but is most commonly measured using self-reported data.10 An array of self-report measures and scales of SWB has been developed across the disciplines, including Likert scales, single-item questions and multi-item indexes that focus attention on specific aspects of one’s life (eg, relationships or work).11 A 2022 review of SWB measurement found that 87.8% of the study authors concluded that refinement of SWB measures was necessary and 24.5% concluded that there was insufficient research on validated measures focused on their specific population.5 These measurement differences are especially notable in research on SWB for people racialised as Black in the USA. Given the relative paucity of research focused on the SWB of the population,11 many questions remain about which measures have been used to assess quality of life for people racialised as Black and whether these measures conceptually and theoretically align.

For example, researchers who operationalise evaluative well-being using the variable life satisfaction (LS) measure it differently and come to opposing conclusions. Some measure LS through an additive index including distinct domains of human life (eg, an additive scale of items asking respondents how much satisfaction they get from their family life, health and physical condition, friendships, non-working activities, the place they live) and find that people racialised as Black report lower LS relative to those racialised as white.12 13 Others measure LS through a single-item Cantril Ladder/‘best possible life’ question (ie, ‘Please imagine a ladder with steps numbered from zero at the bottom to ten at the top. The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?’) and find that people racialised as Black report higher LS relative to those racialised as white.14 15 Multi-item measures differ from single-item questions in that they direct respondents’ attention to specific aspects of their SWB rather than eliciting an overall, evaluative response. As such, the different words in these questions may tap into different underlying constructs.16

Clarity on SWB measurement for people racialised as Black in the USA is one potential pathway that may lead to better overall health among the population. More specifically, understanding SWB reports for Black Americans and how to interpret them relative to other racialised groups in the nation may help to inform future SWB research and drive policy and programmatic interventions that enhance SWB for people racialised as Black, and relatedly their health.

To date, no systematic efforts have been made to evaluate the SWB reports of people racialised as Black in the USA. Past reviews have focused on the relationship between specific determinants understood to shape SWB and the outcome of SWB.1722 Less have detailed SWB reports in general, including how SWB is measured.5 Additionally, to our knowledge, no previously published review has examined how SWB reports differ between people racialised as Black and white in the USA. To provide information that can be used to interpret SWB reports for people racialised as Black and assess racial patterning in SWB reports, we plan a scoping review that will compile published evidence across interdisciplinary academic disciplines to identify how SWB for people racialised as Black in the USA has been defined and measured. These compiled studies will also provide insight into the current gap in understanding of how SWB reports compare for people racialised as Black relative to white. A scoping review is the appropriate method for this research because our goal is to map the ‘key concepts underpinning a research area and the main sources and types of evidence available’ (Arksey and O’Malley p21)23 in a complex research area that has not been reviewed comprehensively before.

Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review is to provide a comprehensive overview of how SWB is operationalised for people racialised as Black or African American in the USA. A secondary objective of this study is to describe the published evidence on SWB for the racialised group and identify how findings compare to individuals racialised as white in the USA. The scoping review will answer the following questions:

How does existing academic literature operationalise (define and measure) SWB for people racialised as Black or African American in the USA?

In studies that report Black–white differences in SWB, what datasets, measures and SWB components are used?

These scoping review findings aim to inform future methodological developments and support the interpretation of SWB reports for people racialised as Black in the USA across SWB components and measures. This protocol details our preplanned methodological and analytical approaches to achieve the objectives.

Methods and analysis

Study design

As our goal in this study is to scope a large body of interdisciplinary SWB literature and clarify measurement concepts, we will conduct a scoping review. Like systematic reviews, scoping reviews take a systematic and structured approach to searching, screening and reporting findings in the literature.24 25 Scoping reviews differ from systematic reviews in that they are useful for answering broader questions,25 like those proposed in this study.

We will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) scoping review methodology26 and will report this review in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).25 27 A PRISMA-ScR checklist for this protocol is provided in online supplemental file 1. This protocol was registered through the Open Science Framework28 as the PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) database does not accept scoping review protocols.26

Original research articles published in English in peer-reviewed journals will be considered, provided they report on qualitative and/or quantitative data. Studies will be included if they: (1) include monoracial people racialised as Black or African American in the sample; (2) explicitly name subjective well-being, emotional well-being, psychological well-being or psychosocial well-being in the title or abstract and in the methods section of the full text; and (3) include a population in the USA.

We limit our review to studies that include monoracial individuals racialised as Black. We use the term ‘racialized’ to draw attention to the process by which these individuals were assigned their Black or African American race.29 30 Studies will be considered eligible for inclusion if they have ‘Black’ or ‘African American’ in the title or abstract and include individuals racialised as Black in the study sample. To improve the clarity of the findings from this review and increase their relevance to people racialised as Black in the USA, we will exclude studies that do not distinguish between monoracial and multiracial Black individuals, as well as studies that rely on aggregated racial categories (eg, ‘Black and other’). We will not limit our review based on the age, gender or other special populations of the research participants (ie, articles that focus on Black youth, Black men and LGBTQ Black people will be included). While we recognise that these age groups and special populations may have distinct patterns of SWB relative to the larger Black population, we expect that papers focused on these subpopulations will still contribute to answering our primary research question about how SWB is defined and measured for monoracial Black people in the USA.

Studies will be excluded if they focus on ‘well-being’ only and not ‘subjective well-being’ or related concepts (emotional well-being, psychological well-being or psychosocial well-being). Given the typical content of methods sections in scientific articles,31 we expect that requiring SWB and related concepts to be named in the methods section will increase the proportion of included articles that describe SWB measurement relative to those that name SWB in the title, abstract or elsewhere in the full text, but do not measure it. Additionally, this inclusion criterion reduces reliance on the study team’s own assumptions about which variables the authors understood to be measures of SWB, thus reducing researcher bias in this study. Still, we recognise that this strict eligibility criterion may exclude conceptually relevant research. Any seemingly relevant studies that do not meet the inclusion criteria will be documented.

Publications such as commentaries, editorials or literature reviews will be excluded and the reason will be documented. We will also exclude grey literature because we are interested in charting literature with a reliably high level of quality and consistent reporting standards as this is most likely to contribute to our study objectives.5 32 By limiting included items to peer-reviewed journal articles that report findings from qualitative or quantitative data, we increase the likelihood that our scoping review will include studies that define and measure SWB variables. While restriction to English language studies may introduce bias into our review, logistical limitations (ie, time, funding, translation ability) preclude us from including studies in other languages.33 We will not restrict our review based on the year of study publication. Multicountry studies that include the USA are also eligible to be included.

Information sources and search strategy

Using an a priori research protocol28 designed by a health sciences librarian (RH) with peer review by two other health sciences librarians with expertise in evidence synthesis methodology and database searching, relevant articles were identified on 17 April 2024 from five interdisciplinary databases: PubMed (includes MEDLINE), APA PsycINFO (ProQuest), Sociological Abstracts (ProQuest), EconLit (EBSCOhost) and Scopus. A search strategy for identifying relevant evidence based on the three topics of people racialised as Black, subjective well-being and measurement is provided in online supplemental file 2. Database characteristics were considered and the search strategy was adapted to the search syntax and controlled vocabulary of each database.

The preliminary scoping search conducted in April 2024 revealed an adequate amount of literature to address the research questions outlined in this protocol. RH uploaded all identified studies into Covidence, a web-based collaboration software program for managing literature reviews.34 The software removed duplicates and provided our study team with a final set of 5242 articles to screen in the title and abstract phase. After title and abstract screening, the full text was retrieved and uploaded to Covidence. Articles not available through the authors’ home institution were requested via Interlibrary Loan.

Study selection

The eligibility criteria described above will be used to screen articles for inclusion in this scoping review. Two reviewers will independently analyse the citations for inclusion in three phases. First, to ensure quality and consistency in screening, TNF and TE will independently screen a test set of 50–100 randomly selected items to clarify our understanding of the eligibility criteria and screening process. In the second phase, studies will be screened based on a review of the titles and abstracts, and irrelevant records will be excluded. The third phase will include a full-text review of the studies that were not excluded in the second phase. The full-text review will focus on including studies that name SWB or related concepts in the methods section and disaggregate results by race/ethnicity for people racialised as Black. At each phase, disputes will be discussed in weekly meetings with the two reviewers. When necessary, unresolved disagreements will be further reviewed by at least one additional author and resolved through discussion. Reasons for exclusion of full-text articles that do not meet the eligibility criteria will be recorded and tracked in Covidence.

On completion of the full-text screening, the database searches will be updated to retrieve any publications added to the databases since 17 April 2024. While two authors screen the new abstracts, RH will conduct backwards and forwards citation searching on all included studies using Scopus to include recent publications after 17 April 2024. Any studies that fit our inclusion criteria will be added to this review. We anticipate completing the full and final search in December 2026. The results of the search will be reported in full in the final scoping review and the process will be presented visually in a PRISMA flow diagram.27

Data extraction

The included studies will be extracted by one reviewer and cross-checked by another (TNF)35 using the data extraction tool embedded in Covidence. Predefined data extraction criteria developed by the research team will be used (box 1). The preliminary data extraction tool was adapted from the template provided in the JBI Manual26 to encompass all variables relevant to the research questions and objectives of the scoping review. Before full extraction, we will pilot a small number of eligible citations among our team to test the appropriateness of the data charting form.36 The full study team will meet monthly during data extraction to review progress and discuss any disagreements.

Data synthesis

We will report the results of our scoping review using PRISMA-ScR checklists25 and a PRISMA flow diagram. The findings will be presented quantitatively and qualitatively visually through graphs, figures and/or tables. Data extracted on study characteristics (eg, author, year, journal, study design, population) will be presented in a table overview, alongside a brief description of study conclusions. Data extracted on SWB measurement (eg, SWB definition; variables, scales and SWB component; dataset) will also be presented in tables. As much as possible, these data will be grouped by SWB component and specific measure of SWB.35 Articles that focus on special populations of people racialised as Black (eg, youth, mothers) will be presented separately and organised by SWB component, measure and special population. We will report the number and proportion of included studies that use each dataset, variable, scale and SWB component.

We will synthesise the results of our second objective separately to provide an overview of studies that assess Black–white differences in SWB. We will use the extracted summary data of SWB reports for people racialised as Black and white to synthesise the studies into groups according to those that report higher, lower or the same SWB for people racialised as Black relative to those racialised as white. We will describe each study’s population characteristics and list the dataset, measures and SWB definitions and components used. Finally, we will discuss the implications of the findings for both study objectives, make an effort to identify remaining knowledge gaps in the SWB literature and highlight directions for future SWB research. The specific presentation format (eg, tables vs figures) and extracted data that is included will be determined after the full-text articles have been reviewed. An agreement on further synthesis and reporting beyond what is described in this protocol will occur among the study team after data extraction.

Patient and public involvement

The design of this scoping review study does not involve patient recruitment or public involvement.

Ethics and dissemination

This scoping review will provide detailed information about how SWB is measured, what is reported for people racialised as Black in the USA and how these reports compare to SWB reports of people racialised as white. The results of this scoping review will identify measurement inconsistencies and research gaps that may inform future methodological work and policy-relevant research. As this scoping review will generate knowledge from published literature and does not involve new data collection with human subjects, formal review by our institution’s review board is not required.

The dissemination plan for this scoping review is focused on researchers, practitioners and decision makers interested in examining SWB for people racialised as Black or African American. The findings will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals, presented at scientific conferences, shared through non-academic channels (eg, social media) and condensed into summaries that will be shared with key partners in the field.

If we need to amend this protocol following its publication, we will provide the date of the amendment, describe the change(s) made and report the rationale for the change(s) in any publication that results from this scoping review protocol. We will also upload documentation on Open Science Framework to clarify deviations from the preregistered protocol. Finally, all metadata that results from this scoping review (eg, data extraction tables) will be made available either at the end of the project or at the time of manuscript publication on the Open Science Framework repository.
