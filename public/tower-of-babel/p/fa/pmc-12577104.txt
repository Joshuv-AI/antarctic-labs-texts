Re-evaluating CD57 as a marker of T cell senescence: implications for immune ageing and differentiation
Authors: Gaëlle Autaa, Daniil Korenkov, Josine van Beek, Isabelle Pellegrin, Béatrice Parfait, Debbie van Baarle, Odile Launay, Eric Tartour, Victor Appay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ageing is accompanied by a decline in immune function, associated with susceptibility to infections and malignancies, and reduced vaccine efficacy. These immunological changes, affect multiple components of the immune system, particularly T lymphocytes, which exhibit altered subset distributions and accumulate senescent features. CD57, a surface glycoprotein expressed on T cells, has emerged as a potential marker of terminal differentiation and senescence used for immunomonitoring in infection or cancer contexts. However, the use of CD57 as a marker of T cell senescence remains unclear. To investigate this, we analyzed CD57 expression on CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in healthy donors from two independent cohorts, considering cellular differentiation, age, cytomegalovirus status, and other senescence markers. Our findings reinforce the association between CD57 expression, T cell differentiation, and CMV seropositivity, but not with chronological age. Although CD57 is associated with altered proliferation and survival in all T cell differentiation subsets, it does not fully align with a senescent phenotype. Therefore, we propose that CD57 may be better appreciated as a marker of immunological age. Moreover, the interpretation of CD57 expression must account for CMV serostatus to avoid misleading conclusions, especially in oncology and ageing research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12979-025-00542-8.

Introduction

Life expectancy has greatly increased in the last two centuries, and the number of people over 80 years is expected to triple between 2020 and 2050 worldwide [1]. However, ageing is a risk factor for the onset of multiple pathologies. For instance, advanced age is associated with increased prevalence of infectious and malignant diseases, which is thought to reflect the decline of immune defenses with ageing [2–5]. Moreover, although older adults are a primary target for disease prevention efforts, they often exhibit a lower responsiveness to vaccination [6–9]. Numerous alterations of our immune system occur over time and eventually converge to weaken its efficacy [10, 11]. Most, if not all, compartments of our immune system, including cells and organs, undergo quantitative or qualitative impairments with ageing. These range from a decline in lymphocyte production to functional deterioration of effector cells from both the myeloid and lymphoid lineages, as well as disruptions in cellular homeostasis [12, 13]. Altogether, these alterations define the process of immunosenescence, which may be more accurately termed immune ageing to avoid confusion with cellular senescence, a distinct phenomenon characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest, often associated with telomere shortening, DNA damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Assessing markers linked to immune ageing can provide an estimate of an individual’s immunological age, a measure of the immune system function relative to time, which is influenced by genetic factors, history of infections or diseases, and lifestyle variables such as stress, diet, and sleep, and which should be clearly distinguished from chronological age. Defining best immune ageing biomarkers could therefore enable us to predict diseases and identify most vulnerable people at risk of developing ageing associated pathologies. T lymphocytes have emerged as the most affected immune compartment with ageing [14, 15]. This is illustrated by the strongly unbalanced homeostasis between naive and effector memory subsets and the accumulation of T cells with senescence related properties [12, 16–25].

Among potentially relevant markers on T lymphocytes, the expression of the surface glycoprotein CD57 (also known as HNK-1 or Leu-7) is being increasingly used to characterize T cells in different contexts. Its expression on T cells has been associated with terminal differentiation (i.e. expressed on effector memory cells re-expressing CD45RA), chronic immune stimulation, ageing or even cellular senescence [26–28]. CD57+ T cells may reflect a burned-out cytotoxic response, pushed towards senescence, and high levels of these cells are sometimes linked to poor disease outcomes and hyperinflammation [29–31]. Overall, CD57 is becoming a common marker for identifying senescent T cells, especially in infection and cancer contexts. Monitoring its expression on T cells could potentially serve as a biomarker for disease severity or progression.

However, CD57+ T cells were also shown to be able to proliferate [32, 33]. Moreover, CD57+ T cells are expanded in response to cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, so their presence may actually be a marker for prior CMV infection [34, 35]. The full story around the expression of CD57 and its interpretation may thus be more complex than initially anticipated, deserving further investigation considering the potential and growing use of this marker for the immunomonitoring of cohorts. To bring new insights into CD57, we have performed a comprehensive analysis of its expression on CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in combination with cellular differentiation, age and CMV serostatus of individuals, as well as markers associated with cellular senescence in two independent cohorts of healthy donors. Our data confirm a strong association between CD57 expression and T cell differentiation and CMV seropositivity, but not age. Independently of T cell differentiation, CD57+ cells present altered proliferative and survival potential, but cannot be defined as senescent. Our results support the interest in monitoring the expression of CD57, as a marker, not of cellular senescence per se, but rather of immunological age.

Results

Factors influencing CD57 expression on T lymphocytes

We studied two independent cohorts of healthy subjects (n = 87 for C1 recruited in France and n = 49 for C2 recruited in The Netherlands), aged 18 to 88 years, which were both separated in two groups of age (< 65y and ≥65y) (Table 1). C1 presented a typical distribution of T cell subsets with age, characterized by increased frequencies of highly differentiated effector memory RA cells and increased CD57+ T cells in the ≥65y group, while only trends were observed in C2. The latter cohort presented a more even distribution of CMV seropositive donors between the < 65y and ≥65y groups compared to C1, which had more CMV positive subjects in the ≥65y group.

CD57 expression was assessed on four classical subsets of T cell differentiation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells based on the expression of CD45RA and CCR7 (Fig. 1A). As previously shown, CD57 was mostly expressed on effector memory T cell subsets (CD45RA−CCR7− and CD45RA+CCR7−), and mainly of the CD8+ lineage (Fig. 1B and Figure S1A). However, age per se did not appear to be a determining factor of its expression. Instead CMV seropositivity had a clear impact on CD57 expression on effector memory T cell subsets (Fig. 1C and Figure S1B). This is further supported by analysing the two cohorts together according to age and CMV seropositivity, where no differences were found between age groups (Fig. 1D and Figure S1C). CD57 expression on T cells from healthy donors therefore depends mainly on cellular differentiation and CMV infection, but less on ageing. Of note, naive like (CD45RA+CCR7+) and central memory (CD45RA−CCR7+) cells from a few donors displayed modest but obvious CD57 expression, highlighting that it is not strictly a feature of effector memory T cells, but can also occur at early stages of T cell differentiation. The CD45RA+CCR7+ cell subset comprises naive but also stem memory T cells, which can be differentiated according to the expression of CD95 [36]. Our data indicate that both these subsets can present expression of CD57 (Figure S2). Although these cells represent in general few events, they give us the opportunity to see if CD57+ early differentiated or effector memory T cell subsets share similar properties.

Immune ageing properties of CD57+ T cells

To better characterize the link between CD57 expression and cellular senescence, we assessed in CD57+ T cell subsets features that are usually associated with senescent cells, namely the activity of senescence associated-β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) by flow cytometry (Fig. 2A) and the proliferation capacity upon TCR mediated stimulation (Figs. 3 A and 3B). Data on flow cytometry assessed SA-β-gal is scarce in T lymphocyte subsets. One study reported that aging humans exhibit an accumulation of senescent CD8⁺ T cells characterized by elevated SA-β-Gal activity [37]. These SA-β-Galhigh cells displayed hallmark features of cellular senescence, including telomere dysfunction, increased p16 expression, and impaired proliferative capacity. Moreover, their gene expression profile closely resembled the senescence-associated signature previously identified in human fibroblasts. Here, we found that SA-β-gal activity in cells can be quite variable, but generally increases with T cell differentiation. There were also trends towards a higher activity in more differentiated cells of the CD8+ T lineage compared to the CD4+ T lineage, as well as in older individuals compared to younger individuals (Fig. 2B and Figure S3A). In all T cell subsets that express CD57, SA-β-gal activity is more pronounced (Fig. 2C and Figure S3B). Overall, highly differentiated CD57+ CD8+ T cell subsets present highest levels of SA-β-gal activity. Assessment of T cell subset proliferation capacity was performed upon activation with anti-CD3 antibodies, in the absence of costimulatory receptor or cytokine mediated stimuli to avoid the influence of the secondary or tertiary signals. For subjects from C1, proliferation was measured by looking at the upregulation of the cell cycling associated nuclear antigen Ki67, 24 h after stimulation (Fig. 3A). In C2, it was assessed using the dilution of the cell proliferation dye (CPD), 3 days after stimulation (Fig. 3B). Both approaches yielded comparable trends and observations. While decreased proliferation capacity from naive like to more differentiated CD8+ T lymphocyte subsets was not very marked in CD57− cells, the expression of CD57 was clearly associated with reduced proliferation capacity in all subsets, particularly in the more differentiated ones (Fig. 3C and D). Similar tendencies were observed with CD4+ T lymphocyte subsets although the proliferation capacity of these cells remained in general superior compared to CD8+ T cells. Overall, more differentiated CD57+ CD8+ T cell subsets present therefore the lowest proliferative potential. However, not all CD57+ T cells lost their capacity to proliferate. Considering the high SA-β-gal activity and reduced proliferative potential within CD57+ T cells, some of these cells may eventually be senescent or on the edge of becoming senescent, in particular at late stages of differentiation. Overall, while CD57 expression does not define senescence per se, it may rather reflect T lymphocytes undergoing cellular ageing, i.e., cells that present shortening telomeres [30, 38], increasing SA-β-gal activity and decreasing proliferative capacity, as a result of chronic activation and differentiation.

Last, we analyzed the susceptibility to activation induced cell death of non-proliferating (i.e. closest to senescence) versus proliferating (i.e. non-senescent) CD57+ CD8+ T cells. For this purpose, intracellular expression of activated caspase-3 (cCasp-3), which plays an important role in the initiation of apoptosis, as well as MCL-1 and BCL-xL, which are key anti-apoptotic proteins of the BCL-2 family, was assessed upon TCR mediated stimulation in Ki67− or Ki67+ CD57+ and CD57− cell subsets of the CD8+ (Fig. 4A) and CD4+ T cell lineages (Figure S4A). Compared to Ki67− CD57− T cells, Ki67− CD57+ T cells showed simultaneous increased levels in cCasp-3, MCL-1 and BCL-xL expression (Fig. 4B and Figure S4B). This highlights that divergent pathways, pro-apoptotic and pro-survival are at play in the cells that are the closest to senescence. Intriguingly, this pattern tended to wane with cellular differentiation. Early differentiated non-proliferating CD57+ T cells may thus be most resistant to cell death compared to highly differentiated Ki67− CD57+ T cells upon activation. In contrast, proliferating Ki67+ CD57+ CD8+ T cells showed decreased levels in Casp-3 activation compared to CD57− CD8+ T cells, suggesting that they may be less prone to die (Fig. 4C and Figure S4C). However, they showed also significantly lower MCL-1 levels compared to CD57− CD8+ T cells independently of T cell differentiation. Overall, this highlights the intricate relationship between T cell differentiation, cellular ageing and survival, and the complexity of the fate awaiting the different cell subsets upon stimulation.

Discussion

The present study of CD57 on T cell subsets in two independent cohorts highlights its complex expression, which varies according to several factors, in particular cellular differentiation and CMV seropositivity, but not ageing. The apparent association between CD57 expression on T cells and chronological age is more related to the higher prevalence of CMV seropositivity and increased frequencies of highly differentiated cells in older people than advanced age itself. Consequently, its expression is thus more related to biological or immunological age than chronological age, making it a valuable marker for monitoring immune system status.

CD57⁺ T cells have typically undergone numerous rounds of replication, and as a result, exhibit reduced proliferative capacity, telomere shortening [30, 38], and increased senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity [37]. While these features are observed across all T cell subsets regardless of differentiation stage, they become more pronounced as cells progress toward terminal differentiation. This is consistent with the observation that repeated antigenic stimulation drives T cell differentiation, highlighting a process of cellular ageing that unfolds in tandem with differentiation, most visibly in CD57-expressing cells. The expression of CD57 in naive-like cells and their increase in older people are of interest and highlight qualitative alterations of this compartment with ageing. This is in line with recent findings by Verdin and colleagues, who reported age related accumulation of naive T cells displaying high SA-β-Gal activity and hallmark features of cellular aging (e.g. nuclear envelope disruption, increased γH2AX foci indicative of DNA damage, and autophagic alterations) [39]. Although both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells undergo similar overarching processes of differentiation and ageing, CD57 expression is more prevalent in CD8⁺ T cells. This suggests a greater tendency for terminal differentiation and cellular ageing of this lineage. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this lineage-specific tendency remain unclear. The specific regulatory pathways that control CD57 expression in different T cell subsets are not yet fully elucidated, nor is it understood why CD8⁺ T cells are more susceptible to acquiring this ageing-associated phenotype. Within the CD57⁺ T cell population, some cells may be senescent or approaching a senescence state. Nevertheless, it is an oversimplification to equate CD57 expression, as well as the effector memory RA (EMRA) phenotype, or the mere activity of SA-β-gal with true cellular senescence. While such associations are frequently invoked in discussions of immune ageing, they risk being misleading. CD57 is not a definitive marker of senescence in the strict biological sense, which entails irreversible cell cycle arrest. Many CD57⁺ T cells are indeed able to proliferate upon activation, do not exhibit strong SA-β-gal activity, or can be found within early differentiated cell compartments. Rather, CD57 reflects a history of prior immune activation, replicative activity, and declining proliferative potential. In this context, it is understandable that a persistent virus such as CMV can drive the expansion of CD57⁺ T cells, many of which may be specific for CMV [40, 41]. Their relative capacity to resist activation-induced cell death, as suggested by our data on the expression of survival associated markers in proliferating CD57⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, align with their accumulation in CMV-seropositive individuals. This is consistent with the well-characterized phenomenon of memory inflation associated with CMV infection [42, 43]. Despite this complexity, CD57 remains a valuable phenotypic marker in immunological research, particularly in the context of antigen-specific T cell responses. For instance, in studies employing peptide-MHC tetramer staining to track T cells specific for viral or tumor antigens, CD57 helps identify highly differentiated, functionally mature subsets often shaped by chronic or persistent antigen exposure [44].

However, caution is warranted when interpreting the frequency of CD57⁺ T cells in bulk populations, i.e., outside of defined antigen specificities, as a biomarker of specific pathological conditions. Chronic infections, especially CMV, strongly drive the progressive differentiation of T cells, leading to phenotypes such as EMRA and the upregulation of CD57 [45–47]. These converging processes highlight CMV as a major driver of immune system remodeling, particularly within the CD8⁺ T cell compartment. This point is well illustrated by the lack of difference in CD57 expression on total T cells between younger and older individuals in cohort C2. This is likely due to the similar frequencies of CMV-seropositive individuals in both age groups. Such a pattern is relatively uncommon, as CMV seroprevalence typically increases with age, as observed in cohort C1. This discrepancy may stem from geographic and socio-economic differences influencing CMV exposure (e.g., between France and the Netherlands) or from recruitment biases between cohorts C1 and C2. As a result, accurate interpretation of CD57 expression on T cells as a biomarker requires careful consideration of participants’ CMV serostatus to account for potential confounding effects. This is particularly relevant in cancer research, where an increasing number of studies (including in melanoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, lung, prostate, breast, ovarian, and colorectal cancers) have reported associations between the frequency of CD57⁺ T cells and clinical outcomes or immunotherapy efficacy [48–55]. These studies often suggest that CD57 expression marks exhausted and senescent CD8⁺ T cells, which are enriched in advanced disease and associated with poor prognosis. However, the presence of CD57⁺ T cells may instead reflect chronic CMV infection, which could contribute to declining immune surveillance with age and indirectly worse cancer outcomes. Moreover, CMV itself might exert direct pro-tumorigenic and oncogenic effects [56–58]. Therefore, it is especially important to account for CMV serostatus in such studies, as this may shed light on a significant yet poorly understood role of CMV in cancer development.

Our study presents several limitations. First, the assessment of proliferative potential using functional assays may not be optimal. The use of frozen-thawed samples could compromise cell quality and their functional responsiveness, potentially contributing to the high interpatient variability observed in both cohorts. The latter was particularly evident when measuring Ki67 expression following short-term, high-intensity anti-CD3 stimulation, a condition that may lack sufficient sensitivity. Moreover, stimulation in these assays was limited to anti-CD3 antibody alone, without incorporating co-stimulatory signals that could enhance the functional responsiveness and proliferative capacity of specific T cell subsets. Additionally, important markers such as CD28, CD27, and KLRG-1 were not included in the phenotypic panels used in this study, which can limit the precise identification and characterization of T cell subsets. The identification of senescent T cells also presents a challenge, as it requires the simultaneous analysis of multiple markers. These include SA-β-Gal activity, proliferative capacity, but also telomere length, γH2AX foci indicative of DNA damage, and mRNA expression of factors involved in cell cycle arrest like p16INK4 and p21, as well as evidence of mitochondrial alterations. A definitive conclusion that a cell is truly senescent can only be made if several of these markers are concurrently positive.

In summary, CD57 expression on T cells represents a complex and multifaceted marker, which reflects cumulative immune history, particularly in the context of persistent infections such as CMV, rather than an indicator of senescence per se. While CD57⁺ T cells are often associated with advanced disease states, this relationship is nuanced and shaped by underlying viral exposures and immune system remodeling. Incorporating CMV status into immunophenotyping studies, especially in oncology and ageing research, is critical for disentangling the roles of immune exhaustion, differentiation, and viral persistence.

Methods

Patients and samples

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from healthy adults participating to SARS-CoV-2 (CoviCompare M) [59, 60] or Influenza (VITAL) [61, 62] vaccine cohort studies in France or the Netherlands respectively. In both cohorts, subjects were healthy adults in stable medical condition. Individuals were excluded if under immune-modulatory treatment, high-dose of daily corticosteroids or receiving chemotherapy in the last 3 years, presenting evidence of being immunocompromised, including recipient of an organ- or bone marrow transplant. Whole blood samples were collected in BD vacutainer tubes with EDTA (BD Biosciences) or Sodium Heparine (Greiner Bio-One, GMBH) before vaccination. PBMCs were isolated using standard density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva) or Lymphoprep™ (Serumwerk Bernburg AG) and then cryopreserved at − 135 °C. Cytomegalovirus seropositivity was established by measuring IgG levels against CMV in serum samples from individuals of each cohort.

Cellular phenotype

T cells were phenotyped ex vivo via flow cytometry using BV711-conjugated anti-CCR7 (clone 2-L1-A, BD Biosciences), BV421-conjugated anti-CD4 (clone RPA-T4, BD Biosciences), BV786-conjugated anti-CD8 (clone RPA-T8, BD Biosciences), BV650-conjugated anti-CD45RA (clone HI100, BD Biosciences), PE-CF594 conjugated anti-CD57 (Clone NK-1, BD Biosciences), and PerCPCy5.5 conjugated anti-CD95 (clone DX2, BD Biosciences) monoclonal antibodies (Figure S2A). Nonviable events were excluded using a LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), along a dumb channel with CD14 and CD19 expressing cells. Data were acquired using an LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo version 10.8.1 (FlowJo LLC).

Measurement of senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity

PBMCs were thawed and cultured for 1 h in RPMI 1640 medium containing 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1 mM nonessential amino acids, 1 mM L-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 25 mM HEPES, and 0.1 µM bafilomycin (all reagents from Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were then incubated for a further 2 h in the presence of SA-β-Gal FITC-Substrate (33 µM, Cell Signaling Technology) followed by staining for cellular phenotype. Data were acquired using an LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo version 10.8.1 (FlowJo LLC).

Measurement of proliferation and survival associated markers

For the measurement proliferation using Cell Proliferation Dye (CPD), PBMCs were were thawed and labelled with CPD eFluor450 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before stimulation with plate-bound anti-CD3 antibodies (clone OKT3; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 3 days, cells were stained with PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-CCR7 (clone 2-L1-A, BD Biosciences), PE-conjugated anti-CD4 (clone RPA-T4, BD Biosciences), APC-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD8 (clone RPA-T8, BD Biosciences), FITC-conjugated anti-CD57 (Clone NK-1, BD Biosciences) and PerCPCy5.5-conjugated anti-CD45RA (clone HI100, BD Biosciences) monoclonal antibodies. Nonviable events were excluded using a LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua BV510 Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proliferation was measured using flow cytometry to quantify the dilution of CPD in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets based on CD57, CCR7 and CD45RA expression. For the measurement proliferation using Ki67, PBMCs were stimulated with plate-bound OKT3. After 24 h, cells were stained for cell surface markers with directly conjugated monoclonal antibodies. Nonviable events were excluded using LIVE/DEAD Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit. Cells were then fixed/permeabilized using a Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stained with PE/Dazzel-conjugated anti-Ki-67 (clone Ki-67, BioLegend), associated with PE-conjugated anti-Cleaved Caspase-3 (clone D3E9, Ozyme), PECy7-conjugated anti-Bcl-xL (clone 54H6, Ozyme), AF647-conjugated anti-Mcl-1 (clone D2W9E, Ozyme) and BV605-conjugated anti-CD3 (Clone UCHT1, BD Biosciences) monoclonal antibodies. Data were acquired using an LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo version 10.8.1 (FlowJo LLC).

Statistics

Statistical significance among the experimental groups was determined using unpaired T-test with Mann-Whitney or one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test correction (GraphPad Prism 7, GraphPad Software) as indicated in each figure legend. P < 0.05 was considered significant.
