Effects of Storage Time, Internal Design and Printing Workflow on Accuracy and Stability of 3D-Printed Dental Models
Authors: Yuming Chen, Hefei Li, Zhihao Zhai, Ziyu Ge, Yanzhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsAccurate, stable 3D-printed models are essential for digital dental workflows. This study evaluated the effects of storage time, internal design and printing workflows on the dimensional stability and accuracy of dental models.MethodsA maxillary typodont was scanned to generate a reference digital model. Three internal designs were created: hollow with perforated base (HB), solid (S) and support structure with perforated base (SB). Sixty models fabricated via Form 3 (SLA) and Straumann P30+ (DLP) printers were stored under controlled indoor conditions and scanned at 7 time points within 4 weeks. Geomagic Control software analysed accuracy (trueness/precision via best-fit alignment and root mean square [RMS] values) and volume.ResultsFor the DLP workflow, trueness and volume significantly changed over time (P < .001). Trueness decreased after 2 weeks for S and SB and after 3 weeks for HB. Volume decreased after 1 week for all designs. Precision showed no significant changes. Volume was negatively correlated with trueness (RMS values) and storage time, while storage time positively correlated with trueness (RMS values) (all P < .001). Conversely, models printed by the SLA workflow showed no significant changes in trueness, precision, or volume over 4 weeks, demonstrating significantly higher stability than that of the DLP workflow (P < .001). Internal design had no significant effect.ConclusionsStorage time significantly affects trueness in the evaluated DLP workflow, with volumetric shrinkage being a highly probable contributing factor, whereas models printed by the SLA workflow remain stable for up to 4 weeks. Internal design showed no significant dimensional effect.Clinical relevanceVariations across specific printing workflows may influence the dimensional stability of dental models. To ensure accuracy, models from the evaluated DLP workflow require use within 2 weeks, whereas the tested SLA workflow is recommended for applications requiring storage or transportation.

Introduction

In recent years, the application of various digital technologies in dentistry has revolutionised traditional dental manufacturing processes. Among these, the rise of 3D printing technology has garnered significant attention. Offering advantages such as low material waste, repeatable production and streamlined workflows, 3D printing is widely considered a primary direction for dental manufacturing.1,2 Currently, 3D printing is routinely deployed in fields such as prosthodontics,3,4 implantology,5,6 and orthodontics7 for fabricating a diverse range of dental products, including dental models, crowns, removable dentures, orthodontic appliances and surgical guides, among others.8

The fabrication of dental products via 3D printing is clinically feasible, yet their quality and long-term stability remain insufficiently studied. Accuracy is a critical determinant of dental products for treatment efficacy and long-term success. Existing evidence indicates that the final accuracy of printed dental products is influenced by multiple factors, such as layer thickness,9,10 printing orientation,11,12 internal design,13 printer type,14,15 printing technology,16,17 material selection,10 and postprocessing methods.18 Dental models are routinely used in clinical practice for diagnosis and prosthesis fabrication. As one of the earliest dental products manufactured via 3D printing, dental models serve as excellent test objects for evaluating dimensional accuracy. Some studies have reported that 3D-printed dental models can already achieve clinically acceptable accuracy.16,19 However, printed models are rarely used immediately and often undergo transport or a period of storage before use. Notably, whether these stored models remain dimensionally reliable over time and how their accuracy changes remain insufficiently studied. In conventional impression workflows, polymerisation shrinkage of elastomeric materials and setting expansion of dental stone are well recognised, prompting the practice of using impressions and casts as soon as possible.20 For 3D-printed dental models, photosensitive resins are processed via stereolithography (SLA) or digital light processing (DLP) workflows.21 The SLA workflow forms the object through a ‘point-by-point curing’ path, whereas the DLP workflow achieves ‘layer-by-layer curing’ of the photosensitive material to form the printed object.22 However, these raw materials require light exposure that triggers polymerisation shrinkage during curing. Additionally, models are often stored under ambient indoor lighting after printing, making the issue of dimensional deformation equally noteworthy. Therefore, the influence of postprinting storage time on the accuracy of 3D-printed dental models still requires further investigation to provide reference evidence for the reliable accuracy shelf life of dental models and even other dental products in future clinical applications.

Furthermore, when considering the effect of storage time on the accuracy of 3D printed dental models, 2 additional factors also need to be considered: internal structure and specific printing workflows. Chen et al.13 found that different internal designs influence the accuracy of printed models. Compared with hollow designs, solid internal designs appear to have a denser internal structure and may exhibit greater resistance to deformation caused by storage time. Additionally, Morón-Conejo et al.23 reported that the accuracy of surgical guides produced using different 3D printing workflows varies. In this study, the extent to which the dimensional shape of 3D-printed dental models changes over storage time is described using the concept of dimensional stability. Whether different structural designs and printing workflows affect the dimensional stability of 3D-printed dental models remains inconclusive. Therefore, this study will further investigate the influence of different internal designs and 3D printing workflows on the dimensional stability of dental models.

Regarding accuracy evaluation, the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) defines this parameter in terms of trueness and precision.24 3D-printed dental models are typically fabricated from digital models obtained via intraoral scans. In this study, trueness characterises the deviation between the printed model and the original digital reference, and precision indicates the consistency among multiple models printed from the same digital file. Furthermore, a best-fit alignment algorithm is used to superimpose the test models onto the reference model. The root mean square (RMS) value of the deviations is then calculated following the ISO-recommended method for 3 dimensional accuracy assessment in dentistry.25 Global 3D RMS values were utilised rather than 2D cross-sectional evaluations because the dynamic deformation of aging models renders reference planes unstable, making a global 3-dimensional assessment mandatory for longitudinal accuracy tracking.

The objectives of this study are to evaluate the effects of storage time, internal structures and specific printing workflows on the accuracy and dimensional stability of 3D-printed dental models. Additionally, the correlation between dimensional accuracy and volumetric changes over time will be analysed to explore the relationship between model deformation and shrinkage. The first null hypothesis is that storage time has no significant effect on accuracy. The second null hypothesis is that internal structures and specific printing workflows do not affect dimensional stability. The third null hypothesis is that no correlation exists between changes in model accuracy and model volume.

Materials and methods

A standard maxillary dental model (Prosthetic Restoration Jaw Model D16-500A[GSF]-GF, Nissin) with half of its teeth replaced by standard abutment plastic teeth was scanned using an intraoral scanner (PrimeScan, Dentsply-Sirona) and then output as an STL format digital model. This digital model was then imported into 3D modelling software (FreeCAD 0.20) to incorporate 3 reference cylinders (height: 3 mm, diameter: 2 mm) onto the buccal surfaces at the midlines of the 2 central incisors, the left first molar and the right first molar, respectively. This model served as the reference digital model and based on this, 3 different types of internal designs were constructed using Model Builder software (software version: 19.3.0, 3Shape) and Lychee Slicer software (software version: 4.1.000, Mango 3D): (1) Hollow internal with perforated base (HB). (2) All solid (S). (3) Internal support structure with perforated base (SB) (Fig.). For design types HB and SB, the shell thickness was set to 1.5 mm and the diameter of the perforation at the base was 2 mm.FigureRepresentative cross-sectional views and physical samples of the 3 studied internal structures. The 3D printers used were calibrated according to the manufacturers’ guidelines before conducting 3D printing. Two types of 3D printers and their corresponding resins were used to fabricate the dental models: (1) an SLA printer (Form 3; Formlabs) with Model Resin V3, and (2) a DLP printer (Straumann P30+; Institute Straumann AG) with P Pro Master Model resin. The build layer thickness was set as 50 µm and all specimens were printed at zero degrees build orientation. For each type of internal design, 10 samples were printed with the sample size determined by referring to comparable studies in the field.26 Thus, a total of 60 physical models were fabricated. Then, all the printed models underwent postprocessing. They were carefully washed using a brush with 90% isopropanol and then cleaned ultrasonically for 10 minutes to remove excess resin. For the SLA workflow, the printed models were postcured using Form Cure unit (Formlabs) at 60 °C for 5 minutes under a 405 nm light source, following the official specifications for Model Resin V3. For the DLP workflow, postcuring was performed using the P Cure unit (Institute Straumann AG) under a vacuum of 50 mbar for 3 minutes, utilising the manufacturer-validated preset program for P Pro Master Model resin.

Each 3D printed physical model was scanned using a high accuracy (5 µm) desktop scanner (D2000, 3Shape) at 7 distinct time points (immediately, 24 hours, 72 hours, 1 week, 2 weeks, 3 weeks, 4 weeks) after postprocessing to get the digital study models and thus 420 STL files were obtained and exported. Between scanning sessions, all 3D-printed dental models were stored in a layered transparent storage box placed on the desktop. All model printing and storage activities were conducted in a windowless, light-controlled environment. The room maintained 24-hour air conditioning and lighting, with the temperature set at 25 °C. The surface illuminance at the storage box was measured at 216.1 lux using a digital lux meter (HP-881D, HoldPeak). The ambient relative humidity was monitored twice daily and maintained at 48.38%±9.05% throughout the entire storage period.

The study models and reference model were all imported into specific software (Geomagic Control 2015; 3D Systems Inc.) for accuracy evaluation. To ensure the common boundaries of 3D models for accuracy evaluation, all the imported models were firstly trimmed by a common plane generated by connecting 3 points separately selected from the top of 3 cylinders of the model. All study models were divided into 42 groups according to printer type (n = 2), internal design type (n = 3) and scan time point (n = 7). In Geomagic Control software, test models and reference models were superimposed for comparison. This process employs a best-fit algorithm to achieve maximum alignment between the point clouds of both models through multiple iterations. The distance between the point clouds is then quantified using the root mean square (RMS) value, enabling a quantitative representation of the differences between 2 models. The larger the RMS value, the greater the deviation between the 2 models. In this study, the average RMS value obtained from 10 superimposed test models with the reference model within each group was used to represent the trueness of that group's models. Meanwhile, the average RMS value derived from realigning 45 nonrepeated pairwise combinations among the 10 test models was used to represent the precision of that group's models. The volume of all acquired 3D models was measured using the ‘Volume measurement’ function within the Geomagic Control 2015 software.

Data analysis was conducted in SPSS 25 software (IBM Corp.). The significance level was set at α = 0.05. A posthoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (v3.1.9.4, Heinrich-Heine-Universität) based on the trueness data to verify the adequacy of the sample size (n = 10 per group). The normality of deviation data was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Linear mixed-effects models were explored, but due to violations of normality and homoscedasticity, the nonparametric Friedman test was retained as the primary analysis. The Friedman test was used to assess significant differences in trueness, precision and model volume across all time points. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed for pairwise comparisons between each time point and the immediate scanning group. The Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust posthoc multiple pairwise comparisons, where each of the 6 subsequent time points was compared to the immediate baseline, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.0083 (0.05/6). The dimensional stability of each model was defined as the absolute difference in RMS of trueness between the 4-week and initial time points, expressed as a percentage of the initial value, calculated as follows: Dimensional stability = |Trueness at 4 weeks − Initial Trueness| / Initial Trueness × 100%. The Kruskal-Wallis 1-way ANOVA and Dunn's test were used to test for significant differences in dimensional stability among different design types for different 3D printing workflows. Mann-Whitney U test was used to test significant differences in dimensional stability between different 3D printing workflows for each design type. The correlation between trueness, volume and time was assessed using Spearman's test.

For models printed by the DLP workflow, the Friedman test indicated statistically significant differences in trueness (all P < .001) and precision (all P < .05) across time points, regardless of the internal design. The RMS values of trueness showed a gradual increase over time for all design types. Posthoc pairwise comparisons revealed that for the HB design, trueness did not differ significantly from the immediate scanning group at time points before 3 weeks, but exhibited significantly higher RMS values at 3 and 4 weeks. For S and SB designs, no significant differences in trueness were observed prior to 2 weeks, whereas significantly higher RMS values were found at 2, 3 and 4 weeks compared to the immediate group. In contrast, subsequent posthoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0083) revealed that precision did not show statistically significant differences at any specific time point when compared directly with the immediate scanning group, regardless of the internal design.

For models printed by the SLA workflow, the Friedman test indicated no statistically significant differences in trueness across time points, regardless of the internal design (all P > .05). Regarding precision, no significant differences were observed across time points for the HB design. In contrast, significant differences in precision were found across time points for both the S and SB designs. Subsequent posthoc pairwise comparisons, however, indicated that for neither the S nor the SB design did precision at any time point differ significantly from that of the immediate scanning group.

Discussion

In this study, significant differences in accuracy were observed at different time points (P < .001) for every internal design when models were printed using the DLP workflow. Therefore, the first null hypothesis was rejected. When the same printing workflow was used, no significant difference in dimensional stability was found among models with different internal designs. However, regardless of the internal design, models printed by the SLA workflow exhibited significantly better dimensional stability than those printed by the DLP workflow (all P < .001). Consequently, the second null hypothesis was partially rejected. For models printed by the DLP workflow, Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between volume and trueness (RMS values), as well as between volume and time. A significant positive correlation was also found between time and trueness (RMS values). Thus, the third null hypothesis was rejected.

The immediate post‑printing state, being free from external mechanical forces or aging influences, represents the benchmark accuracy. Therefore, in this study, the accuracy at each time point was compared only with the immediate post‑printing accuracy to analyse the degree of deviation from this baseline at different storage time points. Additionally, since most restorative models are typically used within 1 month, the observation period in this study was set to 4 weeks to reflect realistic clinical and laboratory delay thresholds. For models printed by the SLA workflow, regardless of their internal design, no statistically significant difference in trueness was observed at any time point compared to the trueness immediately after printing. In contrast, models printed by the DLP workflow exhibited a declining trend in trueness over time, demonstrating a significant deviation from the baseline trueness after a certain period. This phenomenon may be attributed to the distinct printing technologies adopted by the 2 printers. The Form 3 printer employs SLA (point‑by‑point curing), whereas the P30+ employs DLP (layer‑wise curing). The degree of polymerisation completeness in layer‑wise curing may be lower than that achieved through point‑by‑point curing. Consequently, DLP‑printed models could be more susceptible to deformation over time.

For models fabricated using the DLP workflow, those with S and SB internal designs exhibited significant deviations from the baseline trueness starting from the 2‑week time point onward, while models with HB design showed significant differences beginning at the 3‑week time point. This indicates that a storage period exceeding 2 weeks leads to noticeable degradation in the trueness of models printed by the DLP workflow. Joda et al.27 also employed a Straumann P30 printer to produce dental models and similarly observed a continuous decline in accuracy over 28 days, with significant deviation emerging by the third week. Likewise, Lo Russo et al.28 documented significant time‑dependent increases in error of DLP‑printed surgical guides over a 20‑day period. Although these studies differ in specific printed objects or printer models, they collectively support the findings of the present study that the accuracy in DLP‑printed products tends to be affected by storage time. In addition, Chen et al.29 reported a significant decrease in accuracy after 1 month of storage for SLA‑printed surgical guides, which diverges from the finding of the present study. This discrepancy may be explained by their utilisation of an earlier‑generation SLA printer (Form 2), suggesting that even within the same printing technology, the specific printer model can also affect the long‑term dimensional stability of printed items.

Additionally, although the trueness of models printed using DLP workflow decreased over time, the precision did not show a statistically significant difference from the initial precision. This may be because the deformation patterns across individual test models within each group tended to be consistent, thereby preventing significant changes in precision. Accordingly, this study further examined the time-dependent volumetric changes of the 3D-printed models. For DLP-printed models, regardless of the internal design, the volume progressively decreased over time due to shrinkage. Volumetric measurements at time points prior to 1 week did not differ significantly from the initial scan group, whereas volume values at 1, 2, 3 and 4 weeks were significantly lower than those of the initial group. Although the time point at which significant differences emerged in trueness varied slightly, this trend in volume reduction parallels the observed decline in trueness. Spearman correlation analysis confirmed that, regardless of internal designs, volume was significantly negatively correlated with trueness (RMS value) and time, while time was significantly positively correlated with trueness (RMS value). These findings suggest that volumetric shrinkage is a highly probable contributing factor to the decline in trueness over time. In contrast, for models printed using the SLA workflow, no significant differences were observed in either volume or dimensional accuracy at any time point compared to their initial values. This provides further indirect evidence that changes in dimensional accuracy are indeed driven by volumetric contraction, although direct causality cannot be established without material-specific analyses (eg, degree of conversion, residual monomer, water sorption).

For models printed using the DLP workflow, no significant differences in dimensional stability were observed among the 3 internal designs. This indicates that the internal design does not affect the dimensional stability. Even hollow models exhibited dimensional stability comparable to that of solid models, obviating the need for material-heavy solid designs to enhance stability. Additionally, regardless of the internal design, models fabricated with SLA workflow demonstrated significantly better dimensional stability than those produced with DLP. Therefore, for 3D-printed dental products that undergo transportation or require storage prior to clinical use, the SLA workflow may be preferable. Given the constraints of the current sample size, a minor influence of internal design cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to definitively clarify this relationship.

It is important to acknowledge that the present study was not designed to isolate the effect of printing technology from other confounding factors. The 2 printing workflows differed not only in curing mechanism but also in resin composition, exposure parameters and post‑processing protocols, all of which can influence dimensional stability. Thus, observed differences should not be attributed solely to the printing technology itself. Nevertheless, both workflows strictly followed the manufacturers’ recommended protocols, including materials, postcuring equipment and procedures. Under these clinically relevant conditions, differences were still evident. Future studies controlling for material and post‑processing variables are needed to further delineate the intrinsic effects of printing technology.

This study has several limitations. First, only 1 printer‑resin configuration per system and a single full‑arch typodont under controlled laboratory conditions were evaluated, limiting generalisability to other materials, dental products (eg, implant casts, surgical guides), or environments with fluctuating temperature and humidity. Second, although the printing time of each model was recorded and scanning time points were strictly scheduled, the actual digitisation using a desktop scanner was performed manually. Consequently, minor deviations on the order of minutes may have occurred during data acquisition at each time point. Owing to the longitudinal repeated-measures design and the distinct visual and structural differences among the material groups, chronological randomisation and operator blinding were not feasible; however, all 3D alignments and data analyses were executed via fully automated algorithms to eliminate subjective investigator bias. Third, dimensional analysis was restricted to global 3D RMS values without localised 2D cross-sectional evaluations. Because the entire model undergoes continuous, dynamic deformation throughout the 4-week aging process, establishing a stable, reproducible reference plane across all timepoints was impossible. Fourth, the observation period in this study was set to 4 weeks. Longer‑term changes in model accuracy need further investigation. Finally, this research focused only on the accuracy changes of full‑arch dental models over storage time. While prior studies30 have analysed the temporal effects on the mechanical and structural properties of 3D‑printed occlusal devices, further validation is needed for other dental products, such as surgical guides and prostheses, regarding the influence of time on accuracy, physicochemical properties and related performance indicators. Such work is essential to ensure the reliability, quality and longevity of 3D‑printed dental products throughout their intended service life or under extended clinical use.

Conclusions

The following conclusions can be drawn within the limitations of this study:1.The influence of storage time on the accuracy of dental models differed between 2 evaluated printing workflows. Within the 4-week observation period, models printed by the SLA workflow demonstrated no significant changes in either trueness or precision. In contrast, models printed by the DLP workflow exhibited a marked decrease in trueness over time, while their precision remained statistically unchanged.2.Volumetric contraction was a highly related factor contributing to the decrease in trueness of models printed by the evaluated DLP workflow over time.3.The internal design of the models exerted no statistically significant influence on the dimensional stability of dental models printed with either tested SLA or DLP workflow.
