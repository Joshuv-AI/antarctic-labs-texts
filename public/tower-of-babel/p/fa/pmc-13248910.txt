Subcutaneous drug delivery of high concentration antibody products – part 2: formulation, device options, and clinical bridging strategies for patient-centric commercial presentations
Authors: Indrajit Ghosh, Suyash Deodhar, Hiten Gutka, Srini Sridharan, Dilbir Bindra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe landscape of drug delivery is undergoing a transformative shift. As demand for large-volume subcutaneous (SC) biologics continues to surge, the race to develop smarter, patient-centered combination drug products has never been more urgent. Here, we present a comprehensive analysis of SC combination drug product development from a commercial landscape perspective, with a focus on the dynamic interaction between delivery devices, biopharmaceutical formulation attributes, and clinical bridging strategies. From pre-filled syringes and auto-injectors to next-generation infusion pumps and on-body delivery systems, this review maps the full spectrum of delivery platforms, highlighting key factors from patient usability and tolerability to therapeutic performance and real-world preferences that drive trade-offs in the selection of one format over another. Furthermore, formulation strategies to support high-dose SC delivery via increased concentration, volume, or both are explored. Crucially, this review bridges the gap between bench and bedside, demonstrating clinical bridging strategies with multiple case studies that are essential for translating device-formulation decisions into outcomes that are both clinically meaningful and patient-friendly.

Introduction

In 2023, our team published a systematic review of commercial high concentration antibody drug products (HCAPs) approved in the United States (US).1 Building upon that foundational work, this review aims to provide a comprehensive framework for the design of subcutaneous (SC) combination drug products by integrating device and formulation considerations, supplemented by case studies examining bridging studies on clinical performance and patient tolerability. Here, we systematically examine the formulation landscape of SC and intravenous (IV) biologic products approved during 1998 to 2025, highlighting evolving trends in excipient selection such as buffers, surfactants, stabilizers, and viscosity modifiers, that are critical for ensuring stability and usability.1–4 While early on excipients were tasked primarily with maintaining chemical stability during storage, they must now also address challenges such as high viscosity at elevated concentrations, aggregation under mechanical stress during injection, and compatibility with complex device and packaging materials.5–8 We also present case studies of excipient choices and concentrations to support attributes such as ease of self-administration, improved tolerability, and compatibility with diverse delivery devices.5,6,9

SC administration offers clear advantages over IV infusion, including improved convenience, reduced reliance on healthcare infrastructure, and the potential for self-administration, attributes that align with modern patient-centered care models.10,11 However, delivering high-dose biologics via the SC route introduces substantial technical and clinical complexities.12 These include constraints on injection volume, formulation stability at high concentrations (≥150–200 mg/mL), and the need to manage viscosity, aggregation, and particle formation.5,12,13 Such challenges necessitate an integrated approach that combines advanced formulation strategies, innovative device engineering, and robust clinical development. Recent innovations in wearable on-body delivery systems/on-body infusion (OBDS/OBI) platforms have expanded the feasibility of SC delivery for large-volume biologics, enabling controlled infusion of 2–20 mL or more over extended durations.14–16 These technologies support at-home administration, improve tolerability, and reduce healthcare resource utilization, making them central to combination product strategies. Several OBDS platforms have already achieved regulatory approval, signaling a paradigm shift in therapeutic delivery across oncology, immunology, neurocience and other chronic disease areas.14–16

Despite these advances, transitioning from IV to SC administration requires careful lifecycle planning. Early-phase programs often favor IV delivery for speed and predictability, while later-phase transitions demand rigorous bridging strategies, including pharmacokinetic (PK) comparability studies, human factors testing, and proactive regulatory engagement.17–20 Furthermore, assumptions regarding SC volume limits (commonly cited up to 2 ml) are increasingly challenged by emerging evidence and the dose requirements of next-generation biologics, underscoring the need for data-driven assessments of tolerability.21,22 Ultimately, successful development of high-dose SC biologics hinges on balancing formulation complexity, device capability, patient usability, and regulatory alignment.1,23,24

The rapid expansion of the biopharmaceutical market within the scope of high dose, coupled with growing demand for patient-centric and self-administered therapies, has catalyzed innovation in biologic-device combination products, especially within the clinical landscape.9,20,23,25 SC delivery approaches can be classified into three primary domains to support high dose: 1) advanced delivery devices platform capable of administering larger volumes15,26; 2) SC space modifiers/permeation enhancers that transiently alter the extracellular matrix within SC tissue to enhance fluid dispersion, thereby improving tolerability with large-volume SC administration27,28; and 3) high concentration formulation approaches for stabilizing protein therapeutics.1,5 Optimization involves strategic excipient selection, pH modulation, and viscosity-reducing techniques to ensure manufacturability, stability, and injectability.5,29,30 31–33

Development of combination drug product with advanced delivery devices for subcutaneous administration

An evaluation of drugs approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) during 1998 to 2025 identified over 61 examples of biologic-device combination products formulated at high protein concentrations (≥100 mg/mL) (Supplemental Table S1). Small volume subcutaneous delivery devices – prefilled syringes and auto injectors in biologic-device combination products

Prefilled, spring-activated delivery systems represent a cornerstone in the evolution of biologic-device combination products, offering standardized configurations that enhance usability, dosing precision, and safety.18,37 Among these, prefilled syringes (PFSs) serve as the foundational platform, particularly for SC administration of high-concentration biologics.9,43 Standard formats (1 mL and 2.25 mL) often feature safety mechanisms like needle shields to prevent needlestick injuries.37,43 Among FDA-approved biologic-device combinations, more than 50% incorporate needle safety features, reflecting a growing emphasis on user protection and safe disposal practices.9,37 PFS are used manually by healthcare providers or patients and usually include a bulk-filled syringe as the main drug container, along with components like a plunger rod and finger flange.22,43,44 AIs, an advanced form of PFS, integrate patient-preferred features such as needle shielding with digital capabilities, including sensors, mobile applications, and audiovisual aids, to support self-administration.18,37,45 Despite technological progress, patient acceptance and tolerability remain essential for successful adoption.24,46 In Table 1, key features of the commercially available biologics that have successfully leveraged small volume SC delivery, which have predominantly utilized PFSs or small volume AIs along with some vial presentation, with typical injection volumes ranging from 0.4 mL to 2.0 mL, are summarized. To accommodate volume constraints while maintaining therapeutic efficacy, formulations are often developed at high protein concentrations, typically between 100 mg/mL and 200 mg/mL.42

Innovations like the SKYTROFA® autoinjector are reusable, rechargeable, and ready to use. Its design minimizes environmental waste by eliminating disposable pens and batteries. A single full charge delivers up to 4 weeks of use based on one injection per week, and the device is engineered to last up to 4 y or 210 injections, whichever comes first.47,48 Dupixent® (dupilumab) launched as PFS and AI.49 Some patients prefer manual control (PFS), while others favor ease, concealed needles, and quick self-injection (AI)50 PFS is better for slow injection pace, caregiver administration, and visual confirmation; AI appeals to those with needle anxiety or dexterity issues.50 Both formats include needle-protection features.49 AI reduces accidental sticks, while PFS allows manual control and visual confirmation.50,51 Injection site reactions are similar for both.52

While selecting the primary container, compatibility with monoclonal antibody (mAb) and extractable leachable assessments needs to be performed with syringe material.53,54 To accommodate evolving therapeutic needs, larger-volume PFS formats (e.g., 2.25 mL and 5 mL) are under active development, particularly for biologics requiring higher doses.55,56 Needle gauge selection is crucial in SC drug delivery, affecting both patient comfort and injection performance.57,58 Most SC biologics use fine-gauge needles to reduce pain.57,59 Products like Humira® citrate-free use 27 and 29-gauge needles, respectively, Larger gauges (23–26) are used for more viscous formulations, as seen in Xolair® and Cimzia®.57,60–62 These choices reflect a balance between formulation properties, injection force, and patient tolerability. Delivering high-viscosity biologics via SC injection poses engineering challenges, especially for autoinjectors.14,22,63 Devices like YpsoMate® 2.25 for Ajovy® use reinforced springs and flange supports to manage increased extrusion force.64–66 Advanced platforms like BD’s Neopak™ 2.25 mL enable rapid delivery within 10–15 s.63,67,68 Dual-chamber systems in PFSs and cartridges enable reconstitution of lyophilized biologics with limited liquid stability. These systems use separate chambers for drug and diluent, along with a dual-plunger mechanism for mixing.69,70

SC infusion pumps

Controlled SC administration of biologics is achieved using infusion pumps, particularly in clinical trial settings and hospital environments. These systems accommodate volumes ranging from 5 mL to over 100 mL and offer precise delivery for immunoglobulin therapies.16,71

Large volume SC delivery with permeation enhancer

Manual injection remains feasible for volumes up to approximately 2–3 mL per injection site; however, administration beyond this threshold becomes increasingly difficult, potentially compromising usability and long-term patient compliance.15,16,72,73 Tethered SC infusion pumps in the delivery of SC immunoglobulin (SCIG) therapies offer a practical alternative for both healthcare professionals (HCPs) and patients.74,75 Large volume SC delivery has been integrated with enabling technologies such as Halozyme’s ENHANZE® system, which utilizes recombinant human hyaluronidase PH20 (rHuPH20) to transiently degrade hyaluronan in the extracellular matrix.76–78 This enzymatic action enhances tissue permeability and facilitates the tolerability of high-volume SC infusion.78–80 Several approved biologic therapies leverage rHuPH20 to enable SC delivery of antibodies and other large-molecule drugs. These combination products incorporate rHuPH20 at 30,000 units/vial, supporting SC administration.77,78 A positive correlation between rHuPH20concentration and dose volume has been observed, underscoring its critical role in optimizing SC delivery and tolerability for high-dose biologic therapies as described in the formulation Case studies on on-body delivery systems.22,81,81

On-body delivery systems/on-body infusion for subcutaneous administration of biologics

OBDS devices represent a novel class of wearable drug delivery platforms designed to adhere to the skin via an integrated adhesive patch. OBDS are particularly advantageous for biologic therapies requiring large-volume delivery (typically ranging from 2 mL to 20 mL) and extended infusion times with pre-programmed duration.22,82 The hands-free nature of OBDSs alleviates the discomfort and ergonomic challenges associated with holding large volume autoinjectors (LVAIs) for prolonged periods, thereby reducing the likelihood of administration errors.83,84 Furthermore, the ability to accommodate larger volumes facilitates less frequent dosing regimens, which may improve patient adherence.85 Patient comfort is further enhanced using fine-gauge needles (commonly 25–30 gauge), which are associated with reduced injection pain.86 Several OBDS-enabled products have been commercialized (Table 1). A notable example of volume and device evolution is risankizumab (SKYRIZI®), initially approved in PFS (90 mg/ml) and autoinjector formats (150 mg/ml) for plaque psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. For Crohn’s disease, risankizumab has transitioned to a high-dose SC formulation delivered via a wearable OBDS, capable of administering 180 mg/1.2 mL or 360 mg/2.4 mL in under 5 min.36,40,87 Similarly, in neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, higher SC doses may be required to overcome limited bioavailability at the target site, particularly due to restricted antibody penetration across the blood-brain barrier.88,89

OBDSs can be categorized by user interaction level.82 Each category offers distinct advantages and limitations, and the choice of system must align with the drug’s physicochemical properties, dosing regimen, and patient needs. Considerations of key attributes for SC infusion

Infusion rate for SC delivery of high volume

Based on trend analysis of listed SC combination products, Infusion rates and durations vary substantially depending on formulation, delivery device, and therapeutic indication.72,82 Rapid manual injections via AIs and PFSs of drugs such as secukinumab (Cosentyx®) at 6 mL/min (0.1 mL/s) and omalizumab (Xolair®) at 3.6–7.2 mL/min (0.06–0.12 mL/s) typically deliver small volumes (≤2 mL) within 5–10 s.72,91,92 Hyaluronidase-enabled products demonstrate significantly faster delivery rates for larger volumes: VYVGART HYTRULO® achieves 11.2 mL/min with an infusion time of approximately 0.5 min for its 5 mL dose, while Hyqvia® delivers at 1–2 mL/min (60 mL to 120 mL/hr) per infusion site or as tolerated.80,93–96 Other hyaluronidase-facilitated products, including Darzalex Faspro®, Phesgo®, and Rituxan Hycela®, typically infuse at 2–5 mL/min over 3–10 min.80,97–100 OBDS provide controlled, extended delivery for patient convenience, with products like Repatha® (Pushtronex® system) delivered at an infusion rate of 0.7 mL/min (dose of 3.5 mL over 5 min), while Ultomiris® is infused at 0.35 ml/min (10–20 min duration) and Empaveli® is infused at 20–40 ml/h (30–60 min duration).101–103 SCIG therapies administered via programmable infusion pumps demonstrate the widest range of delivery parameters: Cuvitru®, Cutaquig®, and Xembify® are typically infused at 0.17–0.83 mL/min per site (10–50 mL/hr) with rate escalation protocols over 1–2 h.71,80,104–106 Overall, infusion times span from 5 s for rapid auto injector products to 2 h for high-volume SC infusion pump therapies.

Human factors engineering study

Usability assessments and real-world feedback are essential to ensure that drug-device combination products meet the needs of diverse patient populations.107–109 These evaluations must be integrated early and iteratively throughout development to minimize use-related risks and optimize product design.110,111 The FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) require human factors studies to ensure patients can use the products correctly at home.112–114 The labeling includes detailed instructions for use with illustrations. The process begins with a comprehensive user specification, which defines the product description, intended use, user population, use environments, and target indications.107,109 Use-related risks are systematically evaluated through Use-Related Risk Analysis and documented in the Use Failure Modes and Effects Analysis.108 These analyses guide the development of Essential Design Outputs, which ensure that the device meets Essential Performance Requirements, referred to in recent FDA draft guidance as Essential Drug Delivery Outputs.109

Neulasta® Onpro®

Amgen’s on-body injector kit for pegfilgrastim. It consists of a single-use on-body injector that is applied to the patient’s abdomen or upper arm immediately after chemotherapy and automatically delivers a 6 mg dose of Neulasta ~27 h later to stimulate white blood cell production. The Onpro device incorporates Insulet’s OmniPod® patch-pump technology.115 The device uses a small, lightweight, single-use, adhesive-backed injector that delivers 0.6 mL over ~45 min.115,116 The device has a self-adhesive backing and a hidden needle that inserts subcutaneously at the start of dosing.117

Udenyca® Onbody™

This Neulasta biosimilar competes directly with Onpro, offering the same functionality (automated next-day pegfilgrastim dosing). Notably, Udenyca’s on-body injector delivers the 6 mg/0.6 mL dose in only 5 min, significantly faster than Onpro. This “state-of-the-art” device was developed with LTS’s Sorrel wearable injector platform.118,119

Repatha® Pushtronex® system

Amgen’s on-body infusor for evolocumab, which is a PCSK9 inhibitor, enables a 420 mg dose of Repatha to be delivered subcutaneously over ~9 min, instead of being delivered as separate injections. The system consists of a wearable adhesive injector that holds a 3.5 mL prefilled cartridge of Repatha. Once activated, the device inserts a hidden needle and uses a battery-powered motor (West’s SmartDose® electronic platform) to inject the dose into the skin (abdomen, thigh, or arm).101,102,120 The Pushtronex was marketed starting in 2016, but Amgen discontinued it in mid-2024.120

Skyrizi® on-body injector

AbbVie’s wearable injector for risankizumab, an anti-IL-23 mAb. Skyrizi is approved for Crohn’s disease treatment, with an IV induction phase followed by SC maintenance dosing.40 For maintenance, AbbVie introduced an on-body injector with prefilled cartridge to deliver either 180 mg or 360 mg of Skyrizi at home.36 The Skyrizi OBI is a disposable electronic patch that the patient places on the thigh or abdomen.87 The drug comes in a 1.2 mL (180 mg) or 2.4 mL (360 mg) cartridge that clicks into the device. With the push of a button, a hidden needle inserts and the device injects the dose subcutaneously at a controlled rate. The device can deliver 2.4 mL in 5 min.40

Reusable wearable pumps and upcoming technologies

In contrast to the disposable devices above (each tied to a single dose of a specific drug), reusable on-body pumps that can be used for multiple doses or continuous infusion are available. While not yet widely applied to mAbs, they represent a future direction for flexible dosing for clinical development, connectivity, and minimization of the cost of goods.14,121 Sensile D-mine® Pump: This is a reusable patch-like pump used for continuous SC infusion of apomorphine (a small-molecule drug for Parkinson’s disease).122 It demonstrates features that could be applied to mAb delivery.123,124Enable enFuse®: The enFuse is a large-volume wearable injector platform that takes a unique approach. It is a patient-filled device that can deliver 5–25 mL from a standard vial.125 It is intended to be single use per dose, but uses a reusable transfer mechanism. Enable Injections has partnered with multiple pharmaceutical companies to test enFuse for high-volume biologics.126,127 The enFuse has drawn interest because it allows use of the original drug vial (no custom cartridge), potentially simplifying IV to SC bridging.14,128Others: Additional noteworthy technologies include Ypsomed’s YpsoDose® (a 10 mL prefilled electromechanical patch injector with automatic needle safety in clinical testing as of 2023),129 Nemera’s Symbioze® (a prototype wearable pump with a reusable electronic core and disposable 20 mL drug module), and the Subcuject osmotic pump.130

Strategies and formulation considerations - formulation composition in biologics: evolution across presentations (1998–2025)

Three principal strategies are used as levers to enable SC delivery of high-dose biologics: 1) high concentration–low volume, 2) low concentration–high volume, and 3) high concentration–high volume.24 Historically, the high concentration–low volume approach has been preferred due to its compatibility with conventional delivery devices such as PFSs and AIs.138 However, recent trends indicate a growing shift toward high concentration–high volume formulations, particularly for biologics targeting complex diseases such as neurological disorders, where crossing the blood-brain barrier necessitates elevated systemic exposure.88 This section summarizes the excipient strategies that have evolved over the years in the development of high concentration SC biologics from 61 commercial formulation with protein concentration of 100 mg/ml and above (Figure 3). The early era - simplicity and the IV vial (1998–2008)

In the initial phase of biologic approvals, formulations were designed primarily for long-term stability in a vial before reconstitution or dilution for IV infusion. This delivery route permitted relatively large volumes and low protein concentrations, often below 50 mg/mL. Early products like pegfilgrastim and omalizumab utilized a basic set of components: a simple buffer system, a common non-reducing sugar, and a standard surfactant.3,92 The Polysorbate 80 (PS80) surfactant became the conventional standard, chosen for its proven efficacy in reducing interfacial tension and preventing aggregation, particularly in early mAb formulations like golimumab.139,140 The primary delivery for this era, including abatacept.141 and certolizumab pegol,62 was the PFS.

The mid-transition-PFS and the SC concentration challenge (2009–2020)

This phase is marked by an increasing focus of the industry on SC self-administration, driven by the commercial advantage and enhanced patient quality of life offered by home use. This transition placed a critical constraint on formulation design: the need to deliver the full therapeutic dose in a small, patient-acceptable volume, typically 2 mL. This necessitated increasing protein concentrations, often exceeding 200 mg/mL (belimumab62) and 120–150 mg/mL (alirocumab,142 evolocumab102). This concentration shift introduced major physicochemical challenges, including protein aggregation, opalescence, and, most critically, dramatically elevated solution viscosity.143–145 These hurdles forced a strategic milestone in excipient selection.

The histidine buffer standard

The formulation data confirms the near-universal adoption of L-histidine as the buffering agent during this period. Histidine provides effective buffering capacity around the pH 5.5–6.5 range, which is optimal for the colloidal stability of most mAbs (e.g., omalizumab, golimumab, alirocumab).92,139,146,147 It replaced earlier simple phosphate-based systems, which were less effective or carried a higher risk of precipitation under certain high-concentration conditions.144 Histidine’s utility in stabilizing proteins and mitigating aggregation affirmed its position as the default buffer for modern SC biologics.148 Among commercially available products, histidine is the most prevalent buffer, present in 38 formulations, primarily within the pH range of 5.0–6.9 (Supplemental Table S1). Phosphate, the only inorganic buffer used, appears in seven products, predominantly at pH 6.0–7.9, and offers buffering capacity through its multiple pKa values (2.1, 6.9, 12.4). Citrate is used in six formulations across pH ranges of 5.0–7.9, with concentrations typically between 0.002 M and 0.038 M. Acetate, found in 11 products, is used exclusively at pH 5.0–5.9.

Amino acid viscosity modifiers

Elevated viscosity complicates ultrafiltration/diafiltration processes, increases the risk of protein aggregation, and limits injectability through fine-gauge needles commonly used in PFSs and AIs.149 Antibody aggregation remains a critical concern in the development of biologic therapeutics due to its multifaceted impact on product quality and clinical performance.150 Aggregates are often associated with increased immunogenic potential, altered pharmacological activity, and diminished therapeutic efficacy.151 To accommodate these constraints, viscosity-lowering strategies such as the use of excipients like arginine and sodium chloride are often used. L-Arginine, which first appeared in the earlier omalizumab formulation, is also added in several high-concentration formulations (omalizumab, belimumab, satralizumab-mwge).92,152,153 Arginine acts as a protein-protein interaction modifier, shielding or weakening attractive forces between mAb molecules that lead to high viscosity.154 Proline was also utilized as a viscosity modifier and stabilizer, notably in the high-dose Repatha (evolocumab) formulation.102,155 Viscosity typically peaks near the isoelectric point (pI), where electrostatic repulsion is minimized, promoting protein clustering.156 However, the impact of pH is protein-specific and may be modulated by formulation components.5,156 Based on our assessment, commercial antibody products exhibit a pH range of 4.8–8.0, with the most common range being 6.0–6.9.1,3 Buffers such as histidine, phosphate, citrate, acetate, and succinate are used to maintain pH stability, with histidine being the most frequently used due to its favorable buffering capacity near physiological pH. Buffer concentrations typically range from 0.01 M to 0.02 M, exceeding antibody molar concentrations by 10–20-fold to ensure adequate buffering.157,158 Notably, Humira® citrate-free demonstrates that high antibody concentrations can provide sufficient buffering via ionizable side chains, suggesting that buffers may be omitted under specific formulation conditions.39,159 Viscosity reducing agents (VRA) used in the approved high concentration liquid formulations in the US have been summarized in the recent publication.30

Surfactant and stabilizer diversification

Sucrose (abatacept, alirocumab, mepolizumab, dupilumab) emerged as the single most common bulking and stabilizing agent, chosen for its non-reducing nature, high solubility, and effectiveness as a preferred exclusion stabilizer, particularly for liquid formulations. While Polysorbate 80 remained prevalent (golimumab,139 evolocumab,102 mepolizumab,160) stability concerns regarding its potential degradation to peroxides and its subsequent role in oxidizing sensitive mAb residues began to push formulators toward Polysorbate 20 (PS20) (omalizumab,92 alirocumab,142 brodalumab,161 sarilumab),162,163 Poloxamer 188 has also demonstrated strong performance in preventing agitation-induced aggregation and is increasingly considered a valuable alternative in biopharmaceutical formulations.164

Isotonifiers

Isotonifiers, including sorbitol and sodium chloride, are selectively used to ensure physiological compatibility.4 Tonicity and osmolality adjustments are essential for ensuring formulation stability, injectability, and patient comfort.165 Sucrose is the most prevalent osmolality-adjusting agent, found in 52.8% of products. Arginine may serve dual roles in some formulations, functioning as both a viscosity-lowering and tonicity-adjusting agent.4

Chelators

Chelators such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and antioxidants like methionine appear in a few products, contributing to oxidative stability. Diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA), a chelating agent structurally like EDTA, but with five carboxylic acid groups, is used in select antibody formulations to enhance stability by binding trace metal ions that may catalyze degradation.166,167 DTPA is present in Zinplava™,168 Yervoy®169 and Zevalin®.170

The modern landscape: specialization and device compatibility (2021–2025)

In the modern era, excipient strategy must account for compatibility with primary packaging in the combination product. Surfactants like polysorbate are essential for preventing protein adsorption into the syringe material and mitigating the risk of protein aggregation caused by interactions with silicone oil used as a lubricant in PFS and AI (nirsevimab-alip,171 lebrikizumab-lbkz.172) Furthermore, the formulation’s pH and ionic strength is carefully controlled to prevent leaching of trace elements (like tungsten) from glass syringes, which could trigger protein instability.173 Thus, excipients like histidine and arginine are now viewed not only for their stabilizing properties but also for their ability to ensure the long-term safety and integrity of the drug-device combination.

Excipient selection plays a pivotal role in enabling the stability, manufacturability, and injectability of high-concentration mAb formulations. Trend analysis of excipient selection in mAb formulations (Figure 3) reveals a clear preference for histidine as the primary buffering agent, followed by acetate, phosphate, and citrate systems. Among stabilizing sugars, sucrose remains the most widely utilized due to its proven efficacy in maintaining protein integrity during storage and administration. For surfactants, polysorbate 80 dominates as the preferred choice, offering robust protection against interfacial stress and aggregation. To address viscosity challenges inherent to high-concentration formulations, L-arginine has emerged as the most employed VRA, although other VRA categories such as amino acids, salts, and polyols have also been explored to optimize injectability and patient comfort.

Additionally, particle-based technologies, including electrospraying and microglassification offered by platforms such as Elektrofi and Lindy Biosciences, enable ultra-high concentration formulations (>400 mg/mL). These approaches often rely on non-aqueous solvents and require extensive process development, posing challenges for drug product compatibility and regulatory acceptance.174 Recently, Elektrofi technology has demonstrated bioequivalence between protein microparticle suspensions and conventional aqueous formulations.5,174

Case studies: elaborative summary of recent formulation changes in leading biologic therapies

Xolair (omalizumab)

Xolair (omalizumab) is a recombinant mAb that binds immunoglobulin E (IgE), used in the treatment of moderate to severe allergic asthma, chronic idiopathic urticaria, and other selected allergic conditions. Traditionally, Xolair was provided as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) 150 mg vial, stabilized with histidine and sucrose.92 This format requires reconstitution with sterile water prior to administration, a process that demands time, precise technique, and is typically performed by trained healthcare professionals within a clinical setting, posing logistical and convenient challenges for both patients and providers. In 2018, Xolair advanced its administration technology by introducing a ready-to-use 150 mg/mL solution, packaged in a PFS and then AI in 2025.175 The new formulation is buffered with histidine and arginine, designed to maintain protein stability and isotonicity while eliminating the need for onsite reconstitution. This innovation greatly simplifies the medication process. Patients and healthcare providers can administer Xolair directly from the syringe, drastically reducing preparation time and the margin for procedural errors.92,176

Rituxan Hycela (rituximab and hyaluronidase)

In 2017, Rituxan Hycela was introduced to enable the rapid, SC administration of rituximab, a key drug in treating hematologic cancers and autoimmune diseases. Combining rituximab with rHuPH20, an enzyme that transiently increases tissue permeability in the subcutaneous space, allows large-molecule biologics such as rituximab to be administered subcutaneously in 5–7 min, drastically reduced treatment time from hours using IV formulation. Importantly, the solution is citrate-free, which not only maintains drug stability and isotonicity but also reduces injection site discomfort often associated with citrate-containing formulations. Thus, Rituxan Hycela set a new precedent in the field of biologic therapy by providing a rapid, well-tolerated, and highly effective alternative to traditional infusion-based treatment.73,76,97

Herceptin Hylecta (trastuzumab and hyaluronidase)

SC formulation combines trastuzumab (recombinant IgG1) and recombinant human hyaluronidase in a histidine-buffered solution for 600 mg/5 mL doses. SC administration is achieved in approximately 5 min, offering an alternative to conventional IV infusions (Herceptin Hylecta).78,135

Benlysta (belimumab)

Benlysta (belimumab) is a human mAb that targets B-lymphocyte stimulator, thereby reducing aberrant B-cell activity in autoimmune disease. It was first approved in 2011 for systemic lupus erythematosus. The original product was an IV formulation administered in healthcare settings. To enhance patient convenience and access, a high-concentration SC formulation was later developed and approved in 2017153. Belimumab.152,177 was initially available only as a lyophilized powder for IV infusion. Each vial (120 mg or 400 mg) must be reconstituted with sterile water and further diluted for infusion. In 2017, a new SC formulation of belimumab was approved, offering the first self-injectable treatment option for lupus patients. This formulation is a pre-formulated liquid at 200 mg/mL, supplied in 1 mL single-dose PFS or AI pens. Each autoinjector delivers a fixed 200 mg dose, allowing convenient once-weekly SC injections by the patient at home.178 Achieving such high antibody concentration (200 mg/mL) was a significant pharmaceutical achievement.179

Evolution of Humira formulation

Humira (adalimumab) is a leading biologic TNF-α inhibitor used across a broad spectrum of inflammatory diseases, initially formulated as a 50 mg/mL solution delivered via PFS or AI pens providing 40 mg in 0.8 mL per injection. Between 2018 and 2020, innovations were introduced to the Humira formulation and delivery system to enhance patient experience. Central to these improvements was the removal of citrate/phosphate buffers and replaced by less irritating excipients, which substantially reduced injection site pain, as confirmed by patient surveys and clinical studies. Simultaneously, the concentration was doubled to 100 mg/mL, allowing the same therapeutic dose to be administered in just 0.4 mL, markedly decreasing injection volume and further improving comfort.159

Clinical bridging strategy of device-related combination drug products: regulatory considerations and risk mitigation approaches

The integration of drug delivery SC devices into clinical development demands a strategic, risk-based framework. In early-phase studies, including Phase 1, dose escalation is typically conducted via IV and SC routes to characterize safety, tolerability, and PK profiles. Given the substantial investment and regulatory complexity associated with device development, the timing of device introduction generally aligned with the maturity of the drug candidate and the overarching development strategy.108 Clinical bridging studies play a pivotal role in ensuring the safety and efficacy of drug products when transitioning between formulations, delivery systems, or manufacturing processes.180 These studies typically assess bioequivalence (BE) or bioavailability (BA), especially during shifts from IV to SC administration. Regulatory agencies like the FDA and EMA emphasize the importance of patient preference and usability data, particularly when bridging from vial-and-syringe formats to devices like AI or wearable pump/OBDS.181 Ultimately, bridging strategies, whether sequential or parallel, enable faster development timelines and improved patient-centric delivery options, reduction of chair time, while maintaining regulatory compliance and therapeutic consistency.182,183

For well-characterized or previously approved drug candidates, early introduction of the commercial device presentation, potentially as early as Phases 1 or 2, can accelerate regulatory approval and commercial launch.9 This approach is supported by a high level of confidence in the molecule’s safety and efficacy profile, enabling earlier bridging to the final device configuration. Conversely, for novel drug candidates, device integration is typically deferred until later development phases (e.g., Phase 3), once sufficient clinical data has been generated in addition to drug product stability, dosing requirements, and patient usability. To mitigate regulatory risk and prevent delays in product launch timelines, early engagement with health authorities regarding the clinical bridging strategy is critical. Bridging studies may be required to establish bioequivalence, tolerability, and usability between different device presentations. An alternative approach involves initiating Phase 3 trials with a non-final device presentation followed by a dedicated clinical study to support the transition to the commercial device. Such studies should be also designed to demonstrate comparability in bioavailability, patient tolerability, and ease of use, thereby facilitating regulatory acceptance and fostering patient confidence.9

A notable example is daratumumab (Darzalex Faspro®), initially approved as an IV formulation requiring 3–7-h infusions. In 2020, an SC formulation was introduced, enabling administration in under 5 min.184 Currently, the SC version accounts for approximately 93% of daratumumab usage in the United States,185 reflecting strong patient and provider preference. Also, emerging evidence suggests that SC delivery may confer unexpected efficacy advantages in certain therapeutic contexts. For instance, a recent study of amivantamab (Rybrevant®), a bispecific anti-cancer immunotherapy agent, demonstrated superior efficacy with SC administration compared to IV infusion.186 This enhanced response may be attributed to lymphatic absorption pathways, which could potentially motivate immune-mediated activity.

In some cases, a parallel development strategy is used, wherein two commercial device options (e.g., PFS and AI) are developed concurrently, with the AI submission occurring 1–2 y later, supported by comprehensive usability and PK data. The transition from IV to SC delivery typically involves a PK-based bridging strategy, using endpoints such as trough concentration (Ctrough) and area under the curve (AUC), with safety, efficacy, and immunogenicity evaluated as secondary measures.187 Comparative bioavailability or bioequivalence studies are often conducted in healthy volunteers to validate new device configurations.

SC administration of high-dose biologics introduces unique challenges that can influence product performance, patient experience, and overall clinical success. Key delivery attributes, such as injection volume, device type, and injection rate, need to be assessed to eliminate risk associated with PK behavior, local tolerability, and immunogenicity risk.5,144,188 Large injection volumes may increase tissue backpressure and discomfort, while high viscosity can compromise syringeability and device reliability. Similarly, variability in device performance or patient handling can lead to inconsistent dosing and absorption profiles.22,189 A systematic risk assessment of these factors (Figure 4) is essential to ensure robust product design, mitigate potential delays in clinical timelines, and maintain compliance with regulatory expectations. Low volume and high volume SC delivery.

Studies involving large-volume injections of mAbs and other immunoglobulins (up to 50 mL) confirmed that increased volume and rate did not compromise serum concentrations, safety, or tolerability. These findings were consistent across manual and pump-assisted delivery methods (Table 4B). Notably, SC administration of crenezumab at volumes up to 40.0 mL maintained PK and bioavailability integrity with and without rHuPH20 enzyme.197 Collectively, these results support the feasibility of large-volume, high-rate SC injections without compromising PK equivalence, offering valuable insights into the development of next-generation delivery systems.

Considerations for clinical bridging for changing the SC delivery systems

The transition from conventional IV administration or handheld devices to on-body delivery systems for mAbs represents a paradigm shift, as stated previously. As presented in Figure 4, bridging strategies are essential to ensure that this transition maintains therapeutic integrity while meeting stringent regulatory requirements across global markets. Validating a new drug delivery system requires demonstrating equivalence across three key dimensions: pharmacological, immunogenic, and usability.199 PK and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) comparability is established through Phase 1 studies and population PK modeling for confirmation that key exposure parameters (AUC, Cmax, steady state) match the reference regimen.182 Immunogenicity assessments are performed to demonstrate consistently low anti-drug antibody incidence.200 Finally, human factors studies validate that patients can intuitively and safely operate the device including hands-free delivery and reliable adhesion under physical activity.199

Case studies on clinical bridging for on-body devices and auto injectors

Repatha

Repatha (evolocumab) at 120 mg/ml concentration was originally given by SC PFS (140 mg, every 2 weeks). The Pushtronex on-body infusor (developed with West’s Smart Dose technology) was introduced to deliver a 420 mg dose every 4 weeks in one hands-free injection/on-body patch pump.201 Bridging to this OBDS focused on showing that a single 420 mg SC dose via the wearable device is equivalent in exposure and effect to the original dosing methods.35,102,202 Evolocumab’s PK/PD comparability study demonstrated no clinically meaningful differences in LDL-cholesterol lowering between monthly and biweekly regimens, supporting device substitution.101,203

Skyrizi (Risankizumab)

Skyrizi is an IL-23 mAb initially given as SC injections for psoriasis and Crohn’s disease. An OBDS was developed to deliver Skyrizi in a single self-administered dose, which is particularly useful for Crohn’s disease maintenance, as a high SC dose of 360 mg is required.40,87 A Phase 1, 4-arm study in healthy volunteers evaluated different SC injection volumes and speeds using infusion pump medical devices. Results showed no significant effect of injection rate or volume on risankizumab exposure, and no meaningful differences in injection-site pain. Ultomiris (Ravulizumab)

Ultomiris is a long-acting C5 complement inhibitor for PNH and other rare diseases. Its original form is an intravenous infusion. To offer greater flexibility, an SC formulation was developed of ravulizumab with an OBDS. The OBDS consists of two wearable injectors with prefilled cartridges (each ~3.5 mL, 245 mg) that can be worn concurrently to deliver the full weekly SC dose (490 mg) in about 10 min.205 The bridging plan demonstrated that SC self-administration via OBDS is non-inferior to IV infusions in terms of PK and disease control (Table 5). The Ultomiris® SC OBDS has been discontinued from the FDA-approved label as of 2024.

Lecanemab

LEQEMBI® (lecanemab-irmb) is an investigational anti-amyloid mAb developed for the treatment of early Alzheimer’s disease.207 Initial SC studies in healthy volunteers demonstrated a relative bioavailability (rBA) of approximately 0.49, whereas Alzheimer’s disease patients exhibited an increase to ~0.62, reflecting about 12% higher systemic exposure.208,209 Additionally, SC administration has shown an 18% improvement in efficacy compared with IV delivery, enabling a 30% reduction in SC dosing relative to IV (from 720 mg to 500 mg weekly). Further optimization during Phase 3 open-label extension for biologics license application submission introduced a maintenance regimen using a 2 mL auto-injector, reducing the dose from 720 mg to 360 mg weekly which is a 50% reduction.209 These findings emphasize the importance of understanding PK-PD relationships in during clinical development, where AUC at steady state (AUCss) may better predict SC efficacy, while Cmax remains the strongest predictor for IV administration.208,210{Tracy, 2025 #344}.

Present and future perspectives on subcutaneous dose ranges: formulation and delivery system synergy

To overcome the inherent dose limitations associated with SC administration, mAb formulation has been engineered to achieve high concentrations while leveraging advanced delivery systems capable of accommodating larger volumes.22 Recent innovations in formulation science have enabled concentrations approaching 200 mg/mL, which, when paired with commercially viable OBDS/OBI, can support doses up to approximately 5 g using devices with capacities of ~25 mL.14,24 Figure 5 illustrates the current commercial landscape, highlighting these capabilities and their role in meeting clinical efficacy requirements. For immunoglobulin therapies, the achievable dose range is even broader, extending to 20 g or more using SC infusion pumps, which provide continuous delivery over extended periods.81 Looking ahead, as high-dose biologics advance through clinical pipelines, both levers, ultra-high concentration and high-volume delivery must be optimized.22 Future strategies may push protein concentrations from 200 mg/mL toward 500 mg/mL using ultra high concentration technology, paired with next-generation infusion systems.1,24,174 Such configurations could enable administered doses approaching 50 g via infusion pumps and up to ~12.5 g using OBDS/OBI devices. Conclusion

The evolution of SC biologic drug delivery represents a convergence of formulation science, device engineering, and patient-centric design. This review demonstrates that the transition from conventional intravenous administration to advanced SC delivery systems spanning PFS, AI, hyaluronidase-enabled formulations, infusion pump, and OBDS has been driven by the dual imperative of therapeutic efficacy and improved patient quality of life.

Retrospective analysis of FDA-approved biologic-device combination products (2001–2025) underscores a clear trajectory toward higher protein concentrations (≥100 mg/mL), larger deliverable volumes, and increasingly sophisticated device platforms. Excipient innovations particularly histidine buffers, arginine-based viscosity reduction, and optimized surfactant systems have been instrumental in enabling these high-concentration formulations while maintaining physicochemical stability and syringeability.

Clinical bridging strategies, supported by PK comparability studies and rigorous human factors validation, have facilitated the seamless transition between device presentations without compromising therapeutic consistency or immunogenicity profiles.35 Lifecycle management approaches, exemplified by products such as SKYRIZI® and HUMIRA®, illustrate how formulation and device co-development can be strategically aligned to maximize regulatory flexibility and patient adherence.35,39,159

Looking ahead, the integration of ultra-high concentration technologies (approaching 500 mg/mL) with next-generation infusion platforms will be pivotal in expanding SC delivery to address high-dose therapeutic demands, ultimately redefining the boundaries of SC biologic administration, while removing the dose barrier.
