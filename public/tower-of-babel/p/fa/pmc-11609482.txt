The driving mechanism of citizens’ protective behaviors for rainstorm flood disasters in China: An empirical study based on the protection motivation theory and protective action decision model
Authors: Qunying Wu, Rui Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Background/significance]Rainstorm floods have become a high-impact natural disaster and are expected to become more extreme shortly, seriously threatening human safety. Although the government issues timely and precise rainstorm flood warning information, citizens remain indifferent and engage in maladaptive behavior. Therefore, understanding the relationship between emergency information, psychological cognition, individual characteristics, and protective behavior is crucial for effective risk information communication and evacuation guidance.[Method/process]This study utilizes the protection motivation theory (PMT) and protective action decision model (PADM) to develop a structural equation model (SEM) that evaluates the factors driving both protective and non-protective behaviors of individuals to rainstorm flood disasters.[Result/conclusion]The findings indicate that the disaster experience and emergency information received significantly shape how individuals perceive risks, their ability to manage them, and who should be accountable for the disaster. The findings further indicate that how people view their ability to handle a risk situation (perceived coping ability) and who they think is responsible for the situation (perceived attribution of responsibility) directly influence whether they will take protective behaviors. Based on these results, this study puts forward policy recommendations for emergency information release strategies to enhance citizens' willingness to adopt protective behaviors.Keywords: Protective behavior, Non-protective behavior, Rainstorm floods, Protection motivation theory, Protective action decision model

Introduction

With the acceleration of climate change and urbanization, heavy rainfall events become more extreme, and rainstorm flood disasters are one of the most destructive natural disasters in China [1,2]. From 2021 to 2023, according to China's Ministry of Emergency Management, the most frequent natural disasters in China are rainstorm floods. One of the most severe rainstorm floods was the 7·20 Henan rainstorm in 2021, which affected 14.786 million people and caused 398 deaths. Another one is the 7·11 Sichuan rainstorm floods in 2022, which influenced 279,000 people and resulted in 36 casualties. The most recent is the 7·3 Wanzhou of Chongqing rainstorm floods in 2023, which caused 358,000 people to suffer varying degrees of damage. To counteract such extreme weather events, early warning systems play an important role in assisting citizens to prepare for disasters and evacuate in advance. However, numerous citizens in disaster areas remain hesitant to adopt protective behaviors when receiving official warnings, often displaying indifference and delaying action. For example, during July 20, 2021 Zhengzhou rainstorm disaster, the Zhengzhou Meteorological Bureau repeatedly issued red alerts through various channels. However, most citizens still ignored the warning information and continued to take buses and subways, leading to tragic incidents such as drowning in subways, tunnels, and cars. The effectiveness of early warning information was unsatisfactory. This serves as a reminder that we need to deeply understand the relationship between emergency information, psychological cognition, individual characteristics, and protection behavior. Doing so is crucial for designing early warning information release strategies that are more comprehensible, acceptable, and targeted.

In the field of risk management and disaster response research, several social cognitive models explain the driving mechanism of protective behaviors in natural hazards [[3], [4], [5]]. These models include the protection motivation theory (PMT) and protective action decision model (PADM). They describe the entire process from early warning information through cognitive assessment to behavior change, aligning with the research theme of this paper. Rogers' PMT posits those various sources of information drive risk and response assessment, leading to either protective or non-protective behaviors. Risk assessment involves perceiving the threat, including its probability and severity. Response assessment evaluates the ability to mitigate the threat, including response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response cost [6]. Lindell's PADM describes the public's behavioral response to disaster situations. Warning information from various channels, environmental and social cues, and individual experiences affect an individual's psychological perception (including risk perception, stakeholder perception, and protective behavior perception), which form the basis of protective behavior (including protective responses, emotional regulation, information search) [7]. PADM builds upon PMT by incorporating stakeholder perception (e.g., who is responsible for the disaster) and categorizing types of protective behavior. Thus, integrating the above two models can enrich the attributes of cognitive assessment and behavior.

Many studies explore the prerequisites for disaster protective behaviors, including cognitive factors, individual characteristics, and social factors. According to PMT, PADM, and common research perspectives, individual protective behavior is mainly influenced by psychological cognition, such as risk perception, response efficacy, self-efficacy, perceived urgency, emotional state, and stakeholder perception [3,8,9]. However, some scholars introduce the concept of risk perception paradox, which suggests that a strong awareness of risk does not always result in risk mitigation behavior [10]. The paradox may result from cognitive biases and irrationality. For example, individuals may recognize the risks but still be reluctant to evacuate due to attachment to the property, fear of the high costs of evacuation, or a belief that they will not be hurt. There is room for further research on the impact of cognitive bias on risk perception as an explanation for this paradox. Additionally, some research suggests that individual characteristics, such as flood experience and traditional disaster knowledge, are equally important for protective behavior [11]. However, other researchers believe that individuals' memory of disasters gradually diminishes over time. A recent study shows that people can often reinvigorate their flood memories [12]. Thus, the impact of disaster experience on protective behavior remains uncertain. Another perspective highlights social influence as a crucial factor of protective behavior. Researchers believe that social networks provide incentives for individuals to prepare for disasters and take risk mitigation measures [13]. For example, individuals may imitate their neighbors when choosing disaster adaptation strategies [14], and group pressures and social norms can also lead individuals to learn and conform to the choices of others [15]. Moreover, emergency information issued by local governments acts as a priming factor for cognition and behavior, directly influencing the citizens' understanding of the disaster situation and whether they take action. Previous studies explore the relationship between risk information channels, information quality, information valence, risk perception, and behavior [11,16]. However, the decision to initiate protective behavior in response to information is influenced by cognitive bias, personal experience, and psychological perception. What kind of emergency information is effective? How does this information interact with the above factors to drive behavior? Unfortunately, these critical issues are not fully explored in existing research. The heavy rain disaster in Zhengzhou on July 20, 2021, highlights the importance and urgency of addressing these issues.

Through this literature review, the study aims to: (1) explore how emergency information, individual characteristics, such as heuristic bias (where individuals use limited information and readily accessible "convenience attributes" to assess disasters, potentially leading to biased judgments) and disaster experience interact to influence multi-dimensional psychological cognition; (2) analyze the relationship between psychological cognitive dimensions and both non-protective and protective behavior. To achieve these goals, we investigate a diverse sample (N = 915) from three regions in China (Henan, Sichuan, and Chongqing) that experience rainstorm floods. By integrating the protection motivation theory (PMT) and protective action decision model (PADM), a structural equation model (SEM) is developed to explore the driving mechanism behind citizens’ protective behaviors, aiming to thoroughly understand the psychological processes and behavioral orientations of citizens during emergency information processing.

This study supplements the existing literature in the following ways. (1) While previous studies find that the channel and quality of emergency information can affect risk perception, our study provides insights into emergency information by comprehensively examining the attributes of emergency information and its impact on protective behavior under the interaction of individual characteristics and cognitive assessment. It is crucial to improve both the expression and effectiveness of disaster warning information, making citizens more attentive and responsive. (2) We incorporate the perceived attribution of responsibility into psychological cognition. The relationship between coping perception, risk perception, and protective behavior has been debated, with some scholars even proposing a risk perception paradox. Our research improves the understanding of this aspect in the field of risk perception by incorporating individual characteristics, such as cognitive bias and disaster experience, into the frameworks of PMT and PADM.

Conceptual framework and hypotheses

In this study, non-protective behavior is categorized into two types: status quo maintenance and delayed behavior, as outlined by Kahneman and Tversky [17]. According to the protective action decision model (PADM), protective behavior includes three key components: a protective response, emotional regulation, and information search [7]. Fig. 1 illustrates the conceptual framework of this study. According to the existing literature, 19 hypotheses are proposed.

Conceptual model describing hypotheses and driving factors of protective behavior.

The social amplification of risk (SAR) framework shows that emergency information can change perceived risk [18]. According to Wood et al., emergency information comprises three components: information content, information density, and information observed [19]. The information content refers to warning messages that people receive, including threat descriptions, potential damage to families and individuals, as well as advice and instructions on behaviors. Information density indicates the information received through media, friends, government, and communities. Information observed is information obtained by observing the risk mitigation behaviors of others. Liu and Jiao's investigation reveals that emergency information positively influences risk probability and severity [20]. Hypothesis 1 (H1) therefore posits that “Emergency information (EI) directly influences citizens' perceived risk (PR).”

Hypothesis 2 (H2) also postulates that “Emergency information (EI) directly influences citizens’ perceived coping ability (PCA).” Research shows that emergency information contains detailed descriptions of coping behavior, and can make people feel more informed and confident in their ability to respond [21]. Others find that the provision of information on flood and response measures is associated with higher coping assessments [22]. The more comprehensive the social discourse received about rainstorm floods, the higher their perceived coping ability [23].

Hypothesis 3 (H3) posits that “Emergency information (EI) directly influences citizens’ perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR).” The “attribution of responsibility” of the protective action decision model (PADM) includes not only perceived personal responsibility but also “shared responsibility” [24]. It finds that emergency information is positively correlated with attribution of responsibility, including government, enterprise, and personal responsibility [25]. The information received can shape perceptions of government accountability while also increasing personal responsibility [24].

Wachinger points out that disaster experience includes direct experience (such as personally experiencing a disaster event) and indirect experience (such as acquiring disaster knowledge through media and education) [10]. Direct experience positively affects perceived risk. For example, individuals who experience floods, hurricanes, or landslides are more inclined to believe that such disasters will occur nearby [[26], [27], [28]]. Indirect experience can also significantly improve individuals' perceived risk by strengthening their memory and imagination for potential natural hazards [10]. Hypothesis 4 (H4) therefore posits that “Disaster experience (DE) directly influences citizens’ perceived risk (PR).”

Previous disaster experience is also directly related to perceived coping ability [29]. Ogunbode et al. and Seebauer et al. find that the flood experience and disaster knowledge can significantly improve an individual's coping ability [30,31]. Therefore, Hypothesis 5 (H5) posits that “Disaster experience (DE) directly influences citizens' perceived coping ability (PCA).”

Hypothesis 6 (H6) posits that “Disaster experience (DE) directly influences citizens' perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR).” Residents who experience storm damage may feel more responsible for taking precautions [32]. Participation in emergency activities, such as public training programs, can also enhance families’ sense of responsibility.

Simon notes that humans tend to build simple cognitive models, leading to heuristic biases as a result of these simplifications [33]. Grothmanna and Patt find that heuristic biases, such as optimism bias and availability bias, affect individual risk perception and ability assessment [34]. Optimism bias is the tendency for individuals to perceive themselves as being at lower risk compared to others [35]. Availability bias describes how people assess risk based on what they remember. On average, Grothmanna and Patt say that availability bias causes people to underestimate the perceived risk and perceived coping ability [34]. Loss aversion is the tendency to value losses more than gains. For example, the public may choose to ignore risks or underestimate coping abilities because the perceived benefits of not evacuating seem to outweigh the potential disaster impact [10]. Some studies also prove a relationship between the responsibility attribution framework and heuristic bias. For example, Wong et al. and Sharma et al. note that when people are exposed to extensive information about disasters (especially news articles that provide explanations about responsibility attribution), they are more inclined to rely on psychological shortcuts and heuristics to process the information. This can lead people to cluster together to assign responsibility [36,37]. Therefore, Hypothesis 7 (H7) posits that “Heuristic bias (HB) directly influences citizens' perceived risk (PR).” Hypothesis 8 (H8) posits that “Heuristic bias (HB) directly influences citizens' perceived coping ability (PCA).” Hypothesis 9 (H9) posits that “Heuristic bias (HB) directly influences citizens’ perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR).”

Milne et al. report that perceived risk is positively related to non-protective behaviors when reviewing PMT studies of disaster behaviors [21]. Other studies reveal that people with low coping abilities are more inclined to adopt non-protective behaviors [38]. Self-responsibility and stakeholder responsibility are also important to explain non-protective behaviors [39,40]. Andráško finds that although people feel responsible, they still avoid evacuation to protect their families and property [41]. Therefore, Hypothesis 10 (H10) posits that “Perceived risk (PR)directly influences non-protective behavior (NPB).” Hypothesis 11 (H11) posits that “Perceived coping ability (PCA) directly influences non-protective behavior (NPB).” Hypothesis 12 (H12) posits that “Perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR) directly influences non-protective behavior (NPB).”

Perceived risk and perceived coping ability have positive effects on protective behaviors [42,43]. When people perceive a high probability of disasters such as earthquakes or fires and believe they have high coping ability, they are more inclined to engage in protective behaviors [[44], [45], [46], [47]]. Arlikatti et al. and Lindell et al. find that residents believe they are primarily responsible for disaster mitigation, followed by the government and authorities [48,49]. Therefore, Hypothesis 13 (H13) posits that “Perceived risk (PR)directly influences protective behavior (PB).” Hypothesis 14 (H14) posits that “Perceived coping ability (PCA) directly influences protective behavior (PB).” Hypothesis 15 (H15) posits that “Perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR) directly influences protective behavior (PB).”

We also formulate Hypothesis 16 (H16) based on literature showing that non-protective behaviors, such as denial, status quo maintenance, and delay, inhibit the protective behaviors [50]. Hypothesis 16 (H16) thus declares that non-protective behavior (NPB) directly influences protective behavior (PB).

According to the empirical results, Faruk et al. and Westcott et al. find that farmers are less likely to adopt protective behaviors if they are affected by non-protective behaviors, which play an intermediary role between perceived risk, perceived coping ability, perceived attribution of responsibility, and protective behaviors [51,52]. Therefore, Hypothesis 17 (H17) posits that “Non-protective behavior (NPB) plays an intermediary role between perceived risk and protective behavior (PB).” Hypothesis 18 (H18) posits that “Non-protective behavior (NPB) plays an intermediary role between perceived coping ability and protective behavior (PB).” Hypothesis 19 (H19) posits that “Non-protective behavior (NPB) plays an intermediary role between perceived attribution of responsibility and protective behavior (PB).”

Methodology

Data collection

The study investigates the citizens who experience rainstorm floods in Zhengzhou and Xinxiang of Henan Province on July 20, 2021; Mianyang and Ya 'an of Sichuan Province on July 11, 2022; and Wanzhou and Wuxi of Chongqing on July 4, 2023. These severe rainstorm flood events are relatively typical cases in China. The pre-survey was conducted for more than half a month from February 1, 2024, to February 17, 2024, and a total of 101 questionnaires were obtained, of which 94 were valid. These people are not included in the formal survey. SPSS29.0 statistical software is used to test the reliability and validity of the pre-survey results. Cronbach's α of all scales is greater than 0.7, which reaches a good level.

The formal questionnaire was distributed between February 25, 2024, and April 30, 2024. According to China's Ministry of Emergency Management, a total of 15.423 million people have been affected in those areas. The sample size is determined by Scheaffer's equation: n = N/[(N – 1)δ2 + 1], and δ is generally set to 0.05 [53]. Therefore, the theoretical sample size is at least 318 people. Due to the bias of the survey and the existence of invalid questionnaires, the sample size is increased as much as possible to improve the reliability of the results. The researcher visits disaster areas such as Wanzhou, Zhengzhou, and other regions, to distribute questionnaires with the help of local friends, relatives, and classmates. Additionally, questionnaire diffusion is done through WeChat, QQ, Weibo, and other multi-channel platforms. Considering the respondents' preferences and the current high frequency of mobile phones, all the respondents answer the questions through China's professional questionnaire collection platform (Wenjuanxing). For some older and less educated citizens, face-to-face guidance and online communication are used to ensure the effectiveness of questionnaire responses. To ensure the quality of the questionnaire, trap questions are included. Verbal consent is obtained from each respondent before they answer the questions. 1102 questionnaires were obtained, and 915 were valid after excluding those with identical answers, excessively short response times, and incorrect answers to trap questions. This resulted in an effective rate of 83.03 %.

Questionnaire design

We develop a measurement scale based on the maturity scale, pre-investigation, and expert consultation. The questionnaire contains 78 items and 8 subscales, and also includes five personal information questions. The tool uses a five-point Likert scale (5 = Strong agreement, 1 = strongly disagree). Confirmatory factor analysis and higher-order model results

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is a key method for testing the validity of the scale. In CFA, a scale demonstrates convergent validity when each item shows statistical significance for the parameter estimates of its latent variables. This study primarily utilizes AMOS26.0 to perform CFA. The conceptual framework (Fig. 1) presents high-order constructs (HOCs), with standardized metrics used to measure these HOCs to simplify the model. Both first-order and second-order CFAs are addressed respectively, and the target coefficient (T) of each is compared. According to Marsh et al. and Lai et al., a T value (T = χ2 of first-order CFA/χ2 of second-order CFA) close to 1 indicates that the second-order factors model can effectively simplify the original model [69,70]. The second-order CFA of all scales meets the acceptable level, with the T value being approximately equal to 1. Structural equation model

SEM is a convenient technique for testing theoretical models. It enables the visualization of both direct and indirect relationships among all latent variables, thus supporting the testing of research hypotheses [72]. In this study, AMOS26.0 is applied to construct the SEM to verify the driving factors of citizens’ protective behaviors during rainstorm floods. A key step in constructing the SEM in this study involves addressing higher-order structures. In the first stage, a diagram is created that includes the low-order structure and its metrics (IC, ID, IO, DIRE, LEAE, OB, AB, LA, PP, PS, SE, RE, RC, ER, SR, MTSQ, DB, PROR, EMOR, INFS), with these metrics being measured by the corresponding questionnaire items for each scale. In the second stage, latent variables (EI, DE, HB, PR, PCA, PAR, NPB, PB) are incorporated into the model to replace the lower-order structure and its metrics. By calculating the path coefficients of the model, both direct and indirect effects among the variables can be obtained.

In contrast, if there is a low-risk perception (perhaps due to psychosocial processes that normalize risk), people may become accustomed to it and refrain from taking action. They may feel powerless, especially when finances and resources are insufficient, leading to a sense of indifference.

Therefore, H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, and H6 are confirmed. Additionally, the heuristic bias (HB) is negatively associated with the perceived coping ability (PCA) (β = −0.309, p < 0.001), confirming H8. However, H7 and H9 are not supported, as heuristic bias (HB) does not influence perceived risk (PR) and perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR). Perceived coping ability (PCA) and perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR) show a negative effect on non-protective behavior (NPB) (β = −0.500 and −0.177, respectively, p < 0.001 and 0.01), which confirms H11 and H12. However, there is no evidence that perceived risk (PR) has an effect on non-protective behavior (NPB) (H10). Consistent with H13, H14, and H15, perceived risk (PR), perceived coping ability (PCA), and perceived attribution of responsibility (PAR) positively influence protective behavior (PB) (β = 0.231, 0.279, and 0.241, respectively, p < 0.001). Finally, non-protective behavior (NPB) negatively affects protective behavior (PB) (β = −0.176, p < 0.01), which confirms H16.

Discussion

The influence of information source and individual characteristics on cognitive process

SEM analysis shows that emergency information is positively correlated with perceived risk, perceived coping ability, and perceived attribution of responsibility. This aligns with the findings of Wood et al., Liu et al., and Rickard et al. [19,20,24]. Emergency information is closely linked to social media attention. When people frequently receive rainstorm flood warning levels, potential damaging consequences, or detailed coping strategies from various sources (information content, information density), or they observe others actively preparing (information observed), their perceived risk and coping ability will increase. In danger, people often imitate and learn from others to boost their sense of security. This social learning acts as a heuristic cue, providing a psychological shortcut for rapid decision-making [15]. Therefore, the theme and content of rainstorm flood warning information issued by government departments via social media should be timely, accurate, and frequent. Messaging that propagates heroism and collective positive responses can also have a social multiplier effect, significantly motivating citizen action. Additionally, social media often employs a plot frame to highlight the salience of specific problems, including what the issue involves and who is responsible for it. This allows citizens to unconsciously assign responsibility between themselves and others due to the heuristic information processing system. Thus, the release of emergency information should use a narrative framework that emphasizes responsibility attribution to enhance disaster awareness and foster a sense of responsibility among citizens.

The disaster experience positively influences perceived risk, perceived coping ability, and perceived attribution of responsibility. This is consistent with the PMT and PADM. Direct experience (personal experience of hazardous events) and indirect experience (disaster learning) can significantly improve subjective perceptions of rainstorm floods [10]. Disaster experience is closely related to "chronic" disaster risk emotion. Developing "chronic" emotional resilience through the dissemination of extensive response knowledge and responsibilities has become a new approach to disaster risk research [73]. This is important for strengthening community resilience.

The heuristic bias negatively affects perceived coping ability, this finding is similar to Grothmann and Patt's model of private proactive adaptation to climate change (MPPACC) [34]. During the survey, most of the citizens said that their region had long been free from severe rainstorm floods (availability bias). This led them to ignore the dangers of rainstorm floods, driven by unrealistic optimism. They tended to focus on the potential benefits and fear of losses, solving other outstanding personal problems first (loss aversion). Additionally, the lack of professional disaster training and resources contributed to their low perceived coping ability. However heuristic bias cannot drive perceived risk and perceived attribution of responsibility. This may be influenced by the low-cost mental framework [8], where individuals are more concerned about their ability and costs with participation in such measures, thereby neglecting their concerns and responsibilities regarding the risk environment. Therefore, the release of emergency information should include a range of messages, highlighting the rising number of affected people and adequate response resources provided by the government to raise risk perception and meet the social needs of specific groups [74].

The influence of the cognitive process on protective behavior

The result of SEM shows that perceived coping ability negatively affects non-protective behaviors. According to the precaution adoption process model (PAPM), self-efficacy and response efficacy play an important role in deciding whether or not to take action [75]. People who lack confidence in their coping ability tend to rely more on social support and flood control measures, often adopting a “wait and see” approach that delays taking action [76]. The perceived attribution of responsibility is also negatively correlated with non-protective behaviors. During the investigation, many respondents believed that disaster mitigation should primarily be the responsibility of the government rather than themselves. Those who lacked empathy and a sense of responsibility were more afraid of making decisions and preferred to remain detached as bystanders. The result does not support the influence of perceived risk on non-protective behaviors, which is inconsistent with those of Le Dang et al. and Talanow et al. [77,78]. It may be that some respondents are not in the core disaster area and lack social norms to guide them. The perceived risk may not be directly linked to non-protective behavior when disasters do not cause mass casualties nearby.

Perceived risk is a significant factor in protective behaviors. Individuals who perceive higher risk are more inclined to take protective measures against severe rainstorm floods. This tendency can be explained by psychological distance and temporal urgency [8]. When citizens perceive a threat as imminent, they are more likely to respond positively due to internal pressure. Additionally, some respondents noted their awareness of the threat was heightened by observing widespread action among others. Seebauer and Babcicky point out that social norms indirectly influence protective behaviors by shaping risk perception. Perceived coping ability also affects protective behaviors [31]. Individuals' sense of their capacity to manage disasters is crucial for behavior change [79]. This is because people fully understand the coping measures sent by the government and are involved in a participatory exercise. Moreover, perceived attribution of responsibility positively influences protective behaviors. This is consistent with those of Ozdemir et al. and Terpstra et al. [80,81]. Those who feel personal and public stakeholders’ responsibilities to mitigate risk are more likely to follow emergency instructions. The possible reason is that the perceived attribution of responsibility is associated with inherent positive open thinking (AOT), which encourages active engagement in thinking, seeking information, and making decisions. Studies show that individuals who are more open-minded and diligent in their thinking are more responsible for addressing climate change challenges [82]. Additionally, trust in the government is associated with greater engagement in protective behaviors [83]. Therefore, when releasing emergency information, it is crucial not only to highlight the urgency of the disaster but also to pay attention to the content of improving personal confidence and responsibility. It is also important to tailor early warning information to the specific needs of individuals in "high-risk" versus "low-risk" disaster areas.

The mediating role of non-protective behavior

This study shows that non-protective behavior negatively affects protective behavior. Non-protective behavior plays a partial mediating role between perceived coping ability, perceived attribution of responsibility, and protective behavior. It is similar to those of Zobeidi et al., Grothmann et al., and Faruk et al. [23,34,51]. Non-protective behavior pathway may occur in the incipient stage of a disaster event: (1) citizens fail to understand the consequences of the rainfall reported in the warning messages, and they do not know what to do; (2) they maintain a false sense of security and overestimate coping abilities; (3) they shift personal responsibility to others to protect their interests. Once affected by external social norms and with response costs compensated by the government, they may begin to prepare and implement protective behavior. However, if they recognize a significant threat to their life and property, the perceived risk will directly lead to protective behavior, rather than being mediated by non-protective behavior. Therefore, the risk communication departments should focus on the factors that incentivize protective behavior rather than those that influence non-protective behavior. Instead, other studies confirm that the non-protective behavior route and protective behavior route are independent of each other [63]. The mediating role of non-protective behavior is controversial and requires further research for confirmation.

Conclusions

This study finds that (1) Emergency information and disaster experience positively drive perceived risk, perceived coping ability, and perceived attribution of responsibility. While heuristic bias is negatively correlated with perceived coping ability. (2) Perceived coping ability and perceived attribution of responsibility directly influence both protective and non-protective behaviors. Perceived risk positively affects protective behaviors but is not associated with non-protective behaviors. (3) Non-protective behavior plays an intermediary role between perceived coping ability, perceived attribution of responsibility, and protective behavior.

Based on the findings, this paper provides more insights into the practical implications for disaster risk management and emergency information release policies. In general, effective emergency information release should be connected with personal situational awareness. Situational awareness is a deep understanding and self-adaptation of a complex environment [84]. This concept is closely related to personal risk perception, attention, ability prediction, responsibility, and self-enlightenment. To improve communication, the following suggestions are proposed, which involve care, autonomy, and support, leading to greater compliance and continuity in protective behavior. (1) Improving comprehensibility and effectiveness of emergency information. Rainstorm flood emergency information should pay attention to the comprehensibility, acceptability, and simplicity of text and video transmissions. Changing the narrative framework and emphasizing risk issues can significantly enhance risk perception. Repeatedly explaining the rainstorm flood risk levels, causes, serious consequences, situation prediction, and prevention suggestions through both interpersonal and media channels helps citizens fully understand disaster dynamics. Additionally, citizens always observe others’ behavior and conform to social norms, highlighting the collective efficacy of combating floods and the choice of the majority can stimulate participation motivation [85]. (2) Enhancing the dissemination of disaster knowledge and experience. Disaster knowledge and experience are reflected in social capital and historical memory, which show the way of knowledge creation ability. When government departments release disaster response strategies, the traditional cultural memory of disasters (such as stories, songs, folklore, proverbs, cultural beliefs, etc.) should be used to foster social cohesion [86]. This collective memory of disasters has accumulated rich knowledge and experience in disaster response, providing a window of opportunity for emergency education. This approach not only enhances the emotional experience of risk (which is associated with high-risk alertness and psychological resilience) but also strengthens individuals' response belief and sense of responsibility. (3) Addressing cognitive biases. Public views on disaster and risk are socially constructed, and they often ignore certain risks due to cognitive bias and convenience of choice. The release of emergency information should align with these cognitive biases through nudging measures. Find a balance between intuitive and rational thinking systems. For example, by frequently publicizing that rainstorm floods are becoming more severe and extreme, individuals can better understand and remember the risk. When the next disaster occurs, individuals can use "availability bias" to respond to disasters. "Loss aversion" is related to inertia, where people may avoid uncertain options to prevent potential losses. Recognizing "loss aversion" and "choice blindness" suggests that disaster response strategies will attract more attention if it is designed as a framework of "default" and "active" choices.

In this study, a multi-dimensional characteristic model of driving rainstorm flood protective behavior is constructed to thoroughly understand the psychodynamic mechanisms and behavioral orientations of citizens during information processing. This model provides a useful reference for the government departments in releasing effective warning information. However, this study has some limitations and room for improvement. (1) Our survey is conducted several years after rainstorm flood events. Individual views on disaster response may change over time, which means the data collected through questionnaires may not accurately reflect the real-time mindsets of respondents. Future studies should aim to collect data closer to the time of the disaster to better capture immediate behavioral responses. (2) The value dimensions of individual decision-making may vary due to regional and cultural contexts. For instance, citizens in high-risk and low-risk areas have different perceptions of disaster urgency. People living in regions with a rich disaster culture may exhibit greater resilience. These factors lead individuals to have varying disaster experiences, knowledge, and cognitive biases, which in turn affect their psychological perception. Future research should incorporate geographic heterogeneity and sociocultural influences to develop more localized risk communication strategies. (3) The use of SEM in this study also has some shortcomings. The SEM can only verify the simple linear causal relationships, focusing on the overall effects, while ignoring the nonlinear relationships and interaction effects between variables. In the future, machine learning algorithms will be used to discover nonlinear causality that cannot be detected by traditional methods. This approach will help identify the most important factors affecting protective behavior and analyze the interaction and individual effects among variables.
