Seeding and feeding: nutrition and birth-associated exposures shape gut microbiome assembly in breastfed infants
Authors: Lisa F Stinson, Isabella Norrish, Faith Mhembere, Ali Sadiq Cheema, Christopher A Mullally, Matthew S Payne, Donna T Geddes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGut microbiome establishment in early life is influenced by maternal, infant, and environmental factors, with disruptions linked to later disease risk. Although infant diet is a major determinant of microbial composition, longitudinal data in breastfed infants, particularly in the context of birth interventions, remain limited. We profiled 698 stool samples from 84 predominantly or exclusively breastfed infants in the BLOSOM cohort, spanning 10 time points from 1 week to 2 years of age using full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing and targeted qPCR. After an initial volatile period, microbiome composition and diversity stabilized between months 2 and 5. Introduction of solid foods then triggered a marked ecological shift, with significant increases in diversity (p < 1 × 10−11) and broad compositional restructuring. In contrast, weaning had minimal impact on overall microbiota structure but was associated with lower abundances of several Bifidobacterium species, highlighting the sustained bifidogenic effect of human milk. Cesarean delivery was associated with transient reductions in Bacteroides abundance and prevalence, but did not affect Bifidobacterium, likely due to the bifidogenic effects of human milk. Reductions in Bacteroides, however, were not reflected in quantitative analyses, emphasizing the importance of absolute abundance measures. Our results offer novel insight into gut microbiome development under optimal feeding conditions, suggesting that breastfeeding may buffer early-life microbiome perturbations, while diet transitions exert major and lasting effects.KEYWORDS: Infant gut microbiome, breastfeeding, delivery mode, intrapartum antibiotics, infant nutrition

Introduction

The infant gut microbiota play a foundational role in immunological and metabolic programming,1–3 with disruptions during this critical window linked to later-life conditions including allergy, inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, diabetes, and neurodevelopmental disorders.4–10 It is therefore critical to understand composition and variance in the infant gut microbiome to better understand early host-microbe dynamics.

Gut microbiome assembly is governed by ecological principles, with early colonizers influencing the success of subsequent taxa, a process known as the priority effect.11 In this manner, early microbial exposures can shape the trajectory of gut microbiome development into adulthood. Infant gut colonization occurs in a stepwise process,12–18 contingent on both stochastic and deterministic events, including maternal, infant, and environmental exposures.12,19–25 These early influences can have long-lasting consequences for the consortia of gut microbes.26

Among these exposures, mode of delivery has been one of the most extensively studied. Cesarean section delivery is consistently associated with altered gut microbiota composition, typically characterized by reduced or delayed colonization with Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides. 18,27–31 These compositional differences may persist for several months, generally resolving around six months of age, when solid food introduction initiates a shift toward a more adult-like microbiome.32 While initial hypotheses pointed to the absence of maternal vaginal and fecal microbes during CS delivery, accumulating evidence implicates intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) – routinely administered during CS and some vaginal births – as a key contributor to microbial disruption.21,22,30,31,33 Despite the increasing global prevalence of cesarean delivery and the near-universal use of prophylactic antibiotics in surgical births, the interplay between delivery mode and antibiotic exposure remains poorly resolved in longitudinal contexts.

Infant diet is a critical modifier of these early microbial patterns. Breastfeeding exerts profound effects on gut microbiome composition12 due to provision of human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) and other bioactive components that selectively promote growth of Bifidobacterium. HMOs have been shown to modulate priority effects,34 and the influence of breastfeeding can persist well beyond infancy.35 Importantly, previous reports suggest that breastfeeding may ameliorate the effects of delivery mode.22,36–38

The Breastfeeding Longitudinal Observational Study of Mothers and kids (BLOSOM) cohort provides a unique window into gut microbiome development in the context of sustained human milk exposure. This cohort followed 84 exclusively or predominantly breastfeeding mother-infant pairs from birth to two years of age, collecting infant stool samples at 10 timepoints, enabling high-resolution longitudinal profiling from the neonatal period through to toddlerhood. This cohort offers a rare opportunity to examine variations in gut microbiome assembly based on delivery exposures within a highly breastfed population.

Despite a growing body of literature on early-life microbiome development, key questions remain unresolved: How robust are delivery mode effects in the context of sustained breastfeeding? To what extent does the introduction of solids, in the context of continued breastfeeding, alter gut ecology? And how consistent is colonization with beneficial taxa like Bifidobacterium among breastfed infants? Our study addresses these gaps using high-resolution longitudinal sampling, absolute abundance measures via qPCR, and personalized dietary metadata. By isolating the effects of delivery exposures and diet within a uniquely homogeneous breastfeeding cohort, we provide new insight into the interplay between birth mode, intrapartum antibiotic exposure, feeding practices, and microbial succession during this critical developmental window.

Materials and methods

BLOSOM cohort

This study utilized data and samples from the BLOSOM cohort (n = 84). Mothers with no major health conditions and singleton pregnancies who went on to deliver healthy, full-term infants were recruited during their third trimester of pregnancy from Perth, Australia. Recruitment occurred over 2018–2019. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at The University of Western Australia (RA/4/20/4023) and all participants provided written informed consent.

Metadata collection

Participants completed a background questionnaire at recruitment and a follow-up survey on birth and early feeding in the first week postpartum. At each sample collection, mothers reported infant feeding practices and health status.

Stool sample collection

Infant stool samples (n = 698) were collected within the first week of life (days 1–6) and at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 12, and 24 months of age. Stool samples were collected in the home by parents using Becton Dickinson E-swabs. Stool swabs were taken from the diaper within 1 hour of a bowel movement and stored in the participant’s home refrigerator for up to 18 hours before being transported to the laboratory on ice. Upon arrival, swabs were vortexed for 10 seconds to elute fecal matter into the Liquid Amies collection media. The swab media was then aliquoted and stored at − 80°C until further analysis.

Microbiome analysis

Swab media was centrifuged at 40,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was removed, and total DNA was extracted from the cell pellet using the QIAGEN MagAttract Microbial DNA kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The full length 16S rRNA gene was amplified using a two-step barcoding PCR with the primer pair 27F/1492 R, as previously described.39 Samples were randomized across the extraction and PCR plates to minimize batch effects. Barcoded amplicons were multiplexed and purified using Macherey Nagel NucleoMag beads prior to ligation of SMRT bell adaptors and sequencing on a PacBio Sequel II (performed at the Australian Genome Research Facility; AGRF).

Demultiplexed sequence data was processed using mothur version 1.48.0.40 Sequences that fell outside of the biological length of the 16S rRNA gene (1336–1743 bp) and those containing homopolymers of > 9 bases were removed. Alignment was performed using the SILVA reference alignment database (v138).41 Chimeric sequences were identified using VSEARCH42 and removed. OTU clustering was performed with a similarity cutoff of 0.03. Taxonomic identification of OTUs was performed using BLAST43 with a cutoff of > 98% sequence identity and 99% sequence coverage. Taxonomic assignments for OTUs identified in negative extraction and negative PCR controls are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Quantitative PCR

Given that 16S rRNA gene sequence data are inherently compositional in nature, we sought to measure absolute abundances of the important infant gut genera Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides using quantitative PCR (qPCR). Pure stocks of Bifidobacterium breve strain JCP7499 and Bacteroides fragilis strain CL07T12C05 were grown anaerobically in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (supplemented with cysteine and hemin for Bacteroides) at 37°C for 48 hours. Cultures were then streaked onto BHI agar and grown anaerobically at 37°C for 48 hours. DNA was extracted from single colonies using the QIAGEN MagAttract Microbial DNA kit as per manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using the primers and probes described in Table 1. Reactions consisted of 1X QuantaBio PerfeCTa qPCR ToughMix, 0.3 µM each of the forward and reverse primers, 0.15 µM of probe, 8.5 µL of nuclease-free water, and 5 µL of template (or nuclease-free water for negative controls) in a final reaction volume of 20 µL. For both reactions, PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 5 minutes at 95°C, followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 45 seconds (data acquiring). All reactions were performed on the QuantStudio™ 6, and data were analyzed using QuantStudio Real-Time PCR Software v1.6.1 (Life Technologies). Standard curves were constructed with purified DNA using tenfold serial dilutions ranging from 1 to 10−6 ng/µL. Given that qPCR data was not available for all samples, all comparisons of 16S and qPCR data were only performed where both metrics were available (n = 579). The following reagents were obtained through BEI Resources, NIAID, NIH as part of the Human Microbiome Project: Bacteroides fragilis, Strain CL07T12C05, HM-710 and Bifidobacterium breve, Strain JCP7499, HM-1120.

Statistical analyses

All microbiome analyses were performed on subsampled data. Subsampling was performed to 1026 reads, resulting in an average coverage of 91.4%. This excluded 50 low-yield samples, leaving 648 samples for analysis. Details of the participants included in the analysis after subsampling are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Reporting of microbiome methods and analyses follows the STORMS checklist for microbiome studies.46 The checklist is included as Supplementary Table S3.

Unless otherwise stated, all compositional analyses were performed at the OTU level. OTUs which made up an average relative abundance of > 0.5% were included in the analysis (26 OTUs). Differential abundance analyses were performed on center log ratio (CLR) transformed data47. Alpha diversity was measured using OTU richness and Shannon diversity. Beta diversity was measured using Aitchison distance. Holms correction for multiple comparison was applied to all statistical models, and adjusted p values are reported throughout the text unless otherwise stated.

All analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1). To identify factors associated with gut microbiome composition, we first conducted exploratory modeling using linear mixed-effects models. Shannon diversity, richness, and CLR-transformed abundances of the top 26 OTUs ( > 0.5% mean relative abundance) were modeled using each maternal, infant, or environmental variable as a fixed effect, with infant ID included as a random effect to account for repeated measures. Infant age was included as an interaction term. Candidate covariates included: infant age, delivery mode (2-category: vaginal vs cesarean), intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP), maternal age at delivery, maternal pre-pregnancy BMI category, maternal allergy status, presence of furry pets, older siblings, infant sex, and birth season. Postnatal antibiotic exposure was not included in the modeling. While some infants received antibiotics after the neonatal period for various medical indications, these exposures were heterogeneous and incompletely recorded. Therefore, our analyses focused solely on birth-associated antibiotic exposure (IAP), which was systematically recorded and consistently defined across the cohort.

Only delivery mode, IAP, and infant age were significantly associated with microbiome outcomes (adjusted p < 0.1), and these variables were retained for subsequent multivariate longitudinal models. Other covariates were excluded from final models, as they did not meet the significance threshold and would not have contributed meaningfully to adjustment.

To model longitudinal impacts of perinatal exposures, delivery mode was reclassified into three categories: vaginal without IAP, vaginal with IAP, and cesarean section. To examine the longitudinal impact of delivery exposures, linear mixed-effects models were fitted to OTU-level abundances, genus-level abundances (> 0.1% mean relative abundance), Shannon diversity, richness, and microbiome-for-age Z (MAZ) scores. Delivery mode, infant age, and their interaction were included as fixed effects, with infant ID as a random intercept. Absolute abundances of Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium (from qPCR data) were similarly modeled using delivery mode and age. All model outputs are reported in Supplementary Tables S4–S6.

Prevalence of Bacteroides was analyzed using Firth’s penalized logistic regression, with delivery mode and infant age as predictors. Models were run across the full time series and separately at each timepoint.

To assess community-level differences, beta diversity was evaluated using PERMANOVA (adonis2) on Aitchison distances derived from CLR-transformed OTU data. Infant ID was used for permutation stratification and 999 permutations were used. Models included delivery mode and infant age as predictors, with pairwise comparisons performed where relevant. NMDS was used to visualize community structure over time.

Microbial volatility was estimated by calculating Aitchison distances between consecutive timepoints within each infant (e.g., from 1 week to 1 month). The impact of delivery mode and IAP on volatility was tested using Kruskal – Wallis followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction.

To model gut microbiota maturation over time, we trained a random forest regression model on 60% of the cohort’s samples using CLR – transformed OTU abundance data to predict infant chronological age in days. The remaining 40% of samples were used to assess model performance (R2 and root mean squared error). MAZ scores were then calculated for each sample as standardized residuals (z-scores) of the predicted versus actual age, providing an individualized measure of microbiota maturity relative to peers. Positive MAZ scores indicate more mature microbiota than expected for age, while negative scores reflect delayed maturation. This approach follows previously described methods used to assess microbiota development in early life cohorts.12,48 The top 20 taxa contributing to age prediction are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

To evaluate the impact of solid food introduction, we identified three sample categories for each infant: (i) pre-solids – the final sample collected before the infant was reported to have started solid foods; (ii) post-solids 1 – the first sample collected after solids were introduced; and (iii) post-solids 2 – the next available sample after post-solids 1. This relative categorization was necessary due to the variation in solids introduction timing across infants (ranging from 4 to 9 months). By structuring the analysis around personalized dietary transitions rather than fixed age bins, we ensured that microbial changes could be meaningfully interpreted in the context of each infant’s feeding timeline. Analyses were limited to infants with complete sampling across all three stages (n = 40). All infants continued to breastfeed across the three sampling time points of solid food introduction, allowing us to isolate the introduction of solid food from the cessation of breastfeeding. Linear mixed-effects models were fitted to diversity measures, MAZ scores, and CLR-transformed OTU abundances, with solids category as the fixed effect and infant ID as a random intercept. Pre-solids served as the reference category. Beta diversity differences across solids stages were tested using PERMANOVA and pairwise comparisons.

To assess the effect of breastfeeding cessation, we analyzed data from the 24-month timepoint (n = 52). Infants were grouped as breastfed (n = 20) or weaned (n = 32). Univariate linear models were fitted to diversity metrics, MAZ scores, and CLR-transformed OTU abundances, with breastfeeding status as the predictor. A genus-level model was used to validate findings, particularly the observed decrease in Bifidobacterium among weaned children. Community-level differences were also evaluated using PERMANOVA.

Results

We analyzed full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing data from 648 stool samples collected from 84 Australian infants at 10 time points spanning the first two years of life (Figure 1). All infants were born at term, predominantly to Caucasian mothers (87%) (participant characteristics detailed in Supplementary Table S2). This cohort was intentionally designed to study breastfeeding mother-infant dyads under real-world conditions with minimal feeding variability. As such, all infants were exclusively or predominantly breastfed until the introduction of solid foods, with limited formula exposure (range: 20–180 mL/day; mean: 100 mL/day) (Figure 1). Breastfeeding was sustained in nearly all infants at 12 months (94%), and 38% (n = 20) continued to receive breast milk at 24 months.

We first examined the overall composition and developmental trajectory of the infant gut microbiome in this predominantly breastfed cohort (Figure 2A). As expected, Bifidobacterium dominated early microbiota profiles, with a mean relative abundance of 37.8%. Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis accounted for the largest proportion (23.7%). However, we observed considerable inter-individual variation: some infants harbored microbiomes almost entirely composed of Bifidobacterium, while others (10 samples from 7 infants) lacked this genus entirely (range: 0–95.9%; Figure 2B). Quantitative PCR confirmed the presence of Bifidobacterium DNA in all but two samples (range: 0–75.3 ng/µL), and correlated significantly with 16S rRNA gene sequencing data (Spearman’s ρ = 0.63, p < 2.2 × 10−16). Bifidobacterium abundance peaked at 4 months (mean relative abundance 46.6%; absolute abundance 3.7 ng/µL), then declined progressively to 24 months (Figure 3A,B). Notably, the reduction in relative abundance was steeper than in absolute abundance at 24 months, consistent with increasing microbial richness at this timepoint.

These genus-level trends appeared to be driven by B. infantis, whose trajectory paralleled the genus as a whole. This is unsurprising given specialization for HMO metabolism among B. infantis, 49–54 and the timing of solid food introduction from 5 months of age (Figure 1). Bifidobacterium breve, another HMO-adapted species, followed a similar pattern. In contrast, species more typical of the adult microbiota (Bifidobacterium catenulatum, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, and Bifidobacterium faecale) increased in toddlerhood, reflecting adaptation to dietary diversification and the emergence of plant polysaccharides and host-derived glycans as dominant substrates,55–57 (Figures 3C and 4C).

Microbiome composition also varied dynamically over time. Infant age significantly explained overall community structure (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.213, p = 0.001; Figure 4A), and we identified three distinct developmental phases (Figure 4B). The initial establishment phase (1 week to 2 months) was marked by high compositional volatility, with significant shifts in beta diversity (1w vs. 1 m: R2 = 0.012, p = 0.002; 1 m vs. 2 m: R2 = 0.008, p = 0.002). This was followed by a stable phase from 2 to 5 months, during which successive samples did not differ significantly. After 5 months, a transition phase began, characterized by renewed volatility in community structure (5 m vs. 6 m: R2 = 0.009, p = 0.029, 6 m vs. 9 m: R2 = 0.020, p = 0.001; 9 m vs. 12 m: R2 = 0.035, p = 0.001; 12 m vs. 24 m: R2 = 0.223, p = 0.001). Notably, the largest shift in beta diversity occurred between 12 and 24 months (R2 = 0.223, p = 0.001), with 24-month samples forming a distinct cluster in ordination space (Figure 4A), reflecting a marked shift in community structure during the second year of life. Volatility, as assessed by pairwise Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, was highest between 12 and 24 months (Figure 4B), suggesting that toddlerhood represents a secondary phase of microbiota reorganization. This underscores the need for extended longitudinal follow-up to capture the full arc of microbiome maturation.

At the OTU level, 18 of the top 26 taxa demonstrated significant temporal variation across the study period (Figure 4C). Notably, three adult-typical Bifidobacterium OTUs (B. catenulatum, B. faecale, and B. pseudocatenulatum) showed sharp increases at 24 months (p = 0.001, p = 4.62 × 10−23, and p = 7.88 × 10−11, respectively). Escherichia coli, highly abundant during early life (mean relative abundance 12.1% over the first 9 months), declined significantly at 12 and 24 months (p = 0.020 and p = 1.37 × 10−7, respectively). In contrast, late-emerging genera associated with adult-like microbiota, including Akkermansia muciniphila, Blautia wexlerae, Dialister invisus, Ruminococcus gnavus, Ruminococcus torques, and Thomasclavelia ramosa, increased substantially during late infancy and toddlerhood.

Interestingly, two species common in human milk, Streptococcus salivarius and Staphylococcus lugdunensis, were initially abundant in week-1 samples (9.7% and 6.8%, respectively), but declined rapidly thereafter. This pattern supports the hypothesis that the neonatal gut is initially colonized by aerotolerant and facultatively anaerobic taxa, which create a permissive environment for the establishment of strict anaerobes over time.58–60

The infant gut microbiome is shaped by delivery mode and intrapartum antibiotic exposure

In our predominantly breastfed cohort, both cesarean section (CS) delivery and vaginal delivery with intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (VIAP) were associated with modest but statistically significant shifts in gut microbiota composition during early life (CS: R2 = 0.011, p = 0.001; VIAP: R2 = 0.008, p = 0.001). These differences were largely confined to the first two months postpartum (Figure 5).

At the species level, CS was associated with reduced abundance of Bacteroides fragilis (p = 0.045), while VIAP was linked to elevated levels of Klebsiella pneumoniae (p = 0.002) and Phocaeicola (Bacteroides) dorei (p = 0.021) (Figure 6). Both CS- and VIAP-exposed infants also had increased abundance of an OTU matching 99.73% identity to a previously reported Raoultella ornithinolytica strain from preterm infant stool (PMID: 6696637); however, this OTU also aligned closely with multiple Klebsiella species (99.59% identity), suggesting potential misclassification.

Previous studies have consistently reported delayed or reduced colonization by Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides in CS-delivered infants27. Interestingly, in our OTU-level analysis, we observed significant delivery-associated differences in Bacteroides species, but not in Bifidobacterium species. Notably, most existing studies have assessed delivery mode effects only at the genus level. We therefore expanded our analysis by modeling the impact of delivery mode and VIAP on genus-level abundances (Supplementary Figure S2). Consistent with prior work, CS was associated with reduced Bacteroides abundance (p = 0.028). In contrast, the apparent reduction in Bifidobacterium abundance did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons (unadjusted p = 0.013; adjusted p = 0.337). The absence of a significant delivery mode effect on Bifidobacterium colonization likely reflects the bifidogenic influence of human milk and the high rates of exclusive breastfeeding within this cohort.

To further explore the timing of Bacteroides acquisition, we examined genus-level prevalence across timepoints. Bacteroides was significantly less prevalent in CS-delivered infants at 2, 5, 6, and 9 months (p = 0.024, 0.017, 0.043, and 0.003, respectively; Figure 7). While Bacteroides abundance was reduced in the CS group, an opposite trend was observed among VIAP-exposed infants (unadjusted p = 0.034; adjusted p = 0.922; Supplementary Figure S2), suggesting heterogeneous effects of antibiotic exposure depending on delivery mode.

We next validated these findings using quantitative PCR to measure absolute abundances of Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides DNA (Figure 8). No significant differences were detected in Bifidobacterium (CS p = 0.488; VIAP p = 0.341), nor in Bacteroides levels (CS p = 0.222; VIAP p = 0.374) based on delivery mode. These results underscore the importance of confirming compositional shifts observed via relative abundance data with quantitative methods.61–63

Finally, delivery mode had no detectable impact on microbiome maturity or developmental trajectory, as assessed by microbiome-for-age Z scores and volatility metrics (Supplementary Figures S3–S4), further highlighting the apparent resilience of the breastfed infant gut microbiota to perinatal delivery exposures.

Introduction of solid foods and cessation of breastfeeding are significant milestones in infant gut microbiome development

In this cohort, most infants (90%) had commenced solid foods by 6 months of age, with 23% starting by 5 months. To assess the impact of this dietary shift, we compared microbiota composition and diversity across three key stages: samples collected prior to solid food introduction (“pre-solids”), the first sample following reported solids intake (“post-solids 1”), and the subsequent sample (“post-solids 2”). All infants continued to breastfeed throughout this period. Thus, solid food introduction occurred exclusively in the context of ongoing breastfeeding.

Solid food introduction was associated with dramatic restructuring of the infant gut microbiota, particularly evident in post-solids 2 samples (Figure 9). Community composition in post-solids 2 samples progressed significantly from both pre-solids (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.049, p = 0.001) and post-solids 1 samples (R2 = 0.033, p = 0.001), reflecting the establishment of a new microbial state. These changes were accompanied by marked increases in richness (p = 1.68 × 10−13) and evenness (Shannon diversity p = 3.29 × 10−12), consistent with a broad ecological expansion. Interestingly, this microbial diversification was associated with a reduction in microbiome-for-age Z scores, highlighting a temporary divergence from age-typical profiles during this transitional period.

At the taxonomic level, solid food introduction drove broad compositional shifts: 11 of the 26 OTUs analyzed increased in abundance in post-solids 2 samples (Figure 9). Notably, these shifts were absent from post-solids 1 samples, with the exception of Bifidobacterium bifidum, which rose significantly in both post-solids 1 and post-solids 2 samples. These patterns likely reflect delayed and heterogeneous exposure to solids – given the variability in when solids were initiated within each age-defined sampling interval, and the often limited ingestion during early stages of introduction (i.e., “tasting” rather than sustained consumption). The post-solids 2 sample thus represents a more reliable indicator of sustained dietary change and its impact on the gut microbiome.

By 24 months, 61.5% of infants had ceased breastfeeding. While cessation of breastfeeding did not significantly impact gut alpha or beta diversity, it was associated with reduced abundance of three Bifidobacterium species (B. breve, p = 0.014; B. pseudocatenulatum, p = 0.026; B. bifidum, p = 0.049; Figure 10). Genus-level modeling confirmed higher Bifidobacterium levels among toddlers still receiving breastmilk (p = 0.006), underscoring the continued bifidogenic influence of human milk beyond infancy.

Elevated Bifidobacterium species among 24-month-old infants who had continued breastfeeding (purple) compared to those who had weaned (pink).

Discussion

Here, we characterize the dynamics of early-life gut microbiome establishment in a cohort of exclusively or predominantly breastfed children. This feeding pattern, which aligns with global public health recommendations, is rarely achieved in practice, providing a unique lens into microbial community development in breastfed infants. Our findings identify delivery mode, solid food introduction, and weaning as key inflection points in gut microbiome maturation.

In this breastfed cohort, Bifidobacterium was the dominant genus, consistent with its established role as a keystone early-life taxon with immunomodulatory and metabolic benefits.64,65 Infants initially harbored high levels of HMO-metabolizing Bifidobacterium species (B. infantis and B. breve), which were replaced by adult-typical species that are specialized for plant polysaccharide metabolism (B. catenulatum, B. pseudocatenulatum, and B. faecale) as infants transitioned to family foods. Despite the bifidogenic properties of human milk,66–69 we observed striking inter-individual variability in Bifidobacterium levels, with absolute abundances ranging from 0 to 75.3 ng/µL and relative abundances from 0 to 95.9%. Notably, no infant entirely lacked Bifidobacterium across the two-year sampling window, and most exhibited an increase from 1 month of age, followed by a gradual decline after 4 months. This variability was not explained by measured perinatal or environmental factors, including delivery mode, antibiotic exposure, birth season, or maternal BMI. Instead, it likely reflects differences in maternal microbial transmission. Prior studies have demonstrated vertical transfer of Bifidobacterium strains from mother to infant via both the gut and breast milk,70–74 suggesting that low maternal abundance may limit neonatal colonization. Further, breast milk composition is neither static nor uniform across individuals34,75–81 and may differentially support microbial growth. Future work should explore how maternal microbial and milk-derived factors jointly shape Bifidobacterium colonization and persistence in infancy.

The high prevalence (96%) and substantial relative abundance (mean 23.7%) of B. infantis in our Australian cohort is striking, particularly given reports of its near absence in many Western infant populations.82–84 Notably, our abundance levels among B. infantis-positive infants are similar to those reported by among US infants,83 who harbored B. infantis at a mean relative abundance of 21.4% where present, but with a cohort-wide prevalence of only 7%. This contrast suggests that the key difference in our cohort is not the intensity of colonization, but the frequency of acquisition, which may be reduced in the US cohort due to low exclusive breastfeeding rates. One potential explanation is Australia’s very high rate of breastfeeding initiation (96%)85 and the unusually long breastfeeding duration in our study population, both of which are known to support B. infantis colonization and persistence.65 These findings highlight the importance of population breastfeeding practices in shaping microbial transmission and underscore the need for population-specific benchmarks when evaluating microbiota development.

Our data contribute to a nuanced understanding of delivery-associated microbiota perturbations. Cesarean-delivered infants have consistently been shown to harbor reduced levels of Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides. 27,28,66,86,87 This is hypothesized to be due to lack of transmission and persistence of maternal gut microbes in cases of cesarean birth.88 Similarly, intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis has been repeatedly shown to disrupt early infant gut colonization, to a similar extent as cesarean delivery.21,22,27,31,33 However, this literature is complicated by lack of measurement of breastfeeding, which is known to be reduced in mothers who deliver via cesarean section,89–91 and which has previously been shown to modify the effects of delivery mode.22,36–38 Therefore, breastfeeding exclusivity must be taken into account in such analyses. Indeed, in our cohort of predominantly and exclusively breastfed infants, cesarean delivery was not associated with reductions in Bifidobacterium, suggesting that the bifidogenic effects of human milk may buffer early disruptions caused by delivery interventions. In support of this hypothesis, it has been reported that by mid-childhood (3–18 years) delivery mode no longer explains gut microbiota variation, whereas breastfeeding history continues to shape microbial composition.92 These findings suggest that breastfeeding exerts a durable influence on gut microbial ecology, mitigating the long-term impacts of early-life perturbations such as cesarean delivery.

Notably, our findings partially align with those of a large study of 606 European infants,93 which used targeted FISH and flow cytometry to assess absolute abundance. That study found that cesarean section and intrapartum antibiotics were associated with reduced Bacteroides colonization at six weeks of age, but had no measurable effect on Bifidobacterium abundance. This mirrors our observation of no Bifidobacterium differences by birth mode, even when assessed using quantitative PCR. However, in contrast to their finding of persistent Bacteroides depletion, our relative abundance-based signal for Bacteroides disappeared when evaluated using quantitative methods. Together, these findings underscore the importance of incorporating absolute abundance data when interpreting early-life microbiome perturbations, and suggest that the commonly reported depletion of keystone taxa following cesarean delivery may be less consistent than previously thought.

Our OTU-level analysis also revealed species-specific associations between perinatal exposures and Bacteroides taxa, suggesting that not all species respond uniformly to delivery interventions. Specifically, B. fragilis was depleted in cesarean-delivered infants, while P. (Bacteroides) dorei was elevated among those exposed to intrapartum antibiotics. These findings support growing evidence that Bacteroides species differ in their metabolic capabilities, niche preferences, and antibiotic susceptibilities, which may underpin their divergent colonization trajectories following birth interventions. In particular, P. dorei has previously been shown to resist penicillin-class antibiotics,94 potentially explaining its relative enrichment following intrapartum benzylpenicillin exposure in our cohort. These data highlight the importance of high-resolution taxonomic profiling when evaluating perinatal influences on the microbiota, as genus-level summaries may obscure species-specific patterns of ecological response.

While cesarean-delivered infants exhibited reduced relative abundance of Bacteroides, vaginally delivered, antibiotic-exposed infants showed a non-significant trend toward increased relative abundance. These opposing trends may reflect differences in antibiotic class exposure: in Australia, cesarean deliveries typically involve cephalosporins, whereas vaginal deliveries with intrapartum prophylaxis often involve benzylpenicillin, to which Bacteroides demonstrate greater resistance. Thus, benzylpenicillin may have indirectly enriched Bacteroides relative abundance by reducing the abundance of Gram-positive taxa. Conversely, Bifidobacterium is susceptible to both antibiotic classes, possibly explaining the differential responses of these two genera across birth modes in prior studies.31,95 Future work incorporating detailed antibiotic timing, dosing, and maternal microbiota data may better elucidate these relationships. In particular, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiling of maternal and infant strains across different delivery exposures should be included in future strain transmission studies.

Solid food introduction represented the most profound inflection point in microbiome maturation. Notably, microbiota restructuring – including significant shifts in community composition, increased alpha diversity, and expansion of multiple taxa – was not evident until the second sampling point post-solids introduction. This delayed response may stem from the qualitative nature of early feeding behavior, where infants may only sample or mouth foods with minimal ingestion. It may also reflect the heterogeneity in exact timing of dietary transition within each collection window. Our findings align with prior studies showing that dietary diversification drives expansion of fiber-fermenting and metabolically versatile taxa.15,96 Indeed, earlier work has demonstrated that the infant gut microbiota are strongly shaped by the composition of the solid foods offered in the first 18 months of life.97 This reinforces the concept that dietary substrates, rather than age per se, are primary drivers of gut microbial assembly.

While early infancy has long been the focus of gut microbiome research, our findings underscore the importance of the second year of life as a critical window of microbial reorganization. The largest compositional shift in our cohort occurred between 12 and 24 months, with marked changes in beta diversity and a distinct clustering of the 24-month samples. This late-phase volatility likely reflects the cumulative impact of dietary diversification, environmental exposures, and developmental changes in host physiology and immune function. Despite the presence of a complex, fiber-fermenting microbiota by 12 months, the microbiome remained highly dynamic, suggesting that maturation continues well into toddlerhood. These findings reinforce the value of extended longitudinal sampling.

Contrary to suggestions that breastfeeding cessation may be the dominant force in microbial maturation,66 we found that weaning at 24 months was associated only with reductions in three Bifidobacterium species, with no detectable impacts on overall diversity or community structure. These findings highlight the sustained bifidogenic effect of human milk well into toddlerhood, even after the establishment of a complex, solid food-adapted microbiota. Given that many infants in our cohort were still breastfed at 2 years, and that prolonged breastfeeding is recommended by the World Health Organization, there is a compelling need to extend microbiome studies into the third year of life and beyond.

Collectively, these findings provide novel insights into the microbial ecology of the breastfed infant gut, with three key contributions to the field. First, we show that exclusive or predominant breastfeeding buffers the effects of cesarean delivery and intrapartum antibiotics on Bifidobacterium colonization – a departure from earlier studies, likely due to underreporting or heterogeneity in feeding practices. Second, we demonstrate that the introduction of solids, not breastfeeding cessation, marks the most significant and lasting inflection point in gut microbiota maturation, with a delayed but robust ecological shift. Third, we reveal striking inter-individual variability in Bifidobacterium abundance even among breastfed infants, highlighting the importance of maternal transmission dynamics and the need for more granular, strain-level data. Together, these insights refine our understanding of early-life microbial assembly and help contextualize variation in infant microbiome trajectories across global populations and birth settings. A deeper understanding of these dynamics will support targeted strategies to promote healthy microbiome development and, by extension, long-term child health.
