Elicitation of protective immune responses against influenza a viruses in elderly ferrets by adjuvanted recombinant universal influenza hemagglutinin vaccines
Authors: Xiaojian Zhang, Hua Shi, Ted M. Ross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Influenza viruses remain a significant threat to both animals and public health. Each season influenza virus infection causes significant morbidity and mortality, especially in the elderly population. Annual vaccination boosts antibody titers against circulating influenza strains in elderly individuals, albeit the antibody responses are not as robust as those in younger adults. In addition, the elderly have a rapid decline in antibody titers over a 6-month period following an influenza virus immunization with antibody titers often returning to baseline at the beginning of each influenza season. Thus, there is a need for next-generation influenza vaccines that induce broadly and long-lasting protective immunity against influenza viruses in high-risk, elderly populations. In this study, Computationally Optimized Broadly Reactive Antigen (COBRA)-based H1 and H3 hemagglutinin (HA) vaccines were tested in combination with toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists as adjuvants in elderly ferrets with immune memory to historical influenza viruses to assess the breadth of protective antibody responses. These COBRA vaccines were mixed with either TRAC478, which is a combination of two TLR agonists, INI-2002 (a synthetic TLR4 agonist) and INI-4001 (a synthetic TLR7/8 agonist), or SAS, which is a mixture of INI-2002 and a semi-synthetic saponin. Elderly ferrets vaccinated with H1/H3 COBRA HA proteins plus TRAC478, or SAS had antibodies with hemagglutination-inhibition (HAI) activity against a panel of H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses that were significantly higher than animals vaccinated with unadjuvanted HA vaccine only. These elderly ferrets were protected following challenge with influenza virus with little to no detectable signs of disease or weight loss. In addition, there were reduced viral titers detected in nasal washes compared to elderly ferrets vaccinated with unadjuvanted HA proteins. Overall, both TRAC478 and SAS were effective adjuvants to enhance protective antibodies in elderly ferrets compared to animals that were vaccinated with only COBRA HA protein.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12979-025-00539-3.

Introduction

Influenza viruses cause respiratory disease with significant morbidity and mortality in all age group, but particularly in young children/infants and the elderly [1]. As people age, there are changes in immune system at all levels (i.e. tissue, cellular, intracellular, and molecular) that result in the loss of effectiveness of both adaptive and innate immune responses [2]. As a result, the elderly have difficulties mounting immune responses against new pathogens or vaccine antigens [2]. Currently, annual influenza virus vaccination remains the best way to maintain or boost the humoral antibody responses in people during annual epidemics [3]. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends the use of high-dose split inactivated virus or MF-59 adjuvanted influenza vaccines for people age 65 or older since these vaccines induce enhanced immune responses compared to the standard dose influenza vaccines [4–7]. MF-59 is an oil-in-water emulsion adjuvant has been approved for use in influenza vaccines since 1997 that elicits strong Th2-biased responses over Th1-biased responses [8, 9]. However, influenza-specific antibodies elicited by these influenza virus vaccinations in the elderly rapidly decline and return to a low and often unprotected levels [3]. Each season, the recommended strains are updated based on surveillance data to ensure the best match with circulating strains. And since the hemagglutinin (HA) protein is the only component that is standardized in current influenza vaccines, the majority of antibodies induced by these types of vaccines are directed against the HA molecule with little to no antibodies detected against the other surface glycoprotein, neuraminidase (NA) [10]. Elderly people are less likely to induce de novo B cells to new antigens in response to novel antigens due to immune exhaustion and immunosenescence [11–13]. The fast decline of humoral and the lack of cellular immune responses may contribute to the short duration of anti-influenza immunity induced by high-dose or MF-59 adjuvanted influenza vaccines [3, 8, 9]. Thus, alternative strategies (i.e. broadly reactive antigens and improved adjuvants) are needed to generate enhanced and long-lasting immune response against influenza viruses in the elderly population [3].

Both humoral and cellular immune responses play important roles in preventing and clearing pathogens during infection [14, 15]. Adjuvants, a major driver of a balanced immune response, are an essential part of vaccine formulations and play an important role in enhancing immune responses against vaccine antigens (i.e. subunit and peptide-based vaccines) [7]. And combining different types of adjuvants in the same formulation may maximize the adjuvant effects and elicit broader and more robust immune responses [7, 16–24]. In this study, two novel adjuvant combinations were used to enhance immune responses to influenza HA-based protein vaccines. TRAC478 is a squalene-based emulsion contains synthetic TLR agonists, INI-2002 (TLR4 agonist) and INI-4001 (TLR7/8 agonist) [25]. The squalene-based emulsions are safe and boost antibody titers following vaccination [8, 9, 26, 27]. TRAC478 contributes to robust humoral and enhanced cellular immune responses to subunit vaccines in mice [25]. The enhanced cellular immune response to vaccine component provided a chance to establish a long-lived immune response against pathogens in a population with weakened immune system. In addition to TRAC478, a second adjuvant combination was used in this study that incorporated an adjuvant system, known as SAS, which incorporates a synthetic INI-2002 (TLR4 agonist) and saponin. Each adjuvant was mixed with the well described broadly reactive HA immunogens designed using the Computationally Optimized Broadly Reactive Antigens (COBRA) methodology [28–30]. COBRA HA vaccines elicit broad neutralizing antibody responses and provide protection against lethal influenza virus infection in young adult animal models. However, their effectiveness in elderly has not been thoroughly explored. Ferrets, a widely used animal model in the research of pathogenicity and transmission of respiratory viruses (i.e. influenza and SARS-CoV-2 viruses), have an average life span ranging from 5 to 7 years [31–33]. Most of ferrets start to show geriatric diseases between the age of 3 and 4 years old, thus ferrets were considered to be geriatric at 3 years of age or older by veterinarians [33]. In this study, elderly ferrets between 56 and 74 months of age with pre-existing immunity to historical influenza viruses were vaccinated with COBRA HA recombinant proteins mixed with a TRAC478 or SAS adjuvants to elicit functional antibody responses against a broad panel of H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses. All vaccinated animals were challenged with A(H1N1) influenza virus to evaluate the protection provided by the vaccination.

Materials and methods

Viruses and cells

The stock of H1 and H3 subtype viruses were sourced from either the Influenza Reagents Resource (IRR), BEI Resources, or the CDC. The diluted stock viruses were inoculated in specific pathogen-free (SPF) 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs to propagate virus for further experiments. The harvested allantoic fluid was then aliquoted for single-use applications and stored at − 80 °C. To establish the pre-existing immunity to influenza, A/Singapore/06/1986 (SG/86, H1N1) and A/Panama/2007/1999 (PN/99, H3N2) viruses were used to infect ferrets. The following viruses were used in the Hemagglutination Inhibition (HAI) Assay. The H1 virus panel includes A/Victoria/2570/2019 (VC/19, H1N1) A/Guangdong/SWL1536/2019 (GD/19, H1N1), A/Brisbane/02/2018 (BR/18, H1N1), A/California/07/2009 (CA/09, H1N1). The H3 virus panel includes A/Darwin/9/2021(DA/21, H3N2) A/Tasmania/503/2020 (TS/20, H3N2), A/Hong Kong/2671/2019 (HK/19, H3N2), A/South Australia/34/2019 (SA/19, H3N2), A/Kansas/14/2017 (KS/17, H3N2), A/Singapore-IFNIMH-16-0019/2016 (SG/16, H3N2), A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 (HK/14, H3N2), A/Switzerland/9,715,293/2013 (SW/13, H3N2). Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Vaccine design and preparation

Full-length wild-type (WT) influenza A(H1N1) and A(H3N2) HA protein amino acid sequences from human influenza viruses were downloaded from the Global Initiative on Sharing Avian Influenza Data (GISAID) EpiFlu online database [34]. Sequences were organized by their date of collection and aligned using Geneious alignment (v11.1.5) to generate consensus sequences as previously described [29, 30]. Multiple rounds of alignment may be needed to generate a single final consensus.

The optimized coding sequences for COBRA HA proteins (Y2 and NG2) in pcDNA3.3 vectors were expressed in soluble proteins using a HEK-293T cell expression line, as described previously [30, 35, 36]. Proteins were extracted using HisTrapExcel columns with the AKTA Pure System (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden). Purified proteins were concentrated and diluted in phosphate-buffered saline + 0.1% w/v sodium azide (PBSA). Protein concentration was determined using Micro BCA Protein Assay Reagent kits (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA), and aliquots of each protein were stored at − 80 °C until used for vaccination. The purity of each protein sample was confirmed with sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). To formulate the vaccine, TRAC478 or SAS adjuvant (Inimmune, Missoula, MT, USA) was mixed in a 1:1 ratio (125 µL sterile 1× PBS with protein: 125 µL TRAC478 or SAS).

Ferrets experiment

Female Fitch ferrets (Mustela putorius furo) between 56 and 74 months of age were sourced from Triple F Farms (Gillett, PA, USA) after de-scenting and spaying. Ferrets were pair housed with free access to food, water, and enrichment. Ferrets were anesthetized with vaporized isoflurane before bleeds, vaccination, infection, nasal washes, and euthanasia. All animal procedures were performed following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, Animal Welfare Act, and Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories (AUP: A2020 11–016).

Ferrets were infected intranasally with a mixture of SG/86 and PN/99 influenza viruses (5 × 105 per virus/animal) to induce memory cells. Seroconversion in each animal was confirmed with the serum samples collected two weeks post-infection.

Eight weeks post pre-immune infection, ferrets were prime, and boost vaccinated at 4 weeks interval (Fig. 1). At least two weeks after each vaccination, blood was collected in BD Vacutainer SST tubes for serum separation. Purified serum was stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

At 4 weeks after final vaccination, ferrets were challenged intranasally with BR/18 H1N1 influenza virus at 1 × 108 PFU in a volume of 1 mL. Ferrets were monitored, daily for weight loss and disease signs for 14 days post-infection. Nasal wash samples were collected from each ferret on day 3 post-infection. All nasal wash samples were stored at − 80 °C until viral titration. Turbinate, trachea, and lung samples were harvested at day 5 post-infection. The left cranial (Upper) and caudal (Lower) lobes were sectioned into quarters, placed on dry ice, and stored at − 80 °C until viral titration.

Clinical signs with their scores were as follows: nasal discharge/sneezing/diarrhea (0.5; not used for humane endpoint calculation but used for graphical representation), lethargy (1), dyspnea (2), cyanosis (2), neurological signs (3), moribund (3), laterally recumbent (3), failure to respond to stimuli (3), weight loss of 20–25% (2), and weight loss of greater than 25% (3). When a cumulative clinical score of 3 was reached, the animal was humanely euthanized.

Hemagglutination-Inhibition (HAI) assay

The HAI assay was used to characterize the antibody response in ferret serum against HA with either 0.8% turkey red blood cells (TRBCs) or 0.75% guinea pig red blood cells (GPRBCs) (Lampire Biologicals, Pipersville, PA, USA), according to the World Health Organization (WHO) laboratory influenza surveillance manual [37]. Briefly, ferret serum samples were treated with receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) (Denka Seiken, Co., Japan), and then heat-inactivated before assay. After treatment, all serum samples were further diluted to 1:10 with addition of PBS. The RDE-treated serum samples were two-fold diluted with PBS in v-bottom microtiter plates. For A(H1N1) viruses, 25 µL of 8 hemagglutination units (HAU) of virus was mixed with 25 µL of serum serial dilutions. For A(H3N2) viruses, an equal volume of each A(H3N2) virus, adjusted to approximately 8 hemagglutination units (HAU) per 50 µL in the presence of 20 nM Oseltamivir carboxylate (for GPRBCs), was added to each well. The plates were covered and incubated at room temperature for 20–30 min, before red blood cells in PBS were added. Then plates were mixed by gentle agitation, and the red blood cells were allowed to settle at room temperature for 30 min (for TRBCs) or 1 h (for GPRBCs). The reciprocal dilution of the last well that contained non-agglutinated TRBCs or GPRBCs was reported as HAI titer.

Influenza viral plaque assay

Six-well plates were seeded with 1 × 106 MDCK cells per well one day prior to performing the plaque assay. When MDCK cells reached 90% confluency in each well, the plates were washed 2x with DMEM supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (DMEM + P/S) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and infected with 100 µL of each dilution of nasal wash samples. The plates were then incubated at room temperature for 1 h, shaking every 15 min to mix. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and cells were washed twice with fresh DMEM + P/S. Following the second wash, an overlay of 0.8% agarose in 1x MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and supplemented with 1 µg/mL of TPCK trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added into each well. Plates were then incubated at 37 °C + 5% CO2 for 48–72 h. At the end of incubation, the gel overlays were removed from each well, and the cells were fixed with 10% buffered formalin for 15 min and stained with 1% crystal violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 15 min at room temperature. The viral plaques were enumerated as the reciprocal of each dilution. The titers of viral shedding were calculated and presented as plaque forming units (PFU)/mL of nasal wash samples.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for elicited antibody quantification

The 96-well flat-bottom ELISA plates (Immulon 4HBX; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were coated with 100 µL of 1 µg/mL of wildtype HA protein in carbonate buffer (50 mM carbonate buffer [pH 9.5]) overnight at 4 °C. The next morning, the antigen coating solution was replaced with a solution of 1% BSA in PBS with 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST + BSA) to block any non-specific epitopes of the plates. After incubation at 37 °C for 90 min, the blocking buffer was removed and 3-fold serial dilutions of heat-inactivated serum were added to the plate with an initial dilution of 1:500. After incubation at 37 °C for 90 min, plates were washed 3x with PBST, and Goat Anti-Ferret IgG H&L HRP (horseradish peroxidase) (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA) was added at 1:4,000 in PBST + BSA. After incubation at 37 °C for 90 min, plates were washed 3x with PBST, and 2,29-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) substrate in McIlvain’s buffer (pH 5) was added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 10 min, the colorimetric reaction was stopped with the addition of 1% SDS in dH2O, and the absorbance was measured at 414 nm using a spectrophotometer (PowerWave XS; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Microneutralization (MN) assay

Neutralization of multiple viruses by ferret serum were determined in MDCK cells for influenza viruses following WHO protocol. Briefly, two-fold diluted heat-inactivated ferret serum were mixed with 100 TCID50 of each testing virus in a 96-well testing plate. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, MDCK cells were added to virus-sera mixture at 2 × 104/well. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 18 h, the medium was removed, and cells were washed with PBS once. The infected cells were fixed with cold 80% acetone and then washed with PBST (PBS with 0.3% tween 20). The presence of viral protein was determined by ELISA with monoclonal antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) specific to the nucleoprotein (NP) of influenza viruses. Neutralization titers were defined as the highest reciprocal serum dilution which result in at least 50% neutralization of virus infectivity.

Statistical analysis

Data is presented as absolute mean values ± standard deviation (SD). The one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to analyze the statistical differences between groups using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). A “p” value less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant (*, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001).

Results

Vaccination of elderly ferrets with HA vaccines in combination with adjuvants elicited robust protective antibody responses

A cohort of ferrets were aged to 56–74 months of age to investigate the antibody responses to influenza vaccination in the elderly. These elderly ferrets were infected with historical influenza viruses to induce a pre-immune influenza state. All animals seroconverted against SG/86 and PN/99 influenza viruses after pre-immune infection as determined by HAI assay (SFig. 1). Two weeks following the second vaccination, the elderly ferrets that were vaccinated with an H1 and H3 COBRA HA protein vaccine plus adjuvants had anti-BR/18 (H1) or anti-TS/20 (H3) HA IgG titers that were ~ 4-10-fold higher than the elderly ferrets vaccinated with the HA protein only vaccines (Fig. 2). Ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA plus TRAC478 had statistically similar anti HA binding antibodies as ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA plus SAS. All ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA plus either adjuvant had significantly higher anti-BR/18 HA total IgG antibody titers than ferrets vaccinated with the HA proteins (Fig. 2A) and ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA and TRAC478 had significantly higher anti-TS/20 HA binding antibodies (Fig. 2B).

After boost, antisera collected from older animals vaccinated with the bivalent COBRA (Y2 and NG2) HA vaccine formulated with TRAC478 had average HAI titers ≥ 80 (6.32 Log2) against H1N1 viruses isolated from 2009 to 2019 (Fig. 3B). Whereas the elderly ferrets vaccinated with the same bivalent COBRA HA vaccine in combination with SAS had sera with HAI activity ≥ 40 (5.32 Log2) against all H1N1 influenza viruses (except for VC/19) in the panel (Fig. 3C). All adjuvanted influenza vaccines elicited significantly higher HAI antibody responses against most of the H1N1 influenza viruses tested than the protein only vaccine control in the elderly ferrets (Fig. 3A-C). There were no significant differences were identified in HAI titer in ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA plus TRAC478 or SAS (Fig. 3B-C).

After boost, antisera collected from animals vaccinated with COBRA HA plus TRAC478 had average HAI titers of ≥ 40 (5.32 Log2) against 6 out of 8 H3N2 strains in the panel (Fig. 3B). Ferrets vaccinated with the same COBRA HA vaccine plus SAS had 2–4 lower HAI titers against the panel of H3N2 viruses compared to ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA vaccine plus TRAC478 (Fig. 3C). No significant differences were identified between TRAC478 and SAS adjuvanted COBRA HA influenza vaccine groups in eliciting anti-H3 HAI antibody responses in these elderly ferrets (Fig. 3B-C). Regardless of adjuvant used, all ferrets had higher HAI titers than ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA vaccine only (Fig. 3A).

The serum samples collected after boost vaccination were pooled for each group of the vaccinated animals and were tested for their ability to neutralize viral infections in vitro (Fig. 4). The elderly ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA proteins without adjuvant had low, but detectable neutralization titers against 3 out of 4 H1N1 strains and 4 out of 5 H3N2 influenza strains (Fig. 4). Ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA proteins with either adjuvant had antibodies with neutralizing activity against the H1N1 viruses that was significantly higher (20–25-fold) than neutralizing antibody titers elicited in ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA proteins only, albeit the neutralizing patterns were similar (Fig. 4). However, these same ferrets had statistically similar neutralizing antibodies against the H3N2 influenza viruses, regardless of if the vaccine used was mixed with adjuvant or unadjuvanted (Fig. 4).

TRAC478 and SAS adjuvanted influenza vaccines mitigated clinical symptoms against H1N1 influenza virus challenge in the elderly ferrets

Four weeks after the final vaccination, elderly ferrets were infected with BR/18 H1N1 influenza virus and were monitored for 14 days for weight loss and clinical symptoms (Fig. 5). Ferrets vaccinated with adjuvanted influenza vaccines lost between 5 and 10% of their original body weight between days 4–8 p.i. (Fig. 5A). In contrast, ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA protein had significantly more weight loss than ferrets vaccinated with adjuvanted vaccine (Table 1 and SFig. 3) and lost on average, ~ 16% of their original body weight by day 7 post-infection (p.i.). However, few of these elderly ferrets had significant signs of morbidity and low clinical scores (Fig. 5B). At day 3 post-infection, ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA proteins without adjuvant had significantly higher viral nasal wash titers compared to ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA antigens formulated with either TRAC478 or SAS (Fig. 5C). At day 5 post-infection, ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA proteins without adjuvant had detectable viral titers in their turbinate but not in other tissues (Fig. 5D). As a comparison, ferrets vaccinated with COBRA HA antigens formulated with either TRAC478 or SAS had low or undetectable viral titers in all tissues tested (Fig. 5D).

Taken together, all adjuvanted HA vaccines mitigated disease caused by influenza viral infection with less weight loss and reduced viral shedding as compared to the HA protein only vaccine in the pre-immune elderly ferrets.

Discussion

Although, the vaccination reduces the severity of disease in the elderly people, they still have higher rates of influenza-associated morbidity and mortality each season compared to young adults [2, 11, 38]. In this study, COBRA-based HA protein vaccines formulated with one of 2 adjuvants (TRAC478 or SAS), containing multiple synthesized TLR agonists or TLR agonist and saponin-based molecules, were tested protective antibody responses in elderly ferrets with pre-existing immunity to historical influenza strains. Vaccination of adjuvanted HA protein vaccines elicited robust antigen-specific binding and inhibiting antibody responses in pre-immune elderly ferrets, as compared to the vaccination with HA protein only vaccine formulation. Antibodies elicited by the adjuvanted HA vaccines protected the elderly ferrets from severe disease caused by influenza viral infection.

To maintain a diverse immune repertoire and fill peripheral lymphoid compartments, the newly produced naive T and B lymphocytes migrate to secondary lymphoid tissues, such as the spleen, from the bone marrow and thymus [13, 39, 40]. However, in the elderly populations, the involution of the thymus results in the diminished primary lymphopoiesis [12, 41, 42]. Although, the development of B and T cells continues in the secondary lymphoid tissues, the composition and quality of the mature lymphocyte pool in the elderly is profoundly altered. These pools are characterized by reduced antibody avidity and/or number of responding B cells, skewed repertoire of CD8+ T cells, and increased differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into Th17 instead of Th1 and Th2 cells [11, 43]. Thus, the aged immune system needs additional stimulation to enhance both innate and adaptive immune responses to fight newly emerged influenza virus variants or other respiratory pathogens [2, 11]. However, even with the high-dose or MF-59 adjuvanted influenza vaccines, it is still challenging to induce strong and long-lasting immune response against influenza viruses in the elderly population [3]. A strong immune response following influenza vaccination includes both cellular and antibody responses and adjuvants may be the key to reach a balanced Th1 and Th2 response in the weakened immune system. Since, MF-59, an oil-in-water emulsion, elicits strong Th2 response, many new adjuvants induce enhanced Th1 responses [25, 27]. TRAC478 increases the production of IgG2c antibodies and generates robust interferon gamma (IFN γ) levels in mice vaccinated with the receptor-binding domain of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein [25]. TRAC478 and SAS may have similar effects in the elderly ferrets used in this study that resulted in high titer protective immune responses.

Unfortunately, there are limited reagents to measure cellular immune response in ferrets. Several research groups are generating and testing immunological reagent to measure B cell, T cell and cytokine responses in ferrets [44–48]. Future studies may be able to investigate the activation of T-cell population and breadth of B-cell receptor repertoires, as well as the memory T and B cells development and the longevity after TRAC478 or SAS adjuvanted influenza vaccination in elderly population.

In addition to adjuvants, antigen design is also crucial for the elicitation of broadly protective antibody responses since influenza viruses are constantly evolving resulting in new viral variants that antigenicity drift from the vaccine strains, resulting in the low efficacy and narrow coverage of the annual influenza vaccine [49]. COBRA methodology selects antigenic sites from a wide time-period and form a unique combination in a single antigen which induces broader immune responses [28–30, 36]. In this study, HAI antibodies elicited against Y2 COBRA HA vaccine had a broader reactive spectrum to H1 viruses regardless of adjuvants in the vaccine formulation. The Y2 COBRA HA antigen was proved to be a promising universal influenza vaccine candidate as we reported previously [30, 36]. The antibodies elicited by NG2 COBRA HA antigen cover a broad number of H3N2 influenza viruses, except for KS/17, which is consistent with previous findings [36]. The decreased inhibiting antibodies to KS/17 could be associate with the antigenic differences of HA proteins between 3 C.2a and 3 C.3a H3N2 viruses [50, 51]. HA proteins from influenza viruses in clade 3 C.2a have a threonine at amino acid position 128 and an alanine at position 138 in 130-loop, a serine at residue 144 in antigenic site A, as well as tyrosine at site 159 and a threonine at site 160 in site B in HA protein. While HA proteins from influenza viruses in clade 3 C.3a have an alanine at site 128 and a serine at site 138 in the 130-loop, an asparagine at 144 in site A, as well as a serine in site 159 and a lysine at site 160 in site B [50, 51]. NG2 COBRA HA has the same amino acid as the HA protein of 3 C.2a viruses that results in the elicitation of antibodies that effectively recognize the HA proteins in 3 C.2a influenza viruses than HA proteins from 3 C.3a viruses [36]. In this study, both adjuvants enhanced virus-binding, hemagglutination-inhibiting, and neutralizing antibody titers against H1 viruses but have less pronounced effects on antibody titers against H3 viruses. Since, the H1 HA antigen is more immunogenic than H3 HA antigen, H1 HA is always immunodominant in a H1/H3 HA bivalent influenza vaccine. Our previous study has shown that vaccine formulations with multiple H3 HA and one H1 HA COBRA components significantly enhanced hemagglutination-inhibition (HAI) activity against H3N2 viruses [52]. This may be an effective strategy to enhance antibody response to H3 HA antigens and reduce morbidity and mortality in the elderly.

In summary, TRAC478 and SAS adjuvants, when mixed with COBRA HA proteins, elicit high titer protective antibodies in elderly ferrets. These vaccine formulations induce broadly protective neutralizing antibodies that mitigate the symptoms and reduce viral shedding caused by influenza viral infection.
