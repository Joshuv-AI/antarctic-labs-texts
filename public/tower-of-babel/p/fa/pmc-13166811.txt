Segmental femoral fracture management using intramedullary nailing combined with plate fixation: a case report
Authors: Muhammad Ade Junaidi, Wahyu Eko Jatmiko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Segmental femoral fractures are challenging, with a high risk of non-union, requiring tailored treatments such as intramedullary nailing (IMN) and plate fixation. Although this combined approach is promising for tibial fractures, its effectiveness in segmental femoral fractures remains under evaluation. This study aims to evaluate the success rates of IMN and plate fixation in treating these challenging injuries.Presentation of Case:A 52-year-old man presented with a deformed, shortened left thigh, moderate tenderness (Visual Analogue Scale score of 4), and limited hip and knee mobility; ankle and toe movements were normal. X-rays showed a comminuted femur fracture with posteromedial translation. Adding a unicortical plate before IMN can simplify these complex cases and improve outcomes.Conclusion:Combining IMN with bridge plating stabilizes severe segmental femoral fractures and aids healing. Progressive rehabilitation after surgery supports physical and functional recovery, allowing tailored strategies based on fracture type and patient needs. This case adds to the limited clinical evidence supporting this method and may assist surgeons in treating similarly challenging fracture patterns.

Introduction

Segmental femoral fractures, a distinct fracture pattern with an intermediate fragment isolated by fractures at different levels, fall under the AO Foundation/Orthopaedic Trauma Association classification 32-C2[1,2]. More prevalent in younger individuals due to high-energy traumas, effective treatment strategies are crucial[1–3]. In some patients, especially after a severe muscle injury or repeated strain, myositis ossificans can develop within the affected soft tissues. This complication is not uncommon in athletic or high-impact injuries and often presents with stiffness, pain, and delayed recovery of function[4]. Segmental femoral fractures are typically treated with closed reduction and internal fixation using an intramedullary nail to minimize soft-tissue damage. Interest has recently grown in exploring combined fixation strategies for segmental femoral fractures as outcomes with conventional methods remain inconsistent[5–7]. Although trauma care has advanced considerably, there is still no clear agreement on the most reliable fixation construct for these difficult injuries. The literature remains limited, and most available studies report small numbers with variable techniques and results[3,8,9].

During hospitalization, effective management of femoral fractures requires an interdisciplinary approach that integrates orthopedic, geriatric, and rehabilitation care[10]. Such coordinated pathways have been shown to promote early mobilization, standardized postoperative care, and reduced complication rates. In orthogeriatric settings, comprehensive in-hospital management focusing on early physiotherapy and individualized rehabilitation has also been associated with improved functional autonomy and reduced 12-month mortality[11].

This report was prepared in accordance with the SCARE criteria. It describes a case of segmental femoral fracture managed using a combination of IMN and plate fixation. The purpose is to share the clinical and radiological outcomes of this method and to discuss the practical issues encountered during treatment. The patient, who wished to regain full mobility and return to his job, placed strong emphasis on functional recovery as his main goal.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male of Asian ethnicity presented to the ER with thigh pain 2 hours after a motorcycle accident. He had a non-contributory medical and family history, with no known allergies, and reported no previous musculoskeletal injuries, specifically no prior hamstring or thigh injuries. He is a non-smoker and reported occasional alcohol use. The patient was an active individual and expressed significant concern about his ability to return to work and mobility, stating his primary goal was to “walk normally again.”

Upon examination of the left thigh, it was found deformed and shortened, with the lower extremity externally rotated; there was no visible wound or bruise. The thigh was tender [Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score of 4], with normal distal sensory and no signs of shock. Laboratory investigations on admission, including a complete blood count and basic metabolic panel, were within normal limits. The range of motion (ROM) of the hip and knee was limited by pain, but ankle and great toe ROM were normal. A leg length discrepancy of 2 cm was noted. Differential diagnoses included isolated femoral shaft fracture and hip dislocation (which was ruled out clinically). X-ray of the knee revealed a complete comminuted fracture of the distal meta-diaphyseal of the left thigh with translation of the fracture fragment to the posteromedial. Femur radiography showed a complete, segmented fracture of the proximal diaphysis to the distal metaphysis of the femur with shortening and translation of the fracture fragment to the posteromedial (Fig. 1). The fracture pattern was consistent with a high-energy traumatic event. There were no radiographic signs of pathological fracture, such as bone cysts or tumors, and the patient had no history of metabolic bone disease to suggest a non-traumatic cause.

Skeletal traction was applied for 2 weeks. This was done to maintain femoral length, reduce muscle spasm, and allow the significant soft tissue swelling to subside prior to definitive fixation, thereby optimizing the soft tissue envelope for surgery. After that, the surgery of open reduction internal fixation using an intramedullary nail, augmented using a C-wire for the proximal shaft of the femur, combined with ORIF using a bridging plate and a distal locking plate for the distal segment of the femur was done for the patient. The surgery was performed by the senior orthopedic trauma team under general anesthesia. The total operative time was 210 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 750 mL. Prophylactic antibiotics (2 g Cefazolin) were administered 30 minutes before incision, and chemical thromboprophylaxis (low-molecular-weight heparin) was initiated 12 hours postoperatively.

After surgery, the patient was followed up in 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year after surgery. A structured physiotherapy progression was initiated on postoperative day 2 with gentle passive ROM, advancing to active exercises. In the 3rd month after surgery, the ROM of the knee has returned to normal, in knee flexion and knee extension. In this 3rd month, the patient was also able to walk using crutches with partial-weight bearing. Because of the patient’s compliance and discipline in following the rehabilitation program, in the 6th month, the patient was able to walk in full-weight bearing and return to his normal activities. At this 6-month mark, muscle strength in the affected quadriceps and hamstrings was graded at 4+/5 on the MRC scale. The patient’s SCARA (Sanders-Corb-Aparna) score for femoral shaft fractures was calculated at 4, predicting a high likelihood of union and good functional outcome (Figs 2 and 3). We also evaluated the patient’s X-ray examination result in the 3rd month, 6th month, and 1 year after surgery. At the third month, a hard callus was visible at the fracture sites in the distal and proximal regions of the fracture. At the 6th month, the hard callus appeared increasingly consolidated at the fracture sites, especially at the distal fracture site. Evaluation after 1 year post-surgery revealed no clinical signs of infection, no signs of implant loosening, and callus was fully concentrated at all of the fracture sites (Fig. 4). Discussion

Segmental femoral fractures, classified as AO/OTA type 32-C2, are typically caused by high-energy trauma such as traffic accidents or falls from height. Reported rates of nonunion after IMN vary, with some studies describing figures between 3% and 22%. These injuries are often accompanied by additional complications including malalignment, nerve injury, and leg-length discrepancy. Intraoperative techniques such as the use of femoral distractors or temporary reduction devices can assist alignment, although they may also increase the risk of disrupting local blood supply if not handled carefully. During reaming, maintaining reduction and avoiding unwanted rotation of the intermediate fragment remain key technical challenges for the operating surgeon[1,12–14].

In our case, fixation was reinforced using a C-wire in combination with a bridging plate to provide additional support around the fracture zone. This approach follows the concept of relative stability, which allows controlled micromotion to promote secondary bone healing while maintaining proper length and alignment. For adult femoral shaft fractures, IMN remains the standard treatment because it offers stable fixation with minimal soft-tissue disruption. However, in segmental or comminuted patterns, adding a plate can help maintain reduction and improve rotational control during the healing process[15–17]. In this case, the comminutive fracture at the midshaft part was fixed with the IMN and cerclage wire; we overrode the tip of the IMN to the tip of the plate to decrease the risk of stress riser fracture at the peri-implant that caused implant failure[8,9]. Additionally, combining open cerclage wires with intramedullary nails has been proposed for stable fixation in comminuted fractures of the femoral shaft. Moreover, modern polyaxially angular stable plates have been shown to offer significant advantages in stability, particularly in geriatric trauma patients with distal femoral fractures[18–20].

Similarly, promising outcomes were achieved using this technique for segmental femoral fractures, with all patients healing within an average of 18.9 weeks without needing further surgical interventions, maintaining proper alignment and limb length equality[2,3].

Adjuvant bridge plating serves as a supportive mechanism to the IMN, providing additional stability to the fractured segments. Additionally, bridge plating helps in maintaining the mechanical axis, which is vital for proper weight-bearing and functional recovery post-injury[21–23].

The postoperative course was uneventful, with no complications observed, corresponding to a Clavien–Dindo Grade 0 outcome. Deep infection remains the most concerning potential complication in such cases. Should this occur, removal of the implants would likely be required, resulting in a considerable segmental bone defect. Reconstruction in that setting would generally require a staged procedure, often using bone transport through an external fixator based on the Ilizarov technique. Although this approach can be prolonged and demanding, it remains an important salvage option when infection leads to bone loss or non-union[24].

Rehabilitation was carried out on our patients following the surgery performed. In this case, the absence of infection and the maintenance of fixation stability allowed early rehabilitation and progressive weight bearing. Passive in-bed mobilization based on hemodynamic status and patient complaints was initiated post-procedure. During the first week of follow-up, the patient was able to mobilize without weight-bearing using bilateral crutches. The patient consistently engaged in a ROM exercises for the lower extremities. By the 3rd month post-procedure, the patient could progress to partial weight-bearing. The surgical wound showed good healing without significant signs of infection. Minimal discomfort persisted at the 3-month follow-up, with a VAS score of 0–2, notably improved from the initial month’s VAS score of 4–6, which substantially impacted the patient’s rest and sleep. Nevertheless, our patient showed good improvement in both physical and functional outcomes.

This report has several limitations. It is inherently subject to publication bias, since successful outcomes are more likely to be reported. The patient’s good general condition and high compliance may also limit generalizability, as such factors are not universal among trauma patients. In addition, this combined fixation technique requires multiple implant systems and advanced surgical expertise, which may not be readily available in all centers.

Conclusion

The standard approach for adult femoral shaft fractures is closed reduction and internal fixation with an intramedullary nail, despite a nonunion risk exceeding 10%, particularly higher in type C fractures versus type A. Complex femur fracture management lacks a definitive consensus, with segmental fractures exhibiting nonunion rates of 3%–22% post-IMN treatment, alongside complications like malunion and nerve damage. Using a unicortical plate before IMN insertion simplifies segmental fractures, improving outcomes with high union rates and alignment preservation. Additionally, in severe segmental femoral shaft fractures, combining IMN with adjuvant bridge plating promotes stability, aiding in fracture healing. Rehabilitation post-surgery involves progressive mobilization, showing positive outcomes in physical and functional parameters. The relative stability principle emphasizes stable fixation for optimal healing and functional results, tailored to individual fracture patterns and patient characteristics.

In line with the SCARE criteria, the patient expressed satisfaction with the recovery process, noting gradual improvement in mobility and confidence in performing daily activities following surgery and rehabilitation[25].
