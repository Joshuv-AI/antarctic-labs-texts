Assessment of Securidaca longipedunculata (violet tree) effect on semen quality and blood sex hormone levels in indigenous goats
Authors: Kamogelo Shai, Sogolo L Lebelo, Freddy Manyeula, Monnye Mabelebele, Amenda N Sebola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSemen quality, sperm characteristics and profiling of reproductive hormones of indigenous goat bucks (IGB) are of paramount importance for both the sustainable management of livestock populations and the economic well-being of farming communities. The current study assessed the impact of S.longipedunculata leaf extract (SLLE) on semen quality, sperm characteristics, and reproductive hormones in IGB. Sixteen pubertal bucks (12–18 months) were randomly assigned to four treatment groups receiving SLLE at 0, 50, 100, and 125 mg/kg body weight. Results showed that SLLE at 50 and 100 mg/kg improved spermatogenesis, increasing sperm concentration and semen volume. However, higher doses (125 mg/kg) reduced live sperm cell percentages. SLLE significantly influenced sperm motility and velocity but had no significant effect on oestrogen and testosterone levels. These findings suggest SLLE may enhance IGB reproductive performance.Keywords: Securidaca longipendunculata, Sperm cell, Motility, Semen concentration, Goat bucks, Reproductive hormones

Introduction

The reproductive health and performance of livestock are critical factors in the sustainable management of animal populations, particularly in the context of indigenous breeds, which serve as essential genetic resources (Maroto-Morales et al., 2016; Isidahomen & Oguntade, 2018). Enhancing livestock fertility through natural substances has gained increasing attention as a strategy to improve productivity while reducing reliance on synthetic chemicals (Suleiman et al., 2014; Iwu et al., 2020). Various plant-derived compounds have been identified for their potential to enhance reproductive performance, offering a sustainable and ecologically friendly approach to livestock breeding.

S. longipedunculata (violet tree), native to several tropical and subtropical African regions, has long been recognized for its medicinal properties, including its use in ethnoveterinary practices (Maroyi, 2013; Mustapha, 2013; Nordeng et al., 2013; Mongalo et al., 2015). The plant contains bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, and tannins, which have been linked to improved reproductive health and sperm quality. Traditionally, S. longipedunculata has been associated with enhancing male fertility (Togun & Egbunike, 2006; Mongalo et al., 2015), but scientific research on its direct effects on semen quality and reproductive hormones remains limited.

Given the importance of IGB in rural livelihoods, providing nutrition, income, and cultural value, therefore, improving their reproductive efficiency is crucial, particularly in resource-limited settings (Wodajo, 2020; John & Demi, 2013). While these breeds often exhibit lower reproductive rates compared to exotic counterparts (Hegde, 2019), natural interventions such as S. longipedunculata could offer a promising solution.

This study aims to investigate the effects of SLLE on semen quality, sperm characteristics, and reproductive hormone levels in IGB. Understanding the influence of SLLE on goat reproductive health could provide a cost-effective, sustainable means to enhance breeding performance, thereby contributing to food security and the conservation of valuable genetic resources. Based on these considerations, the current study hypothesizes that different SLLE dosing levels influence semen and sperm characteristics in IGB.

Materials and method

Study site and ingredients sources

Mature leaves of S.longipedunculata were collected during the late summer months (February,2021), which is a period associated with optimal vegetative growth and maximal accumulation of secondary metabolites. Mature leaves were specifically chosen because they are known to contain higher and more stable concentrations of bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, tannins, alkaloids, and saponins, which are of pharmacological importance. In addition, their chemical composition is less variable compared to young leaves, thereby providing a more reliable basis for phytochemical and biological evaluations. Leaf samples were harvested from naturally growing plants from the mountainous areas of Gogogo village, Vhembe district (22.7696° S, 29.9741° E), Limpopo Province (−24° 00′ 0.00″ S; 29° 00′ 0.00″ E), South Africa. The permission to collect the leaves were granted from the department of Forestry, Fisheries and Environment (DFFE). Thereafter, the leaves were taken to the University of Limpopo’s Larry Leach herbarium, Polokwane, South Africa for confirmation and authentication of the species and a voucher specimen no was generated (UNIN 121,579). The leaves were spread on a large tray and air dried under shade to prevent nutrient losses due to ultraviolet rays (Dzowela et al., 1995). A commercial grinder (MF 10B, IKA, Germany) was used to grind the dried leaves to pass through 1 mm sieve and stored in air-tight plastic containers prior to extract preparation. Thereafter, the extract was prepared through maceration where the powdered material was weighed and soaked in distilled water at a ratio of 1:5 (w/v) in a sealed glass container. The mixture was kept at room temperature (25–30 °C) for 72 h with intermittent shaking to enhance extraction. Thereafter, the suspension was filtered using Whatman No 1 filter paper, and the filtrate was concentrated on a water bath at 50 °C to obtain a crude aqueous extract. The extract was stored in amber bottles at 4 °C until required for dosing.

Experimental animals, treatments diets, and procedures

The experiment was carried out at the University of Limpopo Experimental Farm (Syferkuil, Limpopo Province, South Africa). The farm is situated about 10 km in the Northwest of the Turfloop campus. During that time, the average temperatures ranged between 5 and 20 ºC (winter) (Kutu & Asiwe, 2010).

Sixteen (16) IGB (Fig. 1) at the pubertal stage (12 to 18 months old; 23 kg ± 2.5 kg), were purchased from Lekwala Projects Pty Ltd (Willows, Limpopo province, South Africa) and used in 90 days feeding trial. The bucks were balanced according to initial weight and randomly allotted to four treatment diets in a completely randomized design with four (4) bucks (each as an experimental unit) per treatment replicated four times. The IGB were dosed with SLLE as follows: 1. 0 (D0SL) = a treatment group without SLLE. 2. 50 (D50SL) = a treatment group with 50 mg of SLLE /kg of body weight; 3. 100 (D100SL) = a treatment group with 100 mg of SLLE /kg of body weight; 4. 125 (D125SL) = a treatment group with 125 mg of SLLE /kg of body weight SLLE. The IGB were fed 2 kg basal diet daily (Table 1) (ram and ewe Q pro feed from NTK) (Polfamix O-K, Trouw Nutrition Polska, Grodzisk Mazowiecki, Poland. The dosing was done once in the morning (07:00- 08:00 hrs) while feed and water were offered ad libitum. The doses used in this study were adapted from a previous investigation in rabbits (Chibuogwu, 2017). To ensure physiological relevance and safety in goats, interspecies scaling principles were applied, considering differences in body size, metabolic rate, and surface area. Goats generally tolerate slightly higher mg/kg body weight doses than smaller species such as rabbits; therefore, the selected range (50–125 mg/kg body weight) was conservative and within expected safety margins. In addition, ethnoveterinary reports and pilot observations in goats confirmed the absence of overt toxicity or feed refusal at these levels, supporting their suitability for evaluating the biological effects of S.longipedunculata leaf extract.

Indigenous goat bucks used in the current study the experimental facility/farm. The goats display varying colour patterns on coats; however, these are common colours for indigenous goats in the study area. These goats are kept in individual holding pens constructed close to each other to allow for socialisation among the goat bucks.

Semen and sperm characteristics

Evaluations were performed every 14 days, that is on: days 0, 14, 28, 42, and 56 between 07:00 and 09:00, before feeding. On each evaluation day (i) semen was collected by electroejaculation into sterile tubes and assessed for: volume, pH, concentration, and kinematics (CASA at 37 °C) within 30 min; and (ii) 5 mL of blood was collected into a plain (non-EDTA) tube for serum biochemistry. All measurements were performed by the same trained observer using the same equipment and settings at each time point. Four semen samples per treatment were collected using electro-ejaculator (Pulsator V, electronic ejaculator, Lane Manufacturing Inc., USA) following the method by Dascanio (2014) and Abril-Sánchez et al. (2019). The semen was then evaluated for quality by the mobile laboratories of the Germplasm Conservation and Reproductive Biotechnologies of the Agricultural Research Council Gauteng province, South Africa.

Semen and sperm cell evaluation

Semen volume: Immediately after collection, ejaculate volume (mL) was read directly from a graduated Falcon® collection tube (cat. no. 352,099, USA).

Semen pH: The pH of fresh semen was determined using a calibrated bench pH meter (pH 11 Series; OAKON®, Singapore).

The sperm colour: the sperm Colour was evaluated using the naked eyes observing directly from the collection tube. Furthermore, fresh ejaculates were maintained at 37 °C and assessed within 30 min; to evaluate for (sperm morphology, viability and membrane integrity), slides were pre-warmed to 37 °C to minimise cold shock. Live/Dead sperm were determined by supravital eosin–nigrosin staining: 10–15 µL semen was mixed 1:1 with stain, smeared, air-dried and examined at ×1000 oil immersion; unstained heads were recorded as membrane intact or live and eosin-positive heads (pink/red heads) as membrane damaged or dead. Live coiled-tailed sperm (functional plasma-membrane integrity) were quantified with the hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST): semen (≈0.05–0.10 mL) was incubated in a 100 mOsm sodium-citrate/fructose solution at 37 °C for 30 min and examined at ×400; sperm showing characteristic tail curling were counted as HOST-reactive. Live proximal droplet sperm were scored on the vitality smear by recording, among live sperm only, the presence of a proximal cytoplasmic droplet (confirmatory Diff-Quik/Spermac smears were prepared when midpiece detail required). For each endpoint, at least 200 sperm per slide were evaluated across random fields, with duplicate slides per ejaculate and the mean reported. Outcomes were calculated as percentages: live = 100×(live/total), dead = 100×(dead/total), live coiled-tailed = 100×(HOST-reactive/total), and live proximal droplet = 100× (live with proximal droplet/total live). Quality control included duplicate counts and periodic second reader checks to ensure ≥0.80 inter-reader agreement (κ).

The sperm concentration (×10⁹/mL) was estimated by spectrophotometry (JENWAY® 6310). Briefly, absorbance of diluted semen was measured in a sodium citrate medium, leveraging the proportional increase in optical density with cell density (Kumar et al., 2013). A 2.9 % (w/v) sodium citrate diluent was prepared (2.9 g in 100 mL distilled water). For each measurement, 3.0 mL diluent was placed in a microcuvette (HemoCue AB®, Ängelholm, Sweden) to zero the instrument; 15 µL of raw semen was then added, mixed gently, and the absorbance recorded. Final concentration was calculated using the laboratory calibration equation: sperm concentration = dilution factor × (34.43 × absorbance − 0.22).

Computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) settings and thresholds

Semen kinematics thus, (ejaculated semen volume, sperm concentration (*106 mL), total sperm count (TSC) (volume *density/ml), the percentage of motile spermatozoa and sperm progressive motility), were quantified using CASA-Sperm Class Analyzer® (V.5.2.0.1) The settings for the CASA system known as Sperm Class Analyzer® (SCA®) system (Masindi, 2012) on pre-warmed slides (chamber depth 20 µm) at 37 °C. Videos were recorded at 60 frames s⁻¹, with a minimum track length of 1.0 s and ≥15 consecutive frames per track; focus, illumination and background subtraction were standardised prior to each run. Detection gates were set to exclude debris (head area 5–60 µm²) and non-sperm events. Motility bands and progression criteria were defined a priori as follows: static <5 µm s⁻¹; slow 5–25 µm s⁻¹; medium 26–50 µm s⁻¹; rapid >50 µm s⁻¹. Progressive motility required VAP ≥50 µm s⁻¹ and STR ≥80 % (i.e., VSL/VAP × 100), while non-progressive motility comprised motile tracks not meeting these thresholds. Kinematic outputs reported were: VCL (curvilinear velocity), VAP (average path velocity), VSL (straight-line velocity), LIN (VSL/VCL × 100), STR (VSL/VAP × 100), WOB (VAP/VCL × 100), ALH (amplitude of lateral head displacement), and BCF (beat-cross frequency). Definitions follow established CASA conventions (Somi et al., 2006; Vyt et al., 2008), with total motility expressed as the percentage of sperm exhibiting any movement (Kathiravan et al., 2008).

Hormonal profiling

For blood collection, the goat's head was tilted at a 30-degree angle to the side by holding the animal under its jaw to allow for easy access to the vein. A 21-gauge needle attached to a 5 mL syringe was inserted through the skin into the jugular vein at a shallow angle, and approximately 5 mL of blood was aspirated and transferred into a plain (non-EDTA) tube. Thereafter, cotton swap with alcohol was used to stop the bleeding and prevent infection into the vein. The blood was allowed to coagulate in kaylite holders at 37 °C. The tubes were then centrifuged at 3500 rpm at 21 °C for 10 min to separate the serum from the blood. A plastic pipette was used to draw the serum into plain tubes and stored at −20 °C for hormonal analysis (To, 2002). The oestrogen and testosterone were profiled using commercial ELISA kits (Accubind® New Jersey, United State, ELISA kit Microwells product code 425–300; 625–300 and 3725–300 respectively). The coefficients of intra-assay variance were 4.16 % and 4.44 % for the oestrogen EIA, and 6.58 % and 7.59 % for the testosterone EIA. The coefficients of the inter-assay-variance were 5.88 % and 11.89 % for the oestrogen EIA and 13, 41 % and 15, 92 % for the testosterone EIA, with a sensitivity of 8pg/mL for oestrogen and 40pg/mL for the testosterone. A parallelism test for both the EIAs were conducted and the serial dilutions of the serum samples resulted in displacement curves that were parallel to the standard curves (the relative variation of the slope of respective trend lines was < 4 %).

Sperm morphology and viability. SLLE dose significantly influenced the proportion of live sperm (P < 0.05), with higher values at D100SL and D125SL and lower values at D0SL and D50SL. No dose effects were detected for dead sperm, live coiled-tailed sperm, or proximal droplet sperm.

Sperm membrane integrity. Dose significantly affected the proportion of membrane-intact sperm (P < 0.05), which was highest at D50SL and D100SL and lowest at D0SL and D125SL, the membrane-damaged sperm did not vary with dose.

Dose–response trends (regression). Regression analyses showed no significant linear or quadratic trends for semen pH or sperm concentration (P > 0.05). Discussion

S. longipedunculata increases semen quality by boosting antioxidant defences (limiting lipid peroxidation), which helps preserve sperm viability, progressive motility, and plasma-membrane/acrosome integrity. Its phytochemicals support mitochondrial function and epididymal maturation, thereby reducing abnormal morphology (Johnson et al., 2021; Aitken and Roman, 2008)

Semen characteristics

The highest semen volume, pH, and concentration observed in IGB dosed with D50SL and D100SL suggest that SLLE up to 100mg/kg body weight significantly enhanced spermatogenesis and reproductive capacity. This improvement may be attributed to flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol, which possess antioxidant properties that protect sperm cells from oxidative damage (Johnson et al., 2021; Aitken and Roman, 2008). Oxidative stress is known to affect sperm morphology and reduce motility and viability. Additionally, the fatty acid content in SLLE (Shai et al., 2024) might have influenced semen volume, as observed by Maldjian et al. (2005), who reported increased semen volume in boars supplemented with fatty acids.

The IGB dosed with D0SL (control), had similar semen volume to those dosed D125SL confirming that a high dose of SLLE did not have an effect on spermatogenesis. Furthermore, this observation may be due to reasonably a hormetic effect, where high polyphenol exposure shifts from antioxidant to pro-oxidant activity and elevates ROS that impair gamete function (Nowak et al., 2022). In addition, flavonoids or saponins at upper doses can depress Leydig-cell steroidogenesis by limiting cholesterol substrate and down-regulating steroidogenic genes (e.g., StAR/3β-HSD), reducing androgen-dependent secretions and spermatogenesis (Kholif, 2023; Martin et al., 2020). Further, condensed tannins at higher intake bind proteins and lower digestibility or trace-mineral availability, indirectly constraining testicular output (Besharati et al., 2022). Consistently, plant-specific data for S. longipedunculata show better semen indices at moderate doses with poorer motility at higher doses in rodent/guinea-pig models (Singwa et al., 2025). In contrast, Chibougwu et al. (2018) in their study of Semen Quality and Testis Morphometry Effects of S. longepedunculata (Fresen) Root Bark Extract in Wistar Rats, reported the dosing levels of S. longipedunculata root bark extracts beyond 100mg/kg body weight to have negative effects on reproductive indices of Wistar Rats. Within the tested range, SLLE improved semen quality up to 50 mg/kg body weight, with higher proportions of live, membrane-intact sperm and no adverse changes in gross ejaculate traits; doses above 50 mg/kg body weight did not confer additional benefit and some indices declined. A normal range pH (6.2–7.8) (Maule, 1996) provides a conducive environment for healthy sperm and function. In this study, is evident that dosing of SLLE improved semen environment for sperm production of IGB dosed D50SL and D100SL. A similar study was reported by Jinadu et al. (2018) when rams were fed Garcinia kola.

The differences in semen colour across treatment groups validate the semen volume results. The creamy semen in D50SL and D100SL suggests enhanced fertility, likely due to favourable semen pH. The watery semen recorded in D125SL could be linked to dilution from frequent ejaculations, as libido analysis (Shai et al., 2024) indicated increased sexual activity in this group. Although the D0SL group had a yellow semen colour, all semen colours recorded in this study fall within the normal range, implying that SLLE did not negatively affect sperm colour.

Sperm motility and velocity

An increase in sperm motility and velocity in D50SL and D100SL compared to the control group suggests that SLLE enhanced reproductive indices, consistent with the findings of Love (2011) and Ingrid et al. (2015), who reported a positive correlation between motility and improved reproductive indices. Similarly, Chibougwu et al. (2018) found increased sperm motility in Wistar rats dosed with 50mg/kg of S. longipedunculata. The sperm total motility range (37–67 %) in this study was lower than the mass motility (80–84 %) reported by Jinadu et al. (2018) in rams, potentially due to species differences. The improvement in motility and velocity at dose 50–100 mg/kg than in controls, consistent with improved semen quality possibly supported by PUFA-related membrane/mitochondrial effects at moderate exposure (Van Tryan et al., 2017; Collodel et al., 2020) however, the lower motility at 125 mg/kg suggests that increased lipid peroxidation susceptibility together with membrane-active phytochemicals at the higher dose outweighed these benefits, indicating a dose-dependent (hormetic) response.

Sperm morphology and viability

The increase in live sperm percentage with incremental doses of SLLE suggests improved sperm survival and reproductive success. This aligns with Saalu (2013), who reported similar results in rats dosed with Vernonia amygdalina. The antioxidant properties of flavonoids in SLLE (Mongalo et al., 2015) likely contributed to these effects by preventing peroxidative damage to sperm cells. S. longipedunculata leaves contain polyphenols with demonstrable antioxidant activity, supporting the view that the extract can scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and limit lipid peroxidation (Obasi et al., 2020). Sperm membranes are PUFA-rich and therefore highly susceptible to ROS-driven lipid peroxidation; curbing this process preserves membrane fluidity and integrity and is associated with better motility (Colladel et al., 2022). Antioxidant protection also helps maintain mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production—key drivers of progressive, energy-dependent flagellar motion (Vahedi et al., 2024). These mechanisms together provide a biologically plausible explanation for the improvements in motility and membrane-intact sperm observed at moderate doses. At higher exposure, however, polyphenols can exhibit hormetic/pro-oxidant behaviour, tipping redox balance toward oxidative distress and attenuating benefits consistent with the diminished responses at 125 mg/kg (Sotler et al., 2019). The lack of significant effects on dead sperm cells, coiled-tailed sperm, and proximal droplet sperm suggests that SLLE does not negatively impact these parameters. These findings align with Chibougwu et al. (2018), who observed increased live spermatozoa in Wistar rats dosed with S. longipedunculata root bark extracts.

Sperm membrane integrity

The observed improvement in sperm membrane integrity suggests that SLLE enhances the protective barrier of sperm cells, ensuring better viability and function. This protective effect may contribute to improved fertilization potential by reducing damage from environmental stressors. The findings support the use of SLLE as a natural supplement to enhance sperm quality and reproductive efficiency in IGB.

Blood serum levels of ooestrogen and testosterone

No statistically significant effect was observed on ooestrogen and testosterone levels, suggesting that SLLE does not interfere with hormone production in IGB. The presence of zinc in SLLE (Shai et al., 2024) could support testosterone synthesis, as zinc is a crucial cofactor in the enzymatic pathways responsible for hormone production (Kachuee et al., 2019). Zinc plays a role in aromatase activity, which balances androgens and ooestrogens (Kurman et al., 2006). However, these findings contradict Ademola et al. (2017), who reported decreased ooestrogen and testosterone levels in fish fed diets containing S. longipedunculata.

Conclusion

SLLE at moderate doses (50mg/kg and 100mg/kg) significantly improves semen and sperm quality in IGB without disrupting hormonal balance. These findings suggest its potential as a natural supplement for enhancing reproductive efficiency in indigenous goat breeds while maintaining ecological sustainability.
