FTO inhibition attenuates renal fibrosis by downregulating ferroptosis activator ACSL4 and profibrotic factor TGFBI
Authors: Dalin Zhang, Chun-Lung Chiu, Ru Wen, Zhengyuan Qiu, Fernando Garcia-Marques, Abel Bermudez, Man Zhao, Hongjuan Zhao, Scott J. Dixon, Donna M. Peehl, Erinn B. Rankin, Sharon Pitteri, James D. Brooks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryRenal fibrosis (RF) is the main pathological feature and a potential therapeutic target of chronic kidney disease (CKD), a prevalent health problem causing a high economic burden to the health care system. Fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) inhibition, either genetically or pharmacologically, significantly reduced collagen deposition, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis marker expression after unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) compared with sham-operated controls in mice. In murine and human kidney epithelial cells as well as in human embryonic stem cell-derived kidney organoids, FTO inhibition reduced erastin-induced ferroptosis by decreasing lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species production by downregulating the ferroptosis driver ACSL4. Moreover, FTO inhibition directly downregulated TGFBI, which was strongly associated with reduced M2 macrophage accumulation after UUO. Our results provide a strong rationale for targeting FTO to alleviate RF in patients subjected to obstruction-related kidney injury, thereby reducing the prevalence of CKD and associated treatment costs and improving the quality of life.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) caused by acute kidney injury (AKI) due to infection, toxic chemicals, ischemia-reperfusion, kidney obstruction, and lifestyle-related diseases such as diabetes is a prevalent health problem causing a high economic burden to the health care system.1,2 It has risen from the 18th cause of loss of years of full health in 2010 to the 10th in 20193 and is projected to become the fifth global cause of death by 2040.4 Currently, there are no targeted therapies that can prevent or reverse kidney damage caused by the main risk factors of CKD.5 Therapeutic strategies that can effectively block or attenuate kidney damage leading to CKD are urgently needed to decrease the mortality rate, improve the quality of life, and reduce the economic burden on CKD patients.

Renal fibrosis (RF) is characterized by the production of excessive extracellular matrix, such as collagen deposited in the tubulointerstitial space and glomerulus area, ultimately leading to the destruction of renal parenchyma and loss of renal function.6 It is the main pathological feature of CDK, irrespective of the initial injury, and a potential therapeutic target of CKD.7 Antifibrotic agents shown to reduce fibrosis in other organs are being tested in CKD clinical trials.8,9 Ferroptosis, a regulated cell death mechanism characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production,10 has been associated with the development of RF in several mouse models, such as unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) and ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI)-induced AKI models.11,12 The therapeutic potential of ferroptosis inhibitors in RF has been a recent focus in CKD management.13 In addition, strong correlations have been found between the presence of M2 macrophages and progressive fibrosis in several human diseases, including systemic sclerosis,14 pulmonary fibrosis,15,16 ovarian fibrosis,17 and liver fibrosis.18 In the kidney, an increased prevalence of M2 macrophages was observed in diverse animal models of RF and CDK patient samples, suggesting that M2 macrophages play a pivotal role in fibrogenesis within the kidney.19,20

The fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) protein, an m6A RNA demethylase that plays an important role in various human diseases such as obesity and cancer,21 may promote UUO-induced RF in mice. Specifically, FTO expression increases after UUO, and FTO deficiency attenuates the fibrogenic responses induced by UUO.22,23,24,25 We previously reported that FTO upregulates glutamine transporter SLC1A5, a ferroptosis activator, in VHL-deficient clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) cells,26 suggesting that FTO promotes ferroptosis in ccRCC cells. In addition, we found that ROS production was upregulated after UUO in mice, consistent with a promoting role of ferroptosis in RF.27 In this study, we determined the effects of genetic or pharmacological inhibition of FTO on ferroptosis and UUO-induced RF using multiple pre-clinical models. Moreover, we identified molecular targets of FTO, including the ferroptosis activator ACSL428 and profibrotic macrophage inducer TGF-induced protein (TGFBI),29 that reveal the underlying mechanisms of FTO action in RF.

Results

Ferroptosis genes are enriched in UUO-induced differentially expressed genes

We previously performed gene expression profiling on mouse kidneys after UUO over a time course of 7 days.30 Here, we determined whether the 501 ferroptosis-associated genes (drivers, suppressors, and markers) annotated in FerrDb,31,32 a manually curated ferroptosis database, were enriched in differentially expressed genes that we previously identified between sham-operated control (SOC) vs. UUO tissues by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analysis. Enrichment was first observed at 5 h after UUO initiation and persisted for 3 days, with the greatest enrichment at 12 h based on normalized enrichment score, nominal p value, and false discovery rate q value (Figure 1A). Of the core genes identified by GSEA that contributed the most to the enrichment at each time point, 75 were found in at least two of the five time points (5 h, 7 h, 12 h, 1 day, and 3 days) (Table S1). Supervised hierarchical clustering of these genes demonstrated that a subset of known ferroptosis drivers was significantly upregulated at the early time points (5–12 h) including the ATF4-CHAC1 pathway,33,34,35,36 ACSL4,28 and HMOX1,37 whereas other genes such as ASNS38 and TNFAIP339 were massively upregulated at much later time points (1–7 days) (Figure 1B). The upregulation of core ferroptosis genes was validated in an independent dataset GSE118341 consisting of transcriptomic (GSE118339) (Figure 1C) and proteomic (GSE126182) (Figure 1D) profiles of kidney tissues from mice subjected to UUO for 3, 7, and 14 days.40 These results demonstrated that ferroptosis-associated transcriptional program was induced after UUO in mice, which may contribute to RF.Figure 1Ferroptosis genes are enriched in UUO-induced differentially expressed genes(A) GSEA analysis showed significant enrichment of ferroptosis genes from 5 h to 3 days after UUO initiation.(B–D) (B) Supervised hierarchical clustering of 75 core genes identified by GSEA that contributed the most to the enrichment at each time point; a subset of core ferroptosis genes was upregulated at transcript (C) and protein (D) levels in an independent dataset GSE118341 from mice subjected to UUO for 3, 7, and 14 days.

FTO inhibition protects against UUO-induced ferroptosis and RF in vivo

FTO protein expression was significantly upregulated in UUO mice compared with SOC in GSE126182 (Figure 2A). In our cohort, FTO protein expression was increased by 4-fold determined by western blot 14 days after UUO compared with SOC (Figure 2B). We performed double immunofluorescent staining using antibodies against FTO and E-cadherin to verify that FTO upregulation after UUO occurred in kidney epithelial cells. The merged images demonstrated an intense FTO signal (red) in E-cadherin-positive cells (green) in UUO kidney tissues. At the same time, little expression was observed in sham control kidney tissues, suggesting that FTO functions in epithelial cells to mediate changes induced by UUO (Figure S1A). We also examined the protein expression changes in other m6A modification enzymes in GSE126182 proteomics dataset. Compared with the control, m6A demethylase ALKBH5 expression did not change significantly after UUO. In addition, m6A methyltransferase METTL3 was also significantly upregulated at days 3, 7, and 14 (Figure S1B), whereas METTL14 expression was not reported in this dataset. We decided to focus on FTO because we previously reported that FTO upregulated glutamine transporter SLC1A5, a ferroptosis activator, in VHL-deficient ccRCC cells,26 suggesting a promoting role of FTO in renal cell ferroptosis. To determine the effects of pharmacological FTO inhibition on UUO-induced ferroptosis and RF, wild-type (WT) mice that had undergone surgically induced UUO were treated with 6 mg/kg FB23-2 (a potent FTO-specific inhibitor at a dose sufficient to suppress acute myeloid leukemia progression in mice46,47) and/or 10 mg/kg Lipro-1 (a well-known ferroptosis inhibitor11,48,49) via daily intraperitoneal injection (Figure 2C). Treatment with Lipro-1 or FB23-2 significantly decreased UUO-induced collagen deposition determined by sirius red staining (Figure 2D) and hydroxyproline assay (Figure 2E) and lipid peroxidation evaluated by 4-HNE (4-Hydroxynonenal) staining (Figure 2D) and malondialdehyde (MDA) assay (Figure 2F).50 In addition, the transcript level of LCN2 (also known as NGAL), an established biomarker of AKI,51 was significantly associated with FTO transcript level in a cohort of 85 normal kidney cortex tissues available at the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Portal (Figure 2G). LCN2 expression was significantly decreased in UUO kidney tissues after FB23-2 or Lipro-1 treatment (Figure 2H). These results suggested that pharmacological FTO inhibition protected against UUO-induced RF and kidney damage by suppressing ferroptosis in vivo. Notably, FB23-2 treatment attenuated upregulation of hydroxyproline (Figure 2E) and LCN2 (Figure 2H) to a significantly larger degree than Lipro-1, suggesting that FB23-2 protects against RF through other mechanisms in addition to ferroptosis inhibition.

To determine the effects of genetic FTO inhibition on UUO-induced ferroptosis and RF, we generated kidney epithelial cell-specific FTO knockout (KO) mice by breeding mice carrying Ksp promoter-driven Cre and loxP-flanked FTO (Figure 2I).52 FTO protein level was dramatically decreased in FTO KO mouse kidney tissues compared with FTO WT kidneys (Figure S1D). The residual FTO protein was likely expressed by other cell types in the kidney tissue such as fibroblasts. As expected, FTO KO mice kidneys showed significantly lower levels of collagen deposition and hydroxyproline level compared with mice with WT FTO after 14 days of UUO (Figures 2J and 2K). In addition, lipid peroxidation evaluated by MDA assay was significantly lower in FTO KO mice compared with FTO WT mice after UUO (Figure 2L). Moreover, ferroptosis activator ACSL4 expression was significantly lower in FTO KO mice compared with WT mice after UUO (Figure 2J). These results demonstrated that genetic inhibition of FTO protected against UUO-induced ferroptosis and RF.

FTO inhibition suppresses ferroptosis in kidney epithelial cells through ROS reduction

In primary mouse kidney epithelial cells generated by enzymatic digestion (Figure 3A), treatment with 2 μM FB23-2 or 5 μM Lipro-1 effectively alleviated ferroptosis induced by 1 μM erastin, a potent ferroptosis inducer (Figures 3B and 3C).53 In human HK2 cells, an immortalized proximal tubule epithelial cell line from normal adult human kidney,54 FB23-2, reversed erastin-induced ferroptosis to a significantly greater degree than Lipro-1 with an EC50 of 1.19 μM (Figures 3D and S2A), indicating that FB23-2 is more potent in suppressing ferroptosis in HK2 cells. Similarly, erastin-induced plasma membrane damage measured by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay was completely blocked by FB23-2 or Lipro-1 treatment (Figure 3E). FB23-2 also suppressed ferroptosis induced by GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 (Figure S2B), indicating a broad protective effect of FTO inhibition on ferroptosis. Moreover, FB23-2 and Lipro-1 reversed erastin-induced ROS upregulation (Figure 3F), suggesting FTO inhibition suppressed ferroptosis by decreasing ROS production in kidney epithelial cells. The Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity assay showed that FB23-2 is not an ROS scavenger itself (Figure S2C), suggesting that FB23-2 suppresses ROS production through other mechanisms than directly trapping ROS. In three independent FTO KO HK2 clones generated using CRISPR-Cas9 technology, FTO protein expression was abolished as shown by western blot (Figure 3H). FTO KO HK2 cells showed significant resistance to erastin-induced cell death (Figures 3I and 3J) and cell membrane damage in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3K). FB23-2 had little effect on erastin-induced cell death in FTO KO cells, while showing significant protection in FTO WT cells (Figure S2D), demonstrating an FTO-dependent protective effect of FB23-2 on ferroptosis in HK2 cells. Moreover, the level of lipid peroxidation (Figure 3L) and ROS production (Figure 3M) after erastin treatment was significantly lower in FTO KO cells compared with WT cells, suggesting that genetic inhibition of FTO protects against ferroptosis in HK2 cells by preventing lipid peroxidation. Finally, both pharmacological and genetic inhibition of FTO significantly decreased the transcript level of LCN2 in HK2 cells (Figures 3N and O) and LCN2 protein expression was also diminished in FTO KO cells (Figure 3P). These results suggest that FTO inhibition protects against kidney injury by alleviating ROS-mediated ferroptosis damage to the kidney epithelial cells.

Proteomic analysis identifies FTO targets in HK2 cells

The global m6A level was significantly increased in FTO KO HK2 cells as determined by an ELISA assay, whereas FTO overexpression showed the opposite effect (Figures S2E and S2F), suggesting that FTO regulates cellular functions through m6A demethylation in HK2. We performed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomic analysis and identified 553 and 398 proteins that were significantly up- and downregulated, respectively, after FB23-2 treatment in HK2 cells (Figure 4A, Table S2). In addition, 921 and 785 proteins were upregulated, whereas 671 and 670 were downregulated, respectively, in two independent FTO KO HK2 cell lines, i.e., sgFTO2 and sgFTO3 (Figures 4B and 4C, Table S3). As a positive control, FTO protein level was significantly downregulated in both sgFTO2 and sgFTO3 HK2 cells (Table S3). Moreover, 161 and 84 proteins were found in all three lists of up- or downregulated proteins, respectively (Figures 4D and 4E, Table S4). More than 60% of proteins up- or downregulated in sgFTO2 and sgFTO3 cells were in common, whereas overlapping between FB23-2 and FTO KO-induced changes ranged from 29% to 48%, suggesting a demethylase-independent function of FTO or off-target effects of FB23-2 in HK2 cells. ALKBH5 expression was downregulated after FTO knockdown but upregulated after FB23-2 treatment. METTL3 was downregulated only in one of the two FTO knockdown HK2 lines, with no change observed in the other line or after FB23-2 treatment. METTL14 expression was not altered after FTO knockdown or FB23-2 treatment. Proteomics analysis revealed that the classical ferroptosis suppressor GPX4 was significantly upregulated in FTO knockdown HK2 cells but not in those treated with FB23-2. Conversely, the classical ferroptosis driver TFRC was markedly downregulated following FTO knockdown, whereas it was upregulated in FB23-2-treated HK2 cells, possibly due to off-target effects of FB23-2. ACSL4 is the only classical ferroptosis driver that was downregulated upon FTO inhibition, whether genetically or pharmacologically. To determine whether the reduction in GPX4 and TFRC protein expression was caused by FTO knockdown-induced decrease at transcript levels of GPX4 and TFRC, we quantified the transcript levels of GPX4 and TFRC by qPCR and observed a significant increase in GPX4 transcript level (Figure S3A) and a significant decrease in TFRC at both transcript and protein levels (Figure S3B). These data suggest that FTO knockdown suppresses classical ferroptosis driver ACSL4 and TFRC while enhancing the classic ferroptosis suppressor GPX4. These changes were not observed with FB23-2 treatment, possibly due to the non-specific inhibition of other enzymes by FB23-2.56

Enrichment analysis provided by FerrDb revealed that proteins upregulated but not downregulated by FTO inhibition were significantly enriched with ferroptosis genes (markers, drivers, suppressors, and unclassified genes) (Figure 4F). Notably, the magnitude of enrichment shown by adjusted p value was much higher for ferroptosis suppressors than drivers (Figure 4F), consistent with the overall protective effects of FTO inhibition against ferroptosis in HK2 cells. Moreover, pathway analysis using DAVID57 identified 67, 97, and 79 KEGG pathways that were significantly enriched with proteins upregulated by FB23-2, sgFTO2, and sgFTO3, respectively, with 50 pathways found in common among all three conditions (Table S5). A number of the enriched pathways have been implicated in ferroptosis regulation such as glutathione metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and HIF-1 (hypoxia-inducible factor 1) signaling pathways, and ferroptosis was also selected as an enriched pathway in FB23-2 and sgFTO2 group (Figure 4G). These results suggest that FTO inhibition may suppress ferroptosis in HK2 cells through multiple pathways.

FTO inhibition protects against ferroptosis by directly downregulating ACSL4

We compared the common differentially expressed proteins after genetic and pharmacologic inhibition of FTO in HK2 cells (Table S4) to ferroptosis drivers annotated in the manually curated ferroptosis database FerrDb. ACSL4 is the only classic ferroptosis driver downregulated upon FTO inhibition either genetically or pharmacologically. In addition, five of the ferroptosis suppressors were upregulated upon FTO inhibition, including ARF6, OTUB1, P4HB, PRDX6, and RRM2. The well-known ferroptosis suppressor GPX4 was significantly upregulated in FTO knockdown but not in FB23-2-treated cells. A classic ferroptosis driver, TFRC, was significantly downregulated after FTO knockdown but upregulated in FB23-2-treated cells, possibly due to off-target effects of FB23-2. Taken together, we prioritized ACSL4 as a target of FTO for further investigation. In FTO KO HK2 cells, protein levels of FTO and ACSL4 were significantly decreased as determined by proteomic analysis and validated by western blot (Figures 5A and 5B), suggesting that ACSL4 is a target of FTO. In addition, FB23-2 treatment completely abolished the upregulation of ACSL4 protein expression upon erastin treatment in FTO WT HK2 cells (Figure 5C). In addition, ACSL4 mRNA level determined by qPCR analysis after FB23-2 treatment was significantly decreased (Figure 5D), suggesting that FTO regulates ACSL4 expression at the transcript level. Consistently, in the cohort of 85 normal kidney cortex tissues at the GTEx Portal, ACSL4 and FTO transcript levels were positively associated (Figure 5E). Moreover, FTO KO enabled HK2 cells to resist erastin-induced ferroptosis, whereas ACSL4 overexpression increased their sensitivity to erastin-induced ferroptosis (Figure 5F). Several m6A modification sites within the ACSL4 transcript were identified using SRAMP, a computational predictor of mammalian m6A sites (Figure 5G).58 FTO KO significantly upregulated m6A levels at site 1,036 determined by site-specific m6A qPCR (Figure 5H), whereas the upregulation at site 481 approached significance for each knockdown target sequence (p = 0.07 and 0.09) (Figure S3C), suggesting that FTO directly regulates ACSL4 expression by demethylating its transcript. MS-based lipidomic analysis identified a panel of lipids that were significantly up- or downregulated in HK2 cells by at least 1.5-fold upon genetic (Figure 5I) or pharmacological inhibition of FTO (Figure 5J). Common downregulated lipids (Figure 5K, Table S6) included known species whose synthesis was affected by ACSL4 such as PE 18:1_20:5 and PE 18:1_22:6 (Figure S3D). These results demonstrated that FTO inhibition protected against ferroptosis in HK2 cells by directly demethylating the ACSL4 transcript, which in turn downregulated polyunsaturated lipid levels required for ferroptosis.

FTO inhibition decreases profibrotic macrophages by directly downregulating TGFBI

TGFBI, a known chemotaxin for monocytes that can differentiate into profibrotic M2 macrophages in the kidney,29 is one of the top five proteins downregulated by FB23-2 and significantly decreased in FTO KO HK2 cells based on proteomic analysis (Figure 6A), suggesting that FTO inhibition may protect against kidney fibrosis (KF) by downregulating TGFBI. Consistently, TGFBI protein was significantly upregulated in the GSE126182 dataset after 3, 7, and 14 days of UUO compared with sham control (Figure 6B). In human tissues, TGFBI transcript levels showed a significant positive correlation with the FTO transcript levels in a cohort of 85 normal kidney cortex tissues from GTEx (Figure 6C) and a cohort of 44 diseased transplant donor kidneys (GSE217427) (Figure 6D). Moreover, TGFBI transcript level was significantly decreased in FTO KO HK2 cells compared with control (Figure 6E). Treatment with 10 ng/mL transforming growth factor β drastically upregulated TGFBI at the transcript level, whereas FTO KO significantly attenuated this upregulation (Figure 6E). Interestingly, FB23-2, but not Lipro-1, treatment significantly suppressed TGFBI expression at the transcript level after UUO in mice (Figure 6F), suggesting ferroptosis-independent regulation of TGFBI by FTO. In our previous UUO dataset, TGFBI transcript level was upregulated as early as 7 h after UUO together with the monocyte marker CD14 and the pan-macrophage marker CD68 (Figure 6H). Notably, FB23-2 but not Lipro-1 treatment diminished UUO-induced increase in CD206 transcript level (Figure 6J), consistent with the ferroptosis-independent regulation of TGFBI by FTO. Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed that TGFBI protein level and the number of CD206+ macrophages increased in mouse kidneys after UUO, and either pharmacological or genetic inhibition of FTO attenuated this increase, whereas Lipro-1 treatment showed little effect (Figure 6K). These results suggest that besides suppressing ferroptosis, FTO inhibition protected against KF by downregulating TGFBI, which in turn reduces the M2 macrophage population and its profibrotic effects after UUO.

FTO inhibition protects against ferroptosis in hESC-derived kidney organoids by downregulating ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis

Human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived kidney organoids exhibit a remarkable degree of similarity with the native human kidney tissue in terms of cell type, morphology, and function.59,60,61 We generated hESC-derived kidney organoids using an established protocol with modification entailing treatment with the GSK-3 inhibitor CHIR99021 followed by FGF9 induction (Figure 7A).62,63 Immunofluorescence staining using antibodies specific to different cell types detected proximal and distal tubules as well as podocytes in the organoids (Figure 7B). qRT-PCR demonstrated decreased expression of stem cell markers such as Sox2 and increased kidney epithelial parkers such as Pax8 during the differentiation period of 20 days (Figure 7C). We then generated FTO KO hESC cells using CRISPR-Cas9 system and derived kidney organoids using the same protocol. Both ACSL4 and TGFBI expression were significantly decreased in FTO KO organoids, confirming that FTO inhibition downregulates ACSL4 and TGFBI expression in hESC-derived kidney organoids (Figure 7D). FB23-2 treatment completely reversed erastin-induced cell membrane damage determined by LDH assay similar to Lipro-1 (Figure 7E), attenuated erastin-induced lipid peroxidation determined by MDA assay (Figure 7F), and decreased ROS production in FTO WT kidney organoids (Figure 7G), indicating that FTO inhibition protects kidney organoids from ferroptosis by decreasing ACSL4-mediated lipid peroxidation and ROS production. Finally, FTO inhibition significantly reduced TGFBI and LCN2 expression at the transcript level as determined by qPCR (Figures 7H and 7I), indicating a protective role of FTO inhibition in hESC-derived kidney organoids. Taken together, our results suggest that FTO may promote RF after UUO by upregulating ACSL4 and TGFBI, which in turn promotes ferroptosis and increases the profibrotic M2 macrophage population, respectively (Figure 7J). Discussion

Our study identified FTO as a key mediator of UUO-induced RF by promoting ferroptosis and profibrotic M2 macrophage populations. Inhibition of FTO, either pharmacologically or genetically, protects against UUO-induced RF by directly downregulating ACSL4 and TGFBI, two vital genes driving ferroptosis and upregulating M2 macrophage accumulation, respectively, at the transcript level by modulating m6A methylation. Our study employed multiple in vitro and in vivo models to elucidate the role of FTO in ferroptosis and RF, including a highly realistic hESC-derived kidney organoid model, therefore providing a strong rationale for targeting FTO as a therapeutic strategy to alleviate RF in patients at risk for obstruction-related kidney injury. Given the important clinical applications that effective FTO inhibitors could have in human diseases, great efforts have been made to identify and develop selective FTO inhibitors in the past years, such as small molecule inhibitor CS1 and PROTAC degrader QP73.64,65 Our results will accelerate the clinical testing of these FTO inhibitors not only in RF but also potentially in other fibrotic diseases.

Several studies have implicated FTO in ferroptosis in various cell types after acute injury. For example, FTO overexpression impairs dihydroartemisinin-induced ferroptosis in hepatic stellate cells and alleviates liver fibrosis in mice,66,67 indicating an inhibitory role of FTO in dihydroartemisinin-induced ferroptosis in liver cells. In contrast, arbutin, a natural antioxidant, inhibits the onset of ferroptosis and ameliorates high-fat-diet-induced liver fibrosis in vivo by downregulating FTO and its target SLC7A11, a known ferroptosis driver, suggesting a promoting role of FTO in high-fat-diet-induced liver fibrosis.68 However, in brain cells, FTO knockdown increased the infarct size of brain tissues and neuronal damage in ischemic stroke by promoting ferroptosis,69 suggesting a protective role of FTO in neuronal cell ferroptosis. The cell type-specific role of FTO in ferroptosis may be due to the differential impact of m6A modification on the expression of different, or sometimes even the same, ferroptosis regulators in different cell types. For example, in kidney epithelial cells, FTO inhibition increased m6A modification in ACSL4 transcripts, resulting in reduced transcript level and protein expression of ACSL4, whereas in a mouse model of ischemic stroke, FTO upregulation decreased m6A level in ACSL4 transcript and also reduced ACSL4 protein expression.69 It seems that the balance between the positive and negative impact of m6A modification on ACSL4 transcript levels in different cell types determines the net up- or downregulation of ACSL4 protein expression and the effects of FTO on ferroptosis in different cell types.70

Several recent studies suggest that FTO upregulation suppresses ferroptosis by downregulating ferroptosis drivers such as ACSL4 and TFRC or upregulating ferroptosis suppressors such as GPX4, SLC7A11, and NRF2 in colorectal cancer, liver IRI, doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, and lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung injury.71,72,73,74 However, a number of reports demonstrate a promoting role of FTO in ferroptosis by downregulating GPX4, NRF2, SLC7A11, and ACSL3 in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Parkinson disease, oral squamous cell carcinoma, and thyroid cancer.75,76,77,78 High FTO and ferroptosis are also associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and diabetic cataracts.79,80 Therefore, there is abundant literature documenting that FTO can promote or attenuate ferroptosis depending on several factors including cell type, tissue type, specific injuries, and interventions. These results indicate that the role of FTO in ferroptosis is highly context dependent.

Mechanistically, FTO can up- or downregulate different ferroptosis drivers/suppressors or even the same ferroptosis regulator in a context-dependent manner. For instance, FTO promotes GPX4 expression in colorectal cancer and ischemic stroke while inhibiting GPX4 expression in oral squamous cell carcinoma.69,72,77 Similarly, FTO enhances NRF2 expression to alleviate doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity while impairing NRF2 expression to promote ferroptosis in Parkinson disease.73,78 Interestingly, FTO regulates the expression of these ferroptosis suppressors by reducing the m6A level of their transcripts, suggesting that decreased m6A level in the transcripts of these genes may confer either increased or decreased protein expression. Alternatively, FTO may regulate the m6A level of other proteins, such as BAP1 and MDM2, which indirectly affect the expression of SLC7A11 in Parkinson disease and Staphylococcus aureus-induced osteomyelitis, respectively.75,81

The mechanism by which FTO regulates ACSL4 expression is still under investigation. Jin et al. demonstrated that FTO knockdown increased the m6A level in ACSL4 transcript, which was recognized by the m6A reader YTHDF3, leading to increased ACSL4 mRNA stability and protein expression in ischemic stroke-induced neuronal injury.69 Similarly, FTO deficiency in older livers exacerbates ferroptosis during IRI by upregulating the m6A level in ACSL4 transcript recognized by the m6A reader YTHDC2, resulting in increased ACSL4 mRNA stability and protein expression,71 although in hepatic fibrosis, the m6A reader YTHDF2 promoted ACSL4 transcript stability and protein expression by recognizing m6A.82 Consistently, in our study, FTO knockdown increased the m6A level in ACSL4 transcript; however, it led to a decreased ACSL4 protein expression through an unknown mechanism, suggesting that increased m6A level in ACSL4 transcript may confer either increased or decreased protein expression as described above for GPX4 and NRF2. These findings underscore the complexity of ferroptosis regulation by FTO in diverse cell types through key components of the pathway.

FTO is expressed by M2 macrophages, and FTO knockdown suppresses M2 macrophage polarization in mice,83 suggesting that FTO directly regulates M2 macrophage abundance in mice. However, our data strongly suggest that reduced M2 macrophage infiltration is largely due to the downregulation of TGFBI in an m6A-dependent manner. Since FTO KO is epithelial cell specific in the mouse kidneys, this rules out the possibility that the reduction in M2 macrophage infiltration in FTO knockdown mice is due to a loss of function of FTO in macrophages. CD206/MCR1+ M2 profibrotic macrophages have been shown to augment fibrosis in both human and experimental diseases.84,85,86 TGFBI, ubiquitously detected in normal human tissues including the kidney, has been associated with several diseases such as fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, and diabetic kidney disease, as well as an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in cancer, partly attributed to its promoting role in monocyte chemotaxis and M2 macrophage differentiation.29,87,88 Our results revealed a strong association between TGFBI transcript and protein levels with the expression of profibrotic CD206/MRC1+ CD163+ M2 macrophage markers in UUO kidney tissues. This may explain the superior protective effects of FTO inhibitor FB23-2 on UUO-induced RF compared with the ferroptosis inhibitor Lipro-1. Additional FTO-regulated molecular targets and cellular processes implicated in UUO-induced RF include SQSTM1 in autophagy,41 TASK-2 in cell cycle,24 and TGF-β1/RUNX1 and lncRNA GAS5 in epithelial-mesenchymal transition.23,25 These observations provided a strong rationale for developing FTO-targeted therapeutics to combat RF from multiple fronts.

Kidney organoids derived from hESCs contain segmented structures with podocytes, proximal tubules, and distal tubules in nephron-like arrangements that recapitulate kidney development and enable the modeling of complex kidney diseases.60,89,90 In particular, kidney organoids have served as an in vitro platform to elucidate mechanisms of AKI and RF with great success.59,91 Recently, it has been reported that orthotopic transplantation under the kidney capsule enables the generation of kidney organoid xenografts with improved anatomic maturity and vascularization, making them the most clinically relevant in vivo models for elucidating molecular mechanisms driving kidney diseases and for the development of pharmacotherapies.92,93,94 In the future, we will investigate the effects of FTO inhibition on ferroptosis and RF using organoid-derived xenografts in vivo to facilitate the clinical translation of our results to patient care.

Limitations of the study

Several additional investigations are needed to address the limitations of our study. First, single-cell RNA sequencing could be performed to confirm the kidney tubule cell-specific knockdown of FTO and the cellular source of FTO upregulation. Second, the role of FTO in AKI and RF could be evaluated in additional acute kidney injuries, such as ischemia-reperfusion. Third, whether FTO expression is correlated with RF in human kidney tissues needs further validation. Finally, more potent and specific FTO inhibitors than FB23-2 are needed for FTO-targeted therapy to ameliorate AKI-induced RF.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse UUO model

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Stanford University. C57/BL6 female mice aged 8–12 weeks were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment within the Animal Care Facility of Stanford University. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Stanford University (Protocol No. 30864). We surgically induced UUO by applying a nontraumatic microvascular clamp on the left ureter in these mice as previously described.27 Sham-operated mice underwent laparotomy and ureteral mobilization without clamping. Left kidneys were harvested 14 days after obstruction. They were bisected and one half was fixed in formalin overnight for subsequent sectioning and the other half was frozen quickly on crushed dry ice before being transferred to a −80°C freezer for RNA and protein isolation. Blood from UUO and control mice was also collected at the end of the experiment.

Therapeutic treatment of mice

WT 6–8-week-old female mice that had undergone surgically induced UUO were randomized based on weight into 3 groups (n=6/group) and treated with either vehicle, FB23-2, a potent FTO-specific inhibitor, or Lipro-1, an effective ferroptosis inhibitor, via daily intraperitoneal injection for 14 days. SOC treated with the vehicle was used as control. A total of 24 mice were used. The number of mice used was determined based on a pilot study showing a near-significant difference with the same treatments using three mice per group. All mice were included in the analysis since no mouse experienced weight loss >=20% which was the exclusion criteria set a priori. All mice were housed in the same location (room and rack) and treated at the same time each day to minimize variations.

Conditional FTO KO model

Female mice with conditional KO of the FTO gene in kidney tubule cells were generated through the crossing of KSP-1 Cre mice (Strain #:012237, The Jackson Laboratory) with FTO-Loxp mice (Strain #:034855, The Jackson Laboratory). Genotyping was done by PCR.

Cell culture and treatment

Primary renal epithelial cells were generated from kidney tissues excised from 8- to 12-wk-old C57/BL6 female mice and cultured in Renal Epithelial Cell Growth Medium 2 (PromoCell C-26030) as previously described.27 HK2 cells were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS. For treatments, cells were seeded in 96-well plates in 200 μL medium and treated with 1 μM erastin, 5 μM Lipro-1 or 2 μM FB23-2 for 24 hours. Cell viability was determined using a Cell Titer Glo kit (Promega G9242).

Generation of human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived kidney organoids

hESC H1 cells (gift from Dr. Ang at Stanford University) authenticated and free of mycoplasma infection, were used to generate kidney organoids by applying an established kidney organoid production protocol with modification.62,63 Briefly, at 40-50% confluency, H1 cells cultured in mTeSR medium (Stem Cell Technologies) and Matrigel-coated culture dishes (Corning) were differentiated into metanephric mesenchyme cells which include nephron progenitor cells by treatment with 8 μM GSK-3 inhibitor CHIR99021 (to Day 4, R&D) followed by 200 ng/mL FGF9 induction (to Day 9, PeproTech). Metanephric mesenchyme cells were then be transferred into suspension culture in 6-well ultra-low attachment plates and further differentiated into kidney organoids through intermediate stages of peritubular aggregates (day 11) and renal vesicles (day 14).

Generation of FTO KO cells by CRISPR-Gas9. FTO KO HK2 and hESC cells were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 system. Three sgFTO sequences were used (Target1: CGGGACCTGGTTAGGATCCA; Target2: CCTACAAGTACCTGAACACC Target3: CCAGGCTCTTTACGGTCCCC) (ABM Goods Inc). The dCas9 construct was obtained from Addgene.100 All lentiviruses were generated using a third-generation system. Briefly, 293T cells were cultured in the presence of chloroquine diphosphate at a final concentration of 50 μM for 5 hours. Lentiviral constructs (17 μg) were mixed with pMDL(11 μg), VSV-G (6 μg), and pREV (4 μg) in HEPES buffered saline with CaCl2 at a final concentration of 0.5M, and the resulting DNA-calcium phosphate co-precipitate was added onto 293T cells. After incubating overnight, the medium was removed and replenished. Two days later, medium containing lentiviruses was collected and concentrated using a PEG-it kit (LV810A-1, System Biosciences). HK2 cells were transduced with dCas9 lentiviral particles in medium containing polybrene at a concentration of 8 μg/mL for 3 days. Lentiviral particles carrying pX333 plasmids containing the FTO guide RNAs were then transduced into HK2 and hESC cells expressing dCas9.

Method details

Western blot

Total protein extraction was performed on frozen kidney tissues or cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (10mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0+1mM EDTA+0.5mM EGTA+1% Triton X-100+0.1% Sodium Deoxycholate+0.1% SDS+140mM NaCl) containing protease inhibitors. Immunoblot analysis was conducted using the following antibodies according to standard techniques as previously described.26

Proteomic profiling by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)

Proteomic profiling in HK2 cells was performed using an Orbitrap Tribrid Eclipse mass spectrometer, equipped with an Orbitrap mass analyzer as previously described.101,102 Briefly, proteins were extracted using a lysis buffer containing 12.5 mM Tris pH 8.0 (Fisher Scientific), 0.5 mM EDTA (EMD Inc.), 7.5 M urea (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1× protease inhibitor and protein concentrations were determined by BCA protein assay according to manufacturer’s instruction (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). 25 μg of protein were reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting tryptic peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid (50 μl, Fisher Scientific) and fractionated using a Dionex Ultimate Rapid Separation liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before being analyzed on an Orbitrap Tribrid Eclipse mass spectrometer, equipped with an Orbitrap mass analyzer. Unprocessed data are available via ProteomeXchange with the identifier PXD051362.

Proteomic data analysis

Following LC-MS analysis, the raw data collected for each cell line was searched using Byonic 4.0.12 against the Swiss-Prot human database (2022, 20644 entries), including 10 ppm of precursor tolerance, and 0.5 Da for fragment tolerance along with fixed carbamidomethyl cysteine, variable methionine oxidation, and deamidated asparagine, and allowing up to two missed cleavages. Peptides with a false discovery rate (FDR) greater than 1% were excluded. Then we perform a relative quantification at the peptide level to integrate them into protein quantification using a weighted average and adjusting each sample protein quantification to a normal distribution of average 0 and standard deviation of 1 N(0,1). Statistical significance was assessed using the Student's T-test, with a stringent cutoff set at a p-value of less than 0.01 to identify proteins of interest for further analysis.

Lipidomic profiling by LC-MS

This assay was performed at UCLA Lipidomic Core as previously described.103 Specifically, the assay was built on the Sciex Lipidyzer Platform which employed a SCIEX 5500 triple-quadrupole (QQQ) with a Shimadzu auto-sampler configured for direct infusion experiments, SelexION ion mobility device, and Shimadzu LC. An in-house data analysis workflow utilizing the mathematical modeling-based isotopic spectral analysis and a combination of 70 lipid standards sourced from Sciex and Avanti Polar Lipids were used to provide accurate, quantitative measurements of over 1400 lipid species across 17 lipid subclasses within a biological sample.

Gene-specific m6A qPCR

Transcripts with m6A modification were enriched with immunoprecipitation using m6 A antibody as previously described.104 qRT-PCR was performed to assess the relative abundance of the selected mRNA in m6A antibody-IP samples and input samples using the following gene-specific primers. The enrichment of m6A mRNA was calculated by the IP mRNA divided by the input mRNA (input %).

Real-time quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR reactions were performed as previously described.30

Collagen level determination by sirius red staining and hydroxyproline assay

Collagen deposition in mouse kidney tissues was determined by Sirius red staining using a Picro Sirius Red Stain Kit according to manufacturer’s instructions (Abcam: ab150681). Briefly, 5-micron-thick formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and hydrated in distilled water before Picro Sirius Red Solution was applied to completely cover the tissue section and incubated for 60 minutes. Sections were then rinsed quickly in 2 changes of acetic acid solution and dehydrated in absolute alcohol. Sections were then mounted and visualized under a standard light microscope. Hydroxyproline from mouse kidney tissues was quantified using a modified assay based on hydrochloric acid (HCl) in Ehrlich's solution as previously described.27 Briefly, kidney tissues were homogenized in 1 mL water for 30 s using a handheld homogenizer, and solid components were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid. After baking at 110°C overnight in HCl, samples were reconstituted in water, and hydroxyproline content was measured using a colorimetric chloramine T assay (Sigma MAK008).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

MDA levels were quantified through a controlled reaction using a thiobarbituric acid kit (TBARS (TCA Method) Assay Kit, Cayman: Item No. 700870) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, 25 mg of tissues or 20 million cells were homogenized in 250 μl RIPA lysis buffer and mixed with 100 μl of TCA Assay Reagent and 800 μl of the color Reagent. After the reaction was complete, the absorbance was measured at 535 nm. RIPA lysis buffer without tissues or cells was used as a control. All samples were evaluated in triplicates. MDA levels from each sample were calculated from a standard curve.

ROS assay

ROS level was evaluated by the Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Abcam: ab113851). Briefly, 1.5 x 105 HK2 cells or cells derived from kidney organoids cultured under different conditions were incubated with the fluorogenic dye DCFDA for 45 min, and fluorescence was detected using fluorescence spectroscopy with excitation/emission at 485 nm / 535 nm. All samples were evaluated in triplicates. ROS levels from each sample were calculated from a standard curve generated using tert-Butyl hydroperoxide.

Quantification and statistical analysis

GSEA analysis

GSEA was performed using GSEA version 3.0 (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea). 501 ferroptosis-related genes from FerrDb V2 (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/) were used as the gene set, with the parameters: n = 1000 permutations, metric for ranking genes: Pearson, where p-adjust < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25 were considered significant.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism software (version 6.0, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). All data are presented as means ± SD, and statistical significance was calculated using a two-tailed paired t-test.
