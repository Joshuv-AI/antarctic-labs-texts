Ancient DNA reveals elite dynastic rule among Iron Age Eurasian Steppe nomads
Authors: Ayshin Ghalichi, Madina Seidualy, Adam B. Rohrlach, Lyazzat Musralina, Guido Gnecchi-Ruscone, Taylor Hermes, Elmira Khussainova, Nurzhibek Kahbatkyzy, Bakhytzhan Bekmanov, Olzhas Iksan, Nazym Altynova, Jonathan Tuke, Matthew Roughan, Baurzhan Baitanayev, Akhan Onggaruly, Gulmira Mukhtarova, Abdesh Toleubayev, Rinat Zhumatayev, Bakkeldi Rysbek, Zainolla Samashev, Arman Beisenov, Choongwon Jeong, Wolfgang Haak, Leyla Djansugurova, Johannes Krause, Ainash Childebayeva

ABSTRACT
The Eurasian Steppe in the first millennium BCE saw the rise of the Scytho-Siberian archaeological horizon, which would come to stretch from the Altai Mountains in the east to the Black Sea in the west. We examined the genetic profiles of Iron Age Scythians to explore how social status shaped biological relatedness and ancestry patterns. We present genome-wide data from 85 individuals (38 elite and 47 non-elite), including 45 newly sequenced individuals and the first genome-wide data for the Scythian “Golden Man.” We identify consanguineous unions, a reduced effective population size, and identity-by-descent links among the elites. Dynastic rule is supported by elite grandparent-grandchild relationships across cemeteries. While ancestries are heterogeneous, elite Iron Age Scythians show lower variation and no detectable patrilocal or matrilocal signal. These findings highlight hereditary status transmission and the emergence of social stratification in ancient nomadic societies.

INTRODUCTION

The first millennium BCE in Central Eurasia witnessed the emergence of confederations of nomadic tribes marked by increased mobility and the intensification of specialized agropastoralism as a system for the exploitation of the region’s ecological zones. This period is associated with the Iron Age Scytho-Siberian archaeological horizon (c. 900 BCE to approximately 200 BCE) characterized by animal-style (zoomorphic) imagery, equine-related artifacts, distinctive weaponry (1, 2) and ranging over a territory of more than 3500 km from the Altai mountains in the east to the Black Sea in the west. The term “Scythian,” mentioned by Herodotus, has been used broadly to refer to both the classical western Scythians of the northern Black Sea and the eastern Scythians of Central Eurasia that include diverse archaeological groups (3). We focus on the early Iron Age Scythian groups that emerged in the beginning of the first millennium BCE in Central Eurasia, including individuals associated with Tasmola, Aldy Bel, Korgantas, Saka, Pazyryk, and Sarmatian archaeological cultures (4). Here, we use the term “Saka” to refer to the Early Iron Age Scythian groups of the Tian Shan region and surrounding steppes in the middle-late first millennium BCE, although in the archaeological literature of the region, the term “Saka” is used for all archaeological cultures of the Iron Age (4). Genetically, Scythian individuals have been shown to represent an admixture of three major ancestry components: (i) the preceding Middle Late Bronze Western/Central Steppe (Steppe_MLBA) ancestry component that can be characterized by Sintashta and Andronovo-associated individuals, (ii) an MLBA Eastern Steppe pastoralist ancestry component associated with the Khovsgol Late Bronze Age individuals from northern Mongolia, and (iii) a southern Central Asian component that can be modeled using individuals of the Bronze Age Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC) of Iran and Turkmenistan (5–9). This tripartite ancestry suggests extensive population movements and admixture in the Iron Age, in comparison to the more homogeneous Bronze Age ancestries.

The Eurasian Iron Age witnessed the emergence of high-status individuals interred in large earthen burial mounds of complex construction. These monumental and elaborate graves, containing opulently adorned individuals, are widely interpreted as evidence of pronounced social stratification and displays of power across Eurasia (10). Examples from Europe include the Celtic burial mounds (11), while rich Scythian mounds (kurgans) are found throughout Central Eurasia. The elite Scythian kurgans are multilayered (often alternating layers of earth, turf, and stones of different sizes), include timber chambers, and tend to be greater than 1.5/2 m in height and 20 m in diameter (12). They often show evidence of a dromos, an entrance leading inside the mound, and some include side chambers with remains of animals and humans (12–14). The artifacts that have been found in such elite contexts consist of pottery, weaponry, equine artifacts, and jewelry, including elaborate gold objects (15). Other shared aspects of the high status of the deceased include the presence of postmortem trepanation, which was likely used to preserve the body for burial and is only found in the elite contexts (16, 17). The existence of shared zoomorphic imagery (recumbent stag, feline, panther, mountain goat, etc.) has been reported as a unifying feature of the elite individuals of the Eurasian Steppe in the Iron Age (18). In comparison, the non-elite kurgans are smaller in size (less than 1 m in height) and simpler in construction, do not have a dromos or side chambers, often do not contain burial artifacts or contain few simple artifacts, and do not show evidence of postmortem trepanation. The notable differences between the elite and non-elite burials indicate the presence of high-status individuals and marked social inequality that is more pronounced compared to the preceding Bronze Age in the region. However, questions remain about how social status was transmitted and legitimized (19). Was the transmission of power kin-based or merit-based?

Here, we assemble a dataset of 85 individuals ranging in date between 900 and 200 BCE, including 38 elite (14 new and 24 published) and 47 non-elite (34 new and 13 published) individuals (Fig. 1, Supplementary Text 1, figs. S1 to S10, and data S1 and S2). We report genome-wide data for the Scythian “Golden Man” and analyze them alongside published individuals from comparable archaeological contexts. We performed a detailed archaeological assessment of each individual in the study, including the published individuals and accounting for contexts affected by looting. Although there is a spectrum in the representation of social status, we defined an individual as being “elite” if they met three or more of the following characteristics that include a large kurgan (>1.5/2 m in height), a substantial number of gold and other artifacts, horse burials, presence of a dromos, and signs of trepanation. An individual not meeting these criteria we defined as “non-elite”. Using a mixture of population genetic analyses, we investigate the genetic structure, patterns of relatedness, evidence of inbreeding, and identity-by-descent (IBD) networks. Notably, this study provides genomic evidence of close biological relatedness among the Early Iron Age Scythian elites from the Central Eurasian Steppe, supporting the existence of dynastic social organization. Similar to the Iron Age Celtic societies in Europe, where richly furnished elite burials with evidence of genetic relatedness have been interpreted as indicators of social stratification and emerging kingship (20), our findings suggest that the early Scythian royal burials reflect hereditary status and centralized political authority.

RESULTS

Scythian population genetics

First, we projected the ancient individuals onto the variation observed in the modern Eurasian populations using smartPCA (21, 22). In general, the individuals in our study are quite heterogeneous in their genome-wide distribution based on the principal components analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2, A and B). Most of the individuals demonstrated clustering within the variation of the ancient Scythian groups, as has been shown before (6), with several examples of outlier individuals (mostly female) with higher levels of Eastern Steppe ancestry (e.g., Khovgsol BA and Ulaanzukh BA). However, when compared to each other by group, the non-elite individuals do show notably more variance along PC1 (P = 0.01), which separates western and eastern Eurasian ancestries (fig. S11A). Geographically, we also notice a trend of higher BMAC-related ancestry levels in individuals found in the south of Central Eurasia and higher Eastern Steppe/Altai MLBA ancestry found more to the east (fig. S11, B and C). On the basis of our genetic analysis, the “Golden Man” (GOM001), who we were able to assign as genetically male (Supplementary Text 2), clusters within the genetic variation of other Iron Age Saka individuals, consistent with the archaeological classification of this individual. Notably, GOM001 exhibits a slight shift toward BMAC-related ancestry along the Saka cline (Fig. 2A). Last, the Unsupervised ADMIXTURE (23) results indicate that the individuals share similar distal ancestry components present in different proportions (Fig. 2C).

Using qpAdm we were able to model 21 individuals using Altai_MLBA as a single source, which itself represents a mixture of Western Steppe Sintashta-like and Eastern Steppe Mongolia_LBA_Khovsgol-like ancestries (21). The remaining individuals were successfully modeled as a two-way or three-way mixture between the three major ancestry components: Eastern Steppe (Mongolia_LBA_Khovsgol and Ulaanzuukh_SlabGrave), Western Steppe [Srubnaya-Sintashta, Russia_Krasnoyarsk, Post_Catacomb (Caucasus), Lola (Caucasus), and Steppe_Prescythian (Caucasus)], and BMAC-related groups (BMAC, postBMAC, Iran_DinkhaTepe_BA_IA_1, Iran_ShahrISokhta_BA1, and Armenia_LBA) (Materials and Methods, data S3 to S5, and Fig. 3). We did not identify consistent differences between the elite and non-elite individuals regarding the presence and distribution of the ancestry components tested, further supporting the observations from PCA of general genetic similarity of the two groups. We could not model individuals grouped by social status, elite and non-elite, using the three-way mixture of Western Steppe, Eastern Steppe, and BMAC-related ancestries (P < 0.001). However, the elite group could be modeled as a two-way admixture between Altai-MLBA and BMAC as sources (P = 0.06) (data S3 to S5).

Biological relatedness in elites and non-elites

We tested the genetic relatedness among the individuals in our database using a combination of several methods: KIN (24), READv2 (25), and BREADR (26). On the basis of the assessment of the results of the various relatedness tests, we identified several related pairs, including six first-degree ([AKB001-NUR002], [ESZ001-ESZ003], [ESZ011-ESZ012], [KSH001-KSH003], [KZL001-KZL003], and [TAL003-TAL004]) and eight second-degree related individuals. We also found instances of identical individuals ([CSP002-CSP005], [TAL001-TAL005], and [SHI005-SHI006-SHI007]), which could be due to burial disturbance from looting, and hence merged these libraries for analysis (data S6 to S8).

To better understand the general population structure of our cohort of elite and non-elite individuals, we used HapROH (27) and assessed the distribution of the runs of homozygosity (ROHs). We identify a number of individuals who likely represent offspring of consanguineous unions (Fig. 4): SHI008 and AKB001 appear to have been the offspring of first-cousin or avunculate unions and KSH001 and KSH003 of a second-cousin union. These individuals are associated with the elite contexts. In general, however, most of the individuals do not show evidence for consanguinity (Fig. 4). Overall, we found that significantly more individuals in the non-elite group were found to have no ROH (P = 0.0188). Among the individuals with evidence of ROH, the elites were shown to carry more ROHs by an average factor of around 3.5 times (P = 0.0062). When considered together, we see that the elite individuals carry blocks of ROHs that are about six times larger, on average (P = 0.0009), than the non-elites (Fig. 4). Effective population sizes (Ne) were also calculated for elite versus non-elite individuals separately using HapROH (27). The estimate of Ne for the elite individuals was found to be 2721 (95% confidence interval: 2136 to 3563), while for the non-elite individuals, it was 9644 (95% confidence interval: 6548 to 15012).

Individuals with coverage on the 1240k sites greater than 0.5× were imputed genome-wide using GLIMPSE (28) and analyzed for shared IBD fragments using ancIBD (29). We confirmed the instances of close biological relatedness that were identified in nonimputed data and found instances of more distant biological connections (data S9). Critically, instances of relatedness between archaeological sites were found more often among the high-status individuals. On the basis of the combination of IBD and other general relatedness estimates, we were able to reconstruct a pedigree of mainly elite individuals spanning several generations and across multiple archaeological sites (Fig. 4). This is especially evident when focusing on the genetic relatedness between a grandfather AKB001 (Akbeit kurgan 1, elite male, >55 years old, postmortem trepanation, 3-m-tall and 25-m-diameter kurgan, buried with gold objects) and his grandchildren: KSH001 (Karashoky kurgan 1, elite, 1 year old, XXY, postmortem trepanation, 2-m-tall and 32-m-diameter kurgan with a dromos, looted) and KSH003 (Karashoky kurgan 6, elite male, 45 to 55 years old, looted kurgan). AKB001 was also found to be the brother of NUR002 (Nurken II kurgan 1, elite male, 35 to 45 years old, postmortem trepanation, 4.3-m-tall and 41-m-diameter kurgan with a dromos, looted), suggesting that the family of this individual held a high social status for at least three generations. These individuals were buried at different cemeteries, 50 to 140 km apart. We found more distant relatives of the fourth and fifth degree who are themselves elite males, SHI008 (Shilikty I kurgan 16, elite male, 8.6-m-tall and 88-m-diameter kurgan with a dromos, griffon jewelry), and females, AKB003 (Akbeit kurgan 7, elite female, 4 to 5 years old, postmortem trepanation, 2.5-m-tall and 21.5-m-diameter kurgan) and KSH002 (Karashoky kurgan 8, elite female, 25 to 35 years old, large kurgan with a dromos).

We further assessed the IBD connections between elite and non-elite individuals by constructing a network (Fig. 5). Individuals were connected in the network if they shared more than two IBD tracts longer than 12 centimorgans (cM) and at least one tract longer than 16 cM, indicating a relationship of approximately the seventh degree. Using exponential random graph models to perform network regression (30), we modeled the probability of individuals being genetically related (connected in the network) using the site, genetic sex, and elite status of the individuals as predictors. Using the Bayesian information criterion, we found that the best-fitting model only required elite status as a predictor and that site and genetic sex were not significant predictors. Using the best-fitting model, we could predict that compared to two non-elites, an elite individual and a non-elite individual are not likely to be related (Z = 0.356), but that two elites are ~11.3 times more likely to be related (Z = 4.24). When restricting the network to just the elite individuals, we found that the null model with no predictors fits best, indicating that site and genetic sex are still not significant predictors of genetic relatedness when only considering elite individuals. Last, we were interested in the distribution of the geographic distances between individuals who were genetically related in the network analyses. We found that related elite/elite pairs are connected over shorter geographic distances than elite/non-elite pairs (P = 0.02), but we could not compare non-elite/non-elite pairs as there was only one such example (Fig. 5B).

DISCUSSION

The emergence of monumental burial mounds has been used to suggest an increased social stratification in Central Eurasia in the Iron Age (31). There is no evidence of the same level of elite burial rites in the preceding Bronze Age, although there are known instances of burials with horses and elaborate weaponry (32). Iron Age kurgans with heights greater than 12 to 15 m and diameters up to 105 m (33) highlight the existence of a means to organize and provide resources for the large number of people needed for their construction (31). The presence of catacombs with passageways to enter the grave, side chambers containing remains of potential relatives and animals, and the evidence of postmortem trepanation and mummification only in the elite contexts further highlight the ritual importance of the burial rites in early Scythian societies (12, 34, 35).

One of the most notable archaeological findings in Central Eurasia is the discovery of the Issyk kurgans and the “Golden Man” who was buried there. The Issyk burial ground is located ~50 km east of Almaty, Kazakhstan, on the left bank of the Issyk river, containing 45 so-called royal mounds (36). The Issyk kurgans were excavated starting in 1969 and, on the basis of the artifacts, were dated to the fourth to third century BCE and associated with the Iron Age Saka culture. The main Issyk kurgan (6 m high and 60 m in diameter) consisted of a central burial and a southern burial. The central burial was looted multiple times, while the southern burial was not disturbed (36) and contained the individual later named the “Golden Man.” Radiocarbon dating firmly places the “Golden Man” (GOM001) at 400 to 300 BCE, in line with the late Iron Age Saka period. The “Golden Man” had rich burial artifacts: a wooden burial chamber, made from Tian Shan fir trees, containing more than 4000 gold ornaments, an iron sword and dagger, a bronze mirror, various pieces of pottery, and wood, silver, and bronze vessel (36). A silver bowl with 26 letters was recovered from the burial chamber, but the writing on the bowl has not been deciphered to date. The sword of the “Golden Man” was decorated with griffin heads (36). Similar griffin imagery was found at other elite Iron Age sites throughout Central Eurasia, such as Shilikty (SHI008, Kurgan 16) and Arzhan-2 (37). Briefly, kurgan 16 of Shilikty, archaeologically dated to eighth to seventh centuries BCE, was ~88 m in diameter and nearly 9 m high with three construction layers and included abundant gold artifacts, stylized after animals (see detailed elite burial descriptions in Supplementary Text 1) (38). Aristeas and Herodotus mention “gold-guarding Griffins” among the nomadic population of the Eurasian Steppe, which could be seen as a reference to the elite nomadic individuals of the Scythian era (39). We were able to place the “Golden Man” within the genetic variation of the Iron Age Saka, albeit with more pronounced BMAC-related ancestry. Because of low genome-wide coverage, more precise estimates of genetic ancestry were not feasible. However, we did estimate the genetic sex of the individual to be likely male than female, resolving a long-standing debate around the genetic sex of the “Golden Man” (40).

In general, all of the individuals in our study could be modeled as a two- or three-way admixture of three distinct ancestry sources: Eastern Steppe, Western Steppe, and BMAC-related ancestry. Moreover, we could also model some individuals using only the Altai-MLBA ancestry (a mixture of Sintashta-like and Khovsgol-like ancestries), suggesting a degree of genetic continuity in the region. These findings are in line with a previous study of the Scythian genetic profiles, further highlighting their genetic diversity across the known Eurasian genomic variation (6). When grouped, the elite and non-elite individuals could not be modeled as the mixture of the three ancestries, highlighting the heterogeneity seen in the individual ancestry modeling. However, we did observe a trend for less heterogeneity among the elites compared to the non-elites, highlighting the potential role of the elites in maintaining continuity during the time of intense genetic mixture. We also note a greater presence of females among the individuals with increased levels of Eastern Steppe ancestry (seven of nine are genetically female, mostly non-elite). This finding is supported by osteological findings (41), although less precise than genetic estimates of ancestry and admixture, and by historical sources where instances of alliance building between the ancient Chinese polities with the nomadic polities have been described as taking place through intermarriage (42). It is possible that we are seeing evidence for this practice going further back in time, perhaps emerging in the Iron Age.

Elite females, like males, likely held an important role in the society of the Iron Age Scythians. In the context of our dataset, females represent almost half (42%) of the elite group (16 of 38 individuals) and have been found buried in large kurgans with dromoi, signs of postmortem trepanation, horses, and rich burial artifacts. One notable example of an elite female is the 30- to 35-year-old “Princess of Urdzhar” (URD002) who was found in eastern Kazakhstan in a damaged 16-m-diameter kurgan revealed by a rescue excavation and later named the Tasaryk kurgan (43). She was discovered in 2013 and was dated to the fifth to fourth century BCE. The “Princess of Urdzhar” was buried wearing an elaborate gold headdress with Scythian-style zoomorphic ornaments, similar to the finds associated with the “Golden Man” (43). She was also buried with stone altar and remains of medicinal plants, which were interpreted to have a shamanistic role (Supplementary Text 1). The existence and important role of high-status females in the Scythian society are also supported by historical records of Herodotus and others (44–46).

We observe the genetic structure in the Scythian populations analyzed in the study using IBD, with the elite individuals forming a subgroup within the larger general population. This observation is supported by the higher IBD connectivity, wherein the elite individuals are more likely to be related to each other than to the non-elites. Our dataset contains sites with both elite and non-elite individuals, and we find most of the genetic relatedness among the elites from different sites compared to the elites and non-elites from the same sites and non-elites from the same sites.

The results of our ROH analysis revealed higher levels of ROH in the elite individuals, including some who were offspring of first cousins or avuncular unions. We also estimated a smaller effective population size for the elite group relative to the non-elites, suggesting a higher degree of endogamy within the elite class. We find that geographic distances among the related elite individuals were statistically shorter compared to the elite/non-elite pairings. This finding further strengthens the notion of geographical centralization for the elite burials and kin-based elite burial practices, as has been seen in the Siberian “Valley of the Kings” in Tuva, Russia (47). Furthermore, we find grandfather-grandchildren among individuals who were buried at different cemeteries but share the characteristics of being elite. We interpret this as evidence for the emergence of dynastic rule among the steppe elites of the Iron Age, which sheds light on the ways in which social status was passed down and validated in ancient nomadic societies (19).

Together, these findings suggest that among the Iron Age Scythians, the elites represented a subgroup with a higher likelihood of relatedness and dynastic continuity among its members when compared to the non-elites. The results presented here further our understanding of the rise of social inequality and differentiation among ancient Eurasian nomadic groups (48, 49) and highlight the practices of the earliest nomadic groups of Central Eurasia in the first millennium BCE. Last, our findings contribute to the body of knowledge on the emergence of social inequality in the past.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

For the purposes of this study, we defined the “elite” group using the following criteria: kurgans exceeding 1.5/2 m in height, the presence of a dromos (passageway providing access to the dead), side chambers, and inclusion of rich grave goods, which, in some cases, comprise thousands of gold objects, pottery, and weapons (50). In addition, elite individuals could be identified by the signs of postmortem trepanation, a practice associated with embalming and mummification likely performed for extended mourning (17, 51). In comparison, the non-elite burials were smaller in size (often 0.2 to 0.5 m in height), typically featured simpler construction without elaborate architectural elements, and often lacked burial artifacts or contained only a few utilitarian items such as basic pottery or iron knives. These individuals did not show evidence of postmortem trepanation, nor did they commonly include sacrificial compartments or animal remains. It is important to acknowledge that non-elite individuals may also have held significant roles within their societies. Their burial alongside the elite Scythians suggests that they, too, may have had a certain degree of social status, e.g., warriors and priestesses. Nevertheless, a distinction remains between the “elite” and other individuals when comparing burial practices and grave goods. Last, many of the elite burials have been looted in the past and, in some cases, multiple times (52). Therefore, we incorporated evidence of past looting in our elite/non-elite status assignments (data S1).

Sample processing was done in a dedicated ancient DNA laboratory following a standard protocol for ancient genomics (53, 54). All samples were processed in designated clean laboratory spaces using the uracil-DNA glycolylase (UDG)-half double-stranded and single-stranded non-UDG library protocols (55, 56). Samples were then analyzed with the 1240k capture for ~1.2 million single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across the genome (57, 58) and Twist Capture (59).

We extracted DNA and built double-stranded UDG-half and single-stranded non-UDG libraries following standard ancient DNA protocols (60, 61) for 48 new individuals (14 elite and 34 non-elite) broadly associated with the Iron Age Scythian contexts from Central Eurasia between 900 and 200 BCE. We then performed capture enrichment for 1.2 million positions in the genome (1240k and Twist Capture) (57, 59, 62) and combined our dataset with 37 published individuals from similar archaeological and cultural contexts (24 elite and 13 non-elite) (data S1). In total, we assembled a dataset of 37 elite and 48 non-elite individuals from 21 sites across Kazakhstan and Russia (Supplementary Text 1 and figs. S1 to S10). Notably, new ancient DNA data were generated for elite individuals from sites: Issyk (n = 1, GOM001 “Golden Man”), Urdzhar (n = 1, URD002 “Princess of Urdzhar”), Akbeit (n = 2, AKB002 and AKB003), Nurken (n = 1, NUR003), Shilikty (n = 2, SHI004 and SHI008), Bakybulak (n = 3, BKB001, BKB003, and BKB004), Berel [n = 2, BRE016 and BRE028 (Berel, Kurgan II)], Serekty (n = 1, SRK002), and Koktobek (n = 1, KOT002) (Fig. 1). Most sites analyzed include both elite and non-elite individuals (BKB, BRE, CSP, ESZ, KOT, KSH, KYZ, NUR, SHI, and SRK), while few contained only elite [Akbeit (AKB), Arzhan-2, Segizsay (SGZ), and Taldy (TAL)] or non-elite contexts [Birlik (BIR), Kyzyl (KZL), and Aksu], as well as single elite (BKT001, GOM001, KKM001, and URD002) burials (data S2).

Following the sequencing of the data, samples were processed using the nf-core/eager pipeline version 2 (63). Briefly, raw fastq files with sequencing data were tested for quality using FastQC (64), adapter clipping was done using Clip&Merge (65), and samples were then mapped to the human genome hs37d5 using bwa aln (66) with the ancient DNA parameters: -o 3 -n 0.001 -l 16500. We trimmed two bases from read ends of double-stranded UDG-half samples using TrimBams (67) and obtained pseudohaploid genotype calls using pileupCaller (https://github.com/stschiff/sequenceTools). For the single-stranded non-UDG data, pseudohaploid genotype calls were determined using the –singlestrandmode option in pileupCaller on untrimmed data.

The ancient DNA status of the data was confirmed using mapDamage version 2 (68) that showed typical ancient DNA damage patterns, i.e., C-T and G-A deamination patterns that are more pronounced at the ends of each read. Contamination from modern DNA sources was tested using ANGSD for males (69), contammix (70), and schmutzi (71). Moreover, unbiased sex determination was also used as a tool to confirm the authenticity of the data. To estimate biological relatedness among the samples, we used BREADR (26), READv2 (25), KIN (24), and ancIBD (72).

Newly generated samples were merged with already available published datasets for ancient DNA analysis (AADR version 62) (73). We ran smartpca (22) on the merged dataset wherein the new ancient individuals were projected upon the variation calculated using 187 modern Eurasian populations from (74, 75), together with relevant ancient populations. For estimating Eurasian genetic variation, plotting PC1 versus PC2 allows us to separate western and eastern Eurasian ancestries, whereas plotting PC1 versus PC3 better differentiates the amount of southern Central Asian ancestry.

Moreover, we ran Unsupervised ADMIXTURE (23) in the ancient individuals only while restricting the SNPs found to be associated with ancestry (76) and removing individuals with missingness greater than 0.98 using plink (77). We ran five replicates of ADMIXTURE on k-levels 1 through 20. The k-level with the lowest cross validation error rate was used for visualization.

We used qpWave and qpAdm [ADMIXTOOLS version 1520 (78)] to identify possible ancestry streams and to quantify these admixture proportions with the “allsnps: NO” parameter, which calculates all underlying f-statistics on the basis of the overlapping SNPs shared among all groups in each test. We used the following populations as the outgroups set: Mbuti.DG, Italy_Epigravettian.AG.BY.AA, Russia_YuzhniyOleniyOstrov_Mesolithic.AG, Turkey_Marmara_Barcin_N.AG, Iran_GanjDareh_N.AG, Baikal_EN, China_NEastAsia_Coastal_EN.AG, and WSHG_Botai. Models with P values greater than 0.05 and ancestry proportions between zero and one were considered feasible. In case of single-source models, the validity of the model was tested by f4-statistics of the form f4(target, model; test, Mbuti), where “test” is the remaining outgroup populations. When multiple feasible models were identified, we report the most parsimonious ones, those with fewer sources, and source populations that were spatially and temporally proximate.

We then used qpAdm to model genetic ancestries and to estimate the admixture proportions for the individuals in our study. Although Iron Age Central Eurasian individuals are dispersed in the PC space with high genetic variability, ADMIXTURE analysis indicates that they share the same distal genetic ancestry components in different proportions (Fig. 2C). To account for this variability in our proximal modeling, we included multiple groups to represent each of the three major ancestry components previously described (5, 6). A series of one-way, two-way, and three-way mixture models were tested with these three ancestry components: Eastern Steppe (Mongolia_LBA_Khovsgol and Ulaanzuukh_SlabGrave), Western Steppe [Srubnaya-Sintashta, Russia_Krasnoyarsk, Post_Catacomb (Caucasus), Lola (Caucasus), and Steppe_Prescythian (Caucasus)], and BMAC-related groups (BMAC, postBMAC, Iran_DinkhaTepe_BA_IA_1, Iran_ShahrISokhta_BA1, and Armenia_LBA) (Materials and Methods and data S3 to S5).

Using the 1240k capture data, we assigned mitochondrial and Y-chromosomal haplogroups (Y-hgs) (data S2) to individuals in our dataset to investigate patterns of maternal and paternal genetic variation. Y-hgs were determined using the method described by Rohrlach et al. (79) and according to the ISOGG (version 15.73) list of SNPs characteristic of specific Y-hgs (https://isogg.org/tree/), with assignments made for individuals with coverages greater than 50K SNPs. Mitochondrial haplogroups were assigned using Haplogrep3 (80) on the basis of the 1240k data, mapped to the Revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS) NC_012920.1.

For estimation of the genetic sex of GOM001, we calculated the ratio of sequencing reads mapping to the Y chromosome relative to the total number of reads mapping to both sex chromosomes (Ry) for one shotgun library (GOM001.A0101.SG1) and two 1240k libraries (GOM001.A0101.TF1 and GOM001.A0101.TF3) (fig. S12). For the library GOM001.A0201.RM1.1, we used an approach similar to those of Rohrlach et al. (81) and Roca-Rada et al. (82) to compare the proportion of reads mapping to the autosomes and the X and Y chromosomes, denoted p=(pA,pX,pY), to similar published data (81, 82). By modeling p using a Dirichlet prior distribution, and assuming that for a fixed p, the overall number of reads mapping to the autosomes and chromosomes X and Y, denoted N=(NA,NX,NY), follows a multinomial distribution, then the posterior distribution is the conjugate Dirichlet-Multinomial distribution, i.e., N∣p∼DM(N,p), where N=NA+NX+NY. We estimated the parameters for two prior distributions for the proportion of reads for both genetically male and female individuals, denoted pXX and pXY. We also included a case for Jacobs syndrome, XYY, using pXYY=g[(1,1,2).pXY], and Klinefelter syndrome, using pXXY=g[(1,2,1).pXY], where g() normalizes a vector to have a sum of one. Using prior classifications proportional to the frequency of Jacobs and Klinefelter syndromes (both 1:1000 “male” births), we were then able to assign a posterior probability for the assignment of GOM001 to be XX, XY, XYY, or XXY.

We used HapROH (27) to determine the ROHs in the ancient samples from Central Asia, restricting the analysis to samples with coverage of more than 400,000 SNPs on the 1240k capture. We then performed statistical analyses comparing the elite and non-elite individuals using a zero-inflated exponential distribution. Analysis details and scripts can be found here: https://github.com/BenRohrlach/ROH_tests.

Individuals with coverage greater than 0.5× were imputed using GLIMPSE (83) and following the default parameters with the 1000 Genomes dataset as a reference (84). We used ancIBD (72) to look at IBD sharing of the individuals in this together with published data from Central Asia. The IBD connections among the elite and non-elite individuals were further assessed using exponential random graph models as per (30). Data were visualized using R version 4.5.1 (85) and the packages ggplot2 version 3.5.2 (86) and ggnetwork (87) version 0.5.13.
