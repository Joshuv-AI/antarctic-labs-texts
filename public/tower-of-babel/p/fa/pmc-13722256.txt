Bottleneck analysis of community-based management of severe acute malnutrition program in the tribal state Jharkhand
Authors: Kumari Asha Kiran, Neha Priya, Manisha Kujur, Anit Kujur, Vidya Sagar, Shalini Sunderam, Reema Kumari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Throughout the world, the majority of deaths among under-five children are accredited to malnutrition. Malnutrition is a serious condition for children in India; it makes the diseases more fatal and increases the severity of the disease also. Malnutrition is the intrinsic cause of 68% of child deaths in India. Community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) is a line of action focusing on the treatment and prevention of malnutrition. CMAM has been introduced in over 60 countries throughout the world and is an important part of community mobilization pivotal to the prevention of malnutrition. This paper emphasizes the place of CMAM in India in addition to treatment and the bottlenecks faced in its utilization by beneficiaries as well as service providers.Methods:The study was conducted from April 2021 to December 2021. The study was conducted by purposive random sampling covering 305 AWCs and 16500 children of two blocks of West Singhbhum district, Jharkhand. Of these, 28 Anganwadis were visited and 1985 children were screened by monitors taken from SCOE (State Centre of Excellence for SAM management), PSM Dept, RIMSResults:One of the important findings of the study is that only 2.5% of children were suffering from severe acute malnutrition, which means that regardless of bottlenecks faced, the prevalence of malnutrition declined. Our analysis had shown bottlenecks over the four determining factors for the treatment of SAM. The main supply-side bottlenecks identified were the stock-outs of commodities (THR and medicines), nonfunctional instruments, and improperly trained health workers. The support of ANMs and the Lady Supervisor was also lacking, resulting in ineffective counseling, irregular and low-quality SAM treatment, and delivery of services.Conclusion:This study explains different bottlenecks coming in the delivery of CMAM services and provides a detailed understanding of factors guiding the application of the CMAM program for the betterment of severely malnourished children. The AWWs need to be trained more efficiently. A basic exam can be introduced to assess their functional standard. However, working conditions and remuneration of AWC workers need to be improved to bring it to par with the work given. The findings of the study are expected to help policymakers take appropriate corrective measures to make the CMAM more effective and easily applicable, thereby bringing down the rate of malnutrition.

Introduction

Worldwide about half of all deaths among under-five children were accredited to malnutrition.[1] This is transcribed into 3 million children’s lives lost every year. In our country, malnutrition still exists as an important health problem for children. India carries the burden of almost half of the world’s wasted children (i.e. 25.5 million out of 50.5 million) (Development Initiatives, 2018).[2] Malnutrition is the intrinsic cause of 68% of children’s deaths in India (Swaminathan et al., 2019).[3] This has happened despite the country’s tremendous economic boost in recent years. The national estimates indicate that there is 35.5% of stunting and 21% of wasting in India—which is much more than those across other similar developing countries ranging from 25% and 8.9%, respectively.[4] There had been a decline in the ratio of children who are underweight from 42.5% in NFHS-3 (2005–06) to 35.7% in NFHS-4 (2015–16) and 32.1% in NFHS-5 (2020–21), the amplitude of malnutrition is still high.[5]

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) can be defined as very low weight for height (i.e. < -3 z scores of the median WHO growth standards), having a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) <115 mm, by seeing signs of severe wasting or the presence of nutritional edema.[6] Decreasing child mortality and improving maternal health depend heavily on reducing malnutrition, which is responsible for major deaths among children under five years of age. Important drivers of child malnutrition in India are incomplete mothers’ diets, low birth weight (LBW), and inappropriate complementary feeding. It is calculated that only about 54.9% of infants are exclusively breastfed in the initial six months, and 9.6% of under two-year children months get a suitable diet (IIPS and ICF, 2017).[4]

Across the country, the basic level of health workers, whether they are auxiliary nurse midwives (ANMs) working at health centers or community-level Anganwadi workers (AWWs) working at Anganwadis, regularly managed the growth and development of children. Under the National Supplementary Nutrition Programme (SNP), all children get four supplementary nutritional packets and the moderately malnourished children get two extra nutritional supplement packets, while severely malnourished children (SAM) with or without medical complications are sent to the nearby malnutrition treatment center (MTC) for further attention, treatment, and nutrition.[7] This system is limited by the high cost of inpatient care of SAM children, lesser reach out, higher costs for families (including travel, loss of wages due to the absence from work, and household responsibilities), and lack of good and acceptable inpatient facilities in remote village areas. To address these limitations and make it operationally feasible to reach and treat most of the SAM children, initiatives have been taken in India to test the World Health Organization (WHO) advocated community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) approach (WHO, 2013).[7]

In India, CMAM was first introduced in 2009 by Médecins Sans Frontières as an emergency response in Braul block of Darbhanga district, Bihar, following the Kosi floods. This program advocated inpatient treatment for complicated SAM and the provision of home and Anganwadi-based treatment for uncomplicated SAM. This led to the achievement of a cure rate of 57.4%, a defaulter rate of 36.2%3, and a 0.8% mortality rate, showcasing the better cure rates among non-defaulting children.[8] Also, most (80–90%) of SAM children have been observed to have medically uncomplicated states, further strengthening the need to have community-based management. After this, many developmental organizations have introduced the CMAM program on a pilot test basis in other states like Maharashtra, Odisha, Jharkhand, and Rajasthan.[9]

The Government of Jharkhand (GoJ) and National Health Mission (NHM) along with the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) started CMAM implementation with the POSHAN tracker strategy. Treating SAM children without any medical complications for 8–12 weeks, in the community itself, using the already available nutritional supplement, i.e. Ready to Use Therapeutic Food (RUTF), was the main focus of this program.[10]

The community-based approach includes the timely detection of SAM in the community and treating those without medical complications with RUTF or other nutrient-dense diets in their home only. CMAM is a comprehensive approach that, on one hand, focuses on prevention and, on the other hand, the treatment of SAM children in a domiciliary way.[11]

Despite these interventions, the issue of childhood malnutrition in India remains alarmingly high. It is even worse in Jharkhand, with malnutrition among under five not decreasing at an appreciable rate. This trend is a cause for concern and necessitates immediate attention. NFHS-5 reports a decrease in children suffering from acute malnutrition from 29% in 2015–16 to 22% in 2019–21. Similarly, the prevalence of children with severe acute malnutrition has fallen from 11.4% to 9.0% during the same period.[5] However, there is still a sizable portion of malnourished children who are not able to access services or get improvement from them. Jharkhand has malnourishment rates higher than the national average. This could be due to inaccessible healthcare facilities, improperly trained healthcare professionals, and intermittent availability of essential medical supplies. The overall care standards require extensive improvement, emphasizing the need for strengthening healthcare services to address the high prevalence of severe acute malnutrition in Jharkhand. So, we are doing a bottleneck analysis to identify constraints and inefficiencies in the system that limit the capacity and productivity of the CMAM program and focus on the problematic and good-performing AWCs to identify the lacunae and implement workable strategies, respectively.

The present study is a take on the position and experiences of the CMAM program in Jharkhand especially in West Singhbhum in terms of preventive vs curative, integrative approach, and multisectoral approach. This can be an ideal and well-established program considering its different aspects, such as alternative feasible solutions, convergent action, multiple stakeholders’ work, and their accountabilities. So, we have tried to determine the factors influencing the prognosis and recovery rate of the SAM children in post-COVID pandemic stage.

Materials and Methods

A cross-sectional observational study was conducted in Anganwadi centers coming under Chaibasa Sadar and Khuntpani blocks of West Singhbhum district, Jharkhand, and adjoining houses of SAM children. Total duration of our research was nine months from April 2021 to December 2021. The study population included all healthcare workers of Jharkhand (AWWs) who are working with CMAM during our study period and parents of SAM children enrolled at the visiting Anganwadi center. Purposive sampling was performed to collect data, based on different parameters. CMAM program was started in the two out of 16 blocks coming under the West Singhbhum district of Jharkhand. These two blocks, such as Chaibasa Sadar and Khuntpani, have around 168 and 137 AWCs in them, which are included in the study. A total of 30 AWCs from the two blocks were selected based on the following criteria [Figure 1].

Selection criteria of Anganwadis (n = 28)

So, a total of 30 AWCs were expected to be studied, but due to difficulty in reaching far-off areas and nonavailability of AWW, we were not able to cover some selected centers. Thus, we were able to study 28 AWCs [Figure 2]. Two mothers/caregivers of SAM children were identified from each AWC, to ask about the facility availed and counseling received. Participants who were working under CMAM and were willing to participate in our study were included after obtaining informed consent. We excluded the parents of SAM children enrolled under CMAM who were not available at the time of the visit from our study.

Actual samples of Anganwadis covered (n = 28)

After obtaining ethical clearance from the Institutional Ethical Committee, a total of 15 monitors were taken from SCOE (State Centre of Excellence for SAM management), PSM Dept, RIMS, and District coordinators. Then, five monitoring teams were made, and in each team, three people were allotted for each visit (one for AWC and two for HH (household) visit). A micro-plan was made for collecting the data from all AWWs who were contacted during the study period. Details of the AWCs included their name, the number of SAM children enrolled, and were taken from the AWW after the approval of the District Coordinator of the West Singhbhum district. BNA Checklist developed by the National Centre for Excellence for Management of Severe Acute Malnutrition; Kalwati Saran Children Hospital, New Delhi, was used for assessment. The BNA Checklist consists of assessments at three levels (block, AWC, and household). The household checklist contains basic information about the child (age, weight, enrolment), food stock supplements, adherence to correct dose of medicines, and status of follow-up visits.

After taking proper informed consent from all the AWWs, pretested semi-structured questionnaire from checklist was administered to them. They were asked about take-home ration, drugs administered, last training, anthropometric tools status, and assessment of skill required. Similarly, the monitors visited the households of SAM children to see their living conditions and assess the parents/caregivers’ knowledge and practices regarding malnutrition by BNA checklist tool. The improper practices found in SAM households were addressed and children’s mothers were counseled regarding the lacunae found. Similarly, AWWs were trained regarding the proper anthropometric measurement and given nutritional counseling.

Data analysis

Data were entered and template were generated in MS Excel. Qualitative data were represented in the form of frequencies and proportions and necessary pie diagrams, bar chart was drawn. The quantitative data were represented in the form of necessary histograms, line diagrams, etc.

Results

A total of 28 AWCs, 8 from Khuntpani block and 20 from Sadar Chaibasa, were covered during the monitoring during the last three quarters of the year 2021. Due to difficulty in reaching far off areas and nonavailability of AWW at the time of visit, we were not able to cover some selected centers and other nearby center was studied in their place.

Malnutrition status

In the period of April 2021 to December 2021, supportive supervision team had planned to visit 30 AWCs but was able to visit only 28 AWCs due to feasibility issues. Also, the team was not able to coordinate with a few designated Anganwadi centers, so other nearby available centers were taken for study. Of the 1985 children who were screened by AWWs in this period for severe acute malnourishment (SAM), about 49 (2.5%) children were identified as SAM. All the identified children who passed the medical and appetite test were enrolled in the CMAM program. One child was enrolled in the CMAM program after returning back from the MTC. Availability and utility of services at AWCs

Of all the AWCs, 89% reported disrupted supply of take-home ration (THR) for last two years (COVID-19 pandemic period). None of these children received THR in the first six months of study. Some centers, like Loharda A, Loharda B, Sangajata B, and Rudihid, shared that they got THR in the month of October, but the supply was not regular.

In all the AWCs (Loharda A, Loharda B, Sangajata B, Rudihid, Bhoya, Bunumda, Abru, Jamunbera, Latarsika, Chetansika), infantometer, stadiometer, and Salter scale were available. However, most of the equipment was partially functional. As a result, the level of error observed in the anthropometric measurements was quite high. Digital weighing scale was available at 16 centers of which only 4 were working. Other than the two AWW of Sadar Chaibasa, which were using a digital scale, the rest all AWWs were still using the Salter scale for anthropometric measurement. All the AWCs (Loharda A, Loharda B, Abru, Sangajata B, Latarsika) showed the WHO Growth Chart but were not aware of the utility of the Z-score. [Figure 4]

Knowledge and skill assessment of AWW

All the AWWs said that they have received training in CMAM program. When taking weight all AWW checked zero error, 93% AWW weighted the child with minimum clothes, 89% AWW entered weight measurement data in the record immediately, and 93% AWW weighted when the Salter scale was stabilized and not touching the ground. We observed that in 81% cases current anthropometric measurement was taken in our presence and the last weight measurement was taken and recorded in the register by AWWs matched closely. In 96% AWC, infantometer and stadiometer were used properly in measuring length/height. Almost half had the Z-score chart (weight for height/length) but were not able to use the Z-score chart to identify SAM children. AWWs were still using the Growth Chart (Weight for Age) for classifying SAM, which is less sensitive to identify SAM. All the AWWs followed the correct procedure for the identification of edema in SAM children [Figure 5].

Knowledge and skill assessment of AWWs

Most AWWs said that children enrolled in the CMAM program should be weekly followed up but were not able to do so every time due to other duties, and 93% AWWs have kept updated entries in the treatment card. Seventy-five percent AWW said that they prepare and share monthly reports with ICDS/Lady Supervisor or update them on the online platform.

ANM was responsible to conduct the assessment of medical complications, scrutinize patients for CMAM enrolment/MTC referral, distribution of medicine, and individual counseling. However, most of this work was being done by AWWs. The AWWs only counseled the mothers regarding the exclusive breastfeeding, infant and young child feeding, hand washing, toilet training of infants, personal care, and hygiene practices. Most of the AWW agreed that the program has increased their workload, but since they had to visit nearby houses in the village only, they could handle the same. The AWWs were almost following the correct procedures, but there were still some discrepancies noticed. The individual counseling provided to mothers was not directed toward their specific problem, rather a generalized set of instructions/advices were given to all. This made the mothers’ less reliant on the health system and moved toward traditional healers. All the AWWs visited the child’s house regularly. The Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) supplied medicine under the direct supervision of a female health worker.

Discussion

The CMAM approach is a novel way to address acute malnutrition, with less reliance on facility-based intervention. When CMAM is sustainably integrated with other interventions and used in conjunction with potent intersectoral strategies to address acute malnutrition, its implementation and scale-up are improved, which increases the cost-effectiveness of the treatment and assures the compliance among parents due to the place being a familial environment. This study sheds light on the variables that might affect community-based management of children with acute malnutrition in two blocks (Sadar Chaibasa and Khuntpani) of West Singhbhum district of Jharkhand state.

During our supervisory visit to AWCs, we found that a total of 1985 children were screened, of which 49 (2.5%) children had severe acute malnutrition and were enrolled in CMAM Program. The AWWs were knowledgeable about the anthropometric measurements and followed the proper technique in anthropometric measurement of a child. Still, it reflects poor screening outcome (only 2% SAM children), given highest prevalence of SAM children (11.4%) estimated by National Family Health Survey 2019–21. The confirmation and enrolment of SAM children is supposedly the work of ANMs but was being done by the AWWs everywhere. The reason behind this practice was the absence of training of ANMs in CMAM, while AWWs had received the same. Similar result was reported by another study from Chakradharpur, where ASHAs were doing the screening of SAM children, but all SAM children were not been referred.[14] ANMs were overburdened with the vaccination and other activities related to COVID prevention, so CMAM training was not prioritized then.

Community-based management of SAM is based on three pillars: 1) nutritional therapy, 2) medicinal therapy, and 3) counseling. We assessed all the three aspects during our analysis. Nutritional therapy included giving special take-home ration (THR) for SAM children. None of the children received any THR. This result is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia, where health extension workers reported stock-outs of key commodities (i.e., ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF)) in the majority of districts assessed. At the regional level, there is sufficient RUTF, but in the field, there are issues with logistics and supply distribution.[1516] In another study done in Anganwadis of 20 states of India, 31.4% reported that no supplementary food was given to the enrolled malnourished children.[17]

The medicinal therapy included administration of nine medicines (amoxycillin, iron and folic acid, multivitamin, albendazole, zinc, vitamin A, paracetamol, and ORS). However, the medicinal supply was also not regular as the supply chain was disrupted and most of the centers closed during COVID-19 pandemic have just reopened after a long time while others were yet to open. In contrast, another study done in India found that 77.5% AWCs had children’s medications on hand while 29% were visited by doctors.[171819]

Counseling was the most important part of the whole treatment, as changing the mentality of the community and making them aware, regarding the importance of nutrition, is most essential to changing behavior. All the AWWs contacted in the study had received at least one training in the last 6–12 months, and the majority were using correct methods of assessment. All the workers were providing counseling to the mothers, but it was not problem-specific. Further, in another study done in Anganwadis of 20 states of India, 99% of AWCs provided necessary counseling to mothers regarding malnutrition, locally available nutritious foods, sanitary practices, and different chronic diseases.[1720]

Conclusion and Recommendation

Our analysis explains the different bottlenecks in the delivery of services coming under the CMAM program that operates at the Anganwadi facilities. It also provides a detailed understanding of the variables that can guide how the CMAM program can be applied to severely malnourished children.

After verifying the children’s health records of AWCs, comparing actual weight measurements done by the study teams, with those done by workers and the availability of infrastructure in AWCs, the following recommendations can be suggested for improving the AWCs: The AWC should be located at an accessible place, planted with trees and vegetable plants and protected with a fence. AWCs should have provision for basic services like safe drinking water, toilets, mats, electricity/power supply, toys, and books. The AWWs should be adequately trained especially on maternal counseling and must be literate and techno-friendly and their monthly honorarium should be revised or they should at least get some compensation for additional work. The Anganwadi workers should be employed preferably in AWC-related works only not in non-related works. AWCs should have functional equipment in the AWC for better screening mechanisms and an efficient system for equipment maintenance should be there.

In Anganwadi and malnutrition treatment centers across the nation, SAM children are already being treated, by linking CMAM activities with the ANC, immunization, infant and young child feeding (IYCF) training, and growth monitoring program, its uptake can be improved as has been conducted in Ethiopia, Sierra Leone, Malawi, Nepal, and Ghana, we can also repeat the same success in curbing malnutrition.[21]
