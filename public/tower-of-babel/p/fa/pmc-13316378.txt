Right care, right place, right now: The role of acute medicine in urgent and emergency care
Authors: Anika Wijewardane
Published: 2026

I am honoured to have been invited to guest edit this themed edition on acute medicine for the Future Healthcare Journal, and I am extremely grateful to the editorial team for their support in bringing this issue together.

Acute medicine as a specialty has never been more important. Urgent and emergency care services are under enormous pressure. Demand continues to rise, hospital capacity is stretched, and delays in care have become normalised to an extent that would once have been unimaginable. These pressures are visible not only inside hospitals, but also in public conversation and across social media, where corridor care and overcrowded emergency departments have become symbols of a health system under strain.

In this context, acute medicine provides one of the things that the system most urgently needs: prompt expert assessment, timely diagnostics and patient-centred care, delivered in a way that supports safe patient flow and early discharge where appropriate.

Despite this, many acute medicine services have drifted from their original purpose.1, 2, 3 Over time, pathways have fragmented, core functions have been diluted, and the potential of acute medical units (AMUs) and medical same-day emergency care (SDEC) services has not always been realised.

Acute medicine is also difficult to neatly define or study. It is operationally complex, dependent on people and systems, and highly variable between organisations. It will never produce the same simple evidence base as a single intervention or procedure. Yet the cumulative evidence, and the lived experience of clinicians and patients, consistently demonstrates that well-functioning acute medical services improve patient care, reduce avoidable admissions and enhance overall hospital performance.

During site visits, conversations through the Society for Acute Medicine (SAM) and discussions with colleagues across the UK, I increasingly hear the same themes: burnout, frustration and a sense of losing control of acute medicine services that were originally designed to bring order and clarity to the front door of the hospital. Much of this stems from the feeling that acute medicine is not consistently recognised or invested in as a core hospital function.

Encouragingly however, there are now also clear signs that this is beginning to change. Across a number of organisations, services are being reset with stronger clinical leadership, clearer models of care and a renewed focus on early expert decision-making.4, 5

This belief in acute medicine, both as a specialty and as a model of care, has shaped much of my professional life, and ultimately led to the opportunity to guest edit this issue. Bringing this edition together has also highlighted many of the elements that underpin effective acute medical care: strong clinical leadership, skilled multidisciplinary teams, point-of-care diagnostics, digital capability and systems designed around early expert decision-making.

Understanding how we arrived here is also important. As Vicky Price describes in her paper, Acute internal medicine in focus: the people, the purpose, the progress,6 acute medicine developed as a specialty from a simple but powerful idea: that bringing patients together in the early phase of illness, under focused specialist care, improves outcomes. That principle remains just as relevant today, perhaps even more so. Patients increasingly present with multimorbidity rather than single-organ disease, highlighting the growing importance of acute physicians and multidisciplinary teams skilled in managing complex medical illness.

The specialty itself has continued to evolve in response to this changing patient need. SAM, the UK professional body representing acute physicians and acute medicine services, has played an important role in shaping this development. A revised higher specialty training curriculum now includes skills such as bedside ultrasound, enabling acute physicians to deliver earlier and more effective decision-making.7 Guidance from SAM has also helped define the standards for AMU and medical SDECs, supporting the model of care focused on early expert review and high-quality admission avoidance.8, 9

With a now well-established training pathway producing physicians trained in acute medicine, there is growing recognition that modern acute care increasingly depends on expertise in rapid assessment, risk management and early decision-making in clinically complex patients. SAM has increasingly highlighted the value of strong acute medicine leadership within AMUs and medical SDECs, recognising the specific operational, clinical and leadership skills required to deliver effective front-door care.10

Clinical leadership beyond front-door care is equally important. Acute medicine requires representation not only within AMUs and medical SDECs, but also in strategic decision-making. Without acute physician input into commissioning, operational planning and hospital design, services risk being designed around broader organisational pressures that do not always align with the realities of delivering urgent and emergency care.

This message runs throughout many of the papers in this edition. Carasco et al and Clare et al describe the importance of acute physicians leading and shaping services.11, 5 In particular, the description of a newly designed hospital built around the principles of acute medicine is a powerful example of what can be achieved when acute care is placed at the centre of hospital design, rather than added as an afterthought.

As care increasingly shifts closer to the community, and acute hospitals become more integrated with wider health systems, this leadership will become even more important. Acute physicians need to be involved earlier in the patient journey and more centrally in service design if we are to build systems that genuinely reflect the realities of acute care.

Digital capability matters. But digital transformation is not always the same as improvement. Too often, organisations become more digital without becoming more effective. As Subbe et al describe in this edition, in acute medicine, digital systems should help clinicians make rapid decisions, improve visibility of the patient journey and support flow through the hospital.12 If they do not do this, they add complexity rather than value.

Point-of-care diagnostics are equally important. As demonstrated here by Atkin et al, these tools enable real-time decision making13 and reduce reliance on traditional ʻadmit to assess’ models of care. Together, these approaches allow earlier and more accurate decisions, reducing unnecessary admissions and improving patient experience.

At the centre of all of this are our patients. Patients do not think in terms of pathways, specialties or organisational structures. They simply need urgent care when they need urgent care, and emergency care when they need emergency care. This is reflected powerfully in the patient perspective by Susan Astbury included in this edition, Seen, sorted and sent home – A patient’s desired outcome from urgent and emergency care.14 For patients, the experience of navigating urgent and emergency care can too often feel fragmented and difficult to understand, with pathways designed in isolation from one another and patients moved between services in search of the right care. We therefore need to prioritise building systems around patients, rather than expecting patients to adapt to the structures of the system itself.

I am extremely proud to have brought this issue together. More than anything, it reflects the passion, resilience and belief that still exists within acute medicine, despite the pressures facing services across the UK. It highlights not only the challenges we face, but also the progress being made and the opportunities ahead.

I am deeply grateful to all the authors for their insight, generosity and leadership, and for the work that they, and the wider acute medicine community, continue to do every day for patients and staff working at the front door of the NHS.

Acute medicine was created to bring order, expertise and timely care to the front door of the hospital. We should not lose sight of how important that remains.
