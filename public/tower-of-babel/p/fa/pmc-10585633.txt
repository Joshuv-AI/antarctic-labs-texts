Regulating effects of chlorinated drinking water on cecal microbiota of broiler chicks
Authors: Wei Shuang Meng, Xinxin Sui, Yingying Xiao, Qiangqiang Zou, Yan Cui, Tieliang Wang, Zeliang Chen, Desheng Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In this study, 2 types of drinking water were provided to broiler chicks to evaluate the relationship between the bacterial load of drinking water and cecal microbiota. One type of drinking water was untreated, while the other type was daily treated with sodium dichlorocyanurate (50 mg/L). A total of 240 broiler chicks were divided into 2 groups based on their initial body weight. There were 6 replicates in each group, and each replicate cage contained 20 birds. Each cage was assigned to a different floor of the battery cage. On the final day, water samples were collected from each replicate cage at the opening of the drinking cup height, and one bird was selected from each replicate cage to obtain cecal content samples for measuring microbiota composition using the 16S rRNA technique. We found that drinking water treated with sodium dichlorocyanurate significantly reduced the richness and diversity of microbiota and diminished/disappeared most gram-negative bacteria. Broiler chicks that consumed chlorinated drinking water exhibited changes in the composition of cecal microbiota, with Alistipes serving as the marker species in the cecal content of broiler chicks that consumed untreated water, whereas AF12 served as the marker species in the cecal content of broiler chicks that consumed chlorinated drinking water. Functional prediction using the MetaCyc database and species composition analysis of metabolic pathways showed that changes in 7 metabolic pathways were related to the abundance of Providencia. Therefore, we concluded that chlorinated drinking water reduced the bacterial load in drinking water, thereby altering the cecal microbiota composition and regulating the metabolic activity of broiler chicks.

INTRODUCTION

The “all-in and all-out” strategy is a scientifically and efficiently managed feeding method in modern poultry husbandry. Disinfection procedures are conducted at batch intervals during feeding. However, routine cleaning procedures typically emphasize the sanitation of floors and cages while neglecting the waterline (Fan et al., 2022). Water is essential for organisms to maintain their fundamental metabolic activities and is delivered through water pipes. The quality of drinking water is undeniably linked to the health and performance of animals (Maes et al., 2019). Additionally, drinking water is the preferred medium for administering medications and water-soluble nutrients (Vermeulen et al., 2002), which can also serve as sources of nutrients for microorganisms (Van Eenige et al., 2013). Consequently, disregarding the disinfection of waterlines carries a significant risk of microbial contamination in the drinking water. Pathogens can easily spread through waterborne transmission, posing a direct threat to the health of animals (Sparks, 2009; Van Eenige et al., 2013).

The drinking water system operates as a relatively closed system, making it resistant to mechanical cleaning methods such as water guns. In routine production practices, several strategies are commonly employed to enhance water quality. Among these strategies, acidified and magnetized drinking water have proven effective in inhibiting pathogen growth in drinking water (El-Katcha et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2022). Providing animals with water treated using these methods can lead to improved growth and production performance while reducing the spread of pathogens (Van Bunnik et al., 2012; Jassim and Aqeel, 2017; El-Katcha et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022a). In comparison to these methods, chlorinated drinking water is widely used in animal husbandry due to its ease of dispersal, cost-effectiveness, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties (Tsai et al., 1992; Maes et al., 2019). Its safety and efficacy are well-documented (Byrd et al., 2003; Barreiro et al., 2012). Sodium dichlorocyanurate, approved by both the US Environmental Protection Agency and the World Health Organization for routine drinking water treatment, stands out as a promising disinfectant for maintaining water quality (Clasen and Edmondson, 2006).

The development of intestinal microbiota in animals is profoundly influenced by their early living environments (Ballou et al., 2016). Reducing the bacterial load in the surrounding environment, and consequently decreasing the intake of pathogens, is advantageous for establishing a healthy intestinal microbiota ecology. This, in turn, offers a potential solution for maintaining or even enhancing animal growth and animal production performance within an antibiotic-free environment.

High-throughput 16S rRNA sequencing is a dependable method for bacterial identification. The 16S rRNA gene, found in all bacteria, offers a conserved sequence region that can be selectively targeted using broad-range polymerase chain reaction primers (Hou et al., 2013). This sequencing technique has proven valuable in characterizing the species composition of diverse bacterial communities in studies focused on bacterial diversity (Dang et al., 2023).

Therefore, we conducted a comparative analysis of the bacterial load in chlorinated drinking water and untreated drinking water using the 16S rRNA technique. Our objective was to investigate the connection between the bacterial load of drinking water and the composition of the cecal microbiota in broiler chicks. Our hypothesis posited that chlorinated drinking water would exhibit a significantly lower bacterial load than untreated drinking water. Additionally, we anticipated that broiler chicks consuming chlorinated drinking water, as opposed to untreated drinking water, would demonstrate a notable reduction in pathogen abundance and a substantial increase in the abundance of beneficial cecal bacteria.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental Design

A total of 240 broiler chicks with similar initial body weights were included in this study. The birds were divided into 2 groups, with each group consisting of 6 replicates, each containing 20 birds. The experimental period lasted for 42 d. All the birds were housed in 3-floor battery cages, with each cage measuring 1.25 in length, 0.80 m in width, and 0.50 m in height. These cages were placed in an environmental controlled environment, where the room temperature was initially set at 33°C and gradually reduced by 3°C each week until it reached 22°C. The relative humidity was maintained at 65%. Throughout the experiment, the birds had continuous access to both feed and water. Birds were assigned to various floors of the battery cage based on the replicates, ensuring that each replicate cage had its dedicated waterline. Two types of drinking water were provided during the experimental period. The first type was untreated drinking water. The second type was drinking water that underwent daily treatment with sodium dichlorocyanurate at a concentration of 50 mg/L. The sodium dichlorocyanurate used in this study was purchased from Jinzhou Zhongke Gene Detection Service Co., Ltd., located in Jinzhou, China. The experimental protocol and procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards and were approved and overseen by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jinzhou Medical University, located in Jinzhou, China.

On the final day, samples of drinking water were collected from each replicate cage at the opening of the drinking cup height. Additionally, one bird was selected from each replicate cage to obtain a cecal content sample.

Water Quality Analysis

Water samples were collected from the central taps of each treatment group, specifically at the starting point of the water line supply, on d 1, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42. These samples were collected using 10 mL glass flasks. Following sample collection, we employed a pH meter (Orion Star A211, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) to measure the pH of each sample. Additionally, we conducted colorimetric tests to measure both free residual chlorine and total chlorine levels. Turbidity was assessed using a turbidimeter (Model 09012150083, CT 12).

16S rRNA Analysis

The collected samples underwent DNA extraction using a DNA kit from TIANGEN Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). To assess the concentration and purity of the extracted DNA, we employed a Qubit 2.0 spectrophotometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and conducted 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the DNA samples were diluted with sterile water to achieve a concentration of 1 ng/μL and were then stored at −20°C until further analysis. Quantitative PCR was employed to amplify the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the extracted DNA, using specific full-length universal forward (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and reverse primers (5′-GGACTACGVGGGTVVTCTAAT-3′). The PCR products were purified using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (cat# 28706, Qiagen, Germany), and the purity of the PCR mixture was assessed using the Qubit 2.0 dsDNA HS Assay Kit (cat# Q32854, Invitrogen). For the analysis of cecal microbial community structures, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) at Novogene Bioinformatics Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Raw data were initially processed by removing low-quality reads using Cutadapt software version 1.9.1. Chimeric sequences were subsequently trimmed through alignment and detection. High-quality reads were then clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at a 97% sequence identity threshold using Uparse v7.0.1001. Taxonomic assignment of representative sequences was carried out using QIIME v1.9.1. To determine an appropriate sequencing depth that encompassed the full extent of microbial diversity, we generated rarefaction curves for each sample using R software (version 1.9.1). For assessing alpha diversity, we utilized a range of indices including Chao1, Faith_pd, Observed_species, Pielou_e, Shannon, and Simpson. Beta diversity was evaluated using the phylogenetic qualitative Bray-Curtis abundance dissimilarity metrics. To identify significant differences between groups, we conducted LEfSe analysis employing LEfSe software, setting a screening value, represented by the linear discriminant analysis score, at 3.5. For comparisons of bacterial differences between groups, the Wilcoxon test was applied. Functional predictions of cecal microbiota were made based on the MetaCyc database. To identify distinctions in metabolic pathways, we utilized metagenomeSeq analysis. Species composition of metabolic pathways was compared using the Wilcoxon test. Additionally, the difference in water quality parameters among groups was analysis by Student's t-test procedure provided by SAS Institute Inc. (Cary, NC). Results were presented as the means ± standard deviation. Significance was established with a probability value below 0.05.

DISCUSSION

Impact of Sodium Dichlorocyanurate Administration on Drinking Water Microbiota

Based on the results of alpha- and beta-diversity observed in this study, it is evident that drinking water treated with sodium dichlorocyanurate significantly reduced the richness and diversity of the microbiota, which aligns with findings from the study conducted by Maes et al. (2019). This reduction in microbiota diversity can be attributed to the administration of chloride, as supported by previous research (Stopforth et al., 2007; Finstad et al., 2012). Chloride has been demonstrated as an effective means to reduce bacterial load due to its ability to interact with enzymes involved in cellular respiration (Banwart, 1989). Additionally, bacteria can metabolize chlorate ions, leading to the production of intracellular ions that are toxic to cells (Anderson et al., 2000). Our results corroborate these findings, further highlighting the role of chloride administration in reducing bacterial load in drinking water.

We did not discover any novel bacteria in the drinking water based on the results of the species composition and LEfSe analyses. Notably, among the marker species identified by LEfSe analysis in untreated drinking water, a majority were opportunistic and/or true pathogens, such as Flavobacterium (Waśkiewicz and Irzykowska, 2014), Caulobacter (Moore and Gitai, 2019), Mycoplana (Kämpfer et al., 2014), Novosphingobium (Rutebemberwa et al., 2014), Acidovorax (Wisplinghoff, 2017), Herbaspirillum (Hernández et al., 2019), Bdellovibrio (Dashiff and Kadouri, 2011), and Acinetobacter (Weber et al., 2016). Furthermore, the marker species found in chlorinated drinking water were bacteria known for their biofilm-forming characteristics. For instance, Sphingomonas has been associated with the degradation of copper pipes in drinking water distribution systems (White et al., 1996) and possesses the ability to metabolize various carbon sources to survive in nutrient-limited environments (https://microbewiki.kenyon.edu/index.php/Sphingomonas). Ralstonia’s capacity to form biofilms allows it to thrive in the environment and is linked to antibiotic resistance (Lebeaux et al., 2014). In summary, the administration of sodium dichlorocyanurate in drinking water appeared effective in reducing the prevalence of harmful microorganisms. However, it may have limited effects on bacteria with biofilm-forming capabilities.

Furthermore, the Wilcoxon test analysis of microbiota in chlorinated drinking water aligns with the previously mentioned observations. In untreated drinking water, a significant presence of pathogens was detected. Additionally, apart from Sphingomonas and Ralstonia, the analysis revealed the presence of 2 types of bacteria with biofilm-forming abilities in chlorinated drinking water: Brevibacillus (Narisawa et al., 2008) and Paenibacillus (Panichikkal et al., 2021). Brevibacillus’s ability to form spores enhances its resistance to various sanitation agents (Azizbekyan et al., 2015; Kiliç and Cİhan, 2020). On the other hand, Paenibacillus can produce exopolysaccharides and generate polysaccharide aqueous solutions with high kinematic viscosities (Yegorenkova et al., 2011). This aligns with findings reported by Zhang et al. (2022b), where chlorinated tap water was found to result in more substantial biofilm pollution compared to untreated surface water. Chlorinated tap water exhibited biofilms with a densely compact morphology, low permeate flux, and high thickness, with an enrichment of slime-producing bacteria, such as Xanthobacter (Wiegel, 2006), in the biofilm of chlorinated tap water.

Therefore, we concluded that sodium dichlorocyanurate administration was successful in reducing the richness and diversity of microbiota in drinking water. However, it also suggests that its effectiveness may be limited when dealing with bacteria that have biofilm formation capabilities.

Impact of Different Drinking Water Types on Cecal Microbiota in Broiler Chicks

The administration of sodium dichlorocyanurate effectively reduced the richness and diversity of microbiota in drinking water. However, there were no discernible differences in the richness and diversity of microbiota within the cecal contents of broiler chicks. Furthermore, our analyses did not uncover any novel intestinal bacteria. Marker species identified by LEfSe analysis shed light on the cecal microbiota composition. Alistipes emerged as the marker species in cecal content from broiler chicks that consumed untreated drinking water. Alistipes is associated with intestinal inflammation and has been enriched in fecal samples from a mouse model of spontaneous Crohn's disease ileitis (Rodriguez-Palacios et al., 2015). In contrast, AF12 was identified as the marker species in cecal content from broiler chicks that consumed chlorinated drinking water. AF12 is a probiotic strain, positively correlated with the butyric acid ratio in mouse feces, and has been linked to the transmissible beneficial effects of fecal microbiota transplantation (Lai et al., 2018). In light of these findings, it is reasonable to conclude that chlorinated drinking water has positive regulatory effects on the cecal microbiota of broiler chicks. This conclusion is in partial agreement with the studies conducted by Barreiro et al. (2012) and Guo et al. (2022).

The Wilcoxon test analysis of microbiota in cecal content samples yielded significant findings. Notably, the abundance of the Acinetobacter genus increased in the cecal contents of broiler chicks that consumed chlorinated drinking water. Acinetobacter is a potential pathogen associated with inflammatory bowel disease (Qi et al., 2022) and exhibits strong antibiotic resistance. Studies like Raplee et al. (2021) observed an increase in the abundance of the Acinetobacter genus in the feces of mice treated with ampicillin. Additionally, Glover et al. (2022) suggested that Acinetobacter is adept at coping with environmental stressors, can utilize various nutrient sources, and can establish colonization even in the presence of other microorganisms. Therefore, the rise in Acinetobacter abundance in the intestine should be carefully considered, even in cases where animals consume chlorinated drinking water. Furthermore, the Wilcoxon test analysis of microbiota from cecal content also revealed that Dysgonomonas and Providencia were reduced in the cecal content of broiler chicks that consumed chlorinated drinking water. Dysgonomonas is positively correlated with levels of intestinal inflammation and prethrombotic markers (Nakajima et al., 2022). Providencia, on the other hand, is a known pathogen linked to acute intestinal diseases (Kholodkova et al., 1977) and has been shown to adhere to intestinal epithelial cells and invade cells, contributing to its pathogenicity in intestinal dysfunction (Zhang et al., 2022c). In conclusion, broiler chicks that consumed chlorinated drinking water experienced a reduction in the abundance of Providencia and Dysgonomonas, which are beneficial for intestinal health. However, it is important to monitor the colonization of Acinetobacter in the intestine, as its abundance increased when broiler chicks consumed chlorinated drinking water. This increase may be attributed to its exceptional ability to withstand unfavorable environments.

Functional Prediction of Cecal Microbiota in Broiler Chicks Consuming Chlorinated Drinking Water

The functional prediction of cecal microbiota and the analysis of species composition within metabolic pathways have revealed the key role of Providencia and/or unclassified_Enterobacteriaceae in regulating these pathways. As mentioned previously, Providencia is a known pathogen associated with intestinal inflammation (Zhang et al., 2022c). Moreover, Providencia has been shown to produce the enzyme 4-hydroxyphenylacetate 3-monooxygenase (EC: 1.14.14.9) (Kalnins et al., 2018). This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of 4-hydroxyphenylacetate to 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetate, which is the initial step in the degradation of 4-hydroxyphenylacetate for use as a carbon source (Díaz et al., 2001). Providencia also produces immunogenic lipopolysaccharides (Burns et al., 2015), and various types of lipopolysaccharides have been isolated from the Providencia genus (Kocharova et al., 2008). Furthermore, Providencia has demonstrated efficiency in heterotrophic nitrogen removal under aerobic conditions (Zhao et al., 2010). In this study, it can be inferred that broiler chicks consuming chlorinated drinking water exhibited a suppression of signal pathways such as 3-HYDROXYPHENYLACETATE-DEGRADAT, LPSSYN-PWY, PWY-5845, PWY-5850, PWY-5860, PWY-5862, and PWY-5896. This suppression could be linked to the reduction in the abundance of the Providencia genus in the cecal content. Additionally, we identified an unclassified_Enterobacteriaceae genus in the cecal content of broiler chicks that consumed chlorinated drinking water. This bacterium appears to play a crucial role in regulating metabolic pathways, potentially involving 4-hydroxyphenylacetate degradation, ornithine degradation, menaquinol biosynthesis, demethylmenaquinol biosynthesis, and sulfoquinovose degradation, based on the study's findings.

In summary, this study provides valuable insights into the effects of chlorinated drinking water on intestinal health and metabolic activity in broiler chicks. The findings highlight the effectiveness of chlorination in reducing bacterial load in drinking water, although its impact on biofilm-forming bacteria appears limited. Consumption of chlorinated drinking water by broiler chicks has beneficial effects on intestinal health by reducing pathogen abundance, but vigilance is required concerning the increased abundance of Acinetobacter, which may indicate emerging antibiotic resistance. Moreover, the study's functional prediction and species composition analysis underscore the role of cecal microbiota, particularly the Providencia genus, in regulating metabolic activity. The unclassified_Enterobacteriaceae genus detected in this study warrants further investigation due to its significant role in influencing metabolic processes.
