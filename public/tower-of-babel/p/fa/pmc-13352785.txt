Sex differences in multimorbidity: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tu Nguyen, Wei Jin Wong, Mark Woodward, Katie Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmerging evidence suggests that sex and gender may influence the development, expression, and impact of multimorbidity through a range of mechanisms. This review aimed to examine the differences between women and men in the prevalence, risk factors, and patterns of multimorbidity, and sex differences regarding the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in Ovid Medline and Embase from inception until June 2025. Studies in human beings which reported data separately for women and men related to multimorbidity were included. Where possible, estimates from the included studies were pooled using inverse-variance weighted meta-analysis. Where available, the sex differential was quantified as the women-to-men ratio of prevalence, relative risks, hazard ratios or odds ratios. This review is registered at PROSPERO, CRD42024553043.ResultsThis review identified 37 studies of 5,828,856 individuals (60% women) from 24 countries. We summarised the findings on sex differences according to: (1) prevalence of multimorbidity, (2) risk factors for multimorbidity, (3) patterns of multimorbidity, and (4) the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes. The pooled multimorbidity prevalence in the 31 general population studies were 0.45 (95%CI 0.35–0.56) in women and 0.39 (95%CI 0.30–0.50) in men, pooled women-to-men prevalence ratio for multimorbidity was 1.12 (95%CI 1.02–1.23). In studies in populations with an index disease, the prevalence of multimorbidity was much higher: 0.83 (95%CI 0.63–0.93) in women and 0.82 (95%CI 0.48–0.96) in men. There were limited studies examining sex differences in the patterns and risk factors for multimorbidity, and on the impact of multimorbidity on outcomes.ConclusionsOur systematic review reveals a higher prevalence of multimorbidity in women than men. The increased likelihood of multimorbidity in women compared to men is a complex issue influenced by multiple factors and highlights the need for further research on this topic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27782-7.

Introduction

Multimorbidity, commonly defined as the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual [1], poses a growing challenge to healthcare systems worldwide, particularly in aging populations [2]. It is associated with increased healthcare utilization, reduced quality of life, polypharmacy, and higher mortality rates [3, 4]. The growing population of older people with multimorbidity requires a more targeted approach that considers the complex interactions of biological, psychological and social factors influencing chronic disease trajectories. Despite the rising prevalence and clinical significance of multimorbidity, the role of sex and gender in shaping patterns, outcomes, and management of multimorbidity remains under-explored and often under-reported in research and practice [5].

Emerging evidence suggests that sex and gender may influence the development, expression, and impact of multimorbidity through a range of mechanisms, including hormonal differences, health-seeking behaviours, access to care, and socioeconomic factors [6]. In some communities, the influence of gender roles and expectations significantly impacts disease management and health outcomes, adding another layer of complexity to the clinical situation [7]. Women, for example, may experience delays in diagnosis or treatment due to under-recognition of symptoms [8] while men may be less likely to seek care due to cultural expectations around masculinity [9]. By highlighting these distinctions between women and men, we aim to inform more equitable and personalized approaches to prevention, diagnosis, and care, and to identify key knowledge gaps for future research.

This review aims to examine the differences between women and men in the prevalence, risk factors, or patterns of multimorbidity, and the differences between women and men regarding the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes. For simplicity, in this article we talk only about sex differences. Although we acknowledge that sometimes “gender” would be a more appropriate word to use, “sex” is what most studies report. Furthermore, we talk about women and men, again acknowledging that “females” and “males” are often considered to be more appropriate labels [10].

Methods

This systematic review followed the PRISMA guidelines, and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024553043).

Search strategy and selection criteria

An electronic literature search was conducted in Ovid Medline and Embase from inception until 6 June 2025, using keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) associated with multimorbidity and sex differences (see the Search Strategy in the Supplementary Table 1).

We considered studies eligible for inclusion if they were conducted in people and report data separately for women and men related to multimorbidity. Exclusion criteria were abstract-only papers (conference, editorial, author response), or articles without full text available. There were no restrictions on the age of the participants or the types of study design.

Data extraction and quality assessment

All references selected for retrieval from the databases were managed using Covidence. Study titles and abstracts were screened independently by 2 members of the research team (TN, WJW), based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full texts of qualified publications were read and selected for the final decision to include after discussion among these two reviewers. Any disagreement was solved by discussion with the third member of the team. Quality assessment of the included studies was independently performed by two reviewers (TN and KH), utilising the National Institutes of Health Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies (Supplementary Table 2). Any disputes were reconciled by a third member of the team.

For studies that contained data on the prevalence of multimorbidity in women and men, we extracted information of the year of publication, first author’s name and country, study settings, sample size and age, definition of multimorbidity, prevalence of multimorbidity into Table 1.

Data analysis

Estimates from studies that contained data on the prevalence of multimorbidity in women and men were pooled to get overall prevalence estimates of multimorbidity by sex and the women to men prevalence ratio computed. Subgroup analysis was conducted for country income levels (low- and middle-income countries, LMICs vs. high-income countries, HICs), and region (non-Asian countries vs. Asian countries). A sensitivity analysis was conducted among studies defining multimorbidity as ≥ 2 chronic conditions. Another sensitivity analysis summarised results from studies that reported the prevalence of multimorbidity by age groups in women and men. These analyses were performed with R Statistical Software (version 4.3.1).

Due to lack of sufficient quantitative data, the results for other aims (sex differences in risk factors and patterns of multimorbidity, and sex differences in the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes) were summarized narratively. Where available, the sex differential was quantified as the women-to-men ratio of relative risks, hazard ratios or odds ratios [11].

Role of the funding source

This study was not funded.

Results

A total of 1186 abstracts were obtained, 155 full texts were reviewed, and 37 relevant studies were included in this review (Fig. 1). We summarized the findings on sex differences in four areas: (1) prevalence of multimorbidity, (2) risk factors for multimorbidity, (3) patterns of multimorbidity, and (4) the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes.

Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process

Among the 37 studies included, 34 [12–45] contained data of multimorbidity prevalence in women and men. Five studies reported sex differences in risk factors for multimorbidity [13, 24, 39–41], and 6 described sex differences in the patterns of multimorbidity [18, 19, 34, 46–48]. Sex differences in the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes in women vs. men were examined in 6 studies [15, 21, 27, 28, 33, 37].

Prevalence of multimorbidity in women vs. men

Among the 34 studies with data on prevalence, 30 were conducted in general populations without any index disease [12–42], 3 focused on populations with an index disease (acute coronary syndromes [43], ischemic stroke[45], type 1 diabetes [44]), and 1 study was conducted among two groups of individuals - those with schizophrenia and a reference group from the general population [20]. Most of these studies defined multimorbidity as having at least 2 chronic health conditions among a predefined list of conditions. The number of chronic conditions in this list varied across studies, and ranged from 5 to 114.

The pooled prevalence of multimorbidity in the 31 general population studies in women and men were 0.45 (95%CI 0.35–0.56) and 0.39 (95%CI 0.30–0.50), respectively. The pooled women-to-men prevalence ratio for multimorbidity was 1.12 (95%CI 1.02–1.23) (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Subgroup analyses among studies conducted in HICs versus LMICs, and in Asian versus non-Asian countries showed consistent findings: women were more likely to experience multimorbidity than men, with these sex differences being more pronounced in HICs and non-Asian communities (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

The prevalence of multimorbidity in women and men in the 4 studies in populations with an index disease was much higher compared to those reported in studies in general populations, with a non-significant difference between women and men. The pooled prevalence of multimorbidity in these 4 studies were 0.83 (95%CI 0.63–0.93) in women and 0.82 (95%CI 0.48–0.96) in men. The pooled women-to-men prevalence ratio for multimorbidity was 1.05 (95%CI 0.97–1.13) (Supplementary Figs. 3,4,5).

Sensitivity analysis

Among the 34 included studies, only one study (van den Bussche 2011) defined multimorbidity with ≥ 3 chronic conditions. Omitting this study had no effect on the prevalence estimates (Supplementary Figs. 6,7,8).

Only 7 studies provided information on the prevalence of multimorbidity by age and sex. We have summarized these findings in Supplementary Table 3. Results are heterogenous, which is not unexpected due to smaller sample sizes on age/sex disaggregation. However, women have higher prevalence in more age-specific groups than do men and there is a tendency for the women-men difference to rise with age (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Sex differences in risk factors for multimorbidity

The specific risk factors of interest in the 5 included studies were physical activity, handgrip strength, adiposity indicators (obesity), dental visit, and salivary telomere length.

In a cross-sectional analysis, Autenrieth and colleagues used data from 1007 women and men aged 65–94 years from the population-based KORA-Age Augsburg Study (Germany) to investigate the association between physical activity and multimorbidity [24]. Multimorbidity was defined as having ≥ 2 out of 13 chronic conditions, and physical activity scores were calculated based on the self-reported physical activity scale for the elderly (PASE). Increased PASE score was associated with reduced risk of multimorbidity among men (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 0.73, 95% CI 0.60–0.90), but not in women (adjusted OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.83–1.33) [24].

In a longitudinal analysis of 3977 participants aged ≥ 50 years from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA, waves 2–8), Liu and colleagues examined sex-specific in the associations of handgrip strength and asymmetry with incident multimorbidity, with a median follow-up of 7.8 years .39 Multimorbidity was defined as having ≥ 2 out of 15 chronic conditions. They found that low handgrip strength was associated with increased risk of multimorbidity among both women (hazard ratio [HR] 1.19, 95% CI 1.03–1.38) and men (1.20, 95% CI 1.03–1.40), p-interaction = 0.71. However, handgrip strength asymmetry increased the risk of multimorbidity in women (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.07–1.41), but not in men (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.81–1.09), p-interaction = 0.01.39

In another prospective longitudinal cohort study with a mean follow-up of 8.0 years, Ma and colleagues examined 382,678 adults aged ≥ 38 years without multimorbidity at baseline from the UK Biobank and found curvilinear associations of hip circumference, trunk fat percentage and arm fat percentage with the incidence of multimorbidity in men, and curvilinear associations of hip circumference and waist-to-height ratio with the incidence of multimorbidity in women [40].

Limo and colleagues conducted a cross-sectional analysis, using data from the first follow-up wave (2018) of the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (N = 44,815) and found a protective effect of dental visits in the past 12 months protect against multimorbidity in women. This effect was stronger in women (prevalence ratio 0.70, 95%CI 0.65–0.76) compared to men (prevalence ratio 0.81, 95%CI 0.76–0.87) [13].

Niedzwiedz and colleagues conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 5495 participants aged ≥ 50 years of the US Health and Retirement Study (2008–2014) to investigate sex differences in the association between salivary telomere length and multimorbidity. They found that longer telomeres were associated with lower risk of multimorbidity that included psychiatric problems among men (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.28–0.96), but not women (OR 1.19, 95% CI 0.77–1.83) [41].

Sex differences in the patterns of multimorbidity

Velek and colleagues followed 6,094 participants aged ≥ 45 years from the Rotterdam Study for the occurrence of ten diseases (cancer, coronary heart disease, stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease-COPD, depression, diabetes, dementia, asthma, heart failure, parkinsonism) [46]. Two thirds of the participants developed multimorbidity in their remaining lifetime, with women at nearly double the risk of multimorbidity involving depression than men. The most common pair of diseases among women was depression and dementia (14.9% of participants with multimorbidity), while the most common pair of diseases among men was COPD and cancer (12.5%).46

Moore and colleagues conducted a cross-sectional analysis based on a survey among 11,734 long-term stay residents aged ≥ 65 years of US nursing homes (2004–2005). They found that for the most common 2-disease combinations, combinations containing arthritis, osteoporosis, and thyroid disease were more common in women, while COPD combinations were more common in men [47].

Zhong and colleagues examined sex and socioeconomic differences in the prevalence and patterns of multimorbidity among 19,559 respondents aged over 45 years in 2018 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study [19]. They identified four patterns: relatively healthy class (respondents with a substantially lower prevalence of all chronic diseases), respiratory class (respondents with a higher prevalence of lung disease and asthma), stomach-arthritis class (respondents with a higher prevalence of stomach diseases and arthritis), and vascular class (respondents with a higher prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, stroke and heart disease). The study revealed that women were more likely to be in the stomach-arthritis class (compared with the relatively healthy class), with a relative risk ratio of 1.60 (95%CI 1.28–1.99).

Abad-Diez and colleagues conducted an observational retrospective multicentre study based on diagnostic information obtained from electronic medical records of patients at 19 primary care centres in Spain [34]. They found that women had a higher prevalence of the mechanical pattern (including anxiety and neuroses, arthropathy, cervical pain, dermatitis, eczema, dyslipidemia, diverticular disease colon, gastroesophageal reflux, low back pain, osteoporosis, thyroid disease, varicose veins) and psychogeriatric pattern (including behaviour problems, cardiac arrhythmia, cerebrovascular disease, chronic ulcer of the skin, congestive heart failure, dementia and delirium, iron deficiency, osteoporosis, Parkinson’s disease), as well as a higher degree of pattern overlapping. In contrast, the prevalence of the cardiometabolic pattern (including atherosclerosis, cardiac arrhythmia, congestive heart failure, diabetes, gout, hematologic disorders, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, iron deficiency, obesity, other cardiovascular disorders) was higher in men compared to women [34].

Peltier and colleagues used national data from the US Veterans Health Administration to examine sex differences in multimorbidity associated with opioid use disorder (N = 48,408, 5.8% were women) [48]. They found that female veterans had a higher rate of psychiatric diagnoses, notably mood, anxiety and personality disorders, and higher rates of pain-related diagnoses (such as headaches and fibromyalgia), while male veterans were more likely to have concurrent, severe medical co-morbidities (hepatic disease, HIV, cancers, peripheral vascular disease, diabetes and its related complications, and renal disease).

Tang and colleagues conducted a cross-sectional study of 2,208 participants aged ≥ 30 years from rural North China, with an aim to investigate the prevalence of chronic diseases and the multimorbidity patterns of chronic diseases. They found that the most common dyad disease patterns in women were hypertension and arthritis (8.2%), hypertension and diabetes (7.6%), and hypertension and chronic digestive disease (6.8%), while in men the common patterns included hypertension and stroke (9.5%), hypertension and heart disease (6.5%), and hypertension and chronic digestive disease (4.7%) [18].

Sex differences in the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes

The types of health outcomes examined in the 6 included studies were emergency service use, depression (2 studies), obesity, physical function, and food insecurity.

Doheny and colleagues aimed to examine socio-demographic differences in the frequent use of emergency department (ED) care by older adults in a population-based study of 356,375 individuals aged ≥ 65 years in Sweden in 2014 [37]. Although the primary aim was not to examine sex differences in the impact of multimorbidity on frequent ED use, the adjusted models revealed that the impact of multimorbidity on frequent ED use was similar among women and men. In individuals aged 65–79 years, the adjusted ORs of multimorbidity for frequent ED use were 1.23 (95%CI 1.21–1.24) in women vs. 1.19 (95%CI 1.17–1.20) in men, and in those aged 80 or older, the adjusted ORs of multimorbidity for frequent ED use were 1.21 (95%CI 1.20–1.23) in women vs. 1.15 (95%CI 1.13–1.17) in men. The women to men ratio of adjusted odds ratios was thus 1.03 (95%CI 1.02–1.05) in those 65–79 years and 1.05 (95%CI 1.03–1.07) in those 80 or older.

Carayanni and colleagues examined sex differences in depression and its associated factors in a cross-sectional study of 360 older adults (aged ≥ 60 years) in Greece and found that the adjusted ORs of multimorbidity (defined as having ≥ 3 chronic conditions) on depression were 3.31 (95%CI 1.57–7.01) in women vs. 5.99 (95%CI 2.27–15.83) in men [27]. The women to men ratio of odds ratios was calculated as 0.55 (95%CI 0.16–1.88). In contrast, Rueda-Salazar and colleagues examined 2,263 older individuals (aged ≥ 60 years) from the Longitudinal Survey of Social Protection, Chile and found that multimorbidity (defined as having ≥ 2 chronic conditions) increased the risk of depression in women than in men: the average life expectancy free of depressive symptoms decreased by 5.8 years in older women (multimorbidity: 13.1 years, 95%CI 11.4–14.7, non-multimorbidity: 18.9 years, 95%CI 16.8–21.2), compared to 4.2 years in older men (multimorbidity: 11.8 years, 95%CI 10.4–13.3, non-multimorbidity: 16.1 years, 95%CI 14.5–17.8).15

Murmu and colleagues used data of 715,831 women and 100,656 men from the National Family Health Survey in India to examine sex differences in the prevalence of obesity and its association with multimorbidity. They found that multimorbidity (defined as having ≥ 2 chronic conditions) was significantly associated with increased odds of obesity in men (adjusted OR 1.47, 95%CI 1.13–1.89), but not in women (adjusted OR 1.09, 95%CI 0.94–1.26) [28].

Ge and colleagues examined sex differences in associations between multimorbidity and physical function domains among 1940 community-dwelling adults (aged ≥ 21 years) in Singapore and found that multimorbidity (defined as having ≥ 2 chronic conditions) had a stronger association with reduced overall physical function and advanced lower extremity function in women than in men, which suggests that the advanced lower extremity function in women is more vulnerable to multimorbidity than in men.33.

Kantilafti and colleagues examined the association between multimorbidity and food insecurity among 1255 individuals aged ≥ 18 years in Cyprus and found that women with multimorbidity (defined as having ≥ 2 chronic conditions) were more likely to experience food insecurity (adjusted OR 1.81, 95%CI 1.04–3.17), compared to men (adjusted OR 1.51, 95%CI 0.77–2.99) [21].

Discussion

Sex differences in the prevalence of multimorbidity

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, pooled data from 31 general population studies found women were more likely to have multimorbidity than men. The overall findings are supported by the limited data from age-stratified data. Sex differences in the aging process may help explain the observed discrepancy. Women generally live longer than men, but this longevity paradoxically increases their lifetime risk of developing multimorbidity. Socioeconomic factors may also play a significant role in this difference. Low education and increasing deprivation were consistently associated with increasing risk of multimorbidity [49]. Women, particularly those in LMICs, usually have reduced access to the socioeconomic resources that ensure good health and wellbeing compared to men [50]. Barriers to healthcare access, such as low income and education, may delay the diagnosis and treatment of health conditions [51, 52]. Family duties or caregiving responsibilities that women usually assume may hinder their adherence to chronic disease treatment plans [53]. Furthermore, women may have different coping mechanisms in response to stress and illness than men. Research shows that women usually experience more stress than men and their coping styles tend to be more emotion-focused [54]. While some strategies may be adaptive, others, such as avoidance and rumination, can have negative impacts on physical health, contributing to the development of various chronic conditions [55]. These factors can lead to increased stress, reduced access to healthcare, and a lower prioritization of personal health, ultimately heightening the risk of multimorbidity among women. On the other hand, the increased prevalence of multimorbidity in women may be partly because women (especially in high-income countries) tend to seek health care more readily than men, and hence receive more diagnoses [56].

Sex differences in the patterns of multimorbidity

The limited studies in this review showed that there were sex differences in the patterns of multimorbidity, with musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal disorders and mental health being more common in women, and chronic lung disease and cardiometabolic being more common in men. These discrepancies can be attributed to differences in life expectancy, hormonal factors, and varying exposures to risk factors between the sexes [57, 58]. Multimorbidity often requires patients to manage care with multiple specialists, which can result in fragmented healthcare if not properly coordinated. Women may encounter difficulties navigating healthcare systems, especially when mental health conditions are part of their multimorbidity [52]. In addition, mental health conditions can exacerbate physical conditions and reduce treatment adherence.

Sex differences in risk factors and impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes

Our reviews indicate a gap in research related to sex differences in risk factors and impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes. The evidence available suggests a strong focus on sex differences in physical activity, with inconsistent findings on sex differences. In a systematic review of risk factors for multimorbidity in adulthood from 68 studies in 23 countries, Grande and colleagues reported that older age, low education, obesity, hypertension, depression, and low physical function were associated with increased risk of multimorbidity [59]. There is a need for further studies to understand sex differences in risk factors for multimorbidity. Regarding the impact of multimorbidity on outcomes, evidence from the included studies suggests that women with multimorbidity tend to be more vulnerable, with increased risk of emergency service use, physical impairment, and food insecurity compared to men. These disparities highlight the need for targeted interventions aimed at supporting women with multimorbidity.

Implications for research and clinical practice

Our analysis highlights the need for more research on multimorbidity that is disaggregated by sex to better understand the unique disease trajectories and treatment responses of women. To address the higher burden of multimorbidity in women, healthcare systems need to adopt a sex-sensitive approach that acknowledges and addresses the unique challenges faced by women. Healthcare providers should tailor treatment plans to meet the specific needs of women with multimorbidity, considering both biological and psychosocial factors. Preventive measures should be emphasized to reduce the onset of chronic conditions in women. Regular screenings, health education, and lifestyle interventions are crucial for identifying risk factors early and preventing the progression of disease. Flexible care models, such as telehealth or community-based services, should be promoted to improve access for women who have barriers to care, such as lack of insurance, transportation, or time due to caregiving roles [60, 61]. Policymakers should prioritize research and funding for sex-specific health issues [62]. Investing in women’s health research can provide new insights into multimorbidity and help guide effective interventions [63]. Acknowledging sex differences in health, enhancing individualized treatment, promoting preventive strategies, and advocating for equitable policies can help improve outcomes and reduce the impact of multimorbidity for women.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to summarize the evidence of sex differences in the prevalence, risk factors, and patterns of multimorbidity, and its impact on health outcomes. The literature search conducted across two large databases, ensured a comprehensive collection of studies. The study protocol, including inclusion and exclusion criteria were strictly adhered to minimize bias and maximize the relevance of the studies obtained. However, our study has several limitations. By restricting our searches to English-language studies, we may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages. Additionally, there could be inconsistencies among the reviewers during the study selection process. To address this, we followed closely to the review protocol and maintained regular communications and cross-checks among team members to mitigate any potential discrepancies. Furthermore, the results may be limited by potential publication bias, where only positive or significant results on sex differences were published. We acknowledge that additional social markers, such as race/ethnicity, education, income, and geographic context, are important contributors to heterogeneity in multimorbidity patterns. A key consideration is the limited use of explicit theoretical frameworks in the studies included in this review. Most of these studies primarily adopted biomedical or behavioural approaches, which focus on individual risk factors and clinical characteristics. Although these perspectives provide valuable insights, they may not fully account for the broader social, environmental, and structural determinants that influence the observed relationships. Frameworks such as ecosocial or intersectional approaches, which examine how social conditions, inequalities, and exposures interact over the life course, are rarely used. This lack of diverse perspectives may restrict the interpretation of findings and hinder the understanding of the complex, multilevel mechanisms underlying the observed associations. Future research would greatly benefit from the integration of more comprehensive theoretical frameworks to better capture these interactions and to inform more effective interventions.

Conclusion

Our systematic review reveals a higher prevalence of multimorbidity in women than men. This issue appears to be complex, influenced by various clinical and social factors. Understanding these complexities is essential for developing effective healthcare strategies that cater to the specific health needs. Further research, and systemic reviews, are needed to explore sex differences in risk factors for multimorbidity, the patterns of multimorbidity, and the impact of multimorbidity on health outcomes. Our study emphasizes the importance of addressing gender inequalities within health systems and societies as a crucial step toward promoting equity. This effort is essential for strengthening fair and inclusive institutions, aligning with the goals of the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development.
