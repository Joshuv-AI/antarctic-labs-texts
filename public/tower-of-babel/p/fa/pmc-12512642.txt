E. coli as a uropathogen in patients with diabetes mellitus, its prevalence and antimicrobial resistance profile in Ethiopia: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Nuhamin Alemayehu Tesfaye, Mulat Belete Demessie, Minimize Hassen, Tewodros Solomon, Abel Andualem, Tesfalem Lukas Gebreegziabher, Mengistie Yirsaw Gobezie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrinary tract infections (UTIs) are prevalent among patients with diabetes mellitus (DM), with E. coli being the most common uropathogen. Understanding its antimicrobial resistance profile is crucial for empirical treatment, especially in resource-limited settings like Ethiopia. The study aimed to determine the prevalence of E. coli in UTIs among diabetic patients in Ethiopia and to assess its antimicrobial resistance patterns.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023445772). A comprehensive search of PubMed, Google Scholar, Hinari, SCOPUS, and EMBASE was performed for studies published between January 1, 2014, and January 19, 2024. Inclusion criteria encompassed observational studies on microbiological investigations in UTI patients with DM conducted in Ethiopia. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist.ResultsEleven cross-sectional studies involving 2,830 diabetic patients were included. Among these, 492 bacterial isolates were identified, with 171 being E. coli. The pooled prevalence of UTIs caused by E. coli in diabetic patients was 52% (95% CI 36–74%, I² = 68.7%). Subgroup analysis revealed higher odds ratios in the Southern Nation Nationalities and People Region (SNNPR) and studies conducted after 2019. Nitrofurantoin exhibited the highest efficacy (0% resistance), while Ampicillin showed the highest resistance (98%). Significant heterogeneity was noted (I² = 68.7%, p < 0.001). No publication bias was detected, and sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the findings.ConclusionsE. coli is a significant uropathogen in diabetic patients with UTIs in Ethiopia, exhibiting high resistance to commonly used antimicrobials like Ampicillin. These findings underscore the need for routine antimicrobial susceptibility testing to guide empirical treatment and curb resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11714-8.Keywords: E. coli, Urinary tract infection, Diabetes mellitus, Antimicrobial resistance, Ethiopia, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) defined as the presence of significant bacteria in urine specimen and refers to the proliferation of an infectious agent within the urogenital system with or without structural or functional abnormalities of the urinary tract that results in irritative and voiding symptoms [1, 2].

UTIs present in various forms, including acute uncomplicated infection, pyelonephritis, complicated UTI, asymptomatic bacteriuria, and bacterial prostatitis in men [3]. They are a common bacterial infection globally, affecting 150 million people annually and causing significant morbidity [4, 5]. The economic burden is substantial, with UTI hospitalizations costing around $2.8 billion annually in the USA. Additionally, rising antimicrobial resistance in both outpatient and inpatient settings exacerbates the issue [6–8].

UTIs bring a significant and serious health-related problem in diabetic patients because of the numerous effects of this disease on the glomerulus and renal tubules and host immune system [9, 10]. Patients with diabetes are more prone to bacterial urinary tract infections due to bladder inflammation and dysfunction attributed to presence of glucose in the renal tubules and that serve as a fortunate means for bacterial growth. Other studies suggest that sugar in the urine encourage to increased bacterial colonization and infection in the urinary tract [11].

As the studied articles report in Ethiopia depict that the overall prevalence of bacterial UTI among patients with DM accounts a significant percentage ranged from 9.8% to 33.8%. Of them, the most frequently isolated uropathogen was Escherichia coli (E. coli) [9, 11–15].

Antimicrobial drug resistance poses significant challenges in low-income countries like Ethiopia due to the high prevalence of infections, irrational use of antimicrobials, over-the-counter availability of these drugs, and poor infection prevention practices. Consequently, this issue complicates treatment outcomes, raises treatment costs, and limits therapeutic options [9, 12, 13].

Several studies conducted in Ethiopia from various clinical settings indicate an increase in the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance patterns of E. coli isolated from urinary samples in in patients with DM [9, 11, 14, 16].

The resistance of E. coli in UTIs among diabetes mellitus patients to commonly used antimicrobials lacks comprehensive data in Ethiopia, making empirical treatment challenging. In developing countries, infectious disease treatments often rely on empirical methods due to limited resources for evidence-based therapies. Consequently, there is no comprehensive nationwide study showing the pooled proportion of E. coli among uropathogens and its antimicrobial resistance in UTIs in patients with DM. Therefore, updated information on the prevalence of E. coli and its antimicrobial resistance patterns for commonly utilized antimicrobials in Ethiopia is crucial. The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to compile available data and establish the pooled prevalence and antimicrobial resistance of E. coli in UTIs among patients with DM in Ethiopia.

Methods

Reporting

The study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis [17] guideline for reporting (Supplementary file-1). Furthermore, the protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis has been registered in the Prospero database under the registration number PROSPERO 2023: CRD42023445772.

Databases and search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in electronic databases including PubMed, Google Scholar, Hinari, SCOPUS, and EMBASE from January 20 to January 30, 2024. The search strategy was collaboratively developed by three authors (NA, MB, & TS), and executed by two other authors (MY & MH). Specific key terms and their synonyms were used, such as E. coli, Escherichia coli, Enterobacteriaceae, gram-negative bacteria, uropathogen, AND UTI, urinary tract infection, AND DM, diabetes mellitus, AND Ethiopia. Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were employed in the search strings.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The search included studies published from January 1, 2014, to January 19, 2024. Inclusion criteria were: [1] microbiological investigations in UTI patients with diabetes mellitus and/or reports on the antimicrobial susceptibility of E. coli; [2] observational studies, including cross-sectional studies, cohorts, or surveillance designs; [3] studies conducted in Ethiopia; [4] studies published in English. Exclusion criteria were: case reports, case-control studies, reviews, commentaries, editorials, and conference abstracts.

Study selection and quality assessment

Endnote version 20.5 [18] Reference Manager was used to eliminate duplicate studies. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors, MY and NA, who selected articles for full-text review. The full texts of the remaining articles were then obtained and assessed for eligibility for the final meta-analysis by two investigators, MB and MH. The quality of the screened studies was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for studies reporting prevalence data. The following criteria were used for appraising the selected studies: (I) Appropriateness of the sampling frame for addressing the target population, (II) Sampling technique and adequacy of the sample size, (III) Detailed description of study subjects and setting, (IV) Sufficient analysis of the data and validity and reliability of methods used for investigating E. coli, (V) Techniques employed for assessing the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profile of E. coli, and (VI) Adequacy of statistical analysis used. Disagreements were resolved through consensus. Studies scoring five or more out of nine JBI score points were considered to have a low risk of bias.

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out by two authors, MY and MB, using a standardized data extraction format. To ensure accuracy and reliability, any discrepancies were resolved by repeating the procedure. Articles that met the inclusion criteria were summarized into tables, which included the following information: authors, study period, publication year, study design, study setting, study population, study region, sample size, number of bacterial isolates, number of E. coli isolates and their respective resistant strains for tested antimicrobials, prevalence of resistance for tested antimicrobials, and antimicrobial sensitivity test standards employed.

Data analysis

To estimate the prevalence of E. coli among UTIs in DM patients and its AMR prevalence for tested antimicrobials, we employed a weighted inverse variance random-effects model [19] to account for variation between studies. However, the assumed random-effects model did not adequately address the heterogeneity of the included studies. To further adjust for the observed variation, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on the region where the studies were conducted and the types of DM. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using a forest plot, meta-regression, and the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [20]. The significance of heterogeneity was assessed using a Q test, with a p-value less than 0.05 indicating significance. The findings were visually presented using a forest plot. To assess publication bias, we employed a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test, with a p-value less than 0.05 in Egger’s test suggesting significant publication bias. Additionally, we applied trim and fill analysis to check for missed small studies [21]. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure the stability of the summary estimate by excluding individual studies. The meta-analysis was performed using STATA version 17 [22] Statistical software.

The outcome of interest and study population

The study population for this systematic review and meta-analysis consists of patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus (DM) suspected of having a UTI. The primary outcome of interest is the odds ratio (OR) of E. coli as a uropathogen among these patients, calculated by dividing the total number of E. coli isolates by the number of bacterial isolates other than E. coli. The secondary outcome of interest is the prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant E. coli for the tested antimicrobials, calculated by dividing the number of resistant E. coli isolates by the total number of E. coli isolates tested. Antimicrobial sensitivity testing was conducted according to the international clinical laboratory standard.

Result

Characteristics of included studies

We identified a total of 665 potential studies, comprising 161 articles from PubMed, 21 from Hinari (Research4Life), 130 from EMBASE, 277 from Scopus, and 75 from Google scholar. The search outcomes and reasons for exclusion during the study selection process are depicted in Fig. 1. Ultimately, 11 articles were included to evaluate the prevalence of E. coli among diabetic patients in Ethiopia. All included studies adopted a cross-sectional study design, with three specifically conducted in the Amhara region [23, 24], two in Oromia [25, 26], four in SNNPR [27–30], and two in Addis Ababa [31, 32]. The studies encompassed a total of 2,830 diabetic patients, from whom 492 bacterial isolates were identified, including 171 E. coli isolates from urine samples. Quality of the included studies

All studies were evaluated using the JBI critical appraisal checklist for prevalence data. The assessments with the JBI quality appraisal checklists indicated that none of the included studies were of poor quality, resulting in no exclusions from the meta-analysis (Supplementary file-2).

Meta‑analysis

The estimated odds ratio of E. coli in urinary tract infections among patients with diabetes in Ethiopia

In This systematic review and meta-analysis included eleven studies examining 2830 diabetic patients for UTIs. Among these patients, 492 bacterial isolates were identified. The pooled rate of UTIs caused by E. coli in diabetic patients in Ethiopia was 0.52 (95% Confidence Interval (CI) 0.36–0.74, I² = 68.7%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis based on different regions of Ethiopia revealed relatively higher rate of E. coli 0.72 (95% CI 0.44, 1.18) in SNNPR. Conversely, the lowest rate of 0.24 (95% CI 0.10, 0.57) was reported in Oromia (Fig. 3). Additionally, a subgroup analysis conducted based on the study periods demonstrated a significant difference in the pooled rate of E. coli from the total isolates among the groups conducted after the year 2019 which is the time at which covid-19 pandemic occurred with 0.65(95% CI 0.4, 1.04) OR respectively (Fig. 4).

Heterogeneity analysis

The included studies demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I² = 68.7%; p < 0.001). This heterogeneity was not sufficiently addressed by the weighted inverse variance random-effects model. To further investigate the heterogeneity, we utilized a forest plot for subjective assessment and performed subgroup analysis. Additionally, we conducted meta-regression analysis using sample size and the number of isolates, yielding p-values of 0.996 and 0.849, respectively, indicating no significant heterogeneity (see Table 2; Fig. 5).

Publication bias

We evaluated publication bias by visually inspecting the funnel plot (Fig. 6) and performing Egger’s regression test, which resulted in a p-value of 0.325 for the OR of E. coli from the total isolates, indicating no publication bias. To further confirm this, we conducted a trim and fill analysis, which did not add or remove any studies, suggesting that there are no missing small studies.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of E. coli isolated from urine samples of diabetic patients in Ethiopia

We systematically collected data on the sensitivity of E. coli to ten antimicrobials and conducted an analysis to determine the pooled resistance estimates. Our findings revealed that Nitrofurantoin exhibited the highest efficacy with a resistance rate of 0.0% (95% CI −0.01, 0.01), followed by ceftriaxone at 11% (95% CI 5, 16). In contrast, Ampicillin was the least effective, with a resistance rate of 98% (95% CI 96, 100), followed by CAF at 68% (95% CI 57, 78), Tetracycline at 58% (95% CI 31, 86), Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid at 52% (95% CI 30, 73), and Co-trimoxazole at 48% (95% CI 31, 64). Moderate resistance levels were observed in quinolones (Ciprofloxacin and Norfloxacin each at 23%) and the aminoglycoside Gentamicin at 32%, as illustrated in Table 4.

Discussion

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) threatens the effectiveness of infection treatments and is a public health issue with national and global dimensions [33]. In low-income countries, AMR often manifests in microorganisms commonly transmitted within communities, including those causing pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs), diarrheal diseases, tuberculosis and sexually transmitted infections [34, 35]. UTIs, a critical medical concern, result from microbial colonization in the urinary system. Diabetic patients are particularly susceptible to bacterial UTIs due to compromised host defenses and elevated urine glucose levels. Effective treatment and infection control in these patients require prompt examination of uropathogens and their antibiograms. Escherichia coli (E. coli) are common uropathogens in Diabetes Mellitus patients, necessitating detailed study for appropriate management and infection control [36, 37].

Significant variations in the prevalence of E. coli in diabetic UTI cases were identified, ranging from 12.1% in 2017 [26] to 63.6% in 2021 [32]. Temporal changes in E. coli prevalence can be influenced by changes in diagnostic practices, healthcare accessibility, and regional differences in disease burden. Improved reporting systems and evolving healthcare policies over time could have enhanced the detection and documentation of E. coli infections, contributing to variations in reported prevalence. Additionally, socio-economic and environmental factors likely influenced the prevalence rates in different regions, highlighting the complexity of E. coli distribution over the study period.

Our systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a pooled incidence of UTIs caused by E. coli in diabetic patients in Ethiopia of 52%, indicating a significant risk for these individuals. Contributing factors to this high prevalence may include suboptimal glycemic control, limited healthcare access, and delayed diagnosis and treatment [38]. Similarly, a study conducted in India, E. coli was the most commonly identified organism in UTIs among diabetic patients (64.3%), followed by Staphylococcus aureus (21.4%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (14.3%) [39]. In Italy, Pisa University-Hospital reported a 54.1% prevalence of E. coli in diabetic patients with UTIs [40]. A study from Nepal found a 49.01% prevalence of E. coli among diabetic patients at a tertiary care center [41]. In Mogadishu, Somalia, a cross-sectional study showed a 67.5% prevalence of uropathogens in diabetic patients, with E. coli being the most common [42]. In Cameroon, a hospital-based study revealed that the prevalence of E. coli UTIs was significantly higher in diabetics with unbalanced blood sugar levels (15.41%) and balanced levels (9.73%) compared to non-diabetics (0.87%) [43].These findings underscores the heightened risk and prevalence of E. coli UTIs among diabetic patients, emphasizing the need for improved glycemic control, better healthcare access, and timely infection management to mitigate this risk.

Our systematic analysis of antimicrobial sensitivity in E. coli from diabetic patients in Ethiopia revealed significant resistance variations across different antibiotics. Nitrofurantoin exhibited the highest efficacy with no resistance (0.0%), making it a reliable treatment option for UTIs. Ceftriaxone also demonstrated low resistance at 11%. Conversely, Ampicillin was the least effective, with a high resistance rate of 98%. High resistance was also noted for CAF (68%), Tetracycline (58%), Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (52%), and Co-trimoxazole (48%). Moderate resistance levels were found for Ciprofloxacin and Norfloxacin (23% each) and Gentamicin (32%). These findings underscore the need for tailored antibiotic therapy based on local resistance patterns and emphasize the importance of ongoing surveillance to inform effective UTI treatment strategies in diabetic patients. Almost similar resistance patterns have been observed in several other countries. In Nepal, in-vitro antimicrobial susceptibility study in diabetic patients revealed Nitrofurantoin was effective against E. coli, while Ampicillin, Nalidixic acid, and Ciprofloxacin were poorly effective against isolated uropathogens [41]. In Italy, studies found high resistance rates to Ampicillin and Co-trimoxazole, followed by Ciprofloxacin and Nitrofurantoin in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients [40]. Additionally, studies from Somalia reported high resistance rates to commonly used antibiotics like Ciprofloxacin and Amikacin in E. coli isolated from diabetic patients [42].These consistent findings across different regions highlight the global challenge of antimicrobial resistance in managing UTIs, particularly among diabetic patients. Continuous monitoring, effective antibiotic stewardship, and development of new therapeutic strategies are crucial to combat this growing issue and ensure effective treatment outcomes [44].

The subgroup analysis of different regions in Ethiopia revealed notable variations in the odds ratios (OR) of E. coli prevalence among diabetic patients. The Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPR) exhibited a relatively higher OR of 0.72 whereas Oromia reported the lowest OR of 0.24. These variations may be attributed to differences in socio-economic factors, such as education and access to clean water and sanitation. Additionally, disparities in healthcare infrastructure, including the availability of diagnostic facilities, antibiotic prescription practices, and infection control measures, likely play a significant role. Additionally, a subgroup analysis based on study periods highlighted a significant difference in the pooled OR of E. coli isolates from studies conducted after 2019, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, showing an OR of 0.65. This rise may indicate shifts in healthcare practices, antibiotic stewardship, and infection control measures during the pandemic, emphasizing the necessity for ongoing monitoring and specialized strategies to manage UTIs in diabetic patients effectively [45].

The heterogeneity analysis of the studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis revealed substantial variability (I² = 68.7%; p < 0.001), which was initially not mitigated by the random-effects model. However, further exploration through forest plots, subgroup analysis, and meta-regression indicated no significant sources of heterogeneity based on sample size and number of isolates. The absence of publication bias, as evidenced by Egger’s regression test (p = 0.325) and the trim and fill analysis, reinforces the robustness of our findings. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the overall odds ratio (OR) for E. coli prevalence in UTIs among diabetic patients in Ethiopia remained consistent, regardless of the exclusion of individual studies. This consistency across various analytical approaches strengthens the validity of our conclusions and underscores the reliability of the observed high incidence and antimicrobial resistance patterns of E. coli in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion and recommendation

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide comprehensive insights into the prevalence and antimicrobial resistance patterns of E. coli in UTIs among diabetic patients in Ethiopia. The pooled incidence of UTIs caused by E. coli in this population is notably high, highlighting the vulnerability of diabetic patients to such infections. Regional variations were evident, with the SNNPR showing the highest odds ratio, whereas Oromia exhibited the lowest. The study also underscored a significant change in the odds ratio post-2019, likely influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic. The antimicrobial resistance analysis revealed a concerning trend, with high resistance rates to commonly used antibiotics like Ampicillin and moderate resistance to quinolones and aminoglycosides. Conversely, Nitrofurantoin and ceftriaxone showed promising efficacy against E. coli.

Given the high prevalence of E. coli in UTIs among diabetic patients in Ethiopia and the alarming antimicrobial resistance patterns, there is an urgent need for targeted interventions. Health authorities should prioritize the development and implementation of region-specific antibiotic stewardship programs to curb the misuse of antibiotics and slow down the progression of resistance. Additionally, routine surveillance of antimicrobial resistance should be established to monitor trends and guide empirical therapy effectively. Enhanced public health education focused on proper antibiotic use and diabetes management is also essential to reduce the incidence of UTIs and improve overall health outcomes for diabetic patients. Further research should aim to investigate the underlying factors contributing to regional variations and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on UTI prevalence and resistance patterns.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study effectively addresses the significant contribution of E. coli to UTIs in diabetic patients, a prevalent etiology. It comprehensively assesses the antimicrobial resistance profiles of E. coli, providing valuable insights for the empirical management of UTIs in this patient group. The study’s thorough approach includes protocol registration, heterogeneity analysis, publication bias assessment, and sensitivity analysis, enhancing its validity and reproducibility. However, there are notable limitations, including substantial heterogeneity between the included studies and a lower number of isolates tested in some primary studies. Additionally, the primary studies predominantly used the disc diffusion method for antimicrobial sensitivity testing, which is less preferred compared to the dilution method.
