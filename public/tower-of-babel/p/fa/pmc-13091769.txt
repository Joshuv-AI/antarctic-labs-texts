Voriconazole-resistant Purpureocillium lilacinum endophthalmitis in an agricultural worker: A case report
Authors: Qingqing Xu, Jiachun Su, Zhewei Sun, Fan Yang, Ying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpureocillium lilacinum is an emerging opportunistic pathogen with a distinct ocular tropism, most commonly causing keratitis. We report a case of refractory endophthalmitis in an agricultural worker caused by a voriconazole-resistant strain of P. lilacinum with a detailed genomic sequence. After failure of initial voriconazole therapy, the patient was successfully managed with surgical intervention and a novel, multi-route posaconazole regimen, comprising systemic (intravenous and oral), topical, and intravitreal administration. This case underscores the emergence of voriconazole resistance in P. lilacinum endophthalmitis and demonstrates the successful use of a comprehensive posaconazole-based therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Endophthalmitis is a severe, vision-threatening intraocular infection that can result in irreversible vision loss. Purpureocillium lilacinum (P. lilacinum, formerly known as Paecilomyceslilacinus) is a saprobic, hyaline hyphomycete with a ubiquitous environmental presence, commonly found in soil and decaying material worldwide [1]. This fungus displays a distinct ocular tropism, with most reported human infections presenting as keratitis. But published cases of P. lilacinum endophthalmitis are scarce especially in China, with only isolated cases of keratitis and cutaneous involvement documented to date [2,3]. For this fungi, comprehensive genomic data from clinical isolates remain absent worldwide. This suggests that this specie is understudied.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old male agricultural worker presented with a two-month history of progressive blurred vision and photophobia in his left eye. Prior to referral, he had been diagnosed with unilateral uveitis and treated with subconjunctival corticosteroid injections and intravitreal therapy (unspecified agents) without significant improvement; his visual acuity had declined to light perception only. Ocular examination at our hospital confirmed endophthalmitis, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of vitreous specimens identified P. lilacinum as the causative pathogen. The patient had no history of contact lens use, ocular trauma, eye surgery, or systemic disease. Empirical treatment was initiated with intravenous voriconazole (200 mg every 12 hours), topical voriconazole 0.1% hourly, and a single intravitreal voriconazole injection (0.05 ml of 0.1%). After 10 days, therapeutic drug monitoring confirmed adequate serum voriconazole levels (2.57 μg/mL; reference range 0.7–5 μg/mL), yet ophthalmological evaluation showed worsening fungal infiltration in the anterior chamber (Fig. 1A). The patient subsequently underwent left vitrectomy with silicone oil tamponade. Cytological analysis of the vitreous fluid showed necrotic neutrophils and abundant filamentous fungi (Fig. 1B), and repeat mNGS confirmed P. lilacinum with a high read count (272 sequences). Due to clinical progression and persistent infection, voriconazole resistance was suspected. Voriconazole was discontinued and replaced with intravenous posaconazole (300 mg daily after a loading dose of 300 mg every 12 hours on day one), supplemented by hourly topical posaconazole (17.9 mg/mL intravenous formulation). Antifungal susceptibility testing demonstrated resistance to all tested agents except posaconazole (minimal inhibitory concentration [MIC] 0.5 mg/L; Fig C-D). The patient received three intravitreal injections of posaconazole (2 mg/mL). Ocular pain associated with the concentrated topical posaconazole formulation necessitated a reduction in concentration to 5.9 mg/mL, administered three times daily. Subsequently, the patient developed profuse purulent discharge and swelling pain in the left eye. A corneal perforation was diagnosed, leading to enucleation of the left eye with scleral shell implantation. Postoperatively, the swelling and discharge resolved completely with no systemic spread of infection. The patient was discharged on a two-month regimen of oral and topical posaconazole. At follow-up, he remained systemically well with no evidence of infection recurrence.

Genomic DNA of the P. lilacinum clinical isolate (designated HSDZ) was extracted using the CTAB method and sequenced on both DNBSEQ-T7 and PacBio Sequel IIe platforms by Shanghai Winnerbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The species identity of the clinical isolate HSDZ was confirmed by aligning its internal transcribed spacer region (extracted from the whole genome sequencing assembly) against the P.lilacinum type strain NRRL 895 (GenBank: NR_165946.1), yielding 100% sequence identity and 100% coverage. The assembled genome comprised 115 scaffolds with a total length of 38,280,548 bp, an N50 of 684,842 bp, and a GC content of 58.32%. Phylogenetic analysis using RAxML version 8.2.9 placed HSDZ as a sister clade to P. lilacinum genomes GCA_033847395 and GCA_042920535, confirming morphological monophyly (Fig. 1E). The complete genome sequence has been deposited in GenBank under accession number JBOZXC000000000.

Discussion

P.lilacinum is increasingly recognized as an emerging pathogen in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals. In non-transplant patients, infections have been linked to implanted medical devices, including central venous or peritoneal catheters, intraocular lenses, prosthetic cardiac valves, and cerebrospinal fluid shunts [4]. The fungus is also used in pesticides due to its nematophagous properties, and exposure to plant material—especially via trauma—has been identified as a significant route of acquisition [5]. The patient in our study is a farmer and his occupational exposure to vegetative matter may represent a previously underrecognized transmission route for ocular P. lilacinum infections, warranting heightened clinical suspicion in agricultural populations presenting with atypical ocular inflammation.

Current clinical guidelines recommend voriconazole as first-line therapy for P. lilacinum infection [6]. Empirical evidence indicates that most cases of P. lilacinum keratitis respond well to combined oral and topical voriconazole (10 mg/mL) therapy, achieving clinical resolution without surgical intervention [7]. In vitro antifungal susceptibility of 17 P. lilacinum clinical isolates showed posaconazole and voriconazole had the lowest MIC (median 0.1mg/L and 0.6mg/L, respectively), whereas amphotericin B, flucytosine, and fluconazole were the least active, and susceptibility to echinocandins varied considerably [8]. However, emerging evidence indicates that in vitro resistance to voriconazole does occur, and clinical improvement has been documented in some cases following a switch to posaconazole suspension [9]. The use of posaconazole in the treatment of P. lilacinum endophthalmitis is quite rare, only one oral posaconazole was reported in the published literatures [10]. In our study, the patient failed to respond to voriconazole therapy. Consequently, voriconazole was promptly discontinued and replaced with intravenous posaconazole at 300 mg daily. This was later combined with oral administration of 100 mg posaconazole enteric-coated tablets once daily. Subsequent MIC testing revealed that the P. lilacinum isolate was susceptible only to posaconazole and resistant to all other tested antifungal agents including voriconazole. Serum posaconazole levels were maintained within a safe therapeutic range, and no adverse effects were observed in the patient. To our knowledge, this represents the first documented use of combined high-dose intravenous and oral posaconazole for the successful treatment of P. lilacinum endophthalmitis.

In contrast to previously reported cases that used topical ocular posaconazole suspension, we employed intravenous posaconazole formulation for both topical eye drops and intravitreal injection. This approach was chosen to avoid the complex excipients present in the oral suspension and to achieve higher intraocular drug concentrations. The final posaconazole concentrations used were 5.9 mg/mL for topical administration and 2 mg/mL for intravitreal injection, both of which were well tolerated. Following this intervention, no increase in fungal colonization or exudate in the anterior chamber was observed. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the intravitreal concentration and tolerability of posaconazole, offering valuable clinical insights for its intraocular application in refractory fungal endophthalmitis.

Aliet al. reported that prior steroid use in keratitis patients was associated with delayed presentation and significantly poorer visual outcomes [11]. Similarly, early corticosteroid administration has been linked to rapid clinical deterioration in cases of P. lilacinum endophthalmitis [12]. Our patient also received corticosteroid treatment during the early disease stage. Despite subsequent aggressive multimodal therapy—including topical and intravitreal antifungal agents combined with surgical intervention—the infection progressed irreversibly, ultimately requiring therapeutic evisceration. This case emphasizes the critical need for early microbiological diagnosis and strict avoidance of empiric corticosteroids in suspected fungal endophthalmitis to prevent devastating outcomes.

Despite the clinical significance of P. lilacinum, global case reports remain limited, and comprehensive genomic data for clinical isolates are notably absent from the literature. Currently, the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database contains only 12 publicly available whole-genome sequences of P. lilacinum, predominantly from environmental sources such as aquatic systems, terrestrial soils, and sedimentary deposits. To address this gap, we sequenced the genomic DNA of the clinical isolate from our patient. This work provides the first publicly available whole-genome sequence of a P. lilacinum strain linked to human endophthalmitis, offering a valuable genomic resource for future studies on its antifungal resistance mechanisms and pathogenic virulence.

Conclusion

P. Lilacinum represents an exceptionally rare but vision-threatening cause of fungal endophthalmitis. Clinical management is particularly challenging due to the organism's intrinsic resistance to many antifungals, delays in microbiological identification, and the confounding effects of prior corticosteroid use. This case underscores the critical importance of antifungal susceptibility testing for P. lilacinum isolates to guide therapeutic decisions and assess prognosis. The genomic resource provided in this study will serve as a valuable reference for future research into ocular fungal pathogenesis and facilitate the investigation of species-specific resistance mechanisms.
