Productivity benefits of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease prevention in China
Authors: Yi Zhu, Zan Li, Shengliang Chen, Xuran Huang, Yixiang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough COPD places a significant health and economic burden on Chinese society, its associated productivity losses among working-age adults have not been estimated at the population level.MethodsDynamic life table models, utilizing country-specific data from published literature and public datasets, estimated the gains in productivity-adjusted life-years (PALYs), and associated cost savings from preventing incident COPD among China’s working-age population (20–63 years in men and 20–58 years in women) from 2021 to 2030. Productivity losses, encompassing excess mortality, reduced labor force participation, presenteeism, and absenteeism, were aggregated to determine PALYs lost. Comparing model outputs with and without COPD incidence yielded the potential benefits of prevention. To evaluate the labor saved from preventing smoking and PM2.5 exposure, two additional analyses were conducted to estimate the productivity gains if China achieved its 2030 smoking reduction targets and if PM2.5 levels aligned with WHO guidelines.ResultsFrom 2021 to 2030, the projected elimination of incident COPD among China’s working-age population would yield an estimated 2.13 million PALYs gained (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.80–2.42 million), translating to a $104.38 billion (95% CI: $88.35 - $119.03 billion) contribution to China’s GDP. For both sexes, the saved productivity derived from controlling incident COPD was consistently highest within the 55–59 age bracket, and lowest within the 25–29 age bracket. Independently, achieving recommended 2030 targets for smoking prevalence and PM2.5 levels was estimated to result in gains of 456,158 (95% CI: 439,031–472,576) and 1,091,622 (95% CI:1,057,836-1,121,879) PALYs, respectively, with corresponding economic savings of $24.33 billion (95% CI: $23.44-$25.20 billion) and $58.10 billion (95% CI: $56.36-$59.65 billion).ConclusionsCOPD prevention extends productive life years and enhances workforce productivity across all working-age groups. Policymakers should recognize COPD’s substantial impact on labor output and implement targeted interventions to mitigate these economic losses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24594-z.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition that imposes a substantial and growing burden worldwide [1]. Although largely preventable and treatable, it remains linked to prolonged disability, diminished quality of life, and significant economic and social burden. In 2019, an estimated 391.9 million individuals aged 30–79 years were living with COPD globally, with more than 80% residing in low- and middle-income countries [2]. China accounts for the largest share, with a prevalence of 8.6% among adults aged 20 years and 13.7% among those aged 40 years and older [3]. The economic impact in China is particularly severe, with total direct and indirect costs projected to exceed US$3.4 trillion between 2020 and 2039 [4].

While the clinical and economic burden of COPD is well documented, its broader socioeconomic consequences, particularly related to workforce productivity and societal functioning, remain understudied. COPD contributes to productivity loss through absenteeism (missed workdays due to acute exacerbations and chronic disability), presenteeism (reduced efficiency at work), functional decline, premature retirement, and increased mortality [4–7]. These effects are compounded by the progressive nature of the disease and its impact on health-related quality of life.

To comprehensively capture such productivity-related losses, productivity-adjusted life years (PALYs) have emerged as a novel health economic metric that integrates both mortality and morbidity impacts on productivity. PALYs have been successfully applied to estimate the societal burden of diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular diseases [8–11]. However, despite COPD’s high prevalence, frequent underdiagnosis, and substantial impairment of function, no study has yet employed PALYs to quantify its full productivity-related burden.

This study addresses the gap by applying the PALY framework to assess the productivity losses attributable to COPD in China. In doing so, we also simulate the potential productivity gains achievable through targeted prevention strategies, namely smoking reduction and air pollution control. These two leading modifiable risk factors, especially relevant in China given its high smoking rates (50.8% among males) and elevated PM₂.₅ exposure, offer a strategic entry point for reducing the disease burden [12, 13]. By focusing on working-age adults, this study aims to provide actionable evidence for optimizing resource allocation, informing workplace and policy interventions, and guiding long-term public health planning in China’s evolving demographic and epidemiological landscape. In times of global economic uncertainty and productivity slowdown, such insights are increasingly vital for sustainable health and economic development.

Study design and methods

Model overview

We constructed dynamic, age-specific and sex-specific life tables to estimate years of life lived, PALYs, and associated economic costs in China from 2021 to 2030 among the working-age population (men aged 20–63 years and women aged 20–58 years). The life tables were stratified into two subpopulations: individuals with COPD and those without the condition.

To reflect the dynamic nature of demographic change, the models incorporated: (1) projected population changes over the follow-up period, including age- and sex-specific mortality trends; (2) new COPD cases based on age- and sex-specific expected trends; and (3) cohort entry at age 20 years and exit at the retirement age. In 2024, China announced a phased plan to raise the statutory retirement age to 63 years for men (from 60) and to 55 or 58 years for women (from 50 to 55), depending on occupation. The model assumed retirement at age 63 for men and 58 for women to fully account for COPD-related productivity loss [14]. Thus, the baseline included males aged 10 to 63 years and females aged 10 to 58 years. Individuals aged 10–19 years in 2020 were also included, because they would progressively enter the working-age population (≥ 20 years) during the 10-year projection period. For example, individuals aged 10 in 2020 would reach 20 years of age by 2030, the final year of the projection.

This study employed two comparative models to estimate the potential benefits of COPD prevention: a counterfactual scenario in which COPD incidence was set to zero throughout the study period, and a reference scenario reflecting current epidemiological projections. Differences in outcomes (years of life, PALYs, and associated economic costs) between the two scenarios represented the potential gains achievable through effective COPD prevention. The key model inputs and their data sources are shown in Table S1. The World Health Organization (WHO) standard 3% annual discount rate [15] was applied to all years of life lived, PALYs lived and related costs.

Data on COPD prevalence in 2020 and incidence from 1990 to 2021 were sourced from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021), reported in five-year age intervals and by sex [17].

To generate continuous age-specific estimates, values for each five-year age group were assumed to represent the midpoint of the interval (e.g., age 22 for the 20–24 group) [8, 9]. These were then interpolated to single-year ages using quadratic regression for incidence rate and exponential regression for prevalence, based on model fit assessed by R2.

To project the incidence of COPD from 2021 to 2030, we applied a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model using integrated nested Laplace approximations. This approach has shown improved accuracy and coverage relative to alternative forecasting methods [18, 19]. Analyses were conducted with the BAPC package in R, following standard procedures established in previous studies [19, 20].

Mortality rates

Baseline mortality data were derived from the 2020 China Census [16]. Temporal trends in population mortality risk over the modelling period were projected using the EAPC for China, calculated from the United Nations World Population Prospects forecast [21].

Temporal trends in rates were assessed using a log-linear regression model, from which the EAPC was derived. The model was specified as Y = α + βX + ε, where Y denotes the natural logarithm of the rate, X is the calendar year, α is the intercept, β is the coefficient indicating the trend over time, and ε is the random error. The EAPC was computed as 100 × [exp(β) − 1], quantifying the average annual rate of change [18].

Mortality estimates for COPD and non-COPD subpopulations were derived by combining population-wide mortality data with COPD-specific excess mortality risks. First, we obtained a fixed-effect pooled hazard ratio (HR) of 1.40 (95% CI 1.28–1.53) from a meta-analysis assessing the association between mortality and various COPD diagnostic criteria [22]. The pooled hazard ratio was then applied to estimate mortality rates for the two subpopulations. Labor force participation

To estimate productivity loss, we applied sex- and age-specific labor force participation rates in five-year intervals, covering males aged 20–63 years and females aged 20–58 years. Labor force participation in China was sourced from the International Labor Organization (ILO) estimates for 2020 [24, 25]. Labor force participation was lowest in men aged 60–64 years (55.9%) and in women aged 55–59 years (55.3%). The participation was highest in men aged 30–34 years (91.2%) and in women aged 40–44 years (84.4%).

Productivity indices

Consistent with prior studies [8–10], we assessed productivity loss attributable to COPD using two indices: the COPD-related labor force dropout rate, which quantifies the reduction in workforce participation among individuals with COPD compared to those without; and the productivity index, which represents an individual’s work output on a scale from 0 (no productivity) to 1 (full productivity), capturing performance impairments due to health limitations.

Although numerous studies have explored the impact of COPD on work productivity [26–29], a consolidated quantitative estimate has been lacking. To address this gap, we performed meta-analyses to derive two key measures: the COPD-related labor force dropout rate and composite productivity index. The pooled labor force dropout rate among individuals with COPD was 92.794%. The latter incorporated both absenteeism and presenteeism components.

Absenteeism referred to the number of workdays missed annually due to COPD and was calculated as a proportion of the total possible working days (245 days/year in China) [9], yielding an estimated productivity reduction of 0.846% based on an average of 2.073 lost days per year. Presenteeism captured self-reported reductions in on-the-job productivity and was estimated at 4.5%. Together, these figures indicated a total productivity loss of 5.346%, corresponding to a productivity index of 0.947.

In contrast, those without the condition were assumed to experience no productivity losses, maintaining a dropout rate of zero and a productivity index of 1. These relative measures were subsequently applied to the 2020 ILO data, stratified by sex and age, to estimate workforce participation in populations with and without COPD. More methodological details were provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Estimation of PALYs and value of PALYs

To estimate the PALYs lived by the population, the number of years spent in the labor force was calculated by multiplying the years of life lived by the corresponding labor force participation rate [9, 11]. These labor force years were then adjusted by productivity indices to derive PALYs [8, 9].

Due to the absence of data distinguishing between full- and part-time employment, all individuals were assumed to be engaged in equivalent full-time (EFT) work. Consistent with previous research [9, 11, 23], we assumed that the economic value of each PALY was equivalent to the annual GDP per worker. Data on per-worker GDP in China from 2010 to 2023 were obtained from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Compendium of Productivity Levels [30]. Based on these data, we calculated the EAPC and used it to project GDP per worker from 2024 to 2030 (Shown in Table S2).

Sensitivity and scenario analyses

To evaluate the potential productivity gains from mitigating risk factors, we modelled two intervention scenarios. The first aligned with the targets of the “Healthy China 2030” initiative, which aims to reduce the national smoking rate to 20% by 2030 [31]. The second followed the WHO’s air quality guidelines [32], setting a target annual mean concentration for PM2.5 at 15 µg/m³. In the model, we assumed a linear decline in smoking prevalence and ambient PM2.5 exposure over the 10-year projection period. These reductions in exposure were expected to lead to decreases in both COPD incidence and all-cause mortality. The health benefits resulting from these reductions were quantified using potential impact fractions (PIFs), which were applied to age- and sex-specific incidence and mortality rates to estimate the avoidable disease burden attributable to each intervention [33]. Specifically, PIFs were used to adjust baseline rates by multiplying the original COPD incidence or all-cause mortality rates by (1 − PIF), yielding the expected rates under the reduced exposure scenarios. The difference between the baseline and adjusted rates reflects the proportion of cases or deaths potentially averted due to the intervention. More methodological details were provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

In addition, sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore the impact of key input data on years of life lived, PALYs, and associated costs. First, the annual discount rate was increased to 5%, consistent with the WHO’s standard recommendation [15]. Second, GDP per worker was assumed to remain constant at 2020 levels. Third and fourth, both COPD incidence and mortality rates were held fixed at their 2020 values. Lastly, prevalence estimates were replaced with figures derived from a national population-based survey [3] to evaluate the influence of alternative disease burden assumptions on the outcomes.

Probabilistic sensitivity analyses

To reflect uncertainty in model inputs, we performed 1,000 simulation iterations. In each run, key parameters were randomly sampled from normal distributions defined by the respective standard errors. For parameters where CIs were not available, we assumed a 10% standard error around the mean [34]. From the resulting distributions of estimated life years, PALYs, and costs, we reported the median values along with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles as the point estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata and R software, with two-sided tests applied throughout.

Discussion

Prevention of COPD among China’s working-age population from 2021 to 2030 was projected to generate substantial gains in productivity-adjusted life years and associated economic value, despite no observed change in total years of life lived. These findings highlight that even over a relatively short-term horizon, COPD prevention can yield meaningful productivity benefits, primarily through improved labor force participation and reductions in absenteeism and presenteeism.

While direct comparisons are limited, our findings align with existing literature on productivity and the economic consequences of COPD. Evidence from diverse settings consistently shows that individuals with COPD have lower employment rates, take more sick leave, and experience reduced work performance compared to those without the disease [5, 35, 36]. A recent modelling study projected that the global indirect costs attributable to COPD could reach US$15.43 trillion cumulatively by 2050 [37]. These observed productivity losses are biologically plausible: persistent respiratory symptoms, such as dyspnea, chronic cough, and fatigue, reduce functional capacity, while systemic inflammation, hypoxemia, and comorbidities (e.g., depression, muscle wasting, cognitive decline) further impair individuals’ ability to sustain employment and perform effectively at work [29, 38, 39]. Building on this evidence, our study goes a step further by quantifying the productivity impact of COPD at the population level, offering a comprehensive societal perspective on its economic burden.

Differences in labor productivity by age, sex, and socioeconomic status are well established, and our study reinforces these disparities in the context of COPD-related productivity loss [40–42]. Men accounted for nearly twice the PALYs and associated economic value saved compared with women, likely due to higher COPD prevalence, longer working life, and greater labor force participation [3, 8]43– [45]. Additional losses may arise from men’s higher likelihood of employment in physically demanding jobs, where COPD has a stronger impact on work capacity [46, 47]. We also found that eliminating COPD yielded the greatest productivity gains among individuals aged 55–59 years, irrespective of sex. Although direct evidence is lacking, a modelling study offers relevant insight, projecting that adults aged 50–59 years would account for the highest indirect absenteeism costs over a 20-year period from 2019 to 2038 in the United States [48]. This pattern may be partly explained by the relatively high incidence of COPD and sustained labor force participation in this age group. Further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms. While our model did not include socioeconomic stratification, prior research indicates that individuals from lower-income or lower-education backgrounds are more likely to face hazardous working conditions, limited access to care, and greater difficulty maintaining employment during illness [41, 49]. These findings underscore the need for targeted prevention strategies that account for demographic and socioeconomic differences to maximize the health and economic benefits of COPD control.

Regarding annual gains in productivity-adjusted life years and economic value, the projected benefits rose initially, peaked in 2024–2026, and gradually declined toward 2030. This trend reflects the interaction between age-specific COPD incidence, labor force dynamics, and the timing of prevention effects. In the early simulation years, preventing COPD in high-incidence age groups with sustained labor force participation, particularly those aged 50–59 years, led to increasing productivity gains. This pattern aligns with our stratified analysis, which identified adults aged 55–59 years as contributing the largest PALY gains. However, in later years, many of these individuals approached or reached retirement, reducing their contribution to workforce productivity. At the same time, younger cohorts (10–19 years old at baseline) entered the labor force. Although included in the prevention scenario, these individuals had very low COPD incidence [17], and thus the associated productivity gains were limited. Taken together, these demographic and epidemiological transitions explain the decline in annual PALY gains observed in the latter part of the projection period.

Risk factor reduction strategies targeting tobacco use and ambient air pollution could offer meaningful gains in workforce productivity and associated economic value, as demonstrated by our scenario-based analysis. Although our results suggest greater productivity gains under the PM₂.₅ reduction scenario, these differences should be interpreted with caution. First, the apparent advantage of PM₂.₅ reflects, in part, key differences in the modelling assumptions: PM₂.₅ exposure was applied uniformly across the entire working-age population, whereas smoking exposure and its reduction were modelled based on a narrower at-risk group defined by age-specific prevalence. Second, the feasibility, cost, and timeline for achieving these reductions may vary substantially between air pollution control and tobacco control interventions, limiting direct comparability [50]. As such, the estimated economic gains should be viewed as indicative of the potential scale of benefit rather than as a definitive ranking of policy effectiveness. Nevertheless, strategies aimed at lowering smoking rate and air pollution should be considered as long-term investment rather than an expenditure, since they are likely to reduce healthcare spending and increase productivity in the long term [11], as well as improving health equity and helping sustain social welfare systems [10]. From a resource allocation perspective, our estimates can support more informed and objective decision-making when determining the resources needed to reduce COPD incidence and improve COPD-related outcomes.

The smoking reduction scenario also revealed stark sex disparities: of the total PALYs saved, 447,462 (98%) were attributed to men, while only 8,706 (2%) were attributed to women. This imbalance is consistent with China’s highly skewed smoking prevalence, in which over half of men smoke compared with fewer than 5% of women [12]. While male populations also demonstrated greater gains in the COPD prevention and PM₂.₅ scenarios, the smoking scenario substantially amplified these disparities due to the sex-specific nature of exposure. In terms of age-specific patterns, both smoking and PM₂.₅ scenarios showed greatest productivity benefits in older working-age groups, aligning with the burden distribution observed in the COPD prevention scenario. Notably, annual PALY and economic gains peaked later in the simulation period (2027–2028), a trend shaped by the assumption of linear exposure decline over time. Unlike the COPD prevention scenario, where incidence was immediately halted, the risk reduction in smoking and PM₂.₅ scenarios accumulated gradually, leading to a delayed but sustained benefit. Together, these findings underscore not only the scale of productivity losses attributable to modifiable risk factors, but also the importance of demographic and temporal dynamics in shaping the trajectory and distribution of intervention benefits.

There are several features for this study. First, it is the first to quantify the productivity impact of COPD at the population level using PALYs. This measure combines various determinants of productivity (mortality, presenteeism, absenteeism, labor force participation), and focuses on the working-age population, offering new evidence for health resource decisions. Second, we assessed the potential PALYs benefits under different intervention scenarios. By modeling the preventable PALYs loss related to smoking and PM2.5 exposure, we estimated the potential return on investment of smoking cessation and air quality improvement efforts. Third, we used an open dynamic cohort model. This allowed us to simulate the natural development of COPD in population by accounting for trends in incidence, prevalence, and mortality, providing a more realistic projection of future productivity burden driven by new COPD cases. Finally, we estimated the productivity burden across different age and sex subgroups. This helps to identify the key subgroups for targeted action.

The current study also has some limitations. First, we used data from the GBD 2021 for COPD incidence, and prevalence due to the lack of updated national data. To address potential bias, sensitivity analyses using data from a national COPD survey was conducted, showing similar results. Second, due to the limited availability of relevant studies on the productivity burden of COPD in China, we conducted a meta-analysis of existing international data to estimate these effects as a proxy for the Chinese population. These may not fully reflect the situation in China. Third, our model could not distinguish productivity losses caused by COPD from those due to comorbid conditions. Fourth, due to the unavailability of data, certain input parameters in our model, such as the estimation of COPD mortality risk, were not differentiated by age and gender. This simplification may affect the accuracy of our estimates to some extent. However, given the relatively short 10-year timeframe, the impact of these assumptions on the overall findings is minimal, as mortality rates are less sensitive to such variations within this period. Fifth, as many individuals with COPD in China remain undiagnosed [3], our estimates of the productivity burden might be underestimated. Sixth, our estimation of the productivity impact of ambient air pollution assumed uniform PM₂.₅ exposure across the entire working-age population. While this approach reflects population-level exposure averages, it does not capture spatial or occupational heterogeneity in exposure, potentially underestimating the burden in high-risk subgroups. Seventh, we assumed reductions in smoking and PM₂.₅ exposure affect all-cause mortality, which may underestimate the direct benefits among COPD patients. However, to ensure consistency in population-level mortality, we proportionally distributed the mortality reduction across COPD and non-COPD groups based on their relative risks, meaning individuals with COPD still received a substantial share of the benefit. Finally, due to the lack of stratified data, our analysis did not incorporate SES-specific estimates. Given the strong evidence linking lower socioeconomic status with increased COPD risk and limited access to care, future research should incorporate SES-specific exposure patterns, disease burden, and productivity losses to better guide equitable policy interventions.

Conclusion

This study underscores the substantial societal and economic burden of COPD in China and highlights the significant productivity and GDP gains that could be realized through prevention efforts, particularly among the working-age population, providing critical evidence to support action in China and other high-burden countries. This evidence can inform national health and development planning, strengthen the economic rationale for pollution and smoking control policies, and support the integration of respiratory health into broader workforce and environmental agendas. Future research should assess the scalability, cost-effectiveness, and real-word implementation pathways of such interventions, and explore the differential impact of COPD across subpopulations, industries, and geographic regions. A more nuanced understanding of these variations will be essential for designing targeted, equitable, and sustainable strategies to reduce the burden of COPD and improve population health outcomes.
