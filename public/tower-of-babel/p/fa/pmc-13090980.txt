Isolated Acute Exertional Compartment Syndrome of the Extensor Carpi Ulnaris Muscle
Authors: Tara Saxena, Tanya Cruz, Dalibel Bravo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Compartment syndrome encompasses a spectrum of disease. The most recognized subset of compartment syndrome is acute traumatic compartment syndrome, which is typically readily diagnosed by trained practitioners. A less-known type of compartment syndrome is acute exertional compartment syndrome, as the clinical presentation is secondary to heavy activity rather than trauma, can be isolated to a single muscle, and does not resolve with rest and supportive care. Acute exertional compartment syndrome is often complicated by a delay in diagnosis due to a lack of awareness on the subject. We present a case of acute exertional compartment syndrome of the extensor carpi ulnaris muscle treated with isolated muscle fasciotomy with symptom resolution. Awareness of the diagnosis and treatment allows for improved patient outcomes.

Compartment syndrome is an orthopedic emergency characterized by an increase in fascial compartment pressures that leads to compromised circulation, tissue necrosis, and possible limb loss. Acute exertional compartment syndrome (AECS) is a rare form of acute compartment syndrome, often leading to delayed diagnosis. Although more commonly occurring in the lower extremities, it can also occur in the upper extremities.1

Specifically in the forearm, there are three compartments: the volar compartment, the dorsal compartment, and the mobile wad. Although compartment syndrome will typically involve one of these compartments entirely, there have been subcompartments described. One of these subcompartments is the extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) muscle, making the ECU a candidate for isolated AECS.2 This case report describes a healthy 45-year-old man with isolated ECU AECS demonstrating its rarity, diagnostic ambiguity, and importance of early recognition and surgical intervention.

Case Report

A healthy 45-year-old right-hand dominant man who works in commercial lobster fishing presented to the emergency room with severe right forearm pain after 4 days of heavy labor lifting lobster cages in hot and humid conditions. Of note, this is the patient’s profession and not an unusual amount of activity. On physical examination, the ECU muscle compartment was tense. However, the remaining muscles in the dorsal compartment were soft, as were the remaining compartments. He was able to make a composite fist with pain. He had pain with passive wrist flexion. There were no wounds or skin changes. Sensation was intact, and pulses were equal to the contralateral side. Inflammatory markers and renal function were within normal limits. His creatinine kinase was elevated at 1,233 U/L (reference range, 26−308 U/L). Computed tomography scan and magnetic resonance imaging were ordered by the emergency department, which demonstrated isolated edema to the ECU muscle (Fig. 1). He was initially diagnosed with rhabdomyolysis and treated with fluids, pain control, ice, and elevation. The following morning, his pain had only minimally improved despite the above treatment, and he was beginning to develop a burning pain along the ulnar forearm and hand, which was indicative of dorsal ulnar sensory nerve involvement. His creatinine kinase had minimally increased to 1,590 U/L. At that point, he was clinically diagnosed with AECS isolated to the ECU muscle based on his symptoms and physical examination. He was taken to the operating room for fasciotomy under general anesthesia. A skin incision was made over the ECU starting approximately 6 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle and extending halfway down the forearm to the musculotendinous junction. After skin incision, the ECU muscle was noted to be bulging within the fascia (Fig. 2), but all other muscles were normal appearing. The fascia to the ECU muscle was incised throughout the length of the muscle belly. The dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve was identified and protected. The muscle was healthy pink appearing at the end of the case (Fig. 3). The proximal and distal ends of the incision were closed with 2-0 nylon, and the remaining central 8 cm were closed with a black sponge wound vac and vessel loops using a Jacob’s ladder technique with staples (Fig. 4). After surgery, the patient had immediate pain relief and no further pain medication requirement. He was able to make a composite fist without pain and had a normal neurovascular examination. On postoperative day 5, he returned to the operating room for irrigation and closure of the remainder of the incision with nylon sutures. He was placed in a soft dressing and allowed to perform activities of daily living with his right arm. His sutures were removed 2 weeks after surgery. He had full elbow, forearm, and wrist range of motion compared to the contralateral side, and he was allowed progressive return to activity at that time (Fig. 5). Because of his profession involving exposure to ocean water, he was not cleared to return to the lobster boat until 4 weeks after surgery to reduce the risk of a soft tissue infection. The patient had complete resolution of symptoms at his 12-week follow-up and had returned to his prior level of activity at work. Discussion

While acute traumatic compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency, AECS may have a more indolent presentation. It can be deceiving as the remainder of the compartment will be soft, compressible, and nontender in contrast to acute traumatic compartment syndrome, which tends to have more diffuse involvement. AECS differs from chronic exertional compartment syndrome in that symptoms do not resolve after cessation of exercise.3,4

There are two cases of isolated ECU AECS in the literature to date. The first case report of isolated ECU AECS was in 1977, referred to as exercise myopathy. In this case, the patient was a 37-year-old man who had been rowing a canoe the day prior to presentation. He was sent home from the emergency department initially and presented the following morning with persistent symptoms and was then taken to the operating room for fasciotomy of the ECU muscle. At the time of surgery, the muscle was pale with punctate bleeding. The patient had full return of strength 1 week after surgery.5

The second case was a 35-year-old mechanic who was doing heavy hammering the day prior to arrival. He presented with altered sensation. On release of the compartment during surgery, the muscle was dusky, and the biopsy demonstrated muscle necrosis. He was closed temporarily with skin substitute, and then returned to the operating room 3 days later for skin closure. Despite having myonecrosis on biopsy, the patient had complete return of function and sensation by 2 months.4

There are multiple case reports of isolated muscle AECS both in the upper and lower extremities.4,6 Parhar et al6 reported a case of AECS in bilateral deltoid, supraspinatus, and trapezius muscles after heavy exercise during his occupation as a firefighter, followed by burpees. The ECU muscle may be more prone to AECS compared to other forearm muscles, as there is commonly a separate dense subcompartment. This is seen in the anconeus and extensor pollicus longus muscles in the dorsal forearm compartment as well.3

All cases reported to date of AECS have been in younger men. This is consistent with studies on acute traumatic compartment syndrome, with an average age of 36 and a male-to-female ratio of 5.5 to 1.7 This reason for this is multifactorial including higher muscle mass, thicker fascia and intermuscular septa due to higher collagen content.8

Acute traumatic and atraumatic compartment syndrome is managed with fasciotomy without skin closure and return to the operating room for subsequent debridement versus closure or grafting. In contrast, when treated surgically, chronic exertional compartment syndrome is treated with fasciotomy and skin closure.3 An early study by Sheridan et al9 did not identify any increased risks when “closed” fasciotomies were performed for compartment syndrome compared to open fasciotomies, although the authors did report that this was likely confounded by selection bias as closed fasciotomies were likely only performed when swelling was minimal. There is currently insufficient literature to determine if AECS can be treated with fasciotomy and skin closure in a single rather than staged procedure. In our case, we performed a delayed closure out of an abundance of caution with immediate resolution of symptoms and eventual return to full activity.
