Enzyme-mimetic nanodots catalytically alleviate stress-induced hypertension via anti-neuroinflammation effects
Authors: Jiayi Xu, Liang Chen, Zhangyan Ren, Rui Guo, Chen Yu, Wei Guo, Dongshu Du, Yu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStress-induced hypertension (SIH), driven by reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) accumulation and neuroinflammation in the rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), lacks effective targeted therapies. Here, we synthesize ultrasmall manganese-doped carbon dots (Mn@CDs) (~ 2.5 nm) with multienzyme-mimetic activities, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase. Mn@CDs enable effective scavenging of diverse types of ROS/RNS, thereby protecting rat neuroblastoma B104 and microglial GMI-R1 cells from H2O2-induced damage. Mn@CDs treatment also suppresses the cell apoptosis (via regulating Bcl-2/Bax and cleaved caspase-3) and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) of GMI-R1 cells. In SIH rats, injection of Mn@CDs into RVLM normalizes arterial/systolic/diastolic blood pressure and renal sympathetic nerve activity. Mechanistically, Mn@CDs downregulate NOX2/NOX4 within RVLM region and alleviate oxidative stress, thus reversing microglial activation and reducing neuronal apoptosis. Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases in modern society, affecting diverse populations and serving as a critical risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [1–3]. The etiology of hypertension is multifactorial, among which reactive oxygen species (ROS) emerge as a key pathogenic mediator. ROS are a family of oxidative molecules predominantly produced as byproducts of physiological metabolic reactions or via enzymatic pathways [4]. At supraphysiological concentrations, ROS induce profound detrimental effects in vivo, including neuronal damage, inflammatory response exacerbation, apoptotic cell death, and disruptions to cellular redox homeostasis [5–7], all of which are highly relevant to hypertensive pathogenesis. The RVLM serves as the central integrative hub for regulating pressure-sensitive sympathetic nerve outflow, thereby playing an irreplaceable role in both short-term hemodynamic adjustment and long-term blood pressure homeostasis [8]. Accumulating evidence suggests that aberrant oxidative stress and sustained neuroinflammation in the RVLM are not merely associated with, but actively drive the pathogenesis of neurogenic hypertension [9, 10]. Notably, SIH, a clinically relevant subtype linked to chronic psychological or environmental stressors including occupational, social, economic, and environmental factors, arises from sustained sympathetic activation that drives oxidative stress, autonomic imbalance, and systemic inflammation [11]. Despite its relatively well-defined pathophysiology, the therapeutic strategies, specifically targeting the resulting RVLM-centered neuroinflammation remain underdeveloped. Given the suboptimal pharmacokinetics of small molecules drugs, the development of non-pharmaceutical intervention strategies represents an alternative route for resolving neuroinflammation in SIH [12, 13].

To maintain cellular and systemic redox homeostasis while mitigating the adverse effects of excessive ROS accumulation, living organisms are pare equipped with a sophisticated endogenous antioxidant defense system, which includes key enzymes responsible for scavenging or neutralizing ROS, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), etc [14]. However, endogenous antioxidant enzymes often face limitations under pathological conditions, such as susceptibility to denaturation and insufficient activity during severe oxidative stress. Nanozymes have emerged as promising candidates to address this gap by virtue of their biomimetic catalytic activities to various antioxidant enzymes [15]. To date, numerous nanozymes have been demonstrated to effectively scavenge ROS and exert therapeutic effects in biological systems [16–20]. Particularly, the applications of antioxidant nanozymes in alleviating inflammation-associated neurodegenerative disorders and other inflammation-driven pathologies have garnered growing attention in the field of nanomedicine, owing to their superior stability, tunable catalytic efficiency, and longer retention time compared to endogenous antioxidant enzymes. Nevertheless, the intervention efficacy of such antioxidant nanozymes on SIH has scarcely been explored.

Carbon-based nanomaterials have attracted significant research interest due to their unique properties, including excellent biocompatibility, biosafety, and high stability [21]. As zero-dimensional nanomaterials, carbon dots (CDs) have demonstrated not only high biocompatibility and easy of synthesis, but also multiple enzyme-like catalytic activities [22]. Specifically, CAT- and SOD-mimicking CDs have been extensively utilized for the nanocatalytic treatment of inflammation-associated diseases [23–26]. Our group reported that manganese-doped CDs can effectively inhibit cell pyroptosis and intervertebral disc degeneration progression [27]. Inspired by that, we hypothesized that the enzyme-mimetic nanodots hold promise in catalytically suppressing SIH via anti-neuroinflammation effects. Here, the green manganese-doped nanodots (Mn@CDs) were synthesized by a facile hydrothermal method [28]. The multienzyme-mimetic antioxidant properties of Mn@CDs were evaluated. Subsequently, the intracellular antioxidant and neuroprotective effects of Mn@CDs were systematically evaluated. Last, the neuroprotective and antihypertensive effects of Mn@CDs in SIH rats were evaluated (Fig. 1).

Results and discussion

Characterization of Mn@CDs

Mn@CDs were synthesized by the method mentioned above. The synthesized Mn@CDs showed good dispersion and stability (Figure S1). TEM images revealed that the synthesized Mn@CDs were spherical and well-dispersed, with an average particle size of ~ 2.5 nm (Fig. 2a). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images further showed distinct lattice fringes with a spacing of ~ 0.21 nm (Fig. 2b). The average hydrodynamic size of Mn@CDs, as determined by DLS, was ~ 5 nm, which is slightly larger than the TEM observations due to the hydration shell (Fig. 2c). XPS analysis identified four major peaks corresponding to Mn 2p (642 eV), O 1s (532 eV), N 1s (400 eV), and C 1s (235 eV), with elemental compositions of 2.07% Mn, 17.87% O, 2.92% N, and 77.15% C, respectively (Fig. 2d). High-resolution XPS scans of the Mn 2p region suggested that Mn in Mn@CDs primarily exists in the form of Mn³⁺ and Mn⁴⁺ (Fig. 2e). Mn3+ is an important component of Mn-SOD (SOD2), a natural antioxidant enzyme in cells, which plays an important role in scavenging intracellular ROS [29, 30]. Meanwhlie, compounds containing Mn4+, such as MnO2, have been shown to exhibit ROS scavenging and antioxidant effects [31]. These observations validate the successful fabrication of ultrasmall Mn@CDs, and indicate that Mn@CDs has an antioxidant potential.

Enzyme-mimicking activities of Mn@CDs

Subsequently, the Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC) of Mn@CDs was evaluated using the ABTS assay. At concentrations ranging from 20 to 320 µg/mL, the antioxidant activity of Mn@CDs increased with concentrations, with 320 µg/mL Mn@CDs significantly suppressing almost 80% ABTS·⁺ formation (Fig. 2f). Naturally, Mn@CDs also exhibited antioxidant activity in DMEM (Figure S2). The CAT-like activity of Mn@CDs was assessed by measuring their ability to decompose H₂O₂. Mn@CDs demonstrated a noticeable ability to catalyze the decomposition of H₂O₂ into O₂ (Figure S3). Mn@CDs also showed concentration-dependent H₂O₂ decomposition activity at concentrations ranging from 20 to 160 µg/mL (Fig. 2g). Notably, over 60% of H₂O₂ (100 µM) can be rapidly decomposed by Mn@CDs at the concentration of 160 µg/mL.

The SOD-like activity of Mn@CDs was evaluated using the WST-8 assay. At concentrations ranging from 40 to 100 µg/mL, Mn@CDs exhibited obvious SOD like activity and accelerated the reaction rate in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure S4). The inhibition percentage, calculated based on the slope of absorbance changes, increased with Mn@CDs concentration (Fig. 2h). The data indicates that Mn@CDs can mimic the catalytic activity of SOD to eliminate O₂·⁻. The POD-like activity of Mn@CDs was evaluated by measuring their ability to oxidize ABTS in the presence of H₂O₂. At concentrations ranging from 20 to 100 µg/mL, the absorbance of the reaction system increased linearly with Mn@CDs concentration, and the reaction rate also showed a concentration-dependent increase (Figure S5a-b), demonstrating a linear relationship between reaction rate and concentration (Fig. 2i). Meanwhile, Mn@CDs also exhibited typical pH-related differences in POD-like activity (Figure S5c), with the activity being relatively limited under physiological conditions. Therefore, we speculate that the SOD- and CAT-like activities are the primary contributors to the overall antioxidant capacity of Mn@CDs.

Additionally, the ability of Mn@CDs to scavenge two common ROS, O₂·⁻ and ·OH, was assessed using ESR spectroscopy. ESR spectroscopy revealed that Mn@CDs significantly weakened the spin resonance signals of O₂·⁻ and ·OH, indicating effective scavenging of these radicals (Fig. 2j and k). The RNS scavenging capacity of Mn@CDs was evaluated using the DPPH assay. As expected, the decrease in the DPPH absorption peak showed a positive correlation with Mn@CDs concentration (Fig. 2l), and the statistical analysis further support this result (Figure S6). All above results suggest that the developed ultrasmall Mn@CDs can catalytically scavenge various types of ROS/RNS, holding promising in alleviating neuroinflammation, while the primary enzyme-mimetic activities of Mn@CDs originate from Mn doping, whereas undoped CDs exhibit no such activities, as previously reported in our work [27].

In vitro cytoprotective effect of Mn@CDs

Given the antioxidant effects of Mn@CDs, its cytoprotective effect was evaluated using an oxidative damage model established by treating cells with H₂O₂. Experiments were conducted using two cell lines: rat neuroblastoma cells (B104) and rat microglial cells (R1). H₂O₂ has concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, and the optimal concentration for inducing oxidative damage was determined using the CCK-8 assay. The H₂O₂ concentration that reduced cell viability to approximately 50% of the control group was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3a, b). The cytotoxicity of Mn@CDs was first assessed using the CCK-8 assay. The data revealed that Mn@CDs had no negative effect on cell viability in the absence of H2O2, even at a high concentration of 100 µg/mL (Figure S7). In comparison, Mn@CDs pretreatment significantly enhanced the viability of both B104 and R1 cells after H₂O₂ treatment, demonstrating significant cytoprotective effect against oxidative damage compared to untreated cells (Fig. 3c, d). TUNEL assays on B104 cells further confirmed that Mn@CDs reduced H₂O₂-induced apoptosis (Fig. 3e), with quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity supporting this observation (Fig. 3g). Additionally, Annexin V-PI staining and flow cytometry analysis showed that Mn@CDs decreased the proportion of cells in both early and late apoptotic stages after H₂O₂ treatment, increasing the percentage of viable cells by 31.6% (Fig. 3f).

The protective effects of Mn@CDs were also analyzed by WB assay. Cleaved caspase-3, Bcl-2, and Bax are well-established markers associated with apoptosis. Caspase-3 plays a critical role in the apoptotic cascade, and the activated caspase-3 is a key executor of apoptosis [32]. Cleaved caspase-3 and Bax are pro-apoptotic proteins, while Bcl-2 is anti-apoptotic. The ratio of Bcl-2 to Bax (Bcl-2/Bax) serves as an indicator of apoptosis. WB analysis revealed that, compared to the positive control group, Mn@CDs-treated B104 cells showed downregulated expression of cleaved caspase-3 and Bax, along with upregulated expression of Bcl-2, resulting in a significantly increased Bcl-2/Bax ratio (Fig. 3h, i). These findings suggest that Mn@CDs could regulate the expression level of these apoptotic markers to be closer to normal cells. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Mn@CDs can effectively inhibit H₂O₂-induced apoptosis in vitro.

In vitro ROS scavenging and anti-inflammation ability of Mn@CDs

The cytoprotective effects of Mn@CDs are presumably attributed to their antioxidant enzyme-mimetic activities. Thus, the ROS scavenging ability of Mn@CDs in vitro was validated using the H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress model in B104 cells. DCFH-DA, a fluorescent probe that reacts with intracellular ROS to produce green fluorescence, was first used to measure intracellular ROS levels. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in fluorescence intensity in B104 cells after H₂O₂ stimulation, which was effectively reversed by Mn@CDs (Fig. 4a). Similarly, confocal microscopy observations showed strong green fluorescence in H₂O₂-treated cells, whereas Mn@CDs-pretreated cells showed significantly reduced fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4b). Statistical analysis of the relative fluorescence intensity further confirmed these findings (Fig. 4d). The lipid oxidation level can reflect the oxidative stress in cells, while MDA is a product of lipid oxidation. The detection of MDA content in B104 cells showed a significant increase of the MDA content in B104 after H2O2 stimulation, and Mn@CDs can suppress this increase (Fig. 4e).

Oxidative damage can lead to apoptosis, and a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) is an early hallmark of this process. JC-1, a fluorescent probe used to measure ΔΨm, emits red fluorescence when ΔΨm is high and green fluorescence when ΔΨm is low. The shift from red to green fluorescence indicates mitochondrial depolarization. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that H₂O₂-stimulated B104 cells showed increased green fluorescence compared to the control group, while Mn@CDs reduced the portion of cells with elevated green fluorescence (Fig. 4c). These results indicate that Mn@CDs can effectively scavenge intracellular ROS and protect cells from oxidative damage in vitro.

Aberrant oxidative stress also triggers inflammatory responses, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by microglial cells. IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 are common pro-inflammatory cytokines. WB analysis showed that the expression levels of those cytokines in Mn@CDs-pretreated GMI-R1 cells were significantly downregulated compared to the positive H2O2-induced group, normalizing the levels identical to that observed in the untreated control group (Fig. 4f, g). These findings suggest that Mn@CDs can alleviate H₂O₂-induced inflammatory responses in microglial cells in vitro.

In vivo antihypertensive ability of Mn@CDs

Next, a SIH rat model was established to evaluate the in vivo antihypertensive effect of Mn@CDs. A comprehensive biosafety experiment was conducted before further in vivo studies. Histopathological examination of major organs revealed no obvious tissue damage or inflammatory changes. Hematological analysis showed no significant alterations in blood cell counts, and serum biochemical profiles indicated normal liver and kidney function. These results demonstrate the favorable biocompatibility of Mn@CDs for central nervous system application (Figure S7-S12). Mn@CDs or saline were injected into the bilateral RVLM of Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. After a 2-day recovery period, the rats were subjected to noise-electric shock stimulation for 15 days, followed by subsequent experiments (Fig. 5a). Physiological parameters, including blood pressure, were measured using femoral artery catheterization. The results showed that, compared to the control group, SIH rats showed significant increases in blood pressure-related indices, including arterial blood pressure (ABP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), as well as elevated heart rate (HR) and enhanced renal sympathetic nerve activity, confirming the successful establishment of the SIH model. In contrast, rats pretreated with Mn@CDs showed significant reductions in these physiological parameters compared to the SIH group (Fig. 5b-h). These findings demonstrate that Mn@CDs can effectively lower blood pressure in SIH rats.

Under normal conditions, microglial cells in the central nervous system (CNS) shows a highly branched morphology. However, in response to inflammation or injury in the CNS, microglial cells become activated, characterized by shortened and reduced branches, enlarged cell bodies, and acquisition of phagocytic capabilities [33]. Therefore, the morphology of microglial cells in the RVLM can serve as an indicator of hypertension. Immunofluorescence staining of RVLM tissue sections was performed to label microglial cells, and their morphology was analyzed using confocal microscopy. Sholl and skeleton analyses revealed that microglial cells in the RVLM of normal rats showed a complex, highly branched morphology. In contrast, microglial cells in SIH rats showed reduced complexity and enlarged cell bodies. Pretreatment with Mn@CDs restored the morphology of microglial cells to a state closer to the microglial cells of normal rats (Fig. 5i- j, Figure S13). These results indicate that Mn@CDs can alleviate microglial activation, which may be attributed to their enzyme-mimetic antioxidant effects.

In vivo ROS scavenging and cytoprotective effect of Mn@CDs

Given the satisfactory antihypertensive effects of Mn@CDs, the therapeutic mechanisms were further investigated in terms of in vivo ROS scavenging and cytoprotective effects. While the in vitro system is induced by acute H₂O₂ treatment, the chronic SIH condition involves sustained production of diverse ROS species. The broad-spectrum enzyme-mimicking activities demonstrated in vitro possibly enables Mn@CDs to play an effective role in the complex oxidation environment in vivo.

The NADPH oxidase (NOX) protein family, which generates superoxide, is one of the primary sources of ROS in the mechanism of neurogenic hypertension. Among these, NOX2 is expressed in microglial cells and participates in immune regulation and homeostasis maintenance, while NOX4 is found in neurons and is involved in key physiological processes such as oxygen sensing [4, 34]. Therefore, the expression levels of NOX2 and NOX4 in the RVLM can indicate the degree of ROS production and oxidative stress. WB results revealed that the expression levels of NOX2 and NOX4 in the RVLM of SIH rats were significantly upregulated compared to the control group. However, Mn@CDs treatment downregulated their expression, reducing the levels closer to the control group. These findings suggest that Mn@CDs can suppress the expression of ROS-related proteins in the brains of SIH rats, potentially contributing to ROS scavenging (Fig. 6a-c).

Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory effects of Mn@CDs were assessed by measuring the levels of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory markers in the RVLM. WB analysis revealed that SIH rats showed upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CD86) and downregulated expression of the anti-inflammatory marker Arg1 in the RVLM compared to normal rats. Pretreatment with Mn@CDs markedly reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory markers and upregulated the expression of anti-inflammatory Arg1 (Fig. 6d, e). Immunofluorescence staining of RVLM tissue sections further demonstrated that the expression of IL-1β in the SIH group was approximately 150% higher than that in the control and Mn@CDs groups, indicating that Mn@CDs can inhibit the expression of inflammatory factors in SIH (Figure S14). Collectively, these results suggest that Mn@CDs can modulate microglial morphological changes and alleviate neuroinflammation in vivo.

In vivo cytoprotective effects of Mn@CDs were evaluated using WB analysis, TUNEL staining, and immunofluorescence imaging. First, the expression of apoptosis-related proteins in the RVLM of different groups was assessed. WB data showed that the expression levels of pro-apoptotic proteins (Cleaved Caspase-3 and Bax) were significantly upregulated in the RVLM of SIH rats compared to the control group, while Mn@CDs treatment downregulated their expression. Conversely, the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was downregulated in SIH rats but maintained at higher levels in the control and Mn@CDs-treated groups. Mn@CDs treatment increased the Bcl-2/Bax ratio compared to the SIH group, indicating their anti-apoptotic effects (Fig. 6f, g).

TUNEL staining of RVLM brain sections was performed to assess neuronal apoptosis. The images revealed that the number of apoptotic neurons in the SIH group increased by approximately 47%. By contrast, Mn@CDs treatment significantly reduced the number of apoptotic neurons, with only a 4% increase compared to the control group (Fig. 6h, i). Immunofluorescence staining of Cleaved Caspase-3 in RVLM sections further supported these findings. The expression of Cleaved Caspase-3 in SIH rats was approximately 3.5 times higher than the control group, whereas Mn@CDs pretreatment significantly reduced its expression (Fig. 6h, j). Overall, these findings indicate that Mn@CDs pretreatment may inhibit apoptosis by modulating the neuroinflammation in RVLM and the expression of apoptosis-related factors, ultimately contributing to blood pressure reduction.

Conclusion

Hypertension, as one of the most prevalent chronic diseases, is a major risk factor to cardiovascular diseases. The direct cause of primary hypertension is the excessive production of ROS in regions of the central nervous system responsible for sympathetic regulation, such as RVLM, leading to oxidative stress and inflammation. Targeting this RVLM-centered pathological mechanism, our study presents a transformative strategy for SIH by developing a biocompatible Mn@CDs nanozyme synthesized from food-grade precursors. Mn@CDs integrates superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase mimetic activities into a single multifunctional platform. The results demonstrated that Mn@CDs can efficiently scavenge ROS/RNS, suppressing ABTS·⁺ formation, decomposing H₂O₂, and attenuating ·O₂⁻/·OH signals. Their cytoprotective effects and antioxidant mechanisms were validated using H2O2-induced neuronal (B104) and microglial (GMI-R1) cells. The biological evaluations indicated that Mn@CDs can restore mitochondrial membrane potential and inhibit cell apoptosis by modulating the Bcl-2/Bax-cleaved caspase-3 axis. The pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) expression in activated microglia was also inhibited by Mn@CDs treatment. In vivo experiments corroborated that bilateral RVLM injection of Mn@CDs normalized arterial, systolic, and diastolic blood pressure as well as renal sympathetic nerve activity in SIH rats. Additionally, Mn@CDs further targeted the root pathogenic mechanisms by downregulating NOX2/NOX4 in the RVLM to alleviate oxidative stress, thus reversing microglial activation (restoring their branched surveillance morphology) and diminishing neuronal apoptosis in vivo. Based on these results, we speculate that the therapeutic effects of Mn@CDs on SIH originate from the Mn-doped structure, which confers broad-spectrum ROS scavenging activity, and this initial antioxidant action subsequently alleviates neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis as downstream cascades. This work validates Mn@CDs as a new nanozyme-based catalytic strategy for SIH intervention and establishes enzyme-mimetic carbon dots as a versatile platform for neuroinflammation-associated cardiovascular disorders.

Methods

Synthesis of Mn@CDs

Mn@CDs were synthesized by a hydrothermal method. Briefly, 2 mmol manganese gluconate and 1.5 mmol L-aspartic acid were dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water and stirred for 30 min. The solution was then transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 4 h. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm after cooling, and the supernatant was collected and further purified by dialysis.

Material characterization

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, the dried Mn@CDs were uniformly dispersed in water and dropped onto a copper grid coated with an ultrathin carbon film; after drying at room temperature, the morphology and structure of Mn@CDs were observed and imaged using TEM. For dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis, the hydrodynamic diameter distribution of Mn@CDs was measured using a Zetasizer analyzer. The absorption spectrum of Mn@CDs was recorded using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, with deionized water as the baseline. The Raman spectrum of Mn@CDs was recorded using a Raman spectrometer with an excitation wavelength of 532 nm. For Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis, Mn@CDs were mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) powder, pressed into a thin pellet, and analyzed using the FTIR spectrometer. For X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis, Mn@CDs were dried on a silicon wafer before analyzed by XPS.

TAC assay

The TAC of Mn@CDs was evaluated using the ABTS assay. ABTS as an oxidative indicator, can be oxidized to ABTS·⁺, which has absorption peaks at 734 nm and 405 nm. The presence of antioxidants inhibits the formation of ABTS·⁺, allowing for the measurement of the TAC of Mn@CDs. ABTS working solution was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and diluted with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Different concentrations of Mn@CDs were prepared. The reaction mixture was added to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance at 734 nm was measured over time. The relative antioxidant capacity was calculated based on the absorbance of the control groups.

CAT-like activity assay

The CAT-like activity of Mn@CDs was evaluated by measuring their ability to decompose H₂O₂. CAT is an intracellular enzyme that decomposes H₂O₂ into H₂O and O₂, thereby preventing the formation of toxic ·OH in the presence of Fe²⁺. The residual H₂O₂ concentration was quantified by measuring the formation of a purple product resulting from the reaction between Fe³⁺ and xylenol orange with a characteristic absorption peak at 560 nm. Different concentrations of Mn@CDs were mixed with H₂O₂ and incubated in the dark for 6 h. The residual H₂O₂ was quantified using a hydrogen peroxide detection kit, and the absorbance at 560 nm was measured. The relative antioxidant capacity was calculated based on the control groups.

SOD-like activity assay

The SOD-like activity of Mn@CDs was evaluated using the WST-8 method. As an indicator, WST-8 reacts with O₂·⁻ generated by xanthine oxidase-catalyzed oxidation of xanthine, forming a formazan dye with an absorption peak at 450 nm, allowing for the assessment of the SOD-like activity of Mn@CDs. Different concentrations of Mn@CDs were mixed with the WST-8/enzyme working solution and the reaction initiation solution. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured continuously for 30 min, and the inhibition percentage was calculated.

POD-like activity assay

The POD-like activity of Mn@CDs was evaluated using ABTS as a chromogenic substrate. POD is an endogenous ROS-scavenging enzyme that decomposes H₂O₂ through redox reactions, the decomposition product of H₂O₂ can oxidize ABTS to ABTS·⁺. In the absence of POD, mixing H₂O₂ and ABTS does not produce significant color change within a short time. This property was utilized to evaluate the POD-like activity of Mn@CDs. A mixture of ABTS solution, H₂O₂, and deionized water was prepared and added to a 96-well plate containing different concentrations of Mn@CDs. The absorbance at 734 nm was measured over time, and the reaction rate was calculated.

RNS scavenging assay

The RNS scavenging capacity of Mn@CDs was evaluated using the 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay. DPPH, a stable nitrogen radical with a characteristic absorption peak at 520 nm, was used to assess the RNS scavenging capacity of Mn@CDs. Different concentrations of Mn@CDs were mixed with DPPH solution and incubated for 30 min. The absorbance at 519 nm was measured, and the relative antioxidant capacity was calculated.

Free radical sScavenging assay

For hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging: a mixture of FeSO₄, 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrrolineN-oxide (DMPO), and H₂O₂ was prepared as the control group; Mn@CDs were added to the mixture, and the reaction was analyzed using electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy. For superoxide radical (O₂·⁻) scavenging: A mixture of xanthine, xanthine oxidase, and DMPO was prepared as the control group; Mn@CDs were added to the mixture, and the reaction was analyzed using ESR spectroscopy.

Cell culture

The rat neuroblastoma cells line B104 and rat microglia cells line GMI-R1 were regularly cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were cultured in the cell culture flask at 37 °C in a humid cell incubator filled with 95% air and 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 48 h, and 0.25% trypsin solution was used to collect the cells.

Cell viability

The cell viability was assessed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. B104 and GMI-R1 cells were transferred into the 96-well plates with a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL at 37 °C. After the required treatment and culture, the culture medium was replaced by 100 µL of fresh DMEM containing 10 µL of CCK-8 working reagent. After an incubation for 30 min, the absorbance at 450 nm were measured and the relative cell viabilities were calculated.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Protein samples were extracted from cells and tissues ues RIPA lysis buffer containing 1% Phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride (PMSF). Precast sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels were used for electrophoresis. Then proteins were transferred into a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. After transfer, the PVDF membrane was blocked and then incubated with primary antibodies including Cleaved Caspase-3 (1:500 dilution), Bcl-2 (1:1000 dilution), Bax (1:1000 dilution), TNF-α (1:1000 dilution), IL-1β (1:1000 dilution), IL-6 (1:500 dilution), CD86, (1:1500 dilution), Arg1 (1:1000 dilution), NOX2 (1:1000 dilution) and NOX4 (1:500 dilution) at 4 °C for 16–18 h, followed by secondary antibodies including goat anti-rabbit lgG- HRP (1:5000 dilution) and goat anti-mouse lgG- HRP (1:5000 dilution) for 2 h. Finally, ECL working solution was applied to the membrane, and protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system (5200, Tanon Science & Technology, Shanghai, China). Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cell slides or brain sections were permeabilized with PBS containing Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature. After permeabilization, the samples were blocked with 10% horse serum in PBS at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by additional blocking at room temperature for 1 h. The samples were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1.5 h in the dark. At last, the samples were mounted and observed under a confocal microscope.

TUNEL staining

TUNEL staining was performed using a one-step kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. During apoptosis, DNA endonucleases are activated, cleaving cellular DNA. The exposed 3’-OH ends of fragmented DNA can be labeled with FITC-conjugated dUTP catalyzed by TdT, allowing detection by fluorescence. After sample preparation and permeabilization, the samples were treated with the detection solution and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. The detection solution was removed, and the samples were washed, mounted, and observed under a confocal microscope.

Animal housing and SIH model establishment

Male adult Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old, 230–260 g) were purchased from Aniphe Biolaboratory Inc. (Jiangsu, China). The rats were housed in standard laboratory conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle). For the SIH model, rats in the SIH group were placed in cages with metal grid floors and subjected to intermittent electric shocks (50–80 V, 5–10 s intervals). Noise was synchronized with the electric shocks at an intensity of 88–98 dB, with a 10% probability of no noise or shock during each stimulation session. Rats were exposed to two stimulation sessions per day (9–11 AM and 3–5 PM), each lasting 2 h, for 15 consecutive days. Control rats were placed in the same stimulation cages for the same duration but without exposure to electric shocks or noise.

Bilateral RVLM microinjection

Rats were anesthetized with 10% chloral hydrate (4 mL/kg) and placed in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). The hair near the top of the rat skull was removed. After disinfection, a small opening with a length of about 1–1.5 cm was opened longitudinally. After the connective tissue was removed, the skull suture was exposed, and the positioning point was marked (located 3.7–4.0 mm caudal to the lambdoid suture, 2 mm lateral to the midline, and 8.0 mm ventral to the surface of the dura) [35], the electric skull drill was used to drill holes at the positioning point, then Mn@CDs was microinjected into the bilateral RVLM. After the injection, the wound was sutured and the rats were injected with antibiotics.

Recording of physiological data

Rats were anesthetized with mixed anesthetics and fixed in a supine position on a temperature-controlled experimental table using rubber bands. The femoral artery was exposed, and a small incision was made in the arterial wall using micro-scissors. An arterial catheter was inserted into the incision and secured. The catheter was connected to a pressure transducer linked to a PowerLab physiological recording system to record femoral arterial blood pressure, heart rate, and other physiological parameters.

Recording of renal sympathetic nerve activity

After recording physiological data, rats were placed in a right lateral position, and the abdominal aorta and left kidney were exposed. The renal sympathetic nerve was carefully identified and suspended on a platinum-iridium wire electrode. The nerve and electrode were covered with Wackier Silgel 932 (WackerChemie AG Co., Ltd., Germany) to isolate them from surrounding tissues and minimize interference. Renal sympathetic nerve signals were recorded using a PowerLab physiological recording system (Castle Hill Pty. Ltd., Australia).

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between groups were assessed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests. Significance levels were set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, with ns indicating p > 0.05. For WB band intensity analysis involving three groups, unless otherwise specified, the comparison between the positive control group and the other two groups was based on the less significant result.
