Postoperative cardiac arrest after cardiac surgery: Can we improve our rescue rate?
Authors: Parth B. Patel, Caroline R. Gross, Abhinav Kumar, Mehdi Oloomi, Zdravka Zafirova, David H. Adams, Robin Varghese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDespite improvements in rescue strategies, postoperative cardiac arrest in adults undergoing cardiac surgery has not been examined in the contemporary era. This study assesses the incidence, outcomes, and predictors of survival in patients who had an in-hospital cardiac arrest after cardiac surgery.MethodsAdult patients with cardiac arrest after open surgery from January 2012 to May 2019 at a single institution were studied. Patients were excluded if they underwent heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device implantation, experienced preoperative or intraoperative cardiac arrest, received preoperative or intraoperative temporary mechanical circulatory support, or were deemed as do not resuscitate or care was withdrawn before the arrest.ResultsOf 9946 patients, 162 (1.6%) experienced a postoperative arrest with failure to rescue in 59 patients (36.4%). The etiology of cardiac arrest was cardiovascular in 101 patients (62.3%), respiratory in 27 patients (16.7%), metabolic in 15 patients (9.3%), other in 12 patients (7.4%), and unknown in 7 patients (4.3%). Thirteen patients (8.0%) were placed on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation postarrest with only 4 (30.8%) surviving to discharge. Patients were more likely to die in-hospital if they had postoperative, prearrest organ dysfunction (adjusted odds ratio, 7.08, 95% CI, 2.92-17.16).ConclusionsAlthough the incidence of cardiac arrest was low, more than one-third of patients failed to be rescued. Prearrest major organ dysfunction portended a significantly worse prognosis. Early recognition of a nonthriving patient and rapid escalation of specialized care may be critical in successful rescue from cardiac arrest and aborting catastrophic outcomes.

Central Message

Although the incidence of cardiac arrest after cardiac surgery is low, more than one-third of patients failed to be rescued, with increased mortality among those with prearrest major organ dysfunction.

Perspective

Despite advancements in critical care, patient management strategies, and temporary mechanical circulatory support, FTR after postoperative cardiac arrest remains high. Early recognition of a nonthriving patient and subsequent rapid escalation of specialized care appear to be critical, because they may abort catastrophic outcomes and ensure successful rescue aftar cardiac arrest.

In the United States, more than 400,000 patients annually undergo cardiac surgery.1 Although the mortality associated with cardiac surgery has declined over the last few decades, cardiac arrest in the postoperative period remains a concern.2 The reported incidence of in-hospital cardiac arrest after cardiac surgery ranges from 0.7% to 8%.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Approximately 50% of these patients survive to discharge, which is higher compared with postoperative cardiac arrest in other surgical fields.3,5, 6, 7, 8, 9

The improvements in outcomes among these patients may be due to the advancements in the early recognition and management of cardiac arrest in the intensive care unit (ICU).2,5,10,11 In particular, refined advanced cardiovascular life support algorithms specific to cardiac surgery patients, robust specialized intensivist support, and the ability to rapidly deploy extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) mechanical support have likely reduced patient morbidity and mortality postcardiac arrest.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Despite these advances, short-term mortality and major morbidity due to stroke, acute renal failure, cardiogenic shock, and ischemic liver/bowel injury remain high.3,6,7,16, 17, 18

Outcomes after cardiac arrest in postoperative cardiac surgery patients have not been adequately examined in the contemporary era. Understanding the causes and outcomes of postoperative cardiac arrest in addition to the predictors for survival may allow for further improvements in outcomes. In this study, we set out to characterize the incidence, etiologies of arrest, and predictors of survival in patients who had a cardiac arrest after cardiac surgery during their index hospitalization at an academic medical center. Furthermore, we attempted to understand some of the factors leading to death after cardiac arrest, also known as a failure to rescue (FTR), by comparing survivors and nonsurvivors of cardiac arrest.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We included all patients aged 18 years or more between January 2012 and May 2019 from Mount Sinai Hospital who had a cardiac arrest after cardiac surgery during the same hospital stay. All patients included had an open procedure where an incision was made via a sternotomy or thoracotomy. The use of cardiopulmonary bypass was not required in the cases of coronary artery bypass surgery. Those who underwent heart transplantation, left ventricular assist device implantation, or transcatheter procedures did not qualify for inclusion. Cardiac surgery was classified on the basis of the first cardiovascular operation of the hospital admission (index operation).

Cardiac arrest was defined as any event in which a patient experienced a chaotic cardiac rhythm with pulselessness, requiring external chest compressions or defibrillation. Patients were excluded if in the preoperative or intraoperative setting, they experienced cardiac arrest or underwent the placement of a temporary extracorporeal mechanical circulatory support device. Six patients were also excluded because they were deemed as do not resuscitate or care was withdrawn before the cardiac arrest. For patients with recurrent cardiac arrests during the index hospitalization, only the first arrest was classified.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (IRB# STUDY-23-00,318) on October 13, 2023, with a waiver of informed consent.

Data Collection and Outcome Variables

The data for this study were obtained from the hospital's prospectively collected database from January 2012 to May 2019. The database included demographic data, preoperative morbidities, operation details, postoperative outcomes, and survival to discharge. Medical records were queried to determine the putative cause of cardiac arrest, postoperative day of cardiac arrest, presence of preoperative in-hospital arrests, and postoperative, prearrest, and postarrest morbidities, which included ischemic stroke, acute renal failure, ischemic liver or bowel injury, and cardiogenic shock. Postoperative, prearrest, and postarrest morbidities were analyzed individually and collectively as single or multiple organ dysfunctions. Prearrest morbidities were defined as postoperative morbidities that existed before the cardiac arrest, and postarrest morbidities were those that developed after the cardiac arrest. The primary outcome for this study was survival to hospital discharge. If a patient died in-hospital after the arrest, it was deemed as FTR. The secondary outcome was postarrest major morbidities including neurologic, renal, liver/bowel, and cardiac status at discharge.

Statistical Analysis

Patients who experienced cardiac arrest were categorized according to whether or not they survived to discharge. Death after the cardiac arrest was classified as FTR. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. Continuous variables that were normally distributed were presented as mean and SD, and skewed variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Demographic data, preoperative morbidities, operation details, and postoperative, prearrest, and postarrest outcomes were compared between survivors and nonsurvivors using the chi-square test for categorical variables, Student t test for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables.

Multivariate logistic regression models were performed to assess risk factors associated with mortality before discharge among patients with cardiac arrest. The regression models included the following demographics: patient characteristics, operation details, and postoperative, prearrest outcomes: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, preoperative renal failure requiring dialysis, heart failure, moderate or severe chronic lung disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, surgical incidence, surgical status, preoperative ejection fraction, prior myocardial infarction (MI), postoperative, prearrest major morbidity, and recurrent in-hospital cardiac arrest. The results of the multivariate logistic regression models are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs and associated P values. All P values were 2-sided. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM Corp).

Results

Of 9946 patients who underwent cardiac surgery during the study period, 162 patients had a postoperative arrest yielding an incidence rate of 1.6%. FTR occurred in 59 patients (36.4%) who had a cardiac arrest and did not survive to discharge.

Before their postoperative cardiac arrest, 25 survivors (24.3%) had at least 1 major new organ dysfunction, which included one of the following: ischemic stroke, acute renal failure, ischemic liver/bowel injury, and cardiogenic shock (Table 3). This was significantly less than the 34 nonsurvivors (57.6%) who experienced new major organ dysfunction (P < .001). Patients who did not survive their cardiac arrest were significantly more likely to have prearrest cerebrovascular accident, acute renal failure, ischemic liver or bowel injury, and cardiogenic shock.

After cardiac arrest, 23 patients (14.2%) failed to be resuscitated and died. Among the 139 patients who were rescued from their initial cardiac arrest, 74.1% survived to discharge. Of those patients who did not survive to hospital discharge, 61.0% were rescued from their initial arrest. Postarrest, major morbidities were assessed among 139 patients who were successfully resuscitated. Nonsurvivors were significantly more likely to experience postarrest dysfunction in every major organ system.

A total of 13 patients (8.0%) were placed on ECMO support postarrest. Patients who received ECMO were more likely to experience in-hospital mortality (9 [15.3%] vs 4 [3.9%]; P = .01).

Predictors of Postcardiac Arrest In-Hospital Mortality

We examined the risk factors associated with mortality before discharge (Figure 1 and Table E1). Age 80 years or more (aOR, 3.87, 95% CI, 1.06-14.08), diabetes (aOR, 3.49, 95% CI, 1.30-9.32), and preoperative cerebrovascular disease (aOR, 5.27, 95% CI, 1.56-17.84) predicted death after arrest. Reoperative surgery was associated with higher in-hospital mortality (aOR, 2.92, 95% CI, 1.05-8.09). Discussion

Our study assessed the outcomes after cardiac arrest in postoperative cardiac surgery patients spanning over a period of 7 years. In a quaternary care center with complex cardiac surgical cases, we found a low incidence of postoperative cardiac arrest (Figure 2). Other series looking at cardiac arrest after isolated coronary bypass surgery report an incidence of 1.6%,16 whereas 1 statewide database reported an incidence of 5.2%.5 In our study, the incidence of cardiac arrest was 1.6% with the main etiology of arrest being cardiovascular related followed by respiratory causes, both of which accounted for approximately 80% of all causes.Figure 2Summary of key findings.

Summary of key findings.

Cardiovascular causes included postoperative pulseless tachyarrhythmia and bradyarrhythmia, univentricular and biventricular failure, coronary ischemia, cardiac tamponade/mediastinal bleeding, vasovagal episode, and pulmonary hypertensive crisis. At our institution, for patients with univentricular or biventricular failure refractory to medical management, we have shifted our practice to institute early temporary mechanical circulatory, which includes intra-aortic balloon pump, extracorporeal ventricular assist device, or ECMO based on the degree of cardiogenic shock. When an arrhythmia is suspected due to possible coronary obstruction or patients present with over coronary ischemia, we routinely refer them for emergency coronary angiogram with subsequent percutaneous coronary intervention if a lesion is detected to avoid cardiac arrest and minimize further extremis.

Our study sought to examine why some patients survived cardiac arrest while others failed to be rescued. Although we were able to rescue 139 patients (85.8%) after the initial arrest, only 103 patients (63.6%) survived to discharge, meaning 36 (22.2%) of the patients who were successfully rescued from their initial arrest experienced subsequent events leading to their in-hospital death. Although we do not have data on the duration of resuscitative efforts in each patient, one could postulate that patients who were initially resuscitated but subsequently died likely had protracted resuscitative efforts leading to worse postarrest organ dysfunction.

As defined by Silber and colleagues,19 FTR represents death after an adverse event. Subsequent research has shown that hospital complication rates do not necessarily correlate with mortality.20 In their article on mortality rates after emergency surgery at the University of Michigan, Sheetz and colleagues20 found that those hospitals with the highest overall mortality rates also had the lowest FTR rates. This suggests that in attempting to rescue a patient from a complication after surgery, there are modifiable and nonmodifiable risk factors. Nonmodifiable risk factors would include age, BMI, comorbid conditions, and the complexity of a given operation. We found age, preoperative diabetes and cerebrovascular disease, and reoperative surgery increased the risk of FTR after postoperative cardiac arrest. Indeed, age more than 80 years was predictive of FTR, likely a result of the additional comorbid conditions that are due to age. Interestingly, we observed that patients with a BMI more than 30 kg/m2 and those with a prior history of myocardial infarction were less likely to experience in-hospital mortality. The survival benefit associated with higher BMI might suggest a possible obesity paradox among cardiac arrest patients because increased nutritional reserve among this cohort might protect them the increased metabolic stress encounter in the postarrest period. Although Mariscalco and colleagues21 did not specifically examine cardiac arrest in their analysis of the effects of BMI on cardiac surgical outcomes, they observed not only lower mortality but also decreased rates of low cardiac output in patients with BMI 30 to less than 35 kg/m2 and BMI 35 to less than 40 kg/m2. As for patients with a prior MI, a history of MI itself does not confer a survival advantage. Of 59 patients who had a prior MI, approximately 70% underwent CABG or CABG with concomitant valve surgery, which was significantly greater than those no history of MI. Thus, these patients may present with correctable causes of cardiac arrest, and their decompensation may be promptly recognized given their known diagnosis of coronary artery disease. Stecker and colleagues,22 as part of the Oregon Sudden Unexpected Study, observed that among 1466 patients who experienced sudden cardiac arrest, an established diagnosis of coronary artery disease was associated with a 50% higher odds of survival from cardiac arrest.

On the contrary, modifiable risk factors can be altered to improve the rescue rate in a given patient. In our analysis, a patient's clinical status before their arrest appeared to influence the ability to rescue them from death. Patients who experienced postoperative dysfunction in at least 1 organ major system had greater risk for in-hospital mortality. Within this lie factors such as time to identify a nonthriving patient or a complication, the ability to escalate care to a specialized unit, and access to specialists to consult on a given complication in a timely manner. Knowing this, we have elected to keep patients after a complex operation or with multiorgan dysfunction (end-stage renal disease, cardiomyopathy, previous stroke, symptomatic peripheral arterial disease) in the ICU for a longer period of time despite the possibility of an earlier transfer to the stepdown floor.

When examining FTR after a major complication or event, early recognition of a deteriorating clinical condition is essential in attempting to abort a catastrophic outcome. The earlier one can identify a condition and possible interventions the higher the likelihood of a favorable outcome in a given patient. In their study examining urgent re-angiography after cardiac surgery, Gaudino and colleagues23 performed 40 early postoperative angiograms of 5275 patients (0.7%), of which approximately 40% presented with hemodynamic instability and the remainder had acute ischemia. In 82.5% of cases, they were able to identify coronary ischemia within a mean time of 51 hours after the index operation and subsequently intervene when possible to minimize myocardial injury. In this case, when coronary ischemia is highly suspected postoperatively, urgent coronary angiography necessitates a cardiac catheterization laboratory that is staffed with appropriate resources to quickly react. This often requires a cardiac anesthesiologist to manage the patient while in the catheterization laboratory. One can easily see that there are many levels of support required to undergo urgent procedures or interventions, some of which may not be available at smaller hospitals. Likewise, having access to board-certified intensivists day or night has been shown to decrease mortality.24 Trained staff can identify postoperative complications that may occur overnight, such as bleeding, but more importantly provide the expertise to address these complications and know when to call in additional support to intervene as needed.24 These examples illustrate the importance of appropriate expertise and manpower in rescuing patients from complications. Interestingly, in our analysis, the location of cardiac arrest did not affect in-hospital mortality. For survivors, cardiopulmonary etiologies accounted for 90.9% of arrests in the ICU and 84.6% on the stepdown unit for survivors. This is a stark comparison to 58.8% of cardiac arrests in the ICU and 75.0% on the stepdown unit for nonsurvivors. We postulate that postarrest, cardiopulmonary etiologies allowed corrective measures to be quickly used regardless of the location of cardiac arrest, thus rescuing patients from a subsequent catastrophic outcome.

Postcardiac arrest, patients who failed to be rescued were significantly more likely to experience in-hospital cerebrovascular accident, acute renal failure, ischemic liver or bowel injury, and cardiogenic shock. Care was escalated for 13 patients (8.0%) who required emergency institution of ECMO to further minimize postarrest complications. At our institution, patients are considered for ECMO when they present with refractory cardiac arrest or develop refractory shock postresuscitation with a putative cause of arrest that is either cardiovascular or respiratory in nature. Patients are excluded if they have multiorgan failure or sepsis at the time of index cardiac arrest. Cardiac surgeons cannulate patients centrally or peripherally depending on which procedure would be deemed most expedient. The survival for the patients undergoing ECMO during the study period was grim, with only 4 patients (30.8%) surviving to discharge. The in-hospital survival after ECMO that we report is similar to other studies examining the implementation of ECMO after postoperative cardiac arrest, which have been reported to be 30 to 33%.12,25 Among the approximately one-third of patients who do survive to discharge, ECMO represents a critical tool for rescue, especially at quaternary care centers, where it can be rapidly deployed by trained staff in an effective manner that minimizes interruptions with other simultaneously occurring rescue strategies, such as chest compressions.12 However, based on prior studies, among the two-thirds of patients who die in-hospital, the primary etiology of mortality was multiorgan failure with up to approximately 50% of these patients experiencing severe neurological dysfunction before death.12,25 Thus, selectively implementing ECMO after discerning a patient's nonmodifiable and modifiable risk factors might allow certain cohorts of patients to receive the benefit of ECMO while preventing the care from being unnecessarily prolonged among those who will eventually die.

Although a multitude of strategies can be used to rescue patients after cardiac arrest, it is important that in the process of doing so, the patient's long-term outcomes are kept in mind. One-year survival for cardiac surgery patients who experience postoperative in-hospital cardiac arrest is 45% to 69%.3,4,10,16 Predictors for mortality at 1 year primarily are similar to the predictors of in-hospital mortality that we reported. In particular, they include older age, preoperative cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, urgent or emergency operative status, and postoperative coma and renal dysfunction.4 The similarities between the predictors of in-hospital and 1-year mortality seem to suggest that the same nonmodifiable and modifiable risk factors are at play for patients who die in-hospital and those who survive to discharge but die within 1 year. The difference between these 2 populations might be in the severity of these factors, which in turn impacts how quickly a patient dies. Furthermore, neurological dysfunction continues to remain a major concern among patients who survive to discharge and at 1 year postarrest. Yonis and colleagues,26 who examined 1892 patients experiencing an in-hospital cardiac arrest, found that 517 patients (27.3%) survived to 30 days postarrest, of whom 454 (87.8%) did not develop any anoxic brain injury or require nursing home admission. Among this subset of patients, the risk of anoxic brain injury or nursing home admission at 1 year postarrest was 4.6%. In our study, 10 (9.7%) of 103 patients who survived to discharge experienced a cerebrovascular accident. Although we did not assess neurological function at 1 year postarrest, the findings by Yonis and colleagues26 provide reassurance that survivors of in-hospital cardiac arrest with no neurologic morbidity have a low risk of developing it within 1 year of cardiac arrest.

Our study has a number of strengths. This is one of the few studies that have examined cardiac arrests in the contemporary era where refined Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support algorithms specific to cardiac surgery patients, specialized intensivist support, and the ability to rapidly deploy ECMO have been implemented to minimize mortality and complications from postoperative cardiac arrest. Our study population was surgically diverse, and patients underwent a variety of different operations. This provides a real-world reflection in surgical cases among cardiac arrest patients seen at other quaternary care centers. We identified several preoperative comorbidities and modifiable factors as predictors of in-hospital mortality, thus supporting efforts to anticipate and address these factors as a way to further minimize postarrest complications.

Limitations

Despite these strengths, our study did have certain limitations. We recognize that our study is based on a single institution with a modest sample size, which might not reflect cardiac arrest outcomes at other institutions where different surgical and postoperative management might be used. Additionally, during the study time period, ICU and postoperative critical care practices at Mount Sinai Hospital have evolved to match patient needs and as resources allowed. These changing practices might have differentially influenced patient outcomes but could not be adequately studied given their subjective nature. In identifying predictors of in-hospital mortality, we were unable to adjust for critical care training backgrounds as well as ICU organizational factors, including staffing, discrepancies in the timely identification of cardiac arrest and escalation of care, and any differences in prompt access to specialized care at the time of escalation of care.

Conclusions

Although the incidence of cardiac arrest after cardiac surgery was low in approximately 10,000 patients at a quaternary care center, more than one-third of patients failed to be rescued. Patients with prearrest major organ dysfunction had a significantly increased risk of in-hospital mortality. Early recognition of a nonthriving patient and subsequent rapid escalation of specialized care appears to be critical, because it may abort catastrophic outcomes and ensure successful rescue if the patient were to arrest. Further efforts focused on methods to improve rescue after arrest are needed in the contemporary era where refined Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support algorithms, improvements in intensivist support, and rapid deployment of ECMO have already been instituted to minimize postarrest complications.
