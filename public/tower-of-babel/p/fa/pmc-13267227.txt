Circular economy approach: repurposing modified lemon peel waste for cobalt(II) capture from simulated solutions
Authors: Ahmed S Elkholy, Rasha S Kamal, Mohamed S Yahia, Manar El-Sayed Abdel-Raouf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiochar from different agrowastes has been extensively used to eliminate various pollutants from wastewater. In the present work, raw lemon peels (RLP) were activated by microwave irradiation then converted into activated biochar (RLP-BC) by pyrolysis. Afterwards, both RLP and RLP-BC have undergone microwave-assisted chemical modification by hydrogen peroxide and nicotinic acid. These biosorbents were denoted as RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP, and N-LP-BC respectively. They were characterized using different analytical techniques including IR, SEM, and EDX to investigate their properties. Surface topography of selected sorbents was examined using AFM, and their BET data were provided. These biosorbents were then tested for their ability to remove Co(II) from aqueous solutions under varying conditions. The adsorption experiments showed that the modified lemon peel materials showed adsorption affinity for Co(II) ions in alkaline medium, and the highest removal efficiency was achieved at pH 9. The adsorption equilibrium of Co(II) onto the biosorbents was described by the Langmuir isotherm, while adsorption kinetics were modeled using a pseudo-second-order approach for RLP-BC, N-LP, and N-LP-BC adsorbents. Thermodynamic parameters were also assessed. Results indicated that chemically modified biochar was the most effective in Co(II) removal. OH-LP-BC exhibited the highest Qmax (28.9 mg g−1), while N-LP-BC showed the lowest (14.5 mg g−1). Reusability tests demonstrated that these sorbents could be reused up to four times with removal efficiencies exceeding 50%. Furthermore, efficacy of the investigated sorbents in removal of Co(II) from a sample of fertilizer wastewater was explored at the optimized conditions. Our finding proves the suitability of the green sorbents as possible candidates for wastewater treatment application.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Cobalt(II), Removal, Agro-food waste, Microwave, Lemon peel, Biochar, AFM

Introduction

The quality of water and soil, as well as plant growth, animal, and human health, are highly threatened by the release of heavy metals from various human activities into the environment. The inability of heavy metals to degrade biologically as some organic pollutants and their bioaccumulation through food chain induce drastic hazards to the entire ecosystem [1]. Cobalt is one of the top-five toxic heavy metals (lead, mercury, chromium, arsenic and cobalt) with a particular detrimental systemic effects associated by complex clinical conditions, primarily involving impairments in the neurological, cardiovascular, and endocrine systems (e.g., hearing and vision impairment) [2]. Cobalt is one of the most toxic heavy metals that can be discharged into the environment through different industrial activities such as mining, paint and leather industries [3–5]. Governments have tightened some environmental regulations to limit wastewater discharge and support initiatives to remove heavy metals and other pollutants from wastewater [6–11]. The urgent need for a high removal performance, cost-effective operation, and minimum production of secondary byproducts has led previous research to identify adsorption as a well-known and commonly utilized technology [12, 13]. Agricultural residual materials (ARMs) have been emphasized as the right choice with unique qualities of accessibility, environmental friendliness, and biocompatibility [14]Several works been conducted on the preparation of adsorbents based on agricultural wastes, including maize straw [15], orange peel [16], pomegranate peels [17], potato peels [18] apple peels [19] waste straw [20] and chitosan [21]. On the other hand, the elimination of Cobalt ions from polluted media has been investigated by several authors [22–24]. However, the source material, such as biomass, can be thermally converted through carbonization, a charring process that turns biomass into char by pyrolysis, in order to create activated carbon. The char is then “activated” using physical or chemical procedures to induce porous structure for improved effectiveness [25]. Biochar is a carbonaceous product that results from the thermochemical conversion of biomass in an oxygen-deficient environment. In view of its porosity and surface properties, biochar has been identified as a promising eco-friendly adsorbent for wastewater treatment. In contrast to chemical activation, which uses chemicals to create activated carbon, physical activation employs carbon dioxide or steam as activating agents. Chemical activation is preferable over physical activation since it yields more activated carbon. In this regard, commercial activated carbon exhibits good adsorption properties for water contaminants such organics and heavy metals. Adsorption may be considered one of the best techniques for heavy metal removal in terms of its simplicity, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness in comparison with membrane filtration or advanced oxidation processes. Thermochemical processes such as pyrolysis and hydrothermal treatment of biomass yield biochar that has been utilized as a cheap adsorbent to remove organic pollutants and heavy metals for water remediation [26]. When compared to conventional heating techniques, microwave heating depicts a number of benefits, including effective homogeneous and quick heating [27], higher reaction rates, better selectivity, and less energy usage [28]. On the other hand, biomass is subjected to a series of chemical reactions during hydrothermal carbonization, including hydrolysis, dehydration and decarboxylation of the hydrolyzed products, condensation, polymerization, and aromatization of the intermediate compounds from degradation processes such as dehydration and decarboxylation, and so on [29]. Lemon peel waste, generated in large quantities by the food industry, represents an attractive precursor for developing environmentally friendly. In this regard, LP modified with citric acid was used for removal of lead ions [30], and arsenate ions [31, 32]. Also, activated LP was also used to remove cobalt ions [33] and lead ions [34]. In addition, the removal of nickel, lead, and cadmium ions from rice was successfully achieved using carbon-activated lemon peel that had also been subjected to phosphoric acid treatment, lemon peel modified with Al2O3 nanoparticles was employed for elimination of cadmium ion from aqueous solutions [35]. Despite the extensive studies carried out on the usage of biochar-based sorbents, few studies have been conducted to comparatively evaluate the efficacy of raw, chemically modified, and microwave-assisted biochar derived from lemon peel under similar experimental conditions [36, 37].

The innovation of this work relies on a thorough comparison between the removal performance of neat and modified lemon peels towards cobalt cations as one of the top toxic pollutive heavy metals. Cobalt has been used extensively in different applications such as the manufacturing of rechargeable batteries, electroplating, pigmentation, and catalysis. The enhanced demand for cobalt in modern technological applications has led to concerns over its discharge into aquatic systems. Higher levels of cobalt in water systems may trigger ecological and health hazards, thus emphasizing the need to establish effective methods for its removal. Thus, the objective of this study is to systematically investigate structural modification strategies and assess its impact on Co(II) adsorption performance under optimized conditions. Moreover, the adsorption process was assessed by different analytical techniques such as the FTIR, SEM, EDX and the AFM. Several variables were considered throughout the experimental plan including effect of pH of the medium, sorbent dose, cobalt ion concentration and temperature. In addition, the removal performance of the sorbents was tested in a real fertilizer wastewater sample to assure the applicability of these cheap and sustainable sorbents in wastewater treatment application. Briefly, this study aims to investigate the valorization potential of lemon peel waste as an environmentally friendly biosorbent material for the removal of cobalt ions from aqueous solutions. The novelty of this study is related to comparing raw, chemically, and thermally treated lemon peel wastes for adsorption potential and its possible application in a circular economy context.

Experimental

Materials

Lemon peel (LP) obtained from fresh fruit juice shops- Cairo. Cobalt chloride, CoCl2; 99.9% (Fisher Scientific, USA), AlCl3; 99.9%, Fisher Scientific, USA), H2O2, nicotinic acid, Fisher Scientific, USA, sodium hydroxide (NaOH; 99.0%), and hydrochloric acid (HCl; 36.5–38%). All the chemicals were used without further purification. All aqueous solutions were prepared using distilled water. Additions of HCl or NaOH were used to control the pH of the mixture.

Microwave synthesis of lemon peels biochar

Initially, the lemon peels were immersed in distilled water for 24 h to remove dirt and then dried in oven at 105 ± 2 °C until a constant weight to remove humidity and volatile oils. After wards, the dried LP was ground in a coffee grinder and sieved to a 0.6 mm size. Pyrolysis experiments were conducted on half of the mass of the dried powder in a domestic multimode on–off microwave oven (Daewoo—KOG-1N4A -Microwave) operated at a Output power: 1000 W. The obtained Biochar (denoted as RLP-BC) was employed as the activation’s initial promote source (Suriapparao and Vinu 2015). The biochar was ground and sieved to a uniform size fraction of ~0.05 mm. It was then rinsed several times with distilled water to assure purity and then dried at 105 °C. After activation, the reactor was cooled to room temperature (24 ± 2 °C) prior to recover the activated char. The initial heating at 120 °C was selected for drying the lemon peel material. The thermal conversion of the biomass into biochar was achieved by microwave-assisted treatment, which favors carbonization of the biomass.

Activation of the raw and activated lemon peels

The surface modification was achieved either by hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (20%) or nicotinic acid (0.2 M) as following: About 50 mL of the modifier solution was added to 0.1 g of biochar, and the mixture was stirred for 12 h at 300 rpm. The samples were vacuum filtered after being impregnated with the modifier solution, and the solids were microwave-treated at 600 W for 10 min. Biochar from LP pyrolysis that was used as-produced (or untreated) (C) as well as biochar that had merely been exposed to H2O2 or nicotinic acid for 24 h and dried (without microwave treatment) were also used as control samples. Batch adsorption experiments

Optimization of the adsorption process was attained by integrating the adsorption parameters altogether. In this regard, definite mass of the adsorbent (0.1 g) with desired volume solution containing Co(II) ions [22.218–49.272 mg/L] at room temperature (24 °C) and the mixture was then agitated at 300 rpm in an automatic shaker. The ranges of experimental conditions studied in the present work under which the adsorption of cobalt ions can be effective, such as solution pH (2–9), adsorbent dosage (0.1–0.5 g), contact time (0–240 min), and initial cobalt ion concentration (22.2–49.3 mg/L), were chosen according to the ranges commonly reported in the literature in studies of adsorbents derived from biomass for the removal of heavy metals. The ranges of the experimental parameters chosen in the present work allow a comparison of the adsorption of cobalt ions with that reported in the literature. After a definite time, the concentration of the Co(II) ions left in the solution was measured by using ICP. Reusability investigation

The removal performance of the green sorbents at repeated applications was investigated as following. Simply, the metal-loaded sorbent was incubated in 20 mL of 1 N HCl for 60 min at room temperature while being stirred at 250 rpm. Filtration was employed to remove the sorbent from the solution. It was then carefully cleaned with double-distilled water and used again for four cycles. The metal ion in the filtrate was determined by using an ICP device.

[49] found that the elimination of organic elements and development of resistant aromatic biochar was also evident by FT-IR. According to Kim et al. The AFM was applied to investigate the surface modification process and to confirm the adsorption process via comparing the topography features and the height measurements between metal-free and metal-loaded samples. Figure 6a–d displays the surface features of neat LP, LP-BC, OH-LP-BC before and after adsorption respectively. The data reveals that the surface topography of LP and LP-BC are very close to each other with slight increase in height measurement which infers that carbonization process did not alter the surface morphology. Determining the pH of zero charge (pHpzc)

Figure 7 displays the point of zero charge (pHpzc) for 0.1 g adsorbent/50 mL electrolyte solution of 0.1 M NaCl. Positive charges exist on the surface of carbonaceous materials below pHpzc and negative charges exist above pHpzc (Khandaker 2018). When anions are removed from an aqueous solution, it is preferred for the adsorbent surface to be positively charged and vice versa (Khandaker 2018). The pHpzc values determined for RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP and N-LP-BC adsorbents are equal 3.5, 5.8, 8.1, 7.0, 5.9 and 5.5, respectively. It was observed that the pHpzc values of OH-LP and OH-LP-BC were higher than other adsorbents. This observation may be attributed to excess of OH− groups introduced upon modification with H2O2.

Biochar yield

Following pyrolysis, the yield of biochar produced was determined as Eq. (3).

The impact of microwave pyrolysis of untreated and treated lemon peel on the yield of biochar was also verified. The yield percentage (%) of different samples decreased as follows 38.52 (R-LP-BC), 8.91 (OH-LP-BC) and 7.52 (N-LP-BC) ± 0.7%. The significantly lower yields observed for chemically modified samples (OH-LP-BC and N-LP-BC) compared to untreated biochar (RLP-BC) can be attributed to microwave-assisted oxidative decomposition and enhanced decarboxylation reactions occurring during chemical modification. The introduction of oxygenated functional groups increases structural instability of lignocellulosic components, leading to cleavage of polymeric chains and formation of low-molecular-weight volatile compounds such as CO2, CO, CH4, and H2 during pyrolysis. Consequently, greater mass loss occurs, resulting in reduced solid biochar yield. A greater output of biochar (OH-LP) was evident as a result of the biomass only being partially charred, which results in less volatile loss [60]. Under microwave pyrolysis dehydration and the low output of biochar was caused by an acceleration of the heat decomposition of biomass into lower molecular weight organic compounds and gases (CH4, H2, CO2, and CO) [60].

Besides BET analysis, the micropore characteristics were also studied by the Dubinin–Astakhov equation, which is based on the potential theory of adsorption proposed by Polanyi and considers the adsorption process on microporous materials as a micropore filling process instead of a multi-layer adsorption process on the surface of the material. The Dubinin–Astakhov equation makes it possible to calculate the micropore volume (W₀) and the characteristic adsorption energy (E), which reflect the energy heterogeneity of the microporous material. The micropore volume calculated by this equation represents the internal micropore filling and not the external surface area, and therefore the calculated micropore surface area may differ from or even be greater than the BET surface area due to the different theoretical assumptions used by these two models.

Micropore surface area was calculated using the Dubinin–Astakhov (D–A) model derived from nitrogen adsorption isotherms. The D–A method estimates micropore volume based on a pore-filling mechanism and assumes slit-shaped pore geometry. Therefore, the reported micropore surface area values may differ from BET surface area values due to differences in theoretical assumptions between multilayer adsorption (BET) and micropore volume filling (D–A). Though the complete nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm curves are not given, the results of structural characteristics obtained by BET analysis are given and discussed to understand the role of surface properties on Co(II) adsorption. The low BET surface areas found in the sorbents synthesized in this study are typical for most biosorbents synthesized from agricultural wastes [ref]. In biosorbents synthesized from wastes, adsorptive capacity may be dominated by surface functional groups rather than surface areas. The relatively small variation in BET surface area suggests that adsorption performance is mainly influenced by surface functional groups rather than significant structural changes [62, 63].

Effect of pH

The adsorption of metal ion species in an aqueous solution onto an active sorbent is significantly influenced by the solution’s pH due to the effect of pH on the chemical features of the active sites and the nature of binding between active sites and the metal cations. In light of this, the impact of solution pH on Co(II) adsorption by different carboneous materials (RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP and N-LP-BC when they present singly in aqueous solution) was examined between the pH 2.0–9.0 range. As shown in Fig. 8b, Co(II) binding affinities with the six adsorbents were strongly influenced by the pH of the solution. The adsorbents exhibit minimal cobalt ion adsorption in neutral or alkaline environments. For pH ≥ 7, the phenomenon is caused by the shift in potential in the adsorbent’s surface and resulted in a considerable increase in adsorption capacity. Adsorbent surface potential steadily shifted from positive to negative as pH increased from 7.0 to 9.0. The strength of the adsorbent’s adsorption to cobalt ions depends on how negatively charged it is. For pH = 9, practically all of the cobalt ions in the solution have been eliminate. Yuan et al. [66] have demonstrated that cobalt ions can be found in a variety of forms, including Co2+, Co(OH)+, Co(OH)2, and Co(OH)3, depending on the pH. Cobalt hydroxide is one of the cobalt ion forms that are present when the pH is 9. Although cobalt hydroxide precipitation may occur at alkaline pH, several pieces of experimental evidence confirm that the removal mechanism in this study is predominantly adsorption-driven rather than bulk precipitation. FTIR spectra of metal-loaded sorbents showed noticeable shifts in –OH, –COOH, and –NH2 functional groups, indicating direct coordination between Co2+ ions and surface functional groups. Furthermore, SEM–EDX analysis confirmed the presence of cobalt uniformly distributed on the biosorbent surface rather than forming separate crystalline precipitates. In addition, control experiments conducted under identical pH conditions in the absence of sorbent showed negligible cobalt removal, confirming that precipitation alone does not account for the observed removal efficiency. Therefore, surface complexation is considered the dominant mechanism. The ideal pH of 9.0 was chosen for excess adsorption investigations because Cobalt(II) is more readily precipitated at a higher pH. In our study, the maximum removal percentage of about 50, 72.413, 85.303, 89.74, 59.588 and 66.238% is achieved at pH value beyond 9.0 for the different carbons, RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP and N-LP-BC, respectively. Relative results were reached by [67].

Effect of contact time

The removal of Co(II) as a function of time (25 °C) is studied at 303 K employing 0.25 gm of the sorbent material and at the optimized conditions (a pH of 9.0 and an initial Co(II) concentration of Co = 36.942 ppm). The data reveals that the changes in the removal percentage increases over time and attained maximum value after 240 min in the systems Co-RLP (37.1%), Co-RLP-BC (43.154%), Co-N-LP (40.969%) and Co-N-LP-BC (52.82%). On the other hand, the equilibrium time of cobalt ions adsorption on Co-OH-LP (78.669%) and Co-OH-LP-BC (72.41%) was only 180 min. In the experimental outcomes, there were two stages that could be distinguished: the first stage showed a relatively quick increase in the Co(II) sequestration around 60 min, which was caused by the presence of a large number of active sites available for metal uptake on the adsorbent, and the second stage showed an almost constant Co(II) removal efficiency caused by the occupation of majority of active sites and reaching equilibrium. Similar two-step adsorption of heavy metal ions has been reported in the literature by a number of investigators. However, the time it took to reach equilibrium varied depending on the type of adsorbent used [7, 9, 43].

Effect of the Co(II) ion concentration

The impact of the starting concentration of Co(II) ions on the percentage of removal by the green sorbents was also investigated because heavy metal ions might exist in the environment at various quantities. The application conditions applied throughout the experimental works are as following: pH of 9.0, volume of Cobalt solution was 25 mL, Co(II) concentration ranging from 22.218 to 49.272 mg/L, constant adsorbent dosage of 0.25 g, suitable agitation time according to the contact time, and at temperature (303 K). It can be seen that, the increase in the initial concentration from 22.218 to 49.272 mg/L resulted in a decrease in the Co(II) removal from 40.885 to 33.025%, from 53.074 to 35.054%, from 88.878 to 51.29%, from 86.353 to 41.143%, from 41.632 to 39.113% and from 56.26 to 49.261% for RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP and N-LP-BC, respectively. This can be discussed on the basis of number of active sites available for adsorption. Because there aren’t enough available adsorption sites in relation to the excess Co(II) ions, it is believed that a high concentration can prevent metal ions from moving to the surface of carbon. An opposite trend was observed for the amount of Co(II) uptake with increase in the initial concentration from 22.218 to 49.272 mg/L. The Co(II) uptake (qmax, mg/g) by RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP and N-LP-BC was increased from 0.9084 to 1.627, from 1.1792 to 1.727 mg/g, from 1.9747 to 2.527 mg/g, from 1.9186 to 2.072 mg/g, from 0.925 to 1.927 mg/g and from 1.25 to 2.427 mg/g, respectively. This may be explained by the SEM images of surface morphology. These outcomes are consistent with the preceding reports [68, 69].

Adsorption isotherm

To guarantee the consistency of the adsorption behavior, the applicability of the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models was comparatively evaluated in terms of their fitting quality instead of depending on a single statistical measure. It is worth mentioning that various isotherm models reflect different physicochemical hypotheses, such as the homogeneity, heterogeneity, and adsorbate–adsorbate interactions on the adsorbent surface, etc. Therefore, the difference in the fitting quality does not necessarily indicate the contradiction but the complexity of the adsorption process. Four adsorption isotherms were applied in order to describe the adsorption process. These are; Freundlich [70], Langmuir [71], Temkin [72] and D–R [73] models. The equations for these isotherm models and the relevant data are presented in Table 7. According to the Freundlich isotherm model, molecules are adsorbed on heterogeneous surfaces and interact with one another. It is associated with physical contact. According to the Langmuir isotherm model, which defines the adsorption process, adsorption takes place at a small number of homogenous surfaces with sites that are equivalent and relatively identical, and there is no mutual interaction between the adsorbed molecules. According to the Temkin model, the amount of energy required for adsorption reduces linearly as surface area is covered. This concept works well with the chemisorption process [74]. Overall, the selection of the most representative isotherm model was based on comparative fitting consistency and theoretical relevance rather than marginal statistical differences. The adsorption behavior appears to reflect partially heterogeneous surface characteristics with possible localized monolayer coverage, which explains why more than one model may adequately describe the system without implying contradiction.

The C values over 0 indicated that adsorption involves both intraparticle diffusions (metal ion diffusion via the adsorbent pores) and external (metal ions diffusing through the aqueous solution towards the adsorbent’s outer surface) [82]. Influence of temperature

It is well established that adsorption process is highly affected by the temperature from several points. High temperature can impart some energy to the dispersed ions, it can also affect the surface of the sorbent materials. Thus, the influence of temperature (303–333 K) on adsorption rate and capacity was investigated with sorbent concentration 0.1 g/25 mL, cobalt solution of 36.942 mg/L at pH value of 7.0 for 200 min. It is shown that raising the temperature promoted the rate of ion migration, allowing Co(II) to diffuse into the carbon’s internal hole under the influence of the concentration difference between the solute and the carbon’s surface. Thus, the adsorption rate of Co(II) gently increased with an increase of the temperature from 303 to 323 K. The adsorption of Co(II) and the adsorption capacity were 65.34, 77.968, 98.129, 56.133 and 77.8%, and 2.4138, 2.88, 3.625, 2.073 and 2.874 mg/g, respectively at 323 K [84].

Mechanism of Co(II) ions sorption

Clarification of the functional groups contributing to Co(II) adsorption, the FTIR spectra of RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP and N-LP-BC adsorbents were examined both before and after adsorption of Co2+, which were shown in Fig. 1. Comparing the spectrum before and after adsorption, it can be seen that the change of adsorption intensity of functional groups of the adsorbent’s peaks (–NH2, –COOH and –OH) in the above discussion demonstrated that these groups were participated in adsorption process. The adsorption process is primarily governed by Lewis acid–base coordination between Co2+ ions and the electron-donating functional groups present on the biosorbent surface, the FTIR analysis of the adsorbent before and after Co2+ ions adsorption showed that the main complexing sites were –NH2, –COOH, and –OH. The functional groups are Lewis bases, while the heavy metal ions (Co2+) have vacant orbitals that can operate as a Lewis acid and take electron pairs. Consequently, electron transfer may be used to create the coordination [88]. Therefore, the dominant adsorption mechanism involves chemisorption-driven surface complexation, where Co2+ ions act as Lewis acids interacting with lone pair electrons on –NH2, –COOH, and –OH functional groups. Although minor electrostatic or ion-exchange interactions may occur depending on surface charge conditions, they do not represent the primary adsorption pathway. It must be noted that the applicability of the pseudo-second-order model does not necessarily validate the existence of chemical bonds between the Co(II) ions and the surface of the sorbent material. Thus, the adsorption process may not be considered purely chemisorption but rather surface interaction-controlled. Chitosan was used to examine how to remove lead, copper, and cadmium(II) from aqueous solutions, and they discovered similar findings. [89] used the polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)/chitosan magnetic composite for the removal and recovery of Co2+ ions from wastewater, through study of FTIR spectra, they discovered the substantial shift of –NH2 following the adsorption of Co2+ ions. While OH-LP-BC low adsorption capability was a result of its high oxygen concentration. Acidic groups, particularly carboxy groups, may form water clusters by hydrogen-bonding, which would reduce the OH-LP-BC accessibility and affinity [90]. The removal mechanism is likely dominated by electrostatic attraction at optimal pH, surface complexation with oxygen-containing groups, and partial ion exchange processes. Although the adsorption tests were carried out in an alkaline environment, the removal of Co(II) ions is mainly associated with adsorption mechanisms and not precipitation. This is confirmed by the interaction between metal ions and surface groups containing hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, as determined using FTIR analysis. Based on the characterization results, the adsorption mechanism may involve electrostatic attraction, ion exchange, and surface complexation between Co(II) ions and functional groups present on the biosorbent surface.

Conclusions

The major goal of this research is to investigate the workability of some biosorbents based on raw and modified lemon peel towards cobalt ions from aqueous solutions under different application conditions. The experimental outcomes show that under ideal application conditions, biochar made from lemon peel biomass residue was highly effective in Co(II) uptake. The adsorption performance increased by increasing the temperature and pH of the medium up to pH 9. Four kinetic models were applied to describe the adsorption process and the data reveal that Langmuir isotherm best fit the adsorption equilibrium of Co(II) onto RLP, RLP-BC, OH-LP, OH-LP-BC, N-LP and N-LP-BC bio adsorbents, while the adsorption kinetics were characterized by a pseudo-second-order model. The parameters of thermodynamics were also evaluated. 80.6% was the maximum percentage of cobalt removed from fertilizer wastewater. Results show that activated biochar may be produced quickly and effectively using microwave pyrolysis as effective sorbents for elimination of toxic heavy metals from water streams. The data also depicted high reusability of the investigated sorbents up to four cycles. At last, the tested sorbents showed good workability when applied for removal of cobalt from fertilizer wastewater. The qmax values achieved by the investigated lemon peel sorbents were very comparable to those mentioned in the literature. Comparatively speaking, the sorbent material derived from lemon peel is superior compared to commercial activated carbon materials in terms of raw material availability and lower cost, as well as waste valorization in a circular economy context. Though a techno-economic and life-cycle analysis is beyond the scope of this study, it is highly recommended for future works in assessing its feasibility and potential impact on the environment.

Although it has been proven in this study that it is technically viable to use lemon peel waste to produce adsorbents, it should be noted that in order to fully assess the environmental and financial viability of the proposed circular economy concept, techno-economic and life cycle analysis should be carried out in the future. Future studies should investigate the influence of competing ions such as chloride, sulfate, nitrate, and phosphate to evaluate adsorption selectivity under realistic wastewater conditions. Future work may focus on evaluating the adsorption performance of the prepared sorbents in complex wastewater systems and investigating their long-term regeneration stability.
