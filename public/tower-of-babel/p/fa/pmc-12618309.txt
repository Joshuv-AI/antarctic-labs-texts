Treatment choices after endoscopic resection for pMM/SM1 esophageal squamous cell cancer: a retrospective study in China
Authors: Meichen Liu, Jing Wang, Qi Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment choices for patients with pathological muscularis mucosa/submucosal layer (pMM/SM1) esophageal squamous cell cancer (ESCC) after endoscopic resection (ER) are controversial.Materials and methodsA single-center retrospective study at Beijing Cancer Hospital in China was conducted. Between February 2012 and November 2023, we retrospectively recruited patients with pMM/SM1 ESCC who underwent ER with or without subsequent adjuvant therapy. Survival was compared between groups.ResultsOverall, 93 patients were screened, and 73 patients were included. Compared with the follow-up group, patients who underwent radical surgery (SR) immediately after ER demonstrated significantly shorter overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) (OS, P < 0.01; DFS, P < 0.001). High-risk patients (pMM/SM1 patients with an invasive cancer length ≤ 10 mm) may benefit from chemoradiotherapy (CRT), but low-risk patients may not.ConclusionThe length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm may be a newly identified high-risk factor. CRT after ER is recommended for high-risk patients but not for low-risk patients. Introduction

Endoscopic resection (ER) is currently the standard treatment for early-stage esophageal squamous cell cancer (ESCC) because of its advantages of safety, short recovery time, and organ preservation. Multiple guidelines [1–3] suggest that ESCC with an invasion depth categorized as pathological muscularis mucosa/submucosal layer (pMM/SM1) is a relative indication for ER because of the higher risk of lymph node metastasis (LNM). However, the reported risk of LNM varies widely in pMM/SM1 patients, 0–21.1% for pMM patients and 8.3–53.1% for SM1 patients [4–10]. Importantly, the rates of LNM detected in surgical samples may be greater than those detected in endoscopic resection samples due to differences in the standards for pathological section preparation. Consequently, some studies have indicated that the prognosis of pMM patients without lymphovascular invasion (LVI) is comparable to that of patients with pEP/LPM [11].

Current guidelines vary on the necessity of receiving adjuvant therapy after ER for pMM/SM1 patients. The Guidelines of the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society [1] recommend adjuvant therapy after ER for pMM patients with LVI or all pSM1 patients. However, owing to insufficient evidence, no recommendations can be made regarding adjuvant therapy for low-risk pMM patients. In contrast, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [12] guidelines suggest that adjuvant therapy may not be required after ER for pMM/SM1 patients who do not exhibit indicators of poor prognosis. Therefore, our study examined 93 patients with pathologically confirmed invasion depths categorized as pMM/pSM1 to explore potential treatment options after ER.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study was a single-center retrospective study conducted at Beijing Cancer Hospital in China. We retrospectively recruited 93 consecutive pMM/SM1 patients from February 2012 to November 2023 (Fig. 1). We excluded patients with (1) adenocarcinoma, (2) concomitant with other advanced malignancies, (3) insufficient clinical information (e.g., no follow-up data), or (4) a follow-up time < 6 months. The median follow-up time for overall survival (OS) was 38.5 months, and the median follow-up time for disease-free survival (DFS) was 38 months. A multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluated each patient receiving adjuvant therapy. The MDT preferentially recommended adjuvant therapy for patients with LVI(+) or invasion depth reaching the SM1. A total of 12 patients underwent adjuvant therapy immediately after ER; 3 patients underwent radical surgery (SR), 1 patient received radiotherapy (RT), 1 patient received chemotherapy (CT), and 7 patients received chemoradiotherapy (CRT). SR refers to radical resection that includes lymph node dissection. The radiation dose and area were unknown for 2 patients; the remaining 6 patients received radiotherapy at 50.4 Gy, encompassing the esophageal lesion and the surrounding areas, as well as the mediastinal lymphatic drainage region. Platinum-based chemotherapy was administered in two cycles.

Procedures and pathological evaluation

ER was performed by highly experienced endoscopists. All patients underwent endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD). ESD was performed through single-channel upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (GIF Q260J; Olympus Corp.) using a high-frequency generator (VIO 200S; ERBE Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany). An ENDO CUT Q (3:2:4 = effect/cutting duration/cutting interval) was used for submucosal dissection. A premixed sterilized solution of glycerol (10% glycerol and 5% fructose, Cisen Pharmaceutical, Shandong, China) and indigo carmine was injected into the submucosa (NM-200 L-0423; Olympus Co.). The lesion margin was marked with a Dual Knife (KD-650 L; Olympus Co.). The lesion was subsequently incised and dissected using the Dual Knife until ESD was complete. A gastric tube was placed, and oral hormones were administered to prevent stenosis in patients with lesions greater than 3/4 circumference.

Specimens obtained from ER were sliced into continuous blocks at intervals of approximately 3 mm, and sections with a thickness of 3 μm were taken from all the blocks and stained with hematoxylin‒eosin. pMM was defined as a lesion invading the muscularis mucosa, and pSM1 was defined as a lesion invading the submucosa within 200 μm of the muscularis mucosa [13]. The whole tumor was divided into invasive and noninvasive regions. The invasive region was defined as the part of the tumor that broke through the basement membrane. The length of invasive cancer and the length of the whole tumor were recorded for all patients.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with R version 4.41 and SPSS version 27.0. OS was defined as the date of ER to death from any cause. DFS was defined as the date of ER to recurrence/metastasis or death from any cause. The survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier estimation with the log-rank test. When 20% of the cell counts in a 2 × 2 table were less than 5 or at least one cell count was equal to 0, Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the differences between groups; otherwise, the χ2 test was used. All the statistical comparisons were performed using two-tailed tests, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics between adjuvant therapy and follow-up groups

Table 1 summarizes the clinicopathological characteristics of the adjuvant therapy and follow-up groups, revealing no significant differences in clinical characteristics such as gender, age, and drinking history, and pathological characteristics such as the length of invasive cancer, the length of tumor, and margin status. However, patients who had deeper invasion, LVI-positive, or ER performed after 2018 were significantly more likely to choose adjuvant therapy (P < 0.001, P = 0.006, and P = 0.048, respectively).

Recurrence and mortality

After ER, 9 patients experienced recurrence and/or metastasis. Among these patients, 4 patients (44.5%) experienced recurrence, 3 patients (33.3%) experienced metastasis, and 2 patients (22.2%) experienced both recurrence and metastasis. All patients with recurrence or metastasis received salvage therapy. At the end of the follow-up period, all patients with local recurrence survived; 2 patients with distant LNM survived, while 1 patient with bone metastasis died; and all patients who experienced both recurrence and metastasis died. The success rate of salvage therapy was 66.7% (6/9).

Prognostic factors of survival

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the length of invasive cancer was a prognostic factor for OS (log-rank, P = 0.025) (Fig. 2h) and possibly a prognostic factor for DFS (P = 0.059) (Fig. 3h). However, the length of tumor had no significant differences in either OS or DFS (P = 0.659, P = 0.540, respectively) (Figs. 2g, 3g). Furthermore, the macroscopic type IIa was related to significantly worse OS (P = 0.047). Other clinical factors, such as gender, age, and time of ER, revealed no significant differences in OS or DFS (Figs. 2 and 3). Pathology factors, such as invasion depth and LVI status, had no significant differences in OS or DFS (Figs. 2 and 3). Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 provide further OS and DFS analyses regarding drinking history, lateral margin (LM) or vertical margin (VM), and differentiation.

Adjuvant therapy in the whole group

Owing to the preference for CRT as adjuvant therapy among patients who underwent ER after 2018, those patients had relatively shorter follow-up periods. Supplementary Fig. 3a and b show that patients do not benefit from adjuvant therapy, with significantly shorter OS and DFS than those who did not receive adjuvant therapy (P < 0.01, P = 0.046, respectively). Further analysis of the impact of different treatments on prognosis revealed that patients did not benefit from SR (OS, P < 0.01; DFS, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4a and d). Patients who received CRT may have a longer DFS (P = 0.652) but no significant difference in OS (P = 1) (Fig. 4f and b). Additionally, since only one patient received CT and one patient received RT, no significant associations between these therapies and patient prognosis were found.

Adjuvant therapy in patients with the length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm

A total of 29 patients had a length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm. Among these patients, no significant differences in clinical or pathological characteristics were observed between the adjuvant therapy group and the follow-up group (Supplementary Table 1). Patients with a length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm did not experience benefit from treatment (Fig. 5a and d). Further analysis indicated that patients did not benefit from SR specifically (OS and DFS, P < 0.001). Additionally, owing to the shorter follow-up period for patients receiving CRT, there were no significant differences in OS or DFS between the CRT and follow-up groups (P = 1 and P = 1, respectively) (Fig. 5).

Prognosis of treatment methods for length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm. OS: a treatment status, b SR, c CRT. DFS: d treatment status, e SR, f CRT

Adjuvant therapy in patients with the length of invasive cancer > 10 mm

A total of 44 patients had a length of invasive cancer > 10 mm, and all 44 patients survived. Patients who had factors such as deeper invasion, LVI-positive, or ER performed after 2018 tended to receive adjuvant therapy (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2). However, Kaplan‒Meier analysis revealed that these factors were not associated with DFS (P > 0.1) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Similar to patients with a length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm, patients with a length of invasive cancer > 10 mm did not benefit from SR (P = 0.025). Additionally, DFS was not significantly increased in patients receiving CRT (P = 0.675) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our study indicated that high-risk factors, such as LVI-positive, margin positive, poor differentiation, and length of tumor, that are recommended by various guidelines do not affect the survival of pMM/SM1 patients. Invasive cancer is the aggressive part of the whole tumor, and the length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm is considered a high-risk factor. Furthermore, neither high-risk nor low-risk patients derived any benefit from SR. An exploratory analysis revealed that patients with a length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm might benefit from adjuvant CRT, whereas those with a length > 10 mm might not. Overall, follow-up is acceptable for low-risk pMM/SM1 patients, with 66.7% completing successful salvage therapy after recurrence or metastasis.

Controversy 1: Identifying high-risk factors. The 2020 Japanese guidelines [1] identified LVI-positive as a high-risk factor, whereas the 2022 European guidelines [2] proposed that at least one of the following conditions need to be met to classify a patient as high risk: undifferentiated, LVI-positive, or VM-positive. The 2023 American guidelines [3] recommend that poorly differentiated, ulcerated patients be considered high risk. Moreover, the 2024 Chinese guidelines suggest that VM-positive, LVI-positive, and poorly differentiated patients should receive adjuvant therapy after ER. Currently, all guidelines agree that LVI-positive is a high-risk factor for pMM/SM1 patients due to a heightened risk of LNM. However, our study revealed that LVI-positive was not associated with a worse DFS or OS, which could be attributed to the small sample size. Poorly differentiated or undifferentiated tumors are viewed as high-risk factors in all guidelines except those of Japan. However, some studies have indicated that poor differentiation or undifferentiation does not significantly increase the LNM rate [14, 15]. This finding is consistent with our study, which revealed that differentiation status was not associated with DFS or OS in pMM/SM1 patients. The Chinese and European guidelines recommend VM-positive as a high-risk factor, but debate about what constitutes VM-positive is ongoing. At present, there is no evidence in esophageal cancer research that a tumor distance of less than 1 mm from the resection margin should be considered a positive margin. In our study, a free margin was considered positive only if R1 or Rx resection was performed. We did not observe any effect of VM-positive on DFS or OS. The length of tumor is believed to be unrelated to survival outcomes, which is consistent with our findings that patients with a length of tumor ≥ and < 3 cm had no significant differences in DFS or OS. However, further analysis revealed that patients with a length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm had a poorer prognosis. One possible explanation is that general tumor length calculations include both invasive and noninvasive regions. We hypothesized that both invasive and noninvasive cancer influence patient survival. However, our study found that the length of total tumor was not associated with survival, whereas the length of invasive cancer exhibited a significant correlation with survival. This suggests that invasive cancer, rather than noninvasive regions, maybe the primary determinant of survival. Conventionally, the longer cancers tend to have deeper invasion. Then, shorter cancers having deep invasion may indicate more aggressive biological behavior and higher metastasis potential. This observation may potentially explain the poorer survival observed in patients with the length of invasive cancer ≤ 10mm.

Controversy 2: Whether to accept adjuvant therapy. The Japanese [1] and American [3] guidelines recommend that all pSM1 patients receive adjuvant therapy, regardless of their risk factors. Moreover, these guidelines suggest that pMM patients with high-risk factors should receive adjuvant therapy immediately after ER. On the other hand, the Chinese and European guidelines advise adjuvant therapy only for pMM/SM1 patients with high-risk factors. Notably, the European [2] guidelines categorize pMM/SM1 patients without high-risk factors as low risk candidates for resection. A large retrospective study [16] reported that the 5-year metastatic recurrence rates for LVI-negative and VM-negative pMM/SM1 patients who did not receive adjuvant therapy after ER were 2.6% and 4.3%, respectively, which are much lower than the reported rates of 10.5% and 28.6%, respectively, after SR. Unlike early gastric cancer patients, in whom distant metastases are more common, early ESCC patients experience mainly local recurrence (65.2%), with a success rate of salvage therapy of 73.3% [16]. Our findings are consistent with these data, as we observed that 44.5% of patients experienced local recurrence, with a salvage therapy success rate of 66.7%. Several retrospective studies [17, 18] have indicated that pMM/SM1 patients with high-risk factors could benefit from adjuvant therapy after ER. Conversely, low-risk patients did not appear to gain any advantage from adjuvant therapy, which is consistent with our observations. Although the short follow-up period did not produce statistically significant results, high-risk patients appear to benefit from CRT.

Controversy 3: SR or CRT. All the guidelines recommend making decisions according to the specific wishes of the patient and thorough multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions. However, reports of survival results comparing patients who undergo adjuvant SR versus those who undergo CRT are conflicting, as summarized in Table 2. In earlier studies, SR offered better prognoses than CRT did [19–21]. A large retrospective study published in 2021 [22] confirmed that the 5-year OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates for adjuvant SR were superior to those for CRT (OS: 94.3% vs. 79.0%, P = 0.039; RFS: 91.1 vs. 88.5%, P = 0.298). This difference may be attributed to the effectiveness of SR in controlling local recurrences and LNM. As radiation and chemotherapy techniques have advanced, these findings have changed. A study published in 2022 indicated that CRT outperformed SR in terms of OS and RFS [23]. Another study from the same year revealed that low-risk patients who received adjuvant SR had worse OS and no significant improvement in progression-free survival (PFS). No clear benefit for OS and PFS was observed among high-risk patients who underwent adjuvant SR [15]. Our study also suggested that neither low-risk nor high-risk patients benefit from SR. Given the diminishing advantages of SR, such as increased perioperative mortality risks and declines in quality of life, the role of CRT has gradually become more prominent. A prospective study, the JCOG508 trial [24], published in 2023, confirmed that CRT could achieve an OS similar to that of SR. A 2025 large-scale multicenter propensity score-matched study revealed that CRT provides marginally better prognoses than SR does in low-risk patients, whereas high-risk patients show the inverse pattern, but these differences lack statistical significance [25]. Our study indicates that high-risk patients may benefit from CRT but not SR.

This study has several limitations. This study was a single-center retrospective study with a small sample size. First, the patient backgrounds were not well balanced between the groups. Second, the SR group had fewer patients than the CRT group did, possibly because of varying preferences for adjuvant therapies over time. Therefore, these results should be interpreted cautiously because of potential bias. Large-scale prospective randomized controlled trials are necessary to address these limitations.

Conclusion

We systematically reviewed existing research and combined it with our outcomes to provide possible insights regarding adjuvant therapy. In addition to LVI, a length of invasive cancer ≤ 10 mm may be a newly discovered high-risk factor. We recommend incorporating the length of invasive cancer into the standard pathological description. Furthermore, SR may not be an appropriate adjuvant therapy option. In recent years, our center has favored CRT as adjuvant therapy, which may be a suitable choice for high-risk patients but is not recommended for those at low risk. Overall, a comprehensive evaluation of ER indications should be rigorously performed. Only patients with ESCC at cTisN0M0 or cT1aN0M0 should be subjected to ER. When post-ER pathology reveals high-risk features, the MDT should determine appropriate adjuvant therapies. This systematic strategy reduces both overtreatment and undertreatment risks while optimizing survival outcomes with minimal complications.
