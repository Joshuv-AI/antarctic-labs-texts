The TgWRKY24 transcription factor is involved in releasing seed dormancy by responding to abscisic acid signals in Tulipa thianschanica
Authors: Zhengguang Sun, Yi Ji, Shuang Liu, Yang Yan, Yang Zheng, Li Xue, Jiajun Lei, Jun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Tulipa L., a prominent bulbous flower of the Liliaceae family, exhibits profound dormancy traits in its seeds, which significantly constrains its breeding potential. While cold temperature and hormone treatments have been shown to effectively alleviate this dormancy, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.ResultThe transcription factor TgWRKY24, a member of the WRKY family, was identified in T. thianschanica seeds, and its functionality was validated. TgWRKY24 subcellularly localizes to the nucleus, and its expression is inhibited by abscisic acid (ABA). The germination of T. thianschanica seeds affected the virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) system targeting TgWRKY24, resulting in increased ABA content. Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays demonstrated the interaction between TgWRKY24 and TgCYP707. TgWRKY24 facilitates seed germination by transcriptionally activating TgCYP707, leading to a reduction in endogenous ABA levels.ConclusionThese findings indicate that TgWRKY24 plays a modulatory role in regulating ABA during seed germination. These findings are important for exploring the molecular mechanism involved in breaking the dormancy of tulip seeds and enriching the germplasm resources of tulips.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07483-w.

Introduction

Seed dormancy is a critical characteristic that determines the environmental conditions required for germination and is influenced by genetic factors and various environmental variables [1]. Baskin categorized seed dormancy into five types: physiological dormancy (PD), morphological dormancy (MD), morphophysiological dormancy (MPD), physical dormancy (PY), and combined dormancy (PY + PD) [2]. The cessation of dormancy is essential for the initiation of seed germination.

Seed germination commences when desiccated seeds imbibe water, culminating when part of the embryo, typically the radicle, protrudes through the seed coat. Upon water absorption by mature dry seeds, three sequential hydration stages occurred: rapid initial uptake in stage I, a stable phase in stage II, and further water assimilation during cotyledon elongation in stage III. Dormant seeds are able to progress to stage III, thereby preventing germination [3]. External stimuli are essential for overcoming seed dormancy, as temperature, light, water, and phytohormones play crucial roles in regulating the balance between dormancy and germination [4–8]. Additionally, specific endogenous seed substances such as nitrates and reactive oxygen species can expedite seed germination [9]– [10]. The internal factors involve the interplay between endogenous plant hormones and genes. Phytohormone concentrations depend on gene expression levels, and changes in gene expression activate specific phytohormones. Hence, modulating phytohormone levels via gene expression may effectively regulate seed germination [4].

Abscisic acid (ABA) and its responsive genes play key roles in seed dormancy and germination [11]. As a sesquiterpenoid compound derived from C40 carotenoid precursors, ABA initiates biosynthesis within chloroplasts, with the oxidized carotenoid (such as lutein) serving as the immediate precursor [12]. Although ABA improved the tolerance of plants to stress, elevated ABA levels can hinder plant growth during prolonged stress. Nevertheless, ABA remains essential for plant growth and development by inducing dormancy during seed maturation and regulating dormancy release and germination in response to environmental stimuli [13].

The WRKY transcription factor (TF) family is a crucial regulator of various plant processes, including responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, senescence, seed dormancy, and germination [14]. WRKY proteins are characterized by the presence of one or two WRKY structural domains, containing a specific WRKYGQK amino acid sequence and a zinc-finger motif at the N-terminus [15, 16]. Recent advancements in ABA research have highlighted the pivotal role of specific WRKY transcription factors in ABA signaling pathways at multiple levels [17]. Over the past two decades, research on WRKYs has focused predominantly on their involvement in plant defense mechanisms [18, 19]. Notably, MdWRKY31 significantly enhances apple tree sensitivity to ABA under drought conditions [20]. GhWRKY17 regulates ABA signaling and cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in response to drought and salinity [21], whereas OsWRKY5 functions as a negative modulator of ABA-mediated drought stress tolerance [22].

Recent research has indicated that WRKY transcription factors are crucial for processes such as dormancy, germination, postembryonic morphogenesis, flowering, gametophyte development and seed production [23]. The absence of OsWRKY29 results in elevated ABA levels, suppressed OsWRKY29 expression, and increased seed dormancy in rice [24]. Conversely, the overexpression of OsWRKY50 promotes seed germination and renders seedlings insensitive to ABA [25]. Additionally, the expression of AtWRKY6 is downregulated during seed germination and early maturation but is induced in response to exogenous ABA. Furthermore, overexpression of AtWRKY6 enhances the sensitivity of Arabidopsis thaliana seeds and seedlings to ABA [26]. Both AtWRKY18 and AtWRKY60 increase A. thaliana sensitivity to ABA, thereby inhibiting seed germination and root elongation while increasing tolerance to salt and osmotic stress. However, the role of AtWRKY40 in germination and growth assays differs from that of these genes [27].

The ornamental bulb plant Tulipa L., indigenous to Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Altai Mountains, possesses a significant historical legacy dating back to its introduction to Europe from Turkey in the mid-16th century. This introduction led to the ‘tulip mania’ phenomenon in the Netherlands during the 17th century [28]. This shows the charm and popularity of tulips around the world. The ongoing exploration of tulip breeding has revealed numerous wild germplasm resources, but the potential of T. thianschanica as a valuable wild tulip germplasm resource native to China remains largely unexplored in breeding and associated studies. T. thianschanica exhibits exceptional adaptability and stress resistance, making it an invaluable breeding resource. Although tulips propagate primarily asexually through bulb division, seed propagation is essential for the breeding of new varieties [28]. Nevertheless, the significant dormancy of tulip seeds presents challenges for seed reproduction, emphasizing the need to investigate the molecular mechanisms governing seed dormancy in tulip species.

In this study, we investigated the fluctuations in five phytohormones during the germination of T. thianschanica seeds. The transcription factor TgWRKY24 was identified from T. thianschanica seeds via transcriptome data screening and homologous cloning. Notably, ABA treatment suppressed the expression of TgWRKY24 in the seeds, highlighting the regulatory role of ABA. The functional analysis revealed that TgWRKY24 promoted seed germination, which was confirmed through VIGS experiments in T. thianschanica. TgWRKY24 was observed to decrease ABA levels in seeds by expediting ABA breakdown via interactions with TgCYP707A. These findings demonstrated the critical role of TgWRKY24 in T. thianschanica seed germination, elucidated its interaction with ABA, and expanded our understanding of the molecular mechanisms regulating the release of seed dormancy in tulips. Furthermore, this research has significant implications for the utilization of tulip germplasm resources and advancements in tulip breeding.

Results

Role of ABA in breaking dormancy in T. thianschanica seeds

The T. thianschanica seeds exhibited optimal germination under low-temperature and high-moisture conditions. Variability in the initial seed germination rate was observed at 30 d after different treatments, and the germination rate progressively decreased with increasing concentrations (50, 100, and 200 µM) of ABA (Fig. 1A and B). Throughout the germination period (1, 10, 20, 30, and 40 d), the contents of 5 endogenous phytohormones associated with the ABA content clearly fluctuated, and the highest content was observed at 30 d, with the lowest content observed at 20 d (Fig. 1C). Conversely, the endogenous GA3 content exhibited an inverse pattern compared with that of ABA from day 1 to day 20 but exhibited a similar trend on days 30 and 40 (Fig. 1D). In contrast, the endogenous IAA content reached its minimum on day 10 and peaked on day 40. Notably, during seed germination, the endogenous IAA content decreased, followed by an increase, with the lowest content observed on day 10 and the highest on day 40 (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the SA content fluctuated, reaching its highest level on day 40 and lowest level on day 30 (Fig. 1F). Throughout seed germination, the ZT content exhibited a minimal trend, with consistently low levels observed for IAA, SA, and ZT (Fig. 1G). Therefore, the analysis of germination rates and phytohormone concentrations in T. thianschanica seeds revealed that ABA inhibited the process of seed germination.

Transcriptional factor analysis of transcriptome differences

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on seeds of Tulipa tianschanica subjected to 4 °C stratification for 1 d (PD, physiological dormancy), 20 d (DR, dormancy release), and 40 d (ND, non-dormancy). Transcription factors (TFs) with differential expression were screened, and comparisons were made across the three periods—with a specific focus on the 1-day (PD, physiological dormancy) vs. 40-day (ND, non-dormancy) comparison. This analysis revealed a large number of TF members belonging to six families, with the following counts (PD vs. ND): C2H2 (20/9), ERF (12/13), bZIP (14/6), WRKY (16/3), NAC (13/5), and bHLH (13/4) (Table 1).

Identification and screening of the WRKY family

By analyzing the differential transcription factors in the transcriptome of T. thianschanica seeds, particular attention was given to the transcription factor WRKY family. A total of 15 members from this family were screened via evolutionary tree, motif and domain analysis (Fig. 2A, B). Considering the quantity of WRKY domains and the varieties of zinc finger domains, the genes were classified into three classes. Class I included TgWRKY4, TgWRKY32, TgWRKY24, and TgWRKY46. Class II included TgWRKY11, TgWRKY23, TgWRKY27, TgWRKY28, TgWRKY34, TgWRKY40, TgWRKY6, TgWRKY65, TgWRKY69, and TgWRKY75, whereas TgWRKY70 was categorized as Class III (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, altered expression levels were observed in certain family members during seed germination (Fig. 2C).

Tissue-specific expression pattern analysis of TgWRKYs

Four WRKY family members were subjected to tissue-specific expression analyses. qRT‒PCR analysis revealed substantial variations in the expression levels of TgWRKY4, TgWRKY11, TgWRKY23, and TgWRKY24 across different tissues of T. thianschanica. Notably, TgWRKY4, TgWRKY11, and TgWRKY24 presented the highest transcription levels in the seeds, whereas TgWRKY23 presented the highest expression in the roots (Fig. 4A).

Subcellular localization of TgWRKY24

The subcellular localization of TgWRKY24 was examined by expressing the recombinant vector (35 S::TgWRKY24-GFP) and the control vector (pCambia1300-35 s-GFP) in N. tabacum leaves. The GFP fluorescence from the control vector presented a uniform distribution throughout the cell, whereas the 35 S::TgWRKY24-GFP vector presented intense fluorescence, which was specifically localized within the nucleus (Fig. 4B).

TgWRKY24 is responsive to alterations in ABA levels

qRT‒PCR was performed on T. thianchanica seeds subjected to stratification, those treated with 100 and 200 µM ABA, and untreated control seeds. The results revealed that the relative expression of TgWRKY24 significantly decreased after ABA treatment compared with that in control seeds. Moreover, the relative expression of TgWRKY24 gradually decreased with increasing ABA concentration (Fig. 4C).

Verifying the function of TgWRKY24

To validate the function of TgWRKY24, VIGS experiments were conducted on T. thianschanica seeds to confirm its functionality. The results revealed a substantial decrease in the rate of seed germination in the silenced seeds compared with the unloaded seeds (Fig. 5A, B), and the ABA content in the seeds was significantly greater (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the transcription of TgNCED, a gene associated with ABA biosynthesis, demonstrated negligible changes compared with that in the controls (Fig. 5F), whereas the expression of TgCYP707A, encoding an 8’-hydroxylase in the ABA metabolism pathway, was downregulated (Fig. 5E).

TgWRKY24 interacts with TgCYP707A

To elucidate the function of TgWRKY24 in seed germination, yeast two-hybrid assays involving TgWRKY24 and TgCYP707A demonstrated that all four strains grew normally on SD/-Leu-Trp media. In contrast, the combination of BD-TgWRKY24 and AD-T resulted in impaired growth on SD/-Leu-Trp-His-Ade media, whereas the pairing of BD-TgWRKY24 and AD-TgCYP707 resulted in normal growth. These findings indicate a mutualistic interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 6A). The construction and injection of the NE-TgWRKY24 and CE-TgCYP707 vectors into tobacco leaves for co-expression via the BiFC assay resulted in the observation of YFP signals, further confirming the interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

ABA is a key hormone in the germination of T. thianschanica seeds

Stratification and cold storage are effective methods for breaking seed dormancy and promoting germination in T. thianschanica seeds, as corroborated by studies by Kritskaya [29] and Li [30]. Under ambient temperature and moist conditions, minimal germination was observed, in contrast to optimal germination after extended exposure to low temperature and moisture. Notably, germination was negligible at 20 d, modest at 30 d, and markedly increased after 40 d (Fig. 1A, B). The critical germination period spanned from 20 to 40 d, during which the levels of ABA, GA3, SA, IAA, and ZT were monitored. Throughout this period, the hormone levels followed the sequence GA3 >ABA >SA >IAA >ZT, with significant fluctuations observed for several hormones, except ZT. Specifically, during the crucial 20-day phase, ABA levels increased by 5712.49% and decreased by −89.70%, whereas GA3 increased by 1035.79% and decreased by −35.03%. The growth rates for IAA were 19.45% and 2.64%, and those for SA ranged from − 90.78% to 4450.02% (Fig. 1C). ABA presented the highest rate of change during germination and was ranked second only to GA3 in terms of overall concentration. Given its well-documented role in seed dormancy and germination across species [31]– [32], ABA may play a crucial role in regulating the germination process.

During seed germination, the endogenous ABA content initially increased from day 1 to day 10. This elevation was likely attributed to the initial dry state of the seeds, the subsequent maturation processes, and the swelling, which may have contributed to this increase. The increased ABA levels potentially inhibited germination, possibly because of the osmotic conditions surrounding the seeds or the inherent ABA levels within the seeds [33]. ABA plays a crucial role in positively regulating reserve accumulation while concurrently inhibiting embryo growth, promoting drought tolerance, and maintaining primary dormancy. ABA degradation typically occurs between day 10 and day 20, alleviating dormancy and allowing the seeds to germinate under favorable conditions [12, 34]. The ABA content subsequently increased between days 20 and 30. During this phase, ABA may act as a germination signal, or its increase in seed content may respond to environmental changes, potentially inducing dormancy under unfavorable conditions. However, further investigations could be necessary to fully elucidate the reasons for the increase in ABA during this period [34–36]. After 30 d, a decrease in ABA content by 40 d correlated with the germination of most seeds (Fig. 1A-C). Additionally, previous studies have consistently shown that exogenous ABA application delays seed germination [36].

Studies on dormancy-related genes in response to ABA in T. thianschanica seeds

Transcriptome analysis revealed that six transcription factor families, C2H2, ERF, bZIP, bHLH, NAC and WRKY, all had a significant number of family members that underwent content changes during seed germination, potentially contributing to the transition from seed dormancy to germination, directly or indirectly influencing hormone metabolism (Table 1). Among these families, the WRKY family, characterized by its substantial membership, has received particular attention in recent ABA research. These specific WRKY transcription factors are firmly positioned within the ABA-responsive signaling pathway and operate at multiple regulatory levels [17, 37].

Consequently, our investigation focused on the WRKY family and conducted comparative analyses of their gene sequences, which confirmed the presence of the characteristic WRKY structural domains across all the members (Fig. 2A). The correlation analyses between these WRKY family members and the ABA content during seed dormancy release (Tab. S2) facilitated quantitative assessments, identifying several members that exhibited consistent patterns in ABA content changes during seed germination (Fig. 3). Phylogenetic tree analyses integrating the A. thaliana WRKY family proteins revealed the clustering of TgWRKY4, TgWRKY11, TgWRKY23, and TgWRKY24 with AtWRKY44, AtWRKY11, AtWRKY23, and AtWRKY33 (Fig. S2). These four members were differentially expressed across various tissues of T. thianschanica, with TgWRKY4, TgWRKY11, and TgWRKY24 being predominantly expressed in seeds (Fig. 4A). Notably, AtWRKY33 deficiency in A. thaliana has been associated with reduced resistance to gray mold due to elevated ABA levels resulting from this gene deletion, highlighting its negative regulation of ABA [38].

TgWRKY24 breaks dormancy in seeds by negatively regulating ABA

TgWRKY24, spanning 1287 bp, encodes a 429-amino-acid protein with a relative molecular weight of 48.40 kDa. The protein exhibited a lipid coefficient of 22.66, a hydrophilicity coefficient of −1.067, and a theoretical isoelectric point of 6.96, indicating its relatively water-soluble and structurally unstable nature (Fig. S1). As a member of the WRKY family, TgWRKY24 comprises two WRKY motifs and two C2H2 structural domains (Fig. 2A, B). In this study, TgWRKY24 was cloned from T. thianschanica seeds and, consistent with other WRKY proteins [20], was localized to the nucleus (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the gene presented varying expression levels across different tissue types of T. thianschanica, with the highest relative expression in the seeds (Fig. 4A). The treatment of the seeds with ABA under controlled conditions resulted in a decrease in the relative expression of TgWRKY24 in the pretreatment group compared with that in the control group (Fig. 4C), suggesting potential negative regulation of TgWRKY24 by ABA. These findings are in line with previous research [39].

The VIGS experiments conducted on T. thianschanica seeds revealed that reducing the relative expression of TgWRKY24 led to a marked increase in the ABA content within the seeds, accompanied by a significant decrease in the seed germination rate (Fig. 5A-D). The NCED and CYP707A gene families, which are pivotal in ABA metabolism, play critical roles in diverse ABA-mediated plant developmental processes, including seed development and germination [32, 39]. The NCED family encodes the key enzyme (9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase) involved in ABA biosynthesis, which is essential for regulating ABA levels in plants [40–42]. In particular, PoNCED acts as a rate-limiting factor in this biosynthetic pathway [43]. Studies of SlNCED2 silencing in tomatoes have revealed significantly lower ABA levels than in controls, highlighting the crucial role of NCED genes in ABA synthesis [44]. Furthermore, the binding of FaWRKY48 to the FaNCED1 promoter, which contains a WRKY-responsive cis-element, regulates ABA synthesis in strawberry [45]. The CYP707A family encodes 8β-hydroxylases, which are critical for the regulation of plant ABA metabolism and associated physiological processes [46]. In Arabidopsis, AtCYP707A2 transcript levels increase from late to mature dry seeds, with AtCYP707A1 being pivotal for reducing ABA levels during mid-maturation, AtCYP707A2 regulating ABA levels from late maturation to germination, and AtCYP707A2 inducing a decrease in ABA content during the break of seed dormancy by NO [47, 48]. The ectopic overexpression of VvWRKY37 in transgenic poplar delayed bud germination, suppressed the ABA catabolism gene CYP707A, and increased endogenous ABA accumulation. NaWRKY70 enhanced disease resistance in tobacco by inhibiting NaCYP707A4 expression [49, 50]. After silencing by TgWRKY24, the content of TgCYP707A increased significantly, and it was verified that TgWRKY24 interacted with TgCYP707A; thus, it can be inferred that TgWRKY24 breaks dormancy and promotes seed germination by reducing the content of ABA in seeds.

In this investigation, the silencing of TgWRKY24 through a VIGS assay resulted in notable upregulation of TgCYP707A, a gene linked to ABA breakdown. The corroborative evidence of the interaction between TgWRKY24 and TgCYP707A was ascertained through yeast two-hybrid and BiFC assays. These collective findings underscore the role of TgWRKY24 in stimulating seed dormancy release and hastening germination by facilitating ABA catabolism.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed that TgWRKY24 plays a role in decreasing ABA levels in T. thianschanica seeds, thereby breaking seed dormancy. The interaction between TgWRKY24 and TgCYP707A leads to a reduction in the endogenous ABA content, consequently promoting seed germination. This investigation is essential for fully understanding the molecular regulatory mechanisms of ABA signal transduction and dormancy release in T. thianschanica, warranting comprehensive validation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

Seeds and bulbs of Tulipa thianschanica were collected in 2017 from Zhaosu County, Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture, Xinjiang, China (80°08’ E, 43°15’ N) and have been preserved at the Tianzhushan Experimental Base of Shenyang Agricultural University (123°34’12’’ E, 41°49’48’’ N). The collection of these germplasm resources was in full compliance with the relevant regulations in force at that time, and all experimental materials used in subsequent studies were sourced from the aforementioned Tianzhushan Experimental Base. The samples for tissue-specific gene expression analysis were obtained from various plant parts, including fibrous roots, bulbs, stems, leaves, stamens and petals. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

After sterilization, the healthy seeds were subjected to germination trials at 4 °C and high humidity. The experimental setup included distilled water as the control group (CK) and ABA solutions at concentrations of 50, 100, and 200 micromolar (µM) as the experimental groups, with each treatment replicated three times. The samples were systematically collected at intervals of one day (1 d), ten days (10 d), twenty days (20 d), thirty days (30 d), and forty days (40 d) and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80 °C until further experimentation.

Germination rate determination

Seed germination at 4 °C was evaluated by placing the seeds in Petri dishes containing two layers of filter paper and adding 5 mL of distilled water to each dish. The germination assessments were conducted periodically at 10-day intervals.

Determination of endogenous plant hormone contents

The samples were freeze-dried in liquid nitrogen and were finely powdered. The powder (0.5 g) was subsequently subjected to liquid extraction via a 2 mL water: methanol (1:4, v/v) mixture. After sequential extractions lasting 16, 2, and 1 h at 4 °C, the collected supernatants were combined. The eluent was then vacuum-dried at room temperature under pressures ranging from − 0.01 to 0.09 MPa. After drying, 2 mL of a water: acetonitrile mixture (9:1 v/v) was added to the residue. After thorough dissolution and mixing, the solution was centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 2 min. The resulting supernatant was aspirated with a syringe, passed through a 0.22 microporous organic filter membrane three times, and subsequently analyzed via appropriate instrumentation.

The negative ion mode was used to quantify the endogenous plant hormones gibberellic acid (GA3), ABA, and salicylic acid (SA), whereas the positive ion mode was used to determine indole acetic acid (IAA) and zeatin (ZT). The mobile phases were A: water, B: acetonitrile, C: 0.1% aqueous methanol, and D: methanol. The samples were introduced into an autosampler at 8 °C, with the column temperature set at 40 °C, the flow rate at 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume at 4 µL. The gradient elution profile was set as follows: from 0 to 0.3 min, 10% solvent B and 90% solvent C; from 0.3 to 3 min, a linear transition from 10% to 60% B and from 90% to 40% C; from 3 to 5 min, a linear change from 60% to 80% B and from 40% to 20% C; from 5 to 7.1 min, maintaining 80% B and linearly decreasing from 40% to 20% C; and from 7.1 to 8 min, maintaining 10% B and 90% C. The peak areas of the respective quantified ions were quantified via the external standard method after performing the analysis in triplicate via the retention times and characteristic ions of the hormones.

RNA extraction and RT‒qPCR analysis

The total RNA from the samples was extracted via the OminiPlant RNA Kit (DNase I) (CWBIO, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The first strand of cDNA was subsequently synthesized via the HiScript III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China). The quantitative primers were designed via Primer3 Input and validated through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The gene expression levels were quantified via these primers, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate, both biologically and technically. Relative expression levels were determined via the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Screening of TgWRKY24

The genes were identified from the seed transcriptome data of T. thianschanica via ORF-Finder, and domain analysis was conducted via NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The sequence comparison was performed via DNAMAN V6, and the multiple sequence alignment results were visualized via Jalview software. Motif analysis was performed via MEME Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/). The associations between the genes and the ABA content during germination were investigated via OmicStudio (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool). Homologous proteins of A. thaliana were obtained from the TAIR database (https://www.Arabidopsis.org/Blast/index.jsp). The evolutionary trees were constructed via the maximum likelihood method via MEGA 7.0 software.

Cloning and sequence analysis of TgWRKY24

The protein analysis was performed via several online tools. ExPASy-ProtParam (https://web.ExPASy.org/protparam/) was used to analyze the amino acid sequence for properties such as hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity. SignalP 5.0 (SignalP 5.0 - DTU Health Tech - Bioinformatic Services) was used to predict and assess the signal peptide structure of TgWRKY24. The secondary structure of the protein was analyzed via NPS@(https://www.npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=npsa_sopma.html), and a tertiary structure model was generated via SWISS-MODEL (https://www.swissmodel.ExPASy.org/). The full-length TgWRKY24 sequence was amplified via a primer designed by Vazyme (https://www.crm.vazyme.com/cetool/simple.html).

Subcellular localization of TgWRKY24

The complete coding sequence (CDS) of TgWRKY24 was integrated into a modified pCambia1300 plant expression vector. This construct, under the control of the CaMV35S promoter and encompassing the green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter gene, yielded the fusion vector TgWRKY24-GFP. The recombinant vector and a control vector, pCambia1300-35s-GFP, were subsequently introduced into tobacco leaves (Nicotiana tabacum) via Agrobacterium infiltration. The transformed tobacco leaf cells were visualized via a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP5-II, Germany).

Virus-induced gene Silencing (VIGS)

To silence TgWRKY24 in tulip seeds, a 309 bp nonconserved fragment located 698–1007 bp downstream of the start codon (ATG) of TgWRKY24 was amplified and inserted into the pTRV2 vector. The obtained pTRV2-TgWRKY24 fusion vector, along with the empty vectors pTRV1 and pTRV2, was individually introduced into Agrobacterium GV3101. The pTRV1 bacterial suspension (OD600 = 1.0) was mixed with pTRV2 or pTRV2-TgWRKY24 at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and suspended in MES buffer (200 mM acetosyringone, 10 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM MES, pH 5.8). The bacterial suspension was incubated in the dark at 28 °C for 3–4 h. The tulip seeds were vacuum-infiltrated with the bacterial mixture, washed with distilled water, and cultured in the dark at 28 °C for two days before being transferred to 4 °C for phenotypic observation. Each treatment involved a minimum of 300 tulip seeds per treatment.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays

The full-length CDS of TgCYP707A was combined with the vector pGADT7 to form the recombinant vector pGADT7-TgCYP707A; the full-length CDS of TgWRKY24 was combined with the vector pGBKT7 to form the recombinant vector pGBKT7-TgWRKY24. The prey (pGADT7-TgCYP707A) and the bait (pGBKT7-TgWRKY24) were transferred into the Y2HGold yeast strain according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Pyeast, China). The yeast strains were subsequently plated on SD/-Trp-Leu media at 30 °C. Three days later, the yeast clones were transferred to SD/-Trp-Leu and SD/Trp-Leu-His-Ade (supplemented with 20 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole) media to test for possible interactions.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays

The stop codon-deleted TgWRKY24 and TgCYP707A were cloned and inserted into the pSPYNE-35 S and pSPYCE-35 S vectors, respectively. pSPYNE-35 S-TgWRKY24, pSPYCE-35 S-TgCYP707A and pSPYCE-35 S were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101, and positive clonal plaques were identified. The tobacco leaves were impregnated in accordance with the groups of pSPYNE-35 S-TgWRKY24 & pSPYCE-35 S and pSPYNE-35 S-TgWRKY24 & pSPYCE-35 S-TgCYP707A. The tobacco was cultured for 2 days, after which the fluorescence was observed via a laser confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data are presented as the means ± standard errors. Statistical analyses were carried out via IBM SPSS Statistics version 27, where the significance level was established at P < 0.05.
