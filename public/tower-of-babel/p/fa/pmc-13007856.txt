A wolf in sheep’s clothing—unmasking cardiac sarcoidosis through pacemaker dysfunction: a case report
Authors: Tardu Özkartal, Marco Bergonti, Daniele Faccenda, Maria Luce Caputo, Giulio Conte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac sarcoidosis may present with arrhythmias and conduction disease years before the diagnosis is established. Device-related issues, such as elevated pacing thresholds, can be misleading and may reflect myocardial inflammation rather than true lead malfunction.Case summaryA 49-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation underwent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation in 2018 for syncope and alternating bundle branch block. Introduction

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) is a heterogeneous disease that may present with conduction abnormalities, arrhythmias, or subtle structural changes long before a diagnosis is established. Early clinical manifestations are often non-specific, making timely recognition challenging and potentially leading to delays that affect outcomes. Multimodality imaging and careful evaluation of unexplained conduction disease are essential. The following case illustrates these diagnostic difficulties and highlights the complexities of managing cardiac involvement in systemic sarcoidosis.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation underwent dual-chamber pacemaker (MicroPort Teo DR, Shanghai, China) implantation in 2018 (Summary figure) because of recurrent syncope and alternating right and left bundle branch block. Given her young age and presence of both atrial arrhythmias and conduction disease, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, which revealed extensive septal fibrosis (Figure 1). Coronary angiography and myocardial biopsy, performed after pacemaker implantation, demonstrated normal coronary arteries and no histological abnormalities except for non-specific fibrosis. No further diagnostic work-up was pursued, and the patient was followed by her cardiologist.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging performed before pacemaker implantation in 2018 showing extensive, rather patchy fibrosis of the septum (arrows).

In 2020, she presented at her cardiologist with recurrent presyncope. Device interrogation revealed markedly elevated right ventricular (RV) pacing thresholds, and she was referred to our centre for lead revision. The RV lead was extracted and replaced in a slightly different position, after which the patient was again followed by her local cardiologist.

In December 2024, at the age of 56 years, the patient was hospitalized at our centre for syncope. Device interrogation demonstrated very high pacing thresholds with intermittent loss of capture (LOC) due to sub-threshold pacing, along with pacing dependency—99% atrial and 100% ventricular pacing. Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal left ventricular systolic function without significant valvular disease or pulmonary hypertension. However, two small, localized left-sided aneurysms were noted at the level of the interventricular septum (Figure 2; Supplementary material online, Video S1).

Echocardiography showing a modified apical five-chamber view. At the interventricular septum, two small, localized, left-sided aneurysms can be appreciated (arrows).

A new extraction and re-implantation procedure was declined by the patient. Consequently, implantation of an additional RV lead was planned. During the intervention, however, an asymptomatic occlusion of the superior vena cava (see Supplementary material online, Video S2), probably caused by mechanical stress from the pacemaker leads and/or the previous extraction procedure, rendered implantation impossible, and the procedure was abandoned. The following day, a leadless pacemaker (Medtronic Micra AV2, Dublin, Ireland) with atrial sensing capability via accelerometers was implanted. This configuration enabled appropriate atrioventricular (AV) sequential pacing—atrial pacing through the existing transvenous system and ventricular pacing in VDD mode (atrial-tracked ventricular pacing) through the newly implanted leadless pacemaker (Figure 3).

(A) The X-ray with the old dual-chamber transvenous pacemaker and the new leadless pacemaker implanted at the level of the right ventricular apex (blue circle). (B) The 12-lead electrocardiogram with atrial pacing through the transvenous pacemaker (red circle) and the ventricular pacing through the leadless pacemaker in VDD mode (blue circle), resulting in correct sequential atrioventricular pacing. The transvenous pacemaker was programmed to AAIR-DDDR at 60–120 b.p.m., whereas the leadless pacemaker to VDD at 50–130 b.p.m. The programmed vector was 2 + 3 with A3 and A4 thresholds at 3.8 and 2.1 m/s2, respectively and A3 window at 790 ms. Once the transvenous pacemaker threshold normalized, the programming was changed to DDDR at 60–120 b.p.m., whereas the leadless pacemaker was programmed to VVI at 30 b.p.m. as back-up.

The patient was discharged in stable condition. Given the history of early-onset AV conduction disease, myocardial fibrosis on the 2018 cardiac MRI, and presence of localized septal aneurysms on echocardiography, further evaluation was pursued to investigate a possible infiltrative or inflammatory process. Positron emission tomography–computer tomography (PET–CT) demonstrated increased metabolic activity involving the myocardium, lungs, and pulmonary lymph nodes (Figure 4), raising suspicion for systemic sarcoidosis.

Positron emission tomography-computer tomography performed after a carbohydrate-free diet of 48 h and overnight fasting using an fluoro deoxyglucose (FDG) dose of 2.5 MBq/kg with image acquisition performed 60 min after 18F-FDG injection. Left upper pannel: pulmonary nodule with elevated metabolic activity (SUVmax of 6.3 g/mL). Left lower pannel: elevated metabolic activity of pulmonary lymph nodes (SUVmax 10.2 g/mL). Right-sided pannels: elevated metabolic activity at the cardiac level (arrows) with a SUVmax of 4.8 g/mL. These findings are suggestive of systemic sarcoidosis with pulmonary and cardiac involvement.

Bronchoscopy with biopsy of both pulmonary mass and lymph nodes revealed non-caseating granulomas with multinucleated Langhans-type giant cells, confirming the diagnosis of systemic sarcoidosis. Immunosuppressive therapy was initiated by our colleagues from the pulmonology department in February 2025 right after the diagnosis, initially with corticosteroids alone (30 mg of prednisone/day, corresponding to 0.65 mg/kg). Due to a spontaneous lumbar vertebral compression fractures (L1 and L2), mycophenolate (250 mg b.i.d. for 7 days, followed by 500 mg b.i.d.) was introduced by her pulmonologist to allow steroid dose reduction to initially 15 mg/day.

Genetic testing for cardiomyopathies revealed no pathogenic variants, further supporting the diagnosis of CS.

Although the patient consistently claimed to be asymptomatic, over the subsequent months, her functional capacity improved, with a 10% increase in the 6-min walking test. Furthermore, the RV pacing threshold of the transvenous lead gradually improved, eventually normalizing to 1.25 V × 0.5 ms (Figure 5); of note, throughout this period, the pacing impedance remained stable at ∼510–530 Ω, and sensing was absent, i.e. the patient was pacemaker dependent. Because transvenous pacing produced a narrower QRS complex and to prolong battery life of the leadless pacemaker, normal AV sequential pacing (DDDR) via the transvenous pacemaker was restored, and the leadless pacemaker was reprogrammed to VVI mode at 30 b.p.m. as a backup. After 6 months of immunosuppressive therapy, repeat PET–CT (see Supplementary material online, Video S3) showed complete resolution of myocardial metabolic activity and partial regression of pulmonary and lymph node uptake compared with the prior scan, consistent with favourable treatment response. Prednisone was subsequently tapered to 7.5 mg/day by the treating pulmonologist, while mycophenolate was maintained at 500 mg b.i.d. A follow-up PET–CT will be performed ∼6 months after the previous examination to further guide treatment decisions.

Discussion

This case highlights three major challenges in the management of cardiac sarcoidosis (CS): recognizing and diagnosing the disease early, differentiating inflammatory threshold fluctuations from true lead dysfunction, and maintaining AV synchrony when conventional transvenous options are no longer available.

Diagnosing CS remains difficult because of its heterogeneous presentation and the limited sensitivity of endomyocardial biopsy. Conduction abnormalities, ventricular arrhythmias, or otherwise unexplained myocardial fibrosis may precede the diagnosis by years. In the present case, the patient’s early-onset conduction disease and septal fibrosis on cardiac MRI in 2018 suggest that sarcoidosis was likely already present but unrecognized at that time. Both the 2024 European and American Scientific Statements1,2 emphasize that such findings in individuals ≤60 years should prompt early multimodality imaging with cardiac MRI and fluorodeoxyglucose - positron emission tomography (FDG–PET) to evaluate for inflammatory cardiomyopathy, even in the absence of systemic involvement. Early identification is essential, as prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy can prevent disease progression, stabilize conduction, and reduce arrhythmic risk.

A second key challenge illustrated by this case is the interpretation of elevated pacing thresholds. The patient underwent lead extraction and re-implantation in 2020, a procedure that was likely unnecessary in retrospect, as the high thresholds might have been due to active myocardial inflammation rather than lead failure. Granulomatous inflammation can cause oedema, while the chronic phase may lead to fibrotic replacement, both of which can disrupt electrode–myocardial interface and raise capture thresholds.3 If recognized early and treated with immunosuppression, thresholds may normalize. Awareness of this mechanism is essential to avoid unnecessary interventions. In this patient, normalization of thresholds following corticosteroid and mycophenolate therapy supports the concept that threshold instability reflected reversible inflammation rather than lead dysfunction.

The third challenge concerned pacing management in the context of superior vena cava occlusion, which precluded further transvenous lead implantation. A hybrid pacing configuration was successfully implemented, combining atrial pacing through the pre-existing transvenous pacemaker with ventricular pacing in VDD mode using a newly implanted leadless pacemaker. Although not a conventional approach, European guidelines acknowledge the expanding role of leadless pacing in case transvenous access is limited.4 This case demonstrates that a combined transvenous–leadless configuration can provide effective AV synchrony and reliable long-term pacing support when traditional options are unavailable.

Management in this case was consistent with guideline-based therapy. Corticosteroids remain first-line treatment for CS, and steroid-sparing immunosuppressive agents, such as methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate, are recommended to minimize long-term steroid exposure and maintain remission.1,2 Serial FDG–PET imaging, as performed here, is endorsed by both major guidelines to monitor treatment response and guide tapering decisions. The patient’s complete resolution of myocardial uptake and normalization of pacing thresholds after 6 months of therapy confirm effective disease control.

In summary, this case exemplifies the contemporary approach to CS as outlined in recent international consensus statements. It underscores that elevated pacing thresholds may signify active inflammation or fibrosis rather than lead malfunction, and that early suspicion and comprehensive imaging are key to timely diagnosis and treatment. Elevated pacing thresholds might otherwise be misinterpreted as evidence of lead malfunction, potentially leading to unnecessary extraction procedures, which could be avoided with prompt recognition of the underlying disease process.

Furthermore, this case illustrates that hybrid pacing strategies combining transvenous atrial and leadless ventricular systems can be feasible and may provide physiologic solutions in anatomically challenging scenarios, such as absence of venous access. Multidisciplinary, guideline-directed evaluation remains essential for optimizing outcomes in this complex disease.

Conclusion

This case illustrates two important clinical lessons.

First, in patients with CS, elevated pacemaker pacing thresholds may reflect transient myocardial inflammation and fibrosis rather than true lead dysfunction. Recognizing this distinction is crucial to avoid unnecessary lead revision or extraction procedures, particularly when other features suggest an inflammatory substrate. Serial evaluation of thresholds and consideration of inflammatory activity through imaging, such as PET–CT, can help guide appropriate management.

Second, in selected complex cases where conventional transvenous lead re-implantation is not feasible, the combined use of a transvenous pacemaker for atrial pacing and a leadless pacemaker in VDD mode for ventricular pacing can provide effective and reliable AV synchrony. This hybrid approach represents a feasible and safe alternative for maintaining physiological pacing in challenging anatomical or clinical circumstances.
