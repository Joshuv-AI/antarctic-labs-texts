Activity-based proteomics analysis revealed that DNJ directly targeted ATP5F1 to alleviate high glucose induced cardiomyocyte injury
Authors: Xue Li, Yiwei Chen, Huixin Yang, Xianjie Hou, Ruyu Wang, Baorui Cao, Jinxiang Han, Meina Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a significant complication of diabetes mellitus, characterized by impaired cardiac function and mitochondrial injury. The natural alkaloid 1-deoxynojirimycin (DNJ) has exhibited cardioprotective potential; however, its specific molecular targets in cardiomyocytes are yet to be identified.PurposeThis work sought to discover the direct targets of DNJ in cardiomyocytes and elucidate the molecular mechanism behind its cardioprotective activity.MethodsWe first established a cardiomyocyte injury model induced by high glucose. Next, activity-based protein profiling (ABPP) was employed to screen for proteins that directly bind to DNJ. The interactions between DNJ and candidate targets were verified using surface plasmon resonance (SPR), cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), and molecular docking. Finally, the functional outcomes were validated through small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown and plasmid overexpression.ResultsABPP screening identified mitochondrial F0 complex subunit B1 (ATP5F1) as the principal binding target of DNJ. SPR analysis confirmed a high-affinity interaction between DNJ and ATP5F1 (Kd = 52.6 nM). Furthermore, CETSA demonstrated that this binding occurred in living cells. Treatment with DNJ (120 µg/mL) significantly ameliorated high glucose-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Knockdown of ATP5F1 via siRNA attenuated the protective effects of DNJ. Conversely, ATP5F1 overexpression potentiated these protective effects.ConclusionsATP5F1 was identified as a direct functional target of DNJ for the first time. DNJ provides cardioprotection by mitigating mitochondrial malfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis via its direct interaction with ATP5F1. Background

DCM is a severe cardiovascular consequence of diabetes mellitus, marked by progressive structural and functional cardiac abnormalities in the absence of substantial coronary artery disease, ultimately resulting in heart failure [1–3]. Although existing glucose-lowering medications proficiently control systemic hyperglycemia, there is a significant unmet requirement for therapy that specifically addresses and rectifies the inherent metabolic imbalance in cardiomyocytes to avert or reverse the course of DCM.

DNJ, a natural alkaloid extracted from mulberry leaves, is conventionally acknowledged for its α-glucosidase inhibitory properties and glucose-reducing effects [4–6]. Nevertheless, growing evidence suggests that the therapeutic potential of DNJ extends beyond glycemic regulation. DNJ was shown to mitigate early cardiac fibrosis in diabetic animals by regulating protein N-glycosylation [7]. It was demonstrated to target OPA1, facilitating mitochondrial cristae reformation and thereby alleviating cardiac hypertrophy linked to mitochondrial malfunction [8]. Moreover, DNJ markedly enhanced heart function and decreased fibrosis in a mouse model of DCM [9]. A protective function against sepsis-induced cardiac damage was also evidenced, facilitated by the suppression of the JAK2/STAT6 signaling pathway [10]. These studies collectively identify DNJ as a good possibility for DCM therapy. The specific molecular target of DNJ in cardiomyocytes and its exact cardioprotective mechanism are still unclear.

ABPP is an effective instrument ideally suited for this purpose. This method utilizes functionalized small-molecule probes to directly and impartially identify their associated active proteins in physiologically appropriate settings, rendering it especially useful for uncovering the targets of natural products [11–14]. ABPP facilitated the groundbreaking identification that berberine alleviates ulcerative colitis by targeting IRGM1 to block the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [15]. This approach identified AKR7A2 as the direct target of glycyrrhizic acid and clarified its mechanism in combating liver fibrosis [16].

This study revealed that DNJ significantly protected cardiomyocytes from high glucose-induced damage. In addition, we identified the underlying molecular process using ABPP. Overall, our findings provide fresh mechanistic insights into the function of DNJ and indicate prospective therapeutic targets for DCM treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and processing

H9c2 rat cardiomyocytes (item number ZQ0102) were purchased from Zhongqiao Xinzhou Company. Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS), and maintained at 37℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Upon reaching 50% confluence, cells were pretreated with DNJ (120 µg/mL) for 24 h, followed by stimulation with high glucose (75 mM) for an additional 24 h. After treatment, cells were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Cell viability assay

H9c2 cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at a density of approximately 3,500 cells per well. After a 24 h incubation at 37℃ with 5% CO₂, cells were exposed to different amounts of glucose. Each experimental group was evaluated in a minimum of three replicate wells. Subsequent to an additional 24 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (CT0001-A, Sparkjade Biotechnology, China) was introduced to each well and incubated for 1 h under the previously specified conditions. The absorbance at 450 nm was ultimately quantified using a microplate reader (Tristar LB 942, Berthold, Germany).

Protein labeling

H9c2 cells were treated with the activity-based probe DNJ-P or vehicle (DMSO) for two h. Following UV cross-linking (365 nm, 10 min), total cellular proteins were then extracted. Subsequently, the isolated proteins were incubated with a click chemistry reagent mixture comprising TAMRA-azide (20 mM), TCEP (500 mM), TBTA (500 mM), and CuSO4 (500 mM) for 1 h at ambient temperature in the absence of light. Following the click reaction, 5× loading buffer was introduced, and the samples were denatured via incubation at 95℃ for 10 min within a metal solution. Subsequently, the proteins were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, the gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) dye (P1305, Solarbio). In the competitive labeling experiments, cells were pre-incubated for 1 h with or without free DNJ, followed by a 2 h treatment with the DNJ-P probe.

Target identification based on LC-MS/MS

H9c2 cells were allocated into two groups: the probe-only group and the competition group. Following treatment, total cellular proteins were extracted after UV cross-linking (365 nm, 10 min). The proteins were then subjected to a click chemistry reaction by incubating with a mixture containing TAMRA-azide (20 mM), TCEP (500 mM), TBTA (500 mM), and CuSO4 (500 mM) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, biotinylated proteins were enriched using streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. The enriched proteins were digested with trypsin to generate peptides suitable for mass spectrometry analysis. Finally, the resulting peptide samples were analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on a Thermo Scientific instrument.

MDA, GSH and SOD were detected

The levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) in cell lysates were determined using a commercial assay kit (Jiangsu Jiangcheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was assessed with a corresponding assay kit from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Similarly, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was quantified using a kit from the same provider (Beyotime).

Transfection of siRNA

Gene knockdown was achieved by transfecting cells with target-specific siRNAs (designed and manufactured by GenePharma, Suzhou, China) using the INTERFERin® reagent (Polyplus Transfection, France). This transfection process was done on H9c2 cells. Following 24 h of incubation, the transfected cells were submitted to additional studies.

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS

The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed in live cells using the JC-1 fluorescent dye (Beyotime, China). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, a JC-1 working solution was prepared at a 1:1000 dilution and applied to the cells. Cells were then incubated at 37℃ for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescence was detected using specific excitation/emission wavelength pairs: 525/590 nm for JC-1 monomers (green) and 585/590 nm for J-aggregates (red, indicative of high ΔΨm). Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

To detect intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), cells were loaded with the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (10 µmol/L) by incubation at 37℃ for 30 min in the dark. Subsequently, cells were washed three times with serum-free medium. Fluorescence signals were then visualized and quantified using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Staining of mitochondria

Cells were labeled with MitoTracker Red and Hoechst 33,258 to visualize mitochondria and nuclei, respectively. The cells were treated with dyes at 37℃ for 30 min in the dark, then washed three times with serum-free media. A laser scanning confocal microscope was used to get dual-channel fluorescence pictures of mitochondria (red) and nuclei (blue).

Cell thermal shift assay

For the CETSA, H9c2 cells were cultured in 6-well plates, exposed to high glucose (75 mM) for 24 h, and subsequently co-incubated with DNJ (120 µg/mL) for an additional 24 h. Subsequent to treatment, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were rinsed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before harvesting. The collected cells were resuspended in PBS with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails and distributed equally into nine PCR tubes. Each tube underwent distinct heating temperatures (from 37℃ to 61℃ in a gradient) for 3 min, then equilibrated at 25℃ for 3 min. Subsequently, the cells underwent three rounds of freeze-thaw lysis utilizing liquid nitrogen to guarantee full disruption. The lysates underwent centrifugation at 12,000 g for 25 min at 4℃. The resultant supernatant was combined with 5×SDS loading buffer (to achieve a final concentration of 1×) and denatured by heating at 95℃ for 10 min. The materials were ultimately analyzed via Western blot using an antibody targeting ATP5F1.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from H9c2 cells using the SPARKeasy Cell RNA Rapid Extraction Kit, and its concentration was quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the specified cDNA synthesis kit (AG0305, Sparkjade, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using a 2×SYBR Green qPCR mixture, in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Sparkjade, China). Gene expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), which served as the endogenous reference control. Additional file 1: Table S1 contains a list of primer sequences.

Western blotting

H9c2 cells were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE gels, and the separated proteins were transferred to a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane. Membranes were incubated with antibodies against BAX (1:1000), Bcl-2 (1:1000), ATP5F1 (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:4000), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies (1:1000). Antibody binding was visualized using enzyme-linked chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The interaction between DNJ and ATP5F1 was examined by SPR assays utilizing a single-cycle kinetic assay on a Biacore S200. DNJ (3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 nmol/L) was serially diluted on the CM5 series S sensor chip surface at a flow rate of 30 µL/min for 60 s. The protein was dissociated from the chip surface using a 10 mM glycine hydrochloride solution (pH 2.0) for 600 s. Binding and dissociation constants were derived by globally fitting the data to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model utilizing Biacore Insight assessment software (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA).

Molecular docking

The Swiss-Model program was utilized to generate the three-dimensional protein structure of Atp5pb, employing human ATP synthase (PDB ID: 8H9U) as a template. The 2D chemical structure of DNJ was created using the JSME chemical editor and subsequently transformed into a 3D structure, which underwent energy optimization with the MMFF94 force field. The GHECOM program predicted possible binding pockets on the protein surface, and semi-flexible molecular docking was conducted using the DOCK 6.9 program to generate 10,000 molecular conformations. Cluster analysis was conducted on various conformations, and ultimately, a scoring function was chosen to assess the best binding conformation as the final outcome.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.5 was used to perform statistical analyses. The measurement data was reported as mean ± standard deviation. First, the normal distribution and homogeneity of variance tests were performed on the data from each group. The variance homogeneity was assessed using the Levene test. A nonparametric test was conducted. P-values < 0.05 or < 0.01 indicated statistical significance.

Results

ABPP proteomic analysis confirmed that ATP5F1 was the target protein of DNJ

To establish a cell model of DCM, we initially exposed the cells to a gradient glucose concentration (25–150 mmol/L). As demonstrated in Fig. 1A, at a glucose concentration of 50 mmol/L, cell viability reduced dramatically as compared to the control group and decreased dose-dependently as concentration climbed. Among these, 75 mmol/L glucose may cause stable and reproducible damage in cells; hence, it was chosen as the concentration for further modeling. In the high-glucose environment, we tested the effect of DNJ concentrations (0–480 µg/mL) on cell viability (Fig. 1B). As the concentration of DNJ increased, the cell viability of cells with severe glucose damage gradually improved. The recovery impact was most significant at 120–240 µg/mL and reduced at 480 µg/mL. To rule out DNJ cytotoxicity, we treated cells with DNJ alone in normal glucose conditions (Fig. 1C). Under normal glucose conditions, DNJ at 120 µg/mL did not significantly affect cell viability. However, DNJ administration at 480 µg/mL decreased cell viability. In the high glucose situation, 120 µg/mL of DNJ significantly increased the cell viability, while 480 µg/mL showed a lesser effect. In summary, DNJ possesses excellent safety and protective features at low doses, but at higher doses, it might induce cytotoxicity. 120 µg/mL was determined as the working concentration for further tests.

ABPP was utilized to clarify the direct targets of DNJ. Utilizing the parent core structure of DNJ, we engineered and synthesized a functionalized probe (DNJ-probe) with a terminal alkynol moiety for biotin conjugation via click chemistry, along with a photoresponsive diindanyl group for covalent cross-linking upon UV exposure (Fig. 1E). Significantly, the DNJ-probe demonstrated biological activity akin to DNJ, affirming that structural alterations did not influence its pharmacological efficacy (Fig. 1D).

The ABPP experimental technique is shown in Fig. 1F. H9c2 cells cultivated with the DNJ probe under increased glucose conditions were subjected to UV light to aid in the probe’s covalent cross-linking with the interacting protein. Following cell lysis, biotin tags were introduced into probe-labeled protein complexes by click chemistry, followed by efficient enrichment with streptavidin magnetic beads. Following vigorous washing to remove non-specific binding, the resultant target protein complexes were trypsin digested before being identified and quantified using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of the DNJ probe increased with concentration. At 800 µM concentration, the protein band was visible with adequate fluorescence intensity. As a result, this was chosen as the best working concentration for the DNJ probe (Fig. 1G). In a competing experiment, the probe’s labeling specificity was verified by treating cells with increasing doses of free DNJ, which reduced the probe’s labeling signal in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1H).

A comparative proteome analysis of the prob-labeled and free DNJ competition groups identified a number of candidate proteins with preferential binding. Among the choices, ATP synthase F0 complex subunit B1 (ATP5F1) stood out for its highest enrichment fold and significant competitive inhibitory impact (Fig. 1I), prompting its selection as the key target for further functional confirmation. The original protein MS list can be found in Additional file 2.

DNJ directly targets and regulates ATP5F1 in H9c2 cardiomyocytes

To ascertain the direct interaction of DNJ with ATP5F1 within a physiologically relevant cellular environment, we initiated a CETSA. Compared with cardiomyocytes in the control group, cardiomyocytes in the DNJ-treated group presented a much higher melting temperature (Fig. 2A-B). This directly proves that DNJ binds to ATP5F1 and keeps its shape stable in living cells. Subsequently, the in vitro binding affinity of DNJ to the recombinant ATP5F1 protein was determined using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) technology. The results showed that there was a direct interaction between DNJ and ATP5F1, with an equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) value of 52.6 nM, indicating a high binding affinity between the two (Fig. 2C). Molecular docking simulations further revealed that DNJ could form hydrogen bonds with key amino acid residues such as Leu88, Glu91, and Ile95 on the ATP5F1 protein, thereby stably binding to a hydrophobic pocket of ATP5F1. The calculated binding free energy was − 32.49 kcal/mol (Fig. 2D). Finally, in a high glucose-induced damage H9c2 cell model, the effects of different concentrations of DNJ on the expression level of the ATP5F1 protein were detected. The results showed that high glucose treatment could significantly reduce the protein level of ATP5F1 in the cells, while DNJ treatment could reverse this trend. Among them, the improvement effect at a concentration of 120 µg/mL was the most significant (Fig. 2E-F).

Knockdown of ATP5F1 can inhibit DNJ-mediated improvement of mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes

To investigate the impact of DNJ on high glucose-induced damage in H9c2 cardiomyocytes, we created four siRNA sequences targeting ATP5F1 (si-ATP5F1-1 ~ 4) and tested the most effective knockdown efficiency sequence after transfection into H9c2 cardiomyocytes. The results in Fig. 3A-C reveal that si-ATP5F1-4 had the most significant knockdown impact at both the mRNA and protein levels. As a result, the ATP5F1 knockdown sequence employed in future experiments was si-ATP5F1-4, with the NC siRNA transfection group serving as a negative control. The CCK-8 results showed that, compared to the DNJ treatment group, knockdown of ATP5F1 decreased DNJ’s boosting effect on cell survival, and cell viability was similar to that of the high glucose group.

Subsequent mitochondrial function and structure studies revealed that DNJ administration counteracted the high glucose-induced drop in mitochondrial membrane potential, which was abolished by ATP5F1 suppression (Fig. 3F-G). Meanwhile, DNJ’s beneficial effect on the decrease in intracellular ATP level under high glucose circumstances was likewise dependent on ATP5F1 expression (Fig. 3E). Morphological investigation using MitoTracker Red staining demonstrated that DNJ maintained the reticular mitochondrial network in high-glucose circumstances. In contrast, mitochondria in ATP5F1 knockdown cells showed fragmentation, which was not alleviated by DNJ (Fig. 3H). Transmission electron microscopy studies revealed that DNJ preserved the structure of mitochondrial cristae while decreasing their expansion, which was not detected in ATP5F1-knockdown cells (Fig. 3I).

Knockdown of ATP5F1 can inhibit DNJ-mediated improvement of oxidative stress and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes

Mitochondrial dysfunction is an important trigger of oxidative stress in cells. To further clarify whether the antioxidant effects of DNJ were dependent on ATP5F1, we examined the redox state of cells. The DCFH-DA staining results showed that high glucose stimulation led to a significant accumulation of reactive oxygen species in the cells. DNJ intervention could reduce the level of reactive oxygen species, and knocking down ATP5F1 could reverse this protective effect (Fig. 4, A and C). Mitochondria-specific SOX staining was used to detect mitochondrial-derived reactive oxygen species, and the changes were consistent with those of the total reactive oxygen species detection (Fig. 4, B and D). Then, we detected the key markers of oxidative stress using biochemical kits and found that high glucose treatment led to an increase in MDA levels in the cells and a decrease in SOD and GSH activities. The same improvement was obtained after DNJ treatment, but after knocking down ATP5F1, the antioxidant effect of DNJ was inhibited (Fig. 4, E-G).

Persistent oxidative stress is a critical indication for apoptosis. Based on this, we looked into whether DNJ inhibited high glucose-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis via ATP5F1. Initially, quantitative analysis was performed using Annexin V/PI double-staining flow cytometry. The results showed that the total apoptosis rate of cells in the DNJ group was lower than in the high glucose group; however, knocking down ATP5F1 appeared to decrease DNJ’s anti-apoptotic action (Fig. 4H). TUNEL staining validated this observation; DNJ reduced DNA break-positive cells caused by high glucose levels, whereas ATP5F1 knockdown cells did not (Fig. 4, I-J). Key regulatory proteins in the apoptotic cascade were examined via Western blotting to clarify the molecular process. The findings indicated that in a high-sugar environment, the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 diminished, whereas the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and activated Caspase-3 escalated, along with an increased release of mitochondrial Cyto-c into the cytoplasm. DNJ was able to reverse these alterations in protein expression; however, in cells with ATP5F1 knockdown, DNJ did not induce significant changes in the levels of these proteins (Fig. 4, K-O). Overall, these data suggest that ATP5F1 is essential for DNJ to exert its protective effects against high glucose-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes.

Overexpression of ATP5F1 can improve mitochondrial dysfunction in H9c2 cardiomyocytes

To verify the functional importance of ATP5F1 in protecting the DNJ heart, we constructed an H9c2 cardiomyocyte model overexpressing ATP5F1 (Fig. 5A-C). Initially, cell viability was assessed in elevated glucose circumstances. Figure 5D illustrates that, in contrast to the control group with an empty vector, the exclusive overexpression of ATP5F1 markedly mitigates the reduction in cell viability induced by elevated glucose levels, indicating that augmenting ATP5F1 function confers a protective effect. Significantly, the combination of ATP5F1 overexpression with DNJ restored cell viability to its highest level, indicating a synergistic protective effect.

The MitoTracker Red staining structure demonstrates that ATP5F1 overexpression substantially improves mitochondrial morphological damage caused by high glucose levels and restores mitochondrial network structure (Fig. 5G). The mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1 staining, and it was discovered that overexpressing ATP5F1 effectively restored the membrane potential drop produced by high glucose, as well as having a synergistic impact with DNJ (Fig. 5, E-F). Furthermore, overexpression of ATP5F1 can counteract the decrease in intracellular ATP levels produced by high glucose, which is consistent with ATP5F1’s role as an ATP synthase subunit (Fig. 5H).

Overexpression of ATP5F1 can improve oxidative stress and apoptosis of H9c2 cardiomyocytes

Considering that mitochondrial failure frequently precedes oxidative stress and apoptosis, we further assessed the impact of increased ATP5F1 expression on these two ultimate pathogenic pathways.

In high-sugar environments, overexpression of ATP5F1 can reduce the accumulation of total and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, lower MDA levels, and increase SOD and GSH concentrations. This shows that it can successfully treat oxidative stress diseases induced by high sugar levels. The aforementioned improved benefits are similar to those of the DNJ treatment group, implying that ATP5F1 overexpression has a comparable antioxidant impact (Fig. 6A-G).

Based on the effective alleviation of oxidative stress, we further analyzed the final event of cell apoptosis. The results showed that overexpression of ATP5F1 could significantly reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis induced by high glucose. The apoptotic rate by flow cytometry and the number of TUNEL-positive cells both decreased significantly, and the anti-apoptotic effect was not significantly different from the DNJ group (Fig. 6H-J).

The results of the apoptosis pathway protein detection showed that overexpression of ATP5F1 exhibited a significant anti-apoptotic effect under high glucose conditions. The Western blot results (Fig. 6K) indicated that compared with the control group transfected with the OE-NC, overexpression of ATP5F1 significantly upregulated the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 while downregulating the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3. Additionally, by separating the cytoplasm and mitochondrial components, we examined the distribution of Cyto-c (Fig. 6L). The results showed that overexpression of ATP5F1 effectively inhibited the release of Cyto-c from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm.

Collectively, our tests with modest interference and overexpression demonstrate that ATP5F1 is both necessary and sufficient to facilitate the protective effects of DNJ, thereby identifying it as a crucial target for the coordinated enhancement of mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and cell death.

Discussion

DCM, a significant cardiovascular complication associated with diabetes, is distinguished by disruptions in myocardial cell metabolism, progressive fibrosis, and a decline in cardiac function, potentially culminating in heart failure [17, 18]. Present clinical management predominantly focuses on the regulation of blood glucose and systemic metabolic parameters [19, 20]; nevertheless, targeted therapies aimed at cardiomyocytes to rectify intrinsic injury are still scarce. Consequently, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying DCM and developing targeted interventions holds considerable clinical importance.

DNJ, the primary active compound present in mulberry leaves, has historically been employed in hypoglycemic treatment owing to its α-glucosidase inhibitory properties [21–23]. Recent research indicates that DNJ also exerts a direct cardioprotective effect independent of its hypoglycemic action [8]. To systematically elucidate its molecular mechanism, we designed and synthesized a novel DNJ probe and employed ABPP in conjunction with LC-MS/MS and proteomics techniques [24] to identify the mitochondrial ATP synthase F0 subunit (ATP5F1) as the binding target of DNJ. Through SPR and CETSA, we verified the direct interaction of DNJ with ATP5F1, demonstrating high affinity and stability both in vitro and within cellular environments. Molecular docking further elucidated the fundamental structural mechanism of their interaction.

ATP5F1 is a fundamental subunit of the F0 complex of mitochondrial ATP synthase [25, 26] and plays a direct role in preserving the functional coupling between the F1 catalytic head and the F0 proton channel, thereby facilitating the efficient conversion of the proton motive force into ATP [27, 28]. In cardiomyocytes, which rely extensively on oxidative phosphorylation for energy production, the functional integrity of ATP5F1 is essential for sustaining ATP levels, mitochondrial membrane potential, and overall energy homeostasis [29, 30]. Early studies [31] demonstrated that a defect in cardiolipin synthesis can disrupt its interaction with ATP5F1 in the diabetic condition, thereby impairing ATP synthase activity and resulting in disturbances of myocardial energy metabolism.

In a high glucose environment, myocardial cells will have mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress imbalance, and decreased antioxidant capacity, which will eventually lead to myocardial cell damage and apoptosis [32]. Mitochondria are the center of energy metabolism in cardiomyocytes, and the integrity of their morphological structure is the basis for maintaining their functions [33]. Transmission electron microscopy showed that the mitochondrial morphology of normal H9c2 cardiomyocytes was regular, and the cristae structure was clear. After high glucose intervention, mitochondria were generally swollen, and cristae structure was fractured and sparse. DNJ pretreatment could effectively reverse the above damage. MitoTracker fluorescent staining further confirmed that high glucose caused fragmentation of the mitochondrial network, consistent with the report by Xie et al. [34], while DNJ restored its filamentous network morphology. After ATP5F1 knockdown, the protective effect of DNJ was significantly weakened. Overexpression of ATP5F1 could effectively reverse the mitochondrial damage induced by high glucose. These results suggest that DNJ may maintain mitochondrial structural integrity by targeting ATP5F1. The decrease of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) is an early marker of mitochondrial dysfunction [35]. In this study, it was found that high glucose treatment led to a significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential and blocked ATP synthesis, which was consistent with the results reported previously [36]. DNJ intervention could effectively restore membrane potential and ATP level, and the protective effect of DNJ was significantly inhibited after ATP5F1 knockdown. However, overexpression of ATP5F1 increased membrane potential and ATP level. DNJ may maintain the energy metabolism homeostasis of cardiomyocytes by targeting ATP5F1.

In a high glucose environment, mitochondrial dysfunction can induce excessive generation of ROS and oxidative stress damage [37]. In this study, high glucose treatment led to increased ROS and MDA levels and decreased SOD activity and GSH content, while DNJ intervention could reverse the above changes, which was consistent with previous reports [38, 39]. Knockdown of ATP5F1 attenuated the antioxidant effect of DNJ, while overexpression of ATP5F1 alleviated the oxidative stress injury. Mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive activation of oxidative stress often lead to apoptosis [40]. The present study found that high glucose treatment led to an increase in the ratio of Bax/Bcl-2, up-regulation of cleaved caspase-3 expression, release of cytochrome c from mitochondria to the cytoplasm, and a significant increase in cell apoptosis rate, which was consistent with the apoptotic characteristics observed in previous diabetic cardiomyopathy models and cardiomyocytes treated with high glucose [41, 42]. DNJ intervention could effectively reverse the above changes. When ATP5F1 was knocked down, the anti-apoptotic effect of DNJ was significantly weakened. However, ATP5F1 overexpression mimicked the partial effects of DNJ, restored the Bax/Bcl-2 balance, reduced cleaved caspase-3 expression, inhibited cytochrome c release, and reduced apoptosis. These results suggest that DNJ may improve the mitochondrial dysfunction induced by high glucose, inhibit oxidative stress, enhance antioxidant capacity, and ultimately reduce apoptosis in H9c2 cardiomyocytes by targeting ATP5F1.

Overall, our data show that DNJ targets ATP5F1 and hence influences downstream mitochondrial energy metabolism, ROS homeostasis, and apoptotic signaling pathways (Fig. 7). As a result, this study not only identifies a novel molecular mechanism of DNJ in the treatment of DCM, but it also lays the groundwork for the development of new therapeutic alternatives for metabolic cardiomyopathy by targeting ATP5F1. However, the research still has several limitations: (1) This study was primarily conducted in the H9c2 cell line; while the model is reliable, it must be validated in primary cardiomyocytes. (2) The binding sites predicted by molecular docking must be confirmed via site-directed mutagenesis experiments. (3) This study relied heavily on in vitro experiments. In the future, an animal model of diabetic cardiomyopathy (such as STZ-induced diabetic mice) will be developed, and the in vivo efficacy of DNJ and its impact on cardiac function will be systematically evaluated, with the target-dependent effect confirmed using ATP5F1 cardiomyocyte-specific knockout mice.

Mechanistic model of the cardioprotective effects of DNJ by directly targeting ATP5F1. Under high glucose stress, cardiomyocytes undergo injury characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in loss of membrane potential, reduced ATP production, and secondary oxidative stress. This REDOX imbalance further triggers the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. This study identified ATP5F1, the core subunit of ATP synthase, as a direct functional target of DNJ. Binding of DNJ to ATP5F1 alleviates the entire pathological cascade: it protects mitochondrial integrity and, reduces ROS production, and inhibits activation of apoptosis. Small interference and overexpression experiments together established ATP5F1 as a central mediator of the protective effect of DNJ against diabetic cardiomyopathy

Conclusions

This investigation substantiated that DNJ demonstrably mitigated cardiomyocyte injury induced by elevated glucose levels, as evidenced by the amelioration of mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Through targeting ATP5F1, the DNJ system modulates downstream mitochondrial energy metabolism and cellular survival signaling cascades, thereby eliciting its multifaceted myocardial protective actions. This discovery not only offers a direct rationale for elucidating the molecular mechanism of DNJ in the treatment of DCM but also establishes a crucial premise for the development of a novel therapeutic strategy for metabolic cardiomyopathy, specifically targeting ATP5F1.
