ZNF33A activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promotes BCAA catabolism in colorectal cancer
Authors: Weihua Yu, Jionghuang Chen, Xiaofan Pu, Hongdan Shen, Liping Cao, Linghua Zhu, Haiping Pei, Xihua Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAberrant activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a hallmark of colorectal cancer (CRC), but the upstream regulators and associated metabolic consequences remain incompletely defined. Branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism has been implicated in CRC progression, yet its regulation and functional role remain controversial. This study investigates the role of the transcription factor ZNF33A in CRC pathogenesis.MethodsZNF33A expression was examined in CRC tissues, cell lines, and public databases. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments were conducted in vitro (CCK8, EdU, soft agar, reporter assays, Co-IP, ChIP, BCAA assays) and in vivo (Lgr5-GFP mouse models and AOM/DSS-induced CRC). Statistical analyses included Student’s t-test, ANOVA, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.ResultsZNF33A was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues and correlated with poor prognosis. Overexpression of ZNF33A promoted proliferation, colony formation, and BCAA consumption, while its down-regulation suppressed tumor growth, stemness, and organoid formation. Mechanistically, ZNF33A bound β-catenin, enhanced β-catenin/TCF transcriptional activity, and induced expression of BCAT1, a key BCAA-catabolizing enzyme. Suppression of β-catenin or BCAT1 abrogated ZNF33A-driven oncogenic effects.ConclusionsZNF33A drives CRC progression by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and reprogramming BCAA metabolism via BCAT1. These findings identify ZNF33A as a potential therapeutic target and highlight BCAA metabolic modulation as a promising strategy for CRC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02773-8.

Background

Metabolic reprogramming is an important feature of colorectal cancer (CRC) [1]. Changing the metabolism of BCAAs (valine, leucine and isoleucine) significantly affects the malignant progression of tumors [2]. The intake of BCAAs as part of daily diet is positively correlated with all-cause mortality among CRC patients [3, 4]. However, BCAAs have also been shown to have an inhibitory effect on tumor progression in the early stages of CRC [5, 6]. Therefore, the functions of BCAAs in CRC need to be further elucidated.

Enzymes involved in the metabolism of BCAAs have also received extensive attention. At present, the most deeply studied enzymes include BCAT1/2, BCKA, and BCKDK [7–10]. BCATs are responsible for the transfer of amino groups in BCAAs to α-KG, thus generating BCKA and glutamate [7, 8]. Subsequently, the BCKDH complex catalyzes the oxidation of BCKA, which is a rate-limiting step in BCAA catabolism [10]. These metabolic enzymes are strictly regulated at multiple levels, such as transcription and protein stability [11]. The BCAT1 gene is methylated in the blood DNA of CRC patients and has the potential to serve as a diagnostic marker for CRC [12]. However, the functions of BCAT1 in the progression of CRC are unknown.

The core of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is β-catenin [13, 14]. At rest, the cytoplasmic destruction complex (composed of APC, Axin, GSK3β and CK1α) is responsible for the phosphorylation of serine and threonine residues at the N-terminus of β-catenin and its degradation [13, 14]. When the Wnt ligand binds to the receptor Frizzle on the cell membrane and the coreceptor LRP5/6, the C-terminus of Frizzle recruits DVL, which in turn recruits Axin, resulting in the disintegration of the destruction complex [13]. As a result, β-catenin accumulates in the cytoplasm and is then transferred to the nucleus, forming a transcription complex with TCF4 and binding to the sequences of TCF binding elements (TBEs) in the promoter regions of target genes (Cyclin D1, c-Myc, etc.) to regulate its transcription [13, 14]. Due to the inactivation and mutation of APC, as well as the active mutation of β-catenin, the signal is constitutively activated, which in turn promotes the growth and metastasis of CRC cells [15].

Multiple studies have confirmed the regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling on cancer metabolism. For example, in pancreatic cancer cells, Wnt/β-catenin signaling activates the mevalonate pathway, promoting Kras activation and pancreatic cancer progression [16]. In the progression of liver cancer, Wnt/β-catenin signaling activates one-carbon metabolism and promotes the malignant progression of liver cancer [17]. These previous studies have shown that targeting the downstream metabolic pathway of Wnt/β-catenin signaling is important for the treatment of tumors.

To reveal the mechanism of the pathogenesis of CRC and explore new therapeutic targets, the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database was used to screen genes significantly associated with patient survival in CRC, one of which is the zinc finger protein ZNF33A. ZNF33A is a transcription factor localized in the nucleus and endoplasmic reticulum (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000189180-ZNF33A). To date, ZNF33A is only known to promote tumor progression and BET inhibitor resistance in triple-negative breast cancer [18]. This study aims to elucidate the expression, function, and underlying molecular mechanism of ZNF33A in CRC.

Methods

Cell culture

Normal colorectal epithelial cells (NCM460), CRC cells (HT29, HCT116, HCT8, and SW480), and 293T cells were obtained from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The cells were cultured in a constant temperature incubator (5% CO2, 37 °C). Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 8000 according to the instructions. Wnt3a conditioned medium was prepared by L-Wnt3a cell culture according to the method described by ATCC.

Clinical samples

CRC tissues and paired adjacent tissues were taken from Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. All tissue samples were obtained after patients signed informed consent forms. The relevant experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2024-Y-1011).

qPCR

RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen), after which 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Kit (Takara) according to the instructions. The SYBR Green Kit and the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Richmond, CA, USA) were used for qPCR. With actin as an internal reference, the expression of the target gene was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The sequences of primers used in the experiment are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry

The tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and placed in EDTA solution for antigen recovery at 100℃ for 30 min. After naturally cooling to room temperature, endogenous peroxidase was blocked with an endogenous peroxidase blocker for 15 min, and the sections were washed twice with PBS. The tissue sections were incubated with anti-ZNF33A antibody (Sigma, HPA056638, 1:100), anti-SOX9 antibody (Sigma, HPA001758, 1:100), and anti-GFP antibody (Abcam, ab290, 1:100) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the tissue sections were washed twice with PBS and incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. The signal was developed with 3,3,0-diaminobenzidine (DAB). All tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin for nuclear staining. The staining intensity and protein expression level were automatically scored using the vectra2 system.

ZNF33A overexpression or ZNF33A knockdown

The CDS of ZNF33A was inserted into the pLVX-IRES-puro vector. The shRNA targeting ZNF33A was designed on the sigma website and inserted into the pLKO.1-puro vector. The sequence of shRNA is listed in Supplementary Table 1. The lentivirus was packaged with 293T cells. The packaging vectors were psPAX2 and pMD2.G. The collected virus solution was concentrated by PEG8000 and centrifuged at 1600 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was dissolved in 2 ml of DMEM. The cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 50%-60%. The next day, the cells were incubated with 400 µl of lentivirus in a constant temperature incubator overnight. Two days after lentivirus infection, the cells were then cultured with puromycin (1 µg/mL) for 4 days. The stable cell line was screened, and the expression of ZNF33A was detected by Western blot.

CCK8 assay

The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 103 cells/well before being cultured in a constant temperature incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). The next day, the old culture medium was replaced with culture medium supplemented with 10% CCK8 and incubated in the incubator for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured every day after the start of the experiment.

EdU assay

The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and detected using the Cell-Light EdU Apollo567 In Vitro Kit (Reebok, C10310-1), after which they were photographed and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope.

Soft agar colony formation assay

When the confluence reached 60–70%, the cells were digested, and a cell suspension was prepared. The lower layer gel (20% FBS, 40% 2 × RPMI 1640 (Basal Medium Eagle), and 40% 1.25% agar) was prepared and added to a 24-well plate with 400 µL/well. The plate was placed in an incubator at 37 °C until the gel solidified. The upper layer gel (25% FBS, 37.5% 2 × RPMI 1640, 37.5% 1% agar, and 0.8% 2 mM L-glutamine) was prepared and mixed thoroughly with the cell suspension. After mixing, 400 µl (containing 1 × 103 cells) was added to each well, and the plate was placed in a constant temperature incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) for 10–14 days. Five visual fields were randomly selected under the microscope for clone counting.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

When detecting the interaction between exogenous proteins, 48 h after transfection, the cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer (50 mM Tris HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, and protease and phosphatase inhibitors), after which the supernatant was collected and incubated with Flag-beads (Sigma, A2220) or HA-heads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88836) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the beads were washed three times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40) and heated with 1 × loading buffer at 100 °C for 5 min, after which the supernatant was collected for Western blotting.

To detect whether there is any interaction between endogenously expressed proteins in CRC cells, the cells were treated and lysed with IP lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Aliquots of proteins were added to the corresponding primary antibodies and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, 40 µL of Protein A/G beads (Bimake, B23202) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with wash buffer, after which 1 × Loading Buffer was added for Western blot.

GST pull-down

Cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, protease and phosphatase inhibitors). The supernatant was collected and incubated with 10 µg of GST-ZNF33A fusion protein overnight at 4 °C. The next day, Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare) were washed three times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 1% NP-40) and incubated in cell lysis buffer for 4 h. The beads were then washed three more times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 1% NP-40), added to 1 × loading buffer, and heated at 100 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was then collected for Western blotting.

HE staining

In turn, the sections were put into xylene and gradient ethanol for deparaffinization and hydration. Then, the sections were stained in hematoxylin staining solution for 3 min. After washing with tap water and differentiation with 1% hydrochloric acid in alcohol for a few seconds, they were rinsed with tap water and returned to a blue color with 0.6% ammonia water. Finally, the sections were stained in eosin staining solution for 1–3 min. The sections were dried, dehydrated, mounted, and photographed under a microscope.

Genetic mouse model

The mouse Zfp68 gene shares high homology with human ZNF33A. We knocked out the Zfp68 gene in mice to study its function in vivo. CRISPR/Cas9 technology was used to modify the Zfp68 gene. The brief process is as follows: the CRISPR/Cas9 system and donor were microinjected into fertilized eggs of C57BL/6 mice. The fertilized eggs were then transplanted to obtain positive F0 mice. A stable F1 generation mouse model was obtained by mating positive F0 generation mice with C57BL/6 mice. Villin-Cre mice and Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Zfp68f/f mice were bred with Villin-Cre mice to knock out the homolog of human ZNF33A in the intestinal epithelium. Heterozygous Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 mice harbor an Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 “knock-in” allele that abolishes Lgr5 gene function and expresses EGFP. These mice were valuable for marking Lgr5-expressing stem cells in the small intestine and for lineage tracing purposes. All mice were bred in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at a constant temperature of 25 °C and were provided with adequate water and food.

Whole body irradiation (WBI)

Mice (12 weeks old) were anesthetized, placed into a ventilated Plexiglas pie container and underwent 12 Gy whole-body irradiation (WBI) using a Shephard 137Cs-ray irradiator at a dose rate of 4 Gy/min, following the biosafety guidelines of the Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The mice were killed 3 days after WBI, and the small intestine was removed for histological analyses.

Organoids

The mice were sacrificed. The proximal half of the small intestine was isolated, longitudinally cut, and washed with ice-cold PBS until the contents were all clean. The small intestinal mucosa was rinsed 10 times with cold PBS. The small intestine was cut into small pieces of 2–4 mm with scissors, and the pieces were placed in a 50-ml tube. The small pieces were washed with 30 ml of ice-cold PBS. After they precipitated to the bottom of the tube, the precipitates were retained. This step was repeated 5–10 times until the supernatant became clear. Then, 30 ml of cold crypt isolation buffer (EDTA, 2 mM) was added for incubation at 4 °C for 30 min. The collected debris was precipitated, and the supernatant was discarded. After that, 20 ml of ice-cold PBS was added and pipetted up and down. Then, we determined whether there was a crypt structure under an inverted microscope. The above steps were repeated until most of the crypts entered the supernatant. Filtration was performed with a 70-µm cell strainer, and the crypt structure was collected with a 50-ml tube incubated with BSA. The resulting crypt supernatant was then centrifuged (at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C). The pellets were resuspended in 10 ml of ice minimal medium and transferred into a 15-ml centrifuge tube. The centrifuge tube was centrifuged at 200 × g for 2 min. The pellets were resuspended in Matrigel and transferred to a 24-well plate at 37 °C. After being left to stand for 15 min, feeding medium (1×N2 supplement, 1×B27 supplement, 1 mM N-acetylcysteine, 50 ng/mL EGF, 100 ng/mL Noggin, and 1 µg/mL R-spondin) was added.

Model of AOM/DSS-induced intestinal carcinoma

Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water during the course of the experiments. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 12 mg/kg body weight AOM dissolved in physiological saline. Five days later, 2% DSS was given in the drinking water over 5 days, followed by 16 days of regular water. This cycle was repeated twice. Body weight was measured every 3 days, and the animals were sacrificed at the indicated time intervals for histological analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the Committee on Animal Experimentation of the Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, and performed in compliance with the university’s Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

TopFlash reporter gene

Cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 50% and then co-transfected with 0.1 µg of expression vector, 0.05 µg of reporter plasmid and 0.02 µg of Renilla luciferase. Twenty-four hours after transfection, the cells were treated with Wnt3a conditioned medium for 8 h, and the activity of the reporter gene was detected by a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Beyotime, RG088M). The experiment was performed three times.

ChIP

Cells were seeded in a 10-cm dish. When the confluence reached 90%, the cells were cross-linked with 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and incubated with 125 mM glycine at room temperature for 3 min. The cells were washed twice with precooled PBS. The cells were collected with 2 ml of DTT (100 mM pH 9.5 Tris-HCl, and 10 mM DTT) solution and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were resuspended in 150 µL of SDS (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS) lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The DNA fragments were spliced into segments with an average length of approximately 500 bp using 12% power ultrasound and were then detected using the ChIP-IT Expressshearing Kit (Active motif, 53008). The binding of β-catenin to the promoter of BCAT1 was analyzed by qPCR.

Measurement of BCAAs

The branched chain amino acid assay kit was purchased from Abcam (ab83374), and experiments were performed according to the instructions. In brief, cells (4 × 106) were homogenized with 200 µL assay buffer. After this, they were put through a centrifuge at 14,000 rpm for 15 min to remove cell debris and other insoluble materials. The cell lysis buffer was mixed with assay buffer, enzyme mix and WST substrate mix (background control without enzyme mix). The reaction mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature and protected from direct light. The OD was then read at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the background reading was subtracted from the sample readings. Finally, the BCAA content was calculated according to the standard curve.

Analysis of the reporter activity

During the construction of the promoter and mutant of BCAT1, the BCAT1 gene transcription start position (at -1 bp) upstream 1000 bp was cloned and inserted into the pGL3-Basic vector. Potential β-catenin binding sites (TBE1 and TBE2) were mutated by PCR. When detecting the activity of the BCAT1 promoter, the cells were seeded in a 24-well plate. After 24 h, 0.1 µg of promoter or mutant BCAT1, together with 0.05 µg of TK Renilla and 0.25 µg of β-catenin, was transfected into each well using Lipo8000. After 48 h, the reporter gene activity was detected by the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Beyotime, RG088M), and the experiment was performed three times.

WB

Cells were washed twice with PBS and lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation, the supernatant of the cell lysate was collected, and the protein concentration was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were subjected to SDS‒PAGE, and the separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies, including β-catenin (Proteintech, 51067-2-AP, 1:1000), myc-tag (Proteintech, 16286-1-AP, 1:1000), BCAT1 (Proteintech, 13640-1-AP, 1:1000), BCAT2 (Proteintech, 16417-1-AP, 1:1000), tubulin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-5286, 1:4000), ZNF33A (Abbexa, abx030283, 1:1000), Flag (Proteintech, 66008-4-lG, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-lG, 1:1000), HA (Proteintech, 81290-1-RR, 1:1000), and GST (Proteintech, 10000-0-AP, 1:5000). After that, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1–2 h. The immunosignals were detected using a chemiluminescent substrate (Millipore, WBKLS0050) and analyzed using Image Lab software.

Tumorigenesis assay

Nude mice at 6 weeks of age were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment with 7 mice in each group. Cells were inoculated on one flank of the nude mice, with 10⁷ cells per inoculation site. Tumor size was measured and calculated every 4 days. The mice were sacrificed on day 48 after the experiment began, and the tumors were collected and weighed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS 23.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA). Categorical variables by two-tailed χ² test, and group means by two-tailed Student’s t-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The expression of ZNF33A was upregulated in CRC

The expression pattern of ZNF33A in human tissues was first investigated by using the HPA (human protein atlas) database. Analyzing the public database revealed the extensive expression of ZNF33A in human tissues (Fig. 1A). Then, the mRNA levels of ZNF33A in 31 CRC tissues and paired adjacent tissues were detected. The results showed upregulation of ZNF33A mRNA in CRC (Fig. 1B), which was consistent with the observations obtained from immunohistochemical (IHC) staining (Fig. 1C). Also, the levels of ZNF33A protein were elevated in the clinical CRC tissues (Fig. 1D). In cell lines, ZNF33A was highly expressed in CRC cells (HT29, HCT116, HCT8, and SW480) when compared with normal colorectal epithelial NCM460 cells (Fig. 1E). Consistently, in the GEPIA database, high expression of ZNF33A was significantly associated with poor prognosis of CRC (Fig. 1F). These observations prompted us to study roles of ZNF33A in the progression of CRC.

ZNF33A promotes the proliferation, growth and colony formation of CRC cells

To investigate the functions of ZNF33A in CRC, ZNF33A was first overexpressed in HCT8 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 2A), and then, the effect of ZNF33A overexpression on the growth of HCT8 and HCT116 cells was detected by CCK8 assay. The results showed that overexpression of ZNF33A promoted the growth of CRC cells in liquid medium (Fig. 2B). Cell growth is determined by both proliferation and apoptosis. Then, the effect of ZNF33A overexpression on the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells was examined by EdU assay. It was found that after overexpression of ZNF33A, more EdU was incorporated into the DNA of the CRC cells, suggesting that overexpression of ZNF33A promoted the proliferation of CRC cells (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, overexpression of ZNF33A promoted the anchorage-independent growth of CRC cells (Fig. 2E and F). Taken together, ZNF33A promoted the growth of CRC cells.

Knockdown of ZNF33A inhibited the proliferation, growth and colony formation of CRC cells

To investigate the functions of endogenously expressed ZNF33A in CRC, the expression of ZNF33A in HCT8 and SW480 cells was knocked down (Fig. 3A), after which the effect of knockdown of ZNF33A on the growth of HCT8 and SW480 cells was detected by CCK8 assay (Fig. 3B). The results of CCK8 assay showed that knockdown of ZNF33A inhibited the growth of CRC cells in liquid medium. Consistently, the EdU assay showed that after knockdown of ZNF33A, the EdU incorporation into the DNA of HCT8 cells decreased, suggesting that knockdown of ZNF33A inhibited the proliferation of CRC cells (Fig. 3C and D). Furthermore, knockdown of ZNF33A inhibited the anchorage-independent growth of CRC cells (Fig. 3E and F). Collectively, downregulation of ZNF33A inhibited the malignant phenotype of CRC cells.

Knocking out the homolog of human ZNF33A in mouse intestinal epithelial cells inhibits the stemness of intestinal stem cells and the pathogenesis of CRC

To explore the biological functions of ZNF33A in vivo, the homolog of human ZNF33A was knocked out in mouse intestinal epithelial cells using Villin-Cre mice (Fig. S1). After knocking out the homolog of human ZNF33A, the mouse intestinal epithelial villi were significantly shortened, suggesting that knocking out the homolog of human ZNF33A is likely to affect the stemness of stem cells (Fig. 4A). Sox9 is a crypt marker(19). By staining the small intestinal epithelium with anti-Sox9 antibody, it was found that the crypts became even smaller after knocking out the homolog of human ZNF33A (Fig. 4B). To further track the changes in stem cells, Lgr5-GFP mice were crossed with the mice which was knocked out the homolog of human ZNF33A, after which the expression of GFP was detected by immunohistochemistry to observe the number and location of stem cells. The results showed that GFP-positive cells were significantly reduced after knocking out the homolog of human ZNF33A (Fig. 4C and D). On the other hand, the clearance of intestinal mouse villi was induced by radiation, and the regeneration of small intestinal villi was observed to evaluate the self-renewal ability of intestinal stem cells. Knockout of the homolog of human ZNF33A inhibited the regeneration of mouse intestinal villi after irradiation treatment (Fig. 4E). In addition, crypts in the small intestinal epithelium were isolated for organoid formation assays. After knockout of the homolog of human ZNF33A, the number of formed organoids decreased (Fig. 4F and G). AOM/DSS treatment can induce CRC in mice. Knocking out the homolog of human ZNF33A inhibited AOM/DSS-induced CRC (Fig. 4H-I). In summary, deleting the homolog of human ZNF33A dysregulated the homeostasis of mouse intestinal epithelial cells.

ZNF33A interacted with β-catenin

The dysregulation of stem cells in the intestinal epithelium after knocking out the homolog of human ZNF33A prompted us to investigate whether ZNF33A regulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which controlled the cell fate of stem cells(20). This hypothesis was then proven by examining the interaction between ZNF33A and the components of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. The interaction between exogenously expressed ZNF33A and β-catenin was detected by coimmunoprecipitation in 293T and HCT8 cells (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, in the GST pull-down assay, the fusion protein GST-ZNF33A could interact with endogenously expressed β-catenin (Fig. 5B). More importantly, endogenously expressed ZNF33A interacted with β-catenin, which was enhanced upon the Wnt3a treatment (Fig. 5C). These results indicated that ZNF33A formed a complex with β-catenin. Moreover, we have demonstrated that C-terminus of ZNF33A mediated the interaction between ZNF33A and β-catenin (Fig. 5D). Then, the biological significance of the interaction between ZNF33A and β-catenin was explored. The expression of ZNF33A promoted the interaction between β-catenin and TCF4 (Fig. 5E). Finally, we examined whether β-catenin mediated the biological function of ZNF33A. Knockdown of β-catenin expression in HCT8 cells relieves the promotion of anchorage-independent growth induced by overexpression of ZNF33A (Fig. 5F and G). In summary, β-catenin interacts with ZNF33A and mediates its biological functions.

ZNF33A activates the transcriptional activity of the β-catenin/TCF complex

Our studies have shown that the expression of ZNF33A promotes the interaction between β-catenin and TCF4. The effect of ZNF33A expression on the transcriptional activity of the β-catenin/TCF4 complex was explored. Overexpression of ZNF33A activated the activity of Topflash reporter (Fig. 6A). Knockdown of ZNF33A downregulated the expression of Axin2, CCND1, and c-Myc, the downstream target genes of the β-catenin/TCF4 transcription complex, and overexpression of ZNF33A promoted the expression of these target genes (Fig. 6B). In the chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays, ZNF33A was found to bind CCND1 promoter, and overexpression of ZNF33A promoted the binding of β-catenin to the CCND1 promoter region (Fig. 6C), while knockdown of ZNF33A inhibited the binding of β-catenin to the CCND1 promoter (Fig. 6D). Collectively, ZNF33A positively regulated the transcriptional activity of the β-catenin/TCF4 complex.

ZNF33A upregulated the expression of BCAT1 and promoted the consumption of BCAAs

To reveal the downstream biological events of ZNF33A, we analyzed the genes whose expression was significantly correlated with that of ZNF33A with the help of the GEPIA database. BCAT1 is one of the candidate genes (Fig. 7A). Therefore, the effect of ZNF33A on the expression of BCAT1, the rate-limiting enzyme of BCAA metabolism, was investigated. The results showed that overexpression of ZNF33A upregulated the expression of BCAT1, while knockdown of ZNF33A inhibited the expression of BCAT1 (Fig. 7B and C). However, ZNF33A did not regulate the expression of BCAT2 (Fig. S2). Consistently, knockdown of ZNF33A significantly reduced BCAA consumption in HCT8 and SW480 cells (Fig. 7D). Finally, whether BCAT1 mediates the biological function of ZNF33A was investigated. Knockdown of BCAT1 expression in HCT8 cells overexpressing ZNF33A relieved the promotional effect of ZNF33A on the anchorage-independent growth of HCT8 cells (Fig. 7E and F). These observations demonstrated that BCAT1 was a downstream effector of ZNF33A.

ZNF33A upregulated the expression of BCAT1 through β-catenin

To investigate whether ZNF33A upregulated the expression of BCAT1 through β-catenin/TCF4 signaling, the promoter of BCAT1 was analyzed. In the BCAT1 promoter, TCF binding element (TBE) sequences were found at both − 369 and − 232 bp (Fig. 8A), suggesting that β-catenin possibly regulates the transcription of BCAT1. The promoter of BCAT1 was cloned, and a reporter assay was performed. The results showed that overexpression of β-catenin activated the promoter of BCAT1 in HCT8 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 8B). By mutating these two TBEs, it was found that the mutation of TBE1 (TBE at -232 bp) relieved the activation of the BCAT1 promoter by β-catenin (Fig. 8C), suggesting that β-catenin binds to TBE1. This result was further confirmed by ChIP assay (Fig. 8D). Next, we explored whether ZNF33A upregulates BCAT1 expression through β-catenin/TCF signaling, and the results showed that the knockdown of β-catenin relieved the induction of BCAT1 by ZNF33A (Fig. 8E). This finding suggests that ZNF33A upregulates BCAT1 expression through β-catenin/TCF signaling.

Then, we examined whether ZNF33A exerted its oncogenic functions in vivo through β-catenin and BCAT1, we knocked down β-catenin and BCAT1 in HCT116 cells with ZNF33A overexpression. Discussion

In CRC, the inactivation and mutation of the APC tumor suppressor gene leads to aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby promoting the growth and metastasis of CRC [21]. It seems that Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a potential target for the treatment of CRC. However, Wnt/β-catenin signaling also plays an important role in the maintenance of intestinal epithelial homeostasis [22]. Therefore, inhibiting this signaling pathway will lead to serious side effects. On the other hand, due to the inactivation and mutation of APC, the β-catenin destruction complex in the cytoplasm is in a state of disintegration, meaning that the promising target can only be identified downstream the destruction complex. This study showed that ZNF33A interacted with the β-catenin/TCF transcription complex and upregulated the expression of BCAT1 to reprogram the metabolism of BCAAs. Therefore, it is possible to provide new insights into the treatment of CRC from the perspective of tumor metabolism.

The most important finding of this study is the regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling on the metabolism of BCAAs. At present, the functions of BCAAs in CRC remain controversial. Kang et al. found that the deletion of BCAT1 promotes the growth and tumorigenicity of CRC cells [23], and Long et al. found that BCAA intake was positively correlated with mortality in CRC patients [3, 23]. However, Rossi M et al. collected data from 1953 CRC patients (including 442 patients with sigmoid colon cancer) and analyzed the relationship between BCAAs and CRC [24]. They found that there was no correlation between BCAA intake and mortality in CRC but that BCAA intake did reduce the incidence of sigmoid colon cancer [24]. The results of the current study further support this finding. ZNF33A promotes the malignant phenotype of CRC by upregulating BCAT1 through Wnt/β-catenin signaling. The reprogramming metabolism by Wnt/β-catenin signaling has recently received extensive attention. For example, Deng et al. reported that Wnt/β-catenin signaling promotes the progression of pancreatic, liver, and breast cancers through activating mevalonate metabolism, one-carbon metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle [16, 17, 25]; they explored the therapeutic effects of statins [16], inhibitors of the key one-carbon metabolism enzyme MTHFD1[17], and IDH inhibitors on tumors [25]. These studies have shown that targeting Wnt/β-catenin signaling from the perspective of tumor metabolism is highly feasible.

The functions of ZNF33A are largely unknown. Overexpression of ZNF33A in TNBC patient tissues led to a worse prognosis, and ZNF33A promoted cell growth and facilitated the resistance of cancer cells to inhibitors of bromodomain and extraterminal domain (BET) in TNBC [18]. This study combining with our study improved our understanding of ZNF family proteins.

Conclusions

In summary, this study reveals the regulatory effect of ZNF33A on Wnt/β-catenin signaling and the metabolism of BCAAs in CRC, suggesting that targeting the metabolism of BCAAs may be a good strategy for the treatment of CRC due to the aberrant activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling.
