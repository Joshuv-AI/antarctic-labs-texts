Real‐world prediction of early‐onset dementia by health record data: A multi‐center machine learning study
Authors: Xuewen Rong, Weijin Zhang, Shanjie Luan, Du Feng, Ningning Wang, Jingyu Xiao, Junyi He, Jun Liu, Liming Shu

BACKGROUND

Dementia is an acquired impairment in multiple cognitive domains, sufficient to interfere with social or occupational functioning. 1 With global population aging, the burden of dementia is steadily increasing. 2 Dementia has two key subtypes based on the age at disease onset: Early‐onset dementia (EOD), also known as young‐onset dementia, which occurs before age 65; and late‐onset dementia (LOD), which occurs at age 65 or older. These two subtypes exhibit considerable heterogeneity in clinical features and underlying mechanisms. 3 LOD has drawn more research attention due to its higher prevalence in older adults. In contrast, EOD remains under‐recognized and less studied. Important risk factors for EOD include social and clinical factors, such as alcohol use, educational level, socioeconomic status, stroke, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and depression. 4 , 5 Clinically, EOD often presents with aphasia and a higher frequency of atypical phenotypes, including visuospatial, language, executive, behavioral, or motor dysfunction. Pathological differences between EOD and LOD have also been documented. 3 , 6 Overall, EOD patients tend to experience more severe cognitive deficits and worse functional outcomes than those with LOD. 7 Diagnosing EOD faces significant challenges. Due to the younger age at onset and atypical features, misdiagnosis or diagnostic delay is frequent, with average delay potentially exceed 4.4 years. 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 In a pathologically confirmed early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (EOAD) cohort, 53% of patients with atypical symptoms were misdiagnosed, and only 5% reported cognitive symptoms within 5 years preceding diagnosis in another study. 6 , 11 These delays severely limit timely interventions and increase the disease burden in younger patients. Although less prevalent than LOD, EOD exerts a disproportionately high personal, social, and economic impact, intensified by the younger age at onset. 12 Consequently, tools to identify individuals at increased risk of EOD are critically needed. Moreover, the rapid progression and severe functional decline of EOD underscore the urgent need for robust prognostic models to inform clinical decisions, optimize care planning, and guide resource allocation. However, the development of predictive and prognostic models specifically for EOD remains lacking. Existing machine learning–based models focus on general dementia and pay limited attention to EOD, frequently relying on complex predictors derived from homogeneous cohorts. 13 , 14 , 15 This study aims to develop EOD‐specific risk‐prediction and prognostic models to address the gap. We integrated data from five large‐scale cohorts—UK Biobank (UKB), China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), Health and Retirement Study (HRS), Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), and Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA)—covering populations from the U.K., China, the United States, Europe, and South Korea. These cohorts represent more than 50% of global EOD cases. An ensemble algorithm was employed to enhance model robustness and reliability. We selected 26 simple and accessible predictors from the health record data without the need for specialized equipment or professional testing.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: A PubMed search indicates that few scalable, low‐cost tools have been specifically developed for assessing risk and prognosis in early‐onset dementia (EOD), particularly within diverse, real‐world populations. Existing models for EOD focus primarily on early‐onset Alzheimer's disease and are often based on selected cohorts and advanced predictors. Although some general dementia prediction models have been proposed, many are derived from relatively homogeneous populations and rely on complex or resource‐intensive variables. In addition, early‐ and late‐onset dementia differ considerably in their underlying pathophysiology, age at onset, and associated sociodemographic characteristics. Because most general models are optimized for late‐onset dementia, their applicability to EOD may be limited. These observations highlight the need for risk and prognostic models specifically tailored to the characteristics and clinical challenges of EOD.

METHODS

Study population and datasets

In this study, we utilized data from five large‐scale, population‐based aging cohorts to develop EOD risk prediction and prognostic models: UKB, 16 CHARLS (Waves 2–5), 17 HRS (Waves 11–15), 18 KLoSA (Waves 4, 6, and 7), 19 and SHARE (Waves 4–8). 20 UKB is a prospective cohort of more than 500,000 participants in the UK, with extensive baseline assessments and linkage to National Health Service (NHS) electronic health records. CHARLS, HRS, KLoSA, and SHARE provide comparable biennial survey data across China, the United States, Korea, and Europe, respectively. These cohorts include middle‐aged and older adults (typically aged ≥40 or ≥45 years) and collect harmonized data on sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, medical history, cognitive and functional status, and psychosocial factors. Data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database were used solely to illustrate the disease burden of EOD. 21

We selected predictors that were commonly available across the five cohorts to ensure consistency in model development. For the risk prediction model, data from UKB, CHARLS, HRS, and KLoSA were pooled for model training and internal validation. The SHARE dataset was withheld as an independent external validation cohort to assess model generalizability, which was not involved in model training or internal validation. For the prognostic model, all five cohorts were combined and randomly partitioned into training and internal validation sets. (Figure 1A, B).

Together, these harmonized, interview‐based cohorts provide a unique foundation for global aging research, enabling robust, cross‐national modeling of disease risk and progression. Detailed descriptions of the five cohorts are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Definition of EOD outcomes

EOD is defined as dementia with symptom onset before the age of 65. However, due to its diverse and frequently atypical clinical presentations, EOD remains difficult to recognize and diagnose. 3 Given the significant diagnostic delays reported in prior studies, 22 , 23 , 24 we expanded the EOD definition to include dementia onset before age 70, aiming to better capture delayed diagnoses and improve case identification (Figure 1C). This approach is consistent with previous research and supports the reliability of our classification. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Given that the included databases lack detailed clinical diagnosis records, we used the year of survey collection in the questionnaire as a proxy for diagnosis year. Accordingly, diagnostic delay is to be expected, and the application of an extended diagnostic window is both appropriate and necessary for these data sources. To maintain the precision of outcome, our prognostic model employed the conventional EOD definition (symptom onset before the age of 65).

In UKB, dementia outcomes were identified using algorithm‐based classifications and hospital record linkages, incorporating the following diagnostic codes: 42,018; 130,840; 130,842; 42,020; 130,836; 42,022; 130,838; and 42,024. The main dementia subtypes included Alzheimer's disease (AD), vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and other forms of dementia. Definitions of dementia outcomes varied slightly across the other cohorts. In HRS, dementia was defined using the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS), where respondents scoring 0–6 on the 27‐point scale were classified as dementia. 29 In CHARLS and SHARE, probable dementia was defined according to the operational criteria adopted in the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). 30 , 31 Based on the prior studies, participants were stratified into age subgroups (50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69 years). Individuals scoring more than 1.5 standard deviations (SD) below the subgroup mean were classified as probable dementia cases. In KLoSA, dementia was defined by the Korean Mini‐Mental State Examination (K‐MMSE) score (score ≤17). 32

Based on these criteria, individuals with a diagnosis before the age of 70 were classified as having EOD (Figure 1A, B). The end of follow‐up was defined as the first occurrence of either a dementia diagnosis or death, with data censored at this event date up to 2024. In the subsequent prognostic analysis, the event of interest was defined as all‐cause mortality.

Feature selection

We conducted a systematic review and harmonization of variables available across the five cohorts. The features selected encompassed sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, and basic physical or behavioral measures. All selected variables were accessible via self‐report or routine interview, requiring no specialized clinical assessments or laboratory tests. To ensure comparability across cohorts, especially regarding medical history, where direct mappings (e.g., from UKB) were inconsistent due to varied medical terminologies across databases, several derived features were constructed. The final set of features included: age, sex, educational attainment, history of hypertension, diabetes or hyperglycemia, cancer, chronic lung disease, chronic heart disease, stroke, arthritis, and psychiatric disorders; height, weight, former and current smoking status, alcohol consumption, body mass index (BMI), number of household members, employment status, hearing aid use, left‐ and right‐hand grip strength, frequency of vigorous physical activity per week, rural or urban residence, pain experience, and marital status. Detailed definitions and harmonization procedures for all variables are provided in the Table S1.

Data preprocessing

Given the relative rarity of EOD and its high disease burden, we merged individual cohorts into composite datasets to increase sample size and enhance population diversity for model training and validation. 33 For the risk prediction model, data from UKB, CHARLS, HRS, and KLoSA were pooled and randomly split into the training set and internal validation set at a 7:3 ratio. The SHARE dataset was retained exclusively as an external validation cohort to assess generalizability.

To prevent information leakage and maintain cohort‐specific integrity, we first evaluated patterns of missingness, followed by multiple imputation using the mice R package with predictive mean matching. Five imputed datasets were generated. Notably, BMI was excluded from within‐set imputations to avoid introducing correlated biases. For continuous variables, outliers were detected and removed using the 1.5×interquartile range (IQR) rule prior to modeling.

Due to a limited number of progression events, the prognostic model was developed using the full merged dataset comprising all five cohorts. This dataset was similarly partitioned into training and internal validation subsets using the same 7:3 random split.

Risk prediction model construction and validation framework

To address the pronounced class imbalance in the outcome variable, we applied a suite of advanced ensemble learning techniques, including both boosting and voting‐based approaches. Specifically, we implemented the EasyEnsemble algorithm (with 50 estimators) in Python to integrate predictions from 11 base classifiers: Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees, XGBoost, AdaBoost, CatBoost, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, Elastic Net, Linear Discriminant Analysis, Naïve Bayes, and Multi‐Layer Perceptron. EasyEnsemble constructs multiple balanced subsets by under‐sampling the majority class and fits base learners on each subset, with aggregated predictions improving minority class detection. 34 Each base model underwent fivefold cross‐validation on the training set for hyperparameter tuning, using average precision as the scoring metric. Soft and hard voting strategies were applied with the results of the ensemble models. The model achieving the highest area under the receiver‐operating characteristic (AUROC) on the internal validation set was selected, and the optimal classification threshold, determined by the Youden index, was applied to the external validation cohort (SHARE) to assess generalizability. 35 Model discrimination was further evaluated by plotting cumulative incidence curves stratified by predicted risk levels.

Prognostic model construction and validation framework

To model EOD progression, we applied four survival analysis algorithms: the Cox proportional hazards (CoxPH), 36 gradient boosting machine (GB) for survival, 37 RF, 38 and support vector machine (SVM) for survival. 39 CoxPH is a semi‐parametric model that estimates hazard ratios under the assumption of proportional hazards. GB and RF are ensemble learning methods designed for right‐censored data, capable of modeling non‐linear effects and complex interactions without parametric constraints. SVM for survival employs kernel‐based techniques to estimate time‐to‐event functions in high‐dimensional feature spaces.

Model performance was evaluated using the time‐dependent area under the curve (AUC) at 3, 5, and 7 years. The model with the highest mean time‐dependent AUC in the internal validation set was selected. 40 In addition, time‐dependent AUCs were generated to visualize discrimination performance across follow‐up time.

Risk stratification was performed using the optimal cutoff derived from the survminer R package in the training set, delineating high‐ and low‐risk groups. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted for each group, and differences were tested for significance using the log‐rank test. 41

Sensitivity analysis

To further validate the generalizability and robustness of the prediction model, we conducted five model‐based sensitivity analyses. First, we split the mixed internal validation set into four smaller subsets according to their sources (UKB, CHARLS, HRS, and KLoSA) to compare model performance across different populations. Second, within the internal validation set, we applied a stricter definition of EOD by restricting cases to those with onset before age 65, thereby providing a more rigorous test of model robustness. Third, given that our model defined EOD using objective indicators, we replaced the TICS‐based definitions of dementia in HRS and CHARLS with clinical diagnosis records to evaluate model performance in predicting clinically diagnosed cases. Finally, given the predominance of UKB participants in the sample, we compared two additional common modeling strategies: (1) We trained the model solely on UKB data and validated it in the other cohorts. (2) We balanced the number of participants from UKB and the other datasets to equalize feature learning across populations. Within the training set, data from UKB and the other cohorts were balanced at a 1:1 ratio. To ensure accurate evaluation, the remaining UKB data were used as the valid set.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with IQRs (Q1–Q3), and group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, with group comparisons performed using 2×k contingency tables via chi‐square tests (with Yates’ correction) or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate.

Predictive model performance was evaluated using AUROC and area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC). Although the AUROC assessed overall discrimination, the AUPRC was used to account for class imbalance. Additional metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, were calculated based on the Youden index–optimized classification threshold.

The Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) analysis was conducted to assess feature importance and directionality. Prediction outputs from CatBoost (for risk) and RF (for prognosis) were regressed against input features to obtain SHAP values, which were visualized via beeswarm plots and bar charts.

Subgroup fairness was examined by computing AUROC and AUPRC across strata defined by median‐split continuous variables and original categories of categorical variables.

All analyses were conducted using R (v4.4.2) and Python (v3.13), with visualizations generated in R, Python, and BioRender (https://www.biorender.com/). 42 A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Prediction of future EOD risk and cumulative hazard

We evaluated the predictive accuracy of the simplified feature set for future EOD in the internal and external validation sets. The performance of various ensemble machine learning algorithms is presented in Figure 2A and Table S4. Among them, the CatBoost algorithm demonstrated the best performance in terms of both AUROC (0.814, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.798−0.830), AUPRC (0.070, 95% CI: 0.069−0.071), and precision (0.024, 95% CI: 0.022‐0.026) (Figures 2C, D, and S1). Therefore, CatBoost was selected as the final model for EOD risk prediction. Using a stricter definition of EOD (onset before 65 years), CatBoost achieved superior performance in the internal validation set after removing noise and mislabeled cases. We interpret this as a better reflection of the model's capability to identify typical EOD (see Section 3.4).

Sensitivity analysis of the risk prediction model

In the first sensitivity analysis, the model demonstrated consistently strong and robust performance across the four smaller internal validation subsets (UKB, CHARLS, HRS, and KLoSA). Notably, performance was highest in HRS, representing the U.S. population (AUROC: 0.769; AUPRC: 0.156). When applying the stricter definition of EOD (onset age <65 years), model discrimination remained highly stable with a slight improvement compared to the original internal validation results (AUROC: 0.892, 95% CI: 0.882−0.901), with precision showing a similar trend (AUPRC: 0.089, 95% CI: 0.088–0.090). The model also maintained stable discrimination when predicting clinically diagnosed EOD cases (AUROC: 0.822, 95% CI: 0.805−0.840), although precision was somewhat reduced (AUPRC: 0.027, 95% CI: 0.026–0.028). However, under alternative modeling strategies, performance was markedly poorer. Specifically, the model trained solely on UKB performed unsatisfactorily when applied to the other cohorts. With the exception of certain models in SHARE, the majority yielded AUROCs below 0.700. The best model (CatBoost) after balancing samples achieved only modest performance in the mixed internal validation set (AUROC: 0.629). The relevant results are summarized in the Supplementary Information.

Prediction of prognosis and stratification of risk groups

To predict the prognosis of individuals with EOD, we further applied four models—RF, GB, RF, and SVM. Among these, RF demonstrated the best performance. In the internal validation set, RF achieved an AUROC of 0.649 for 3‐year prediction, with strong performance maintained at 5 years (AUROC: 0.656) and 7 years (AUROC: 0.659) (Figure 4). Based on consistent superiority in time‐dependent AUROC across all time points, RF was selected as the optimal model for EOD prognosis prediction.

Variable importance

We performed SHAP analyses to interpret feature contributions in both the risk prediction and prognostic models. The relative importance and direction of each predictor are illustrated in the SHAP summary plots. In the risk prediction model, the top five most influential features were age, gender, education level, work status, and marital status. Notably, older age, male, and the presence of pain were associated with higher EOD risk, whereas active employment was strongly linked to reduced risk (Figure 3A).

Figure 3B presents an individual‐level SHAP force plot that demonstrates how each feature contributed to the model's risk prediction for a specific case. For this participant, age, history of psychiatry, and hearing disorders increased the predicted risk, whereas gender, work status, education level, pain experience, and hypertension exerted a protective effect. A higher SHAP value reflects a stronger positive contribution to the model output, whereas negative SHAP values indicate a downward influence on the predicted risk.

In addition, our findings align with established evidence, supporting the association of higher education level, moderate alcohol consumption, and stronger grip strength with decreased incidence of EOD. Conversely, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and psychiatric disorders were associated with elevated EOD risk. 4 , 5

SHAP analysis of the prognostic model identified work status, age, smoking history, BMI, and height as the top five predictive features. Among these, being employed was strongly associated with a more favorable prognosis (Figure 5A). A comparison of SHAP patterns between the risk and prognostic models highlights the consistent and critical roles of work status and age features in both the development and progression of EOD.

DISCUSSION

Main findings

To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first attempts to develop and externally validate machine learning–based models for both risk prediction and prognostic estimation of EOD. Despite its significant personal and societal burden, EOD remains underdiagnosed and understudied, particularly with regard to early identification and outcome prediction. In this large‐scale, prospective, and multi‐cohort study, we developed and validated machine learning models for both risk prediction and prognostic estimation of EOD. By leveraging harmonized data from five population‐based longitudinal cohorts, we identified key predictors and established robust, interpretable models based on non‐invasive and easily obtainable variables.

The CatBoost‐based risk prediction model achieved high discriminative performance in internal validation and maintained strong generalizability in external validation using the SHARE cohort. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings. Subgroup analyses further supported the fairness and generalizability of the model across sex, age, education, and comorbidity strata. For prognostic estimation, the RF model demonstrated the best overall performance across 3‐, 5‐, and 7‐year time points. Kaplan–Meier analyses confirmed the model's ability to stratify patients with EOD into significantly different risk groups in both training and validation sets.

By focusing specifically on EOD and integrating multiple large‐scale, harmonized datasets, our study provides novel, practical tools for early identification and long‐term outcome prediction in this underrepresented patient population.

Comparison with previous studies and application

As far as we are aware, predictive models specifically designed for general EOD remain largely unexplored. Current efforts in EOD risk prediction have predominantly focused on EOAD, a subtype of EOD. As EOAD constitutes a “rare condition within common diseases,” these studies are often constrained by small and highly selective sample sizes, which may compromise the generalizability of findings to broader populations. Furthermore, these EOAD models typically rely on advanced and expensive technologies that are not widely accessible, including optical coherence tomography angiography, 43 structural or functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), tau positron emission tomography (PET; 18F‐Flortaucipir), amyloid PET (18F‐Florbetaben or 11C‐Pittsburgh compound B), 44 and whole‐genome sequencing. 45 Incorporating genetic and advanced neuroimaging data, these studies have offered important insights into EOD and enabled accurate risk prediction in well‐defined population subsets. However, such approaches may be less feasible for large‐scale community screening, particularly in under‐resourced settings or primary care environments.

Although general dementia prediction models demonstrate robust performance, 13 , 14 , 15 , 46 they may face three challenges: (1) inherent limitations similar to those observed in EOAD‐specific models; (2) potential heterogeneity and misclassification introduced by the fundamental pathophysiological and demographic differences between EOD and LOD; and (3) limited applicability to EOD due to models’ optimization bias toward LOD where positive cases are more prevalent.

In contrast, our study offers two key advantages. First, our model relies solely on easily accessible predictors, all of which can be obtained via brief interviews or self‐administered questionnaires—without the need for clinical personnel, imaging, or laboratory testing. This may support the feasibility of our tool for community‐level deployment and scalable risk stratification. Second, we developed and validated our models using harmonized data from five large, nationally representative cohorts, which provided a diverse, multinational dataset comprising more than 400,000 healthy controls and more than 2600 EOD cases. This large and heterogeneous population base enhances the robustness, generalizability, and real‐world applicability of our models across different health care systems and sociocultural contexts.

Notably, despite not incorporating high‐cost genetic or neuroimaging markers, our model indicates a modest advantage over a previous EOAD model that includes up to 15 genetic mutations (AUROC: 0.78), 45 highlighting its potential utility as a scalable, low‐resource screening tool for early identification of individuals at risk for EOD.

Model selection and stability

Machine learning has been applied increasingly to all‐cause dementia prediction in recent years. 47 Although existing evidence suggests that machine learning and traditional regression methods show comparable performance for all‐cause dementia, no direct evidence is available for EOD. 48 , 49 In this study, we compared conventional regression models with machine learning methods. Compared with the linear model such as logistic regression and CoxPH, CatBoost and RF were selected as the final predictive models due to their superior performance.

Furthermore, the sensitivity analyses provided strong evidence for the robustness and generalizability of the risk prediction model. In particular, the use of a stricter EOD definition yielded a marked improvement in model performance, suggesting that the model can reliably and effectively predict typical EOD. This phenomenon is common in imbalanced machine learning studies. 50 Lenient EOD definitions and heterogeneous case criteria across databases may introduce mislabeled positives, which reduce the model's discriminative ability and accuracy. 33 , 51 Therefore, although a stricter definition inevitably led to a smaller sample size, the results more faithfully reflected the true performance of the model. This finding is consistent with the subgroup analysis, in which younger participants also demonstrated better discrimination and precision.

Important predictors

As we noted, our SHAP analyses were broadly consistent with findings from the established literature. Notably, our results reinforce the critical role of age in both the onset and prognosis of EOD, aligning with well‐documented associations observed in LOD. 5 In addition, our study highlights employment status as a significant protective factor for both EOD risk and outcomes. One possible explanation is that individuals who are actively employed—particularly in cognitively demanding roles—may benefit from enhanced contextual memory and greater cognitive reserve, thereby lowering their risk of developing dementia. 52 Furthermore, our results identified marital status—specifically being married—as another important but often overlooked protective factor against EOD, which is consistent with prior findings in broader dementia research. 53

Comparison with studies on LOD highlights both shared and distinct features between EOD and LOD. In a study predicting LOD among older adults in rural China, alcohol consumption and higher education were identified as protective factors, consistent with the associations observed for EOD. 15 However, whereas female sex was significantly associated with increased risk of LOD, our findings suggest that being female serves as a protective factor for EOD. Similarly, smoking was reported as protective in LOD, while in our study, it emerged as a risk factor for EOD.

Volunteer bias and imbalance

Volunteer bias is a widely recognized yet often under‐discussed limitation in large‐scale population cohorts. 54 Although our study integrated data from multiple longitudinal datasets, the UKB constituted the majority of the sample. UKB is known to exhibit substantial volunteer bias, with prior research indicating that disease prevalence is significantly underestimated when benchmarked against general population data. For example, UKB participants are less likely to be obese, smoke, or consume alcohol daily, and they report better overall health. 55

In our study, this volunteer bias was directly reflected in the substantially lower EOD prevalence observed in UKB compared to expected population rates, resulting in a highly imbalanced dataset (Figure 1A). The extreme class imbalance reduced the number of positive cases available for model training, thereby increasing the difficulty of accurate risk identification. To address this challenge, we applied advanced ensemble learning algorithms for model development while explicitly avoiding sampling‐based methods—which risk introducing noise or statistical artifacts—thereby ensuring model robustness and generalizability. 56 Consequently, the reported model performance may represent a conservative estimate under the highly imbalanced data structure. Therefore, it is reasonable to anticipate improved model performance in real‐world settings with higher disease prevalence or more balanced class distributions.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It is the first to develop and validate risk and prognostic models specifically for EOD, addressing a critical gap in the field. The use of harmonized, large‐scale data from five international cohorts improves model robustness and cross‐population applicability. All predictors were simple, non‐invasive, and interview‐based, supporting scalability in primary care and low‐resource settings. Ensemble and other learning methods were used to manage extreme class imbalance, with systematic comparison across the algorithms. The risk prediction model demonstrated strong generalizability, confirmed by external validation in the SHARE cohort and consistent performance across key subgroups.

However, several limitations should be considered. First and foremost, given the low incidence and relative rarity of EOD, it is inevitable that some degree of misclassification will occur in practical applications of the model. The psychological and ethical implications of misclassifying an incurable neurodegenerative condition must be considered with great caution. 57 , 58 Second, the primary dataset (UKB) is subject to volunteer bias, which is likely to contribute to class imbalance. Third, most predictors were self‐reported or derived from secondary electronic health records, with potential for inaccuracy or missing data. Race and ethnicity were not included as model features due to feature absence. Fourth, the definition of EOD varied across cohorts. This may compromise diagnostic consistency and influence model performance. Finally, despite including more EOD cases than previous studies, the absolute number remains limited due to the disease's rarity. Future studies using larger, more representative datasets are warranted to validate and expand these findings. In the future, with the inclusion of populations that have a higher pre‐test probability of EOD, an online tool may be developed and tailored for use in these high‐prevalence groups.
