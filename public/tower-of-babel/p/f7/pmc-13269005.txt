Copper isotope shifts in biofluids track Alzheimer's pathology
Authors: Esther Lahoud, Frédéric Moynier, Brandon Mahan, Tu‐han Luu, Dimitri Rigoussen, Marie Le Borgne, Claire Paquet, Christopher Fowler, Ashley I Bush
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAltered copper metabolism has been reported in Alzheimer's disease (AD), with post mortem brain tissue showing depletion of 65Cu compared with 63Cu. Whether such isotopic shifts can be detected in biofluids remains unknown.METHODSWe analyzed copper isotope composition in human cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and serum from AD patients and controls using multi‐collector inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (MC‐ICP‐MS) and single quadrupole inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (SQ‐ICP‐MS). Samples included 22 CSFs (10 AD, 12 controls), 54 sera (28 AD, 26 controls).RESULTSAD CSF was enriched in 65Cu (+0.32‰, p = 0.02), correlating inversely with CSF amyloid beta (Aβ)42/40 ratios. Serum from AD patients was depleted in 65Cu (−0.23‰, p = 0.013), trending inversely with amyloid positron emission tomography burden. Serum and CSF δ 65Cu diagnostically discriminate between AD and controls (area under the curve = 0.8, Youden's J ≈ 0.5 SD, p ≤ 0.0002).DISCUSSIONCopper isotope excursions in brain parenchyma are mirrored in CSF and serum. These shifts reflect amyloid pathology and highlight isotopic copper analysis as a potential minimally invasive biomarker for AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid, biometals, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), copper, isotope, serum

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurogenerative disease affecting nearly 58 million people worldwide. Because there is still no known cure, a major issue is to diagnose the disease early enough for treatments to slow the disease. The search for early and innovative in vivo biomarkers is essential. The diagnosis of AD relies on the confirmation of brain amyloid burden using positron emission tomography (PET) or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers. 1 PET biomarkers assess amyloid pathology using the PET Centiloid scale. The Centiloid framework provides a standardized quantitative measure of amyloid beta (Aβ) deposition across different PET tracers and imaging centers, enabling direct comparison of amyloid burden between studies and clinical settings. 2 The CSF biomarkers Aβ1–42 (Aβ42), Aβ42/40 ratio, total tau (t‐tau), and phosphorylated tau species (p‐tau) 3 , 4 correlate with brain amyloid burden. 5 A typical AD biological profile is characterized by increased CSF p‐tau and t‐tau levels associated with decreased CSF Aβ42 (relative to Aβ40) level.

Biomarkers of AD neuropathology are not limited to amyloid and tau abnormalities but also include many other potential markers of pathophysiological mechanisms such as synaptic loss and neuroinflammation. 6 Among these, levels of inorganic elements and their isotopes in blood and CSF were examined in previous studies to determine whether perturbations noted in the brain tissue in AD 7 are reflected as fluid biomarkers. These have included Hobin et al. 8 who reported disease‐related shifts in whole blood Cu isotopic signatures in AD and 9 who identified alterations in CSF Cu isotopic composition in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis but no change in mild cognitive impairment (MCI). These studies provide important groundwork but were lacking in clinical and AD biomarker details, such as quantification of markers of amyloid burden.

Biologically relevant metals such as copper, iron, and zinc are highly concentrated in amyloid plaques, where they are coordinated by Aβ. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 The accumulation of Aβ plaques in the brain in AD has been proposed to induce a change in the aggregate ionic bonding environment and so modify the isotopic proportions of these metals. 7 , 14 Stable Cu isotopes exist in a relative abundance ratio of 69.17 65Cu:30.83 63Cu. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 15 The copper isotopic excursion from natural abundance (δ 65Cu) for normal human brain tissue is +0.60 ± 0.13‰ (2 standard deviations [SD]), n = 10. 7 It was recently observed that human AD brain tissue is significantly depleted in 65Cu (δ 65Cu = +0.35 ± 0.13‰ [2 SD], n = 10), compared to normal control tissue (p = 0.0002), 7 with δ 65Cu calculated as in Equation (1):

Isotopic redistributions in AD might generalize, since the isotopic proportion of potassium in human serum from AD subjects is significantly lighter than in healthy controls. 16

We hypothesized that the perturbation in the brain Cu isotope composition would be transferred to biofluids, serum, and CSF, as previously proposed. 8 , 9 To this end, we investigated Cu isotopic markers in CSF and serum in the context of AD and, to extend the foundational studies, related these signatures to established clinical biomarkers of AD, including PET Centiloid measures and CSF Aβ42/40 ratio, t‐tau and p‐tau181.

METHODS

Human CSF (n = 22) and serum (n = 54) samples from clinically characterized AD patients and non‐AD controls from two independent cohorts were analyzed. Details of sample collection, clinical classification, copper purification, isotopic and concentration measurements, and statistical analyses are provided in the Supplementary Methods. 14 , 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

Copper isotopic signatures in the CSF samples are summarized in Table 3, raw data in Table S3, and plotted in Figure 1A. AD CSF samples expressed higher δ 65Cu values compared to NC, with an average δ 65Cu of 0.62 ± 0.90‰ (2 SD) and 0.30 ± 0.71‰ (2 SD), respectively. The Mann‐Whitney U test confirmed a significant difference between both groups (p = 0.0191, Figure 1A, Table 3). CSF samples of AD patients have a heavier Cu isotope composition compared to those from NCs. The levels of total Cu were not different between AD and NC groups (Figure 1B, Table 3). No significant influence of sex or age was observed (Figures S1B,C). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) for CSF δ 6 5Cu yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82 and a Youden J index of 0.50, indicating a good ability to discriminate between AD from NC subjects 22 , 23 , 24 and a diagnostic cut‐off of 0.5 SD difference from the mean of the NCs (Figure 1C). The 95% confidence interval (CI) is 0.65 to 0.99 with p = 0.0002 (DeLong test).

The LARI participants enrolled as NCs in this study, and while they had cognitive symptoms, they were not demented and had normal CSF biomarkers (Table 1). To investigate the appropriateness of these controls, we compared the CSF δ 65Cu values for our NCs (n = 12) with those from Sauzéat et al. (2018). 9 The Mann‐Whitney U test showed that the two control groups were not significantly different (p = 0.48), Figure S2A.

To facilitate comparison between our results and previously published work, we combined our CSF δ 6 5Cu dataset with the literature. 9 The merged dataset is presented in Figure S2B. After removing an outlier at −1.24‰ identified using Grubbs’ test, we performed a Mann‐Whitney analysis on the combined data (p = 0.0303), confirming that AD subjects exhibit heavier copper isotopic compositions in CSF compared with controls.

For the serum samples, a systematic difference was observed between the LARI and AIBL datasets, regardless of disease status. Specifically, the mean δ 6 5Cu values from the LARI samples were consistently 0.41 units lower than those from the AIBL samples for non‐AD and 0.42 units lower for AD samples (Tables S2A,B). The underlying cause of this offset remains uncertain. Potential explanations may include differences in sample collection protocols or population‐specific factors such as dietary habits across geographic regions. Analyzing the raw δ 6 5Cu data from the two cohorts separately, the LARI cohort δ 6 5Cu values (–0.47‰) trended lower in AD subjects compared to controls (–0.23‰); p = 0.1875, Mann‐Whitney test, with an absolute difference of 0.24 units (Table S2A; Figure 2A). Serum δ 6 5Cu values (–0.05‰) also trended lower in AD subjects compared to controls in the AIBL cohort (0.18‰); p = 0.0898, Mann‐Whitney test, corresponding to a similar difference of 0.23 units (Table S2B; Figure 2B). No significant influence of sex or age was observed (Figure S1A and E). When pooling the unadjusted raw serum data from both LARI and AIBL cohorts together, we found that the serum δ 6 5Cu raw values were significantly lower in AD patients compared to controls (AD [mean] = −0.29; HC+NC [mean] = 0.01; p = 0.0157, Figure S3). We considered whether δ 6 5Cu correlated in serum and CSF values from the same individuals, but these samples all came from the LARI cohort, and only five individuals had companion values. There was no correlation from this underpowered analysis (Figure S4).

We calculated z‐scores for all serum δ 6 5Cu values and then pooled the cohort datasets (Figure 2C; Table S2C). When integrating datasets originating from different cohorts, differences in analytical conditions, baseline values, instrumentation, calibration, sample preparation, or demographic composition can influence the absolute values measured. Calculating z‐scores standardizes datasets by expressing each measurement relative to the mean and standard deviation of its respective control cohort. The normalization removes cohort‐specific offsets and scale differences, allowing the combined datasets to be comparable. After this adjustment, the serum δ 6 5Cu values were significantly lower in pooled AD compared to the pooled HC+NC cohorts (AD[mean] = −1.74; HC+NC [mean] = −0.06; p = 0.0002, Figure 2C). The ROC curve yielded an AUC of 0.79 for δ 6 5Cu (z‐scores) values with a Youden J index of 0.49, indicating a good ability to discriminate between AD and non‐AD subjects 22 , 23 , 24 in this sample set with a diagnostic cut‐off of ∼0.5 SD difference from the mean of the non‐AD subjects (Figure 2E). The 95% CI is 0.68 to 0.91 and p = 3.6 × 10− 7.

The serum δ 65Cu values were not impacted by age and sex for both AD and HC+NC controls (Figure S1A,B). CSF δ 65Cu values for both AD and NCs also showed no significant dependence on age or sex (Figure S1C, E). However, while AD occurs more frequently in women, 25 we only had two AD men in our CSF sampling set (Figure S2E), and therefore we could not validly assess the impact of sex on Cu elemental and isotopic signatures.

Effect of AD on total Cu concentrations in CSF

We measured the total Cu concentrations of the 22 human CSF samples (Table 3; Figure 1B [in ppb, ng/g]). There was no significant difference between total Cu levels in AD compared to NCs (p = 0.18; Figure 1B). No correlations between age and CSF total Cu concentration were observed for either AD subjects (Spearman's ρ = −0.51, p = 0.1140) or NC subjects (ρ = 0.00, p = 1) (Figure S1C).

Effect of AD on total Cu concentrations in serum

We measured the overall copper concentrations of 54 serum samples. The results and z‐scores are presented in Table S2C and Figure 2D. There was no significant difference between total Cu levels (z‐scores) in AD compared to HC+NC controls (Mann‐Whitney U test, p = 0.7357; Figure 2D).

Evidence that CSF and serum δ 6 5Cu excursions are caused by brain amyloid but not tangle pathology

Correlation trend between CSF δ 65Cu and Aß42/40 ratio in AD

The CSF Aβ42/40 ratio reflects the relative abundance of Aβ42, a key component of amyloid plaques, to Aβ40, which is more abundant in biological fluid but less enriched in plaques. A decreased Aβ42/40 ratio in the CSF indicates amyloid deposition in the brain, improving diagnostic accuracy compared to Aβ42 alone. 3 , 26 Since AD increases δ 65Cu in the CSF (Figure 1A), knowing the Aβ42/40 ratio in these samples (Table 1), we determined whether δ 65Cu correlated with this proxy for amyloid burden. For AD patients, Spearman's rank correlation revealed a significant negative association between δ 6 5Cu and the CSF Aβ42/40 ratio (ρ = −0.66, p = 0.038), indicating that heavier Cu isotope compositions tend to coincide with lower Aβ42/40 ratios (Figure 3A). There is no evidence of a correlation in NCs (R 2 = 0.25, p = 0.0944; Figure 3A). There is no correlation between total CSF Cu concentrations and Aβ42/40 ratio for AD subjects (R 2 = 0.17, p = 0.2070). These data imply that amyloid pathology, seen in AD but not NCs, selectively withdraws the lighter isotope of Cu (63Cu) from CSF, causing a higher proportion of 65Cu.

Correlation analyses between serum δ 65Cu and PET score

The Centiloid scale normalizes amyloid burden measurements to a range of 0 to 100, where 0 represents a typical amyloid‐negative control and 100 corresponds to the average amyloid burden in AD patients. 2 Centiloid values can exceed 100, reflecting higher Aβ plaque burdens than those observed in typical AD patients. 27 Pearson correlations revealed a significant negative correlation between serum δ 65Cu and Centiloid burden in the AIBL AD subjects but a significant positive correlation in the HCs (Figure 3B). After removal of an outlier (exceeding Grubbs’ threshold and noted in blue in Figure 3B), the correlations in AD (p = 0.083) HC (p = 0.058) approached statistical significance (Spearman rank correlation). The trends remain of interest but will need to be confirmed with larger datasets.

DISCUSSION

Comparison with previously published CSF and serum copper isotope data

Our findings for CSF δ 65Cu values were at variance from literature. 8 , 9 Several factors might account for this discrepancy. Copper isotopic composition has been shown to be influenced by multiple lifestyle and physiological parameters, including medication, alcohol consumption, diet, and physical activity. 28 Such sources of variability may distort changes in smaller cohorts or in datasets with limited clinical stratification. The cohort size from the literature 9 was similar to our own study. Larger, well‐controlled cohorts will be needed to fully resolve disease‐related trends. When we merged our raw data with data in the literature, 9 the significant increase in CSF δ 65Cu values in AD that we observed in the LARI cohort was preserved (Figure S2B).

Additionally, in the literature, 9 the severity of biomarker derangement and details on the clinical stage were not provided. As a progressive disorder, the pathophysiological status of AD patients can vary substantially as they become symptomatic. 5 , 27 , 29 This heterogeneity may influence biochemical signatures in CSF, including metal isotopic compositions. Therefore, differences in disease progression between the respective cohorts may have contributed to the variances between our two studies.

Our discrepancy with the CSF and serum copper isotope composition in AD reported by Hobin et al. 8 may be explained by differences in cohort number (n = 10 patients vs 8 controls in Hobin et al.; n = 11 AD vs n = 12 CSF samples and n = 28 AD and n = 26 control serum samples in this study) and disease stage between the respective cohorts. Furthermore, the patients 8 were classified as having MCI due to AD, and no details of the levels of their CSF AD biomarkers were provided. Our AD subjects were demented and, thus, at a more advanced stage had marked changes in their AD biomarkers and, therefore, were likely to have more amyloid pathology, which, we hypothesize, alters isotopic compositions.

Correlation of copper isotope composition with AD biomarkers

Correlation between δ 65Cu and Aß42/40 ratios in CSF

We observed a significant negative correlation between δ 65Cu and Aβ42/40 in AD CSF (Figure 3A). Thus, higher amyloid burden in AD is associated with 6 5Cu enrichment (higher δ 6 5Cu) in CSF (Figure 1A). In contrast, AD brain parenchyma is depleted of 65Cu compared to controls. 7 From our current findings, we consider that Aβ plaques might bind 63Cu preferentially, inducing enrichment of the lighter isotope in AD brain parenchyma, 7 thereby enriching the 65Cu heavy counterpart in the extracellular fluid fraction (CSF). Cu(II) binds to three histidine residues in the N‐terminal region (Aβ16), 30 and histidine residues themselves do not exhibit specific binding preferences for Cu(II) isotopes. 31 Thus, the possibility that Aβ might preferentially bind 63Cu, hence withdrawing it from the extracellular fluid, awaits experimental confirmation.

In contrast, CSF markers of tangle pathology (t‐tau, and p‐tau181) were not associated with CSF‐δ 65Cu (Figure S5) despite their marked elevation in the AD cohort compared to the NC cohort (Table 1). This increases the likelihood that the amyloid pathology is the specific proteinopathy that might account for the δ 65Cu departures in AD (Figure 1A). Additionally, there was no correlation between Aβ42/40 ratio and either t‐tau or p‐tau181 (Figure S6).

However, the isotopic changes observed in our study may also reflect broader Cu transport and coordination processes occurring within the brain in AD or changes in CSF or blood composition. For example, while Cu and Zn accumulate within Aβ plaques, 11 proteomic analyses also describe changes in metallothionein (MT) isoforms MT‐1/2 and MT‐3 in AD. 32 , 33 A particularly strong link exists for MT‐3, which is highly expressed in the central nervous system (CNS) and interacts directly with metal‐loaded Aβ:cell‐free studies show that Zn‐loaded MT‐3 can remove Cu from Aβ‐Cu(II) complexes via a “metal‐swap” reaction, reducing Cu(II) to Cu(I) and thereby suppressing Aβ‐driven reactive oxygen species production and toxicity. 34 This could support a neuroprotective role for MT‐3 against the oxidative stress induced by Cu‐mediated Aβ pathology. Experimental and theoretical studies demonstrate that 6 3Cu preferentially binds to soft, sulfur‐donor ligands, 35 , 36 , 37 whereas 6 5Cu is enriched in complexes involving nitrogen‐ or oxygen‐based coordination environments typical of Aβ. Therefore, the 6 3Cu enrichment observed in brain tissue, with corresponding depletion in CSF, could arise from preferential binding to sulfur‐rich proteins, particularly MTs, or from Cu transport processes into and out of the brain, rather than exclusively from direct Cu coordination with Aβ.

Another possibility is that the coordination of Cu by serum or CSF proteins is altered, possibly by oxidative stress. Elevated CSF acute phase reactants, like ceruloplasmin, a major Cu‐coordinating protein, are associated with AD 38 and may perturb the average coordination chemistry of Cu in the fluid.

Relationship between serum δ 65Cu and PET centiloid score

For the AD samples from the AIBL cohort (Table S4), we observed a negative trend (p = 0.083) between serum δ 6 5Cu and amyloid Centiloid burden (Figure 3B), decreasing proportionally with the accumulation of amyloid plaques. 2 , 27 This serum δ 6 5Cu may, to some extent, reflect amyloid burden in the brain, albeit less directly and less precisely than PET imaging itself. We hypothesize that neuritic amyloid or congophilic angiopathy (seen in AD but not controls) might selectively obstruct the efflux of 65Cu from the brain to the serum or the influx to the brain of the 63Cu isotope, with either possibility inducing a lower proportion of the heavier 65Cu, which is normally enriched in healthy serum (Figures 2 and 3B).

However, we also observed the opposite trend (p = 0.058) between serum δ 6 5Cu and PET Centiloid values for HC subjects (Figure 3B). Notably, even within healthy cohorts, PET Centiloid values are not uniformly zero but display measurable variability that reflects early or subthreshold amyloid deposition observed during normal aging. Cognitively unimpaired individuals can show Centiloid values up to 20 to 30 without meeting diagnostic thresholds for AD. 39 In non‐diseased individuals, Centiloid score might reflect low‐level or diffuse amyloid deposition associated with normal aging rather than pathological accumulation. Such early amyloid changes may influence biochemical processes differently from the neuritic plaque pathology seen in AD. As a result, the isotopic biomarker may respond differently from the underlying mechanisms in the two groups, one reflecting normal aging processes and the other reflecting disease‐specific amyloid pathology, leading to a reversal of what could be a physiological association. Larger datasets are warranted to validate these intriguing trends.

Blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction is recognized as a key component of AD pathophysiology, with several studies demonstrating increased permeability and disruption of neurovascular integrity in patients with AD. 40 , 41 This could also impact the bidirectional exchange of metal species between the CNS and the plasma, perturbing isotopic fluxes.

Overall copper levels in CSF and serum AD and controls

Similarly to other studies, 42 , 43 we observed no changes in the total copper levels in the CSF with AD (Figure 1B). According to these results, while they might perturb Cu isotopic distribution, Aβ plaques do not affect homeostatic levels of total copper transfer from the brain to the CSF. Future in vitro studies of the impact of synthetic Aβ on Cu isotope enrichment would be beneficial to provide further insight into the operative mechanisms.

Furthermore, no significant differences in total copper serum concentrations between AD subjects and HCs were identified (Figure 2D). Some studies reported higher total serum copper concentrations in AD, 44 , 45 whereas others described lower levels. 8 The unchanged total Cu pool does not preclude isotopic fractionation due to perturbed Cu–protein coordination, which is predominantly by ceruloplasmin in serum. Notably, in the baseline and 18‐month cohorts of AIBL, the total serum Cu was unchanged in AD (n = 186) compared to HCs (n = 695), but the non‐ceruloplasmin bound fraction of Cu (representing ∼5% of total serum Cu) was significantly decreased, 46 potentially contributing the isotopic changes observed. Inconsistencies across the literature suggest that total copper levels may be highly variable among individuals and influenced by cohort‐specific factors and that total concentration alone may not be a reliable marker for AD status.

CSF and serum δ 65Cu values overlap between Alzheimer's and controls: future perspectives

Our results regarding the differences in CSF and serum δ 65Cu values between AD and controls are significant (respectively p = 0.02 and p = 0.0002) (Figures 1A and 2C). ROC analysis reports good diagnostic discrimination with AUCs of 0.8 and a Youden's J of 0.5 SD (Figures 1C and 2E). Further sampling is needed to determine the accuracy of the values as diagnostic markers. However, this study is the first to jointly evaluate Cu isotopes in both CSF and serum together with established amyloid biomarkers (Aβ42/40 and amyloid PET) and may therefore provide additional diagnostic insights. Adding more parameters, e.g., by incorporating different isotope systems such as K, Zn, and Ca, might also improve biomarker performance. 47

Future studies would benefit from comparing multiple isotopic systems in parallel, as such an approach may provide deeper insights into the underlying biochemical mechanisms.

Serum and CSF Cu stable isotopes as medical biomarkers

Other recent studies highlighted stable copper isotopic compositions (δ 65Cu) in human serum as biomarkers, such as lower δ 65Cu values in the serum of breast and colorectal cancer patients compared to healthy controls, 48 or that patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) exhibited serum enriched in 63Cu, while tumor tissues were relatively enriched in 65Cu. 49

The main limitation of this study remains the limited number of patients and the variability of their profiles. We also considered the impact of age because our previous work had found that δ 65Cu increased in the aging mouse brain between 6 and 12 months (n = 30). 17 We found no relationship between age and serum or CSF δ 65Cu in our current cohorts (Figure S1A, C, and E). This discrepancy may reflect the greater genetic homogeneity of laboratory mice and the lack of age equivalence between mouse and human cohorts.The mice were 20 to 50 years old in human equivalency, whereas our human cohorts ranged from ∼50 to 85 years. The aged‐dependent elevation in brain δ 65Cu might plateau after age 50 in humans. There were no sex differences, 17 which was consistent with our current findings (Figure S1B and E).

CONCLUSIONS

We have shown that in the human CSF and serum, the natural copper isotope ratio could depend on brain amyloid burden. The CSF of AD patients is enriched in 65Cu compared to controls, mirroring the enrichment in 63Cu in the brain of AD patients. The serum of AD patients is enriched in 63Cu compared to controls. Moreover, serum δ 65Cu relates to markers of brain amyloid with a negative correlation in AD, while CSF δ 65Cu relates to markers of brain amyloid with a positive correlation in AD. The Cu isotopic signature of the CSF might derive from the accumulation of isotopically light 63Cu in the Aβ‐plaque pathology of AD brain tissue.
