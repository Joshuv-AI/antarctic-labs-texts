Evolution of cross-tolerance to metals in yeast
Authors: Anna L. Bazzicalupo, Penelope C. Kahn, Eully Ao, Joel Campbell, Sarah P. Otto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceOrganisms in a changing environment often face shifts along multiple environmental axes. Being able to predict which combination of environmental changes are likely to allow for species survival and which elevate the risk of extinction is important for protecting the environment. We conduct evolutionary experiments in which yeast face toxic concentrations of metals and must adapt or go extinct. The lines that survive are then tested for their ability to tolerate other environmental changes. We identify metals that accumulate adaptive mutations that often enable tolerance to other metals and those that rarely do. We also identify multiple independent mutations in specific pathways that facilitate tolerance to other metals, providing insights into the processes that promote survival across environments.

Organisms evolving in the presence of a particular stressor accumulate genetic changes that, while beneficial in that stressor, may have pleiotropic effects that positively or negatively impact fitness in other environments (1). In an increasingly human-altered world, predicting cross-tolerance or cross-intolerance to other environmental stressors is important for both conservation and environmental regulation, providing information about whether adaptation would be easy or challenging when populations face a combination of stressors. Jerison et al. (2) predict that there should be a relationship between the degree of environmental similarity and the amount of cross-tolerance expected. Tracking yeast undergoing experimental evolution in eleven environments, Jerison et al. (2) found, however, that chance played a major role in the probability of generating specialists and generalists, depending on the frequency and fitness costs of mutations providing narrow versus broad tolerance.

Here, we develop and test three hypotheses for the extent of cross-tolerance, using heavy metals as an ecologically relevant class of stressors. Different metals often co-occur in the environment at high levels, especially as industrialization and technological innovation over the past century have led to a massive rise in heavy metal pollution (3, 4). Heavy metals induce diverse stresses that affect a variety of physiological responses, and exposure can be lethal (5). Metal ions can stimulate the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via Fenton-like reactions; these ROS have the potential to damage membranes, proteins, and DNA, becoming lethal when concentrations reach high levels (3). Humans frequently take advantage of the toxic properties of metals, using elevated concentrations for microbial control (e.g., in wine production) (6). Metal pollutants, especially lead and mercury, have been recognized for their distinctly negative effects on human health, leading to global efforts to reduce their rates of use and release into the environment (7, 8). Despite these recent efforts, metals continue to be emitted to the biosphere via mining, smelting, leaching from plastics and electronics in landfills, agricultural runoff, automobiles, and roadwork (3).

Several metals, such as zinc, cobalt, nickel, copper, and manganese, act as micronutrients in the eukaryotic cell, essential in trace amounts for proper cellular function but toxic at high concentrations (9). As micronutrients cannot be completely excluded from cells (10), fine-tuned regulatory systems have evolved to ensure a balance between heavy metal deficiency and excess. Systems to maintain metal ion homeostasis include transporters for import and export, chelators for metal binding, and antioxidants to counteract the main mechanism of metal toxicity, oxidative stress. Evolution of metal resistance can occur rapidly, with adaptations involving several gene pathways and physiological mechanisms (11–14). For example, strains of E. coli evolved in a high concentration of silver nanoparticles gain mutations impacting ion sensing, nucleotide biosynthesis, and transcription (11). Other adaptations include copy number variation in metal-chelating enzymes [e.g., the copper metallothionein gene, CUP1 (15)], aneuploidy (16, 17) and changes in mitochondrial function (18).

Despite some similarities in their effects (e.g., ROS production), exposure to different metals can induce dramatically different consequences. Contact with metallic copper induces extensive cytoplasmic membrane damage in both Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Candida albicans (19). Different metals alter the rate and spectrum of mutations to varying degrees (20). Cadmium toxicity leads to recombinagenic activity, induces deletion mutations via oxidative stress (21), and inhibits mismatch repair of small misalignments (22). Cobalt is mutagenic to mitochondrial DNA but less so to nuclear DNA (23–25). Nickel can cause changes in chromatin structure that alter gene expression (26). Differences in the mechanism of harm of these metals suggest that adaptive mutations selected in one environment may not improve tolerance to other metals. On the other hand, yeast have evolved some general metal response strategies that may allow adaptive mutations with broader pleiotropic benefits across metals [e.g., chelators or transporters that act on multiple metals (27)].

To investigate cross-tolerance, we first generated metal-adapted yeast lines using an experimental design whereby populations were exposed to a single metal at a concentration slightly too high to permit growth, monitoring whether beneficial mutations arose that allowed growth (“evolutionary rescue”). Specifically, we exposed replicate populations of S. cerevisiae to five essential metal cofactors (cobalt, copper, manganese, nickel, and zinc) and one toxic metal (cadmium) at concentrations that caused most populations to go extinct but allowed for evolutionary rescue through rare mutations (Fig. 1). We then studied the prevalence and genetic basis of cross-tolerance and cross-intolerance among these evolved lines (Fig. 1).

We found that evolved cross-tolerance was not well predicted by any of these measures. Instead, the identity of the metal inducing evolutionary rescue was the biggest predictor of cross-tolerance, with evolution in cobalt frequently leading to generalists that better tolerated many metals (cross-tolerance), and evolution in manganese leading to specialists that, on average, performed poorly across metals (cross-intolerance). We also found evidence that mutations in three genes (PHO84, SIW14, VTC4), which are involved in the phosphate metabolism pathway (PHO), occurred multiple times and were associated with significantly greater cross-tolerance than mutations in other genes. Other repeatedly mutated genes were not, however, associated with greater cross-tolerance, indicating that only specific genetic pathways contributed substantially to cross-tolerance. Thus, the particular sequence of environments and genetic pathways shaped cross-tolerance of adapted lines to multiple metal stressors.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Evolution.

For detailed methodology, see SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods. In brief, yeast populations had an ancestral W303 genetic background and were derived from an isogenic MATalpha haploid line (colony isolate) obtained from McDonald et al. (2016) (“OLY077”). 240 single colonies were isolated from this stock, with each colony used to inoculate separate wells in three 96-deep well plates containing 1 mL of YPAD. These starter plates were then allowed to grow overnight in a continuously shaking incubator at 30 °C, from which samples were placed in three deep well plates for each metal at a concentration that did not permit growth.

To determine concentrations of each metal beyond which growth was not typically observed, we generated concentration–response curves using a sample of the ancestral stock, which was cultured, subdivided, and exposed to a series of increasing metal concentrations (see Fig. 1 for concentrations used in the experiment).

Having inoculated the deep-well plates with these metals with 10 µL of starter culture (~80,000-100,000 cells), the plates were shaken (200 rpm) at 30 °C for 14 d. All wells were checked daily, with growth recorded when settled cells were observed at the bottom of the well. Because this initial growth was often faint (<1 mm), we then allowed 24 h to confirm growth, after which the well was manually mixed and sampled for freezer stocks.

To screen for genetic adaptations (as opposed to physiological or plastic responses), we checked whether metal resistance was retained following a passage of 2 to 3 days in YPAD. Lines that did not grow following a return to the experimental metal concentration were discarded. We also tested each of the evolved lines for respiratory mutations by spot plating them onto glycerol plates (which require respiration for growth) and YPAD agar plates. Those that were able to grow on YPAD but not glycerol plates were categorized as having a petite phenotype.

Fitness Measurements.

All growth assays were performed using a Bioscreen C, a microbiology workstation that automatically tracks growth trajectories by measuring turbidity of liquid cultures in 100-well Bioscreen plates at regular intervals using wide band filters (Oy Growth Curves Ab Ltd., 2009), with automated shaking of plates at 30 °C between readings. Throughout, the fitness measure that we use is the maximum mitotic growth rate obtained by calculating the maximum slope of a smoothed (“loess”) fit to the log of the growth curves obtained from one well in the Bioscreen C (see details in ref. 12 and https://github.com/joelkcamp/crossTolerance_bazzicalupoEtAl for code).

Evolved lines that grew in deep-well plates often failed to grow in the shallow-well Bioscreen C plates at the evolution concentrations for each metal (Dataset S1A), indicating that the shallow wells induced a harsher metal environment [also seen by Gerstein et al. (12)], potentially due to differences in aeration. We thus developed different “test concentrations” for each metal in the Bioscreen C plates by reducing the metal from the evolution concentration by 15% (Fig. 1; some test environments remained harsher than others, see Discussion).

We measured maximum growth rates of evolved lines using samples of evolved populations, scraped by pipet from frozen stock and grown in liquid YPAD at 30 °C overnight. We exposed these cultures (in triplicate) to the test concentrations of all six metals and measured maximum growth rates, yielding a dataset that allows observation of the pleiotropic effects of adaptive mutations to different metal environments in vivo.

Using maximum growth rates relative to the ancestor grown in the same environment, we devised two quantitative measures of cross-tolerance to compare evolved lines: narrow and broad-scale cross-tolerance. For a pair of metals, we define the narrow-scale cross-tolerance as CT(X,Y) = (r¯ Y|X/r¯ X|X + r¯ X|Y/r¯ Y|Y)/2, where r¯ Y|X is the average relative maximum growth rate in test environment Y given an evolution environment in X (Dataset S1B panel B). Broad-scale cross-tolerance CT(X) was calculated from the relative maximum growth rate of each line, averaged across all test environments excluding that line’s evolution environment X (Dataset S1B panel A).

Testing Predictors of Cross-Tolerance.

We expected that narrow-scale cross-tolerance CT(X,Y) would be higher for pairs of metal environments that were more similar. We thus developed predictions using three different measures of similarity between pairs of metals, and tested these predictions against our observations of CT(X,Y) using regression models in R.

Prediction I: Similarity in reactivity of metals predicts cross-tolerance.

Metal ions have various physical properties that may predict similarities among metals in the stress caused to yeast cells. We focused on similarities in oxidation-reduction potential (ORP; unit Eh) to quantify the potential of a medium to transfer electrons (29) and induce oxidative stress. We used this as a proxy for the physicochemical impact of each metal, measuring five replicates for each metal under the same “experiment concentrations” used in the deep-well plates (Dataset S1A and see SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods for measurement details).

Prediction II: Similarity in the sets of genes known to affect metal sensitivity predicts cross-tolerance.

In this analysis, we predicted pleiotropy from gene annotations in the Saccharomyces Genome Database (SGD) (28). A database query for the observable trait “metal resistance” (APO:0000090) yielded a table of annotations from classical genetic studies and systematic mutation sets describing the effects of different mutations on the fitness of yeast in different metals (downloaded from SGD on May 29, 2025, and filtered in R for results relevant to the six metals used in this study).

To account for variation in search effort among metals (i.e., different number of results in the database), the quantitative measure for prediction II (“proportion of positive pleiotropy”) divides the number of genes with positive pleiotropy (overlap in whether function/activity of the gene promotes or inhibits resistance) by the total number of genes in common (i.e., genes with data for both metals). See SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods for details. We expect a positive relationship between the proportion of genes exhibiting positive pleiotropy for two metals in SGD (Dataset S1L) and the cross-tolerance CT(X,Y) for these two metals.

Prediction III: Similarity in metal abundance across environments predicts cross-tolerance.

Because metal pairs that co-occur often in the environment might select for mechanisms that confer tolerance to both metals (30), we predicted that cross-tolerance might evolve more often for metals that positively covary in the environment.

To measure co-occurrence between metal pairs, we downloaded soil data from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) and calculated the correlation in metal concentrations across sites (see SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods for details). We consider these data to represent a plausible set of environments experienced over the full evolutionary history of eukaryotes (not the specific habitats of S. cerevisiae).

Genomic Sequencing.

We extracted DNA and sequenced the genomes of the ancestral strain plus 109 experimentally evolved yeast lines (16 cadmium lines, 16 cobalt lines, 15 copper lines, 22 manganese lines, 17 nickel lines, and 23 zinc lines). As our goal was to characterize cross-tolerance of metal adapted lines, we sought to simplify the analysis of genomic data by sequencing a random colony isolate from each rescued population. Although we are unable to assess genetic diversity within lines, we expect this to be low given the initially isogenic stocks, short duration of the evolution experiment (average of 9.3 d), and the rarity of rescue (average 8% of wells). After processing and filtering (SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods), mutations in protein-coding genes, as identified by SnpEff (31), were retained (see below for intergenic mutations). A total of 449 genic mutations were observed across all lines, involving 408 unique mutations (Dataset S1 B and C).

Gene Ontology Analysis.

We summarized the underlying genetic mechanisms for metal tolerance by analyzing GO terms related to biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components using the GO Slim Mapper tool (https://www.yeastgenome.org/goSlimMapper) in SGD (see Dataset S1D panel A for GO Terms used in analyses downstream). To formally test overrepresentation of GO terms, we used the GO Term Finder tool in SGD for all evolved lines except the highly mutated lines MnBM42 (102 genic mutations) and MnBM14 (36 genic mutations) to avoid skewing the results by these two lines (see SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods for details).

We also annotated each mutation according to whether the gene is known to affect metal resistance (APO:0000090, 1791 genes, SGD, list downloaded on 23 May 2025), phosphorus metabolism (GO:0006793, 480 genes, Amigo 2 (32), list downloaded on 17 June 2025), or repair (GO:0006281, 322 genes, Amigo 2 (32), list downloaded on 17 June 2025).

Parallel Mutations Analysis.

Adaptation to a given heavy metal often involved the same gene (Dataset S1C). We performed randomizations using the logic of Gerstein et al. (12) to calculate the probability of seeing so many genes hit multiple times, given the total number of mutations observed in each metal and the 6,607 genes in the yeast genome. Specifically, we randomized the observed number of mutations across genes and compared the mean number of mutations per gene bearing a mutation to the means from 1,000 randomized datasets (see SI Appendix, S2 Mathematica file for detailed methodology). To be conservative, within each test, identical mutations appearing in multiple lines were counted only once, as some mutations may have been inherited from the starting culture (one well in each metal descended from the same colony, observed in four cases affecting two metals each, SI Appendix, S2) and cross-contamination may have occurred. These calculations allowed us to test whether there was significant parallel use of the same gene(s) during evolutionary rescue in each metal and across all of the metals combined.

Duplication Detection.

We calculated total depth of coverage for each chromosome to estimate and detect chromosomal duplication. We first created a coverage file for each site in each yeast line using the SAMTools pileup program. The files were then processed with a custom Perl script, which adds together the depth of coverage in windows of 1,000 bp. We used the same script to estimate the coverage for the mitochondrial genome of the petite lines in windows of 100 bp.

As CUP1 duplicates fail to align, we estimated a relative copy-number of the CUP1 genes by following the “in silico” protocol (12). The protocol uses the unix command “grep” to count the instances of CUP1 sequences directly in the unaligned fastq files, compared to reference loci (RIX1, DED81, DUR3) also on chromosome VIII. We report estimates of CUP1 compared to the estimated copy-number of our ancestral line OLY077 (18.1 copies relative to the three reference loci).

Intergenic Mutations.

We briefly explored whether intergenic mutations might have contributed to metal adaptation. Intergenic SNPs were filtered using the same criteria as for genic SNPs and were annotated as “MODIFIER” by snpEFF, which listed the position of the mutation as upstream or downstream of nearby genes (often listing several nearby genes). Because the 500 basepairs upstream of a coding gene is considered to be the region most likely to contain cis-regulatory sites in yeast (33) (acknowledging that sites outside of this window may also be regulatory), we subdivided the SNPs into those that were upstream modifiers within 500 bp and those that were upstream modifiers further than 500 bp. We then measured the fraction of upstream modifiers that were associated with genes known to have an impact on metal tolerance, using the set of genes from SGD found experimentally to impact resistance to one or more metals (“metal resistance”, APO:0000090). The fractions of metal-associated genes among close intergenic SNPs (≤500 bp), further intergenic SNPs (>500 bp), and genic SNPs were then compared by χ2 tests.

Results

Evolutionary Rescue, Fitness Profiles, and Cross-Tolerance.

Our goal was to isolate adaptive mutations to each metal by minimizing the time over which evolutionary rescue could occur, thereby avoiding the accumulation of nonadaptive mutations that would obscure the results (e.g., confusing linkage effects with cross-tolerance effects). On average, the lines described in this experiment were detected and isolated after 9.3 days of exposure to high metal concentrations (SD = 2.9).

Out of 1,440 total founding populations (240 replicates × 6 metals), rescue was observed a total of 152 times. Of these, 120 evolved lines were confirmed to grow in deep-well plates at the metal concentration in which they had evolved (Fig. 1 and Dataset S1A) and were subject to further experimentation (21 cadmium, 16 cobalt, 15 copper, 22 manganese, 17 nickel, and 29 zinc lines). The average rate of rescue was 8% (SD = 2%) and was similar across metal environments.

Fitness assays (as measured by maximum growth rate) of all evolved lines and the ancestor in different environments reveal interesting patterns of cross-tolerance and intolerance (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Fig. 2A displays the maximum growth rates obtained in the Bioscreen C for all lines in each metal at the test concentrations (Dataset S1A). The maximum growth rate of the ancestor was similarly low in all environments (gray dots). With few exceptions, all metal-evolved lines still grew better in YPAD than in the metal environment to which they had adapted, indicating that the metals remained harmful to the evolved lines.

The remaining panels show growth rates of each line (connected dots) evolved in a given metal: (D) cadmium, (E) cobalt, (F) copper, (G) manganese, (H) nickel, (I) zinc, with shading highlighting the lines’ performance in their evolution environment.

Fig. 2B displays the average fitness of lines evolved in each metal environment relative to the performance of the ancestor in that environment. In general, lines evolved in a particular metal performed as well or better in that test environment than those evolved in a different metal. Regardless of the evolution environment, metal-evolved lines commonly demonstrated cross-tolerance in manganese and nickel environments (Tukey’s HSD; P < 0.05 for 6/6 mean comparisons of evolved-ancestor in Mn; P < 0.05 for 5/6 mean comparisons of evolved-ancestor in Ni), whereas very few demonstrated cross-tolerance in cobalt and zinc (Tukey’s HSD; P < 0.05 for 1/6 mean comparisons of evolved-ancestor in Co; P < 0.05 for 1/6 mean comparisons of evolved-ancestor in Zn), although growth was generally less in these two test environments (see Discussion), which reduced power to detect tolerance.

In Fig. 2C, the growth rate of an evolved line is displayed relative to its own growth in the metal of the evolution environment. This highlights that some metal-evolved lines performed well across environments (generalists), such as most cobalt lines. By contrast, other metal-evolved lines were more specialized, performing well primarily in the environment to which they had evolved, such as many manganese lines. When tested in permissive conditions (YPAD), generalist lines tended to grow better than specialist lines.

When assayed in permissive media (YPAD), most metal-evolved lines (98 of 120, 82%) had significantly slower growth than the ancestor (left-most points in Fig. 2 D−I; Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.05), indicating the existence of trade-offs between tolerance to high metal concentrations and growth under normal conditions. Replicate lines with similar growth rates in their evolution environment often differed in their tolerance to other metals, as demonstrated by crisscrossing lines in the fitness profiles (for example, three Mn lines – those with mutations in PDR1 and PDR10, involved in drug tolerance—performed well in Cd, while others did not).

Predicting Cross-Tolerance.

We expected that cross-tolerance would be greater for metals that were more similar. We proposed and tested three measures of metal similarity (values given in Dataset S1L and Fig. 3 A, C, and E, raw data in GitHub link): I. similarity in metal reactivity (ORP), measured as the difference between the lowest and highest Eh value for each metal pair; II. overlap of known metal-resistance genes in SGD, measured as the number of overlapping genes known to affect resistance in the same direction; III. correlation in the concentration of metal pairs in environmental samples, based on USGS soil core records. In no case were these predictors significantly associated with narrow-scale CT(X,Y) (the amount of cross-tolerance between pairs of metals), when tested with a regression analysis (Fig. 3).

These similarity indices do not account for the order of exposure, such that lines evolved in metal A and tested in metal B are predicted to have the same amount of cross-tolerance as lines evolved in metal B and tested in metal A. According to our results, cross-tolerance is not generated reciprocally for metal pairs. For example, the copper-adapted lines performed well in manganese, however manganese-adapted lines performed poorly in copper (Fig. 2C). Similarly, cadmium-adapted lines performed well in nickel, but nickel-adapted lines did not show enhanced performance in cadmium.

Thus, even with similar metal stressors, the evolution of cross-tolerance is idiosyncratic and depends on the specific mutations that rescued the yeast lines.

Genetic Analysis.

To better understand the genes underlying evolved tolerance and cross-tolerance, we sampled and sequenced a single representative colony for each of 110 lines (109 evolutionary rescue lines and 1 ancestral strain; 11 lines did not yield enough cells for WGS). After filtering out poor quality genotype calls (Materials and Methods), we recorded several types of genomic mutations including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), indels, aneuploidies, copy-number variants, mitochondrial mutants, and intergenic mutations (see Dataset S1B for genic mutations and Dataset S1K for intergenic mutations).

Genic SNPs and indels.

Across all evolved lines, we found a total of 450 SNPs and indels that passed our quality filters, falling in 331 ORFs (Dataset S1C). Two lines had an order of magnitude more mutations than average (MnBM42 with 102 and MnBM14 with 36, see SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Dataset S1B). Manganese is known to be mutagenic (18, 34), and both lines exhibit mutations in DNA repair genes (GO:0006281, Amigo 2 (32), Dataset S1B): one nonsense mutation in FYV6 in MnBM14 and eight repair gene mutations in MnBM42 (six missense mutations in YRA1, RAD26, TAH11, IRC20, RAD17, MLH3 and two synonymous mutations). These two lines are thus potentially interesting examples of mutator phenotypes evolving in the context of a short-term evolutionary rescue experiment, and we leave their investigation to future study.

Excluding these two lines from further analyses, we found a total of 312 high-quality mutations occurring in 210 genes (Dataset S1C). On average, evolved lines had 3 genic mutations, though this varies significantly among lines evolved in different environments (ANOVA, P = 3 × 10−9; mean ± SE number of genic mutations: 3.63 ± 0.59 in Cd; 5.38 ± 0.5 in Co; 2.33 ± 0.4 in Cu; 3.55 ± 0.62 in Mn; 1.41 ± 0.32 in Ni; 1.65 ± 0.2 in Zn). Our experiment was designed to minimize the number of mutations per line by sampling soon after growth was detected. The purpose of this design was to increase our ability to link mutations with observed cross-tolerance, but because many lines had more than one mutation, we must be cautious in assigning causality to mutations (e.g., seeking additional evidence from independent mutations in a gene, see “Parallel evolution” below).

For most of the 109 sequenced lines, we found at least one mutation that could account for the increased metal tolerance based on information in SGD for each gene (as detailed below and in Dataset S1B). There were eleven lines for which we could not confidently detect any genic mutations despite high average sequence coverage, although three were aneuploid and one was petite (Dataset S1B). Seven of these lines had intergenic mutations (Dataset S1K), but only two of these were upstream of genes known to affect metal resistance (upstream of COF1 in CuBM16 and upstream of LGE1 in ZnBM25). We emphasize that structural mutations or mutations in hard to align regions are missing from our analyses of the short-read sequence data and may have contributed to metal adaptation.

Gene ontology terms.

In addition to single gene annotations, we summarized our dataset by looking for broad patterns of significant overrepresentation of GO terms for cellular components, biological processes, and molecular functions. Analysis of the genes mutated in the rescued lines revealed significant overrepresentation of several GO terms (Fig. 4 and Dataset S1 B, (B)) including: “fungal-type vacuole membrane,” “incipient cellular bud site,” and “vacuolar transporter chaperone complex” among cellular components; “response to stimulus,” “biological regulation,” “cytokinetic process,” and “polyphosphate biosynthetic process” among biological processes; and “phosphotransferase activity,” “ubiquitin-protein transferase activity,” and “MAP-kinase scaffold activity” among molecular functions.

We then asked whether particular GO terms were associated with cross-tolerance. The lines with the highest broad cross-tolerance (top 25%, “generalist”) were significantly associated with a number of molecular functions, including kinase activity, transferase activity, and ubiquitin ligase activity, while the lines with the lowest broad cross-tolerance (bottom 25%, “specialist”) had no associated GO terms for biological processes or molecular functions but tended to affect the plasma membrane (Dataset S1E).

Different metals tended to accumulate mutations affecting different processes (Dataset S1F). For example, we found that mutations in the vacuolar transporter chaperone complex were overrepresented in cobalt, incipient cellular bud site in manganese, symporters in nickel, and cell signaling in zinc (Dataset S1F).

The vacuole was repeatedly identified in the adaptive response to metals, as also found by Ruotolo et al. (35). We found eleven independent mutations in four of the five vacuolar transporter chaperone genes (2 in VTC1, 1 in VTC3, 4 in VTC4, and 4 in VTC5). In addition, the mutation effect size predicted by SnpEff differed significantly for genes associated with the vacuole (GO:0005773) and the vacuolar transporter chaperone complex (GO:0033254, which highly overlaps with genes in the “polyphosphate biosynthetic process” GO:0006799) (χ2 tests, P < 0.001, Dataset S1G), with a disproportionately high number of frameshift and stop-codon mutations in the VTC genes (“HIGH” effect, Dataset S1C).

The VTC genes, as well as three genes (PHO84, SIW14, VTC4) bearing multiple mutations that affect the phosphate metabolism pathway PHO, are all components of a parent GO term “phosphorus metabolic process” [GO:0006793, Amigo 2 (32)]. Lines with mutations specifically in this GO term exhibited significantly more cross-tolerance across metals (broad cross-tolerance mean ± SE: 0.043 ± 0.0046 versus 0.032 ± 0.0026, P = 0.043), consistent with a role of these processes in growth across metals (Discussion).

Parallel evolution.

We next examined genes that accumulated independent mutations, which provides strong evidence for selection. Identical mutations at the exact same site were not considered independent of one another and were counted only once. We recorded 37 genes with independent mutations in multiple lines (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, S2 and Dataset S2), 13 of which were mutated three or more times (Dataset S1H). When testing whether this was a significantly unusual result, we conservatively excluded the highly mutated MnBM14 and MnBM42 lines, to avoid these lines having a disproportionate influence, leaving 24 genes with independent mutations, 9 of which were hit three or more times.

We then tested whether parallel mutations involving the same genes occurred more often than expected by chance in each metal. Copper lines did not accumulate any independent mutations in the same gene, but CUP1 showed parallel gene duplication in many of these lines (see “Amplification of CUP1” below). For lines evolved in each other metal, significantly more genes bore multiple mutations than expected by chance, with the mean number of mutations observed per mutated gene being higher than found in any of the 1000 randomization tests performed, with each test randomly shuffling the observed mutations for that metal among all yeast genes (SI Appendix, S2). Similarly, considering all metals together, the mean number of mutations per gene bearing a mutation was higher than all 1,000 randomizations, indicating strong evidence for parallel mutations contributing to adaptation in these lines.

We next examined features of the thirteen highly mutated genes that bore three or more independent mutations. To consider more genes in these analyses, we included mutations in MnBM14 and MnBM42 (Dataset S1H). Many of these mutations play a role in transport across membranes (Dataset S1H). Of note, PTK2 is required for the activation of PMA1 (36), which is essential for proton transport across the plasma-membrane (a P2-type H+-ATPase), and PHO84 produces a plasma membrane protein involved in transport of inorganic phosphates with some activity as a dicationic metal transporter (37). We then asked whether mutations in these highly mutated genes contributed to broader cross-tolerance. There was no clear association with tolerance (Fig. 5B), but mutations in three particular genes (PHO84, SIW14, VTC4) had significantly broader cross-tolerance than lines lacking mutations in any highly mutated gene (P < 0.05, t-tests, see Fig. 5B). These three genes all affect phosphate metabolism and homeostasis via the PHO pathway (Discussion). Mutations in VTC4 and SIW14 only arose in cobalt, while PHO84 arose in cobalt, nickel, and zinc, suggesting that the high fitness of lines evolved in cobalt (Fig. 2) comes, in part, from independent mutations at these highly mutated genes. By contrast, mutations in the phosphatase gene, CDC25, which controls cell cycle progression, arose at 13 different sites, only in manganese lines, but were associated with significantly narrower cross-tolerance (t test, P = 0.002, see Fig. 5B).

Some of the genes that repeatedly acquired mutations were also observed in the evolutionary rescue study of Gerstein et al. (12) with copper. They observed multiple (≥2) mutations in four genes: VTC1, VTC4, PMA1, and MAM3 [which plays a role in mitochondrial organization and vacuolar sequestration and whose protein localizes to the vacuolar membrane (28)]. We also observed multiple mutations in these four genes (Dataset S1H), but typically involving other metals besides copper (except for one of the eight independent PMA1 mutations).

While the above included parallel mutations that arose in a single metal, we turn now to mutations that arose independently in at least two different metals (SI Appendix, S2 and Dataset S2), as these genes are most likely to contribute to cross-tolerance. Eleven genes accumulated mutations in more than one metal (DNF1, FYV10, KSP1, MAM3, PDR1, PHO84, PMA1, PTK2, SFP1, VTC5, YLR296W), with six additional genes if we include the mutator lines MnBM14 and MnBM42 (HUL5, PPQ1, SNT2, TOM1, YDL199C, YOR1). This is a highly significant degree of parallelism, even counting parallel mutations accumulated in the same metal only once (SI Appendix, S2 and Dataset S2). The lines bearing these mutations were significantly more associated with the “metal resistance” phenotype (APO:0000090; 38 out of 44 lines, versus 37 out of 54 for the remaining lines, χ2 test, P = 0.038), as expected. These lines were not, however, significantly more cross-tolerant (broad cross-tolerance mean ± SE: 0.040 ± 0.003, versus 0.034 ± 0.003, P = 0.23, t test), despite these mutations arising in multiple metals.

Potential mutagenic effects of metals.

Exposure to metals at high concentrations can have a direct effect on the rate and spectrum of mutations (20). We found that the mutational spectra for lines evolved in three of the six metals (cadmium, cobalt, and manganese) were significantly different from the mutational spectrum for yeast reported by Lynch et al. (38) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), with a disproportionate number of G:C → A:T transitions (χ2 goodness of fit test: cadmium P = 0.0001, cobalt P = 0.0009, and manganese P = 8 × 10−5, MnBM42 P = 7 × 10−6, MnBM14 P = 2 × 10−7). Gerstein et al. (12) also did not detect an effect of copper on the mutational spectrum, although effects have been found in phage (39).

The number of genic mutations per line was also higher in these three metals (3.63 in Cd, 5.38 in Co, and 3.55 Mn, excluding MnBM42 and MnBM14) than the other metals (2.33 in Cu, 1.41 in Ni, 1.65 in Zn). Although these differences may reflect a direct mutagenic effect of cadmium, cobalt, and manganese, we note that indirect effects may also be possible, as mutations in “repair” genes [GO:0006281, Amigo 2 (32)] occurred in some of these lines (2/16 Cd, 3/16 Co, 3/15 Cu, 6/22 Mn sequenced lines), but not in Ni (0/12) or Zn (0/23) (Datasets S1 B and C). Some lines also evolved longer in their metal before rescue (Dataset S1B), allowing more time for mutations to accumulate.

Deletion mutations from this study predominantly arose in cadmium. Of the total deletions we identified [61] half were in cadmium lines [31], 13 in manganese lines (one of which was in MnBM42), eight in cobalt lines, six in zinc lines, three in nickel lines, and zero in copper lines. Consistent with this result, in vitro studies of S. cerevisiae have shown cadmium to induce deletions via increased recombinagenic activity (21, 40).

Whole-chromosome duplication.

To assess the role of large-scale genomic changes to metal adaptation, we calculated the depth of coverage across the genome and found aneuploidy (whole-chromosome duplication) evolved in all metals except copper (see chromosome coverage of all evolved lines in SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and summary of chromosome coverage in SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Duplications of the same chromosome evolved convergently in nickel, where four evolved lines had duplications in chromosome XIII and XIV, and in cadmium, where three evolved lines had duplications in chromosome II. While aneuploidy did not arise in our copper-adapted lines, Gerstein et al. (12) found several arose in their copper rescue lines, often involving chromosome VIII bearing the CUP1 metallothionein, a difference that may be due to the strains used (W303 vs BY4741).

To determine whether there was a strong impact of aneuploidy on growth in any metal, we compared the average growth rate of all euploid lines and all aneuploid lines accumulated within our experiment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), as well as lines from the copper-adaptation experiment of Gerstein et al. (12) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). No significant difference in growth rates between euploid and aneuploid cells was observed in any metal (based on t tests), although this is a coarse comparison that does not distinguish between different chromosomal duplications.

Amplification of CUP1.

Gene duplication can also contribute to the evolution of metal resistance, as has been shown previously with repeated amplification of CUP1, a known copper chelator, in several copper-adapted lines (12). We thus examined the fastq files for evidence of CUP1 copy-number variants, relative to the number of copies (~18) estimated in our ancestral strain (Dataset S1I).

We found significantly increased copy-number of CUP1 in seven out of fifteen copper lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B, green highlights in Dataset S1I). Six of the seven lines with an increased CUP1 copy number also had identical SNP mutations in TFG1 and NGG1 (Dataset S1C). While these wells were seeded from different colonies, they were all evolved on the same deep-well plate (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). This suggests that CUP1 amplification in these lines might have occurred through contamination. CUP1 amplification was nevertheless observed in one other well (CuBM15) of the same plate without either the TFG1 or NGG1 mutation (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 and Dataset S1I).

Previous studies have found that the Cup1-p metallothionein can also bind cadmium in vitro and that increased CUP1 expression can increase tolerance to cadmium as well as copper (27, 41). We observed slight increases in CUP1 copy numbers compared to the ancestor in two cadmium lines (21 copies in CdBM45 and CdBM46), one zinc line (21 copies in ZnBM17), and two manganese lines (20 copies in MnBM17 and 21 copies in MnBM27). Curiously, copper lines were not particularly cross-tolerant to cadmium (Fig. 2F) except for CuBM13, which did not exhibit CUP1 amplification. Besides two genic mutations found in other lines from the same plate (NGG1 and RSE1, SI Appendix, Fig. S8), CuBM13 bears an intergenic mutation upstream of the cytoplasmic ubiquitin-protein ligase UBR2, known to influence cadmium tolerance (42) (Dataset S1K).

Mitochondrial genomes.

To test whether mitochondrial function was involved in metal tolerance and cross-tolerance, we tested growth of the 120 metal-evolved populations when glucose was replaced with glycerol, a nonfermentable carbon source. 38 of the metal-evolved lines were unable to grow on glycerol-based media, indicating loss of mitochondrial function during evolutionary rescue. Oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria produces ROS intrinsically (43), potentially contributing to oxidative load when the cell encounters exogenous oxidizers, such as heavy metals. When comparing growth of all grande lines versus all petite lines from our experiment, there is a significant interaction (ANOVA: petite*test environment P = 0.0018, petite P = 0.4, test environment P < 0.0005), with petites performing better, on average, in the metal environments than in YPAD relative to grandes. Examining each metal individually, petites performed significantly better than in YPAD, relative to grandes, in four of the metals (Co: P < 0.005, Cd, Mn, Ni: P < 0.05, ANOVA interaction term). Indeed, petites grew even faster than grandes in cobalt (t test, P-value = 0.02) and marginally faster in manganese (t tests, P-value=0.07) (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A).

All 16 cobalt lines evolved a petite phenotype, along with 17 of the 22 manganese lines, three copper lines, and two nickel lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). To assess whether the loss of mitochondrial function was causally involved in metal tolerance for cobalt and manganese, we assayed growth of ten petite and ten grande lines obtained from a different experiment (not involving metals) (44). We found no significant effect of the petite phenotype on growth in each metal versus YPAD based on an ANOVA (petite P = 0.7, test environment P = 0.0005, petite*test environment P = 0.9) (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B), although again petites tended to be less costly in cobalt than in YPAD.

These results suggest a potential adaptive benefit of mitochondrial loss, particularly in cobalt. Cobalt and manganese stress are known to induce loss of mitochondrial function in yeast (18), which may have also contributed to the loss of mitochondrial function in our experiment.

We compared coverage across the mitochondrial genome in evolved lines. Cobalt evolved lines showed no trace of mitochondrial DNA. However, petite lines in manganese, copper, and nickel showed extensive loss of mitochondrial genes but a remarkable increase in coverage of some breakpoint regions between respiratory genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). In the cases of partial loss with intermittent amplification, respiration genes were always eliminated (i.e., COX1, COX2, COB), both in our lines and in previous studies (45–47), with high coverage in various noncoding regions. Fragmented mitochondrial genomes have also been reported in previous studies showing that the petite phenotype can arise from recombination events between repeats of GC clusters and AT stretches, especially at highly homologous origins of replication (48, 49).

Intergenic mutations.

The number of intergenic mutations across lines (316) was less than the number of genic mutations (414) after filtering (Dataset S1K). The intergenic fraction was, however, enriched relative to the small proportion of intergenic sites in the S. cerevisiae genome [30%; Dujon (50); P < 0.0001 χ2 goodness of fit test with df = 1], consistent with stronger purifying selection against genic mutations during the evolution experiment. Of the intergenic mutations, 181 lay within the 500 bp upstream of a coding gene (associated with 176 genes), while 132 were further than 500 bp upstream (associated with 115 genes); the remaining three mutations were not upstream “MODIFIERS” according to SnpEff.

Based on the set of genes affecting “metal resistance” (APO:0000090, SGD), we found that genes associated with close upstream mutations (≤ 500 bp) are known to impact metal resistance significantly more often (31.8%, 56/176) than genes associated with more distant upstream mutations (20.9%, 24/115; P = 0.04, χ2 test). Indeed, the genes associated with close upstream mutations were only slightly less associated with metal resistance than the genic mutations that arose in our experiments (36.3%, 121/333, P = 0.31, χ2 test).

Multiple independent sites bore mutations within the 500 bp upstream window for two genes (excluding dubious genes): SET5 and VMA13; only VMA13, a component of the vacuolar H± proton pump, is annotated in SGD as affecting metal resistance. In addition, there were fifteen genes bearing upstream changes within 500 bp that were also observed to carry genic mutations. Seven of these genes affect “metal resistance” (APO:0000090, SGD), including: FLC2 (putative calcium channel), OAF1 (oleate-activated transcription factor), PDA1 (pyruvate dehydrogenase), SYG1 (plasma membrane protein of unknown function), TOP1 (topoisomerase I), UBR2 (cytoplasmic ubiquitin-protein ligase), and VTC5 (vacuolar transporter chaperone complex). The remaining eight were BNA6, GSC2, HSP104, ITC1, NAR1, OCA5, RSE1, YPR078C.

While the phenotypic effects of intergenic mutations were not investigated fully, the stronger association with metal tolerance for nearby mutations (≤ 500 bp) than distant ones, as well as the nature of the neighboring genes (e.g., vacuolar transporters genes and ATPase pumps), suggests that some of these regulatory changes may have contributed to metal tolerance.

Discussion

We tested the ability of yeast to adapt to lethal concentrations of six metals. By replicating populations of an isogenic strain, adaptation was restricted to novel mutations, which were further investigated for pleiotropic effects in other metals. We assayed the cross-tolerance of lines evolved in one stressful metal to other metal environments by linking growth rates across environments to mutations observed through whole-genome sequencing of 109 evolved yeast lines, adopting the methods of previous evolutionary rescue studies to detect first-step mutations (12, 51).

Predicting Cross-Tolerance.

We developed three a priori predictions for cross-tolerance based on the similarity between pairs of metal environments in their physiochemical properties, their genetic overlap, and their co-occurrence in the environment. None of these three measures predicted cross-tolerance values. Instead, the exact order of metals encountered (Fig. 2) and the specific mutations acquired played the largest role in the development of cross-tolerance, highlighting the importance of evolutionary stochasticity, as noted by Jerison et al. (2)

In making these predictions, we assumed that mutations evolved in each metal would have similar cross-tolerance for the other metal within a pair. Instead, our observations show that cross-tolerance does not evolve reciprocally, again highlighting the importance of stochasticity. Even yeast populations evolving in the same stressful environment can accumulate different mutations that confer more or less cross-tolerance (2, 52) (see crisscrossing lines in Fig. 2, e.g., for manganese-adapted lines in panel D).

We expected that the genes involved in metal tolerance would span the many strategies organisms evolve to cope with metal stress such as transporters and increased production of antioxidants (10, 11, 13). Indeed, many genes that accumulated mutations in our experiment align with those in the metal resistance literature (e.g., PMA1 (16, 53–55), PHO84 (37, 56, 57), PTK2 (58), VCX1 (59). In addition, we observed evolutionary change in several genes not known in SGD to affect metal resistance (APO:0000090, SGD; Dataset S1B), some bearing multiple independent mutations. Examples include SNT2, which regulates gene expression in response to oxidative stress from peroxide (mutated in ZnBM41 and MnBM42, Dataset S1C), and YOR1, an ATPase-coupled xenobiotic transmembrane transporter (observed in CdBM32, MnBM14, and MnBM42, Dataset S1C).

Common Molecular Pathways of Evolutionary Rescue in Metals.

Our results suggest a wide spectrum of adaptive routes that yeast can take when evolving in metal environments. One commonly changed mechanism is the general stress-response system (60), whose highly conserved signaling networks, such as PKA, MAPK, and TOR pathways (61), are overrepresented in our analysis of GO terms (Fig. 4 and Dataset S1D). These pathways regulate cell growth in response to environmental and cellular cues (e.g., oxidative/genotoxic/heat stress), allowing resources to be diverted from growth and reproduction toward damage repair and maintenance (62). Loss of function in central components of these pathways (e.g. SFP1 (63), CDC25 (64), PBS2 (65), etc.) may prevent transduction of signals that halt the cell cycle in toxic metal environments, allowing for continued reproduction under otherwise toxic conditions (60).

The stress response system also regulates autophagy and the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), both of which target damaged cellular components for degradation. While these functions are repeatedly targeted in our study (significantly for UPS, but not autophagy), the mutations do not imply a favored direction (up- or down-regulation) for degradation of cellular components in metal environments. For example, the TOR and PKA pathways negatively regulate autophagy, and observed loss-of-function mutations are expected to induce robust autophagy activity (66–68). Conversely, most of our mutations in the ubiquitin–proteasome system are predicted to decrease degradation of the targeted cellular components [i.e., due to their high impact, such as frameshift mutations, in HUL5, BSD2, YLR108C (69)]. Whether degradation rates in our metal-adapted lines rose, removing damaged proteins, or fell, allowing growth despite damage, remains unknown.

We found that lines bearing mutations affecting phosphorus metabolism [GO:0006793, Amigo 2 (32)] were significantly more cross-tolerant than the remaining lines. This gene set includes the vacuolar transporter chaperone genes (VTC) genes, bearing 11 independent mutations, as well as the highly mutated PHO84 and SIW14 genes. These genes are all involved in phosphate metabolism and homeostasis via the PHO pathway. The PHO pathway is repressed under high inorganic phosphate (Pi; inorganic phosphate monomer) levels, leading to degradation of the Pi transporter Pho84p (70). Pho84p also transports metal cations (e.g., Mn2+, Cu2+, Co2+, Zn2+), with loss of function linked to metal resistance and overexpression to metal accumulation (37, 56, 71). Loss of VTC and SIW14 function may suppress PHO84 expression or signal Pho84p degradation. The VTC complex synthesizes polyphosphate (polyP) while translocating it into vacuoles (72), and reduced polyP synthesis increases cytosolic Pi, lowering Pho84p activity (73–75). Siw14p is an inositol phosphatase which modifies inositol pyrophosphates (i.e., transforming IP8 into 1-IP7 and 5-IP7 into IP6), key signaling molecules of the PHO pathway (76). Recent work suggests that the principal signal for activation of the PHO pathway is loss of IP8 (77), so loss of function in SIW14 may maintain IP8 levels such that the PHO pathway remains inactive. Thus, one route to broad cross-tolerance appears to involve transport of metal cations, via PHO.

Environmental Determinants of Cross-Tolerance.

Beyond genetic determinants of cross-tolerance, we find that cross-tolerance depends strongly on the environmental challenges that the metal-adapted lines previously and currently face.

First, some metal environments generate lines that are broadly cross-tolerant (generalists), while others generate lines that can only grow in that environment (specialists). Cobalt lines exhibit the broadest cross-tolerance, on average, followed by cadmium, copper, and zinc lines (Dataset S1B). Interestingly, cobalt lines had disproportionately many petites and mutations affecting “phosphorus metabolism” genes (24.4% of Co lines versus 6.9% for the remaining metal lines, Dataset S1C), including many of the highly mutated genes. By contrast, manganese lines had significantly narrower cross-tolerance. This may stem from manganese inducing less oxidative stress, as it is the least redox active of the metals in this study. Furthermore, Mn2+complexed with orthophosphate (Pi) can scavenge ROS (78, 79), reducing ROS-driven selection pressure. Consequently, adaptation to toxic levels of manganese may require addressing other harmful effects, such as disrupted signaling or cell cycling (potentially affected by the many independent mutations in CDC25) or inhibited protein biosynthesis (e.g., BNI4 mutations) (80). Manganese-adapted lines may thus be less protected from ROS when exposed to other metals.

Second, test environments vary in how permissive they are for growth of lines adapted to other metals (Fig. 2A), despite being similarly challenging for the ancestor (gray dots). In particular, the manganese test environment supported growth for most metal-evolved lines. Reduced ROS levels in manganese, due to lower redox activity and manganese-phosphate antioxidant complexes (56), may have benefited lines from other environments by alleviating the need to counter ROS, allowing them to divert resources toward growth. By contrast, the zinc and especially cobalt test environments exhibited low growth for most metal-evolved lines, including lines adapted to these environments. The low growth of cobalt-adapted and zinc-adapted lines when tested in these metals likely reflects the choice of concentrations when switching from the rescue experiments to the Bioscreen for fitness assays. While we reduced each metal concentration by 15% to accommodate the slower growth in the Bioscreen, we infer that this was insufficient to see the full benefits of adapted lines tested in cobalt and zinc. Consistent with this interpretation, the cobalt-adapted lines and zinc-adapted lines performed well when confirming adaptation in the deep-well plates and also exhibited high growth in other test environments. Beyond this technical reason, we note that lines that grew best in cobalt typically had many mutations or mutations in genes linked to numerous GO terms (Dataset S1J), suggesting cobalt’s widespread cellular impact requires extensive adaptation.

Mutational Spectrum and Copy Number Variation.

Exposure to a stressful environment can increase genetic instability and mutation rates (81). We found that evolution in cadmium, cobalt, and manganese altered the mutational spectrum, increasing G:C → A:T transitions. Cadmium also increased deletions rates. While most lines had few mutations, we detected two “mutator” lines (MnBM42 and MnBM14) bearing a high number of mutations, likely due to mutations in key repair genes.

In addition to point mutations, additional strategies for metal adaptation include increased copy number of resistance genes, either through aneuploidies or single gene duplications. Whole-chromosome duplications evolved in several metals (e.g., chromosome XIII in nickel and zinc, chromosome II in cadmium and zinc, Dataset S1B). While it is possible that having extra copies of certain genes may be beneficial in some circumstances, our evidence does not suggest that these chromosome duplications contributed to metal tolerance and may instead be a consequence of altered cell cycles. Gerstein et al. (12) found extensive duplications of whole chromosomes in their copper-adapted lines (particularly, chromosomes II and VIII) but also found no evidence of a beneficial effect of those mutations. Gorter et al. (16) evolved yeast in cadmium and found whole genome duplications and increased copy number of chromosomes XIV, III, and VI but a decreased copy-number of chromosome I. Future experiments that measure growth of artificially constructed aneuploid lines in different metals would be valuable to determine whether specific chromosome duplications contribute causally to metal tolerance.

Given the oxidative nature of metal ions and following previous studies on copper (12), we expected to find amplification of chelator proteins that bind metals and render them biologically inactive. We did indeed find increased copy-number of the CUP1 gene, encoding for the copper binding protein, but this phenomenon only occurred in the copper environment and did not result in higher copper tolerance than copper lines without CUP1 amplification (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Despite previous work suggesting that cadmium might also be neutralized in this way, we found that copper lines with higher copy-number of CUP1 did not exhibit higher performance in cadmium (12, 15, 46).

Conclusion.

It remains an important challenge for evolutionary biologists to predict the combinations of stressors that will most often lead to extinction and those that allow evolutionary rescue. Using 120 evolutionary rescue lines of yeast, we assayed cross-tolerance across six toxic metals. Our simple a priori hypotheses failed to predict cross-tolerance: I. similarity in metal reactivity, II. overlap of known metal-resistance genes in SGD, III. correlation of abundance in environmental samples. Nevertheless, the genetic pathways underlying adaptation and cross-tolerance were revealing. The most commonly mutated gene functions involved stress response and cell cycle signaling, along with ubiquitin machinery for degradation of damaged cellular components. These functions suggest that growth in these extremely toxic environments requires bypassing programs that prevent growth in the presence of stress and damage. Cross-tolerance was highly variable, but broader cross-tolerance characterized lines bearing mutations affecting phosphorus metabolism and the PHO pathway, which is known to affect metal transport. Broader cross-tolerance also characterized lines evolved in certain environments (e.g., cobalt) more than others (e.g., manganese). Thus, while the risk of extinction is expected to rise with the number and extent of environmental toxins, this work demonstrates that there can sometimes be genetic and environmental paths that make survival through cross-tolerance possible.
