Deep learning approach for art style recognition and colour restoration in visual art images
Authors: Huichao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColor, as a core element in artistic images, not only contributes to visual aesthetics but also plays a crucial role in expressing emotions, constructing thematic narratives, and conveying semantic information. While existing deep learning approaches for art style recognition primarily focus on structural and textural features, they often overlook the perceptual and psychological dimensions of color. To address this gap, this paper proposes a color perception–aware deep learning framework for artistic style classification. Our method integrates psychologically motivated color preprocessing—including saturation enhancement and adaptive transformation in HSV and Lab color spaces—into a standard ResNet-50 backbone, enabling the model to better capture stylistically relevant chromatic cues. Evaluated on the WikiArt dataset (27 styles), our approach achieves a test accuracy of 90.2% ± 0.3, outperforming current state-of-the-art methods without resorting to larger architectures or multi-modal inputs. The results demonstrate that explicitly modeling human color perception can yield significant performance gains in art style recognition using only image data. Beyond classification, we discuss the broader implications of precise color feature extraction for applications such as digital restoration, artwork authentication, and creative content recommendation. Finally, we outline future directions in color-aware AI for art, emphasizing its potential in emotional analysis, generative art, and interdisciplinary research at the intersection of computer vision and art history.Keywords: Deep learning, Art image, Feature extraction, Style recognition model, CNN, Color histograms, Emotional analysis, Digital art processingSubject terms: Cultural and media studies, Cultural and media studies, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The art image, as an important carrier of human culture and emotional expression, not only carries rich historical information and aesthetic value but also profoundly reflects the aesthetic concepts and spiritual world of different eras, regions, and individuals. With the rapid development of digital humanities, computer vision, and artificial intelligence technologies, systematic and intelligent analysis and understanding of art images have become a hot topic in interdisciplinary research. Art image processing not only helps in the digital preservation and restoration of cultural heritage but also shows broad application prospects in fields such as art style recognition, authenticity identification, creative content generation, personalized recommendation, and even emotional computing. Especially in the context of the rise of global digital museums, intelligent art platforms, and generative AI art, how to use computational methods to deeply explore the semantic information in art images has become a key bridge connecting technology and humanities.

Among the many visual elements of art images1–3, color is recognized as one of the most core expressive dimensions due to its strong perceptual nature, emotional arousal ability, and cultural symbolic significance. Compared to features such as shape, composition, or brushwork, color often more directly affects the subjective experience and emotional response of the viewer—for example, warm tones are often associated with enthusiasm and vitality, while cool tones tend to evoke a sense of tranquility or melancholy. In addition, different art movements (e.g. Impressionism emphasizing light and color changes, Fauvism pursuing high saturation subjective colors) have distinct stylistic characteristics in their use of color4–8. Therefore, accurate modeling and analysis of color not only helps to reveal the inherent stylistic patterns of artworks but also provides important clues for understanding the artist’s creative intentions and the audience’s aesthetic mechanisms.

However, traditional color analysis methods (e.g. hand-designed color histograms, color moments, or statistical features in fixed color spaces) often struggle to capture the complex, nonlinear, and highly context-dependent color semantics in art images9–18. In recent years, deep learning technologies, especially Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), have demonstrated powerful capabilities in automatic image feature extraction, opening new paths for high-dimensional representation and semantic understanding of color features in art images19–22. Nevertheless, existing research often treats color as part of general visual features, lacking systematic exploration of its unique expressive mechanisms in the artistic context and rarely integrating color psychology, art theory, and computational models for interdisciplinary integration4–8.

In view of this, this paper focuses on the core issue of “color expression and processing in art images,” systematically reviews the functions and significance of color in artistic creation, deeply explores deep learning-based color feature extraction methods, and focuses on analyzing their application effects in typical tasks such as style classification, digital restoration, emotion recognition, and intelligent recommendation. The reason for focusing on color is that it is not only the most intuitive visual fingerprint of artistic style but also a key link connecting technical analysis and human perception—only by deeply understanding how color expresses can machines truly understand art.

Related work

In the field of art image analysis, color has long been a core visual cue for style expression and emotional transmission, attracting widespread attention from researchers. Early color feature extraction mainly relied on hand-designed statistical methods. Among them, color histograms are the most classic and widely used means, characterizing the overall color distribution by counting the frequency of occurrence of each color value in a specific color space (e.g. RGB, HSV, or Lab)23–25. Related handcrafted and hybrid descriptors have also been investigated for automatic art analysis, color segmentation, and digital style representation9–18,26.

To alleviate the sensitivity of histograms to color quantization and the sparsity of zero values, researchers further proposed improved strategies e.g. cumulative histograms, color moments (using low-order statistics e.g. mean and variance), and joint features combining texture (e.g. LBP combined with color channels)23,26–31.

Although these methods can distinguish the macro color tendencies of different art styles to a certain extent, their essence is still limited to low-level, static statistical descriptions, making it difficult to capture the semantic complexity and context dependence of color in art images.

i.e., the brushstrokes interweaving light and color in Impressionist works, the emotionally charged use of high saturation in Expressionism, or the subtle layers of “ink divided into five colors” in Eastern ink paintings, cannot be effectively modeled by simple histograms or moment features. In addition, traditional methods usually require pre-selecting color spaces and fixing feature dimensions, lacking the ability to adaptively understand the function of color in the artistic context.

The development of deep learning technology32, especially the success of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in image understanding tasks, has led researchers to turn to data-driven automatic feature learning paradigms. Unlike hand-crafted features, CNNs can extract multi-level representations from raw pixels through multi-layer nonlinear transformations, capturing multi-level representations from local color blocks to global color compositions, retaining spatial distribution information of colors while implicitly encoding high-level semantics such as style and emotion19–22. In recent years, transfer learning strategies based on pre-trained models (e.g. VGG, ResNet-50) have shown significant advantages in tasks such as art style classification and color restoration, marking a key shift in art image color analysis from manual rules to intelligent perception33,34.

Therefore, this paper will focus on how deep learning breaks through the limitations of traditional color feature extraction to achieve a more refined and semantic understanding and application of color expression in art images.

Semantically consistent color restoration

Additionally, this paper designs a semantic-consistent color restoration module based on Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (cGAN) to automatically colorize black-and-white art images while maintaining semantic consistency with the image content and style35,36.

First, the input grayscale image is a 224 × 224 L* channel (from the Lab color space), then through a style embedding vector to 512 dimensions, derived from the style features extracted by ResNet-50 in Sect. 3.1, and finally outputs a 224 × 224 × 2 a*b* color channel, which can be combined with the input L* to reconstruct the complete Lab image. Specifically, the generator G adopts a U-Net structure, consisting of an encoder and a decoder. The encoder is composed of several convolutional layers for extracting multi-scale features from the input grayscale image; the decoder gradually restores spatial resolution through deconvolution layers and incorporates skip connections to retain detail information. The style embedding vector is fused at multiple levels of the decoder to guide color generation that conforms to a specific art style, following semantic color-feature modeling ideas in art and design applications35–37. Additionally, Style-Adaptive Instance Normalization (Style-AdaIN) layers are introduced at multiple levels of the decoder to dynamically modulate the mean and variance of intermediate features with the style embedding vector, achieving style-guided colorization; finally, the output layer uses Tanh activation to map a*b* to [−1, 1], which is then linearly scaled to [−128, 127] (the standard range of the Lab color space). During the training of a GAN, it is crucial to maintain a balance between the generator and discriminator. If the discriminator becomes too powerful, it will produce values close to 0 or 1, making it challenging for the generator to improve. On the other hand, if the generator is too strong, it might exploit the discriminator’s weaknesses, causing false positives. Therefore, for a robust GAN model, the generator and discriminator should have comparable strengths to ensure stable training. However, HSV-transformed images, despite high model accuracy (87%), score only 4.1, likely because hue shifts (+30°) alter the original artistic intent (e.g., turning blues into greens), which human observers notice and dislike. Similarly, Lab-transformed images score the lowest (4.0), as the desaturation and fixed luminance make artworks appear flat or “washed out.” Notably, YCbCr and blue-channel enhancements maintain moderate human scores (4.1–4.2) despite decent model performance, indicating that certain color manipulations are “machine-friendly” but not necessarily “human-preferred.”

Summary

This study experimentally validates the critical role of color characteristics in the computational analysis of artistic images. By integrating perceptually motivated color preprocessing—such as saturation enhancement and adaptive representation in HSV and Lab color spaces—with a deep learning architecture (ResNet-50), our approach achieves a style classification accuracy of 90.2% ± 0.3 on the WikiArt dataset (27 styles), outperforming existing state-of-the-art methods. Furthermore, our color-aware restoration framework attains an 87% accuracy in reconstructing missing or degraded color regions in classical artworks. In practical applications, an art recommendation system built upon the extracted color features demonstrates measurable improvements in user engagement, increasing click-through rates by 15% and conversion rates by 12%.

These results collectively confirm that explicitly modeling human color perception significantly enhances performance across multiple art-related tasks—including style recognition, digital restoration, and personalized recommendation—without requiring complex model architectures or auxiliary metadata. Looking ahead, we anticipate that deeper feature representations, multimodal fusion (e.g., combining visual, textual, and contextual data), and advances in generative models such as GANs and diffusion networks will further push the boundaries of artistic image understanding. Future work aims to achieve style and sentiment analysis accuracy exceeding 95%, while enabling high-fidelity, semantically coherent art generation and restoration, thereby contributing to the preservation, innovation, and democratization of visual culture.

Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations. First, the WikiArt dataset exhibits class imbalance, with certain artistic movements (e.g., Baroque, Renaissance) underrepresented, potentially limiting the model’s generalization to minority styles. Second, although human perception scoring provides valuable subjective validation, the participant pool—primarily local university students—may introduce cultural or educational biases. Third, our experiments focus mainly on CNN-based architectures and do not yet explore more advanced models such as Vision Transformers or multimodal foundation models.

Future work will address these gaps in three directions: (1) constructing more balanced, cross-cultural art datasets that include non-Western artistic traditions45; (2) integrating vision-language models like CLIP for fine-grained joint understanding of artistic style and emotion25; and (3) extending color-aware feature analysis to generative AI applications, such as controllable artistic synthesis and personalized emotion-driven art recommendation, thereby advancing human–AI collaborative creativity in the arts28,41.
