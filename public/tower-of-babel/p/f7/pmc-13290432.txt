Influenza Antiviral Prescriptions in the US Inpatient and Outpatient Settings During the 2015–2022 Influenza Seasons
Authors: Kyueun Lee, Anirban Basu, Janet A Englund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundsThe antiviral drug prescription landscape has changed as new antivirals for influenza treatment have been approved. Understanding this landscape is important to assess potential under‐ and mis‐utilization and to address gaps in care. This study characterizes patterns in influenza antiviral use in US healthcare settings using a large claims database.MethodsWe included prescriptions of four antivirals: oseltamivir, baloxavir, peramivir, and zanamivir in US healthcare settings between September 2016 and August 2023, using Komodo Health Database. We estimated the percentage of antiviral prescriptions associated with respiratory disease diagnoses, including influenza, COVID‐19, and other respiratory conditions, and the percentage prescribed to patients at high risk of influenza complications. We used multinomial logistic regression to assess the association between patient and prescriber characteristics and the choice of antiviral.ResultsBetween 865,885 and 3,092,822 influenza antiviral prescriptions were filled annually during the 2016–2017 through 2022–2023 influenza seasons among US insured individuals. Oseltamivir was the most prevalent antiviral type. Baloxavir use remained low, accounting for 1.8%–9.2% of total prescriptions since its 2018 approval. Between 55.6% and 75.4% of prescriptions had an associated influenza diagnosis, with the lowest proportion observed during the 2020–2021 season. Between 2.59% and 5.35% of antivirals were prescribed to high‐risk patients. Influenza diagnosis and patient age were associated with prescribing baloxavir rather than oseltamivir.ConclusionsAmong US insured individuals, oseltamivir remained the most prescribed antiviral during the 2016–2017 through 2022–2023 seasons, a pattern consistent with its broader FDA‐approved indication across age groups and care settings relative to baloxavir, peramivir, and zanamivir.Keywords: antiviral, seasonal influenza, therapeutics

Introduction

Influenza infection typically causes mild symptoms such as cough, fever, sore throat, and headaches [1]. Although most people recover from flu within 3–14 days, influenza infections can lead to severe complications such as pneumonia, asthma, myocarditis, encephalitis, and multiple‐organ failure [2]. Severe outcomes are more common in children under 5 years of age, adults over 65 years, and those with underlying medical conditions than other age groups and those without underlying medical conditions [3, 4]. When given within 2 days of symptom onset, influenza antiviral drugs can shorten the duration of symptoms in otherwise healthy outpatients by 0.5–1.5 days and reduce the risk of severe complications leading to antibiotic use [5].

The antiviral drug prescription landscape has changed as new antivirals for influenza treatment were approved. Currently, four antiviral drugs are approved in the United States and recommended for influenza: zanamivir (approved in 1999), oseltamivir (1999), peramivir (2014), and baloxavir (2018). The first three are neuraminidase inhibitors that block a protein essential for influenza virus release from infected cells, whereas baloxavir, the newest antiviral, is a cap‐dependent endonuclease inhibitor that interfere a protein involved in viral RNA transcription [6]. Oseltamivir is taken twice daily for 5 days, but baloxavir, with a prolonged half‐life, offers the convenience of single‐dose therapy [6]. These antivirals differ substantially in their approved age indications and care settings (Appendix Table S1). Oseltamivir has the broadest indication, covering treatment from ≥ 14 days old and prophylaxis from ≥ 1 year old, and is the only antiviral recommended (off‐label) for hospitalized patients. In contrast, baloxavir, zanamivir, and peramivir are labeled for outpatient use only and are restricted to narrower age groups (≥ 5, ≥ 7, and ≥ 6 months for treatment, respectively), with additional contraindications such as underlying airway disease for zanamivir and complicated or progressive illness for baloxavir. Influenza antiviral medication is prescription‐based and hence currently not available over the counter in the United States. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends antiviral treatment for patients who (1) are hospitalized, (2) have severe, complicated, or progressive illness, or (3) are at high risk for influenza complications such as children aged under 2 years, adults older than 65 years, individuals with underlying health conditions such as asthma, chronic lung disease, and heart diseases [7].

Prior studies described influenza antiviral use in various settings and subpopulations. A study on the influenza antiviral prescription rate in outpatient pharmacy in the United States estimated 1.93–28.39 prescriptions per 1000 between 2003 and 2012 [8]. A recent paper showed that 99.8% of outpatient influenza antiviral drugs prescribed in children and adolescents in the United States was oseltamivir [9]. However, there remains a gap in understanding the trend in influenza antiviral use by drug type, as well as the factors influencing the choice of antiviral agents based on patient and prescriber characteristics. In addition, prescribing patterns for influenza antiviral may have changed since the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020. Understanding the changing prescription landscape of influenza antivirals and prescribing patterns is important to assess potential under‐ and mis‐utilization of antivirals and addressing gaps in care in the United States. Hence, this study aims to understand the patterns in influenza antiviral use in all ages and by drug type in the US healthcare settings using a large insurance claim database.

Method

Data Source

We used Komodo Health claims database to identify influenza antiviral prescriptions in inpatient and outpatient healthcare settings in the United States between September 2016 and August 2023. Komodo Health Database includes billions of de‐identified medical and pharmacy claims derived from a wide‐ranging healthcare network across the United States [10]. It contains records for over 320 million US individuals with insurance coverage, including those with private insurance, Medicare, and Medicaid. The database includes both open claims, which capture partial medical and prescription encounters for a given patient, and closed claims, which represent complete datasets of medical and pharmacy claims submitted by closed payer sources.

Influenza Antiviral Prescriptions

Our study population includes patients who were prescribed influenza antivirals between September 1, 2016 and August 31, 2023, in inpatient and outpatient healthcare settings. No age restriction was applied, and patients of all ages who received a qualifying antiviral prescription during the study period were included. Patient age at the time of antiviral prescription was calculated from date of birth and prescription date, and is reported as the mean (standard deviation) in years. We defined the influenza season as September 1 through August 31 of the following year. We included prescriptions of four antiviral drugs that are currently approved and recommended for influenza in the United States: baloxavir, oseltamivir, peramivir, and zanamivir. In outpatient settings, we used brand or generic names to identify relevant prescriptions (Appendix, Table S2). In inpatient settings, we used the HCPCS codes of administration of four drugs (Appendix Table S3) to identify relevant prescriptions. Annual influenza season was defined to be between September 1st and August 31st in the following year.

We estimated the number of prescriptions by antiviral drug type for each season. To account for differences in FDA‐approved age indications across the four antivirals, we stratified prescription rates in Figure 1 by three age groups: < 5, 5–11, and ≥ 12 years. These thresholds reflect the regulatory history of antiviral approvals during the study period: patients < 5 years were below the approved age range for both baloxavir and zanamivir; patients 5–11 years could receive zanamivir throughout the study period (treatment approved ≥ 7 years, prophylaxis ≥ 5 years), but baloxavir only after its August 2022 pediatric expansion; and patients ≥ 12 years were eligible for all four antivirals once baloxavir launched in October 2018.

Respiratory Disease Diagnosis

We estimated the percentage of the antiviral prescriptions that were associated with respiratory disease diagnoses including influenza, COVID‐19, and other respiratory conditions. First, we identified the medical claims of the visit when the outpatient influenza antiviral was prescribed. We assumed that the medical claim with the date of service the closest to the date of prescription within a 7‐day window and with the attending provider's National Provider Identifier (NPI) codes matching to the prescribing provider's NPI is the medical visit that incurred the prescription of antiviral drugs. Inpatient prescriptions were directly recorded in the medical claims. For influenza diagnoses, we included ICD‐10 diagnosis codes starting with J09, J10, and J11. For COVID‐19 diagnosis, we used ICD‐10 diagnosis codes start with “U07”. For other respiratory conditions, we considered acute respiratory infections (J00–J06), pneumonia (J12–J18), other acute lower respiratory infections (J30–J39), chronic lower respiratory disease (J40–J47), lung disease due to external agents (J60–J70), other respiratory diseases principally affecting interstitium (JJ80–J84), suppurative and necrotic conditions of the lower respiratory tract (J85–J86), other disease of the pleura (J90–J94), respiratory complications (J95), and other diseases of the respiratory system (J96–J99). Because not all outpatient prescriptions had the associated medical claims, we estimated the percentage of respiratory conditions among the prescriptions that have valid medical claims within 7 days prior to the prescription (Appendix Table S4).

A patient can be diagnosed with multiple respiratory conditions prior to an antiviral prescription. We first classified prescriptions based on whether they were associated with influenza diagnosis. Among antiviral prescriptions associated with influenza diagnosis, we estimated how many also had accompanying diagnoses of COVID‐19 and other respiratory diseases. For prescriptions not associated with influenza diagnosis, we estimated the percentage that were linked to COVID‐19, other respiratory disease diagnoses, or neither.

High‐Risk Conditions

We estimated the percentage of the antiviral drugs that were prescribed to the patients at high risk of influenza complications. We only considered non‐age‐related high‐risk conditions. We defined patients at high risk to be those who were diagnosed with one or more high‐risk conditions on the date of antiviral prescription, using ICD‐10 codes. The conditions at high risk of influenza complications include asthma (ICD‐10 code: J45), blood disorders (sickle cell disease; D57), chronic lung disease (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cystic fibrosis; J44, E84), neurologic and neurodevelopment conditions (spinal cord, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, other disorders of the brain, intellectual disability, moderate to severe developmental delay, muscular dystrophy, and spinal cord injury; G89.0, G40, F71–F73 G71.00–G71.09, E14.0–S14.15), endocrine disorders (diabetes; E10–E14), heart disease (congenital heart disease, congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease; Q24, I50, I25), chronic kidney disease (N18), chronic liver disease and cirrhosis (K70–K77), metabolic disorders (inherited metabolic disorders, mitochondrial disorders; E88.89, E88.4), obesity (E66, Z68.41–Z68.45, Z68.54), weakened immune condition (HIV, leukemia, and other cancers that require chemo/radiation therapy, immunodeficiency due to drugs, long‐term use of immunosuppressants; B20, C91–C95, C99–D49, D84.821, Z79.62), stroke (I63), and pregnancy (O09, Z34) [11, 12]. We provided the complete list of diagnosis codes for high‐risk conditions in Table S5. The percentage of the antiviral drugs that were prescribed to the patients at high risk of influenza conditions was calculated by dividing the total number of claims with both diagnosis of high‐risk conditions and antiviral prescription by the total number of antiviral prescriptions.

Statistical Analysis

We assessed the association between the prescribed influenza antiviral drug type with patients' and providers' characteristics. We employed multinomial logistic regression with patient age, patient region (South, Northeast, Midwest, Pacific west), high‐risk conditions, age older than 65 years old, age younger than 5 years old, and prescribing provider type (family medicine, pediatrics, internal medicine, emergency medicine, physician assistant, nurse practitioner, and others) as independent variables. We adjusted for the fixed effects of influenza season. Dependent variable is the prescribed antiviral drug type (oseltamivir as a reference level, baloxavir, and zanamivir). We excluded peramivir in the outcome level considering its limited number of observations in the dataset.

Results

Influenza Antiviral Prescriptions During the US Influenza Seasons Between 2016 and 2022

Between the 2016 and 2022 influenza seasons, 865,885–3,092,822 influenza antiviral drugs were prescribed (Table 1). The mean age at prescription ranged 27.9–37.4 years old, and approximately half were prescribed to female patients. The southern region accounted for the largest share of prescriptions (50.5%–63.2%) in our dataset. The most common type of prescriber was family medicine (36.8%–43.5%), followed by pediatrics (18%–24%) and internal medicine (8.6%–13.5%).

Influenza Antiviral Prescription Rate (Per 100,000 Enrollees) in Outpatient and Inpatient Healthcare Setting by Drug Type

Prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020, the 2017–2018 influenza season had the highest prescription rate (Figure 1). There was a sharp decrease in antiviral use in the 2020 season, when a low number of influenza cases were documented, resulting from the COVID‐19 containment policies. Antiviral prescription rates were much higher in the outpatient settings compared with the inpatient settings, with oseltamivir the most prevalent antiviral type prescribed in outpatients. The use of baloxavir increased after its approval in 2018 but remained less common than oseltamivir, accounting for 1.8%–9.2% of total prescriptions; baloxavir use was concentrated in patients ≥ 12 years old throughout most of the study period, consistent with its initial pediatric age threshold prior to the 2022 expansion to ≥ 5 years. The use of peramivir over oseltamivir in inpatient settings increased from 13.6% to 92.6% of total inpatient prescriptions between the 2016–2017 and 2020–2021 seasons.

Percentage of the Antiviral Prescriptions That Were Associated With Influenza, COVID‐19, and Other Respiratory Disease Diagnoses

The vast majority of total influenza antiviral prescriptions were associated with influenza diagnoses (55.6%–75.4%) during the 2016–2022 seasons (Figure 2). In the 2020 season, the lowest percentage (55.6%) of prescriptions had influenza diagnoses. About 33.3%–55.1% of prescriptions had influenza diagnosis without any other respiratory disease diagnoses, whereas the rest had multiple respiratory diagnoses along with influenza. More than 25% of influenza antiviral drugs lacked an accompanying influenza diagnosis during the 2016–2022 seasons. Notably, 13.2%–23.9% of prescriptions were not linked to a diagnosis of influenza, COVID‐19, or any other respiratory disease diagnosis. Since the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020, COVID‐19 diagnosis accounted for 3.8%–11% of influenza antiviral prescriptions.

Percentage of Antiviral Prescriptions Given to Patients at High Risk for Influenza Complication

Among the non‐age‐related clinical high‐risk conditions, five most common conditions include endocrine disorders (diabetes), obesity, weakened immune conditions, chronic liver disease and cirrhosis, and chronic lung diseases (Table 2). Endocrine disorder, the most prevalent high‐risk condition, accounted for 1.41%–3.54% of total prescriptions during the seasons between 2016 and 2022. Obesity and weakened immune conditions accounted for 0.58%–1.26% and 0.17%–0.43% of prescriptions during the same period, following the endocrine disorder. During the 2020 season, the percentage of antiviral prescriptions given to high‐risk patients increased across all high‐risk conditions.

Association Between Patients and Providers Characteristics and Prescribed Antiviral Class

Compared with oseltamivir, the odds of prescribing baloxavir increased by 2% with each additional year in patient's age (Table 3). A confirmed influenza diagnosis was more common among prescriptions for baloxavir than for oseltamivir. High‐risk clinical conditions and age over 65 years were associated with 0.32–0.66 times lower odds of prescribing baloxavir compared with oseltamivir. The odds of prescribing oseltamivir instead of baloxavir were significantly higher for patients under 5 years of age compared with those aged 5 years and older. Across most prescriber types including pediatrics, internal medicine, and emergency medicine, the odds of prescribing baloxavir instead of oseltamivir were 5%–26% lower compared with family medicine. An influenza diagnosis was associated with lower odds of prescribing zanamivir instead of oseltamivir (odd ratio: 0.77; 95% CI 0.77–0.83). In addition, unlike with baloxavir, prescribers in internal medicine were more likely to prescribe zanamivir compared with providers in family medicine.

Discussion

Our study showed that two oral options for influenza treatment, oseltamivir and baloxavir, are the most commonly prescribed antivirals in outpatient settings, with oseltamivir prescribed substantially more often than baloxavir. This pattern is consistent with the two drugs' differing FDA‐approved indications rather than with under‐utilization of baloxavir per se. Oseltamivir is approved for treatment in patients as young as 2 weeks of age and across both outpatient and inpatient settings (the latter as a CDC off‐label recommendation), whereas baloxavir is approved only for outpatient treatment of acute uncomplicated influenza in patients ≥ 5 years old who are otherwise healthy or at high risk for complications, and is not recommended for hospitalized patients, during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or for outpatients with complicated or progressive illness. Even within the population eligible for both agents, however, oseltamivir prescribing predominates, and several factors may contribute to this pattern. Clinical trials have demonstrated that both drugs have similar effectiveness and safety profiles: both reduce the time to symptom alleviation and are associated with mild side effects, including nausea, headache, and vomiting [13, 14]. However, the current IDSA guideline for influenza treatment recommends a 5‐day course of oral oseltamivir but does not include use of baloxavir, as baloxavir's FDA approval coincided with the timing of the IDSA guideline update [15]. In addition, the higher price of baloxavir compared with generic oseltamivir may also limit access to baloxavir [16]. Peramivir, because of its primary use among hospitalized and critically ill patients, was more commonly prescribed in inpatient settings [17].

A substantial proportion of antiviral prescriptions in our study were not associated with an influenza diagnosis code. Several factors may contribute to this observation. ICD‐10 codes are recorded for billing rather than clinical research, and their absence does not necessarily mean influenza was not considered at the encounter, particularly in outpatient settings where coding is less standardized. Antivirals are also indicated for post‐exposure chemoprophylaxis, which would appropriately lack an influenza diagnosis code [15]. In addition, the IDSA guideline recommends starting empiric antiviral treatment for patients who present atypical signs and symptoms associated with influenza complications [15]. Testing is recommended only when the test results would change influenza clinical management and when patients are admitted to hospital with the signs and symptoms of influenza. However, testing is not required to prescribe influenza antiviral drugs and hence, healthcare providers can make clinical diagnoses based on symptoms [18]. Diagnosing influenza apart from other respiratory diseases such as COVID‐19 based on symptoms can be challenging due to their overlapping symptoms. Fever, the most common symptom in influenza, also appears in 88%–98.6% of COVID‐19 cases [19]. Other overlapping symptoms include cough, congestion, and sore throat. Our study found that the influenza antiviral drugs for COVID‐19 were more prevalent in the beginning of the COVID‐19 pandemic, and the rate has decreased since then, as the percentage of total respiratory symptoms caused by COVID‐19 has decreased. Limited access to COVID‐19 treatment in the beginning of the pandemic may explain this trend [20, 21]. Among prescriptions with no influenza, COVID‐19, or respiratory disease diagnosis, the most frequent codes across all seasons were fever of unknown origin (R50), unspecified viral infection (B34), cough (R05), and other general symptoms (R68), which are all compatible with empiric treatment for suspected influenza (Table S6). The fifth, contact with and exposure to communicable diseases (Z20), rose from 9%–15% before 2020 to 32%–40% during 2020–2022, consistent with increased post‐exposure chemoprophylaxis.

Endocrine disorders such as diabetes, obesity, weakened immune conditions, chronic liver diseases and cirrhosis, and chronic lung diseases were the five most common high‐risk conditions associated with influenza antiviral prescriptions. The relative prevalence of these conditions reflects their broader population burden and diagnosis rate [11, 22, 23, 24, 25]. The notable rise in the percentage of prescriptions given to high‐risk patients between 2020 and 2021 may reflect increased awareness of the importance of treating respiratory infections during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The percentages we report should be interpreted as a lower bound, as our claims‐based approach likely underestimates the prevalence of pre‐existing chronic conditions (see Limitations). Our finding of a low percentage of antivirals prescribed for high‐risk groups is consistent with a previous study, which reported that only 15% of high‐risk outpatients with acute respiratory illness received antiviral medication [26].

Our statistical analysis identified factors associated with the choice of antiviral drugs. Compared with oseltamivir, the odds of prescribing baloxavir were higher with a confirmed influenza diagnosis. Broader access to oseltamivir, which has been available for a longer time, and the relatively recent introduction of baloxavir in 2018 may explain the observed prescribing pattern [6]. In addition, greater diagnostic certainty could be required for prescribing a newer antiviral drug such as baloxavir. The strong inverse association between age under 5 years and baloxavir prescription is expected given that baloxavir is not FDA‐approved for children under 5 years of age, and concerns about resistance development in younger children have been described [27]. Similarly, lower odds of baloxavir prescription among patients aged ≥ 65 years and those with high‐risk conditions are consistent with baloxavir's labeling, which does not specifically cover hospitalized patients or those with complicated or progressive illness. Differences across prescribing clinician types including lower odds of baloxavir prescribing among pediatricians, internal medicine physicians, and emergency medicine providers compared with family medicine should be interpreted as composite effects of prescriber and patient population rather than isolated specialty preferences. Pediatricians primarily treat young children outside baloxavir's approved age range for most of the study period, internal medicine physicians primarily treat adults, and emergency medicine providers more often see patients with severe presentations for whom baloxavir is not indicated.

This study has several limitations. Although our data source covers extensive healthcare systems across the United States, it did not capture all antiviral prescriptions nationwide. For instance, individuals who paid out of pocket and did not use insurance for their influenza antiviral prescriptions were not included. Additionally, while Komodo includes Medicare and Medicaid data, the completeness and population coverage of these datasets can vary by state. We identified influenza and COVID‐19 diagnoses associated with influenza antiviral prescription, using ICD‐10 codes. Although the influenza diagnoses code involves influenza testing in inpatient setting, healthcare providers often make clinical diagnoses based on the symptoms [28]. Hence, our estimate on the percentage of antiviral prescriptions associated with influenza or COVID‐19 diagnoses can be potentially biased, especially in outpatient settings. Our high‐risk estimates likely represent a lower bound, as we used only the ICD‐10 codes cited as examples on the CDC webpage and required the diagnosis to appear on the date of prescription rather than within a lookback window. Importantly, our analysis does not fully capture all clinical factors that determine baloxavir eligibility. Although we adjusted for patient age, care setting, and high‐risk conditions, our claims data did not allow us to identify other contraindications such as pregnancy, suspected antiviral resistance, prior antiviral exposure, or complicated vs. uncomplicated illness within the season. Comparisons of oseltamivir and baloxavir prescribing should therefore be interpreted descriptively, rather than as evidence of appropriate or inappropriate utilization.

Conclusion

Among the US insured population, oseltamivir remained the most prescribed antiviral during the 2016–2022 influenza seasons, a pattern consistent with its broader FDA‐approved indication across age groups and care settings relative to baloxavir, peramivir, and zanamivir.
