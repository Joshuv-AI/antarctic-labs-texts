Braiding Knowledge Systems: Integrating Indigenous Wisdom and Sustainable Research for a Regenerative Future
Authors: Elizabeth Edgar-Webkamigad, Jody-Lynn Rebek, Michael R. Twiss, Pedro Madeira Antunes, Isabelle Aubin, Isabel Molina, Jordan M. Barone, Aaron Jones, Audrey Moores
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This Perspective explores and calls for the integration of Indigenous knowledge systems within sustainability science to address urgent environmental, social, and economic challenges. Insights emerged from a collaborative workshop held in Baawaating (Algoma District, Ontario, Canada) with knowledge holders, practitioners, scholars, and community leaders, including members from the Ketegaunseebee (Garden River) and Batchewana First Nations. The paper examines how Indigenous ways of being, thinking, and knowing can enrich sustainability research. This work focuses on mapping parallels and distinctions between Indigenous and other sustainability practices with regard to the planet, the land, and relationships. It explores systems thinking, circularity, and Two-Eyed Seeing as a way to reimagine human–land relationships through place-based knowledge and stewardship. We stress the importance of embedding purpose and intention in scientific inquiry and propose examples such as ceremonial practices, seven-generational thinking, and relational accountability. The Perspective calls for a paradigm shift beyond Western-centric and reductionist approaches, which lead to endless economic growth, toward holistic, interconnected, and intentional frameworks that respect Indigenous worldviews. A series of concrete calls to action urge sustainability researchers to integrate Indigenous knowledge respectfully, foster long-term collaborative relationships, and adopt regenerative and relational approaches in their scholarship. This will contribute to restoring the balance needed among cultures and between humanity and the natural world. Collectively, as sustainability scholars, we want to see meaningful change and encourage a paradigm shift: to embrace inclusive, holistic approaches that contribute to impactful, results-oriented actions that restore health to our world. You can do this through being advocates for change to promote reconciliation, strengthen intercultural relationships, and support greater well-being for all, and, in doing so, protect the planet by reducing ecological harm.

Introduction

Sustainable research is a trans and interdisciplinary field of inquiry that seeks to understand and develop solutions to complex and global challenges whereby environmental, social, and economic systems are negatively and potentially irreversibly impacted. − Sustainability researchers work to address anthropogenic (human-caused) damage (e.g., climate change, invasive species, microplastics, pesticide use, etc.) by discovering ways to restore health to these systems. Currently, advancements toward the United Nations (UN) Sustainability 2030 Agenda are slow or nonexistent, with only 17% of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) progressing to be on track. Sustainability research is rooted in a holistic view of science, which exists in tension with the current mainstream paradigm, referred to here as traditional Western research. Traditional Western research can be described as reductionist, meaning that problems are divided into smaller ones to be solved. , In this Perspective, we use the term “Western” to qualify research practices and epistemologies anchored in Western European and Euro-descendant North American traditions, by contrast with Indigenous knowledge systems.

Indigenous Peoples are present throughout the world. Their ways of knowing, rooted in relationality, reciprocity, and respect for the land and all living beings, provide critical insights to solve complex challenges. Their ideas and perspectives remain marginalized in mainstream discourse as well as research, despite their relevance to addressing the complex changes and multifaceted transformations needed. Tackling systemic and long-term environmental challenges requires more than technical solutions: it demands a paradigm shift in how we relate to the natural world and all Peoples, an approach Indigenous stewards have demonstrated for millennia.

A collaborative workshop, held at Algoma University on October 27, 2023, included members of Ketegaunseebee Garden River and Batchewana First Nations, and scholars, practitioners, and community leaders in Baawaating (“place of the rapids” in Anishinaabemowin), also known as Sault Ste. Marie, in Ontario, Canada. Algoma University is located on the grounds and in the building of the former Shingwauk Indian Residential School (1875–1970), once a place of cultural erasure and suffering. It is now home to cross-cultural educationa special mission bestowed by Chief Shingwauk. Chief Shingwauk’s vision for a “Teaching Wigwam” in Algoma, dating as far back as the early 1840s, reflected a profound aspiration of Indigenous people for cross-cultural education grounded in respect and reciprocity. A true visionary, he imagined a place where Anishinaabe and Western knowledge could coexist. This vision is closely linked to the metaphor inherent in the Two Row Wampum. A wampum is a beaded belt used by Haudenosaunee nations to record agreements, convey knowledge, and symbolize relationships and responsibilities. The Two Row Wampum depicts two vessels navigating down the river of life together, symbolizing the way with which Indigenous and European people should travel, side by side. Each follows its own path, carrying its own identities, without attempting to steer the other but, instead, fostering mutual understanding. During the workshop, we explored the intersection of Indigenous knowledge systems and sustainability sciences, and how many of their concepts echo each other. Key themes emerged to illuminate the necessity of moving beyond Western-centric, positivist and reductionist frameworks grounded on limitless economic growth toward a more holistic, interconnected approach of collaborative research. This work underscored the urgency to address climate and other global change factors by exploring collaboration between sustainability research, regenerative and human survivability efforts, and Indigenous knowledge. Indigenous science is crucial for the well-being of communities and the overall health of the planet. As such, we emphasize holistic, purpose-driven, and relational approaches in this important work because it reconnects our relationships with all forms of life, builds hope, creativity, instills new pathways for research, and provides meaningful, implementable, and healthy solutions. Importantly, this work is rooted in the specific place where we met Algoma University, time, in the autumn season of 2023, and the specific group of people cosigning this work and is a reflection of the situated knowledge we could hence build. , We progressed and created, through listening and sharing each other’s thoughts, and anchoring this work in the rich literature at the intersection of indigenous knowledge and sustainability research, away from historic stereotypes.

This Perspective highlights themes from that workshop and our colorful and diverse collective voices. We first explore three important concepts, essential to this space of collaboration: from a healthy planet to our connections to the land and living world, and finally, our relationships. This journey highlights the parallels between sustainability research and its practice, and Indigenous knowledge, as well as nuances in the way they are expressed. From there, we explore the important question of purpose and intention in scientific inquiry before we call the community to action.

Bridging Knowledge Systems for a Healthy Planet

“Nature does not hurry, yet everything is accomplished”

Braiding Indigenous and Western scientific approaches requires humility, and a concept Melanie Goodchild has expressed as Relational Systems Thinking. Within sustainable research, systems thinking has emerged as an important concept for understanding local phenomena within the larger framework of their social-ecological and economic systems. For instance, in Green Chemistry education, a significant body of research has stressed the importance of considering the whole system (i.e., systems thinking) as the study object. This requires “de-zooming” from individual chemical processes and molecules to situate them within the broader system in which they occur (from quarks to biomes). This integrative approach enriches the scope of sustainable research, aligns with the level of complexity required for science to provide solutions, and characterizes systems as adaptive, emerging, nonlinear, flexible, self-organizing, and feedback loop-driven. Relational Systems Thinking deepens this level of analysis, centered on fostering mutual respect, reciprocity, and collaboration. The system is defined by the quality of relationships that map and shape systems. The way we engage in dialogue, interdependently weave together our relationships, and make decisions all impact our systems, especially our relationships.The imbalance in our Lands reflects the imbalance within ourselves due to the disconnected way we live, learn, and work ... when we draw on the knowledge from our whole personour emotional, spiritual, physical, and mental selveswe form healthy relationships, make better decisions for the Land and for future generations.

This holistic perspective defines the ways systems relate to and influence one another; it is circular. This integrative approach reveals ways our systems connect, interact, and shape each other over time. New concepts such as Doughnut Economics emphasize the interdependence of the whole system, recognizing that independent parts encompass the entire system. This reshapes our economy, reframing success as thriving within ecological limits while meeting the fundamental material needs of humanity, rather than striving for endless growth. Approaches that integrate Indigenous relationality, systems thinking, and complexity science all offer pathways to the sustainable transformations needed, founded in adaptability, humility, and reciprocity.

Holistic approaches provide a hopeful, cross-cultural, and collective approach to complex issues. Goodchild shares that we must “embrace a complexity mindset informed by Indigenous wisdom”. She continues:Indigenous place-based wisdom and Western science have called for an approach that privileges and honors Indigenous intellectual traditions [···] described variously as braiding, bridging, a circle of relationship, holism, grounded normativity, resurgence, regeneration, insurgency, and humility. Together, solutions emerge from understanding the dynamic, spiritual, and ecological interrelations that guide sustainable, regenerative, and respectful action.

These methodologies aim to rebuild Indigenous communities as part of the research process by collaborating with Indigenous communities to develop innovative Indigenous research methods. Two-Eyed Seeing offers an approach to discovering innovative solutions, as highlighted in this workshop. The “Two-Eyed Seeing” approach values both Indigenous and Western ways of knowing equally for the benefit of all, using the strengths of both knowledge systems to address complex environmental challenges. Barlett et al. describe Two-Eyed Seeing as an integrative and inclusive approach proposed by elders from the Mi’kmaw Nation, Murdena and Albert Marshall, that reconciles and rebalances Indigenous and Western ways, promoting knowledge, wisdom, and deeper learning. In this framework, Western and Indigenous ways of knowing and doing are perceived, presented, and practiced as equal. Two eyes aim to engage in critical sensemaking to discover sustainable solutions, enhance reconciliation efforts, and support strength-based organizational change. ,

Circularity is another popular concept in sustainability research that distances itself from the traditional and linear paradigm of “take-make-waste”, to propose that all things exist in a loop, where their use is perpetually reinvented. , It has a deep connection to Indigenous thinking. Nkwi Flores says, “Circularity is about regeneration and reciprocityan interdependence between humans and nature that prioritizes balance and care over accumulation”. Circularity is intimately linked to reciprocity, integrating land and all living beings as a part of a wide interconnected system, as defined by Relational Systems Thinking. The notion of “giving back to Nature”, central to Indigenous knowledge, links to circularity in that all human actions should be regenerative. The notion of accumulation, and thus waste, central to Western thinking, is in stark contradiction with sustainability, circularity, and reciprocity. Indigenous ways of being provide invaluable insight into the notion of circularity through ancestral practices of reciprocity and regeneration, such as seed banks, crop sharing, or overharvesting control. Regeneration (or regenerative sustainability) refers to an approach to sustainability that seeks not merely to maintain or restore systems, but to enable continually increasing health, wellbeing, and vitality of whole living systems. Grounded in a holistic worldview, regeneration aligns human consciousness, values, and actions with the principles by which living systems thrive, integrating ecological, social, cultural, and inner dimensions of sustainability. Regenerative practices demand a fundamental departure from the paradigm of maximizing growth at all costs. While dominant Western frameworks increasingly acknowledge the language of sustainabilityand have even introduced incremental adjustments to practicesuch shifts largely serve to preserve the prevailing status quo. By contrast, regenerative approaches, grounded in Indigenous knowledge systems, challenge this logic and provide a pathway that resists the superficiality and misguidance of greenwashing. Greenwashing is a term developed in the management research field to describe the “intersection of two firm behaviors: poor environmental performance and positive communication about environmental performance”.

Reimagining Connection to Land

Indigenous knowledge systems emphasize a deep connection to place, viewing land as both a teacher and a relative. Land is more than a physical space; “it is a source of knowledge, a teacher, and a relative” and is integral to Indigenous ways of knowing and being. There is an integral belief that all things, living and inanimate, have intrinsic and profound value. This contrasts sharply with the detachment that often characterizes scientific reasoning, economic theory, and industrial processes. The development of industrialization has brought with it tremendous and impactful social consequences that challenge our relationship with both the Land and one another. Societal detachment from the land is relatively new for humans, and relearning how to establish respectful relationships among all communities and the natural environment is possible and useful. Interestingly, more holistic views have been successfully developed by legal scholars, through developing approaches that grant ecological entitiessuch as riverslegal rights and status, thereby recognizing and treating them as legal persons. Indigenous knowledge systems offer a meaningful way of re-establishing a sense of place and the centrality of life and land. Within sustainable research, this could involve designing research projects that reflect local ecological realities, while respecting the cultural and spiritual significance of natural elements.

On the other hand, there is growing literature recognizing the centrality of land use in addressing sustainability questions. For instance, researchers have established that land system change is one of the nine planetary boundaries and thus a key parameter to consider for sustainability research. This recognizes that Western research continues to grapple with how best to consider the impact on land management. We note here that the mere concept of land needing to be managed contradicts Indigenous ways of living and knowing. The Haida Nation offers an interesting example, as they built a partnership with local and national authorities in British Columbia to cocreate land use strategies to care for their land, Haida Gwaii, a unique and rich ecosystem of islands. Indigenous ways of knowing would be particularly needed in the context of assisted migration debate, proposed for climate change mitigation in forestry. Beyond this example, they could offer a better framework for solution generation and reveal more possibilities than are currently known.

Workshop participants highlighted the need for scientists and researchers to remain grounded in the landscapes they study. The notion of situatedness is an emerging concept in sustainable research and green chemistry, enabling to root the work in the space, time and people involved, as explained and practiced by Gleeson and co-workers. , Scholars remain grounded by engaging themselves within their own local environment, through activities such as maintaining close connection with the natural environment, engaging in Indigenous cultural activities, and understanding their reconciliation efforts, but also through disclosing their relationship to the land and their positionality. , By doing so, they can better understand the cascading effects of their actions on interconnected systems. Stories shared during the workshop illustrated how powerful traditional practices can be: these include a return to ancestral lands for cultural ceremonies and spending time on the land quietly reflecting, walking, and harvesting (foraging, fishing, and hunting). Spending time outdoors and observing nature bring a better sense of the natural cycle and allows one to better assess climate change impact. This reinforces natural connections and inspires stewardship, leading to better land-based practices, such as restoration/mitigation and shows respect for the life and spirit within the land and in us all.

Shifting to Respect, Reconciliation, and Reciprocity Inherent in the Quality of Relationality

Relationality is a foundation and central part of Indigenous ways of knowing, as alluded to above. It is also a key way in which Indigenous thinking can inspire and support sustainability research. Relationships require quality and time, as connections must be genuine to nurture trust and enable meaningful outcomes that strengthen the overall ecosystem. They are founded in respect, reconciliation, and reciprocity and provide meaning and intention for grounded, responsible, and relevant research. , Indigenous peoples are the existing and original stewards of Turtle Island, which is how some refer to North America. They have been living in reciprocity with all creatures for millennia and continue to strive to do so, despite centuries of intentional and unintentional pressures of Western cultural assimilation. We must understand the truth of the history and the genocide that has occurred, learning about the Indigenous lands and communities within the territory where we live. Respect is one of the seven Grandfather teachings. The Children of Shingwauk Alumni Association share that “respect” is acknowledging the sacredness of all living beings and embracing differences with nonjudgment. It also requires respect for all relations; it requires us to recognize this interconnected relationship with one another, all living things, and our obligation to respect the land that nourishes, heals, and protects us, our ancestors, and our future relatives.

Reconciliation is a process of repairing and renewing relationships based on mutual respect, which includes recognition and fulfillment of treaties (when existing) and recognition of historical injustices, some of which have occurred due to harmful research practices. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) was established in Canada to investigate and publicly document the history and legacy of Indian Residential Schools, including genocide, abuse, and long-lasting intergenerational trauma experienced by Indigenous peoples. In revealing these truths, the TRC created a path toward reconciliation through a series of recommendations via the 94 Calls to Action directed to organizations, governments, and all Canadians. This has become a central framework in Canada to rethink relationships with Indigenous people, including in the context of natural science research. Among these, TRC identified harmful past practices related to research - such as exploitative research conducted on Indigenous peoples without consentand called for transformation to ethical, respectful, consented, and collaborative approaches to research rooted in Indigenous self-determination or resurgence. Algoma University, the site of this workshop, carries this legacy directly, embedded in its mission. Algoma University is a living witness of the past, where we now seek truth and reconciliation to address historical harms and foster just, respectful relationships.Reconciliation is about establishing and maintaining a mutually respectful relationship between Aboriginal and non-Aboriginal peoples in this country. For that to happen, there has to be awareness of the past, an acknowledgment of the harm that has been inflicted, atonement for the causes, and action to change behavior. (The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada, 2015, p 1)

It is important to acknowledge that for Indigenous people, the word “research” carries derogatory significance beyond its object and purpose. Smith calls it “one of the dirtiest words in the Indigenous world’s vocabulary”. “When mentioned in many Indigenous contexts, it stirs up silence, it conjures up bad memories, it raises a smile that is knowing and distrustful. It is so powerful that Indigenous people even write poetry about research”. In an academic context, reframing research as knowledge gathering, dissemination as knowledge sharing, and participants as collaborators can open a path to relationship with Indigenous communities. Scholars are encouraged to follow the First Nation Principles of Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession (OCAP), which ensure respectful and reciprocal knowledge sharing. These approaches prioritize reciprocity, respect, responsibility, and trust-building. In that sense, the development of citizen science within sustainability research, as a way to cocreate research via shared data collection, data analysis and management between researchers and volunteers, is a positive model. Another model, translational ecology, refers to collaborative and action-oriented practices in which environmental and social impacts are considered through mediation with local stakeholders and rights holders. This contrasts with traditional Western methodologies, which often produce detachment under the pretense of objectivity.

Finally, reciprocity is the notion of giving back to the land and to Nature. It is an essential principle in the Indigenous perspective, which challenges the extractive practices often inherent in science. As Kovach articulates, “Reciprocity is about researchers giving back to the community, not just in terms of research findings, but in ways that are meaningful to the community”. As discussed during the workshop, research and innovation must honor the land as a relative, rather than strictly as a resource. A great example is biocultural restoration, an approach bridging Western science with Indigenous knowledge for restoring not only the ecosystems, but also human and cultural relationships to place.

The idea of restoration inscribes the work in the long time scale and resonates with the ethos inherent to the concept of sustainability itself, for which impact is measured across generations. For instance, Cannon et al. have proposed an actionable definition of sustainable chemistry as “Sustainable chemistry is the development and application of chemicals, chemical processes, and products that benefit current and future generations without harmful impact to humans and ecosystems”.

The Haudenosaunee Seventh Generation Principle emphasizes a responsibility for individuals and leaders to consider how present actions impact the wellbeing of future generations, and “reflects a commitment to sustainability and interconnectedness”. The number seven is less important than the philosophy it carries, as it asserts and recognizes the amount of time required for meaningful change. A shift in patterns, attitudes, habits, and systems spanning multiple generations can lead toward a more just and regenerative future. Conducting research along these principles respects all living things and considers what we leave behind during our lifetime for other generations. Dr. Alex Wilson of the Opaskwayak Cree Nation explains, “Indigenous land-based education is its own paradigm based on Indigenous worldviews and beliefs and the passing on of knowledge to one another and the next generation”. Indigenous concepts such as buen vivir or “living well” (from Quechua/Aymara), minobimaadiziwin (Anishinaabemowin), and the “good life” frame the ontological frameworks essential for restoring life via sustainability science.

Purpose and Intention in Scientific Inquiry

Another powerful theme of the workshop was the integration of contemplation, intentionality, and purpose into the research process. Traditional Indigenous practices, such as ceremonial acknowledgments and intentional harvesting, offer models for embedding respect and reflection into scientific methodologies. They invite us to slow down into quiet, thoughtful, and holistic thinking, in a way that can align with the slow-science movement. Slowing down is a way to think about what we do and seek intention in our actions. In that sense, sustainability research has explored intentional design as a means to achieve its goals. Yet Western practices usually fall short, possibly because practicing intentionality to reach sustainability remains elusive, and is not experienced concretely. Also, it is the prescribed technology that successfully developed concomitantly with industrialization that has become the dominant driver in our society and that works to distance us from the land and one another.

Indigenous knowledge brings a fresh look at this question, by building respectful relations, learning the history, describing positionality, emphasizing intentionality in material and nonmaterial usages, and prioritizing regenerative processes that restore ecological and human systems. , This is done through Indigenous ceremonial practices (with traditional protocols). While these practices have a spiritual dimension, in the context of this Perspective, we will only discuss their cultural, ethical, relational and intentional aspects, which we think are relevant for sustainability research. Such ceremonial practices can inspire deep interconnectedness with nature, which can enrich science in terms of purpose. We acknowledge and value that science requires explanations that can be tested, measured, and potentially disproven. Ceremonial practices suggested here are not in contradiction with such practices; rather, they serve to reinforce connection and commitment between scientists and what they study. These perspectives can coexist and reinforce each other, in a way that enriches research, its purpose, knowledge, and changes needed. The type of connection that happens in a ceremony is increasingly found in Indigenous research. Traditional protocols like ceremony are important to understand, honor, and respect. As Wilson explains, research can be understood as a ceremony, a ritual observance and cultural practice, wherein knowledge creation is grounded in our relationships and the quality of our interactions to maintain relational accountability. These concepts are not implemented in Western research approaches, where disconnection from the object of study is seen as a necessary means to ensure objectivity. We realize that the current grand challenges we face, such as climate change or biodiversity loss, are not being solved with reductionism. Ceremony offers a framework that is better suited to achieving the needed integral, interconnected, and holistic analysis level. Practicing ceremony in organic chemistry

The following is an example of how an Indigenous perspective could benefit sustainable chemistry science and engineering. Take for example a group of organic chemists. They know that if they discover a new sequence of reactions that opens new approaches to organic synthesis, it would be a remarkable discovery, likely leading to publications and recognition. This is how Western science operates: novel discoveries are a way of advancing science and engineering, and thus discovery is a motivation and a goal. In an Indigenized approach, the process would start with a ceremony. During the ceremony the chemists would discuss the approach that they wish to take, what could be the ramifications if successful, and how necessary the research is to discover this novel synthesis pathway. This may lead to the decision that the research is not warranted or too risky. Similar concepts have also been suggested through the notion of intentional design.

Indigenous scholar Dr. Blair Stonechild states:One can master chemistry textbooks full of formulas. It appears at first glance that Indigenous knowledge has nothing to contribute. Yet, too many chemicals are produced that are destroying the environment not to mention poisoning people. Pharmaceuticals of dubious use are being pumped out primarily to produce profit. If there was Indigenous chemistry, production of chemicals that destroy the environment and kill people would not be allowed. Chemistry infused with an Indigenous perspective and a sacred dimension would ponder whether seven generations would benefit prior to producing drugs.

Working directly with Indigenous colleagues or communities is a way to access such knowledge. Prior to performing research in such a context, a scholar may invite, through traditional protocols and honorariums, a ceremony. Through this thoughtful approach, the intentions and goals of a project become a strong vision for moving forward, beyond a simple verbal commitment. Through honoring Indigenous relationality, reconciliation, and knowledge, collaborators engage in research that is embodied in reciprocal relationships, humanizing our work. , It is important to acknowledge that only Indigenous people can lead such ceremonies. This requires an involvement in forming relationships and participating in local Indigenous community activitiesto build relationships, to understand and to implement traditional protocols, to learn the history, and to be involved with reconciliation efforts. For new practitioners on Turtle Island, this can be a challenging and culturally foreign process, yet to remain rooted in relationship and intentionality, we can cultivate this as an ethical guidance so it may be practiced by all, in all contexts. The notion of intention may also be expressed by researchers in the form of a declaration in their acknowledgment that a specific research entails environmental impacts and that specific measures were taken to mitigate those impacts, as encouraged by Grogan et al.

Conclusion and Call to Action

As sustainability learners and practitioners, we are at a crossroads akin to the prophecy of the Seven Fires, where the choice is between paths of destruction or regeneration. The integration of Indigenous wisdom offers a pathway toward harmony, guided by values of reciprocity, respect, and interconnectedness. We have the responsibility to listen, learn, and act with purpose, humility and courage. Find solitude and quiet to listen to our inner voice and to deeply and actively listen to understand others. We also need to be accountable and hold ourselves ethically responsible for our actions. We must acknowledge and reduce the environmental impact of our research activities (e.g., lab fumes, refrigeration, travel, etc.) by adopting more sustainable approaches and transparently declaring our carbon footprint. , Lab sustainability programs are effective methods to reach actionable results on that front. , Adding to this, we could also declare our commitment to address specific TRC goals through our research, as well as the UN SDGs.

This Perspective calls for the sustainable research and teaching community to deepen its commitment to regenerative practices by honoring Indigenous knowledge systems and to reflect on one’s actions and intentional research and teaching practices, as a leader. Together, through intentional collaboration with one’s community, we can cocreate a better path forward and invent meaningful solutions that restore balance to our shared planet and all the life it gives. As we are laying down this strong call for action, we also reflect on the obstacles for early career scientists to apply these principles, while being in the quest for early grants, tenure, and peers’ recognition.

WE CALL for you to recognize your position, to listen actively, and to openly engage as a humble learner among your relationships.

We CALL for you to declare environmental impacts of your research and measures taken for mitigation. For instance, “I/We would like to acknowledge that completing my/our research entailed multiple environmental impacts.... Several specific measures were deliberately taken by the authors to reduce the environmental impacts ...”.

WE CALL for better integration of Indigenous knowledge system approaches into sustainable research through appropriate frameworks. Consider the use of charters to work with Indigenous knowledge keepers and community members and attend conferences at the nexus of Indigenous knowledge and science, such as the Turtle Island Indigenous Science Conference, which took place in 2024 at the University of Regina.

WE CALL for researchers to transparently acknowledge the ways they reduce the environmental impact of their research activities. We can identify and articulate explicitly the actions taken to minimize, reduce, or counteract the environmental impact research activities have had.

WE CALL for participation in a “Truth Walk,” a land-based, experiential practice in which participants walk together through place, pausing at meaningful sites to share stories, knowledge, and reflections about the histories of the land and its Indigenous peoples. Through collective listening, dialogue, and embodied engagement with place, Truth Walk supports truth-telling and learning as part of ongoing reconciliation. Truth walks are a way to engage in Indigenous activities to learn about the cultural and reconciliation practices of the communities (e.g., First Nation territory, community, public activities). Looking for and participating in Indigenous-led activities, such as Pow Wows or visits to cultural centers and museums, can be a powerful way to engage humbly in dialogue and develop a relational connection. Courses and books, some of which are referenced in this Perspective, are another mechanism for education.

WE CALL sustainability researchers to consider intention and purpose in all aspects of their work, to be truly impactful. Consider the holistic impact of your work: on yourself, your community, and the living ecosystem, and so consider the next seven generations.

WE CALL sustainability researchers to connect, include, and integrate Nature, and all Living Things into their thinking, at the inception, creation, practice, and dissemination of research. We need to remember we are a part of Nature and thus a part of the system we study.

WE CALL on researchers to demonstrate the courage to reach out to stakeholders and the wider communityincluding Indigenous voiceswhen confronted with reductionist and simplistic views of sustainability, amounting to greenwashing. While such initiatives may claim to advance sustainability, they often serve to prolong the same extractive, limitless, and rapid-growth paradigm embedded in the current dominant Western development model. Engaging collaboratively and critically, using evidence-based approaches and metrics, researchers can resist such pressures, uphold accountability, and contribute to meaningful transformations that advance sustainability.

WE CALL for welcoming our Indigenous colleagues in the conversation to cocreate our common future, building with them long-term relationships founded in respect, humility, and reciprocity. Consider integrating Truth and Reconciliation Calls to Action, United Nations Declaration of Indigenous Peoples, SDGs, First Nations Water Declarations, treaties, principles and Indigenous worldviews into your work. Specific actions can be taken to include Indigenous colleagues and students, which include acknowledging barriers, making space, building long-term relationships with communities, and collaborating toward cocreation. ,

Through these calls, taking the extra time and thoughtful consideration necessary to embrace Indigenous worldviews as an experienced or new practitioner, you will add value to your work, we will learn and develop deeper, more impactful, effective solutions to our communities, and shape the Canadian and global academic landscape in a stronger, more meaningful way.
