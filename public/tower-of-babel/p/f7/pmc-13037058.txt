Ophthalmic policy implementation in Ethiopia: barriers and enablers from a mixed-methods stakeholder study
Authors: Zelalem Addisu Mehari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVision impairment remains a major public health challenge in Ethiopia. Although national eye health strategies are aligned with global initiatives, persistent gaps exist between policy intent and service delivery. This study examined the barriers and enablers influencing the implementation of ophthalmic policy from the perspectives of key stakeholders.MethodsA nationwide mixed-methods survey was conducted between June and August 2025 across nine regions of Ethiopia. Of 270 invited participants, 221 responded (81.9%), including ophthalmic professionals (n = 118), patients (n = 82), policymakers (n = 9), NGO/INGO representatives (n = 8), and international experts (n = 4). Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, bivariate analyses, and regression models, while qualitative data were thematically analysed and integrated using a convergent mixed-methods approach.ResultsOverall service satisfaction was high, at 93.8% (95% CI: 90.1–96.1). Patients residing within 5 km of a facility were significantly more likely to report satisfaction compared with those travelling more than 20 km (OR = 18.0; 95% CI: 1.4–236.1). No demographic variables significantly predicted perceived policy implementation in ordinal regression analyses, despite acceptable model fit. Qualitative findings identified chronic funding constraints, workforce shortages, and donor dependency as major implementation barriers, while policy alignment and professional commitment emerged as key enabling factors.ConclusionOphthalmic policy implementation in Ethiopia is primarily driven by systemic capacity and geographic access, rather than individual professional characteristics. Strengthening sustainable financing, improving workforce distribution, and expanding locally accessible services are critical to narrowing policy–practice gaps and advancing equitable eye health in Ethiopia and similar low- and middle-income settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14245-3.

Background

Vision impairment is a major global public health concern with profound health, social, and economic consequences. Globally, an estimated 2.2 billion people live with vision impairment or blindness, of whom at least half have conditions that are preventable or treatable through timely and effective interventions [1]. Cataract and uncorrected refractive error alone account for more than three-quarters of avoidable vision loss worldwide. Beyond its health impact, vision impairment imposes a substantial economic burden, with global productivity losses estimated to exceed US$400 billion annually [2].

The burden of vision impairment is disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which account for over 90% of affected populations [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears a particularly heavy share, reflecting a convergence of a high disease burden, constrained health system capacity, and limited access to specialist eye care services. Although the region carries nearly 25% of the global disease burden, it is served by only 3% of the global health workforce [3]. Eye health personnel—including ophthalmologists, ophthalmic nurses, and optometrists—remain scarce and are predominantly concentrated in urban centres, thereby reinforcing geographic and socioeconomic inequities in service delivery [4].

Health financing remains a central determinant of access to ophthalmic care. Despite political commitments such as the Abuja Declaration, whereby African countries pledged to allocate 15% of national budgets to health, most SSA countries continue to fall short of this target [5]. Out-of-pocket expenditure remains high, accounting for up to 40% of total health spending in many countries—well above the recommended threshold of 20%—placing essential eye care services beyond the reach of many households [6]. Although mechanisms such as community-based health insurance (CBHI) and donor-supported programmes have expanded access in some settings, concerns persist regarding sustainability, equity, and integration within eye health financing systems [7].

In ophthalmology, robust health policies and sustainable financing frameworks are essential for scaling effective interventions. Investments in human resources, decentralised service delivery, and performance-based financing have shown promise in improving service coverage and efficiency in several contexts [8]. However, persistent challenges—including high service costs, weak referral systems, geographic inaccessibility, and insufficient integration of eye health into broader health systems—continue to constrain progress, particularly in resource-limited settings [8].

Ethiopia reflects many of these regional challenges. National estimates indicate that the prevalence of vision impairment exceeds global averages, driven primarily by cataract, trachoma, glaucoma, and uncorrected refractive errors [9]. The country has demonstrated policy commitment through initiatives such as the Health Extension Program, the National Eye Health Strategic Plan, and alignment with global eye health agendas. However, implementation gaps remain pronounced. Eye health financing continues to rely heavily on external donors [10], monitoring and evaluation systems are limited, and the ophthalmic workforce remains unevenly distributed, restricting service coverage in rural and emerging regions [4].

Despite growing global attention to integrated health systems and equitable financing, empirical evidence on the implementation of ophthalmic policy in Ethiopia remains limited. In particular, there is a lack of data linking policy design to frontline implementation, service access, and equity outcomes across multiple stakeholder groups. Addressing these gaps is essential for informing context-sensitive reforms and advancing Universal Eye Health Coverage.

This study addresses these gaps by examining the barriers and enablers of ophthalmic policy implementation in Ethiopia through a mixed-methods, multistakeholder approach. By integrating perspectives from professionals, patients, policymakers, non-governmental organisations, and international experts, the study generates actionable evidence to support more equitable, sustainable, and system-integrated eye health policy in Ethiopia and comparable LMIC settings.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analysed separately, and integrated during interpretation. This design enabled quantitative patterns related to access, satisfaction, and policy implementation to be complemented by qualitative insights reflecting stakeholder experiences and contextual realities.

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted across nine regions of Ethiopia—Addis Ababa, Afar, Amhara, Central Ethiopia, Oromia, Sidama, South Ethiopia, Dire Dawa, and Tigray—selected to represent geographic, administrative, and health system diversity. These regions encompass urban, semi-urban, and rural contexts, varied levels of ophthalmic service availability, and differing degrees of policy implementation maturity.

Analyses were conducted with explicit attention to stakeholder roles, and outcome measures were interpreted within the context of each participant group rather than as uniform perceptions across all groups.

Sample size and sampling strategy

The quantitative sample size was estimated using the single population proportion formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 10% margin of error, and a conservative expected proportion of 50% to maximise variability in the absence of prior national estimates on ophthalmic policy implementation perceptions. This yielded an initial target sample of 270 participants, adjusted to account for potential non-response.

A total of 221 respondents completed the survey (response rate: 81.9%). This included 100 patients and 121 non-patient stakeholders, comprising ophthalmic professionals, policymakers, NGO representatives, and international experts. Ophthalmic professionals were selected proportionally by cadre to reflect the composition of the eye health workforce, while patients were recruited consecutively at service delivery points.

For the qualitative component, 30 key informants were purposively selected from senior clinicians, policymakers, NGO representatives, and international experts based on their direct involvement in the design, implementation, or oversight of eye health policy. While purposive sampling enhanced the relevance and depth of policy-related insights, the findings may not be fully generalisable beyond the selected regions and stakeholder groups.

Survey instrument development and data collection

The research team developed structured questionnaires based on a review of health systems, eye health policy, and implementation science literature, including the WHO Health Systems Framework. Instruments were contextually designed rather than directly adapted from existing validated tools, given the absence of standardised instruments specific to ophthalmic policy implementation in Ethiopia.

Separate but conceptually aligned questionnaires were developed for five respondent groups (patients, ophthalmic professionals, policymakers, NGO representatives, and international experts). Each included closed-ended items to capture sociodemographic characteristics, access to services, satisfaction, and perceptions of policy implementation, alongside open-ended questions that allowed respondents to elaborate on barriers, enablers, and their lived experiences. Patient questionnaires were administered in the local language by trained interviewers using a pre-existing Amharic version of the instrument.

The instruments underwent expert review to assess content relevance and clarity, followed by a pilot test involving a small sample of respondents from each stakeholder group. Feedback from the pilot informed minor wording revisions, improved response options, and ensured cultural and contextual appropriateness. The interviewer administered the final patient questionnaire due to literacy considerations, while the remaining surveys were self-administered using KoboToolbox. The full English version of the survey instrument is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethiopian Public Health Association Institutional Review Board (IRB). All participants were informed about the study objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Written or verbal informed consent was obtained prior to data collection, and a copy of the informed consent form is provided as a supplementary document. Data were anonymised and securely stored, accessible only to the research team.

Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, education, and residence), service-related factors (facility type, waiting time, and distance to facility), professional characteristics (cadre and years of experience), and policy-related perceptions (systemic barriers, international collaboration, and interdisciplinary approaches).

Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS version 29. Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics, while associations were assessed using chi-square tests, t-tests, or ANOVA as appropriate. Binary logistic and ordinal regression models were fitted to identify predictors of satisfaction and perceived policy implementation. Model assumptions were checked, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Qualitative data from open-ended responses were coded and thematically analysed using health systems and implementation science constructs as analytical guides. Findings from the qualitative and quantitative strands were integrated through methodological triangulation to enhance interpretive depth and validity. Themes were developed through iterative coding guided by the analytical framework, with convergence assessed across data sources and coding decisions discussed within the research team.

Result

Participant characteristics

A total of 221 respondents participated in the study, comprising 100 patients and 121 non-patient stakeholders (ophthalmic professionals, policymakers, NGO representatives, and international experts). Ophthalmic professionals (N = 118) included optometrists (36.4%), ophthalmologists (27.1%), cataract surgeons (18.6%), ophthalmic nurses (15.3%), and ophthalmic officers (2.5%). Participants were drawn from nine regions of Ethiopia and were predominantly employed in public facilities (66.4%), followed by those working in NGO-supported institutions (26.8%) and private institutions (6.8%). Nearly 59% of professionals reported more than five years of experience.

Factors associated with ophthalmic policy implementation

Perceived policy implementation was significantly associated with institutional context and resource availability, rather than individual professional characteristics. Respondents working in NGO-supported facilities reported stronger policy implementation compared with those in public institutions (χ² = 14.63, df = 2, p = 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.35). Similarly, resource adequacy—including availability of trained personnel, diagnostic equipment, consumables, and essential medicines—was strongly associated with implementation effectiveness (χ² = 18.47, df = 2, p < 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.40).

By contrast, professional cadre (r = 0.051, p = 0.585) and years of experience (r = − 0.019, p = 0.835) were not significantly associated with perceived implementation, indicating that barriers were primarily systemic and institutional rather than individual in nature.

Policy awareness and professional experience

Overall, 72% of non-patient respondents reported some familiarity with Ethiopia’s National Eye Health Strategic Plan, while only 48% perceived effective implementation within their institutions. Familiarity increased modestly with professional experience. Respondents with more than ten years of experience were more likely to report being “very familiar” with national policy (25.0%) compared with those with less than five years’ experience (3.8%).

Although the overall association between experience and familiarity was not statistically significant (χ² = 9.23, df = 6, p = 0.16), a significant linear trend was observed (linear-by-linear χ² = 4.19, df = 1, p = 0.041), indicating a gradual increase in policy awareness with longer tenure. Correlation coefficients indicated a small effect size (Pearson r = − 0.189, p = 0.040). Full cross-tabulations and test statistics are presented in Table 1.

Institutional and regional barriers to implementation

Across regions and facility types, respondents consistently identified resource-related constraints as the dominant barriers to ophthalmic policy implementation. Overall, 78.8% reported inadequate equipment or supplies, 55% shortages of trained personnel, 51.7% low policy awareness, 43.2% limited political commitment, and 41.5% weak monitoring and evaluation systems.

Qualitative insights on the policy–practice gap

Qualitative findings from policymakers, NGO representatives, and senior clinicians reinforced the quantitative results and helped explain the mechanisms underlying observed implementation gaps. Four interrelated themes emerged:

(1) chronic underfunding and donor dependency;

(2) workforce shortages and maldistribution;

(3) weak referral and infrastructure systems; and

(4) limited data availability and monitoring capacity.

Participants consistently highlighted financial sustainability as a central constraint. As one NGO representative noted, “Most eye care programs depend heavily on donor priorities, which makes long-term planning difficult.” This reliance on external funding was widely perceived as limiting continuity and scale-up of services, particularly in underserved regions.

Workforce constraints were also emphasised across professional groups. An ophthalmic professional explained, “even when policies exist, implementation is limited by shortages of trained personnel outside major cities,” underscoring the mismatch between national policy aspirations and subnational service capacity.

Despite these challenges, policymakers acknowledged strong policy coherence at the strategic level. One policymaker observed, “There is strong alignment between national strategies and global eye health goals, but implementation remains uneven at the facility level.” Collectively, these accounts illustrate how structural and institutional constraints mediate the translation of policy intent into practice.

Barriers and facilitators identified by key informants

Among senior stakeholders (N = 8), funding constraints and workforce shortages were most frequently cited as barriers, followed by referral inefficiencies and gaps in patient follow-up. Facilitators included alignment with Universal Health Coverage and Primary Health Care agendas, NGO-supported training and outreach initiatives, and recent inclusion of eye health indicators within the national health information system. Respondents emphasised the importance of better integration of partner activities into government-led planning to improve sustainability.

Qualitative interviews with NGO and international stakeholders highlighted recurring barriers to ophthalmic care delivery, including fragmented donor funding, short-term project cycles, and limited integration with national health system priorities. Participants emphasised the need for more substantial alignment between external support mechanisms and domestic policy frameworks.

Patient access, satisfaction, and utilisation

Patient-level analysis (valid N = 82) demonstrated how system-level constraints translated into access barriers. Most patients received care at government hospitals (41.5%) or NGO/mission clinics (39%). Satisfaction did not differ significantly by facility type (χ² = 8.50, df = 9, p = 0.484).

Overall patient satisfaction was high (93.8%, 95% CI: 90.1–96.1), and most patients reported willingness to recommend the facility to others. Patients reported moderate-to-long travel distances (mean = 2.63, SD = 0.90) and moderate waiting times (mean = 2.44, SD = 0.71). Common barriers included distance, a shortage of eye-care professionals, long waiting times, and limited awareness. Nearly 40% accessed services through free outreach programmes, though these were described as episodic and donor-dependent.

Integrated quantitative–qualitative synthesis

Across stakeholder groups, findings converged on a central pattern: policy alignment exists, but systemic capacity gaps constrain implementation. Quantitative analyses highlighted the importance of institutional resources and NGO-supported environments, while qualitative findings emphasised financing sustainability, workforce development, and monitoring systems as critical leverage points. Discussion

This mixed-methods study demonstrates that while Ethiopia’s ophthalmic policies are closely aligned with global initiatives such as Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and Vision 2030, their translation into routine practice remains constrained by systemic and structural barriers. The findings suggest that implementation challenges are driven more by persistent limitations in financing, workforce capacity, and institutional coordination, rather than deficiencies in policy design. These results highlight a critical policy–practice gap, underscoring the need to complement strategic alignment with sustained investments in health-system readiness and governance capacity.

Policy alignment and the implementation gap

Although awareness of the National Eye Health Strategic Plan among ophthalmic professionals was relatively high, perceptions of effective implementation were considerably lower. This disconnect reinforces earlier analyses of health policy implementation in Ethiopia, which have consistently shown that policy adoption often outpaces execution due to weak operationalisation, insufficient resource mobilisation, and limited accountability mechanisms [11, 12]. The finding that NGO-supported facilities demonstrated stronger implementation outcomes suggests that external partnerships can temporarily compensate for public-sector capacity gaps. However, as documented in other low- and middle-income country (LMIC) settings, reliance on parallel donor-supported systems may inadvertently undermine long-term sustainability if these initiatives are not fully integrated into national governance and financing structures [13, 14].

From an implementation science perspective, this divergence reflects a classic “implementation gap,” wherein formal policy commitments are not accompanied by the institutional capacity, managerial support, or local ownership required for effective delivery [15]. Ethiopia’s experience aligns with Gilson’s conceptualisation of a “policy-to-practice disjuncture,” emphasising the importance of coordinated governance, decentralised accountability, and predictable financing as prerequisites for translating national strategies into frontline service improvements [12].

Health workforce capacity and distribution

Health workforce capacity and distribution emerged as central determinants of ophthalmic policy implementation. The inverse relationship between years of professional experience and perceived policy effectiveness suggests that more experienced providers may be more acutely aware of systemic constraints, including workforce shortages, inadequate infrastructure, and service delivery bottlenecks. These perceptions are consistent with national and regional evidence documenting chronic shortages of skilled eye-care personnel, marked urban–rural maldistribution, and limited opportunities for continuing professional development [4, 16, 17].

Notably, the convergence of reported barriers across professional cadres indicates that implementation challenges are structural rather than individual. This finding supports broader human resources for health (HRH) literature, which emphasises that without supportive systems—such as equitable deployment policies, career progression pathways, and enabling work environments—individual expertise alone cannot compensate for systemic weaknesses [18]. Addressing workforce-related barriers, therefore, requires coordinated policy responses that extend beyond training to encompass retention, incentives, and institutional support.

Financing and system sustainability

Financial constraints were consistently identified as the most persistent barrier to effective policy implementation. Respondents highlighted donor dependency, irregular supply chains, and underfunded operational budgets—findings that mirror prior analyses of Ethiopia’s health financing reforms and fiscal space limitations [19]. Within the WHO Health Systems Framework, stable and predictable financing is essential to prevent fragmentation and ensure continuity of essential services, including eye care [20].

Although Ethiopia has taken steps to integrate eye health into the Health Sector Transformation Plan II and has adopted pooled procurement mechanisms, the findings indicate that domestic financing remains insufficient to support the comprehensive and equitable implementation of eye health initiatives. This pattern reflects broader national prioritisation dynamics, where competing public health needs constrain fiscal allocations to eye health. Similar challenges have been documented in Kenya, Ghana, Nigeria, and Tanzania, where efforts to integrate eye care into primary healthcare and national insurance schemes have been shaped by available fiscal space and political commitment [21]. These regional parallels underscore that the sustainability of eye health initiatives is closely linked to the structure and stability of domestic financing arrangements.

Patient access and service quality

Patient perspectives revealed that geographic distance, transport costs, and waiting times continue to pose significant barriers to accessing ophthalmic services. These challenges align with the Social Determinants of Health framework, which emphasises how non-medical factors shape health-seeking behaviour and service utilisation [22, 23]. Despite these access constraints, reported satisfaction levels were high, driven mainly by positive interactions between providers and patients, respectful care, and effective communication.

This apparent paradox is well explained by Donabedian’s model of quality of care, which distinguishes between structural, process, and outcome dimensions. While structural limitations persist, strong interpersonal processes—such as empathy and responsiveness [24]. Nevertheless, reliance on relational strengths alone is insufficient to achieve equity. Expanding decentralised service delivery, community-based screening, and transport support mechanisms would help address structural barriers and promote more equitable utilisation.

Partnerships, outreach, and equity implications

Partnerships with NGOs and international actors were found to play a dual role in Ethiopia’s eye health system. On the one hand, they provide essential resources, training, and support for service delivery; on the other, they risk perpetuating dependency and fragmentation if not aligned with national priorities. Consistent with evidence from other LMICs, uncoordinated vertical programmes may exacerbate inequities and undermine system coherence [25].

Outreach and mobile eye-care campaigns, accessed by a substantial proportion of patients, remain an important mechanism for reaching underserved populations. However, their episodic nature limits long-term impact. Institutionalising outreach within regional health budgets and linking campaigns to referral, follow-up, and surveillance systems would enhance sustainability and equity. Evidence from India similarly suggests that vertical interventions are most effective when progressively integrated into broader health system structures, rather than operating in isolation [26].

Policy implications and global relevance

The findings of this study indicate that barriers to ophthalmic policy implementation in Ethiopia are fundamentally systemic rather than technical. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated, long-term reforms across three interrelated domains: (1) institutionalising sustainable domestic financing for eye health within national and regional budgets; (2) strengthening workforce development through equitable deployment, incentives, and continuing professional education; and (3) embedding eye health indicators within the national DHIS2 platform to improve monitoring, accountability, and learning [27, 28].

Comparative evidence from Kenya, Ghana, and Tanzania reveals implementation challenges closely aligned with those identified in this study, including fragmented financing, workforce shortages, and the weak integration of eye care into primary healthcare systems [3, 21]. These parallels suggest that Ethiopia’s experience reflects broader structural patterns across LMIC health systems rather than isolated policy failure. Recognising these shared constraints provides valuable comparative insight and reinforces the importance of context-sensitive, system-oriented reforms to advance universal and equitable eye health coverage [25].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study has several methodological strengths. First, the nationwide mixed-methods design, covering nine regions and multiple stakeholder groups (professionals, patients, policymakers, NGOs, and international experts), enabled a comprehensive systems-level assessment of ophthalmic policy implementation in Ethiopia. Second, the integration of quantitative and qualitative data using a convergent approach strengthened interpretive validity by allowing triangulation between statistical associations (e.g., resource adequacy, facility type) and stakeholder narratives describing policy–practice gaps. Third, the inclusion of patient-level access and satisfaction data provided an important equity lens, linking policy implementation challenges to the lived experiences of service. Finally, the study contributes methodologically by applying an interdisciplinary policy analysis framework that integrates health systems, implementation science, and equity perspectives within the context of eye health.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference regarding relationships between policy implementation and institutional or contextual factors. Reliance on self-reported measures may introduce recall or social desirability bias, particularly in assessments of policy familiarity and the effectiveness of implementation. Although regional representation was broad, uneven sample sizes across regions and stakeholder categories—notably policymakers and international experts—may limit the generalisability of subgroup comparisons. Although the overall response rate was acceptable, differences between respondents and non-respondents could not be formally assessed, and non-response bias may have influenced estimates of satisfaction and perceived policy implementation. Finally, the study focused on perceived implementation rather than direct measurement of policy outputs or clinical outcomes, which should be addressed in future longitudinal and implementation-focused research.

Recommendations

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the implementation of ophthalmic policy in Ethiopia is constrained less by individual professional characteristics and more by systemic capacity, institutional resources, and geographic access. By integrating stakeholder perspectives across policy, service delivery, and patient experience, the study advances understanding of how policy–practice gaps emerge within low-resource eye health systems. The findings underscore the importance of strengthening financing, workforce distribution, and monitoring mechanisms to translate policy alignment into effective service delivery. These insights are highly relevant for Ethiopia and for other low- and middle-income countries seeking to operationalise eye health policies within broader UHC and primary health care agendas.
