Insights into Pre-COVID sudden cardiac deaths-: An autopsy study in South India
Authors: Dharma Saranya Gurusamy, Gopika Venkataraju, Vasuki Selvamurugesan, Shantaraman Kalyanaraman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Sudden cardiac death poses the major healthcare crisis globally and is becoming a significant concern in India. There is a scarcity of comprehensive data on the prevalence, contributing factors, and underlying causes in the Indian context. This study aims to identify demographic patterns, key cardiac pathologies, and associated lifestyle factors, in cases of sudden cardiac death in the South Indian population and to provide insights for public health interventions.Materials and Methods:This single-center cross-sectional study was conducted in a government tertiary care center in South India, involving autopsies registered from 2017–2019 for identification of the cause of death. Demographic data, relevant medical history, and events preceding death were collected from the relatives of the victims and medical records. Patients with a history of trauma, positive toxicological findings, and pathological findings in other organs were excluded from the study. Detailed gross and microscopic examinations of heart samples were performed to assess coronary and myocardial pathologies.Results:The mean age was 48 years, with a male preponderance. Lifestyle factors, including a lack of physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption, were the critical factors contributing to sudden death in the younger age group. coronary artery disease (CAD), predominantly acute Myocardial Infarction (MI), was identified as the leading cause of death. Surprisingly, 41% of the patients with histopathological changes of MI in myocardium showed less than severe stenosis in the coronaries.Conclusion:Coronary artery disease, particularly acute MI, is the primary cause of sudden cardiac death in South India, with lifestyle factors such as smoking, physical inactivity, and alcohol abuse as significant contributors. Higher coronary atherosclerotic plaque burden is associated with major adverse cardiac events. This study underscores the need for molecular autopsies, lifestyle-focused interventions, early CAD detection, and Cardio-pulmonary resuscitation (CPR) training. Establishing a sudden cardiac death (SCD) registry and enhancing resuscitation measures are crucial for reducing its impact.

Introduction

Sudden cardiac death (SCD) is “a natural death due to cardiac causes, heralded by abrupt loss of consciousness, within 1 hour after the onset of acute symptoms or an unwitnessed, unexpected death of someone seen in a stable medical condition less than 24 hour previously with no evidence of a noncardiac cause”.[1] SCD is estimated to account for 50% of cardiovascular deaths[1] and 15%–20% of deaths in Western societies.[2] A questionnaire-based study in South India roughly estimates that the number of sudden deaths in India could be 700,000 per year.[3] Despite sudden cardiac death being a leading cause of mortality in India, epidemiological data and studies on SCD are scarce.

In developed countries, a structured emergency system receives patients; hence, an estimate of in-hospital and out-of-hospital sudden cardiac arrest and death in the first hour of symptoms is available. A postmortem examination is crucial for establishing a diagnosis.[4] With current autopsy guidelines, establishing a postmortem diagnosis is challenging, as nearly 30%–50% of cases show unspecified cardiac findings inadequate to ascertain the cause of death.[5] Identification of cases with subtle autopsy findings may help in assessing the need for other alternative options, such as genetic testing and molecular autopsy, to evaluate rare cardiac disorders and inherited cardiac diseases that play a causative role in SCD. This will aid in the appropriate treatment of family members at risk in cases of inherited cardiac diseases.[6] There is a lack of data on the magnitude, risk factors, and underlying pathology of SCD in the Indian population. The advent of COVID-19 pandemic has further compounded the cardiovascular health landscape. Solving the uncertainties in the cause of sudden cardiac death and characterizing the patterns of SCD may aid in effective risk factor management and therapeutic strategies to reduce cardiovascular mortality in India. This study aims to identify demographic patterns, histopathological findings, and lifestyle factors contributing to SCD in rural populations, establish a baseline for future studies in the Indian context, and provide insights for targeted health interventions.

Materials and Methods

Patient selection

Study design

This was a single-center cross-sectional study conducted at a government tertiary care center in South India, for a duration of 2 years 2017–2019.

Study population

The study population included autopsies registered to identify the cause of sudden death. The study population comprised of unwitnessed cases where the deceased person was seen alive and functioning normally not more than 24 hour before being found dead and witnessed cases with rapid changes in cardiovascular status less than 1 hour before death. Patients with significant pathological changes in other organs and for whom toxicological evaluation indicated a likely toxicological outcome were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated based on a previous study[3] that reported a contribution of 10% deaths by SCD. Based on this and taking 7% relative precision and 95% confidence, a sample size of 75 was calculated. A convenience sampling technique was adopted with the aim of including as many eligible cases as possible during the study period. Data collection was done between April 2017 and October 2019.

Study procedure

Details about the chronological evolution of the events that led to death, time and place of death, previous medical history, details of regular physical activity such as walking and exercise, details of treatment, and prior surgical procedures or interventions were recorded through interviews with bystanders/witnesses to the event, first-degree family members, relatives, and friends in the scene of death, in a structured data collection format with proper consent. The overall study methodology is illustrated in Figure 1 in the supplementary material.

Autopsy

Autopsy was performed via a standard T-shaped incision, and all regions and organs of the body were studied. The heart was studied in accordance with the guidelines of the Royal College of Pathologists, London, under the “Guidelines on autopsy practice: Sudden death with likely cardiac pathology; July 2015.[7] Cardiac weight was assessed after all blood/clots were removed. The pericardium was checked for adhesion, effusion, exudate, and hemorrhage. The pulmonary artery was checked for emboli. The two coronary artery origins and distribution on the heart surface were dissected to exclude congenital anomalies. The hearts were examined for coronary grafts and stents. The chamber wall thickness was measured. The atrioventricular junction was checked for an intact tricuspid valve. The left atrium was checked for an intact mitral valve and prolapse. The mitral valve, atrial/ventricular junction, and the left ventricular outflow tract were checked. The aortic valve and coronary openings were examined. Zones of fresh or previous infarction were identified and quantified. Fat replacement and scarring were detected in the myocardium. Infiltrates were identified by the general thickening and irregular stiffness of the myocardium. Photographs were taken, wherever needed. Mapped blocks of the anterior, lateral, and posterior walls of the right and the left ventricles and septum from a representative mid-ventricular transverse slice and the right ventricular outflow tract were taken for histopathological examination.

The coronary arteries were dissected and cut transversely at 3 mm intervals, and the grade of stenosis was assessed by visual inspection. In cases of complex plaque morphologies, correlation with microscopic evaluation was conducted to assess the stenosis. Coronary occlusion of 70% in hearts with ischemic changes was considered severe stenosis.[7] Coronaries with lesser grades of occlusion were considered to have less than severe stenosis. The coronaries were also examined for rupture, thrombosis, and evidence of acute plaque changes. The coronary artery tissues were blocked and sectioned. The histological features were observed for qualitative and quantitative changes, and photographs were taken.

Sudden cardiac death was concluded as the cause of death when the autopsy revealed changes in the heart with no contributory evidence elsewhere or when no changes were found in the heart and no contributory evidence was identified in other organs.[7]

Definitions

Myocardial infarction (MI): Patients with wavy fibers, interstitial edema, and wavy coagulative necrosis, as shown by cytoplasmic hyper eosinophilia with loss of nuclei and neutrophilic infiltrates, were considered acute changes. Granulation tissue and dense collagen in the myocardium are considered chronic MI changes. Coronary stenosis: Coronary vessels with ≥70% occlusion of the lumen was considered vessels with severe stenosis. Patients with no histological changes associated with infarction in the myocardium but with occlusion of thrombi in the vessels were considered to have MI.[7] Coronary vessels with <70% occlusion of the lumen was considered less than severe stenoses. Cardiac hypertrophy: Patients whose heart weight was >500 g or whose left ventricular wall thickness was >15 mm was considered to have cardiac hypertrophy. Myocarditis: Patients with muscle fiber apoptosis and inflammatory infiltrates were considered to have myocarditis. Structurally normal heart: Autopsies with no cardiac or extracardiac-related findings and all measures within the normal range[89] were considered normal autopsies. Nondiagnostic autopsies: Autopsies with findings that do not meet the criteria for a structurally normal heart, or a specific disease were deemed nondiagnostic autopsies.[9] The findings contributing to nondiagnostic autopsies were unexplained cardiac hypertrophy (LV wall thickness >15 mm), abnormal heart weight (>500 g), the presence of coronary artery atheroma without significant lumen obstruction or ischemic changes in the myocardium, dilated chambers with focal thinning and fibrosis of the ventricular wall. All gross and histological findings were evaluated by two independent observers, who were blinded to the clinicopathological data. In the case of deviations, the observers analyzed the cases together and reached a consensus.

Statistical analysis

The data were gathered and sorted using Microsoft Excel 2013 based on the coronary artery findings and myocardial pathology findings. Statistical analysis were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0.2.0 (Released 2015; IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, United States).

Ethical committee approval

The Institute’s ethics committee [1101/PATHO/2017] approved the study.

Results

A total of 154 sudden death victims were subjected to autopsy, 36 of whom were excluded because they had morphological changes attributable to other etiologies, such as intracerebral bleeding, cerebral edema, pulmonary tuberculosis, peritoneal sepsis, pulmonary embolism, hepatic cirrhosis, renal disease, cancer, and starvation cachexia. Five of the 118 patients had significant toxicological findings, 11 patients had incomplete data on the event, and 2 patients had no relatives to provide sufficient medical history; hence, these patients were not included in the study. Thus, the study population included 100 victims of sudden cardiac death.

Demographic details of the study population

The distributions of demographic characteristics and key risk factors within the study population are tabulated in Table 1. The study included 100 participants, with an average age of 48.48 years (SD ± 13.88) and a maximum number of cases (56%) between 41 and 60 years. There was a male preponderance in the study population (94%), whereas females accounted for 6%. The nature of work varied among the participants, with 38% engaged in heavy work and 62% in light work. Among, the 100 patients, 46% were alcohol consumers, 34% were smokers, 30% had hypertension, and 21% had diabetes. A lack of regular physical activity was significantly observed in most (86%) of the victims.

A significant proportion of individuals aged 21–40 exhibited advanced atherosclerotic lesions and complicated plaques. Those aged 41–60 demonstrated the highest prevalence of severe stenosis, advanced atherosclerosis, and complicated plaques across all major coronary arteries. Discussion

In India, the incidence of sudden cardiac death remains inadequately estimated due to the limitations in vital event registration systems, particularly in rural areas. Our study encompasses 100 autopsy cases over 2 years to identify the causes and risk factors associated with SCD, aiming to inform early risk stratification and management approaches.

The mean age of 48.48 years suggests that the population is predominantly middle-aged. An earlier study in this region reported a mean age of 55 years,[10] whereas, other studies reported ages of 50–60 years[11] and 60 years.[3] Lifestyle-related risk factors such as the absence of regular physical activity (81.18%), alcoholism (46.96%) and smoking (36.36%) were more prevalent in the younger population than traditional risk factors such as diabetes (7.5%) and hypertension (10%) in this study. This finding highlights that younger individuals are impacted by traditional risk factors, such as those in older populations, as well as additional risk factors related to lifestyle that led to more severe and fatal effects in patients with coronary vascular disease. Our data concerning the absence of regular physical activity is like those of various studies in this region.[1213] Like previous studies, our study also emphasizes the adoption of regular physical activity as a potential solution for the burgeoning SCD epidemic in India.[14] MI was predominant, with acute MI (44%) accounting for the largest proportion of cases, suggesting that sudden ischemic events are a critical issue. Previous studies have shown that a substantial proportion of people experience out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) as the first, and the only expression of CAD and myocardial infarction is the key underlying pathology.[15] Sudden death due to MI may be caused by abnormal electrical activity of the myocardium, recurrent ischemia and myocardial rupture.[16] The risk of SCD is known to be greatest for one month following MI.[17] Chronic MI (22%) and acute-on-chronic MI (16%) reflect ongoing or recurrent ischemic injury, indicating the need for better long-term management of coronary artery disease. Enhanced automaticity triggered electrical activity and reentry mediated by myocardial scars can precipitate SCD in hearts with acute and chronic infarction changes.[18] Early identification and management of CAD and reduction of risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases are the best strategies.[16] In patients with OHCA, prompt and early CPR is also of paramount importance.[19] The standard CPR rates reported in studies in India range from 1.3%–9.8%.[2021] In line with previous studies, our observations also emphasize establishing an SCA registry and improving resuscitation measures to improve the survival of OHCA victims.[322]

Cardiac hypertrophy was significant in 43 patients. Cardiac hypertrophy may be due to HOCM, storage diseases and various genetic conditions. It may also be an indicator of cardiac end-organ damage due to long-term exposure to diabetes, hypertension, and milder forms of ischemia.[23]

In line with previous observations, many elderly people had patent coronaries. In elderly populations with unobstructive coronaries, acute coronary spasm, coronary endothelial dysfunction, or hypotension leading to ischemic imbalance can lead to MI in a susceptible senile myocardium, with decreased cardioprotective mechanisms.[24]

Histological examination of the myocardium in 41 cases with less than severe stenosis in the coronaries revealed features consistent with both acute and chronic MI. Our findings support prior landmark studies using invasive coronary angiography, which demonstrated that most culprit lesions in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) do not cause significant arterial narrowing.[25] There is growing support for the idea that the risk of sudden cardiac arrest is linked more to the overall burden of coronary atherosclerotic disease[26] than to the severity of coronary stenosis.[27] Thus, in addition to the anatomical and pathophysiological aspects of atherosclerosis, the cardiometabolic, immunomodulatory and endocrine effects of atherosclerosis in mediating microvascular dysfunction and ischemic conditioning of the myocardium also need to be explored. Hearts with noncontributory findings and normal hearts were observed together in 10% of the patients. These findings present diagnostic challenges to pathologists.[28] Thus, in line with previous reports, our study also supports the inclusion of evaluation of conduction system, genetic testing and metabolomics in the autopsy protocol for SCD.[29]

Our study has a few limitations. The limited sample size with a very small female population may be an underrepresentation of the actual community data, as only a few sudden cardiac death victims are brought for autopsy. The clinical data from bystanders and first-degree relatives may not be complete, which is a limitation inherent to autopsy studies relying on data provided by bystanders and relatives. Histological examinations were carried out on the lesions visible grossly, and other, less significant, smaller lesions might have been missed. The study lacks genetic and molecular data. Broader population-based studies are essential to validate our findings.

Conclusion

This autopsy-based study highlights the significant role of CAD, particularly acute MI, in SCD in rural South India. The findings underscore the prevalence of modifiable lifestyle factors, such as a lack of physical activity, smoking and alcoholism, as critical contributors, especially among middle-aged men. Notably, the high occurrence of less than severe coronary stenosis as a causative factor calls for a paradigm shift in understanding mechanisms, such as microvascular dysfunction, ischemic preconditioning of myocardium and arrhythmia in SCD. The results must be combined with broader population-based studies, including genetic and molecular autopsies, to draw precise conclusions. Community health initiatives aimed at lifestyle modifications, early CAD detection, establishment of robust CAD registries and the advancement of resuscitation measures will be pivotal in addressing this public health concern.
