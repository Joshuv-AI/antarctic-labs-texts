Dietary Sodium Intake and Risk of Incident Heart Failure in the Southern Community Cohort Study
Authors: Leonie Dupuis, Meng Xu, Audrey White, Debra D Dixon, Jane F Ferguson, Xiao-Ou Shu, Danxia Yu, Loren Lipworth, Deepak K Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association of dietary sodium intake with incident heart failure (HF) among individuals at high-risk for HF is not established.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to assess the association between dietary sodium intake and incident HF risk.MethodsWe examined 25,306 Southern Community Cohort Study participants (median age 54 years, 63% female, 69% Black, 87% <$25k annual income) without prevalent HF and with Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services coverage at enrollment (2002-2009). Dietary sodium intake at enrollment was assessed with a validated food frequency questionnaire. Incident HF was ascertained by Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services claims through 2016. Cox modeling was performed with population-attributable fraction of dietary sodium on incident HF calculated.ResultsThe mean dietary sodium intake was 4,269 ± 2,502 mg/day. Over 9.8 years median follow-up, 7,039 (27.8%) individuals developed HF. Each 1,000 mg/day greater sodium intake associated with an 8% (95% CI: 4%-11%) higher risk of incident HF, independent of sociodemographics, diet quality, calories, physical activity, and hyperlipidemia. Further adjustment for hypertension, body mass index, sleep, or coronary artery disease yielded similar results. The dietary sodium association with incident HF was similar among individuals with diabetes (HR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.02-1.15). Population-attributable fraction analysis indicated reducing sodium intake to a maximum of 4,000 mg/day could eliminate 6.6% (95% CI: 3.6%-9.6%) of incident HF over 10 years.ConclusionsAmong low-income Black and White adults in the southeast United States, greater dietary sodium intake associates with a higher risk of incident HF. Modest reduction in sodium consumption may significantly reduce the burden of HF in this high-risk population.Key words: dietary sodium, heart failure, nutrition, prevention

Heart failure (HF) is a highly morbid and lethal condition increasing in prevalence.1 HF incidence is largely attributable to modifiable risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and physical inactivity, suggesting HF may be preventable.2 Within the United States, the greatest HF burden is in the Southeast.3 In the Southern Community Cohort Study (SCCS), a cohort comprised of predominantly low-income, Black, and female individuals, we previously found not only high incidence rates for HF, but also variable associations of modifiable risk factors with incident HF.2,4 For example, hypertension and diabetes strongly associated with the risk of incident HF, whereas the degree of alignment with dietary patterns, for example, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension or Healthy Eating Index, did not.2,5 The latter finding led us to examine sodium as an individual dietary component in association with HF risk.

Sodium is an essential dietary nutrient implicated in health and disease and average population level intake is considered excessive in most countries, although its consumption substantially varies across individuals.6 For most people, consumption of less sodium than that in their typical diet leads to lower blood pressure and incidence of hypertension.7, 8, 9 However, fewer data exist for the association between dietary sodium intake and incident HF.10, 11, 12, 13 Thus, in the SCCS, we examined the risk of incident HF associated with dietary sodium intake and the population-attributable fraction (PAF) of dietary sodium on incident HF.

Methods

Study population

The SCCS is an ongoing, prospective study designed to examine incidence of cancer and other chronic diseases, and to elucidate causes of differential susceptibility to disease in a predominantly low-income and Black population in the southeastern United States for which detailed methods have been previously published.14 Briefly, between 2002 and 2009, from 12 southeastern states, 84,797 individuals aged 40 to 79 years (65% Black, 65% female) were enrolled. The majority of participants (86%) enrolled through Community Health Centers, which provide health and preventive care mainly to the underserved, with the remainder enrolled via general population sampling. At enrollment, participants completed a questionnaire which ascertained sociodemographic and anthropometric characteristics, personal medical history, and lifestyle behaviors including smoking, diet, and physical activity. Questionnaire administration occurred via standardized computer-assisted personal interviews or self-administered mailed questionnaires for Community Health Center and general population sampling participants, respectively. SCCS protocols were approved by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center and Meharry Medical College Institutional Review Boards and participants provided written informed consent. The SCCS data coordinating center has access to all study data, which are available to be shared through an approved data use agreement.

The current analysis included SCCS participants who were: 1) either >65 years, or <65 years at enrollment and either reported being covered by Medicaid or Medicare on baseline questionnaire or had a Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services claim within 90 days of being enrolled; and 2) had complete Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) (≤10 items left blank) and plausible total energy intakes of 600 to 8,000 kcal/d (N = 33,003). We excluded participants with prevalent HF at enrollment (N = 4,572) and those with prevalent end-stage renal disease (N = 51). The analysis included individuals who self-reported as non-Hispanic Black or non-Hispanic White, as the number of participants in other racial groups was too small for adequately powered statistical analysis. Participants with nonsensical follow-up, that is, >180 months, were also excluded (total 3,074 excluded participants), yielding a final analytic cohort of 25,306 participants.

Dietary sodium and other covariates

At enrollment, a validated FFQ was used to assess each participant’s diet, based on 89 food items designed to reflect a majority of foods common to the southeastern United States.15,16 The FFQ was validated through race-concordant 24-hour dietary recalls in the South against nationwide race-blind 24-hour recall testing (the standard system, which was based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [NHANES] and the Continuing Survey of Food Intakes by Individuals [CSFII]), in which the Kappa statistic demonstrated substantial or better agreement (k > 0.73). Intakes of total energy and nutrients were calculated utilizing race- and sex-specific portion size information from NHANES and CSFII as well. For each food item reported on the FFQ, micronutrients, for example, sodium, were computed using the mean value for each food item (in the NHANES and CSFII databases) and then averaged by day to yield estimated daily dietary intake.15 FFQ data were also linked with the USDA MyPyramid Equivalents Database to generate equivalent intakes of food groups, standardized to intakes per 1000 kilocalories, and to calculate the Healthy Eating Index-2010 score, a measure of dietary quality composed of 12 components.17 The score can be used to assess adherence to the Dietary Guidelines for America.16

Participants’ medical history was ascertained at enrollment from self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, high cholesterol, and myocardial infarction or coronary artery bypass surgery (MI/CABG). Physical activity and sedentary time were assessed by a validated physical activity questionnaire from which both total activity metabolic equivalent hours and total sit hours/day were calculated, as previously reported.18 Validation studies have demonstrated the reliability for several self-reported variables in the SCCS through medical records, accelerometers, and biomarkers.14,18

Outcome

Incident HF events were ascertained through linkage of the SCCS with Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Research Identifiable Files.4 Incident HF was defined as the first claim with an International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9th revision discharge code of 428.x in the Medicare institutional, Part B carrier, outpatient-based claims files, or the Medicaid Analytic Extract Inpatient and Other Services claims files, from the SCCS enrollment date to December 31, 2015. Following December 31, 2015, incident HF was defined as the first medical claim with an International Classification of Diseases-10th revision discharge code of I.50, until December 31, 2016.19 Comparability of ICD-10 codes to ICD-9 codes has been established.20 Censoring occurred at the date of HF event, date of death, or end of follow-up period (December 31, 2016), whichever occurred first. Prevalent HF was determined through ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes prior to enrollment, and all participants with prevalent HF were excluded from the study.4

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into quartiles of daily dietary sodium intake with descriptive statistics calculated as counts (percentages), mean (SD), or medians (25th-75th percentile) with comparisons made with Pearson chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests, as appropriate. Correlates of dietary sodium intake as a continuous term in mg/day were examined using multivariable linear regression with factors included in the model ranked according to the chi-square statistics.

The risk of incident HF in association with dietary sodium intake was estimated using Cox proportional hazards. The base model included age, sex, race, and total daily caloric intake (kcal/day). A subsequent model was further adjusted for dietary factors including Healthy Eating Index (without sodium component), alcohol consumption, and dietary potassium intake. A third model included additional adjustment for socioeconomic factors including education (<high school, high school/vocational training/junior college, and college degree or higher), household income (<$15,000, $15,000-24,999, and ≥$25,000), marital status (married/living as married with a partner, separated/divorced, widowed, and single/never married), and neighborhood deprivation index. The fourth model was further adjusted for lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking status [current, former, or never smoker]), and hypercholesterolemia. To account for factors potentially on the causal pathway between dietary sodium intake and incident HF, each of the following was entered into the 4th model individually: hypertension, obesity, diabetes, sleep (hours/day), and MI/CABG. We tested for nonlinear associations between dietary sodium and incident HF. Covariates with continuous distributions were modeled as restricted cubic splines with 4 knots. HRs (with 95% CI) scaled to per 1,000 mg/day greater dietary sodium intake were calculated. To examine whether the association between dietary sodium intake and incident HF varied across subgroups, interaction terms were added individually into model 4 and stratified analyses were performed to obtain effect estimates within each subgroup.

Population attributable risk and 95% CI was calculated using the AFcoxph package in R (The R Project) that utilizes the multivariable-adjusted HRs from the Cox models. All analyses were performed using R (The R Project). Given the large sample size, modeling results reflect those for complete case analysis, with missingness <6.5% overall. Statistical significance was considered present at P < 0.05.

Results

For the overall cohort of 25,306 SCCS participants, mean age (SD) at enrollment was 55.5 (10.3) years and most were females (63%), Black (69%), and had a household annual income of <$15,000 (87.4%). The mean daily dietary sodium intake was 4,269 (SD 2,502) mg/day, with 79.8% of individuals consuming more than the U.S. Dietary Guideline recommended limit of 2,300 mg/day. Over a median follow-up of 9.8 years (maximum 14.9 years), a total of 7,039 (27.8%) incident HF events occurred. Table 2 displays the results of the Cox models for dietary sodium intake and incident HF. From the initial to fourth model, the association of each 1,000 mg/day greater sodium intake with higher risk for incident HF was consistent with HR ranging from 1.07 to 1.09, and also persistently significant with 95% CIs from 1.04 to 1.13. The association between dietary sodium intake and incident HF was linear (Figure 2). The risk of incident HF associated with consuming the cohort average dietary sodium intake of 4,269 mg/day compared with that recommended in dietary guidelines, that is, 2,300 mg/day, was 15% higher (HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.08-1.24; P < 0.001).

As hypertension, diabetes, obesity, coronary artery disease, and sleep duration may each be on the causal pathway between dietary sodium intake and incident HF, these were individually added to model 4 (Table 2). Addition of hypertension, body mass index, MI/CABG, or sleep hours did not substantially attenuate the significant association between greater sodium intake and risk of incident HF. Adjustment for diabetes status did, however, attenuate the association between each 1,000 mg/day greater dietary sodium and incident HF to borderline significance (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 1.00-1.11).

Whether the risk of incident HF associated with greater dietary sodium intake varied across subgroups was examined by inclusion of interaction terms and displayed as a Forest plot (Figure 3). For the majority of subgroups, interaction terms were nonsignificant. The magnitude of the associations between sodium intake and risk of incident HF were stronger among White individuals (HR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.13-1.30) and those in less deprived neighborhoods (Neighborhood Deprivation Index <0.7; HR: 1.13; 95% CI: 1.07-1.19), than for Black individuals (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.99-1.03) and those in more deprived neighborhoods (Neighborhood Deprivation Index ≥0.7; HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.99-1.04). The association of greater sodium intake with incident HF was also significant among individuals with diabetes (HR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.02-1.15; per 1,000 mg/day), but neutral among those without diabetes (HR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.96-1.05; per 1,000 mg/day).

Discussion

In a predominantly low income cohort of middle-aged adults residing in the Southeastern United States at high risk for HF development, our principal findings are as follows: 1) average sodium intake was ∼4,300 mg/day with ∼80% of individuals consuming more than the 2,300 mg daily allowance recommended by the American Heart Association and U.S. dietary guidelines; 2) each 1,000 mg/day greater dietary sodium intake associated with ∼8% higher risk for incident HF, which was largely consistent across subgroups, and independent of comorbid hypertension; and 3) at the population level, reduction of sodium intake to ≤4,000 mg/day could result in ∼6.6% fewer cases of incident HF (Central Illustration). Collectively, these results suggest even modest reduction in sodium consumption may significantly reduce the burden of HF in this high-risk population.

Individuals in the SCCS experience among the highest HF incidence rates in the United States motivating need to better understand modifiable risk factors in this population.2,4 We previously found greater alignment with dietary patterns, such as Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension or Healthy Eating Index, did not significantly associate with incident HF in the overall cohort, raising the possibility that individual dietary components may contribute to HF risk more so than overall dietary pattern.2,5 Sodium is an essential dietary nutrient and its consumption in excess has been implicated in the development of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, atherosclerosis, and sleep disorders, which are all risk factors for HF.8,21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 Preclinical evidence suggests excess sodium induces adverse cardiac remodeling even in the absence of hypertension.28 Furthermore, human studies suggest a positive correlation between sodium intake with left ventricular hypertrophy independent of blood pressure.29 In addition, a handful of preclinical and clinical studies suggest greater dietary sodium intake may be causally related to insulin resistance and diabetes development, which informed our modeling approach of including diabetes as a potential mediator.24,30, 31, 32 Moreover, few reports on the association of dietary sodium intake with incident HF in humans exist and these yielded variable results which further motivated our sequential modeling approach to gain deeper insight.10, 11, 12, 13,33

In the SCCS, we found a significant association between greater dietary sodium intake and higher risk of incident HF. Our finding is congruent with 2 prior studies (Table 4); the NHANES conducted in the United States between 1971 to 1975 with an average 19 years of follow-up and the EPIC-Norfolk study conducted in the United Kingdom between 1993 and 1997 with an average follow-up of nearly 13 years.10,11 The SCCS, NHANES, and EPIC-Norfolk included on average middle-aged individuals, had more than a decade of follow-up time, and each identified >1,000 incident HF events.10,11 In NHANES, greater dietary sodium intake associated with a higher risk for incident HF among overweight individuals similar to our finding in the SCCS in which the vast majority of individuals were overweight.10 Contrasting with the linear association between greater sodium intake and incident HF risk in the SCCS, the EPIC-Norfolk study examined sodium intake in quintiles and found a U-shaped relationship; individuals with more than ∼4,400 or less than ∼2,900 mg/day sodium intake had a higher risk for incident HF compared with those consuming between ∼2,900-3,400 mg/day.11 The reasons for this difference are uncertain but one potential explanation is EPIC-Norfolk estimated dietary sodium intake from spot urine samples from which 24-hour urine sodium excretion was calculated, whereas NHANES and the SCCS used food recall and questionnaires.10,11

Our finding in the SCCS of a significant association between dietary sodium intake and incident HF contrasts with 2 prior studies. The Health, Aging, and Body Composition (Health ABC) study included older adults (mean age ∼74 years) and did not find a significant association between dietary sodium intake, estimated from FFQ at enrollment, and incident HF (HR 1.00; 95% CI 0.92-1.08, per 1,000 mg/day sodium) over 10 years of follow-up.12 Using UK Biobank data, Welsh et al33 examined quintiles of estimated 24-hour urine sodium excretion in relation to incident HF. In 322,624 individuals without prevalent cardiovascular disease, hypertension, or diabetes, a total of 283 incident HF hospitalizations occurred (0.09%) over 7 years of follow-up with no significant associations found across quintiles of sodium excretion and incident HF.33 A different UK Biobank analysis which did not exclude participants with prevalent hypertension or diabetes, found among 413,109 individuals those who reported always adding table salt to food had 1.21 greater risk (95% CI: 1.11-1.33) for incident HF over a median of 12 years follow-up compared with those who never added table salt.13

The collective evidence from SCCS, NHANES, EPIC-Norfolk, Health ABC, and UK Biobank suggest middle-aged individuals at moderate to high-risk for HF development may be most susceptible to the effects of higher dietary sodium consumption. In the SCCS, 27.8% of individuals developed incident HF and greater dietary sodium intake significantly associated with greater risk of incident HF independent of hypertension. Consistent with risk-based consideration, in subgroup analyses we observed a significant association between greater dietary sodium intake and higher risk for incident HF among individuals with diabetes further supporting biologic plausibility as preclinical and relatively small human studies suggest impaired sodium excretion in individuals with diabetes.34, 35, 36, 37 In intermediate risk populations, that is, EPIC-Norfolk and NHANES, 6 to 10% of individuals developed HF over the follow-up periods, and significant associations between sodium intake and incident HF were also observed.11,38 In contrast, among individuals at lower risk for incident HF, including those without diabetes, as was observed by both Welsh et al33 in the UK Biobank and in our SCCS subgroup analysis, greater dietary sodium intake may not be a risk factor for HF. In the Health ABC study, which included the most elderly cohort, dietary sodium may less strongly associate with incident HF risk, perhaps due to competing risks, or already lower sodium consumption compared with those individuals in the SCCS and EPIC-Norfolk.12

PAF analysis accounts for prevalence of a risk factor and its relative risk for an outcome and can, therefore, be used to understand the potential impact on the outcome should that risk factor be removed from the population. For an exposure which is present on continuous scale, such as dietary sodium, we examined a range of threshold sodium intake values in our analysis. Our results indicate if population sodium intake was reduced to a maximum of 4,000 mg/day, this could result in 6.6% fewer cases of incident HF over 10 years. Approximately 1 million new cases of HF are diagnosed in the United States each year and the economic cost of a single case of HF in the United States is estimated at $30,000 per year per patient.39 Extrapolating these data suggests modest reduction in sodium consumption to achieve a population maximum of 4,000 mg/day could result in 66,000 fewer individuals developing HF over 10 years with associated substantial reduction in health care costs.

Study Limitations

Measurement errors of dietary sodium intake estimated using FFQ, which could be due to recall bias, limited food items, and the complexity of mixed dishes, cannot be excluded, although the SCCS used a FFQ designed and validated to account for the regional dietary pattern of the southeastern United States.15 These measurement errors are nondifferential with respect to incident HF and are thus likely to bias results toward the null. Although dietary sodium was assessed using a single baseline FFQ and serial FFQs were not longitudinally conducted in the SCCS, other studies have demonstrated stability of habitual diet over time.40,41 Incident HF was ascertained from ICD-9 and -10 codes, which have been previously used in the SCCS and validated in other cohorts.4,19,42 We were unable to account for time-updated diagnoses of hypertension or diabetes or poor sleep trajectories after enrollment, but this does not negate the association between baseline sodium intake with incident HF, as modifiable risk factors are based on present status rather than future causal pathway events. We cannot exclude residual confounding but appreciate fully controlled randomized trials of dietary sodium for the outcome of incident HF are nearly impossible to conduct due to cost and length of follow-up needed. Although the association between greater dietary sodium and higher risk of incident HF was mostly consistent across the subgroups examined, we acknowledge some heterogeneity and appreciate exploratory subgroup analyses inform hypothesis generation for future investigation. In addiiton, our findings may not be generalizable outside the population studied.

In conclusion, by examining low-income Black and White adults in the southeast United States who are historically understudied, we address an evidence gap regarding the association between dietary sodium intake and the risk of incident HF among resource limited individuals who experience among the highest rate of HF. Our findings that greater dietary sodium intake significantly associates with higher risk of incident HF and that even modest reduction in sodium consumption may substantially reduce the burden of HF in this high-risk population motivates implementation of multilevel public health strategies to achieve lower dietary sodium intake in high-risk resource-limited communities.43

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Greater dietary sodium intake associates with higher risk for incident HF among predominantly low-income adults residing in the southeastern United States, independent of most comorbidities, including hypertension.

COMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE: Modest reduction in dietary sodium consumption to a maximum of 4,000 mg sodium per day may significantly reduce the population burden of new onset HF.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Testing and implementation of effective multilevel strategies for dietary sodium reduction are needed to reduce the public health impact of excessive dietary sodium intake on HF incidence.
