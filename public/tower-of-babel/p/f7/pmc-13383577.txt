Diagnosing metastatic angiosarcoma arising from MPNST using H3K27me3 staining in NF1: illustrative case
Authors: Michael K A Hagan, Robert J Spinner, Jorge Torres-Mora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDMalignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) are malignant tumors that rarely demonstrate heterologous differentiation from angiosarcomatous components. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) are aggressive peripheral nerve sarcomas that are resistant to most therapies and have a poor prognosis if not resected.1,2 While MPNSTs can develop de novo or after radiation therapy, they most often arise in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). NF1 is a rare autosomal dominant disease (1 in 3000) associated with mutations in NF1, a tumor suppressor gene. It can have many dermatological, nervous, and skeletal manifestations, but the development of a multitude of peripheral nerve tumors places the highest morbidity and mortality burden on these patients.3 These tumors include neurofibroma and its variants (plexiform neurofibroma, neurofibroma with atypical features [ancient neurofibroma], atypical neurofibromatosis tumor of uncertain biological potential, and MPNSTs).4 In patients with NF1, progression of the disease can lead to various transformations into MPNSTs, a well-established finding occurring in 8%–13% of patients with NF1 over their lifetime.5 Rare instances of angiosarcomas, another malignancy, have been reported to arise from NF1-associated MPNSTs.6–16 Here we report the first case demonstrating the use of an H3K27me3 stain to aid in the diagnosis of a biopsy only showing heterologous components of MPNST.

Illustrative Case

History of Present Illness

A 25-year-old man presented to the medical oncology service for evaluation of treatment options for NF1. At 8 months of age, the patient was evaluated for an enlarging, pigmented spot on the left low left back that led to the diagnosis of NF1 after extensive, multidisciplinary workup by dermatology, pediatrics, geneticists, and ophthalmology. At 3 years of age, the patient presented to neurology with a right-sided head tilt, drooling, headaches, and delayed motor and speech milestones and was found to have a diminished gag reflex. Imaging revealed a large lumbar subcutaneous plexiform neurofibroma and a cervicomedullary ganglioglioma. At age 5, the patient received radiation therapy to the skull base lesion, followed by oral temozolomide, oral VP-16 therapy, and then a subtotal resection. Pathological evaluation confirmed a ganglioglioma. After the surgery, the patient reported an increase in the preoperative right-sided hemiparesis. At age 10, the patient’s tumor progressed with increased upper extremity weakness, and another subtotal resection was done.

At age 24, the patient was seen by a pediatric neuro-oncologist for follow-up. A PET scan revealed an area of increased avidity in the lumbar subcutaneous region, suspicious of malignancy. The patient underwent an extensive subtotal resection of the mass. Pathological analysis confirmed an MPNST arising from a 9.5-cm plexiform and diffuse neurofibroma with negative PD-L1 stains. Only 3 months after the operation, there was recurrence, and the patient was put on trametinib. However, the lesion demonstrated continued growth, and the medication was discontinued after 6 weeks.

In the setting of restrictive lung disease, the patient had been receiving regular chest CT scans and was followed for nonspecific nodules in the left upper lobe and right lower lobe. At age 25, a chest CT study showed enlargement of right lower lobe and left upper lobe lung nodules (7 mm and 6 mm, respectively). Metastatic MPNST was suspected and the left nodule was biopsied. Microscopic examination showed a fragment of alveolated lung parenchyma with congestion, focal fibrosis, and clusters of alveolar hemosiderin-laden macrophages. No changes were definitive for malignancy.

Imaging

Four months later, at presentation for reconsideration of treatment options, a whole-body PET CT scan showed a considerable increase in size of the MPNST in the left lumbar subcutaneous tissue. It measured 18 × 9 × 16 cm with significant progression of areas of marked central hypermetabolic activity and a new area of moderate central hypometabolic necrosis with a maximum standardized uptake value of 14.6 (Fig. 1A–C). CT chest without intravenous contrast once again showed an increase in the left upper lobe nodule to 2.9 × 2.9 cm, prompting another lung biopsy (Fig. 1D–F).

Pathological Findings

Pathological analysis of the lung mass showed an angiosarcoma without MPNST morphology, suggesting a de novo angiosarcoma. ERG, CD31, and FLI1 had positive immunoreactivity, while S100 and SOX10 were negative. However, in the context of the patient’s MPNST, the pathologist on the case (J.T.M.) strongly suspected metastasis. Further staining for H3K27me3, an epigenetic marker that is lost in a significant amount of high-grade MPNSTs, showed loss of expression in the angiosarcoma tumor cells.17,18 The pathologist then went back to the original lumbar subcutaneous MPNST and found the same loss of H3K27me3 as well as a small focus of angiosarcoma, which had originally been missed. This confirmed the lung nodule was likely a metastasis exclusively of the angiosarcoma heterologous component of the MPNST (Fig. 2).

Treatment

After this discovery, the patient was scheduled to start doxorubicin, a systemic treatment that could treat suspected diffuse metastases with angiosarcoma or MPNST histology. However, the patient ultimately declined and instead pursued natural remedies. Only 3 months after the diagnosis of the metastatic angiosarcoma, the patient died. The family declined autopsy.

Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrated a metastatic angiosarcoma arising from an MPNST without morphological evidence of classic MPNST. This complicated the diagnosis due to the unexpected nature of the metastasis of exclusively a heterologous component of an MPNST. We identified 12 cases of angiosarcomas in patients with NF1, 8 of which demonstrated an association with MPNST and 4 that concluded no association (Table 1).6–16 These cases primarily established the relationship between the angiosarcoma and the MPNST based on the presence of adjacent MPNST architectural components. However, none of these cases reported the use of immunohistochemical stains to exclude the possibility that the angiosarcoma represented an independent metastasis from the original lesion. Here we describe the first case of a metastatic angiosarcoma derived from an MPNST discovered by a loss of H3K27me3 expression.

Lessons

The diagnosis of MPNSTs can be challenging given their heterogeneous histopathological appearance and the lack of a specific immunoprofile in a significant subset of cases. MPNSTs are most commonly observed in their spindle form, characterized by fascicles of spindle cells with varying degrees of cytological atypia, necrosis, and mitotic activity, resulting in variable histological grades. To the pathologist, these features can create diagnostic confusion with other malignancies such as synovial sarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, fibrosarcomatous dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, spindle cell melanoma, or other benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor such as cellular schwannoma and atypical neurofibroma. MPNSTs may also present in an epithelioid form,19 which can be confused with other epithelioid neoplasms such as proximal type epithelioid sarcoma, conventional melanoma, and even carcinoma. To complicate matters, a small portion of MPNSTs demonstrate heterologous differentiation, including skeletal muscle differentiation (so-called malignant triton tumor) and osseous, cartilaginous, and even glandular components, creating diagnostic confusion with a variety of malignancies such as rhabdomyosarcoma, osteosarcoma, and carcinosarcoma. Conventionally, S100 and SOX10 immunohistochemistry stains are used to detect MPNSTs but are only 20%–60% sensitive with low specificity, and, as shown in this study, they have low utility for identifying the metastatic differentiations of MPNSTs.20–25 However, inactivating mutations of SUZ12 and EED, two component genes of the polycomb repressive complex 2, are present in 70%–90% of MPNSTs, which can be detected by loss of expression of H3K27me3 by immunohistochemical analysis, a useful tool to establish a diagnosis, with biopsies only capturing the heterologous components of MPNST.19,20 In this case, the molecular profile of the pulmonary metastasis could have revealed similarities to the primary MPNST, including combined inactivation of NF1, CDKN2A, and mutations in EED and SUZ12. Unfortunately, the limited amount of tissue present in the biopsy, precluded molecular analysis. In addition to differentiating MPNSTs from preexisting neurofibromas, it can also be used to differentiate MPNSTs from high- grade spindle cell sarcomas.20

Loss of H3K27me3 expression not only arises in MPNSTs within NF1 but can also be present in sporadic or radiation-induced forms. Therefore, it is important to consider the histopathological findings in the clinical and radiographic context. While the finding of a heterologous component from an MPNST will not likely have implications for treatment, it can ensure accurate diagnosis that may be therapeutically relevant in the future.
