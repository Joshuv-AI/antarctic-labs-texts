Incidence and prevalence of small fibre neuropathy in the Netherlands: a multiple system estimation study
Authors: Dennis Kool, Janneke G J Hoeijmakers, Catharina G Faber, Ingemar S J Merkies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmall fibre neuropathy (SFN) affects small myelinated Aδ- and unmyelinated C-fibres and causes neuropathic pain and autonomic symptoms. Earlier Dutch estimates, over a decade old, reported a minimum prevalence of 52.95 and an incidence of 11.75 per 100 000 adults. As recognition of SFN has likely increased, this study aimed to estimate the total SFN population in the Netherlands and to provide updated incidence and prevalence rates using a multiple system estimation (MSE) approach.MethodsMSE was used to estimate the total adult SFN population by matching patients from three national databases: the SFN expertise centre, the national SFN registry and the Dutch Neuromuscular Disorders Association. Prevalence was calculated by using total population numbers from Statistics Netherlands, adjusting for mortality with age- and sex-specific survival rates. Annual incidence and prevalence by sex and region were calculated using stratified MSE. Missing diagnosis years in the patient association data were imputed using multiple imputations.ResultsThe prevalence of SFN in 2024 was estimated at 69.69 and the incidence at 3.55 per 100 000 adults in the general Dutch population, with higher rates in females. Incidence increased over time, particularly among females.ConclusionsThis study reveals a higher prevalence than previously reported. The lower incidence compared with previous estimates likely reflects differences in geographic coverage, while the rising trend suggests increasing awareness and recognition of SFN. These findings suggest that SFN represents a larger problem than previously recognised and the burden of SFN emphasises the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Prevalence, EPIDEMIOLOGY, PAIN, NEUROPATHY

Introduction

Small fibre neuropathy (SFN) is a peripheral neuropathy affecting thinly myelinated Aδ and unmyelinated C-fibres, characterised by neuropathic pain and autonomic symptoms that substantially affect the quality of life.1 It is frequently associated with sleep disturbances, depression and functional limitations.2 Despite its large burden, SFN can be difficult to recognise in clinical practice.1

Previous estimates of SFN epidemiology in the Netherlands reported a minimum incidence of 11.73 and a minimum prevalence of 52.95 per 100 000 adults.3 However, these rates were limited to the southern part of the Netherlands and were reported more than 10 years ago. A more recent study in the USA reported lower rates but an increasing trend, suggesting growing awareness and improved recognition among clinicians.4

Up-to-date, epidemiological data on SFN are essential for healthcare planning and clinical research and previously reported rates may no longer be representative of the truth. Traditional approaches to estimating disease frequency rely on registries or single data sources, which rarely capture the complete population.5 6 Multiple systems estimation (MSE) provides a way to estimate the total population size by analysing the overlap between multiple data sources.7

This study aims to apply MSE to three Dutch data sources to estimate the total SFN population in the Netherlands and report updated incidence and prevalence rates.

Methods

Data sources

A nationwide retrospective MSE study was conducted to estimate the number of adults with SFN in the Netherlands using three sources.

Dutch expertise centre SFN cohort: a database, including all referred patients above 18 years old who were diagnosed with SFN based on the Besta criteria until the end of December 2024, was retrieved.8

Dutch national SFN registry: a voluntary nationwide registry of adults with verified SFN diagnoses. All diagnosed in 2024 or earlier were retrieved.

Dutch patient association of neuromuscular diseases (Spierziekten Nederland) registry: members self-report their diagnosis and year of diagnosis. Data are removed if membership is cancelled. Members diagnosed in 2024 or earlier were retrieved in July 2025.

The three sources differ in nature (clinical referral, registry and patient association), which reduces complete dependency between systems while allowing sufficient overlap for modelling.

Record linkage

Patients were matched using exact agreement on surname, date of birth and sex. Ambiguous matches were reviewed manually. Uncertain cases were treated as separate individuals. Other retrieved data (year of diagnosis, age at diagnosis and the region of residence) were prioritised from the expertise centre due to greater certainty, followed by the national registry and then the patient association. Linkage was performed locally at each source to ensure that privacy protection and data were anonymised before analysis.

Multiple systems estimation

MSE is based on the overlap between multiple data sources that register the same target population.7 MSE can include interaction terms to account for dependency when using three sources.9 Therefore, log-linear models with interaction terms were fitted to the three-source capture histories. Model selection was based on the lowest Akaike information criterion. Analyses were performed in R (V.4.5.1).10

Prevalence analysis

MSE was performed on the entire dataset for the main prevalence estimate because stratification can lead to smaller sample sizes and thereby unstable log-linear model fits. Prevalence of SFN was estimated by dividing the total SFN population retrieved from MSE analysis by the adult Dutch population from Statistics Netherlands (CBS).11 However, MSE assumes a closed population (eg, no deaths) and SFN is not known to affect the overall life expectancy.4 Therefore, age- and year-specific survival probabilities from the general Dutch population were applied to account for mortality.12 The age distribution of the unobserved population was assumed to mirror that of the captured cases per year.

Stratified estimates by diagnosis year, sex and region were also calculated for comparison (Supplementary Material).

Incidence analysis

Annual incidence was estimated by performing MSE stratified by diagnosis year and sex. Incidence rates were calculated by dividing estimated yearly cases by the corresponding annual population size, obtained from Statistics Netherlands.11 Linear regression was fitted to assess trends in SFN incidence over time. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data handling

For 43% of patients in the patient association database, the year of diagnosis was missing. Multiple imputations were performed with the MICE package in R using predictive mean matching.13 The resulting total population estimates were combined using Rubin’s rules.14 Complete-case analyses were performed as sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Material).

Results

Observed population

The Maastricht UMC+cohort included 3577 diagnosed patients, the national registry 723 patients and the patient association 551 members. After record linkage, 3947 unique individuals were identified. The distribution across the three data sources is shown in online supplemental figure1.

The mean age of the population was 52.57 years (SD 13.29) and 1296 patients (36%) were males. Patients were distributed nationwide, with 25% residing in the southern Netherlands. Diagnosis years ranged from 2008 to 2024.

Prevalence of SFN

After survival correction, the total SFN population in 2024 is approximately 10 298 patients (95% CI 10 236 to 10 460). This accounts for a prevalence rate in 2024 of 69.69 per 100 000 adults (95% CI 68.59 to 70.78).

The total population per stratum was retrieved (online supplemental table 1). Stratified analyses yielded lower overall prevalence estimates of 57.44 per 100 000 adults in the general population (95% CI 52.69 to 61.18), with a prevalence of 42.73 per 100 000 (95% CI 35.43 to 50.02) for males and a prevalence of 71.78 per 100 000 (95% CI 65.68 to 77.87) for females. Regional analyses demonstrated higher prevalence in the southern Netherlands compared with other regions (online supplemental figure 2).

Incidence of SFN

In 2024, the incidence of SFN for the total population was 3.55 (95% CI 2.40 to 4.69), with an incidence of 0.86 per 100 000 (95% CI 0.56 to 1.67) for males and 6.16 per 100 000 (95% CI 4.19 to 8.12) for females.

Over time, incidence increased significantly in females (annual increase 0.37 per 100 000; p<0.001) and in the total population (annual increase 0.23 per 100 000; p=0.011). Incidence in males did not change significantly. The incidence of SFN per year is shown in figure 1.

Sensitivity analysis

Complete-case analyses showed similar patterns, with slightly lower absolute numbers compared with multiple imputations, as expected. This suggests that imputing missing diagnosis years did not bias the results, while including these patients likely improved estimate accuracy (online supplemental figure 3).

Discussion

This nationwide study provides updated epidemiological estimates of SFN in the Netherlands using MSE. In 2024, the prevalence was 69.69 per 100 000 adults and the incidence 3.55 per 100 000, with a significant upward trend over time, particularly among females.

Increasing rates

Compared with the previous Dutch regional study reporting a prevalence of 52.95 per 100 000, our nationwide prevalence is higher, while incidence appears lower.3 The lower incidence likely reflects the broader geographic coverage of this study. Most patients still originated from the southern Netherlands, surrounding the national SFN expert centre, but even within this region, prevalence was substantially higher than previously reported. The persistent regional clustering suggests that diagnostic awareness and referral patterns may strongly influence detection rates. A genuine rise in frequency would be expected to show a more uniform distribution. While true regional variation cannot be entirely excluded, no known risk factors would explain this clustering. The observed rise likely reflects improved recognition rather than a true increase in underlying disease frequency.

International data further support this trend. A US study reported lower absolute incidence and prevalence rates than observed here but also demonstrated an increase over time.4 Likewise, a Swiss study found a comparable incidence to this study but a higher prevalence, which may be explained by methodological factors, such as the small sample size and limited geographic scope.15 Variation in SFN, with both idiopathic and secondary aetiologies, may also contribute to international differences in epidemiological estimates.1 Together, these findings suggest that while absolute estimates vary across countries, likely due to differences in study design and calculations, the overall pattern points towards SFN being increasingly diagnosed worldwide.

The higher prevalence estimates indicate that SFN is a more significant health problem than previously recognised, imposing a substantial burden on patients who often lack effective treatments.1 These updated epidemiological data highlight the urgency of developing effective therapies and are essential for healthcare planning, resource allocation and optimising clinical trial recruitment.

Limitations

A major strength of this study is the integration of three nationwide data sources combined with MSE, allowing the estimation of unobserved cases. However, several limitations should be considered. Diagnoses in the patient association were self-reported, potentially introducing misclassification. Missing diagnosis years required imputation, although sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of trends. As with all MSE analyses, estimated unobserved cases remain statistical approximations. Furthermore, findings may not generalise beyond the Netherlands. Future research incorporating multiple countries and regions is warranted to generate a more accurate global picture of SFN.

Conclusion

This nationwide study updates SFN epidemiology in the Netherlands, reporting a prevalence of 69.69 and an incidence of 3.55 per 100 000 adults, with an upward trend likely reflecting improved diagnostic recognition rather than a true increase. The elevated prevalence underscores SFN as a significant health concern and highlights the urgent need for effective treatments.
