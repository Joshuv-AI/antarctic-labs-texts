Leveraging MrkA Display on Self-Assembling Protein Nanoparticles for Enhanced Immune Activation
Authors: Valentina Monaci, Davide Oldrini, Fabiola Giusti, Luigia Cappelli, Roberta Cozzi, Gianina Florentina Belciug, Martina Carducci, Makrina Totsika, Omar Rossi, Francesca Cantini, Francesca Micoli, Gianmarco Gasperini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Self-assembling protein nanoparticles (NPs) offer a versatile platform to enhance antigen immunogenicity through a multivalent display. Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) is a critical multidrug-resistant pathogen for which no licensed vaccine exists, posing a significant global health threat. This study aimed to develop and evaluate protein nanoparticle-based vaccine candidates presenting MrkA, a highly conserved type 3 fimbrial subunit and promising Kp vaccine target, to elicit robust protective immune responses. We genetically fused MrkA to four distinct self-assembling protein nanoparticle scaffolds: Helicobacter pylori ferritin, Thermotoga maritima encapsulin, computationally designed mI3, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis dodecin. The resulting MrkA-NPs were expressed and purified, and their structural integrity was confirmed by electron microscopy, dynamic light scattering, and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. These constructs were then evaluated for their immunogenicity in animal models. All MrkA-displaying NPs significantly enhanced MrkA-specific IgG responses, eliciting significantly higher antibody titers compared to recombinant MrkA. Importantly, passive transfer of immune sera from rabbits immunized with MrkA-dodecin NP conferred significant protection against a lethal Kp challenge, resulting in 90% survival compared to the control group. Our findings confirmed the ability of NPs to significantly enhance the immune responses against a bacterial protein such as MrkA, confirming their potential as transformative tools in vaccine development. This study also offers promising results for the development of a vaccine against Kp, addressing antimicrobial resistance and improving global health.

Introduction

Over the past few years, significant advances in vaccine development have been established, reflected in the accelerated response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Beyond the mRNA platform as a key tool to combat COVID-19, several novel technologies are seeking to expand what has worked for approved vaccines, often harnessing naturally occurring elements to trigger immune responses. A promising approach is represented by the protein nanoparticle platform. Protein nanoparticles (NPs) are naturally occurring or computationally designed molecules that have the intrinsic ability to self-assemble into highly stable and symmetric structures. , Protein NPs can be used for displaying antigens/epitopes in multicopy, ordered patterns, mimicking the natural pathogen-associated molecular features and enhancing immune responses. Moreover, NPs represent a cutting-edge technology in vaccine development due to their ability to simultaneously present multiple copies of target antigens to the immune system and to strongly engage B-cell receptors, thereby enhancing functional antibody responses. − In this work, protein NPs were explored as an antigen presentation platform. , At least three different approaches can be used to display the antigen of interest in the external surface of NPs: genetic fusion, chemical conjugation, and protein ligation such as the SpyTag-SpyCatcher system. In this study, we focused on genetic fusion to a panel of different self-assembling NPs, including systems largely described in the literature, such as ferritin and computationally designed mI3, or less described systems such as encapsulin and dodecin, aiming to explore them as innovative delivery systems for bacterial proteins (Figure ).

Cartoon representation of the protein-based nanoparticles tested in this study with their corresponding size and number of subunits. Reported sizes refer to the diameter of each nanoparticle produced in E. coli.

Ferritin is a widely used nonviral platform for vaccine development, composed of 24 protein monomers forming an octahedral structure with both 3-fold and 4-fold symmetry. , The N-termini of each monomer are well exposed and allow for the easy attachment of antigens, while, in contrast, the C-termini are buried inside and therefore unavailable for antigen fusion. Mammalian expression systems are typically used for its production, with Helicobacter pylori ferritin showing strong immune responses when genetically fused with viral proteins such as hemagglutinin.

mI3 is a computationally engineered scaffold developed through iterative mutations and optimization of Thermotoga maritima trimeric aldolase. , It consists of 60 subunits stabilized by numerous polar interactions, forming a highly stable icosahedral structure. Immunization studies with the Plasmodium falciparum cysteine-rich protective antigen (CyRPA) conjugated to SpyCatcher-mI3 confirmed strong antibody responses, validating mI3 NP as a promising scaffold for next-generation vaccines.

Dodecin from Mycobacterium tuberculosis is a 12-subunit, highly stable, spherical homododecameric protein recently studied as a scaffold for antigen display. Its structure and thermostability allow both genetic fusion and chemical conjugation of peptides or proteins to its termini for vaccine and bioengineering applications. Bourdeaux et al. exploited the exposed termini of dodecin to be functionalized through either genetic fusion or introduction of specific conjugation domains to enable post-translational covalent or noncovalent attachment of peptides, proteins, or other chemical moieties to the assembled dodecamer.

Finally, encapsulin from Thermotoga maritima is a 60-subunit protein nanostructure with icosahedral symmetry, recently explored for vaccine applications by displaying meningococcal and influenza antigens. , Its structure allows both external antigen presentation and internal cargo delivery, such as packaging green fluorescent protein while displaying viral epitopes on the surface. Studies in mice showed strong immune responses to both surface and internal antigens, highlighting encapsulin’s promise for multifunctional vaccine design.

Here, ferritin, mI3, encapsulin, and dodecin were selected as NPs representing a range of number of subunits in the final structure (12 to 60 subunits) and diameter size (10 to 34 nm), to be genetically fused to the MrkA protein subunit of type 3 fimbriae from Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp), as a model bacterial antigen. Kp is a WHO critical priority multidrug-resistant pathogen, responsible for most hospital-acquired infections and neonatal sepsis in low- and middle-income countries. , No vaccine is yet available, and more recently, a tetravalent candidate O-antigen (OAg)-based vaccine, named Kleb4 V, and a bivalent candidate K-antigen (KAg)-based vaccine, named CHO-V08, are undergoing clinical testing (NCT04959344, NCT07016152). Currently, more conserved protein antigens such as the type 3 fimbrial subunit MrkA are gaining attention as alternative vaccine targets. Protein antigens are particularly attractive because they can elicit broad protection across different serotypes, overcoming the limitations of polysaccharide-based strategies that are strongly affected by the remarkable variability of Kp serotypes.

MrkA is a 20 kDa well-conserved surface protein expressed in approximately 60% of Kp strains, including hypervirulent isolates. , Additionally, it has been suggested that fimbrial immunogens play a role in stimulating a protective immune response in vivo. MrkA subunits naturally assemble into long polymers as part of the fimbrial structure; however, we previously demonstrated that optimizing MrkA monomeric design to a self-complemented version does not compromise its antigenicity. , In more detail, monomeric MrkA is obtained by moving the donor strand segment from the N-terminus to the C-terminus of the protein sequence, to allow its correct folding. Remarkably, the self-complemented MrkA monomer loses its ability to interact with other monomers via donor strand exchange and thus cannot participate in the formation of fimbrial structures. ,

Traditional protein subunit vaccines often suffer from suboptimal immunogenicity and a limited ability to induce long-lasting immune responses. As demonstrated by the weak immune responses elicited by monomeric MrkA in our previous study, recombinant proteins often require potent adjuvants to achieve good immunogenicity. Here, the MrkA monomer was fused to the different NP scaffolds, resulting in nanosized structures with controlled and precise antigen multidisplay. We aimed to verify the ability of these NP systems to enhance the immune response elicited by the MrkA bacterial protein and to explore the role of NP size and subunit number. Overall, this work contributes to the identification of an optimal platform and optimal design for a MrkA-based vaccine against Kp.

Results and Discussion

Generation of Different Self-Assembling Protein Nanoparticles Displaying MrkA Protein Antigen

The MrkA coding sequence was genetically fused to the N-terminus of each NP subunit, spaced by a glycine-serine linker to avoid steric hindrance of antigen copies and inserting a 10× histidine tag (His Tag) to allow protein purification by affinity chromatography (Figure a). Out of the four fusion constructs expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells, only MrkA-dodecin was expressed in the soluble fraction and was successfully purified as a properly assembled NP, as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyses (Figure b, Table , Figure S1). To enable the production of the other NP designs, the Expi293 mammalian expression system was investigated. Additional constructs were therefore designed by adding a eukaryotic leader sequence for antigen secretion in the supernatant and optimizing codon usage for mammalian cell expression. All the fusion constructs tested, including dodecin, were expressed and purified with increased solubility as well as proper assembly compared to the E. coli system. TEM and DLS confirmed that MrkA-dodecin, MrkA-ferritin, MrkA-mI3, and MrkA-encapsulin produced in Expi293 cells were well-assembled NPs (Figure b, Table , Figure S1), implicitly demonstrating that the constituent proteins are correctly folded and have not undergone denaturation. Finally, the recombinant MrkA monomer was produced in both E. coli and Expi293 cells to be used as a control in the following in vivo studies. MrkA both on NP and as the recombinant version was designed as a monomeric form using the donor strand complementation approach, as reported in our previous works. ,

All purified antigens were further analyzed via Western blotting with an anti-MrkA monoclonal antibody (Figure c), confirming the recognition of a single band for each NP subunit. Interestingly, a high molecular weight band was observed in the MrkA-dodecin NP, suggesting high NP stability even in reducing conditions. Notably, proteins produced in Expi293 cells run at a higher apparent molecular weight than expected, suggesting the presence of post-translational modifications such as glycosylation, typical of mammalian expression systems.

Structural Properties of MrkA-Dodecin Nanoparticle Were Investigated by Differential Scanning Fluorimetry (NanoDSF) Analysis and Solution NMR Spectroscopy

E. coli-derived MrkA-dodecin was selected for in-depth structural characterization due to its robust expression, consistent assembly, and ease of manufacturing. Differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF) analysis and solution nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy were exploited in order to structurally characterize this construct. NanoDSF confirmed the presence of a melting temperature for the MrkA monomer at 87.5 °C and for MrkA-dodecin at 90.1 °C, indicative of folded antigens (Figure a). 13C–15N isotopically enriched MrkA-dodecin was produced for NMR experiments: the purity of the protein sample was evaluated and confirmed by SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure b). A 400 μM portion of 13C–15N labeled MrkA-dodecin buffered in 50 mM sodium phosphate, 100 mM NaCl pH 7.0, was first used to record a standard solution NMR 1D experiment to assess the correct folding of the protein (data not shown). The specific 2D NMR experiments were acquired previously on the 13C–15N-MrkA monomer and then compared with the ones of 13C–15N labeled MrkA-dodecin, taking advantage of the complete chemical shifts assignment of the MrkA monomer (BMRB ID 52205). The HMQC spectrum of 13C–15N-MrkA showed that the chemical shifts of the methyl groups overall overlapped with the chemical shifts of the 13C–15N-MrkA-dodecin, suggesting that the global fold of MrkA (PDB ID 9HW9) in the MrkA-dodecin construct is maintained (Figure c).

MrkA-Dodecin Elicits a Stronger Immune Response Compared to MrkA Recombinant Protein in Mice and Rabbits

Immunogenicity of MrkA-dodecin produced in E. coli was tested in mice in comparison to the corresponding monomeric version, administered in two injections at day 0 and 28 with 10 μg dose of MrkA. The antigen dose choice was based on our previous study where 5 μg of MrkA resulted in poor and variable immunogencity in mice. The adjuvants explored were AS01 and Alhydrogel. MrkA-dodecin was additionally tested without adjuvants. The MrkA monomer was overall a poor immunogen, with the majority of mice classified as nonresponders in the presence of AS01 or low- and variable-responders in the presence of Alhydrogel, as verified by anti-MrkA total IgG ELISA (Figure a). MrkA-dodecin adjuvanted with AS01 or Alhydrogel induced significantly higher anti-MrkA IgG in comparison to MrkA monomer with the corresponding adjuvants, both at day 27 and day 42 (MrkA vs MrkA-dodecin in AS01 day 27 and day 42 P value ≤ 0.0001; MrkA vs MrkA-dodecin in Alhydrogel day 27 and day 42 P value = 0.0009; 0.0027) (Figure a). Moreover, no differences were observed between the IgG responses raised by MrkA-dodecin in the absence or presence of AS01 and Alhydrogel as adjuvants, at both days 27 and 42 (Figure a). In addition to ELISA analysis, sera from mice immunized with MrkA-dodecin adjuvanted with AS01 or Alhydrogel and unadjuvanted were tested for their ability to recognize native type 3 fimbriae expressed on the cell surface of Kp strains by flow cytometry. Fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis confirmed that sera generated against MrkA-dodecin are able to recognize and bind MrkA naturally displayed on the bacterial surface (Figure b).

IgG subclasses (IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgG3) and IgM analyses were also performed on the same panel of sera to verify if the use of a different adjuvant system could determine differences in the quality of the elicited antibody. No statistically significant differences for all the IgG subclasses were detected among the 3 analyzed groups (Figure c). Overall, results were very similar in all groups: both at day 27 and day 42, the majority of the elicited IgG was IgG1 with a percentage above 75%. No differences were observed among the groups also for the IgM analysis, both at day 27 and 42 (Figure c). Importantly the total IgG to IgM ratio increased after a second injection for all formulations.

MrkA-dodecin and the MrkA monomer were also compared in rabbits. Animals were injected twice at day 0 and 28 with 15 μg dose of MrkA. In this animal model as in mice, MrkA-dodecin induced a significantly higher anti-MrkA IgG response in comparison to the MrkA monomer at both day 27 and day 42 (Figure d). However, in rabbits, MrkA was able to elicit a remarkably higher immune response compared to the one elicited in mice. Also in this case, flow cytometry confirmed the ability of rabbit sera to recognize native type 3 fimbria on Kp (Figure e).

To directly assess the functional protective capacity of the elicited antibodies, a passive immunization challenge experiment was performed using immune sera from rabbits vaccinated with MrkA-dodecin. Naive CD1 mice received a subcutaneous transfer of pooled immune sera from MrkA-dodecin-immunized rabbits or preimmune sera (negative control), before intraperitoneal challenge with a lethal dose of Klebsiella pneumoniae clinical isolate IL02364192. Strikingly, mice receiving immune sera from MrkA-dodecin vaccinated animals demonstrated significantly enhanced survival with 90% of protection. In contrast, the majority of mice that received preimmune sera were euthanized by day 2 post-challenge, and only 20% reached study end at day 4 post-challenge (Figure f). Interestingly, passive transfer of MrkA-dodecin immune sera resulted in mice with no detectable CFU in the blood 4 days post-challenge and significantly lower CFU in the spleen compared to mice treated with preimmune sera, further confirming the ability of antibodies against MrkA-dodecin to control infection.

Impact of Nanoparticle Size and Subunit Number on the Immune Response Elicited by MrkA in Mice

MrkA-mI3, MrkA-encapsulin, MrkA-ferritin, and MrkA-dodecin produced in Expi293cells, differing in size and number of MrkA monomers displayed, were tested in mice to investigate the contribution of these different NPs to the immunogenicity of MrkA, compared to MrkA monomer. Moreover, MrkA-dodecin produced in E. coli was also included in the study as an additional test group. Mice were vaccinated twice with a 2.5 μg dose of MrkA at day 0 and day 28. A lower MrkA dose than in the previous mouse study was selected to allow better discrimination of the different NPs' immunogenicity. The adjuvant used for the formulations was Alhydrogel.

All NP systems produced in Expi293 cells induced a significantly higher anti-MrkA IgG response in comparison to MrkA at both day 27 and day 42. No statistical difference was detected in the anti-MrkA IgG response elicited by encapsulin, mI3, ferritin, and dodecin systems produced in Expi293 cells at day 42. In contrast, at day 27, MrkA-encapsulin elicited a significantly higher IgG response compared to MrkA-dodecin (Figure a).

To further evaluate the correlation between the anti-MrkA immune response with NP size and multidisplay, linear regression analyses with the ANOVA test were performed. By comparing the NP multidisplay with the IgG response at day 27, the model revealed a significant positive correlation between the two variables (P value = 0.03), indicating that higher numbers of subunit comprising the NP (NP subunits were associated with increased anti-MrkA IgG levels; Figure b). This correlation was no longer evident at day 42 (Figure S2). On the contrary, when comparing the immune response with particle size, no positive correlation was observed at either day 27 or day 42 (Figure S2).

Sera were analyzed by FACS to assess the binding to native type 3 fimbria on the surface of Kp. Also in this case, sera generated against MrkA-NPs were able to recognize and bind MrkA naturally displayed on the bacterial surface (Figure c).

Finally, anti-MrkA total IgG titers elicited by E. coli- vs Expi293- derived MrkA-dodecin were compared. Interestingly, MrkA-dodecin produced in mammalian cells elicited a significantly higher anti-MrkA-specific IgG response at day 42 compared to the one produced in E. coli (Figure d), possibly indicating that post-translational modifications associated with mammalian expression can have a positive impact on immunogenicity in mice.

Conclusions

This work investigates the use of protein-based NPs to enhance the immunogenicity of MrkA as a model bacterial antigen. Dodecin, ferritin, mI3, and encapsulin NP systems were employed to assess their ability to enhance the immune response against MrkA and to evaluate the potential influence of MrkA density and NP size on the elicited immune response. MrkA is an oligomeric protein naturally exposed to the surface of Kp bacteria. Previously, this protein antigen was engineered as a self-complemented monomer to improve its solubility and consistency of production, key features for a viable vaccine candidate. , The resulting monomeric recombinant protein was evaluated in vivo in mice and resulted to be a poor immunogen, thus underscoring the need to explore NP-based display strategies to enhance its immunogenicity.

Antigen display on nanoparticle scaffolds can be achieved through various methods, including chemical conjugation, noncovalent protein ligation systems (e.g., SpyTag-SpyCatcher), and genetic fusion. Each method has distinct advantages and disadvantages. Chemical conjugation, while versatile, can lead to heterogeneous products, uncontrolled orientation, or potential damage to sensitive epitopes due to harsh reaction conditions or nonspecific linkages. Protein ligation systems, such as SpyTag-SpyCatcher, offer site-specific and covalent attachment, providing better control over orientation and stoichiometry compared to random chemical methods. However, they often involve additional steps for producing and purifying both the tagged antigen and the catcher-modified nanoparticle. Genetic fusion was our approach of choice to produce the MrkA-NP constructs, enabling precise control over antigen presentation and ensuring a highly ordered and repetitive display of MrkA on NPs, without the requirement of additional production steps.

By testing MrkA-NPs in both bacterial and mammalian expression systems, MrkA-encapsulin, MrkA-mI3, MrkA-ferritin, and MrkA-dodecin were all successfully produced and correctly assembled as NPs displaying MrkA in Expi293 cells, while only MrkA-dodecin was expressed as a soluble protein in E. coli. We initially tested MrkA-NP expression in bacterial cells to achieve faster and higher-yield production; however, we experienced limitations in proper folding of big and complex assemblies for MrkA-NPs with the only exception of MrkA-dodecin, where MrkA retained its proper folding upon NP display as verified by NMR and nanoDSF. It is important to acknowledge that our initial attempts at bacterial expression did not involve extensive, construct-specific optimization of parameters such as IPTG concentration, induction temperature, or expression time for each individual nanocage. Given that different NP scaffolds can have markedly different folding and assembly requirements, it is highly probable that this lack of individualized optimization contributed to the observed difficulties with the more complex MrkA-NP designs in E. coli and could potentially bias yield and quality comparisons. To ensure accurate assembly and facilitate a robust comparison across all MrkA-NP designs, we transitioned to a mammalian expression system (Expi293 cells). This system proved to be highly effective, allowing for the accurate assembly of all tested MrkA-NP constructs, including those that were problematic in bacteria.

MrkA-dodecin produced in E. coli was first tested in mice, and the results showed that the fusion to the larger dodecin scaffold significantly enhanced the anti-MrkA IgG response, regardless of the adjuvant used for formulation. The immunogenicity of MrkA-dodecin was also evaluated in rabbits. Interestingly, in contrast to what was observed in mice, the recombinant MrkA monomer was able to induce a measurable anti-MrkA antibody response; however, MrkA-dodecin still elicited significantly higher IgG titers, confirming the results obtained in mice. Anti-MrkA sera from both mice and rabbits were able to recognize the native MrkA fimbriae on the surface of Kp, confirming that the optimized monomeric design preserves epitopes relevant for bacterial recognition. Most importantly, passive transfer of rabbit polyclonal sera elicited by E. coli-derived MrkA-dodecin resulted in 90% survival of mice after lethal intraperitoneal challenge with a K. pneumoniae clinical isolate, thus conclusively demonstrating the protective efficacy of anti-MrkA-NP antibodies.

The robust total IgG response elicited by MrkA-NPs underscores their significant immunogenic potential. While our initial assessment of IgG subclasses provides some insight into the qualitative nature of the humoral response, it is important to acknowledge that a deeper mechanistic understanding of the immune activation, particularly regarding specific antibody functional attributes (e.g., avidity and complement fixation) and the contribution of cellular immunity, was not extensively explored in this study. Future work will focus on comprehensively characterizing these aspects, including T-cell responses and a broader range of functional antibody assays, to fully elucidate the immune mechanisms underpinning the observed protection. Additionally, assessment of the breadth of protection across numerous diverse K. pneumoniae clinical isolates will be a valuable area of future research.

Next, we aimed to compare the immunogenicity of MrkA displayed on NP scaffolds differing for antigen multiplicity and dimension, parameters previously described to influence B-cell activation. Indeed, fusion of antigens to a larger scaffold promotes efficient uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and prolonged retention within lymphoid follicles, while the repetitive arrangement of antigens facilitates cross-linking of multiple B-cell receptors. Together, these mechanisms enhance B-cell activation and antibody production, mimicking the highly ordered antigenic structures found on bacterial or viral surfaces and thereby promoting stronger immune recognition. We found that higher antigen density correlated with stronger immune responses, whereas NP size, within the tested range of 17 to 35 nm, did not show a significant effect. However, it is crucial to interpret this finding with caution, as this analysis was based on a relatively small number of distinct NP designs (n = 4). Moreover, this apparent correlation was not maintained or became considerably weaker at later time points, specifically by day 42 (Supporting Information), suggesting that other factors may play a more dominant role in long-term immunogenicity or that the relationship is not linear across all time points. Future work will include dose-ranging studies to allow for a deeper comparison between the different NPs, which will also be crucial for understanding dose–response relationships and for evaluating whether immune responses are potentially at saturation.

Finally, our results indicated that MrkA-dodecin produced in Expi293 cells elicited a significantly higher anti-MrkA-specific IgG response compared to that derived from E. coli. This difference suggests that expression system-specific factors, such as post-translational modifications like glycosylation, may contribute to the observed enhancement in immunogenicity. However, without more extensive comparative studies specifically designed to dissect these mechanistic differences, it would be premature to definitively claim the overall superiority of one expression system based solely on this finding.

Overall, our results demonstrate that the multivalent display of MrkA on protein-based NPs noticeably enhances its immunogenicity while preserving structural integrity, highlighting the potential of NP scaffolds as versatile and effective platforms for the development of next-generation vaccines against Kp and other bacterial pathogens.

The broader field of NP engineering consistently draws from nature’s intricate protein assemblies and mimic structures. This concept of hierarchical assembly is further explored in artificial helical polymers, which form various chiral nanostructures (e.g., nanospheres, nanotubes, nanofibers) that emulate the inherent chirality and macroscopic functions of biological helices like DNA and proteins, enabling applications in drug delivery, cell imaging, and chiral recognition. This controlled construction at the molecular level, often referred to as “molecular architectonics”, is also pivotal in creating enzyme-mimetic catalyst architectures. The precise three-dimensional organization and the “second coordination sphere” surrounding active sites play a crucial role in significantly boosting catalytic activity, as observed in engineered systems utilizing small molecules, peptides, and protein scaffolds like ferritin.

Beyond the promising results for a MrkA-based vaccine against Klebsiella pneumoniae, this research underscores the profound implications of engineered protein NPs as a versatile and potent vaccine platform. The demonstrated ability to significantly enhance the immunogenicity of a challenging bacterial antigen like MrkA opens new avenues for addressing other critical pathogens where traditional vaccine approaches have fallen short, particularly those implicated in antimicrobial resistance. The modularity of this genetic fusion-based platform suggests its potential for rapid adaptation to new or emerging threats, allowing for the display of various target antigens from diverse pathogens. This work thus represents a significant step toward developing more effective, broadly protective, and rapidly deployable vaccines, ultimately contributing to global public health. Looking forward, the principles established here regarding optimal antigen display, multivalent presentation, and improved B-cell activation could be extended not only to a broader spectrum of infectious diseases but also to other immunological applications, such as allergen-specific immunotherapy or even cancer vaccine development.

Methods

Design and Expression of MrkA and MrkA Nanoparticles

The corresponding genes of MrkA and MrkA-NPs investigated are listed in Table S1, Supporting Information. All fusion constructs were ordered at Twist Bioscience as synthetic genes cloned into the expression vector pET29b­(+) plasmid under the control of the isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) inducible T7 promoter. A 10× Histidine tag (His Tag) was added to facilitate purification by affinity chromatography. The plasmids were used to transform E. coli BL21 (DE3) chemically competent cells by ThermoFisher Scientific. Cell growth was performed in HTMC medium (glycerol 15 g/L; yeast extract 30 g/L; addition of 20 g/L K2HPO4, 5 g/L KH2PO4, 5g/L MgSO4 and 1 M KOH) at 30 °C with 180 rpm shaking. When the culture reached an OD600 of 0.8–1, 1 mM IPTG was added to induce protein expression, and the cells were incubated at 20 and 18 °C, for 20 h, respectively, for MrkA and MrkA-NPs. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. Pelleted cells were resuspended in 50 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 30 mM imidazole at pH 7.4 (10 mL for each gram of bacteria pelleted) and lysed by sonication on ice for 35 min alternating 35 cycles of 30 s pulse and 30 s stop (1 cycle for 1 mL) and then clarified by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 15 min. The supernatants were filtered using a 0.22 μm filter and then purified.

The corresponding genes of MrkA and MrkA-NPs were also ordered as synthetic genes cloned into the pTwist cloning vector under the control of the Cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter to perform transient protein expression in suspension of Expi293 cells. To ensure to have a complete secretion of proteins of interest in the cells supernatant, a murine IgK leader sequence was added to the coding sequence of the proteins. In addition to that, a 10× His Tag was added with the only exception of mI3 because of the low exposure of N- and C- termini. Expi293 cells were plated with a confluency of 2.5–3 × 106 cells/mL and viability around 98% in a 125 mL flask, resting for 2–3 h with agitation at 37 °C and 8% CO2 before proceeding with transfection. Cells were transfected with 1 μg of nucleic acid per 3 × 106 cells/mL encapsulated in ExpiFectamine and incubated for 3 days. On the day after transfection (18–22 h post-transfection), ExpiFectamine293 Transfection Enhancer 1 and ExpiFectamine293 Transfection Enhancer 2 were added to the suspension to slow down cellular division. Three days post-transfection, cells were removed by centrifugation at 200g for 10 min, and supernatants were collected. Despite the typical harvest time of 6 days to reach maximum titers for many secreted proteins, the transient transfection in Expi293 was set to a final time point of 3 days post-transfection.

Purification and Characterization of MrkA and MrkA Nanoparticles

A first step of purification through immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) was used to isolate the proteins of interest containing the His Tag. The filtered supernatants obtained from both E. coli and Expi293 cell expression were loaded in a HisTrap FF affinity chromatography column by Cytiva. The column was washed with an imidazole gradient and proteins eluted with 500 mM imidazole and NaCl, pH 7.4. Peak fractions were pooled, concentrated by Amicon (Millipore), and applied to the second step of purification, a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). A Superdex75 Increase prepacked column by Cytiva was chosen for MrkA, while for MrkA-NPs, a Superdex200 prepacked column with an isocratic elution in PBS (phosphate-buffered saline) pH 7.4 was used. Peak fractions were pooled together and characterized. For MrkA-mI3, purification was made of 2 consecutive SEC steps. First, SDS-PAGE analysis of the samples was performed. 10 μL of sample + NuPAGE Lithium dodecyl sulfate (LDS) sample buffer 4× (Invitrogen) and NuPAGE Sample Reducing Agent 10× (Invitrogen) were boiled for 3 min at 100 °C and then loaded in NuPAGE Novex Bis Tris 4–12% gels. The SDS-PAGE gels were run in 2-morpholinoethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer at 150 V for approximately 45 min and then stained with ProBlue Safe Stain Giotto Biotech. SeeBlue Plus2 prestained Protein Standard (Invitrogen) was used as a protein ladder. Moreover, the final purified proteins were analyzed by Western blotting using the anti-His antibody or anti-MrkA human mAbs St1C1 as a primary antibody. SDS-PAGE gels were transferred on an iBlot3 Transfer Stacks nitrocellulose membrane (Invitrogen) and let to adsorb for 9 min. The membrane was then blocked with 10% milk in PBS and 0.05% Tween detergent. The binding with anti-His and anti-MrkA primary antibodies, diluted respectively 1:2000 or 1:1000 in 3% milk, was followed for 1 h at room temperature with shaking, and then the membrane was washed three times with PBS and 0.05% Tween for 10 min each. The secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was then added (diluted 1:2000) in 3% milk to the membrane for 1 h. Three additional wash steps were performed, as above, before adding the Thermo Scientific Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate in order to acquire the signal using ChemiDoc imaging system.

For MrkA-NPs, DLS and TEM analyses were performed. DLS measurements were acquired with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, Herremberg, Germany) equipped with a 633 nm He–Ne laser operating at an angle of 173°. Scattering light detected at 173° was automatically adjusted by laser attenuation filters. For data analysis, the viscosity and refractive index (RI) of the PBS buffer solution (at 25 °C) were used. The software used to collect and analyze the data was Zetasizer, version 7.11. The temperature was set to 25 °C. 40 μL of sample at 50 μg/mL protein content was characterized in duplicate in single-use polystyrene microcuvette (ZEN0040, Alfatest) with a path length of 10 mm. The electron microscopy analysis was performed with a 5 μL loading of sample concentrated at 20 ng/μL onto a 300 square mesh glow discharged copper grid for 30 s. The excess was blotted, and the grid was negatively stained using NanoW for 30 s. The samples were analyzed using a Tecnai G2 spirit, and the images were acquired using a Tvips TemCam F216 (EM Menu software).

Structural Characterization of MrkA-Dodecin by Nano Differential Scanning Fluorimetry (NanoDSF) and Solution NMR Spectroscopy

NanoDSF analysis was performed by using a Tycho NT.6 instrument (Nanotemper). Samples at 0.2 mg/mL total protein were loaded into capillaries and subjected to a linear temperature ramp from 35 to 95 °C (30 °C/min) to induce protein unfolding. The increase in intrinsic tryptophan or tyrosine fluorescence was monitored, and the melting temperature (T m), corresponding to the midpoint of the transition, was evaluated. The Tycho NT.6 system uses the first derivative view to automatically detect the inflection temperature as a function of the increasing temperature. A peak in the first derivative view corresponds to the detected T m.

The MrkA-dodecin construct was produced uniformly labeled with 13C–15N by growing E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells containing the pET29 based MrkA-dodecin expressing plasmid in ISOGRO 13C–15N-rich medium (Sigma). The expression and purification protocol applied to the labeled protein is the same as the recombinant protein produced in HTMC medium. 500 μL of sample concentrated 400 μM + 10% D2O was inserted in a 5 mm NMR tube, and NMR experiments were performed. The heteronuclear multiple quantum correlation (HMQC) spectrum was acquired at 298 K on an Advance 950 MHz Bruker spectrometer, then processed using the standard Bruker software TOPSPIN, and analyzed through CARA. These spectra were acquired also for 13C–15N-MrkA and compared.

MrkA and MrkA-Nanoparticle In Vivo Studies

The animal studies were performed at an AAALAC-accredited GSK Animal Facility under an ethically reviewed animal project approved by the Italian Ministry of Health and were run in accordance with GSK Policies of Care, Welfare, and Treatment of Animals. MrkA and MrkA-dodecin, previously produced and characterized, were tested in mice. Groups of 10 CD1 mice (female, 4–6 weeks old) were subcutaneously (SC) vaccinated once or twice with 10 of MrkA in a 200 μL total volume at study days 0 and 28. AS01, which is a liposomal adjuvant containing the immunostimulant monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) and an active ingredient called QS21 extracted from the bark of Quillaja Saponaria (QS), or Alhydrogel (an aluminum hydroxide wet gel suspension) was evaluated as adjuvants. Antigens were formulated 2 days before each vaccination, and PBS pH 7.2 was used as the diluent. A concentration of 0.7 mg/mL Alhydrogel (in terms of Al3+) and concentration of 2.5 μg/dose AS01 (in terms of QS21 and MPL) were used. Antigens were formulated 2 days before injection, and S separated formulations were prepared for day 0 and day 28 immunizations. The prepared formulations free of viable bacteria were supplied to the GSK ARC animal facility in falcon tubes stored at 2–8 °C. The formulations were also tested for endotoxin levels, pH, and osmolality before immunization.

MrkA and MrkA-dodecin were also tested in rabbits. Groups of 8 New Zeeland female rabbits were intramuscularly vaccinated twice with 15 μg of MrkA in 500 μL of total volume at study days 0 and 29. Antigens were formulated 2 days before vaccination, and separated formulations were prepared for day 0 and day 28 immunizations. PBS was used as an antigen diluent, while Alhydrogel at a concentration of 0.7 mg/mL (Al3+) was used as an adjuvant. Prepared formulations free of viable bacteria were supplied to the GSK ARC animal facility in 5 mL Eppendorf tubes. Preimmune sera were collected and pooled the day before the first immunization.

MrkA, MrkA-encapsulin, MrkA-ferritin, MrkA-mI3, and MrkA-dodecin produced in Expi293 cells were evaluated in an in vivo study in mice. In this study, 6 groups of 8 female CD1 mice, 4–6 weeks old, were used and were vaccinated subcutaneously with 200 μL of formulated antigens (dose 2.5 μg of MrkA) at day 0 and day 28. Antigens were diluted with PBS pH 7.2 and formulated with Alhydrogel at the concentration of 0.7 mg/mL (Al3+). The first bleeding was done at day 27, the final one was performed at day 42, and then mice were euthanized.

Anti-MrkA ELISA Assay and Statistical Analysis

Collected sera were analyzed by ELISA for anti-MrkA total IgG and total IgM content using MrkA as a plate coating antigen. 384-well microtiter plates were coated with MrkA as a coating antigen at a concentration of 15 μg/mL in PBS buffer (40 μL in each well). Plates were incubated overnight at 2–8 °C, washed once with PBS-0.05% Tween20 (Sigma-Aldrich), and blocked with 100 μL/well PBS containing 5% milk powder (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at 25 °C. The plates were washed and then incubated with sera thereof in various dilutions at 25 °C for 2 h, washed, and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C with antimouse IgG (total IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgG3) and antimouse IgM Alkaline Phosphatase (Sigma-Aldrich) diluted respectively 1:7000 and 1:1000 in PBS-0.05% Tween20–0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich). After washing, the plates were developed with a solution of p-nitrophenyl phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, 1 tablet for 20 mL of Milli-Q water) at 25 °C for 1 h. Then, the absorbance was measured by using a microplate reader. Each step of dispensing and washing was done by using a Hamilton Robotic liquid handler and Agilent BioTek plate washer. Results are expressed in ELISA units (EU/mL) determined relative to a calibrated standard curve run in duplicate within each plate. One ELISA unit equals the reciprocal of the dilution of the standard serum giving an OD490 nm–405 nm of 1 in the assay.

Statistical analysis of ELISA responses was performed using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The nonparametric Mann–Whitney two-tailed test and Kruskal–Wallis analysis with posthoc Dunn’s test were used respectively to compare two or multiple groups. The Wilcoxon matched-pair signed rank two-tailed test was used to compare results from the same group at different time points.

MrkA Characterization of Animal Sera on a Klebsiella pneumoniae Strain by Flow Cytometry

Kp strain NCTC11679 (K61:O3) was grown overnight at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium and then were diluted to 0.05 OD/mL and grown until 0.2 OD/mL for about 1 h. A total of 1 OD of bacteria was pelleted and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4000g for 10 min. After washing, bacteria were fixed with 4% (w/v) formaldehyde for 30 min at 4 °C. After that, bacteria were washed with PBS + 1% (w/v) BSA, plated in a round-bottom 96-well plate (100 μL/well, ThermoFisher Scientific), and incubated with mice or rabbit sera (after the second injection) as the primary antibody (100 μL/well) at a dilution 1:500 in PBS-BSA for 1 h. Finally, samples were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488 goat antimouse or antirabbit IgG diluted 1:500 in PBS-BSA for 30 min and fluorescence was measured using a Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data was analyzed by using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences) and plotted by using GraphPad Prism 7.

Passive Transfer of Polyclonal Rabbit Sera and Challenge of Mice

Groups of 10 female CD1 mice 8–9 weeks old received subcutaneously pooled polyclonal rabbit sera 24 and 2 h before challenge (0.4 and 0.2 mL, respectively) derived from immunization with MrkA-dodecin or preimmune rabbit sera as negative control. Mice were challenged intraperitoneally with 1 × 106 CFU of the clinical Kp isolate IL02364192 (kindly provided by Wits VIDA Nkanyezi Research Unit, Johannesburg, South Africa). To prepare the inoculum, the strain was streaked onto LB agar and incubated at 37 °C ON; the following day, five colonies were inoculated into 5 mL of LB and grown ON at 37 °C with shaking. The ON culture was then diluted into 100 mL of LB to an initial OD600 of 0.05 and incubated at 37 °C with shaking for ∼1.5 h until reaching ∼0.5 OD600. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation for 10 min and resuspended in 20 mL of PBS supplemented with 1% gastric mucin. Clinical signs were monitored daily after inoculation, and any adverse findings were recorded. Animals that met predefined humane end points were euthanized according to ethical requirements. CFU was counted in the blood 24 h after the infection and in the spleen at the end of the study or immediately after euthanasia for animals that reached humane end points. Arbitrary titers of 107 and 109 CFU/mL were assigned to the blood or spleens of animals that were found dead. Protective efficacy was evaluated up to day 4 post-challenge. The mice challenge study was performed at the Fondazione Toscana Life Science Animal Care Facility under the animal project 8/2025-PR, approved by the Italian Ministry of Health.
