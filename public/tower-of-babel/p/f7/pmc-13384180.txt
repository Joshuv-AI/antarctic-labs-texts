ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo understand pregnant women’s perception of the quality of antenatal care (ANC) services in terms of service provision and experience in Sudan, a country with one of the highest rates of maternal deaths worldwide.DesignA cross-sectional facility-based study.SettingPublic primary healthcare centres in Khartoum state, Sudan.Participants473 pregnant women attending ANC visits. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a standardised questionnaire.Primary outcome measuresThe perceived quality of ANC services assessed across two continuous domains: service provision score and care experience score.ResultsThe mean score for service provision was 19.96±3.03 out of 56. The great majority of the participants did not have their height or weight measured at any ANC visits 85% and 64.5%, respectively. About 87.1% and 82.5% of the participants, respectively, had blood and urine tests during each ANC visit. However, only 52.6% of the women reported receiving tetanus injections. The percentage of women receiving ultrasound examination at each visit was 88.4%. The mean score of care experience was 58.68±8.22 out of 84. Three-quarters of the women (74%) understood the purpose of the tests performed. The majority felt free to ask questions (87.3%). But only half and a third of the women were told about the signs of pregnancy complications and where to go in case any complications occurred, respectively. In linear regression, being married and older age had significant positive effects on women’s perception of care provision (p=0.004 and p=0.003, respectively). Several factors were associated with their negative perceptions. These factors need to be further investigated and addressed to improve the quality of ANC provided.Keywords: Public health, Pregnant Women, Quality in health care

Background

Maternal mortality remains a major global public health challenge. Approximately 90% of maternal deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa and Asia, where the lifetime risk of maternal death is estimated to be one in 39 women, reflecting persistent inequities in access to and quality of maternal healthcare services.1 Sudan has one of the highest rates of maternal death worldwide, with 1107 deaths per 100 000 live births.2 In Sudan, preventable obstetric complications—such as haemorrhage, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, infection and unsafe abortion—are leading direct causes of maternal death. In contrast, conditions such as malaria, anaemia and hepatitis contribute indirectly.2 3

Maternal mortality disproportionately affects women in rural, poor and marginalised communities. Delays in seeking care, reaching health facilities and receiving adequate treatment after arrival remain major contributors to maternal deaths in the country. Birth difficulties—which may be readily avoided and treated—are to blame for more than 60% of maternal fatalities in the nation. One in four Sudanese women who give birth endures severe bleeding, and more than one in three women undergo protracted labour. In two-thirds of the cases of maternal deaths that have been examined, seeking medical attention and finding and getting to a facility have taken longer than expected for the women.1

Antenatal care (ANC) is a critical intervention for reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. It provides an important platform for early detection and management of pregnancy-related complications, health promotion, disease prevention and preparation for childbirth.4 High-quality ANC enables early identification and management of pregnancy-related complications, supports healthy behaviours during pregnancy and facilitates timely referral when needed. Core components of ANC include routine clinical assessments, laboratory investigations, tetanus immunisation, micronutrient supplementation, health education and psychosocial support.

The WHO has updated its recommendations from a minimum of four ANC contacts to a minimum of eight contacts to reduce perinatal mortality and to improve women’s experience of care (UNICEF data).5 These recommendations aim to reduce perinatal mortality and improve women’s satisfaction with care. Despite this, the proportion of women receiving at least four antenatal care visits varies widely across countries, ranging from under 30% in countries in sub-Saharan Africa to over 90% in countries in Latin America and the Caribbean (UNICEF data).6 7 In Sudan, 75% of pregnant women receive at least one ANC visit, and 47% of those receive four visits. Young Sudanese women are more likely to die in childbirth due to their limited access to reproductive health services.1

Pregnancy-related avoidable morbidity and mortality rates were still too high at the beginning of the Sustainable Development Goals era in 2016. Even while significant progress has been made, nations must build on and accelerate these achievements and broaden their goals beyond mere survival to maximise the well-being and potential of their citizens. Additionally, voluntary family planning to space births, the provision of proper ANC, the assurance of expert attendance at delivery and the support of emergency obstetric care, when necessary, can all help to lower maternal and newborn mortality.8

Although improving the quality of maternal healthcare is critical for achieving national and global maternal health targets, there is limited evidence from Sudan regarding women’s perceptions of the quality of ANC they receive. While the burden of maternal mortality in Sudan is well documented, evidence on the quality of ANC—particularly from women’s perspectives—remains scarce. Existing studies in Sudan have largely focused on ANC coverage and service utilisation rather than the content, experience and perceived quality of care. Moreover, little is known about which groups of women benefit most from improvements in the quality of ANC or how sociodemographic factors influence women’s perceptions of care. These gaps limit policymakers’ ability to design targeted quality-improvement interventions. Understanding women’s experiences and perceptions is essential, as perceived quality influences service utilisation, adherence to recommendations and health-seeking behaviour. Therefore, this study aims to assess women’s perceptions of the quality of ANC services—focusing on both service provision and experience of care—among women attending primary healthcare centres in Khartoum State, Sudan.

Methods

Study area

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted at primary healthcare centres in Khartoum state from 30 October to 29 November 2023. Khartoum state: a city with an area of 22.142 km² is the capital of Sudan and is located in the heart of Sudan at the confluence of the Blue Nile and White Nile. It contains seven localities: Khartoum (the capital of the state), Omdurman, Sharq an-Nil, Jabal Awliya, Karary, Ombada and Bahri.

Study setting

The study was conducted at public primary healthcare centres providing ANC services in Khartoum State. At the time of the study, there were 432 functional primary healthcare centres across the state offering preventive and curative services, including ANC. These centres deliver services such as reproductive health care, childhood immunisation, nutrition services and management of common illnesses. There are 432 primary healthcare centres in the state, providing preventive and curative health services to insured and uninsured patients. According to the WHO, there are 1.5 primary healthcare facilities per 10 000 people in Sudan. These centres offer packages of services, including childhood immunisation, nutrition, reproductive health, integrated management of childhood immunisation, management of common illnesses and prescription of necessary drugs.9

Source and study population

The source population comprised all women of reproductive age attending ANC services at public primary healthcare centres in Khartoum State during the study period. The study sample comprised 473 women attending ANC services during the data collection period, who were approached by trained data collectors and assessed for eligibility using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All eligible women who agreed to participate and provided consent were enrolled until the required sample size was reached.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were women who attended at least one ANC visit at the selected primary healthcare centres and whose most recent pregnancy ended within the previous 12 months, including women with live births, stillbirths or miscarriages occurring at or after 24 weeks of gestation. Exclusion criteria included women with an ongoing pregnancy at the time of data collection, women whose most recent pregnancy ended more than 1 year before the survey and women who were unable to participate in the interview due to severe illness or communication difficulties. The unit of analysis in this study was the individual woman who attended ANC services and met the eligibility criteria.

Sampling

Sample size and sampling technique

The study sample was calculated using Cochran’s formula for the unknown population: n0=required sample size (initial estimate), z: the z-value (1.96 for 95% confidence level), p: the fraction of the population (as a percentage) that displays the attribute of interest (set at 0.5), q=1p (fraction without the attribute), e: desired level of precision, the margin of error (0.05 for 5%). With a 95% CI, 50% response distribution, 0.05 margin of error and a non-response rate of 14%, the final sample size was set at 473. This figure incorporated a design effect to account for the multistage cluster sampling design and the intra-cluster correlation between participants recruited from the same facility.

Multistage cluster sampling was used to select the sample, facilities and patients. The sampling frame consisted of all public primary healthcare centres providing ANC services in the selected localities of Khartoum State. Stage one involved the random selection of three localities from the seven localities of Khartoum state: Khartoum, Bahri and Omdurman. Stage two was the selection of primary healthcare centres; because the number of participants was relatively equal across centres, at least three centres were randomly selected from each locality. A total of nine primary healthcare centres were included in the study. Three centres were randomly selected from each of the three selected localities (Khartoum, Bahri and Omdurman). The selected facilities were public primary healthcare centres that routinely provide ANC services. Stage three was the selection of participants; within each selected health facility, participants were recruited using consecutive sampling, whereby all eligible women attending ANC services during the data collection period were invited to participate until the allocated sample size was reached.

Data collection techniques and tools

A face-to-face interview questionnaire was used. Informed consent was obtained on the same day as the interview. The quality of ANC was assessed using a standardised questionnaire adapted from previously published, validated tools for assessing ANC quality10 (online supplemental file 1). The questionnaire was divided into two sections: the first contains participants’ sociodemographic information, and the second contains indicators to assess the quality of ANC services. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and then translated into Arabic. To ensure accuracy and consistency, a back-translation into English was performed.

Experts in ANC reviewed the translated version to assess clarity, relevance and content validity. Minor revisions were made based on their feedback. The questionnaire was then pilot-tested among 30 participants who were not included in the final analysis to evaluate clarity and comprehension of the questions. Based on the results of the pilot testing, small adjustments were made to improve wording and ensure better understanding before distributing the final version of the questionnaire.

Data quality assurance

Public health experts reviewed the questionnaire for content validity and clarity. Data collectors were trained before data collection. Daily supervision and review of completed questionnaires were conducted to ensure completeness and consistency. Data entry was double-checked to minimise entry errors.

Data analysis

The SPSS V.26 is used for data entry and statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to present the data in tables and figures. Percentages, frequencies, means and SD were used to characterise sociodemographic information on the women and the quality of ANC visits. Additionally, linear regression was used to assess the association between the study variables. The dependent variables in the study were women’s perception of care provision and experience of care, while the independent variables were residency, education, occupation, marital status, parity and age. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Informed consent from participants

Informed verbal and written consent was obtained from each patient after explaining the purpose of the research; each participant was given the option to withdraw from the study at any time. Participants were not harmed in any way, and the confidentiality of their information was ensured at all stages of this research.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

The majority of participants were multiparous (78.4%). Among them, 37.2% had four or more previous births, 30.5% had two births and 26.7% had three births, while only 5.7% were primiparous. Most women initiated ANC during the first trimester (81.4%), whereas 4.7% began care in the third trimester. Pregnancy outcomes were predominantly live births (91.1%), while 6.8% resulted in abnormal outcomes and 2.1% in stillbirths. Most participants (82.7%) reported no complications during delivery or the postnatal period (further details in table 1).

Regarding the location of ANC, 44.6% of women reported attending private clinics, followed by public primary healthcare centres (30.7%) and public hospitals (20.5%). Assessment of the quality of ANC service provision

Assessment of ANC service provision was based solely on women’s self-reported experiences during ANC visits (online supplemental table 1, online supplemental file 2). Most participants (85.0%) reported that their height was not measured during ANC visits. Nearly two-thirds (64.5%) reported that their weight was not measured, while only 17.3% indicated that their weight was measured at every visit.

Laboratory investigations were more consistently provided. Blood tests were reportedly performed at every visit for 87.1% of women, and urine tests for 82.5%. Slightly more than half of participants (52.6%) reported receiving tetanus toxoid vaccination during the current pregnancy, while 30.4% had completed tetanus vaccination before pregnancy, and 11.2% did not receive it at all. Iron supplementation was widely provided, with 93.2% of women reporting receipt of iron tablets.

Ultrasound examination was among the most frequently reported services, with 88.4% of participants reporting that an ultrasound was performed at every visit. Smaller proportions reported receiving ultrasound examinations occasionally (4.7%), sometimes (3.0%), rarely (2.7%) or never (1.3%).

Overall, while most women received at least one component of ANC, the consistent delivery of the full recommended ANC package was uncommon, indicating gaps in comprehensive service provision (further details in online supplemental table 1, online supplemental file 2).

Experience of ANC

Regarding the experience of care (online supplemental table 2A and B, online supplemental file 2), 74.0% of participants reported understanding the purpose of the tests performed during ANC visits. Only 29.2% were informed of their weight measurement results. Blood pressure results were communicated to 82.5% of women, while 7.6% reported never being informed. Among those who underwent urine testing, 87.9% were informed of the results, whereas 6.1% were not. Nearly all women (89.6%) were informed of their blood test results.

Most participants felt free to ask questions during visits (87.3%), although only 67.0% were explicitly asked whether they had any questions. Respectful treatment by healthcare providers was reported by 93.4% of women, and 93.9% stated that they were not treated differently based on personal characteristics. A friendly attitude from providers was reported by 63.4% of participants, and 85.0% reported being able to discuss concerns privately. The cleanliness of health facilities was rated positively by 93.0% of women.

Counselling on pregnancy-related risks was less consistent. Only 53.9% of participants were informed about danger signs during pregnancy, and 37.2% were not told where to seek care in case of complications. Education on birth preparedness, nutrition and breastfeeding was reported by 30.9%, 43.6% and 30.2% of participants, respectively. Most women (84.1%) reported being informed about what to expect during pregnancy and delivery (further details in online supplemental table 2A and B, online supplemental file 2).

Regression of antenatal care quality measures on variable predictors

Regarding the perceived provision of care, analysis revealed that age, educational attainment, occupation and marital status were significant predictors. Increasing age was positively associated with higher perceived provision of care scores (B=0.08, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.13, p=0.003). Educational level also showed a significant association, with illiterate women reporting significantly lower provision scores than women with an education level above university (B=−2.33, 95% CI −3.59 to −1.07, p<0.001). Among occupational groups, teachers reported significantly lower provision scores compared with the ‘others’ category (B=−3.22, 95% CI −5.45 to −0.98, p=0.005). Finally, marital status was a significant factor; both single women (B=5.43, 95% CI 0.96 to 9.89, p=0.017) and married women (B=4.72, 95% CI 1.54 to 7.90, p=0.004) reported significantly higher perceived provision scores when compared with divorced women.

For the experience of care, significant predictors included residency, education, parity and age. Geographic location played a role, with women residing in rural areas reporting significantly lower experience-of-care scores than those in urban areas (B=−1.76, 95% CI −3.47 to −0.05, p=0.044). A notable educational gradient was observed; illiterate women (B=−10.50, 95% CI −13.62 to −7.39, p<0.001) and those with only primary education (B=−5.06, 95% CI −8.23 to −1.89, p=0.002) reported markedly lower experience scores compared with women with an education level above university. Furthermore, parity significantly influenced outcomes, as nulliparous women reported lower care experience scores than multiparous women (B=−2.01, 95% CI −3.69 to −0.33, p=0.019). Similar to the provision of care, older age was associated with higher experience scores (B=0.13, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.26, p=0.038) (further details in table 2)

These findings indicate that sociodemographic factors play an important role in shaping women’s perceptions of both ANC provision and experience.

Discussion

This study provides important evidence on the perceived quality of ANC in public primary healthcare centres in Sudan, highlighting substantial gaps in both service provision and experience of care. Despite relatively high contact with antenatal services, many women did not receive all essential components of care, particularly routine physical examinations, counselling and preventive interventions.

The study demonstrated that the average score for ANC service provision was low, at 19.96±3.03 (maximum score is 56), among women attending ANC visits. This could be understood in the light of the incomplete basic services the women received during their visits. For instance, the vast majority of participants did not have their height or weight measured at all during ANC visits. This was contrary to other studies, in which the majority had their height and weight measured at every visit.10 Moreover, only half of the women in our study received tetanus injections, which, according to Sudanese guidelines, is considered one of the minimum services to be provided during ANC visits.8 However, on the other side, the majority of women had their blood pressure measured and had urine tests all the time during the antenatal visits, which is not the case in the study conducted in Kenya, where only half and a quarter of them had blood pressure measurements and urine tests, respectively, at every visit.10 Another interesting finding in our study was that a great proportion of the women had ultrasound scans in all of their visits. This might be attributed to the fact that the majority of women lived in urban areas and went to healthcare facilities where ultrasound machines were available and functioning at all times.

Regarding the experience of ANC received, the average score was 58.68±8.22 (maximum score is 84). Most, but not all, of the participants were told about the results of their tests; this finding was consistent with Afulani’s study.10 Furthermore, although the majority of women stated that they felt free to ask questions and they were asked if they had any questions, yet half and a third of them respectively were not told about signs of pregnancy complications or where to go in case they encountered any complications. The same proportions were noticed in a study by Abdel-Tawab.8 The reason might be a deficiency on the healthcare provider’s side, with them dealing only with what is in front of them and not providing comprehensive ANC that follows the guidelines. One way to validate this point is that only one-third of the women received birth preparedness, nutrition and breastfeeding education. Lack of counselling on these important topics might have negative implications, bearing in mind that almost three-quarters of pregnant women in Sudan give birth at home, according to a situation analysis conducted in Sudan.8

The study showed that women’s age has a significant positive effect on their perceptions of care provision and care experience; the older the women, the more positive their perceptions. This aligns with Afulani’s study,10 where older ages have higher care provision and care experience scores. Interestingly, marital status was found to impact women’s perception of care provision. Both single and married women reported significantly higher care-provision scores than divorced women. This suggests that divorced women may represent a particularly vulnerable group with lower perceived support or care during ANC visits, a finding that warrants further investigation into how social support structures influence care perception. In terms of the experience of care, illiteracy and low educational levels showed a significant negative association; this might be attributed to a lack of awareness in these groups of women. However, the study showed that women in urban areas had a positive experience of care. The reasons for this might be the availability of better-equipped facilities than in rural areas. Regarding parity, nulliparous women (having no children) had a significantly negative effect on their care experience. The reason for that might be because of the stress and anxiety they feel from the pregnancy, as it is their first time, and this could affect their perception of the experience; however, more research needs to be done to investigate the causes. The findings of this study have important implications for maternal health policy and practice in Sudan. The observed gaps in the consistent delivery of core ANC components suggest the need for strengthened supervision, standardised ANC protocols and continuous training of healthcare providers at the primary care level. Improving counselling on pregnancy danger signs, birth preparedness and nutrition could enhance women’s engagement with health services and potentially increase skilled birth attendance. From a policy perspective, incorporating routine monitoring of ANC quality indicators—beyond coverage alone—into national maternal health programmes is essential. Strengthening supply chains for essential equipment and ensuring adequate staffing at primary healthcare centres may further improve the quality and equity of ANC services.

Limitations

Several limitations should be kept in mind when interpreting these findings. Because the study was cross-sectional, it captures women’s perceptions at a single point in time and cannot establish whether the sociodemographic characteristics we examined genuinely shape the quality of care received or are merely associated with it; the relationships reported here are therefore best read as associations rather than causal effects. The assessment of ANC also rested entirely on what women were able to recall about visits that, in some cases, had taken place up to a year earlier, which leaves the data open to recall bias and to the under-reporting or over-reporting of particular services such as height measurement or tetanus vaccination. A related concern is social desirability bias: the interviews were conducted face to face, often in or close to the facilities where women had been seen, and some participants may have been reluctant to speak critically of their providers. This tendency could partly explain the consistently high ratings recorded for respectful treatment, privacy and cleanliness. Interpretation is further complicated by where care was actually received, because although recruitment took place in public primary healthcare centres, a sizeable share of participants reported that their ANC had in fact been obtained in private clinics or hospitals; the experiences captured therefore reflect a mixture of settings rather than the public primary care system alone, and comparisons between facility types should be made with that caveat in mind. Our reliance on consecutive recruitment of women who were already attending services also meant that those who never reached ANC—frequently the most disadvantaged—went unrepresented, so the shortfalls we documented may understate the true gaps in coverage and quality across the population. Geographically, the work was confined to three localities within Khartoum State and drew on a predominantly urban sample, which limits how far the results can be extended to rural communities, to other states or to the many areas disrupted by the ongoing conflict. Finally, although the questionnaire was adapted from previously validated tools and reviewed by local experts, it was not formally re-validated in the Sudanese setting and the internal consistency of the two summary scores was not reported; residual confounding from variables we did not capture—such as household income, distance to the nearest facility and provider-level or facility-level characteristics—cannot be excluded and may have influenced both the care women received and how they perceived it.

Conclusion

While assessing the quality of ANC in Sudan’s primary healthcare centres from mothers’ perspectives, we demonstrate that the average score for the provision of ANC services was low among women attending ANC visits. This could be understood in the light of the incomplete basic services the women received during their visits. Regarding the experience of care received, the average scores were low for education and communication; nevertheless, they were high for privacy and confidentiality. The quality of care varies significantly with parity and across different sociodemographic groups.

Recommendations

This study serves as the basis for larger studies assessing the structural provision of care in ANC clinics. Qualitative methods were recommended to provide an in-depth perspective from the mothers’ perspectives. Furthermore, healthcare providers’ perspectives should be assessed to give a comprehensive assessment of the quality of care.
