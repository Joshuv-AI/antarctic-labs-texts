Association between obesity with physical activity, eating behavior, sleep quality, general health, and the incidence of hypertension among women: a study in rural and nomadic population of western, Iran
Authors: Rastegar Hoseini, Zahra Hoseini, Nariman Rahmani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a global health issue, particularly among women. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between obesity and physical activity, eating behavior, sleep quality, general health, and the incidence of hypertension among women in rural and nomadic areas of Iran.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from August to December 2020 among 2,581 rural and nomadic women aged 18 years and older in Kermanshah, western Iran. A multi-stage cluster sampling method was used to ensure a representative sample. Data were collected using a structured sociodemographic questionnaire, anthropometric measurements, and validated self-report instruments. Participants’ age, marital status, educational level, occupational status, smoking history, and hospitalization in the past year were recorded. Body weight, height, and blood pressure were measured using standardized protocols. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated and categorized according to WHO criteria: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²). Physical activity level (PAL) was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (SF-IPAQ), eating behavior with the Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ-R18), general health with the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-28), and sleep quality with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± SD or percentage. Inferential analyses were performed using non-parametric tests (Chi-square, Kruskal-Wallis) for group comparisons. To evaluate the association between health-related behaviors and BMI categories, multinomial logistic regression models were fitted with normal weight as the reference category, and results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 50.17 ± 13.49 years. The prevalence of obesity was 44.98%, and 29.99% were overweight. Significant differences were observed across BMI categories in age, marital status, education, occupation, smoking, and hospitalization (all p < 0.05). Women with obesity had significantly lower physical activity levels, poorer sleep quality, worse general health, and higher rates of hypertension compared to those with normal weight (p < 0.001 for all). Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that low physical activity was strongly associated with higher odds of obesity (aOR = 4.32; 95% CI: 3.41–5.48) and overweight (aOR = 2.14; 95% CI: 1.68–2.73). Similarly, hypertension, poor sleep quality, and impaired general health were significantly associated with increased odds of obesity (aOR = 3.21, 95% CI: 2.54–4.06; aOR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.88–3.24; and aOR = 2.95, 95% CI: 2.23–3.90, respectively). No significant association was found between PAL and eating behavior (p = 0.705). A sensitivity analysis using binary logistic regression confirmed that obesity was significantly associated with hypertension (aOR = 3.21; 95% CI: 2.54–4.06, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThese findings highlight that low physical activity, poor sleep quality, and impaired general health are significantly associated with obesity and hypertension among rural and nomadic women in western Iran. While a strong inverse relationship was observed between physical activity and obesity, no significant association was found with eating behavior, suggesting that these lifestyle factors may be independently influenced. Public health strategies should prioritize community-based interventions to promote physical activity and improve sleep and mental health in this high-risk population. Future research should focus on longitudinal and interventional studies to develop targeted, culturally appropriate policies for obesity and hypertension prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01713-1.Keywords: Education, Marital status, Smoking, Women’s health, Blood pressure, Sedentary lifestyle

Contributions to the literature • This study provides the first comprehensive evidence on obesity (45%) and hypertension (37%) prevalence among rural and nomadic women in Western Iran—a high-risk, understudied population.• We uniquely identify low physical activity, poor sleep quality, and impaired general health as key modifiable drivers of obesity/hypertension in this group, independent of eating behavior.• Findings highlight urgent needs for culturally tailored interventions targeting physical activity, mental health, and sleep in marginalized women’s communities.• The evidence fills critical gaps in global public health data for nomadic populations and informs Iran’s national non-communicable disease strategies.

Background

Obesity, especially central obesity, is a notably increasing global health issue in many countries which is defined as Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2, and waist circumference ≥ 90 cm [1]. The prevalence of obesity was found to be 20–40% of adults worldwide and this trend is continuing to escalate worldwide [2]. According to the latest reports of the STEPs survey in 2011, more than 27% of Iranian female adults (over 15) are obese [3]. Lifestyle factors, particularly eating behavior, physical activity level (PAL), alcohol consumption, stress, and socioeconomic status, as well as family history of chronic health problems (obesity, diabetes, hypertension, etc.), aging, early marriage, and genetic factors, are among the innumerable epidemiological determinants causes of obesity [4, 5]. It is known that adequate PAL is a major health-promoting factor while a sedentary lifestyle contributes to the increased incidence of obesity and other chronic diseases [6, 7]. However, limited and inconsistent population studies have investigated the effect of physical activity on the progression of obesity and other related diseases in women. Meanwhile, some studies investigate the association between eating behavior with obesity reporting a high prevalence of unhealthy eating habits among obese adults [8]. The relationship between sleep quality with physical and general health has been recognized [9, 10]. Furthermore, prior findings emphasize that short or poor sleep not only exacerbates general health but also impairs metabolism and endocrine function leading to several unfavorable health consequences (i.e. obesity) [11, 12]. Studies have reported a correlation between obesity and low sleep quality [13]; with women being more prone to sleep problems and thus more vulnerable to obesity and other diseases [14, 15]. Low PAL and unhealthy eating habits are also the strongest predictors of hypertension [16]. Furthermore, obesity also affects the incidence of hypertension which is associated with both previous factors [17]. Studies revealed an association between hypertension and the risks of heart complications [18] and argued the importance of preventing hypertension occurrence and development in lowering cardiovascular disease complications and death [19]. You et al. (2018) showed that even short physical activity sessions might reduce the development and progression of hypertension [20]. It has been demonstrated that obesity is negatively associated with the development, progression, and mortality of chronic diseases including diabetes [21], asthma [22], infertility [23], myocardial dysfunction [24], and depressive symptoms among women [25]. Few studies have investigated the underlying causes of obesity among women comprehensively. Due to the impact of women’s health on the family (as a mother) and community, this study aims to determine the sociodemographic and anthropometric characteristics and the association between obesity with PAL, eating behavior, hypertension, sleep quality, general health, and quality of life among rural and nomadic women, Kermanshah Iran.

Methods

Study design and population

This applied research studied western Iran’s rural and nomadic women from August to December 2020 using a multi-stage (Cluster) sampling method to reach the target population and select a representative sample effectively. The initial step involved identifying the population of interest, which consisted of rural and nomadic women in western Iran, achieved by consulting the National Statistics Center of Iran for accurate demographic data. To determine the appropriate sample size, Morgan’s sample size table was utilized, providing a systematic way to ascertain how many individuals should be included in the study based on the total population size and desired confidence level; ultimately, a total of 2,581 rural and nomadic women were randomly selected as samples, ensuring statistical significance for analysis. The multi-stage sampling method included several stages: first, the population was divided into clusters (e.g., different regions or communities), followed by a random selection of these clusters to ensure diversity, and finally, individuals were randomly selected from the chosen clusters to form the final sample. This approach enhanced the sample’s representativeness and facilitated logistical efficiency in data collection across diverse geographical areas. By following these systematic steps, the research ensured that it reached a broad and representative segment of rural and nomadic women in western Iran, thereby enhancing the validity and reliability of its findings. The selection criteria for participants in the study included women aged 18–70 years who were residents of rural and nomadic communities in Western Iran and were willing to provide informed consent for participation, along with availability for follow-up assessments if necessary. Exclusion criteria consisted of women with a known history of secondary hypertension (e.g., due to endocrine disorders), pregnant or breastfeeding women, individuals currently undergoing treatment for hypertension or other cardiovascular diseases, and those with significant cognitive impairments that could hinder participation or data collection. Hypertension was classified based on a standardized protocol for blood pressure measurement, which involved taking three readings at least 5 min apart, with the average used to determine hypertension status. According to the latest guidelines, hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (BP) ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 80 mmHg, or self-reported current use of antihypertensive medication. This human study was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki. All educated participants and the legal guardians of uneducated participants were asked to complete a written informed consent form at the beginning of the study. This study received approval from the Ministry of Sports and Youth of the Islamic Republic of Iran (IR. IMSY. 1400.217.20.1890/18.11.2021).

Initial measurements

First, the purpose of this study was explained to the participants. Then, a self-administered sociodemographic questionnaire including age, marital status, educational level, occupational status, smoking, and hospitalization in the last year was collected. Bodyweight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a weighing scale (Avery beam, Birmingham, England) with minimal clothing. Standing height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a stadiometer (SECA, Germany) without shoes. BMI was calculated as kg/m2 by the following equation: BMI = weight/Height2; BMI was categorized based on WHO criteria: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²)). After sitting on a chair for five minutes, the blood pressure was measured twice with a 10-minute gap using a mercury sphygmomanometer (Diplomat, Riester, Germany) and a stethoscope (4064, Riester, Germany) in a quiet and calm environment. Then, the mean of the two measurements was calculated and recorded [26]. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 80 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication.

Physical activity level

To determine the PAL Short Form of International Physical Activity Questionnaire (SF-IPAQ) was utilized in this study. IPAQ contains 7 questions classifying all types of physical activity lasting longer than 10 min within the last 7 days including leisure time, domestic and gardening (yard) activities, work-related, and transport-related activities [27, 28] into three categories: walking, moderate and vigorous [29]. The Walking category included all types of walking; the Moderate category included carrying light loads, cycling, swimming, volleyball, and all other activities that cause small increases in breathing and/or heart rate; the Vigorous category covers all physical activities that cause large increases in breathing and/or heart rates such as carrying or lifting heavy loads, digging, running, football or construction work. Frequency (days per week) and duration (time per day) were recorded. The Metabolic Equivalent (MET) was calculated in kcal/kg/hour for each activity. Eight METs were considered for vigorous activities, 4 METs for moderate, and 3.3 METs for walking. Then, the score for each activity was calculated by multiplying its METs *frequency (days per week) * duration (time per day in minutes). The total PAL was calculated by adding the Walking, Moderate, and Vigorous activities MET scores. PAL was categorized as low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–1500 MET-min/week), and high (> 1500 MET-min/week) based on IPAQ guidelines [27, 28]. The Persian SF-IPAQ has undergone rigorous testing for validity and reliability, making it suitable for use in research studies focusing on physical activity in Iranian populations.

Eating behavior

In this study, the 18-item Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ-R18) was used that measure 3 different aspects of eating behavior: uncontrolled eating (UE; intended to eat more than usual due to a loss of control over intake combined with subjective feelings of hunger, 9 questions), conscious restriction (CR; restriction of food intake to control body weight or to promote weight loss, 6 questions), and emotional eating (EE; inability to resist emotional cues, 3 questions). It has been validated that the instrument is valid in both obese and non-obese individuals [30]. The responses were coded on a 4-point scale (1 = true, 2 = mostly true, 3 = mostly false, 4 = false) with higher values indicating more of the behavior. The raw scale scores are standardized to a 0–100 scale using the following formula.

Standardized score = [(raw score-lowest possible raw score)/possible raw score range] × 100.

High or low scores on the TFEQ indicate varying levels of specific eating behaviors among individuals, measuring three primary factors: cognitive restraint, disinhibition, and perceived hunger. Higher scores in cognitive restraint suggest a greater ability to control food intake through conscious efforts, such as portion control and avoiding high-calorie foods, with individuals exhibiting lower average energy and fat intake. In contrast, elevated scores in disinhibition reflect a tendency to overeat in response to external cues or emotional triggers, indicating a lack of control over eating behaviors; this factor is associated with higher BMI and can predict weight regain after weight loss, as individuals may struggle with binge eating or consuming food opportunistically. Additionally, higher scores in perceived hunger indicate a stronger perception of hunger, which can lead to increased food consumption, making it challenging for individuals to manage their eating habits effectively. Overall, high scores in cognitive restraint are generally linked to healthier eating patterns and lower energy intake, while high scores in disinhibition and perceived hunger are associated with disordered eating behaviors and higher BMI. Understanding these scores can help inform interventions aimed at weight management and improving eating behaviors. Scores for Uncontrolled Eating, Emotional Eating, and Cognitive Restraint were standardized to a 0–100 scale. For analysis, high scores (above median) were considered indicative of higher behavioral tendency.

General health

To assess and identify health problems within the past two weeks we used a 28-item general health questionnaire (GHQ-28; including somatic symptoms, anxiety and insomnia, social dysfunction, and depression subscales, each with seven questions) which was developed and psychometrically evaluated by Goldberg and Homan, respectively. The responses were scored with 4 points of 0, 1, 2, and 3 thus the scores for each subscale could be between 0 and 21 and the total GHQ-28 score between 0 and 84 in which higher scores represent lower general health and any scores above 23 were considered as impaired general health [31]. The validity of the Persian version of GHQ-28 for assessing the general health of the Iranian population was reported by Taghavi previously [32]. GHQ-28 total score ranges from 0 to 84; a score > 23 was considered indicative of impaired general health.

Sleep quality

To assess the subjective nature of sleeping habits over 1 month the 19-item self-reported Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire was used. PSQI reflects seven aspects of subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbance, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction [33]. The responses were coded on a four-point scale ranging from 0 to 3 for each item. Then, a mean value of the answers to every seven aspects was calculated and the sum of mean values for seven aspects was considered as the global sleep quality score ranging from 0 to 21 with a lower score indicating better sleep quality. The global score of seven indicates the cutoff point between poor and good sleep quality [34]. The reliability of PSQI in diagnosing sleep disorders has been proven in many settings [34, 35]. The Persian GHQ-12 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency and validity, making it a valuable instrument for screening mental health issues in various populations, including young adults in Iran. This tool can help identify individuals who may benefit from further psychological assessment or intervention. Global PSQI score ranges from 0 to 21; a score > 5 indicates poor sleep quality.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 21; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with a significance level set at p < 0.05. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data distribution, which indicated that most variables were non-normally distributed. Therefore, non-parametric methods were applied for inferential analyses. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Comparisons of categorical variables across BMI categories were conducted using the Chi-square test, while differences in continuous variables were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. To evaluate the association between health-related behaviors (physical activity level, eating behavior, sleep quality, general health status) and BMI categories, multinomial logistic regression models were employed, with BMI categorized into four groups (underweight, normal weight, overweight, obese) based on WHO criteria. Normal weight was set as the reference category. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusting for potential confounders including age, educational level, marital status, and occupational status. For sensitivity analysis, a supplementary multinomial logistic regression model was fitted with hypertension (yes/no) as the dependent variable and BMI categories as the main predictor, to assess the risk of hypertension across weight groups. This model is presented in the Supplementary Materials. Although a multi-stage cluster sampling method was used to select participants from diverse rural and nomadic communities, the statistical analyses did not apply cluster-specific weighting or design effects (e.g., robust standard errors) due to the lack of detailed cluster-level variance data and limitations in the application of complex survey modeling within non-parametric and multinomial regression frameworks in SPSS. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the study, which may influence the precision of estimates and their generalizability to the broader population. All reported p-values are two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of anthropometric and physiological variables among 2,581 rural and nomadic women aged 18–70 years in western Iran. Data were collected during a cross-sectional study conducted from August to December 2020 using a multi-stage cluster sampling method. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The mean age of participants was 50.17 ± 13.49 years, reflecting a population predominantly composed of middle-aged and older adults. The average body mass index (BMI) was 28.29 ± 5.42 kg/m², indicating a high prevalence of overweight and obesity. Body fat percentage (BFP) averaged 30.14%, with a waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) of 0.98 ± 0.05, suggesting a high risk of central obesity and associated metabolic complications. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was 129.24 ± 3.00 mmHg, exceeding the threshold for elevated blood pressure (≥ 130 mmHg), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was 85.15 ± 3.18 mmHg, indicating a significant burden of prehypertension and hypertension. The physical activity level (PAL), measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), averaged 556.12 MET-min/week, falling below the threshold for moderate activity (≥ 600 MET-min/week), indicating a predominantly sedentary lifestyle. General health status, assessed via the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-28), yielded a mean score of 44.49 ± 6.33, well above the cutoff of 23 for impaired mental health. Sleep quality, evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), showed a mean global score of 12.55 ± 0.42, exceeding the cutoff of 5, indicating poor sleep quality across the population. These findings collectively highlight a high burden of cardiometabolic risk factors, poor mental health, and inadequate physical activity in this population.

As shown in Table 3, the prevalence of key health-related behaviors and outcomes varies across BMI categories. Physical activity level (PAL), categorized as low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–1500), and high (> 1500), showed a strong inverse gradient with BMI: 93.54% of obese and 72.74% of overweight women had low PAL, whereas 45.74% of normal-weight women engaged in high PAL. Hypertension, defined as SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, DBP ≥ 80 mmHg, or antihypertensive medication use, affected 56.59% of obese and 33.98% of overweight women, compared to only 2.71% of normal-weight individuals, confirming a robust association between adiposity and elevated blood pressure. Eating behavior, assessed via the Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ-R18), revealed that 71.15% of obese and 61.11% of overweight women were classified as uncontrolled eaters, while emotional eating was reported by 22.65% of obese and 26.62% of overweight women. In contrast, 70.74% of normal-weight women exhibited conscious restraint, indicating better self-regulation of food intake. General health status (GHQ-28 > 23) was impaired in 84.2% of obese and 82.7% of overweight women, compared to 74.4% of normal-weight women. Sleep quality (PSQI > 5) was poor in 95.0% of obese and 96.1% of overweight women, with significantly higher scores in all PSQI domains (e.g., sleep latency, disturbance, daytime dysfunction) compared to normal-weight individuals.

The results of the multinomial logistic regression analyses, which examined the association between health-related behaviors and BMI categories using normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²) as the reference group, are presented in Table 4. The model estimated the odds of being overweight or obese as a function of physical activity level (PAL), hypertension, eating behavior, general health status, and sleep quality, adjusted for age, education, marital status, and occupation. All results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Low PAL (< 600 MET-min/week) was strongly associated with increased odds of obesity (OR = 4.32, 95% CI: 3.41–5.48, p < 0.001) and overweight (OR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.68–2.73, p < 0.001), indicating that sedentary behavior is a major risk factor for elevated BMI. Hypertension was significantly associated with both overweight (OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.32–2.40, p = 0.001) and obesity (OR = 3.21, 95% CI: 2.54–4.06, p < 0.001), reinforcing the bidirectional relationship between obesity and cardiovascular risk. Poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) was associated with increased odds of overweight (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.22–2.23, p = 0.002) and obesity (OR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.88–3.24, p < 0.001), consistent with evidence linking sleep disruption to metabolic dysregulation. Impaired general health (GHQ-28 > 23) was also significantly associated with higher BMI categories: overweight (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.41–2.53, p < 0.001) and obese (OR = 2.95, 95% CI: 2.23–3.90, p < 0.001), suggesting a psychosomatic component to weight gain. Emotional eating was positively associated with both overweight (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.08–1.94, p = 0.013) and obesity (OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.52–2.66, p < 0.001), highlighting the role of psychological factors in disordered eating. Notably, no significant association was found between PAL and eating behavior in the regression model, suggesting that physical activity and dietary habits may be independently regulated in this population. These findings support the need for integrated interventions targeting physical activity, sleep, mental health, and emotional regulation to combat obesity in rural and nomadic women.

Discussion

The objective of the current study is to examine the associations between obesity and various factors, including physical activity, eating behaviors, sleep quality, general health, and the incidence of hypertension among women in rural and nomadic populations of Western Iran. This investigation aims to elucidate how these interconnected variables contribute to obesity and hypertension, thereby enhancing understanding of health challenges faced by this demographic. We determined age, marital status, educational level, occupational status, smoking, and hospitalization in the last year based on the different BMI categories in western Iranian rural and nomadic women. Our findings indicate that more than 74% of the study population were obese and overweight, 66.95% had low PAL, and 36.30% have been identified with hypertension which reflects a significant national women’s health problem, especially in the rural and nomadic women of western Iran. The rates of obesity and overweight are comparable to previously investigated studies among the Spanish (17%) [36], and Turkish (44%) [37] populations. However, the prevalence in the present study is still higher. Previous studies also addressed obesity as an important and growing health problem in Rio de Janeiro (39.2%) [38], Egypt (24.1%) [39], Australia (30.5%) [40], Oman (64.4%) [41]; and Iran has been no exception [39, 42]. Based on our findings, the older the women the higher the risk of overweight/obesity which might be due to decreased PAL [43], lower metabolic rate, hormonal disorders related to menopausal, relatively decreased height than younger ages, and unreduced gestational weight gain. Similar to the results of previous studies, overweight/obesity was linked with education; participants with lower education levels showed a higher rate of overweight/obesity that could be explained due to low nutritional knowledge and PALs [44, 45].

The high prevalence of obesity and hypertension among rural and nomadic women in Western Iran can be attributed to several underlying causes that significantly influence this public health issue [41]. Socioeconomic factors play a crucial role, as lower educational levels are associated with higher rates of overweight and obesity, largely due to limited access to information about healthy eating and physical activity, resulting in poor dietary choices and sedentary lifestyles [44, 45]. Economic constraints further restrict access to healthier food options, such as fresh fruits and vegetables, which are often more expensive than calorie-dense processed foods [39]. Cultural influences also shape health behaviors; traditional diets high in carbohydrates and fats contribute to weight gain, while cultural perceptions of body image may favor larger sizes, discouraging weight management efforts [39, 42]. Additionally, there may be less emphasis on physical activity for women, particularly as they age or after marriage, leading to decreased physical activity levels and increased obesity risk. Environmental factors, such as limited access to healthcare services and a scarcity of healthy food options in rural areas dominated by convenience stores, exacerbate these issues. Physiological changes associated with aging, including a natural decline in metabolic rate and hormonal imbalances during menopause, further increase the risk of obesity and hypertension [44, 45]. Psychological factors also play a role; women with lower educational attainment may experience higher stress levels due to economic instability or social pressures, leading to emotional eating or sedentary behaviors as coping mechanisms [43]. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions, such as community-based education programs focused on nutrition and physical activity, as well as improved access to healthcare services and healthy food options in rural areas [37]. Addressing the high rates of obesity and hypertension among this vulnerable population requires a multifaceted approach that considers these socioeconomic, cultural, environmental, physiological, and psychological factors to effectively tailor public health initiatives [46, 47].

In line with other studies, there was a significant difference in the incidence of obesity and overweight between single and married participants in this study; married participants were more prone to obesity and overweight [48, 49]. Although the overall rate of smoking (12.94%) in Iranian women was less than in other countries [46, 47], obese women seem to have higher rates (66.77%) of smoking which could again be a mechanism leading to obesity via altering appetite, eating behavior, and basal metabolic rates (BMR) [50]. In line with the results of Acevedo et al. (2020), the risk of obesity was higher in unemployed women; only 17.70% of the employed while 49.84% of unemployed participants were obese [51]. As shown previously by Moser et al. (2019) [52] and Hamer et al. (2020) [53], this study also showed that obese participants had higher rates of hospitalization in the last year than other BMI classes. Also, as stated by Tozo et al. (2020) the incidence of hypertension in the present study was more common in obese and overweight participants (70.12% and 25.19%), respectively [54]. Additionally, the current study investigated the PAL, eating behavior, the incidence of hypertension, general health, and sleep quality based on the distribution of BMI. The results revealed that women with higher BMI had lower PALs and showed poorer eating behavior, sleep quality, general health, and a higher incidence of hypertension. This study also indicates significant relationships between different PAL with BMI, hypertension, eating behavior, general health, and sleep quality showing high and moderate PAL to have relationships with better status, while low PAL was significantly associated with worst status in all variables.

Our multivariate regression analysis investigated the association between physical activity and obesity, hypertension, eating behavior, general health status, and sleep quality. We found that physical activity was significantly associated with obesity and hypertension, general health status, or sleep quality, but not with eating behavior. The regression analysis showed a significant association between physical activity and obesity. This indicates that individuals with higher levels of physical activity are less likely to be obese than those with lower levels of physical activity. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that physical activity is a significant predictor of obesity. Similarly, we found a significant association between low physical activity and hypertension, general health status, or sleep quality. This suggests that higher levels of physical activity are associated with a lower risk of hypertension. This finding is also consistent with prior research showing that physical activity can help improve blood pressure control. Contrary to expectations, our analysis did not find a significant association between physical activity and eating behavior. These results indicate that physical activity may not be as strongly associated with eating behavior as previously thought. Our findings suggest that physical activity plays an essential role in reducing the risk of obesity and hypertension. However, further research is necessary to determine if physical activity directly affects eating behavior, general health status, and sleep quality, or if other compounding factors contribute to these results.

Strength and limitation

The research was conducted exclusively among rural and nomadic women in Western Iran, which may be considered a limitation. However, this study represents one of the initial comprehensive investigations in the country that utilized recognized and validated measures to assess PAL, eating habits, sleep quality, general health, and the incidence of hypertension across all age groups based on BMI in a large sample. Another limitation of this study is that the broad age range of participants may influence the applicability of physical activity recommendations and METs, as these guidelines can vary significantly by age group. Future research should consider stratifying analyses by age to gain a better understanding of these variations. Additionally, the study lacked data on parenting responsibilities, which could further inform the findings.

Conclusion

Our multivariate regression analysis found a significant association between physical activity obesity and hypertension. However, we did not find a significant link between physical activity and eating behavior. Overall, lower PAL, eating behavior, sleep quality, general health, and higher incidence of hypertension among obese women in rural and nomadic areas of western Iran reinforces the implementation of health education programs about obesity and risk factors and encourages women to be more physically active. These findings underline the importance of physical activity in improving overall health and highlight the need for further research to better understand the relationship between physical activity and other health factors.
