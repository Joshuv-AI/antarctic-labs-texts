Methodological issue in a dental neuromelioidosis outbreak investigation—authors’ reply
Authors: Angel Miraclin Thirugnanakumar, Prabu Rajkumar, Karthik Gunasekaran, Manickam Ponnaiah, Girish Kumar Chethrapilly Purushothaman, Balaji Veeraraghavan, Manoj Murhekar
Published: 2025

We thank Behera and Mohapatra for their response1 to our publication.2 The points raised reflect the inherent complexities of outbreak investigations rather than methodological limitations.

The low CSF culture yield in neuromelioidosis as reported previously might reflect interplay of host, agent and treatment-related factors. Since brain biopsies are rarely feasible, the pathognomonic “tunnel sign” on neuroimaging remains a key diagnostic clue.2

The occurrence of cases following invasive procedures at a particular dental clinic suggests common-source contamination. Although other environmental samples were negative, likely due to disinfection and clinic closure, the positive in-use saline sample suggested a possible epidemiological linkage. Whole-genome sequencing of two clinical and one environmental (saline) isolates showed clonality among them and with the dominant regional strain. This finding aligns with epidemiological, clinical, laboratory, and imaging data. To preserve clarity in strain-phenotype correlation, genome sequences without clinical metadata were excluded from the analysis. While the role of bimABm in neurotropism warrants further study,3 its presence in all outbreak isolates is noteworthy. Absence of bacteraemia in most cases and characteristic radiological patterns support direct neurotropic spread, consistent with existing literature.4,5 Higher fatality among dental cases likely reflects advanced neurological involvement, rather than treatment factors alone.

Furthermore, we also wish to clarify that the findings are intended to strengthen infection prevention practices, and not to cast doubt on the safety of dental care services in melioidosis-endemic, low-resource settings. We hope this helps prevent any misconceptions that could inadvertently contribute to public apprehension towards seeking dental care.
