Surgical treatment of rheumatoid arthritis of the elbow
Authors: Wan Kee Hong, Joong Mo Ahn, Hyun Sik Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory condition that significantly impacts multiple joints, including the elbow, crucial for daily activities. Despite advancements in medical treatments like disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs that delay disease progression, surgical interventions are often necessary to improve the quality of life in RA patients. This review provides a comprehensive evaluation of the elbow, focusing on history taking, physical examination, and radiographic assessments, particularly magnetic resonance imaging findings. We detail the selection and counseling of patients, as well as various surgical options tailored to specific symptoms of RA elbow, including synovectomy for persistent pain due to synovitis, capsulotomy and osteophytectomy for limited range of motion, cubital tunnel release for ulnar neuropathy, and joint replacement or interposition arthroplasty for advanced joint destruction. Additionally, we emphasize the differentiation between RA flare and septic elbow, highlighting the importance of timely and accurate diagnosis. The review underscores the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach, integrating surgical and medical strategies to manage RA elbow effectively.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Elbow, Surgery, Synovectomy, Arthroplasty

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory condition that predominantly affects polyarticular synovial tissues, leading to progressive destruction of joint cartilage [1]. Advances in understanding the pathophysiology of RA have facilitated early detection and intervention, significantly impacting treatment approaches. Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) have notably delayed disease progression and reduced the frequency of surgical interventions [2]. Indeed, studies have reported both a decrease in the number of arthroplasty surgeries among RA patients and an increase in the average age at which these surgeries are performed [3,4].

Elbow involvement in RA escalates, affecting 32% to 61% of patients as the disease progresses more than 10 years [5,6]. Arthritis of the elbow presents unique clinical challenges. The elbow plays a crucial role in positioning the hand in space for daily activities. Therefore, arthritis of the elbow can substantially impact the quality of life, making effective management essential. In clinical practice, evaluating patients with elbow pain requires comprehensive history taking, precise physical examinations, and adequate imaging studies to accurately diagnose and guide treatment decisions.

Common symptoms of RA of the elbow include pain, stiffness, swelling, or instability in the elbow. The inflammatory process is often initiated in the synovium, causing irritative synovitis and discomfort of the elbow. As synovitis progresses, RA can also destroy joint cartilage and eventually affect the underlying subchondral bone. Furthermore, RA may compromise the medial and lateral collateral ligaments, contributing to elbow instability [7].

Given the progressive nature of RA, it is crucial for practitioners to determine the appropriate timing for surgical interventions. Surgical options can be critical for various stages of disease progression, aiding significantly in managing symptoms and preventing further joint deterioration. Understanding when to implement these interventions is essential for optimizing patient outcomes.

MAIN SUBJECTS

Patient evaluation

1) History taking

Evaluation of a patient suspected of having elbow arthritis begins with a detailed history taking focused on the chief complaint. It’s important to inquire about any previous infections, trauma, or surgeries that could affect treatment options. If pain is the primary concern, its exact location, intensity, duration, and any time of day when it worsens, as well as the range of motion (ROM) are evaluated. Pain at the terminal end of ROM may suggest impingement pain caused by osteophytes, whereas continuous pain throughout the ROM could indicate severe arthritic pain. Severe resting pain might suggest an infectious cause. For complaints of joint stiffness, whether it persists all day or is worse in the morning is determined; the latter may indicate inflammatory arthritis. Symptoms of catching or locking could suggest the presence of loose bodies. If instability is the main complaint, identifying positions or movements that exacerbate the symptoms can help infer which structures are injured. Additionally, numbness in the ulnar aspect of the hand prompts consideration of ulnar nerve entrapment in the elbow’s cubital tunnel. The history should also include an assessment of the patient’s current activity level and functional demands to set treatment goals.

2) Physical examination

The elbow is inspected for normal alignment, comparing it to the unaffected side, and checked for the maintenance of a normal valgus carrying angle (5 to 15 degrees). Joint swelling, erythema, ecchymosis, scars, or surgical incisions are observed. Palpation should assess for heat, swelling, and pinpoint tenderness. Using a goniometer, the elbow’s ROM is measured; a functional arc necessary for daily activities includes 30 to 130 degrees of flexion and extension and 100 degrees of pronosupination. If ROM is restricted, whether the endpoint is hard or soft is assessed, which can indicate impingement by osteophytes or limitation by soft tissue contracture or effusion, respectively. Also, crepitus or mechanical symptoms such as locking or catching are assessed, and any angle at which pain occurs is noted. The elbow’s varus, valgus, and axial stability is assessed to evaluate the integrity of the lateral or medial collateral ligament, and interosseous membrane. Progression of arthritis might lead to entrapment of the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel, so testing for Tinel’s sign or symptoms provoked by the elbow flexion test is also important.

3) Radiographic evaluation

Conventional radiograph views such as anteroposterior, lateral, and oblique are required to evaluate the overall alignment, joint congruency, and deformities in the elbow. Radiographs help in identifying common arthritic features like osteophytes and subchondral sclerosis, as well as characteristics specific to RA [8]. For radiological classification, Larsen grading system (Table 1) is commonly used for RA [8,9].

Early stages of RA may show joint space widening due to synovial thickening and joint effusion [10], whereas advanced cases often display joint space narrowing or fusion. Periarticular erosion, a hallmark of RA, tends to increase with the duration of symptoms [8]. The use of DMARDs has been observed to potentially reverse erosion in some cases of RA [11,12]. Osteopenia around the joint, indicative of synovitis and resulting hyperemia, may worsen in advanced stages due to pain-related disuse and can be exacerbated by steroid administration. It is also important to assess whether arthritic changes are eccentric or overall, involving the radiocapitellar or ulnohumeral joints, as RA typically presents with symmetric joint space narrowing and periarticular erosion—distinct from degenerative or post-traumatic arthritis [8].

While computed tomography (CT) scans are less frequently used for diagnosing RA due to their limitations in soft tissue visualization, they are invaluable for assessing the cortical bone structure. In advanced stages, CT can evaluate the extent of bony ankylosis, osteophytes, and loose bodies, and provide a 3-dimensional (3D) evaluation for surgical planning.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may offer better sensitivity to bone marrow edema as well as soft tissue alteration [13]. It is pivotal for differentiating inflammatory arthritis, and assessing the progression in established or definitive RA cases. It allows for detailed visualization of synovitis, extent and severity of inflammation, and the condition of bony structures. Synovitis appears as thickened synovium with a bright signal on T2-weighted images, indicating edema and increased synovial volume, enhanced further by intra-venous contrast on post-contrast images (Figure 1). Contrast-enhanced MRI can distinguish between synovitis and joint effusion by showing early contrast enhancement of the inflamed synovium up to 5 minutes after contrast injection. In later phases, around 10 minutes post-contrast injection, gadolinium diffuses into the synovial fluid, making differentiation more challenging [14]. Synovitis in early RA is likely more peripheral portion of the involved joint in distribution, whereas the later pannus tends to extend into the entire articular surfaces. Hypervascular pannus in early RA usually shows hyper-intensity signal on the fluid-sensitive sequences and marked contrast enhancement on the post-contrast images. In contrast, fibrous pannus in longstanding RA may demonstrate hypo-intensity on the fluid sensitive sequences with less avid contrast enhancement (Figure 2) [15]. MRI is also adept at identifying bone marrow edema in the subchondral bone, visible as a bright signal in fat-suppressed T2W/STIR (T2-weighted short-tau inversion recovery) images [16], and is recommended for predicting subsequent bone damage in RA [17].

4) Clinical or surgical classification

Beyond the radiological classification previously mentioned, Dott et al. [18] proposed a surgical classification for RA. This classification specifies types of RA based on joint morphology according to CT scan or MRI, and the presence of rheumatoid nodules. It categorizes RA by examining the changes in joint structure and the presence of nodular formations which can be identified through these imaging modalities [18].

(1) Classic Soft Tissue Type: Characterized by increased joint laxity, subluxation, malalignment, and instability.

(2) Osteoarthritic Type: Noted for joint stiffness and knobbly hypertrophic joints, yet with preserved alignment.

(3) Nodular Type: Defined by the presence of subcutaneous nodules and tendon involvement, with the joints themselves relatively spared.

(4) Mutilans Type: Marked by significant bone loss and joint destruction, leading to floppy joints and limb shortening.

Surgical options should be tailored to the phenotypical characteristics of each type of RA. For instance, excision of the radial head may be appropriate in the osteoarthritic type, whereas it should be preserved in the soft tissue type. Arthroscopic removal of loose bodies can be considered for nodular types. For mutilans types, joint reconstruction should be contemplated due to the extensive joint destruction and deformity associated with this subtype.

Surgical interventions depending on the chief complaint

The treatment of symptomatic RA of the elbow begins with non-surgical methods, which aim to alleviate pain and improve the mobility of the joint. Lifestyle modifications, such as avoiding stressful activities on the elbow, should be the initial approach. Anti-inflammatory medications are often the treatment of choice for patients in the early stages. Steroid injections into the elbow joint have shown variable effects on RA. The administration of DMARDs should also be considered in this patient population [2].

Surgical treatment becomes necessary when non-surgical treatments fail. Given that RA is a progressive disease, employing appropriate surgical interventions can significantly impact the rate of disease progression and improve the quality of life. The choice of surgery should be based on the patient’s chief complaint, as these can vary despite similar radiologic appearances. For patients with multiple complaints, a combination of the surgeries to be mentioned can be considered. However, since surgery is not a definitive treatment for this disease, both the timing and method of surgical intervention must be carefully considered. It is crucial for practitioners to thoroughly understand the patient’s chief complaints and the limitations of possible surgical outcomes.

1) Pain due to synovitis

When a patient reports pain and swelling of the elbow, and MRI imaging shows that both the bony structure and cartilage are relatively intact but synovitis is present, these symptoms can predominantly be attributed to the synovitis. Synovectomy, the surgical removal of inflamed synovium of joints, tendons, and their sheaths, is the primary surgical option for effectively addressing synovitis. The primary objectives of synovectomy are to alleviate pain, reduce swelling, and potentially slow the progression of joint damage. Arthroscopic synovectomy has been shown to have good long-term outcome in both early (Larsen grades 1 and 2) [19] and advanced stage (Larsen grades 3 and 4) [20]. Although arthroscopic synovectomy has been replacing conventional open synovectomy [21], the open procedure should still be considered in specific cases. These include situations involving concomitant ulnar nerve entrapment or when hypertrophic RA presents with large or inaccessible osteophytes.

The most critical differential diagnosis is septic arthritis of the elbow. Septic arthritis, particularly in cases of monoarticular involvement, has a reported mortality rate of 11%. Without prompt diagnosis and surgical treatment, it can lead to irreversible joint damage, making it a rheumatologic or orthopedic emergency [22]. Both RA flare and septic elbow can present with pain, swelling, and warmth during ROM, making differentiation challenging. It is important to perform joint fluid aspiration before starting empirical antibiotics to facilitate differential diagnosis and pathogen identification. Once septic elbow is diagnosed, treatment involves arthroscopic or open irrigation and debridement of the elbow joint to remove infected tissue thoroughly, followed by an appropriate administration of antibiotics.

Surgical patient selection and counseling: Patients suitable for synovectomy typically exhibit resistant synovitis despite optimized medical therapy, including DMARDs and biologic agents. It is important to select patients who have clear evidence of synovial inflammation with minimal joint destruction, as the benefits of synovectomy diminish in the presence of advanced structural damage. Counseling should focus on the potential benefits of symptom relief and the importance of ongoing medical management post-surgery to maintain the health of the joint.

2) Decreased range of motion

Decreased ROM in RA patients is distinct from the transient joint stiffness often associated with the disease, which typically resolves over time. In the elbow, ROM is evaluated in two planes: flexion-extension and supination-pronation. Comparing the affected elbow with the unaffected side is a useful method to estimate impaired ROM; however, this approach is often challenging in RA patients who may experience bilateral joint involvement. A functional arc of 120 degrees is necessary for performing most activities of daily living. An extension block greater than 30 degrees or a flexion block less than 130 degrees is considered abnormal. Restricted movement of the elbow in RA can result from various causes, necessitating a differential diagnosis that includes loose bodies, fractures, triceps strain, and osteophytes. Prolonged joint inflammation leading to soft tissue fibrosis is a common reason. Additionally, osteophytes at the olecranon tip can hinder the elbow’s ability to extend, while those at the coronoid process may restrict flexion. The presence of loose bodies within the joint can further mechanically obstruct elbow motion. Destruction of joint congruency can also contribute to decreased mobility.

Surgical options to address these issues include capsulotomy (capsular release), osteophytectomy (osteophyte excision), loose body removal, and radial head excision, which can be performed arthroscopically or through open surgery. Capsulotomy is intended to relieve tight structures around the elbow, allowing for improved ROM. Anterior capsulotomy increases extension and posterior capsulotomy increases flexion of the elbow. In cases where mechanical blockage is present caused by osteophytes or loose bodies, osteophyte or loose body removal should be considered, which can be done open or arthroscopically. With the aid of 3D CT scans, surgeons can geometrically analyze which osteophytes or structures are limiting the ROM and perform targeted debridement. Removing osteophytes from the coronoid process or coronoid fossa can aid in improving terminal flexion, while excising the tip of the olecranon process or widening and deepening the olecranon fossa can facilitate recovery of terminal extension. In cases of severe radiocapitellar or proximal radioulnar arthritis, or subluxation of the radial head obstructing ROM, radial head excision can be considered, even though this can cause postoperative valgus instability and subsequent ulnohumeral arthritis [23]. In cases where joint contracture and extensive joint damage is present, more invasive procedures such as total elbow arthroplasty might be necessary to restore function.

Surgical patient selection and counseling: Candidates for surgical intervention to improve motion are typically those who have not achieved satisfactory improvement with physical therapy and medical management. These patients often experience significant functional impairment due to their limited ROM. During counseling, it is important to discuss the potential improvements in motion post-surgery which are typically 10 to 15 degrees more of flexion or extension, while also highlighting the importance of postoperative rehabilitation to maximize the benefits of the surgery and the progressive tendency of the disease that can cause the recurrence of the stiffness.

3) Ulnar neuropathy around the elbow

Cubital tunnel syndrome, or ulnar entrapment around the elbow, is the second most common peripheral nerve entrapment syndrome [24]. Elbow arthritis is one of the causes of cubital tunnel syndrome, and cubital tunnel syndrome associated with RA was first reported by Deandrade and Casagrande [25]. Since then, it has been recognized as a common pathology in RA of the elbow [26]. Patients may present with a range of symptoms from tingling sensations in the fourth and fifth fingers to numbness and intrinsic muscle atrophy. Recently, there have been reports of attritional rupture of the ulnar nerve associated with RA elbows [27]. Unlike cubital tunnel syndrome without arthritis, which results from entrapment at anatomical structures such as the arcade of Struthers, Osborne’s fascia, or the fascia of the flexor carpi ulnaris, cubital tunnel syndrome in RA elbows often arises due to severe synovitis or joint destruction causing mechanical compression of the ulnar nerve. This can lead to a more rapid progression of the disease. As the condition advances to axonotmesis, the prognosis after nerve decompression worsens, making early detection and intervention crucial.

In surgical management, in-situ decompression alone may be insufficient. Instead, combining medial epicondylectomy or excision of protruding osteophytes with anterior transposition of the ulnar nerve is often more effective. This approach addresses both the nerve entrapment and any contributing bony abnormalities, potentially improving patient outcomes.

Surgical patient selection and counseling: When RA patients with elbow involvement report tingling sensations in the fourth and fifth fingers, it is essential to monitor their symptoms closely rather than masking them with neuropathic painkillers like gabapentin. Limiting elbow flexion and using DMARDs to control underlying RA inflammation are recommended initial steps. Should the pain intensify or advanced neurological symptoms such as numbness and weakness develop, surgical intervention should be considered promptly. 4) Advanced Joint destruction

Beyond medical treatments such as DMARDs, if patients do not experience satisfactory pain relief or improvement in limited ROM after attempts at debridement and synovectomy, further surgical options should be considered, including total elbow replacement arthroplasty (TEA) or interposition arthroplasty. These interventions aim to alleviate pain, improve mobility, and enhance the quality of life. Similar to other joint replacement surgeries, total elbow replacement arthroplasty consists of bony resection of the joint surfaces and the insertion of a motion-preserving implant. Most current TEA systems utilize a semi-constrained prosthesis designed by Coonrad and Morrey, where the ulnar and humeral components are interconnected, permitting 5 to 10 degrees of varus-valgus and internal-external rotational laxity [28]. Patients who undergo TEA should avoid lifting weights over 5 kilograms with the operated arm, which restricts the indications for surgery based on the patient’s necessary activity levels. Recent systematic reviews have shown that the survival rate of total elbow implants is 79.2% after an average follow-up of 11.1 years [29]. Other studies have observed survival rates of 92%, 81%, 71%, and 61% at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years, respectively, which are similar to or slightly inferior to outcomes reported for total knee or hip arthroplasties in RA patients [30]. Possible complications include infection, aseptic loosening, instability, periprosthetic fracture, triceps weakening, and ulnar neuropathy, all of which can shorten the lifespan of the implant. Thus, middle-aged patients might require revision TEA within 10 years, and considerations for potential reoperations are essential when planning joint replacement surgery. According to one systematic review, the rates of complications were found to be 6.9% for loosening, 3.4% for deep infection, 2.9% for ulnar nerve issues, and 0.4% for triceps complications [29]. Therefore, for older patients with limited activity, TEA can be an effective treatment option to reduce pain and restore functional ROM.

Treating younger and more active patients is significantly challenging, taking into account their longer lifespan. Interposition arthroplasty can be a viable treatment option. This technique involves inserting a biological membrane, such as anconeus, fascia lata, triceps autografts, or Achilles tendon allografts, instead of a prosthetic implant into the destructed joint [31]. Although interposition arthroplasty allows for heavier weight bearing post-surgery compared to total elbow arthroplasty, it does not completely alleviate pain. Larson and Morrey [32] reported improvements in patients, with the ROM increasing from 51 degrees pre-surgery to 97 degrees post-surgery, and the Mayo elbow performance score rising from 41 to 65. Converting to total elbow arthroplasty after interposition arthroplasty is known to be successful [33].

Elbow arthrodesis is the final destination of the elbow arthritis. This surgical procedure, which fuses the elbow joint, is considered when all other treatment options have failed to provide relief or when the joint is irreversibly damaged. However, it significantly restricts the arm’s movement, limiting the elbow to a fixed position. This loss of motion can impact daily activities, making it essential to carefully evaluate the patient’s lifestyle and needs before proceeding.

Surgical patient selection and counseling: This surgical approach (TEA, interposition, arthrodesis) is considered for patients with advanced joint destruction who have not responded adequately to conservative treatment strategies. Selection should consider the patient’s functional demands, age, and overall health status. Counseling should thoroughly address the potential risks and benefits of surgery, including the possibility of limited ROM and the need for future revisions.

CONCLUSION

The importance of medical treatment in managing RA continues to grow, with advancements in DMARDs significantly improving patient outcomes. However, appropriate surgical interventions remain crucial in enhancing the quality of life for RA patients, even in the relatively early phases of the disease. Surgical options such as synovectomy, joint debridement, ulnar nerve neurolysis and TEA can effectively alleviate pain, improve function, and prevent further joint deterioration when medical treatments alone are insufficient.

Given the complex and multifaceted nature of RA, a multidisciplinary approach is essential in the management of elbow involvement. Collaboration between rheumatologists, orthopedic surgeons, physical therapists, and other healthcare professionals ensures comprehensive care, addressing both medical and surgical needs. This integrated strategy not only optimizes treatment outcomes but also supports the overall well-being of patients, helping them maintain their functional abilities and improve their quality of life.
