The ASP System is Running, But It Needs People, Resources, and Time
Authors: Tae Hyong Kim
Published: 2026

This editorial expands upon discussions presented at the 2026 Joint Spring Conference of the Korean Society for Antimicrobial Therapy and the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases.

Antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASP) have emerged as one of the most effective and evidence-based strategy currently available to address antimicrobial resistance (AMR), one of the greatest threats to modern medicine. In Korea, the launch of the national ASP pilot program in late 2024 marked a historic milestone as the first government-funded stewardship initiative with standardized operational requirements [1]. This achievement reflects decades of academic discussion and growing recognition of the need for responsible antimicrobial use. Historically, ASP in Korea has evolved from a regulatory and restriction-based approach to one that seeks to optimize antimicrobial therapy in a sustainable manner. The success of this transition, however, will ultimately depend on whether sufficient personnel, resources, and time can be secured.

Even before the advent of antimicrobial stewardship in Korea, efforts to regulate antimicrobial use had already emerged from the recognition that antimicrobial agents are limited healthcare resources that must be preserved [2]. A notable milestone occurred in 1998, when antimicrobial stewardship concepts were first introduced into the Korean medical literature through discussions of the 1997 Infectious Disease Society of America guidelines on antimicrobial resistance and the need for organized antimicrobial management committees [3]. Public concern regarding antibiotic overuse became particularly prominent during the late 1990s and early 2000s [4].

In parallel with the implementation of the national separation of prescribing and dispensing system in 2000, various policy measures, including prescription monitoring and public reporting of antibiotic utilization indicators, were introduced to reduce inappropriate prescribing [5]. These initiatives increased awareness of antimicrobial misuse and contributed to reductions in antibiotic use in selected clinical settings. However, they were largely government-driven regulatory measures and were often criticized for placing greater emphasis on cost containment than on the optimization of antimicrobial therapy [56].

From 2011 to 2015, academic societies began developing national clinical practice guidelines to promote standardized treatment approaches. As a result, antimicrobial prescribing could gradually be evaluated not only by the quantity of antibiotics used but also by adherence to clinical guidelines and local epidemiology. The Korean Society for Antimicrobial Therapy (KSAT) played a central role in introducing stewardship concepts, developing treatment guidelines, providing professional education, and fostering multidisciplinary discussions on appropriate antimicrobial use. These activities helped create the intellectual and professional foundation upon which the national ASP program was later built.

While academic societies were developing clinical practice guidelines and promoting evidence-based antimicrobial use, the government began establishing a national policy framework following the WHO Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance adopted in 2015. This led to the implementation of successive National Action Plans on AMR and ultimately the launch of the national ASP pilot program in 2024 through close collaboration with the KSAT and the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases. Built upon this framework, the pilot program introduced standardized stewardship structures based on the CDC Core Elements, multidisciplinary stewardship teams, monitoring systems, and educational activities [78].

Most importantly, for the first time, public funding was specifically allocated to support ASP implementation at the institutional level. This approach differs from the financing model used for infection prevention and control programs in Korea, which are supported through reimbursement-based incentives, as the ASP pilot program relies on direct governmental funding. The rapid participation of hospitals and establishment of stewardship infrastructure demonstrate that healthcare institutions were ready for this transition.

The future of ASP in Korea presents both opportunities and challenges. Through successive national AMR policies and the ASP pilot program, the direction of stewardship development in Korea has become increasingly clear, and standardized stewardship frameworks have now been established through considerable effort. Public funding has encouraged institutional participation, and successful model hospitals are gradually emerging across different regions of the country. Multidisciplinary collaboration among infectious diseases physicians, pharmacists, microbiologists, infection prevention specialists, and data experts is becoming increasingly common. These developments offer hope that ASP will evolve beyond a healthcare policy initiative and contribute to the development of a new culture of patient care.

The pessimistic perspective, however, highlights workforce shortages, administrative burden, institutional disparities, and uncertain long-term funding. These concerns are further compounded by the continuing difficulty of recruiting and retaining infectious diseases specialists, as the field is often perceived as demanding, underrecognized, and inadequately compensated. Korea continues to face shortages of both infectious diseases physicians and trained stewardship pharmacists. Smaller hospitals often lack sufficient expertise and infrastructure to meet stewardship expectations. There is also concern that stewardship activities could become administrative exercises focused on reporting metrics rather than meaningful interventions that improve patient care.

Ultimately, the success of ASP depends on people. A stewardship program may exist on paper without changing clinical practice. Software systems can generate alerts and reports, but meaningful stewardship requires skilled professionals who can influence prescribing decisions. Sustaining such a workforce requires protected time, professional recognition, training opportunities, and viable career pathways. Without these supports, stewardship may remain an administrative requirement rather than becoming an integral part of clinical practice.

The future of ASP will therefore depend not only on maintaining the programs, but also on transforming them from systems primarily focused on restriction and authorization into activities that support better antimicrobial decision-making [910]. In this sense, stewardship is evolving from controlling antimicrobial use to promoting optimal antimicrobial therapy.

AMR is not confined to large academic centers. Community hospitals, long-term care facilities, rehabilitation centers, and smaller regional institutions all contribute to antimicrobial use and resistance patterns. In particular, prolonged antimicrobial exposure in long-term care and chronic care settings presents an increasingly important stewardship challenge.

Future national strategies should therefore support mentor hospitals, regional stewardship networks, educational partnerships, and implementation models tailored to resource-limited settings. A stewardship system that succeeds only in major university hospitals cannot achieve a meaningful national impact. The long-term success of ASP will depend on whether stewardship principles can be effectively disseminated across the entire healthcare system, including institutions with limited personnel and infrastructure.

The launch of the national ASP pilot program marks a historic turning point in Korea. Expansion of stewardship activities appears inevitable. The more important question is whether this expansion will be sustainable and transformative. The future of Korean ASP will not be determined primarily by regulations, software, or organizational charts. It will depend on whether healthcare systems can recruit, train, and support the people who perform stewardship every day.

If adequate personnel, resources, and time are secured, antimicrobial stewardship in Korea can evolve beyond a policy initiative and become a lasting component of healthcare culture. Such a transformation would represent the most meaningful achievement in the national response to antimicrobial resistance. The ultimate goal of ASP is to ensure that every patient receives the most appropriate antimicrobial therapy at the right time, at the right dose, and for the right duration.
