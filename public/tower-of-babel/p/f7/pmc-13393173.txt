Sodium Alginate Nanogels Encapsulating Cholecalciferol (Vitamin D3): A Topical Therapeutic Approach Targeting LOX IMVI and A431 Skin Cancer Cells
Authors: Hadil Faris Alotaibi, Mustafa A. Al-Qadhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study explores sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) as a novel topical therapeutic for skin cancer, targeting LOX IMVI (melanoma) and A431 (epidermoid carcinoma) cell lines. Nanogels were prepared using magnetic stirring and sonication, resulting in a uniform dispersion and enhanced stability. Physicochemical characterization revealed a particle size of 67.6 ± 13.43 nm, a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.30 ± 0.02, and a ζ-potential of −25.00 ± 2.00 mV, confirming good colloidal stability and suitability for topical application. In vitro drug release studies demonstrated a sustained-release profile, with 89.73 ± 2.1% cholecalciferol released over 34 h, as predicted by a first-order kinetic model (R 2 = 0.9871). Cytotoxicity assays showed significant anticancer activity, with IC50 values of 29.51 ± 1.56 and 24.95 ± 1.32 μM for LOX IMVI and A431 cell lines, respectively, while exhibiting reduced cytotoxicity against normal HSF cells (IC50 = 83.88 ± 4.43 μM). Cell-cycle analysis indicated G1/S phase arrest, and Annexin V-FITC/PI staining confirmed apoptosis induction with minimal necrosis. Molecular docking studies validated the mechanism of action, revealing strong binding of cholecalciferol to the vitamin D receptor (VDR) through hydrogen bonding with SER278 and SER275, π–cation interaction with TRP286, and hydrophobic interactions, with a docking score of −11.7 kcal/mol. These findings suggest sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol as a promising, biocompatible therapeutic for the topical treatment of skin cancer, paving the way for future clinical investigations.

Introduction

Skin cancer represents one of the most prevalent malignancies globally, encompassing basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and melanoma. Among these, melanoma remains the most lethal due to its high metastatic potential. Despite significant advances in skin cancer treatment, including surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, current therapeutic approaches remain limited by systemic toxicity, poor drug targeting, and suboptimal therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, there remains a pressing need for innovative therapeutic strategies that minimize systemic side effects, enhance targeted drug delivery, and improve patient outcomes. , Topical therapies, with their ability to deliver therapeutic agents directly to the disease site, are gaining significant attention in oncology, particularly for skin cancer. –

In this context, sodium alginate (Na-alginate), a natural polysaccharide derived from brown algae, has proven to be an ideal candidate for the nanogel formulation. Its biocompatibility and ability to form hydrogels through ionic cross-linking make it attractive for drug-delivery applications, particularly in topical treatments for localized diseases such as skin cancer. , Na-alginate-based nanogels are particularly advantageous because they can encapsulate hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, providing a stable platform for their delivery. The unique structural properties of Na-alginate nanogels also allow for modification to enhance drug loading, release rates, and the ability to penetrate deep layers of the skin. , Vitamin D (VitD3), beyond its well-documented role in calcium homeostasis, has garnered attention for its potential as an anticancer agent. Preclinical in vitro studies involving both human and animal (mouse) melanoma cell lines have consistently highlighted the potential of vitamin D derivatives, including calcitriol and cholecalciferol, in suppressing melanoma cell proliferation. These findings suggest that vitamin D compounds can significantly reduce the tumor growth and development in melanoma. Moreover, the differential efficacy of various vitamin D analogues was noted. Studies have identified that certain analogues, such as 20­(OH)­D3 (cholecalciferol), exhibit a stronger ability to inhibit cancer cell proliferation across multiple melanoma cell lines compared to the active form of vitamin D, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (calcitriol), where 20­(OH)­D3 (cholecalciferol) demonstrated superior growth inhibitory effects. These findings suggest that an alternative vitamin D analogue, cholecalciferol, may offer enhanced therapeutic potential, warranting further investigation to optimize their application in melanoma therapy.

The cumulative evidence underscores the role of vitamin D and its derivatives, specifically cholecalciferol, as promising agents in melanoma treatment. Their effects likely stem from their ability to regulate key cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation, making them valuable candidates for further exploration in melanoma therapy. , However, their clinical application is hampered by poor solubility, instability, and degradation under physiological conditions. Topical formulations offer unique advantages for skin cancer treatment, particularly in early-stage lesions and localized tumors. Unlike systemic therapies, topical agents allow direct drug application to the affected area, minimizing systemic exposure and associated toxicity. Sodium alginate nanogels are ideal for developing topical formulations, given their ability to adhere to the skin surface and penetrate the epidermal layers. Therefore, encapsulating cholecalciferol (VitD3) within Na-alginate nanogels as a topical therapy has the potential to revolutionize skin cancer treatment and address these challenges, providing a stable, controlled, and localized delivery mechanism that enhances its therapeutic efficacy and an effective alternative to current modalities.

This paper outlines the critical need for novel skin cancer therapies. It presents the rationale, design, and evaluation of Na-alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol (VitD3) as a topical treatment for skin cancer. By leveraging the synergistic properties of Na-alginate and cholecalciferol (VitD3), this study proposes a novel, practical, and patient-friendly approach to address the challenges associated with current skin cancer therapies.

Experimental Section

Materials

Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Polyethylene glycol (PEG 400, 50% w/v) was obtained from Merck (Germany). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was supplied by Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sodium alginate was procured from FMC Biopolymer (USA). Analytical-grade chemicals, including absolute ethanol and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 0.002%), were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Doxorubicin (positive control) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). A blank sodium alginate nanogel (prepared without cholecalciferol) was used as a negative control. Instrumentation included a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., U.K.) for particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ-potential analysis, and a bath sonicator (35 kHz, 100 W; Branson Ultrasonics, USA). The MTT assay kit was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, Missouri, USA). LOX IMVI (melanoma) and A431 (epidermoid carcinoma) cell lines, as well as all cell-culture reagents, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide apoptosis detection kits were purchased from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA). Docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina (The Scripps Research Institute) and visualized using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Schrödinger, LLC, USA), utilizing the human vitamin D receptor (VDR) crystal structure (PDB ID: 1DB1) from the RCSB Protein Data Bank.

Methods

Preparation of Sodium Alginate Nanogel Loaded with Cholecalciferol

Sodium alginate nanogel loaded with cholecalciferol was prepared using a combination of magnetic stirring and sonication techniques. Briefly, a 3% (w/w) sodium alginate solution was prepared by dissolving sodium alginate in distilled water under continuous stirring at 500 rpm for 2 h at room temperature to ensure complete hydration. Polyethylene glycol (PEG 400) was then added at a concentration of 5% (w/w) and stirred for an additional 30 min to achieve homogeneity. Although the measured ζ-potential exhibited a relatively low absolute value, the observed formulation stability may be attributed, in part, to the presence of PEG 400, which can adsorb at the droplet interface and form a hydrated steric barrier. Upon droplet approach, overlap and compression of these solvated PEG chains generate repulsive osmotic and entropic forces, thereby reducing droplet–droplet interactions and limiting aggregation or coalescence. Such nonelectrostatic stabilization mechanisms can be effective even in systems with limited electrostatic repulsion. Cholecalciferol (5% w/w) was predissolved in a small volume of PEG 400 and gradually introduced into the sodium alginate–PEG mixture while stirring at 800 rpm for 3 h to ensure uniform dispersion. The resulting mixture was subjected to probe sonication at 40% amplitude for 35 min while maintaining a temperature of 25 °C to prevent thermal degradation of the active ingredient. The final nanogel formulation was allowed to cool to room temperature and stored at 4 °C for further analysis.

Physicochemical Characterization of Nanogels: Particle Size, PDI, and ζ-Potential Measurements

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ-potential of the prepared sodium alginate nanogel loaded with cholecalciferol were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, U.K.) equipped with dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) technology. Samples were diluted with distilled water (1:10 v/v) to achieve the optimal scattering intensity for measurement. The diluted samples were placed in disposable cuvettes for particle size and PDI analysis, and in folded capillary cells for ζ-potential measurement. The measurements were conducted at a temperature of 25 °C, with a scattering angle of 173°, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate (n = 3) to ensure reproducibility. Data were recorded and analyzed using the Malvern Zetasizer software, and the results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to evaluate the surface morphology of the cholecalciferol-loaded sodium alginate nanogel particles. FTIR analysis was not included in the present study, as no chemical modification or new bond formation was expected during formulation. However, this technique may provide additional confirmation of drug–polymer interactions and can be considered in future work. Samples were mounted on aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with gold prior to imaging. SEM analysis was performed under an accelerating voltage of 5–20 kV, and images were captured at multiple magnifications to comprehensively evaluate the surface morphology of the nanogel.

Encapsulation Efficiency (EE%) and Loading Capacity

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of cholecalciferol within the sodium alginate nanogel was determined by measuring the amount of free (unencapsulated) drug in the supernatant after centrifugation. The concentration of unencapsulated cholecalciferol was quantified spectrophotometrically at 265 nm. All measurements were performed in triplicate EE%=(totaldrug−freedrugtotaldrug)×100

The loading capacity (LC%) was calculated based on the entrapped drug content and the carrier matrix composition. The amount of entrapped drug was determined from the measured entrapment efficiency, and the carrier weight was defined as the combined weight of sodium alginate and polyethylene glycol (PEG 400) in the formulation. The loading capacity was calculated using the following equation LC%=entrappeddrugentrappeddrug+carrierweight×100

All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

In Vitro Drug Release

The in vitro release profile of cholecalciferol from the sodium alginate nanogel was evaluated using a Franz diffusion cell system. The donor compartment was filled with the nanogel formulation, while the receptor compartment contained phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C under constant stirring at 100 rpm. A cellulose membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 12,000–14,000 Da was used to separate the compartments. At predetermined time intervals (1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 28, 31, and 34 h), aliquots were withdrawn from the receptor compartment and replaced with fresh buffer to maintain sink conditions. The collected samples were analyzed for cholecalciferol content using UV–vis spectrophotometry at a wavelength of λ = 265 nm. The cumulative drug release was calculated and expressed as a percentage of the total drug loaded. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Drug Release Kinetics Analysis

The release kinetics of cholecalciferol from the sodium alginate nanogel were evaluated by fitting the cumulative release data to four commonly used mathematical models: , zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models. The zero-order model, represented by Qt = Q 0 + k 0 t, assumes a constant drug release rate over time. The first-order model, expressed as ln­(100 – Qt ) = ln100 – k 1 t, describes release proportional to the remaining drug concentration. The Higuchi model, given by Qt = K H t 1/2, assumes a diffusion-controlled release mechanism. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model, Qt = Ktn , characterizes drug release based on the system’s structural and geometric properties, with the exponent n indicating the release mechanism. Nonlinear regression analysis was performed to determine the best-fit model using Python, and the coefficient of determination (R 2) was used to assess the goodness of fit. The model with the highest R 2 value was considered the most suitable for describing the release profile of cholecalciferol from the nanogel.

In Vitro Anticancer Activity against Skin Cancer

Positive Controls in Biological Assays

Doxorubicin (2–10 μM) was used as a validated positive control for the MTT cytotoxicity assay to benchmark assay sensitivity and verify biological responsiveness of the cell models. For the cell cycle and Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis assays, 0.002% DMSO served as the experimental control. No external apoptosis- or cell-cycle-inducing agents (e.g., staurosporine, nocodazole, etoposide) were included, as the purpose of these assays was to directly evaluate the intrinsic effect of the cholecalciferol-loaded nanogel formulation. This approach is widely used in mechanistic flow cytometry studies to characterize inherent biological effects of new formulations.

Control Conditions and Material Viability Considerations

Sodium alginate is a well-established biocompatible and noncytotoxic polymer, and the concentrations used in this formulation fall within ranges consistently reported to maintain high cell viability. In our study, the blank nanogel maintained >90% viability, confirming that the polymeric matrix does not contribute to cytotoxicity. Free vitamin D3 is highly hydrophobic and does not disperse uniformly in aqueous cell-culture medium, leading to precipitation and preventing reliable viability measurement. Therefore, biological assessments focused on the nanogel-encapsulated form of cholecalciferol, which ensures stable dispersion and reproducible evaluation of anticancer activity.

MTT Cytotoxicity Procedure

The anticancer efficacy of the cholecalciferol-loaded sodium alginate nanogel was evaluated against LOX IMVI (melanoma) and A431 (epidermoid carcinoma) cell lines using the standard MTT assay, with doxorubicin serving as the reference standard. LOX IMVI and A431 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 μg/mL insulin, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 100 μL/well and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity. After 24 h, 100 μL of a 10-fold serial dilution of the test samples (0.01–100 μM) was added and incubated for one additional day.

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After removing the supernatants, 50 μL/well of MTT reagent was added and incubated for 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were solubilized using 100 μL of 0.04 N HCl in isopropanol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Robonik P2000 microplate reader. IC50 values were obtained from nonlinear regression curves (GraphPad Prism 5).

Cell-Cycle Analysis of Sodium Alginate Nanogels Containing Cholecalciferol (VitD3)

Cell-cycle analysis assay was performed for sodium alginate nanogels containing cholecalciferol (VitD3) in LOX IMVI and A431 cell lines to inspect its effect on cell-cycle progression. DMSO (0.002%) was used as a control. LOX IMVI and A431 cells were exposed to sodium alginate nanogels containing cholecalciferol (VitD3) at different concentrations for 24 h. Then, the tested cells were collected by trypsinization and washed in PBS. Ice-cold absolute ethanol was used for the fixation of the collected cells. The cells were stained with Cycle TESTTM PLUS DNA Reagent Kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell-cycle distribution was evaluated using a flow cytometer.

Apoptotic Assay of Sodium Alginate Nanogels Containing Cholecalciferol (VitD3)

The apoptosis induction as well as the apoptosis percentage executed by sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol LOX IMVI and A431 cancer cell lines was inspected using an annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide dual staining test , followed by flow cytometry analysis. This method enabled precise classification of cell death modalities in response to treatment. DMSO (0.002%) was used as a control. To detect the apoptosis induced by sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol (VitD3), LOX IMVI and A431 cell lines were seeded and incubated overnight and then treated with sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol (VitD3) at different concentrations for 24 h. DMSO was chosen as the negative control. After that, the cells were collected and washed twice with PBS. The cells were exposed to centrifugation. An apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) was used in this test. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the cells were stained by Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) in the binding buffer for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Using a flow cytometer, Annexin V-FITC and PI binding were analyzed. FlowJo software was used to analyze the frequencies in all quadrants.

Molecular Docking

The docking simulation of cholecalciferol (vit D3) was performed using AutoDock, with the results analyzed and visualized via PyMOL. The human vitamin D receptor (VDR) crystal structure bound to its natural ligand calcitriol (PDB ID: 1DB1) served as the receptor model, offering a high-resolution framework for understanding ligand–receptor interactions. This study aimed to investigate the molecular interactions that underpin the binding of cholecalciferol to VDR and evaluate its implications for targeted topical skin cancer therapy.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences between groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Dose–response curves and IC50 values were generated using nonlinear regression analysis.

Results and Discussion

These results indicate sufficient repulsive forces between particles, reducing the likelihood of aggregation over time.

The polydispersity index (PDI) was 0.30 ± 0.02, indicating a moderately uniform particle size distribution. A PDI value below 0.3 is typically associated with monodisperse systems, and the results suggest that the formulation has an acceptable degree of homogeneity. The slight elevation in PDI may be attributed to variations in cholecalciferol encapsulation and the hydrophilic–hydrophobic balance introduced by PEG.

The developed cholecalciferol-loaded sodium alginate nanogel exhibited a particle size of 67.67 ± 13.43 nm, PDI of 0.30 ± 0.02, and ζ-potential of −25.00 ± 2.00 mV. Particle sizes for alginate-based nanogel systems typically fall within the nanoscale range (approximately 40–150 nm), with PDI values generally below 0.3, indicating acceptable size distribution and homogeneity. Additionally, negative ζ-potential values are commonly observed due to the presence of ionizable carboxyl groups in alginate, typically ranging from −10 to −45 mV depending on the formulation composition, cross-linking density, and preparation method. −

For instance, alginate-based nanogels loaded with therapeutic agents have been reported to exhibit particle sizes of 45–124 nm, PDI values between 0.17 and 0.26, and ζ-potential values from −13 to −28 mV, demonstrating suitable nanoscale characteristics and colloidal stability. Calcium-cross-linked alginate nanogels have shown particle sizes below 100 nm with more negative ζ-potential, likely due to enhanced electrostatic stabilization from increased cross-linking density.

Overall, the physicochemical parameters of the present formulation are consistent with previously reported alginate nanogels. The nanoscale size and moderate polydispersity suggest adequate homogeneity, while the negative ζ-potential contributes to electrostatic repulsion and colloidal stability. Variations across studies are expected due to differences in drug loading, polymer concentration, cross-linking strategy, and processing conditions. It should be noted that direct confirmation of ionic cross-linking in alginate-based nanogels remains challenging because the process involves noncovalent electrostatic interactions that are not readily detectable using conventional spectroscopic techniques. Therefore, the formation of the nanogel system was primarily inferred to be from its physicochemical characteristics.

The optimized cholecalciferol-loaded sodium alginate nanogel exhibited an entrapment efficiency (EE%) of 83.6 ± 2.1%, confirming efficient incorporation of cholecalciferol within the alginate nanogel matrix. This relatively high EE% can be attributed to the lipophilic nature of cholecalciferol, which promotes its retention within the polymeric network and compatibility with the polymer matrix. The loading capacity (LC%) was determined to be 34.3 ± 0.2%. This value reflects the proportion of entrapped drug relative to the total carrier matrix and further supports the effective incorporation of the lipophilic drug within the nanogel system. One-way ANOVA analysis confirmed that differences observed across replicates were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), indicating good reproducibility of the formulation process. The observed entrapment efficiency aligns well with previously reported values for alginate-based nanogel systems, reflecting effective drug–polymer compatibility and efficient incorporation of lipophilic molecules.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of the cholecalciferol-loaded sodium alginate nanogel revealed a heterogeneous surface topology characterized by irregular, folded, and wrinkled morphological features (Figure ). Micrographs at multiple magnifications demonstrated a continuous gel-like matrix without evidence of crystalline cholecalciferol deposits, confirming the successful incorporation of the drug within the alginate network. The absence of sharp or angular particulate structures supports the formation of a cohesive nanogel rather than that of discrete particle aggregates.

SEM micrograph of the cholecalciferol-loaded sodium alginate nanogel at 500× magnification, showing a continuous polymeric surface morphology without crystalline drug deposits. EDS elemental analysis confirmed the presence of carbon, oxygen, sodium, and calcium associated with the alginate–cholecalciferol matrix, with trace metallic signals attributed to instrumentation.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) further confirmed the elemental composition of the nanogel, with dominant peaks corresponding to carbon (50.75%), oxygen (10.63%), sodium (1.47%), and calcium (0.65%), along with trace levels of silicon, chromium, manganese, iron, and nickel. The high proportions of carbon and oxygen are consistent with the organic polysaccharide framework of sodium alginate and the embedded cholesterol molecule. The presence of sodium and calcium reflects the ionic constituents of alginate, while trace metallic elements likely originated from the sample holder and instrument interface rather than from the formulation itself.

Overall, the combined SEM and EDS analyses confirm the structural integrity of the nanogel system and the successful encapsulation of cholecalciferol within the polymeric matrix. The observed surface morphology also supports the homogeneity and stability of the nanogel formulation, in agreement with the particle size and ζ-potential measurements.

In Vitro Drug Release

The in vitro release profile of cholecalciferol from the sodium alginate nanogel demonstrated a controlled and sustained-release pattern over 34 h (Figure ). An initial burst release was observed within the first 6 h, reaching 34.7 ± 1.9%, which can be attributed to the diffusion of cholecalciferol from the surface of the nanogel. This phase was followed by a sustained-release phase, with the cumulative release gradually increasing to 85.2 ± 2.7% at 24 h. The release profile showed a plateau phase after 28 h, where the cumulative release reached 89.73 ± 2.1%, with no significant increase observed between 28 and 34 h. This suggests that most of the drug was released within the first 28 h, and the remaining encapsulated cholecalciferol was either trapped within the gel matrix or required additional time for further diffusion.

Release Kinetics Analysis

The release kinetics of cholecalciferol from the sodium alginate nanogel were analyzed by fitting the cumulative release data to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models to determine the underlying release mechanism. The results demonstrated that the first-order model provided the highest coefficient of determination (R 2 = 0.9871), indicating that the release process is concentration-dependent, with the drug release rate decreasing over time as the concentration gradient diminishes.

The Korsmeyer–Peppas model also exhibited a strong fit (R 2 = 0.9795). The release exponent (n) was calculated from the initial 60% of the release profile and was found to be 0.63. This value indicates a non-Fickian (anomalous) transport mechanism, suggesting that the release of cholecalciferol from the sodium alginate nanogel is governed by a combination of diffusion and polymer relaxation processes. This finding provides important mechanistic insight into the controlled release behavior of the nanogel system.

The Higuchi model (R 2 = 0.9703) further supported a diffusion-based contribution to the release process, demonstrating a linear relationship between the cumulative drug release and the square root of time. In contrast, the zero-order model (R 2 = 0.9291), which assumes a constant release rate, showed the lowest correlation among the tested models, indicating that the release process is not purely time-dependent but is influenced by matrix-controlled transport phenomena.

Overall, the agreement between the first-order and Korsmeyer–Peppas models suggests that the release is primarily concentration-dependent while being modulated by polymer matrix relaxation and diffusion mechanisms. The observed sustained-release profile confirms that the sodium alginate nanogel effectively controls the release of cholecalciferol by forming a stable gel matrix that regulates drug diffusion.

The plateau phase observed beyond 28 h, with no significant further increase in cumulative release, indicates that the majority of the encapsulated drug was released within the first 28 h, consistent with the diffusion-limited release behavior. The slight variations in release percentages across different time points were within acceptable standard deviation ranges, confirming the reproducibility and consistency of the formulation (Figure ).

A formal stability study was not conducted in the present work and represents a limitation; therefore, future studies will focus on evaluating the short- and long-term stabilities of the developed formulation under different storage conditions.

In Vitro Cytotoxic Activity of Cholecalciferol-Loaded Na-Alginate Nanogels (IC50 μM) against LOX IMVI, A431, and Normal Cell Line HSF

The evaluation of sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol (VitD3) revealed promising anticancer activity in vitro, highlighting their potential for targeted topical therapy against skin cancer. At the concentrations used, sodium alginate is inherently noncytotoxic, and the blank nanogel exhibited high cell viability across all tested cell lines, with values of 96.2 ± 0.8% (LOX IMVI), 95.8 ± 1.2% (A431), and 96.53 ± 0.6% (HSF), confirming that the polymeric carrier does not contribute to the observed biological effects.

Free vitamin D3 cannot be reliably evaluated in vitro because of its hydrophobicity and precipitation in aqueous medium; therefore, mechanistic and viability analyses were conducted using the cholecalciferol-loaded nanogel, which ensures proper dispersion and meaningful cellular exposure. The nanogels exhibited IC50 values of 29.51 ± 1.56 and 24.95 ± 1.32 μM against LOX IMVI (melanoma) and A431 (epidermoid carcinoma) cancer cell lines, respectively (Table and Figure ). The dose–response curves in Figure were generated using IC50-based modeling to enable comparative visualization of cytotoxic responses across different cell lines, based on experimentally determined IC50 values.

A critical aspect of any cancer therapy is its selectivity toward malignant cells over normal cells. The sodium alginate nanogels showed a markedly higher IC50 (83.88 ± 4.43 μM) on normal human skin fibroblast (HSF) cells compared to doxorubicin (16.62 ± 0.88 μM), reflecting their superior biocompatibility and reduced cytotoxicity. This favorable safety profile highlights the potential of these nanogels as a safer alternative to conventional chemotherapy.

The observed selectivity is attributed to the biocompatible nature of sodium alginate and the nanogel matrix’s ability to deliver vitamin D3 in a controlled and localized manner. Cholecalciferol (VitD3), known for its role in modulating cell proliferation and apoptosis, likely exerts its anticancer effects through mechanisms involving disruption of cancer cell signaling pathways while sparing normal cells. These findings align with the growing interest in nanotechnology-based delivery systems that enhance the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of therapeutic agents, and are consistent with the increasing body of evidence supporting the role of biopolymer-based nanocarriers in improving drug delivery, minimizing systemic toxicity, and achieving targeted anticancer effects. The sodium alginate nanogels stabilize cholecalciferol (VitD3) and provide sustained release, ensuring prolonged exposure to cancer cells and improving therapeutic outcomes by addressing the limitations associated with its poor solubility and rapid degradation under physiological conditions. The high cytotoxicity of doxorubicin to normal cells (IC50 of 16.62 ± 0.88 μM) underscores the pressing need for safer, localized treatment options. Sodium alginate nanogels, with their ability to deliver drugs directly to the affected site, offer a promising alternative that minimizes systemic exposure and associated adverse effects. Moreover, the nanogel formulation presents a patient-friendly approach to treating localized lesions and early-stage cancers. Furthermore, the substantial difference in IC50 values between cancer and normal cells suggests a significant therapeutic window, reducing the risk of systemic side effects associated with current treatment modalities.

Future investigations should focus on elucidating the molecular pathways underlying the anticancer effects of cholecalciferol (VitD3) when delivered via nanogels. Additional studies could explore the in vivo efficacy and safety profiles of these formulations as well as their potential to synergize with other therapeutic modalities. The significant selectivity and activity observed in vitro strongly support the potential of sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol (VitD3) as an innovative platform for topical skin cancer therapy.

Cell-Cycle Analysis of Sodium Alginate Nanogels Containing Cholecalciferol (VitD3)

In these mechanistic evaluations, DMSO (0.002%) was used as the experimental control for the cell-cycle and apoptosis studies. We did not include a separate pharmacological inducer of apoptosis or cell-cycle arrest, such as staurosporine or nocodazole, because these assays aimed to characterize the intrinsic activity of the nanogel formulation relative to untreated conditions. This is an established and acceptable approach in flow cytometry-based mechanistic studies, , where the primary goal is to determine how the investigational formulation modulates proliferation and apoptosis independent of external stimuli. The impact of sodium alginate nanogels encapsulating cholecalciferol (VitD3) on cell-cycle progression was assessed in LOX IMVI and A431 (epidermoid carcinoma) cancer cell lines. The data reveal significant cell-cycle alterations following treatment with the nanogel formulation, suggesting a mechanism of growth inhibition that could contribute to its anticancer effects.

Treatment of the LOX IMVI cell line with sodium alginate nanogels containing cholecalciferol (VitD3) resulted in 71.92% of cells in the G0–G1 phase, 27.05% in the S phase, and 1.03% in the G2/M phase. This indicates a notable cell-cycle arrest at the G1/S checkpoint, suggesting that cholecalciferol (VitD3) encapsulated in sodium alginate nanogels effectively inhibits cell progression from the G1 to the S phase. The control cells, in contrast, showed 64.55% in G0–G1, 24.37% in S, and 11.08% in G2/M, indicating normal cell-cycle progression with a higher proportion of cells in the G2/M phase.

On the other hand, the nanogel formulation in A431 cells induced 69.59% of cells in the G0–G1 phase, 21.32% in S, and 9.09% in G2/M. This suggests a cell-cycle arrest primarily in the G1 phase, with a reduction in the proportion of cells progressing to the S phase. The control group for A431 cells exhibited 53.76% in G0–G1, 28.44% in S, and 17.8% in G2/M, indicative of a higher percentage of cells in the S and G2/M phases, suggesting that the nanogel formulation exerts a significant influence on cell-cycle regulation, as shown in Table and Figure .

The Docking Analysis of Cholecalciferol (VitD3) in the Active Site of the Human Vitamin D Receptor (VDR)

The docking analysis of cholecalciferol within the ligand-binding domain of the vitamin D receptor (PDB ID: 1DB1) revealed a robust interaction network, primarily mediated by critical interactions. Hydrogen bonding was observed between the hydroxyl groups of cholecalciferol and the side chains of residues SER278 and SER275, which anchor the ligand within the binding pocket. These hydrogen bonds are crucial in anchoring the ligand to the receptor, ensuring a stable interaction within the ligand-binding domain. These interactions also closely resemble the binding mode of the endogenous ligand, calcitriol. Additionally, a π–cation interaction between the aromatic TRP286 and cholecalciferol contributed significantly to the stability and specificity of the complex. This type of interaction is known to enhance ligand binding, particularly in cases where the ligand is nonpolar or has an aromatic structure, as is the case with cholecalciferol. The π–cation interaction strengthens the overall binding affinity by improving the ligand’s fit within the receptor pocket and promoting a favorable orientation of the ligand.

Furthermore, extensive hydrophobic interactions were observed between cholecalciferol and the surrounding nonpolar amino acid residues in the binding pocket. These interactions, involving van der Waals forces and hydrophobic contacts, contribute significantly to the stability of the ligand–receptor complex. The hydrophobic core of the binding site is essential for accommodating the nonpolar regions of cholecalciferol, ensuring effective ligand binding and retention.

The docking analysis yielded a binding score of −11.7 kcal/mol, indicating a favorable interaction between cholecalciferol and VDR. This negative binding energy suggests strong ligand–receptor binding, facilitated by multiple stabilizing interactions that have been identified. Additionally, the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) value of 1.13 Å indicates good alignment between the docked pose and the experimentally observed binding mode, further confirming the reliability and accuracy of the docking results (Figure ).

This understanding of the molecular docking process provides a solid foundation for the design and optimization of sodium alginate nanogels as a drug-delivery system for cholecalciferol. Such a delivery system could significantly enhance the bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy of cholecalciferol for targeted topical skin cancer therapies, with the potential for better clinical outcomes. The study also highlights the relevance of understanding receptor–ligand dynamics for the development of nanomedicines aimed at modulating nuclear receptor pathways in cancer.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates the successful development of cholecalciferol-loaded sodium alginate nanogels with favorable physicochemical properties including nanoscale particle size, acceptable polydispersity, and sufficient colloidal stability. The formulation exhibited high entrapment efficiency and a sustained drug release profile, indicating the effective incorporation of cholecalciferol within the alginate matrix and controlled diffusion-driven release behavior. Kinetic modeling further suggested that the release process is predominantly concentration-dependent with diffusion playing a key role in governing drug liberation.

The novelty of this work lies in the integration of a biocompatible alginate-based nanogel system with cholecalciferol for potential topical application in skin cancer therapy, alongside a comprehensive evaluation of its physicochemical characteristics and release kinetics in relation to the anticancer relevance.

In vitro cytotoxicity results support the potential applicability of the developed nanogel against epidermoid carcinoma cells, highlighting its promise as a localized and controlled drug-delivery platform. However, additional studies are required to fully establish its therapeutic potential. Future investigations should focus on in vivo evaluation, detailed stability studies, skin permeation assessment, and mechanistic biological studies to further validate their efficacy and safety for clinical translation.
