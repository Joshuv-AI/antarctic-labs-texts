The influence of ultrasonic vibration on the microstructure and properties of laser-cladded Fe-Ni-Ti composite coatings
Authors: Zhen Wang, Fengqin Zhang, Shengchao Zang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo enhance the service life of brake discs for rail transit vehicles, an experimental platform for ultrasonic-assisted laser cladding was established. Single-layer and multi-layer overlapping Fe-Ni-Ti coatings were prepared on the surface of 316 L steel with and without ultrasonic assistance. The study investigated the influence of ultrasonic vibration on the microstructure and residual stress of composite coatings, and conducted tests on hardness, frictional wear, and corrosion resistance of the coatings. Results showed that ultrasonic vibration can refine grain structures.Under ultrasonic assistance, residual tensile and compressive stresses of the multi-layer cladding decreased by 12 % and 13 %, respectively. Under ultrasonic assistance, the average microhardness of single-pass samples increased to 515 HV, while the average microhardness of multi-pass overlapping samples increased to 460 HV. After adding ultrasonic vibration, the wear rate of single-layer samples decreased by 18.1 %, and that of multi-layer samples decreased by 12.9 %. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy shows that under the action of ultrasound, the composite coating has better corrosion resistance performance.Keywords: Laser cladding, Ultrasonic vibration, Fe-Ni-Ti coatings, Microstructure, Wear resistance

Introduction

Brake discs for rail vehicles are often subjected to long-term exposure in environments characterized by mechanical wear and electrochemical corrosion [1,2]. The synergistic effect of friction and corrosion accelerates surface damage to components, leading to a significant reduction in their service life [3,4]. In order to prolong the service life of equipment and overcome the limitations of base material performance, methods such as thermal spraying and electrochemical deposition are commonly employed to prepare high-performance strengthening coatings on component surfaces [5,6]. For example, thermal spraying technology is commonly used to solve the problem of low service life of key components in gas turbines and internal combustion engines. Sonu Kanwal et al. developed an economically efficient slurry-based dip coating technique for depositing mullite - based ceramic coatings on P91 steel substrates.The coating sintered at 1000 °C was found to be free from cracks and other microdefects [7]. Among these methods, laser cladding technology is widely used for preparing surface strengthening coatings on mechanical components in various fields such as aviation, marine, and rail transportation, due to its advantages of good metallurgical bonding strength, low dilution rate, and excellent mechanical properties [8,9].

In response to the challenge of regulating the microstructure of laser cladding strengthening coatings, scholars at home and abroad have employed external energy fields such as electric, magnetic, and acoustic fields to assist in controlling the morphology during the laser cladding process. Qian Wang et al. studied the effects of electromagnetic field assistance on the microstructure and properties of laser-clad Ni60 alloy coatings. They found that the coatings with the electromagnetic field had higher microhardness, better corrosion resistance, and wear resistance, with fewer cracks [10]. Chonggui Li et al. used high-frequency micro-vibration laser cladding technology to prepare WC-Ni composite coatings on a Ti-6Al-4V alloy substrate. They found that high-frequency micro-vibration resulted in a more uniform microstructure of the coating, which helped reduce microhardness fluctuations within the cladding layer [11]. Among them, ultrasonic-assisted laser cladding has shown outstanding performance in refining the microstructure, homogenizing element distribution, reducing localized stress, and enhancing microhardness and wear resistance, making it a focal point for researchers [[12], [13], [14]].

Fe-Ni-Ti-C alloy powders are cost-effective, and coatings prepared from them exhibit high wear resistance and corrosion resistance. During the laser cladding process, chemical reactions occur between Ti elements and C, leading to the in-situ formation of TiC ceramic reinforcement phases, further enhancing the mechanical properties of the cladding layer. This combination endows the cladding layer with excellent wear resistance, corrosion resistance, and mechanical properties. In previous studies, the author prepared Fe-Ni-Ti coating on the surface of the component, which significantly improved its wear resistance compared to laser cladding Fe-36Ni coating [15,16]. However, this was still insufficient to meet the requirements for brake discs. In this context, ultrasonic vibration-assisted laser cladding provided a new approach for the authors. Current research reports on ultrasonic-assisted laser cladding of Fe-Ni-Ti-C coatings are relatively lacking, and the mechanism of ultrasonic vibration on wear and corrosion resistance is still not clear, thus requiring further investigation.

The study introduces ultrasonic vibration into the laser cladding process to prepare Fe-Ni-Ti-C composite coatings on the surface of 316 L steel with and without ultrasonic assistance. The influence mechanism of ultrasonic vibration on the microstructure and residual stress of the composite coatings is analyzed, followed by hardness, friction, wear, and corrosion resistance tests on samples with and without ultrasonic assistance. This research provides insights for preparing brake disc coatings with high wear and corrosion resistance.

Experimental materials

Before conducting laser cladding experiments, the sheet was polished to remove surface oxide films, and then cleaned with alcohol to remove surface stains, followed by drying and storage for later use. The cladding powder has a mass fraction of 57 % Fe, 36 % Ni, 6 % Ti, and 1 % C, with uniform particle size, suitable for laser cladding manufacturing.

Ultrasonic-assisted laser cladding process for Fe-Ni-Ti coatings

The experimental setup for ultrasonic-assisted laser cladding is shown in Fig. 2(A) and (B). The experiment employed a fiber laser (RFL-C6000) with a maximum output power of 6000 W and a wavelength range of 1075 to 1085 nm. A coaxial powder feeding cladding head was used, along with a twin-drum rotary pressure powder feeder. The motion and control system consisted of a 6-axis robot (M-20iD) with a trajectory accuracy and repeat positioning accuracy of ±0.02 mm. Ultrasonic assistance was applied through direct contact, utilizing an ultrasonic vibration device comprising components such as an ultrasonic wave generator (HDS-SK-20) and a workbench. Mechanical fixation between the substrate and the workbench was achieved using bolts. Argon gas was used for both powder delivery and atmosphere protection.

Microstructure characterization and performance testing

The laser cladding samples were cut into 7mm × 5mm × 5 mm specimens using a metallographic cutting machine (DK733-A). The specimens were then polished, and subjected to corrosion. The microstructure of the cladding layer was observed using an optical microscope (ZEISS). Measure grain size using Image View software. The composition and residual stress of the cladding layer were analyzed using XRD (Bruker D8 ADVANCE) diffractometer, while the microhardness of the coating was tested using a microhardness tester (HVS1000). Friction and wear tests were conducted using a ball-on-disc tribometer (MMQ-02) with Si3N4 ceramic balls, 4.75 mm in diameter. A load of 50 N was applied, with a motor speed of 200 r/min and a duration of 30 min. Before and after the wear test, the specimens were weighed using an electronic balance (Lichen, FA123C) to obtain the wear mass. Corrosion resistance tests were performed using an electrochemical workstation (CHI760e) with a 3.5 % mass fraction NaCl solution as the electrolyte. A platinum sheet electrode with an effective area of 1 cm2 was used as the working electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode was used as the reference electrode.

Experimental conclusion and discussion

Microstructural analysis of the Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer

Fig. 3 （A-D） shows the macroscopic morphology of the laser-cladded Fe-Ni-Ti coating. The single-pass cladding layer exhibits a smooth and continuous surface, with no significant changes in macroscopic morphology observed due to ultrasonic vibration, and no cracks detected on the coating surface. In Fig. 3(C), the multi-pass overlapping cladding layer displays several fine cracks and noticeable cladding residues at the edges. However, after applying ultrasonic vibration, no visible cracks are observed, and the quality of the multi-pass overlapping cladding layer is significantly improved.

Macroscopic morphology of Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer.: (A) and (C) without ultrasonic assistance; (B) and (D) with ultrasonic assistance.(After applying ultrasonic vibration, no significant cracks were observed on the cladding layer surface, and the quality of the multi-pass overlapping cladding layer was notably improved.)

Fig. 4 depicts the microstructure of the single-pass laser-cladded Fe-Ni-Ti composite coating. In Fig. 4(A), the contour morphology of the single-pass cladding layer is evident, with a rounded profile on the surface. The microstructure of the central region (Region E) of the cladding layer with and without ultrasonic assistance is magnified in Fig. 4(B) and (C), respectively. Without ultrasonic vibration, the central region of the cladding layer is mainly composed of equiaxed grains, albeit with a small amount of columnar grains. With the application of ultrasonic vibration, the distribution of crystals becomes more uniform, leading to a significant reduction in crystal size compared to the non-ultrasonically treated coating, and a finer and denser microstructure. To facilitate comparison of crystal sizes before and after ultrasonic application, the diameters of the grains in Fig. 4 were statistically analyzed using Image J software, with the results shown in Fig. 4(D). The introduction of ultrasonic waves during laser cladding induces pressure fluctuations due to cavitation effects, resulting in the formation of microbubbles and acoustic streaming in the liquid medium. The formation, growth, and collapse of microbubbles lead to rapid transitions between supercooling and superheating in the cladding material, promoting nucleation of grains and thus grain refinement [17]. Moreover, the microjets and shock waves generated during microbubble collapse break up large grains, thereby inhibiting dendritic growth. Additionally, the dispersal and agitation effects of acoustic streaming facilitate uniform solute distribution, preventing segregation phenomena and aiding in grain refinement [18].

Metallographic structure of single-layer Fe-Ni-Ti cladding. (A) Overall morphology of the cross-section of the cladding layer; (B) without ultrasonic vibration; (C) with ultrasonic vibration; (D) Average grain size of the cladding layer (After applying ultrasonic vibration, the crystal distribution became more uniform, and compared to samples without ultrasonic treatment, the crystal size of the coating noticeably decreased, with a finer and denser arrangement of the structure.).

The nucleation rate is influenced by the embryo crystal size r, the critical nucleation size rk, and the free energy ΔG within the melt pool. For crystals, when the energy required for nucleation is lower than the free energy within the melt pool and r > rk, the nucleation condition is satisfied, as shown by Equations (1), (2), (3) [19,20].

In the equation: σ represents the Gibbs-Thomson effect; ΔG denotes the free energy within the melt pool; ΔGc stands for the critical nucleation energy; f(θ) is the nucleation factor; GS signifies the solid-phase unit volume free energy; GL represents the liquid-phase unit volume free energy; ΔHS refers to enthalpy change; ΔSS denotes entropy change; T is the thermodynamic temperature; ΔVS indicates volume change; ΔP represents input power.

When ultrasonic vibration is applied during cladding, the increase in ΔP in Equation (3) leads to an increase in the free energy ΔG, thereby causing a decrease in the critical nucleation size rk and the critical nucleation energy ΔGc. Consequently, the nucleation rate of the cladding layer accelerates, leading to an increase in grain density and a reduction in crystal volume, resulting in refined microstructure.

Fig. 5 shows the metallographic structure of the multi-layer overlapping laser-cladded Fe-Ni-Ti composite coating. Fig. 5 (A) and (B) respectively depict the metallographic structures of the overlapping regions of the cladding layer without and with ultrasonic assistance. A comparison of Fig. 5（C ）and （D） reveals that the overlap cladding layer under ultrasonic vibration exhibits fewer porosity defects and a more uniformly distributed grain structure. During the multi-layer overlapping laser cladding process, the morphology of the overlapping region's microstructure changes due to variations in temperature gradient and solidification rate, exhibiting non-uniform nucleation characteristics in grain growth. The grain size and shape exhibit non-uniformity. When ultrasonic waves propagate in molten metal media, they generate strong acoustic streaming and cavitation effects. These effects significantly alter the flow state within the melt pool, enhancing its mixing effect and thereby optimizing the microstructure of the cladding layer. The cavitation effect of ultrasonic waves can generate tiny bubbles in the melt pool, which subsequently rupture. This process aids in grain refinement and promotes homogenization [21]. Ultrasonic waves can also enhance the heat transfer efficiency of the melt pool, resulting in more uniform distribution of heat within the cladding layer. This helps reduce temperature gradients and thus suppresses the directional growth of dendritic structures [22].

Under the influence of ultrasonic vibration, TiC is evenly distributed within the cladding layer without significant elemental segregation, as shown in Fig. 7(B) and (C). The cavitation bubbles generated by ultrasonic vibration collapse, creating localized high temperatures and pressures that promote the dissolution of the alloy layer on the TiC surface [23]. The dissolved alloy elements are uniformly distributed throughout the cladding layer due to the acoustic streaming effect, preventing uneven elemental distribution during solidification and suppressing segregation.

Analysis of residual stresses in the Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer

The residual stress measurement techniques have been classi-fied as (i) destructive, (ii) semi-destructive, and (iii) no-destructive [24]. In this experiment, XRD non-destructive techniques were used to measure the residual stress on the surface of the cladding layer. Changes in residual stresses in the Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer assisted by ultrasonic vibrations can be observed in Fig. 8. The figure illustrates that from the cladding layer to the substrate direction, residual tensile stress exists in the cladding layer, reaching its maximum value at the center of the cladding layer. As it approaches the substrate, the residual stress transitions into compressive stress, reaching its maximum value at the metallurgical bonding interface of the substrate. Due to the slight difference in physical shrinkage properties between the cladding powder and the substrate material, rapid cooling in the cladding area induces tensile stress, as the cladding layer is constrained by the inherent elements of the cold substrate. In the cladding center, where laser energy is concentrated, more thorough melting occurs, leading to greater constrained shrinkage after cooling, resulting in increased tensile stress values.

Residual stress in Fe-Ni-Ti overlay coating assisted by ultrasonic during laser cladding (After adding ultrasonic vibration, the maximum residual tensile stress and residual compressive stress in the coating are significantly reduced.).

Under ultrasonic assistance, the residual stresses in the cladding layer are lower than those in the cladding layer without ultrasonic assistance. The residual stress in the single-pass laser cladding layer is lower than that in the multi-pass overlapped cladding layer. The maximum residual stresses are observed, with the residual tensile stress and compressive stress in the single-pass cladding layer assisted by ultrasonics reduced by 18 % and 15 %, respectively. The residual tensile stress and compressive stress in the multi-pass cladding layer assisted by ultrasonics are reduced by 12 % and 13 %, respectively.

The acoustic streaming agitation effect of ultrasound can eliminate internal stresses in the coating. During the cladding process, ultrasonic vibration can homogenize the microstructure and reduce stress concentration inside the coating. Through the agitation effect of acoustic streaming, ultrasound can diffuse the nuclei generated by cavitation throughout the entire molten pool, effectively increasing the nucleation rate, homogenizing temperature gradients and composition distribution, and reducing segregation [25]. These effects collectively contribute to reducing residual stresses in the cladding layer.

During the multi-pass overlay process, complex thermal cycling and stress-strain evolution occur between the overlay layer, substrate, and each overlay layer. Thermal expansion and cooling shrinkage of each overlay layer generate corresponding stresses, and during the overlaying process, these stresses accumulate and interact, leading to an increase in residual stress levels. Particularly at the junctions between adjacent overlay passes, stress concentration is more pronounced due to the accumulation of heat and force, thereby increasing the overall residual stress in the entire overlay layer [26,27].

Microhardness analysis of the Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer

The variation of microhardness of Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer assisted by ultrasonic during laser cladding can be observed in Fig. 9. The microhardness distribution along the depth direction of the sample cross-section is shown in the figure, with the testing direction extending from the top of the cladding layer to the substrate. It can be observed that the microhardness in the middle of the cladding layer is the highest. After applying ultrasonic vibration, both single-pass and multi-pass overlapped cladding layers show a significant increase in microhardness. In the case of single-pass cladding layer without ultrasonic assistance, the hardness distribution ranges from 470 HV with a fluctuation range of 40 HV, whereas with ultrasonic assistance, the hardness distribution ranges between 470 HV and 515 HV with a reduced fluctuation range of 25 HV. This represents a 37 % increase in microhardness compared to samples without ultrasonic assistance. For the overlapped cladding layer without ultrasonic assistance, the hardness distribution ranges from 425 HV with a fluctuation range of 50 HV, while with ultrasonic assistance, the hardness distribution ranges from 425 HV to 460 HV with a reduced fluctuation range of 25 HV, representing an 8.2 % increase in microhardness compared to samples without ultrasonic assistance.

Changes in the microhardness of Fe-Ni-Ti laser cladding coatings assisted by ultrasound (Under ultrasonic action, the average microhardness of single-layer samples increased from 470 HV to 515 HV, while that of multi-layer overlapping samples increased from 425 HV to 460 HV.).

The introduction of ultrasound eliminates the phenomenon of sparse tissue in the surface layer of the coating, resulting in a more uniform distribution of surface microhardness and a reduced fluctuation range. Analyzing the changes in the metallographic structure of the coating combined with the application of ultrasound, ultrasound vibration applied during the laser cladding process accelerates the flow rate of the melt and homogenizes the temperature of the molten pool, thereby reducing the cooling rate at different positions of the molten pool. Moreover, the cavitation effect of ultrasound causes changes in temperature and pressure inside and outside cavitation bubbles. The cavitation bubbles absorb heat, expand, and burst, resulting in the formation of high-speed impact flow that shatters dendrites developed around them while increasing the degree of undercooling in the vicinity. The combined effect of these factors leads to the refinement of the coating structure, enhancing its resistance to plastic deformation and consequently increasing its hardness [23]. Furthermore, Under ultrasonic assistance, the grain size of the cladding layer is refined, increasing the number of grain boundaries per unit volume. These grain boundaries act as obstacles to dislocation movement, thereby effectively enhancing the coating's microhardness.

Analysis of the wear resistance of the Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer

The effect of ultrasonic vibration on the friction coefficient of Fe-Ni-Ti laser cladding coatings is shown in Fig. 10(A) and (B). In the initial stage of frictional wear, the friction pair surfaces are relatively smooth, resulting in a low friction coefficient. As the friction time increases, the contact area of the friction pair expands continuously, leading to a rapid increase in the friction coefficient. When the friction pair reaches a relatively stable state, the friction coefficients of single and multiple pass laser cladding samples without ultrasound fluctuate around 0.5, while those with ultrasound fluctuate around 0.4. It can be observed from the graph that the friction coefficient of the laser cladding coating without ultrasound fluctuates significantly, whereas with ultrasound, the variation of the friction coefficient over time becomes more stable. This is attributed to the uneven distribution of coating structure and hardness without ultrasound [28]. As the wear progresses, differences in hardness among different regions of the coating lead to variations in the friction coefficient. Conversely, ultrasound promotes a uniform distribution of coating structure and hardness, resulting in more consistent friction performance [29,30].

The wear rate of the single-pass sample decreased by 18.1 %, and that of the multi-pass sample decreased by 12.9 %.).

Fig. 10(C) illustrates the impact of ultrasound vibration on the wear resistance of single-pass and multi-pass laser cladding coatings. The addition of ultrasound reduces the wear rate of single-pass laser cladding coatings from 0.0828 mg/m to 0.0678 mg/m, a decrease of 18.1 %. It also reduces the wear rate of multi-pass laser cladding coatings from 0.0987 mg/m to 0.0859 mg/m, a decrease of 12.9 %. Additionally, high levels of residual tensile stress can promote the initiation and propagation of cracks within the coating. These cracks not only reduce the overall strength of the coating but also serve as initiation points for wear, accelerating the wear process. As a result, residual tensile stress increases the wear rate and decreases the wear resistance of the coating. Ultrasonic vibration helps to reduce the friction coefficient and wear loss of composite coatings.

Fig. 11（A）and (B) shows the wear morphology of the multi-track laser-clad coating. The primary wear mechanisms observed are abrasive and adhesive wear. In the coating without ultrasonic vibration assistance, the sliding direction's shear force causes surface spalling, with deeper and denser grooves [31]. In contrast, the ultrasonic-assisted sample shows shallower grooves and reduced surface spalling. The deeper grooves in the non-ultrasonic sample indicate a higher wear rate, consistent with Fig. 10（C）. The lower hardness in the non-ultrasonic coating results in deeper grooves during wear, while ultrasonic vibration improves hardness, leading to shallower grooves.

Analysis of the corrosion resistance of the Fe-Ni-Ti cladding layer

Fig. 12(A) shows the dynamic potential polarization curves of the cladding layer in 3.5 % sodium chloride simulated seawater solution. Both samples, with and without ultrasonic assistance, exhibit similar curve trends. The open-circuit potential (OCP) of the non-ultrasonic cladding is −0.915 V, while the ultrasonic-assisted cladding has an OCP of −0.903 V, indicating that ultrasonic vibration enhances the corrosion resistance. The fitted corrosion current density of the non-ultrasonic cladding is 9.16 μA/cm2, whereas the ultrasonic-assisted cladding has a reduced current density of 8.26 μA/cm2, suggesting a lower corrosion rate and improved corrosion resistance with ultrasonic assistance.

Impedance plots of multi-pass overlay composite coatings with and without ultrasound: （A）polarization curve; （B）(C) Bode plot; （D） Nyquist plot （Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy revealed that under ultrasonic action, the multi-pass sample surface exhibited a larger capacitive impedance arc, impedance modulus, and phase angle.）

Electrochemical corrosion is closer to real environmental conditions, where metals are immersed in electrolyte solutions, and corrosion occurs through reactions at the electrodes. Electrochemical testing is employed to study the corrosion resistance of composite coatings. Fig. 10 depicts the impedance plots of multi-pass overlay composite coatings with and without ultrasound. Fig. 12(B) and (C) show the Bode plots of samples with and without ultrasound, while Fig. 12(D) displays the Nyquist curves under the influence of ultrasound. According to electrochemical theory, a larger phase angle in the Bode plot indicates better corrosion resistance of the composite coating, while a larger capacitive arc radius in the Nyquist plot signifies better corrosion resistance of the composite coating. From the Bode plot, it can be observed that the addition of ultrasound vibration to the cladding layer results in larger peaks in both magnitude and phase angle, indicating better corrosion resistance [32]. Moreover, the Nyquist plot reveals that the capacitive arc radius of the cladding layer with ultrasound is the largest, further confirming the better corrosion resistance of the coating with the addition of ultrasound vibration.

Ultrasonic vibration effectively enhances the corrosion resistance of the cladding layer by refining grain size, suppressing segregation, and promoting uniform distribution of elements. Grain refinement increases the number of grain boundaries per unit volume, which contributes to the improvement of corrosion resistance in the cladding layer [33,34]. Ultrasonic suppression of segregation ensures a more uniform distribution of elements in the cladding layer, which is crucial for enhancing its corrosion resistance. Additionally, residual tensile stress in the coating can easily induce corrosion cracks in corrosive environments. These cracks act as pathways for corrosive agents to penetrate, accelerating the corrosion process and thereby reducing the coating's corrosion resistance.

Conclusions

This study investigates the morphology, hardness, wear resistance, and corrosion resistance of single-pass and multi-pass overlay coatings of Fe-Ni-Ti composite on the surface of 316 L steel, with and without ultrasonic assistance.

(1)The cavitation effect of ultrasound refined the grains of the cladding layer, reducing the average grain size of the single-pass cladding layer from 10.3 μm to 3.8 μm. After applying ultrasonic vibration, the crystal distribution in the overlay area of the cladding layer became more uniform, with significantly reduced size, and the arrangement of subgrains within dendrites became finer and denser.

The cavitation effect of ultrasound refined the grains of the cladding layer, reducing the average grain size of the single-pass cladding layer from 10.3 μm to 3.8 μm. After applying ultrasonic vibration, the crystal distribution in the overlay area of the cladding layer became more uniform, with significantly reduced size, and the arrangement of subgrains within dendrites became finer and denser.

(2)After the addition of ultrasonic vibration, the residual tensile and compressive stress in the coating samples decreased accordingly. Additionally, the microhardness of both single-pass and multi-pass cladding layers increased significantly after ultrasonic vibration was applied. Specifically, the average hardness of the single-pass cladding layer without ultrasonic vibration was 515 HV, with a 37 % increase in microhardness. Similarly, the average hardness of the overlapping cladding layer was 460 HV, with an 8.2 % increase in microhardness.

After the addition of ultrasonic vibration, the residual tensile and compressive stress in the coating samples decreased accordingly. Additionally, the microhardness of both single-pass and multi-pass cladding layers increased significantly after ultrasonic vibration was applied. Specifically, the average hardness of the single-pass cladding layer without ultrasonic vibration was 515 HV, with a 37 % increase in microhardness. Similarly, the average hardness of the overlapping cladding layer was 460 HV, with an 8.2 % increase in microhardness.

(3)After the addition of ultrasonic vibration, the fluctuation of friction coefficient in the cladding layer decreased, becoming more stable. Additionally, the wear rate of the single-pass sample decreased by 18.1 %, and that of the multi-pass sample decreased by 12.9 %. The current density of the ultrasonic-assisted coating decreased to 8.26 μA/m2, indicating a lower corrosion rate and improved corrosion resistance under ultrasonic assistance.

After the addition of ultrasonic vibration, the fluctuation of friction coefficient in the cladding layer decreased, becoming more stable. Additionally, the wear rate of the single-pass sample decreased by 18.1 %, and that of the multi-pass sample decreased by 12.9 %. The current density of the ultrasonic-assisted coating decreased to 8.26 μA/m2, indicating a lower corrosion rate and improved corrosion resistance under ultrasonic assistance.
