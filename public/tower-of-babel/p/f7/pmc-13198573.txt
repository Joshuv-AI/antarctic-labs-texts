Pain Reduction Improves Adherence, Quality of Life, and Work Capacity in Rheumatoid Arthritis Patients Receiving Baricitinib: Czech ATTRA Registry Analysis
Authors: Karel Pavelka, Ladislav Šenolt, Iva Váchová, Ewa Haladyj, Lenka Klementová, Jana Šilarová, Jakub Závada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite achieving targets of inflammation reduction, many patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) report persistent pain and reduced quality of life (QoL). Biologic and targeted RA therapies produce similar improvements in disease activity, but varying improvements in pain. Baricitinib is a Janus kinase inhibitor that has demonstrated greater and more pain relief compared to either methotrexate or adalimumab.MethodsThis study examines 24-month registry data from real-world patients receiving baricitinib in the Czech Republic. The aim was to determine the impact of baseline pain intensity on treatment survival and its correlation with QoL and work capacity.ResultsData from 392 patients (83.2% women) were analysed, with the majority (75.8%) having prior experience with biologic/targeted therapeutics. At baseline, disease activity (mean Disease Activity Score 28 Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate [DAS28-ESR] of 5.6 [± 1.2]) and pain Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) (61.2 [± 22.5] mm) were high. Mean DAS28-ESR decreased to 3.3 (± 1.1) after 3 months and 2.7 (± 1.0) after 24 months; mean pain VAS decreased to 36.4 (± 23.4) mm and 30.8 (± 22.7) mm. We observed negative correlations between baseline pain VAS and treatment survival, and positive correlations between baseline pain VAS and work/activity impairment (strong in all components [ρ = 0.73–0.78] except absenteeism [absence from work, ρ = 0.20]). Pain VAS was also negatively correlated with QoL at all timepoints (p < 0.001).ConclusionTreatment with baricitinib provided sustained pain reduction and decreased disease activity in patients with RA. Findings highlight the need for pain-focused treatment goals and suggest earlier intervention may enhance persistence and QoL. Work limitations persisted, highlighting the need for flexible employment policies.

Introduction

Although remission is a key target in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a proportion of patients find that pain and the associated reductions in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) persist even after the achievement of treatment targets of inflammation reduction [1, 2]. Pain is the most common RA symptom and is often cited by patients as their primary concern when seeking treatment, likely due to the major impact of pain on HRQoL [2, 3]. It has gradually become evident that the pain associated with RA has multifactorial causes that extend beyond inflammation alone [1]. Many patients who achieve treatment targets for inflammation reduction continue to experience moderate‑to‑severe pain [2]. This residual pain is often linked to factors such as anxiety, depression, and fatigue, which can negatively affect treatment adherence, quality of life (QoL), and work capacity [2, 3].

The treatment of RA has undergone a revolution in the last 25 years, first with the introduction of biologic (b) and targeted synthetic (ts) disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and second with the adoption of a treat-to-target approach in clinical practice [4–6]. The treat-to-target approach calls for regular assessment of inflammatory disease activity—primarily via composite scores—and making corresponding adjustments in treatment strategy, ultimately targeting full and rapid elimination of inflammation (i.e. remission) [5]. Remission has been the main goal because a lack of inflammation was critical for inhibiting the progression of structural damage in patients with RA [5]. With structural damage now better controlled through modern treatment strategies, clinicians and patients can increasingly prioritize optimizing pain control and enhancing long-term HRQoL and functional outcomes [6].

The need to prevent structural damage and long-term functional decline was identified because uncontrolled inflammation is the main driver of joint destruction [5]. However, current understanding is that pain is a complex set of neural, humoral, and emotional events, involving a complex interplay of noxious mediators, inflammation, peripheral and central sensitization, and synaptic remodelling [7]. By some estimates, treatment-driven improvements in disease inflammatory activity may only account for 40% of the improvement in patient-reported pain [8]. Even for patients with sustained inflammation control (i.e. those in remission), many continue to experience moderate to severe residual pain [7]. Moreover, despite having distinctly different mechanisms of action, targeted and biologic RA therapies have been shown to produce similar improvements in composite scores of disease activity—but varying improvements in pain [2].

In the present analysis, we evaluated data from Czech patients with RA receiving baricitinib treatment to improve the field’s understanding of the real-world impact of pain on RA treatment outcomes. Baricitinib is an oral selective and reversible inhibitor of Janus kinase (JAK) 1 and 2 with widespread approval for the treatment of adult patients with moderate to severe RA. An extensive series of publications has demonstrated that, relative to methotrexate (MTX) or the tumour necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) adalimumab, baricitinib provides significantly greater improvements in patient-reported pain and helps patients attain clinically meaningful thresholds of improvement in pain and physical health significantly more rapidly [8–10]. A multiple mediation analysis comparing the effect of each treatment on the pain specifically attributable to inflammation (calculated using the change in C-reactive protein [CRP], erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], and swollen joint count [SJC]) to the effect on pain as a whole found that, although the effects of each treatment were remarkably similar on inflammation, the effect of baricitinib on patient-reported pain was nearly double that of adalimumab [9].

Methods

Objectives

Our aim in conducting this analysis was to answer three predefined questions regarding real-world patients with RA: (1) What is the impact of baseline pain intensity on baricitinib treatment survival/adherence? (2) How does pain intensity correlate with quality of life (QoL) and work capacity, respectively, before and throughout baricitinib treatment? (3) Does the baricitinib-induced reduction in RA pain correlate with improved QoL and increased ability to work? Throughout this report, we adhere to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [11].

Study Design

Data for analysis came from ATTRA, a collection of clinical registries monitoring the real-world use of biologic treatments across rheumatology centres in the Czech Republic. Overseen by the Czech Rheumatological Society, this valuable source of real-world evidence (RWE) was updated in 2012 to meet the rigorous standards set forth by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) for the collection of registry data regarding b/tsDMARDs use [12].

ATTRA registry data were collected across 55 centres in an open-label, retrospective process. All patients signed informed consent forms prior to the inclusion of their data in the registry. Patients diagnosed with RA by the treating physician met the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria for RA and exhibited a minimum of intermediate RA disease activity (Disease Activity Score 28 [DAS28] CRP ≥ 3.2). All baricitinib-treated patients within ATTRA were included in this analysis. Baricitinib was prescribed at a dose of 4 mg orally once daily by a clinical rheumatologist as either a first-line biologic after inadequate response to MTX or as a second- or higher-line biologic after failure of one or more b/tsDMARDs (e.g. a TNFi). csDMARD treatment was not fixed during the observational course of the study. Patients continued their csDMARDs as per routine clinical practice, and adjustments were allowed at the discretion of the treating physician, with study participation not influencing clinical decision-making.

Follow-up examinations were conducted at 3- to 6-month intervals for 2 years after initiation of baricitinib. At each visit, patients were evaluated for treatment survival, change in DAS28 (ESR), pain VAS, Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ), EuroQol-5D instrument [13], or Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) [14], and achievement of remission or Low Disease Activity (LDA) status.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized with mean and standard deviation (SD) or median with 5th and 95th percentiles for continuous variables (DAS28, pain VAS, HAQ, EQ-5D, WPAI) and percentages for categorical variables (status of remission or LDA). The magnitude of change in pain VAS was analysed using the paired Wilcoxon test with a Bonferroni correction. Correlational analyses utilized Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) to compare selected parameters. Treatment survival was evaluated via the Kaplan–Meier estimation method; differences in the probability of treatment survival were assessed using the log-rank test. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and R (version 4.2.2) with a type 1 error threshold of 0.05.

Ethics Statement

This study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice, the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and local laws and regulations in the Czech Republic. All patients provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the registry, and permissions were obtained to access and analyse registry data.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The final sample was comprised of registry data from 392 patients receiving real-world treatment with baricitinib, of whom 83.2% were women. Median (5th; 95th percentile) disease duration was 10 (1; 30) years and mean age at treatment initiation was 57 (± 11) years. Overall, 74.5% were seropositive for rheumatoid factor (RF+) and 74.2% were positive for anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (anti-CCP+). Prior to initiating baricitinib, 97.7% of the sample were using conventional synthetic (cs)DMARDs, with 93.1% specifically using MTX, and 91.6% of the sample were using glucocorticoids. Most (75.8%) of the patients had previously tried a different b/tsDMARD, while 24.2% were biologic-naïve.

At initiation of baricitinib (the index date), RA disease activity was high (mean DAS28-ESR of 5.6 [± 1.2]; DAS28 > 5.1 in 71.2% of patients). Patients exhibited median CRP levels of 10.6 (0.6; 61.1) mg/L and a median ESR of 25.0 (4.0; 76.0) mm/h. Mean SJC and tender joint counts (TJC) were 7.7 (± 5.1) and 11.3 (± 6.1), respectively. Mean pain VAS was 61.2 (± 22.5) mm. Nearly two-thirds (62.8%) of patients received concomitant baricitinib and csDMARDs, with 79.6% of those receiving MTX. More than half (55.6%) received concomitant glucocorticoids (Table 1). Median follow-up time for the overall sample (N = 392) was 15.6 months or a total of 624.7 patient-years. Treatment Survival

At 12 months, 71.6% of patients remained on baricitinib and 57.1% at 24 months. Median survival on treatment was 35.8 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 26.7–44.9), reflecting treatment survival in the full cohort. The length of treatment survival was negatively correlated with baseline pain (i.e. greater pain at onset was associated with shorter treatment survival); this correlation was seen both when evaluating pain in groups of 20-mm intervals (0–20, > 20–40, > 40–60, > 60–80, and > 80–100; Fig. 1, p < 0.05) and when comparing those who started below or above the acceptable pain threshold of 40 mm (0–40 vs > 40–100 mm, p < 0.05) [1, 10].Fig. 1All data presented as n (%) unless otherwise stated. *Treatment discontinuation was considered an event. Treatment Effectiveness

In the as‑observed analysis (the patients remaining on baricitinib), mean DAS28-ESR decreased from 5.6 (± 1.2) at baseline to 3.3 (± 1.1) after 3 months and 2.7 (± 1.0) after 24 months of real-world baricitinib treatment. Within 3 months, 30.5% of patients had achieved remission (DAS28-ESR < 2.6) and an additional 15.4% achieved a status of LDA (2.6 ≤ DAS28-ESR ≤ 3.2); by month 24, 47.5% were in remission and 23.8% were at LDA. Mean pain VAS decreased from 61.2 (± 22.5) to 36.4 (± 23.4) mm after 3 months of treatment with baricitinib; by 24 months, it decreased further to 30.8 (± 22.7) mm (Table 2). Ad Hoc Analysis of Patients with High Baseline Pain (VAS > 80 mm)

A small subset of patients entered treatment with high levels of pain at baseline (VAS > 80 mm; n = 61/392). Their baseline disease activity was higher than the overall cohort, details are provided in Table 1. These observations are descriptive only and should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and attrition over time.

Correlation of Pain with Work Capacity and QoL

Correlational analysis of the overall sample revealed that, prior to treatment initiation, pain was positively correlated with all components of the WPAI, but to differing degrees. Presenteeism (the presence of limitations in work performance; ρ = 0.76, p < 0.001), total work restriction (ρ = 0.73, p < 0.001), and activity restriction (ρ = 0.78, p < 0.001) were all strongly correlated with baseline pain VAS; in contrast, a notably weaker correlation was observed between baseline pain VAS and absenteeism (i.e. pain was least correlated with absence from work; ρ = 0.20, p < 0.01).

This differential association continued throughout the following 2 years of treatment; as mean pain VAS decreased, it remained strongly and significantly correlated with all WPAI components except absenteeism (Table 2). We also evaluated the relationship between pain VAS and QoL (as assessed by the EQ-5D), and observed a significant, negative impact of pain on QoL across all timepoints (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Treatment Safety

Across the 624.7 patient-years of follow-up, 135 adverse events (AE) occurred, of which the majority were infections (n = 79). Approximately 20.7% (n = 28) of all AE were classified as serious, of which the majority were infections (n = 8) and cardiovascular manifestations (n = 7). No thromboembolic manifestations were observed, and no new toxicity or safety signals were identified that have not been previously observed in published baricitinib studies to date.

Discussion

The real-world effectiveness of baricitinib observed in the ATTRA registry corresponds with findings from randomized clinical trials; both pain and disease activity decreased following the initiation of baricitinib in those patients who responded adequately and did not experience tolerability issues. Treatment survival was negatively associated with baseline pain, prompting further examination of patients with high initial pain. This finding suggests that patients with very high baseline pain may represent a difficult-to-treat (D2T) population with lower persistence on therapy. Recognizing this subgroup underscores the importance of early intervention and comprehensive pain management strategies in clinical practice. Throughout the study, pain remained strongly correlated with all WPAI domains, indicating that patients continued working despite reporting pain‑related impacts on work performance.

The pattern of pain improvement that has been observed following baricitinib treatment here and in clinical trials is important, as over two-thirds of patients with RA report that pain relief is their primary treatment goal [3]. Given that any pain VAS value ≤ 40 mm of remaining pain is defined as within the ‘patient-acceptable symptom state’ [1, 15], most patients included in the present analysis had achieved satisfactory pain levels and typically did so within 3 months of initiating real-world baricitinib treatment. However, it has been suggested that not all DMARDs are equally effective at controlling pain. For example, in the RA-BEAM trial, better pain control was achieved in the group receiving baricitinib + MTX than adalimumab + MTX [9]. Therefore, it is possible that JAK1/2 inhibition may have an antinociceptive effect independent of the anti-inflammatory effect, possibly through inhibition of granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), which signals through JAK2 [9]. In an animal model of osteoarthritis, inhibition of GM-CSF has been shown to suppress pain [16]. Alternatively, the independent antinociceptive effect may be stemming from other aspects of the JAK signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway; for example, in animal models, intrathecal administration of a JAK2 antagonist blocked STAT3 phosphorylation and reduced both mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia [16].

One of the questions examined was how baseline pain correlates with treatment survival. After observing an unexpected negative correlation, we investigated the subgroup of patients with very high baseline pain levels (VAS > 80 mm). This group showed higher disease activity and a notably different distribution of prior b/tsDMARD treatment experience with many having received three or more prior b/tsDMARDs. On the basis of this extensive treatment history, many of these patients would likely meet the criteria for D2T-RA [12] and may explain their lower persistence on therapy.

Another key question being addressed was how pain correlates with QoL and the ability to work, and how the reduction in pain following baricitinib treatment can improve work capacity. It is also important to acknowledge that, in this population with long‑standing RA, some functional limitations may reflect cumulative structural joint damage or residual disease activity rather than pain alone [5]; however, the ATTRA registry does not capture data that allow these contributors to be quantified. Our data support previous findings regarding the relative influence of pain and fatigue on work productivity during baricitinib treatment [2]. However, we have the additional level of detail regarding which components of work capacity are affected and to what degree; our data indicate that while pain remains strongly correlated with presenteeism (presence of limitations), total work restriction, and activity restriction, it does not correlate strongly with absenteeism (absence from work). In other words, despite experiencing limitations due to their pain, patients are not absent from work. During treatment with baricitinib, the correlations between pain and limitations/impairment remained strong; given that patient-reported pain decreased significantly with treatment, performance is likely to have improved in a corresponding manner.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the ATTRA registry captures data only from the initiation of b/tsDMARD therapy onward, meaning disease duration may include many years of prior csDMARD use. Delayed referral to specialist centres, incomplete diagnosis, or prolonged reliance on csDMARDs may therefore explain why some patients with long‑standing RA were b/tsDMARD‑naïve or had received only one prior therapy.

Secondly, this study did not calculate p values or apply alternative statistical methods to compare baseline characteristics (such as the proportion of b/tsDMARD-naïve patients or those with prior b/tsDMARD exposure) between groups. While such analyses could provide additional insights and potentially alter interpretation, they were beyond the scope and resources of this study. Moreover, most real-world registries, including ours, do not routinely capture the level of detail required for these comparisons. In addition, the study did not include comparative analyses with other bDMARDs or tsDMARDs, and the absence of such comparisons represents a further limitation of the available registry data.

A further major limitation is the absence of detailed data on concurrent pain management strategies, including the use of NSAIDs, narcotics, gabapentin, and other analgesics, as well as changes in csDMARDs during the observation period. While glucocorticoid use was captured and reported, most real-world registries, including ours, do not routinely collect comprehensive information on these medications. This restricts interpretation of pain outcomes and treatment persistence, as concomitant therapies may influence both.

It is also plausible that treatment survival was negatively correlated with lack of clinical response, as patients with insufficient improvement in disease activity are generally more likely to discontinue therapy. Although this relationship was not formally tested in our analysis, it is consistent with patterns observed in real‑world RA registries, where inadequate response and adverse events are the most frequent reasons for discontinuation. In the ATTRA dataset, among the 163 patients who discontinued baricitinib, the primary reasons were loss of effect/secondary failure (38.7%), primary inefficacy (26.4%), and adverse events (15.3%). Less common reasons included patient death (3.1%), inability to contact the patient (1.2%), pharmacoeconomic factors (0.6%), and other causes (14.7%). Approximately half of the patients remained on baricitinib at 2 years, and the improvements in disease activity and HRQoL reported reflect outcomes among those who continued treatment. These findings should not be interpreted as representative of all patients initiating therapy, as individuals who did not achieve adequate response or experienced tolerability issues were more likely to discontinue treatment.

Additionally, the analysis compared patients with very high baseline pain (VAS > 80 mm) to the overall cohort, rather than stratifying patients into clinically meaningful pain severity groups (e.g. mild, moderate, severe). Such stratification could provide deeper insights into dose–response relationships between pain intensity and treatment outcomes, but was beyond the scope of this study and will be considered in future work.

Furthermore, this was an observational, retrospective study, which is a study design that will always be hampered by non-randomization and the potential for incomplete information; however, our findings here are consistent with those within a controlled trial environment. We only assessed pain through the patient-rated pain VAS metric, in which patients are asked to indicate their current level of pain on a 100-mm continuum between ‘no pain’ and ‘the worst pain imaginable’ [9]. There is ongoing discussion regarding the limitations of this tool, as it does not provide information regarding the location or type of pain; however, the VAS is well validated and sensitive to change [10].

Lastly, this study was exclusively conducted in the Czech Republic rather than a variety of nations. However, the results are unlikely to be geographically specific; for example, our results are highly similar to the recent Spanish observational study ORBIT-RA, in which remission or LDA status as assessed by DAS28-ESR was achieved in 73.5% of the study sample after 12 months of treatment [4] compared to 68.2% of the ATTRA sample within the same timeframe.

Conclusion

Optimizing long-term HRQoL for patients with RA may require shifting clinical focus from composite disease activity scores to specific measures of pain improvement. A proportion of patients receiving baricitinib reported reductions in pain alongside improvements in disease activity. These observations are consistent with prior evidence suggesting that JAK inhibition may be associated with greater pain improvement compared to TNFi, potentially extending beyond inflammation control [10]. These findings reinforce the importance of incorporating pain-focused outcomes into treatment goals and suggest that earlier intervention may improve persistence and QoL. Importantly, while baricitinib treatment supported continued patient participation in the workforce, significant limitations in work intensity persisted which may be related to functional limitations due to joint destruction in patients with long-standing active RA [5]. This highlights the need for supportive employment and social policies tailored to chronic rheumatic patients, such as flexible working hours, remote work opportunities, adapted workplaces, and access to assistive devices.
