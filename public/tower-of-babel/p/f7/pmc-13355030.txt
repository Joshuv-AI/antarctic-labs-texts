An SSK1-loaded decellularized annulus fibrosus matrix-based PD hydrogel alleviates intervertebral disc degeneration through modulation of NF-κB signaling and ferroptosis
Authors: Guantong Sun, Qingyu Yao, Xiangchen He, Julong Lu, Jianing Yu, Xianhao Sheng, Derong Xu, Chuanli Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a major cause of low back pain and is closely associated with inflammatory activation, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, cellular senescence, and ferroptosis in nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs). In this study, we aimed to clarify the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of SSK1 and to develop an injectable SSK1-loaded hydrogel for localized and sustained treatment of IDD.MethodsAn interleukin-1β (IL-1β)-induced NPC degeneration model was established to evaluate ECM metabolism, ferroptosis, senescence, and inflammatory signaling. Ferroptosis-related rescue experiments, pharmacological modulation of NF-κB and PPARγ, and PPARγ knockdown were performed to investigate the underlying mechanism. SSK1 was incorporated into a poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate/decellularized annulus fibrosus matrix hydrogel (SSK1@PD). The physicochemical properties, cytocompatibility, sustained-release behavior, and therapeutic efficacy of SSK1@PD were evaluated in vitro and in a puncture-induced rat model of IDD using radiographic, histological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical analyses.ResultsSSK1 markedly attenuated IL-1β-induced degenerative changes in NPCs, including ECM degradation, iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, GPX4 loss, and cellular senescence. Mechanistically, SSK1 restored PPARγ expression and suppressed NF-κB activation. PPARγ silencing largely abolished the protective effects of SSK1, whereas pharmacological activation of PPARγ partially reproduced them. The SSK1@PD hydrogel exhibited favorable physicochemical properties, good cytocompatibility, and sustained drug release. In vivo, local injection of SSK1@PD preserved disc height and histological architecture, improved ECM homeostasis, and reduced ferroptosis-, senescence-, and NF-κB-related changes in degenerated discs.ConclusionSSK1@PD alleviates IDD by enabling sustained local delivery of SSK1 and modulating degenerative cellular responses, at least in part through the PPARγ/NF-κB/ferroptosis axis. This injectable biomaterial-based platform represents a promising minimally invasive therapeutic strategy for IDD.The translational potential of this articleIn this study, we propose an injectable hydrogel system for localized delivery of SSK1 to treat intervertebral disc degeneration. By targeting ferroptosis and inflammation while enabling sustained drug release, this strategy effectively alleviates disc degeneration in vivo. The combination of mechanistic targeting and biomaterial-based delivery highlights its potential as a minimally invasive and clinically translatable therapy for IDD.Keywords: Decellularized matrix, Ferroptosis, Hydrogel, Intervertebral disc degeneration, NF-κB signaling, SSK1

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide and represents a major socioeconomic burden on modern healthcare systems [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Among its diverse etiologies, intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is widely recognized as a principal pathological basis for chronic and recurrent LBP [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. IDD is characterized by progressive extracellular matrix (ECM) breakdown, disc dehydration, structural disorganization, and NPC dysfunction, ultimately leading to mechanical instability and chronic pain [9]. Current clinical management of IDD is largely limited to symptomatic relief, including analgesics, rehabilitation, and surgical intervention, rather than to biological restoration of the degenerated disc [10]. Therefore, the development of disease-modifying strategies capable of halting or reversing disc degeneration remains an urgent unmet clinical need.

Increasing evidence indicates that IDD is not merely a passive wear-and-tear process but rather a complex pathological cascade involving inflammatory activation, oxidative stress, cell fate dysregulation, and matrix catabolism [11,12]. In particular, persistent stimulation by pro-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) disrupts anabolic–catabolic balance in NPCs, accelerates senescence-associated phenotypes, and amplifies degenerative remodeling of the disc microenvironment [13]. Beyond apoptosis and necroptosis, ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation and redox collapse—has recently emerged as a critical driver of degenerative diseases, including IDD [[14], [15], [16]]. Ferroptotic injury in NPCs is closely associated with iron overload, depletion of glutathione (GSH), inactivation of GPX4, and excessive accumulation of lipid peroxides, all of which converge to aggravate matrix degradation and disc dysfunction. Notably, inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB are deeply intertwined with ferroptosis-related cellular injury, suggesting that simultaneous modulation of inflammatory signaling and ferroptotic stress may provide a mechanistically powerful therapeutic avenue for IDD [17,18].

Targeting pathological cell states has become an increasingly attractive strategy in degenerative disease intervention. Senescence-associated cells and stress-damaged cells are known to reshape tissue microenvironments through the release of inflammatory and catabolic mediators, thereby reinforcing local degeneration [19]. SSK1, a galactose-modified prodrug previously developed for selective targeting of senescence-associated cells, has shown broad biological activity in suppressing chronic inflammation and ameliorating age-related tissue dysfunction [20,21]. These features make SSK1 a compelling candidate for degenerative musculoskeletal disorders in which senescence-like phenotypes, inflammatory activation, and stress-induced cell injury coexist. However, whether SSK1 can protect NPCs from degeneration-associated ferroptotic injury, and whether such effects are mechanistically linked to NF-κB signaling, remains unknown. The dense avascular structure of the intervertebral disc greatly limits systemic drug accessibility, making local and sustained delivery indispensable for achieving meaningful therapeutic efficacy.

Biomaterial-assisted local therapy provides an attractive solution to this challenge. Injectable hydrogels are particularly promising for disc regeneration because they can be delivered in a minimally invasive manner, conform to irregular defect spaces, and serve as depots for sustained drug release [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Among candidate biomaterials, decellularized ECM (dECM) is particularly appealing because it preserves tissue-specific biochemical cues, including matrix proteins and glycosaminoglycans (GAG), thereby recapitulating key features of the native regenerative microenvironment [[26], [27], [28]]. For annulus fibrosus (AF)- and disc-related applications, decellularized annulus fibrosus matrix (DAFM) offers intrinsic compositional relevance and favorable biological activity. Nevertheless, dECM-derived hydrogels alone often suffer from poor mechanical robustness and rapid degradation, which restrict their translational potential in the demanding biomechanical environment of the spine. To overcome these limitations, integration with synthetic polymers has emerged as a rational strategy to reinforce hydrogel stability while preserving biological functionality. Poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA), owing to its excellent biocompatibility, tunable crosslinking behavior, and structural versatility, is well suited to construct mechanically enhanced composite hydrogels for localized therapeutic delivery [29].

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that SSK1 could alleviate IDD-associated cellular injury by modulating the PPARγ/NF-κB/ferroptosis axis and that its incorporation into a PEGDA/DAFM-based hydrogel would enable sustained local delivery and enhance its therapeutic efficacy. To test this hypothesis, an SSK1-loaded PEGDA/DAFM hydrogel (SSK1@PD) was developed as an injectable local therapeutic platform for IDD. The ability of SSK1 to alleviate IL-1β-induced degenerative injury in NPCs was investigated, and whether its protective effects were associated with the regulation of the PPARγ/NF-κB/ferroptosis axis was explored. A PEGDA/DAFM-based hydrogel was fabricated to improve the local retention and sustained release of SSK1, and its structural, physicochemical, and biological properties were systematically characterized. Finally, the therapeutic efficacy of SSK1@PD was evaluated in a rat model of IDD through histological, radiographic, and molecular analyses. By integrating mechanism-guided pharmacological intervention with tissue-specific biomaterial engineering, this work establishes a localized therapeutic strategy for disc degeneration and provides a promising translational framework for biomaterial-enabled treatment of IDD.

Materials and methods

NPCs isolation, culture, and treatment

Human NPCs were isolated from intervertebral disc tissues obtained from patients undergoing spinal surgery for traumatic spinal cord injury, during which disc excision was required as part of the surgical procedure (age range, 18-60 years). Rat NPCs were isolated from the caudal intervertebral discs of 8-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats, and mouse NPCs were isolated from the caudal intervertebral discs of 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Human, rat, and mouse NPCs were used for the cross-species cell viability analysis shown in Fig. 2B. Unless otherwise specified, rat NPCs were used for all subsequent in vitro experiments. Briefly, nucleus pulposus tissues were carefully dissected, cut into small pieces, and enzymatically digested in 0.1% type II collagenase at 37 °C for 4 h. After digestion, the cell suspension was collected using centrifugation, and the obtained cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C, and passage 2 cells were used for all in vitro experiments.

To establish an in vitro model of degenerative injury, NPCs were stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng/mL) for 24 h unless otherwise specified. To assess the biological effects of SSK1, IL-1β-treated NPCs were incubated with SSK1 at different concentrations (0.5, 1, and 5 μM) for 24 h. Based on the concentration-dependent results, 5 μM SSK1 was used in the subsequent mechanistic experiments unless otherwise specified. For mechanistic studies, cells were additionally treated with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1, the ferroptosis activator erastin, the NF-κB inhibitor NF-κB-IN-1 (50 μM, MCE, HY-138537), or the NF-κB activator PMA (100 nM, MCE, HY-18739), either alone or in combination with SSK1. In hydrogel-related experiments, NPCs were cultured in the presence of DAFM, PD hydrogel, or SSK1@PD under IL-1β stimulation. Untreated NPCs served as the control group.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using a CCK8 assay. Briefly, human, rat, and mouse NPCs under normal or senescence-inducing conditions were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to different concentrations of SSK1 for the indicated period. Senescence-associated conditions included IL-1β, H2O2, etoposide, or replication-induced senescence. At the end of treatment, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing CCK8 reagent, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C according to the manufacturer's protocol. The optical density was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Wells containing reagent; however, no cells served as the background control, and relative cell viability was calculated after normalization to the corresponding untreated group.

Live/dead staining

To evaluate the cytocompatibility of the hydrogel materials, NPCs were cultured with DAFM or PD hydrogel under the indicated conditions. After incubation, cell viability was assessed using a live/dead cell staining kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the samples were incubated with the staining working solution under light-protected conditions, followed by washing with PBS. Fluorescence images were then captured using a fluorescence microscope. Live and dead cells were identified according to the corresponding fluorescent signals, and the staining results were used to qualitatively assess the biocompatibility of the hydrogel materials.

EdU and TUNEL staining

To assess the effects of different treatments on NPC proliferation, an EdU incorporation assay was performed using a commercial EdU staining kit following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, NPCs were incubated with EdU working solution for the indicated time, then fixed, permeabilized, and subjected to the click chemistry reaction in the dark. To evaluate apoptosis, TUNEL staining was carried out using a commercial detection kit. After treatment, NPCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated with TUNEL reaction solution at 37 °C under light-protected conditions. After completion of EdU or TUNEL staining, nuclei were labeled with DAPI. Images were acquired under a confocal microscope, and the proportions of proliferating and apoptotic cells were determined by calculating the percentages of EdU-positive and TUNEL-positive cells, respectively.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

Total RNA was extracted from treated NPCs using a commercial RNA isolation kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and purity of RNA were determined prior to reverse transcription. Complementary DNA was synthesized from purified RNA using a reverse transcription reagent kit, and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was subsequently carried out using a SYBR Green-based detection system. The amplification procedure consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting

After the indicated treatments, NPCs were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for protein quantification using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated using SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies against the target proteins. Following washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent and captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to the internal control.

Immunofluorescence staining

NPCs were seeded on sterile coverslips and processed after the indicated treatments. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and then blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the coverslips were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope, and signal intensity was analyzed with ImageJ software.

Detection of lipid peroxidation using BODIPY staining

To visualize intracellular lipid peroxidation, treated NPCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated with BODIPY 581/591 C11 in the dark for the indicated time. After washing, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired under a confocal microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

To evaluate the senescent phenotype, NPCs subjected to the indicated treatments were analyzed by senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining using a commercial kit in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with the staining fixative, and then incubated with SA-β-gal staining solution at 37 °C overnight. After staining, images were acquired under a light microscope, and the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells was determined by counting blue-stained cells in randomly selected fields.

Determination of ferroptosis-related biochemical indicators

To assess ferroptosis-associated biochemical changes, NPCs or intervertebral disc tissue samples were collected after the indicated treatments and processed according to the requirements of the corresponding assay kits. For cell-based assays, treated NPCs were harvested and lysed, whereas for in vivo experiments, disc tissues were homogenized on ice before centrifugation to obtain the supernatants for subsequent analyses. Lipid peroxidation was evaluated by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using a commercial assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The Fe2+ content and total iron levels were determined using the corresponding iron detection kits. In addition, the reduced GSH content was measured to assess intracellular antioxidant capacity. The absorbance values were recorded using a microplate reader at the indicated wavelengths, and the concentrations of each parameter were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves. The obtained values were normalized to the protein concentration of each sample where required. For fluorescence-based detection of Fe2+ in NPCs, cells were incubated with the FerroOrange probe under light-protected conditions at 37 °C, followed by washing with PBS. Fluorescence images were then captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

shRNA transfection

For loss-of-function experiments, NPCs were transfected with sh-PPARγ or sh-NC. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured under standard conditions until they reached approximately 70% confluence. The cells were then incubated with the indicated shRNA viral particles overnight, followed by replacement with fresh complete medium. After transfection, NPCs were subjected to the corresponding treatments.

RNA-seq and enrichment analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the indicated samples with TRIzol reagent following the standard procedure. RNA concentration and purity were measured, and RNA integrity was evaluated before library preparation. Sequencing libraries were constructed using a commercial RNA-seq library preparation kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to obtain paired-end reads. After quality control and removal of low-quality reads, the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression was quantified, and DEGs were identified using the DESeq2 package. The differential expression results were visualized using volcano plot analysis. To further explore the biological significance of these DEGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out, and significantly enriched functional categories and pathways were used for downstream interpretation of the transcriptomic data.

Preparation and characterization of DAFM

Fresh rat caudal AF tissue was harvested under sterile conditions, washed thoroughly with PBS, and cut into small fragments. The tissue pieces were sequentially treated with 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 24 h, nuclease solution overnight, and 1% Triton X-100 for at least 24 h under agitation to remove cellular components and residual nucleic acids. After extensive washing with PBS, the decellularized AF tissues were freeze-dried, ground into powder, and stored at −80 °C until use.

To confirm the effectiveness of decellularization, native AF tissue and DAFM were evaluated using H&E and DAPI staining to assess residual cells and nuclei. The remaining DNA content was further quantified using a commercial assay kit. In addition, the preservation of ECM components after decellularization was assessed by measuring GAG and collagen contents using the corresponding commercial kits. Collagen content was calculated based on the hydroxyproline level.

Preparation and morphological observation of PEGDA/DAFM hydrogels

To prepare the hydrogel precursor, DAFM powder was enzymatically digested in a pepsin-containing acidic solution under continuous stirring until a homogeneous suspension was obtained. After centrifugation to remove insoluble residues, the solution was neutralized on ice to generate the DAFM-based pre-gel solution. For fabrication of the hydrogel, the DAFM-derived solution was mixed with PEGDA and a photoinitiator to form a uniform precursor solution. The resulting PEGDA/DAFM hydrogel was abbreviated as PD hydrogel throughout this study. For the preparation of SSK1@PD, SSK1 was incorporated into the precursor solution at a final concentration of 5 μM before gelation. The mixture was subsequently crosslinked by 405 nm blue light irradiation to obtain a stable SSK1-loaded hydrogel.

The microstructure of DAFM and PD hydrogels was observed using SEM. Briefly, the samples were freeze-dried, sputter-coated with a conductive metal layer, and then imaged to evaluate their porous architecture and surface morphology.

Swelling behavior of hydrogels

To evaluate the swelling properties of the hydrogels, equal volumes (400 μL) of PD and SSK1@PD hydrogels were first freeze-dried and weighed to obtain their initial dry weight (W0). The dried samples were then immersed in PBS and incubated at 37 °C. At the indicated time points, the hydrogels were removed, and excess surface liquid was gently blotted away with filter paper before weighing to determine the swollen weight (Wt). The swelling ratio was calculated according to the following equation: Swelling ratio (%) = (Wt − W0)/W0 × 100%.

In vitro degradation assay

The degradation behavior of the hydrogels was assessed in vitro under physiological conditions. Briefly, 400 μL of PD and SSK1@PD hydrogels were prepared and weighed immediately to record the initial weight (W0). The samples were then incubated in PBS at 37 °C under gentle shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined time points, the hydrogels were collected, dried, and weighed again to obtain the remaining weight (Wt). The percentage of residual weight was determined using the following formula: Weight remaining (%) = Wt/W0 × 100%.

Compressive stress-strain analysis

The mechanical properties of the hydrogels were evaluated by compression testing. Briefly, DAFM, PD, and SSK1@PD hydrogel samples were prepared with identical dimensions and subjected to uniaxial compression using a mechanical testing instrument. The samples were compressed at a constant loading rate, and the corresponding load-displacement data were recorded continuously. Stress was calculated as the applied force divided by the initial cross-sectional area of the sample, and strain was defined as the change in sample height relative to its original height. The compressive stress–strain curves were then generated to compare the mechanical behavior of different hydrogel formulations.

In vitro release profile of SSK1 from hydrogels

To examine the release behavior of SSK1 from the hydrogel system, SSK1@PD samples (400 μL) were immersed in 5 mL of PBS and maintained at 37 °C in a shaking incubator at 100 rpm. At the predetermined time points, the entire release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The amount of released SSK1 in the collected supernatant was quantified using UV-Vis spectrophotometry at the corresponding absorption wavelength. The cumulative release percentage of SSK1 was calculated according to the following equation: Cumulative release (%) = Mt/M0 × 100%, where Mt represents the cumulative amount of SSK1 released at time t, and M0 represents the total amount of SSK1 initially loaded into the hydrogel.

Animals

Eight-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats were used to establish the in vivo model of IDD. All animal experiments were approved by the institutional animal ethics committee and performed in accordance with the relevant regulations. The animals were randomly divided into five groups: Sham, IDD, PD, SSK1, and SSK1@PD (n = 6 rats per group at each time point). After anesthesia, the coccygeal intervertebral discs were identified and exposed under sterile conditions. A puncture-induced IDD model was established by needle injury to the target disc. Subsequently, 10 μL of PD hydrogel, free SSK1 (5 μM per disc), or SSK1@PD hydrogel containing an equivalent amount of SSK1 was locally injected into the injured discs according to the experimental design, whereas the sham group underwent exposure without puncture. The skin incision was then sutured, and the animals were returned to routine housing. At 1 and 2 months after treatment, the rats were euthanized, and the caudal discs were harvested for X-ray, histological, immunohistochemical (IHC), immunofluorescence (IF), and biochemical analyses.

X-ray evaluation

At the indicated time points, rat tail segments were collected for X-ray imaging to evaluate disc height changes. The disc height index (DHI) was calculated using ImageJ software as the ratio of disc height to adjacent vertebral body height. The obtained DHI values were used to compare the degree of structural degeneration among the different groups.

Histological, IHC, and IF analyses

Harvested disc specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and decalcified in 10% EDTA before paraffin embedding. Serial sections (5 μm) were prepared for subsequent staining. General morphology and matrix preservation were evaluated using H&E and Safranin O/Fast Green staining, and histological scores were assigned based on a modified grading system for disc degeneration.

For IHC analysis, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were subjected to endogenous peroxidase blocking, antigen retrieval, and serum blocking, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, the sections were developed using DAB and counterstained.

For IF analysis, sections were incubated with antibodies, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were captured under a confocal laser scanning microscope. Positive staining was quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student's t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by the appropriate post hoc test, or the Kruskal–Wallis test when the data did not meet parametric assumptions. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All in vitro assays were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Results

IL-1β induces ferroptosis-, senescence-, and degeneration-related changes in NPCs

To establish an in vitro model of degenerative injury, NPCs were treated with different concentrations of IL-1β for the indicated time periods, and the expression of ferroptosis-related genes, senescence-associated genes, and inflammatory cytokines was subsequently evaluated. RT-qPCR analysis showed that IL-1β induced marked transcriptional alterations in NPCs in both a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1A–H). Particularly, the expression of ferroptosis-related genes ACSL4 and TF were significantly upregulated, whereas the expression of antioxidant and ferroptosis-protective genes GPX4, SLC3A2, and SLC7A11 were progressively downregulated after IL-1β stimulation. In parallel, senescence-associated genes, including P53, P21, P16, and LaminB1, were markedly dysregulated, accompanied by robust induction of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18. These findings indicate that IL-1β stimulation triggered a prominent inflammatory, ferroptotic, and senescence-associated response in NPCs.

We next examined whether these transcriptional changes were accompanied by alterations in matrix metabolism and protein expression. Western blot analysis showed that prolonged culture or increasing concentrations of IL-1β markedly reduced the expression of the anabolic ECM marker COL2 and the ferroptosis-defense protein GPX4, while increasing the levels of the catabolic marker MMP13, iron transport-related protein TF, and the senescence-related proteins P53 and P21 (Fig. 1I–L). These results suggest that IL-1β not only impaired ECM homeostasis but also promoted ferroptosis- and senescence-related changes at the protein level.

To further clarify the mode of cell injury induced by IL-1β, NPCs were treated with inhibitors targeting different forms of regulated cell death. Among them, Fer-1 produced the most evident restoration of COL2 expression and reduction of MMP13 expression, whereas inhibition of apoptosis or necroptosis had relatively limited effects (Fig. 1M). Consistently, IL-1β increased MDA, Fe2+, and total iron levels while decreasing GSH content (Fig. 1N and O). Together, these findings support the involvement of ferroptotic injury in the IL-1β-induced NPC degeneration model.

SSK1 exerts a conserved cross-species effect on senescence-associated NPCs and attenuates apoptosis- and degeneration-related phenotypes

To evaluate the biological effects of SSK1, its cytocompatibility was first examined in human, rat, and mouse NPCs under normal and senescence-inducing conditions. SSK1 exerted a broad and condition-dependent effect on NPCs subjected to different senescence-inducing stimuli. In human NPCs, SSK1 had little effect on non-senescent cells, with cell viability remaining close to baseline across the tested concentration range, whereas NPCs exposed to IL-1β, H2O2, etoposide, or replication-induced senescence were markedly more responsive to SSK1 treatment (Fig. 2B). Particularly, etoposide-induced human NPCs showed the highest sensitivity, with viability beginning to decline at 0.05 μM and reaching approximately 50% of baseline at higher concentrations. IL-1β- and H2O2-treated human NPCs also exhibited clear susceptibility to SSK1, with cell viability gradually decreasing to approximately 50%-60% as the concentration increased. Replication-induced human NPCs displayed a relatively milder but still evident response, with viability stabilizing at approximately 60% of baseline at higher doses.

To determine whether the activity of SSK1 differed among species, rat and mouse NPCs were further examined under the same treatment conditions. Similar to human NPCs, SSK1 caused minimal changes in non-senescent rat and mouse NPCs, whereas senescence-associated cells in both species were much more sensitive to treatment (Fig. 2B). In rat NPCs, SSK1 induced a gradual, concentration-dependent decline in viability across all senescence models, with more obvious effects emerging at 0.5-1 μM. At 5 μM, viability was reduced to approximately 50%-70% depending on the senescence trigger, with replication-induced cells showing the strongest response. In mouse NPCs, senescence-associated cells also showed pronounced susceptibility to SSK1. IL-1β-, H2O2-, and etoposide-treated mouse NPCs all exhibited substantial viability loss at higher concentrations, whereas replication-induced cells showed a relatively moderate but consistent decline. Notably, etoposide- and IL-1β-treated mouse NPCs began to respond at relatively low concentrations, indicating comparatively high sensitivity to SSK1. Collectively, these data suggest that SSK1 exerts a selective and broad-spectrum effect on senescence-associated NPCs from human, rat, and mouse sources while sparing non-senescent cells to a large extent.

We next investigated whether SSK1 could improve the functional state of IL-1β-treated NPCs. RT-qPCR analysis showed that IL-1β markedly suppressed the expression of proliferation-related genes, including PCNA, CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, CDK6, CyclinA1, CyclinB1, CyclinD1, and CyclinE1, whereas SSK1 treatment partially reversed these changes in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2C). In parallel, IL-1β disrupted ECM metabolic homeostasis, as evidenced by decreased expression of anabolic markers ACAN, COL2, and SOX9 and increased expression of catabolic genes MMP13 and ADAMTS5. SSK1 treatment significantly restored the expression of anabolic ECM-related genes while suppressing catabolic markers (Fig. 2D), indicating that SSK1 alleviated degeneration-associated phenotypes in NPCs.

Consistent with the transcriptional findings, EdU staining showed that IL-1β significantly reduced the proportion of proliferating NPCs, whereas SSK1 treatment markedly restored EdU incorporation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2E and F). In contrast, TUNEL staining demonstrated that IL-1β induced pronounced apoptotic cell death, which was effectively attenuated by SSK1 treatment (Fig. 2G and H). Together, these results indicate that SSK1 exerts a conserved cross-species effect on senescence-associated NPCs and significantly restores proliferative capacity while attenuating apoptosis- and degeneration-related phenotypes under inflammatory stress.

SSK1 attenuates IL-1β-induced ECM degradation, ferroptosis, and senescence in NPCs

Given the protective effects of SSK1 on IL-1β-treated NPCs, we next investigated whether SSK1 could further alleviate ECM degradation, ferroptosis, and senescence-associated changes under degenerative conditions. RT-qPCR and western blot analyses consistently showed that SSK1 restored COL2 and GPX4 expression while reducing MMP13, TF, P53, and P21 levels in IL-1β-treated NPCs (Fig. 3A–C). These findings indicate that SSK1 alleviated ECM dysregulation, iron-related stress, and senescence-associated changes under inflammatory conditions. To further determine whether SSK1 inhibited ferroptotic injury, biochemical assays were performed to assess lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation. IL-1β stimulation significantly increased MDA, Fe2+, and total iron levels while decreasing GSH content, confirming the induction of ferroptosis-like changes in NPCs. In contrast, SSK1 treatment significantly reduced MDA, Fe2+, and total iron accumulation and restored intracellular GSH levels (Fig. 3D–G), indicating that SSK1 effectively alleviated oxidative lipid damage and iron overload under inflammatory stress.

IL-1β markedly weakened COL2 fluorescence intensity and enhanced MMP13 staining, whereas SSK1 treatment largely reversed these changes (Fig. 3H and I), further confirming that SSK1 protected NPCs from ECM degradation. Since senescence is another key pathological feature of IDD, SA-β-Gal staining was further performed to evaluate the senescent phenotype. As expected, IL-1β treatment markedly increased the proportion of SA-β-Gal-positive NPCs, whereas SSK1 significantly reduced senescence-associated staining (Fig. 3K–M). Collectively, these data demonstrate that SSK1 effectively attenuates IL-1β-induced ECM degradation, ferroptosis, and cellular senescence in NPCs.

Protective effects of SSK1 on IL-1β-treated NPCs are associated with ferroptosis inhibition

To determine whether the protective effects of SSK1 on degenerative NPCs were dependent on ferroptosis inhibition, rescue experiments were performed using the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 and the ferroptosis activator erastin. RT-qPCR analysis showed that IL-1β markedly decreased the expression of COL2 and GPX4 while increasing the expression of MMP13, TF, P53, and P21. Treatment with either SSK1 or Fer-1 significantly reversed these changes, indicating that both interventions effectively alleviated IL-1β-induced degenerative, ferroptotic, and senescence-associated transcriptional alterations in NPCs (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the addition of erastin largely abolished the protective effects of SSK1, as evidenced by reduced COL2 and GPX4 expression and re-elevated MMP13, TF, P53, and P21 levels. Western blot analysis further confirmed these findings at the protein level. Compared with the IL-1β group, both SSK1 and Fer-1 restored the expression of COL2 and GPX4 and suppressed the protein levels of MMP13, TF, P53, and P21 (Fig. 4B and C). However, when erastin was combined with SSK1, the beneficial effects of SSK1 were markedly weakened, indicating that ferroptosis activation counteracted SSK1-mediated protection.

To further verify the involvement of ferroptosis, biochemical assays were performed to assess lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation, and antioxidant capacity. As expected, IL-1β significantly increased MDA, Fe2+, and total iron levels while decreasing GSH content in NPCs. Both SSK1 and Fer-1 effectively reversed these changes, demonstrating reduced lipid peroxidation and iron overload together with restored redox balance (Fig. 4D–G). Consistently, IF staining showed that IL-1β markedly reduced GPX4 fluorescence intensity, whereas treatment with SSK1 or Fer-1 restored GPX4 expression in NPCs (Fig. 4H and I). However, the addition of erastin significantly weakened the restoration of GPX4 induced by SSK1. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the protective effects of SSK1 against IL-1β-induced degeneration and senescence-associated injury in NPCs are closely associated with ferroptosis inhibition.

SSK1 suppresses NF-κB activation and alleviates ferroptosis-related injury in NPCs

To further elucidate the mechanism by which SSK1 protects NPCs against IL-1β-induced degenerative injury, RNA-seq analysis was conducted. The volcano plot revealed a distinct transcriptional profile, with representative genes including Gpx4, p53, Col2a1, and Mmp13 among the DEGs (Fig. 5A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses highlighted biological processes and pathways associated with oxidative stress, ECM organization, ferroptosis, cellular senescence, and inflammatory signaling, including the NF-κB pathway (Fig. 5B and C). Based on the bioinformatics results, we next examined whether SSK1 regulated the NF-κB pathway in IL-1β-treated NPCs. Western blot analysis showed that IL-1β markedly increased the ratios of p-IκBα/IκBα and p-P65/P65, indicating robust activation of NF-κB signaling. SSK1 treatment significantly suppressed these changes, and a comparable inhibitory effect was observed in the NF-κB inhibitor group. In contrast, the addition of PMA largely reversed the inhibitory effect of SSK1 on NF-κB activation (Fig. 5D and E). Meanwhile, IL-1β-induced upregulation of TF, P53, and P21 expression and downregulation of GPX4 expression were significantly alleviated by either SSK1 or NF-κB inhibition, whereas PMA partially abolished these protective effects (Fig. 5D and E). These findings indicate that SSK1 suppresses IL-1β-induced ferroptosis- and senescence-associated signaling at least partly through inhibition of NF-κB activation. We further assessed whether modulation of NF-κB signaling affected ferroptotic injury in NPCs. As expected, IL-1β stimulation markedly increased MDA, Fe2+, and total iron levels and reduced GSH content. Both SSK1 and the NF-κB inhibitor significantly reversed these changes, indicating reduced lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation together with restoration of intracellular antioxidant capacity. By contrast, PMA largely weakened the protective effects of SSK1, leading to re-elevation of MDA, Fe2+, and total iron and a decrease in GSH levels (Fig. 5F–I). These data further support a mechanistic link between NF-κB activation and ferroptotic injury in IL-1β-treated NPCs. Consistently, IF staining showed that IL-1β markedly reduced GPX4 fluorescence intensity in NPCs, whereas treatment with either SSK1 or the NF-κB inhibitor restored GPX4 expression to a comparable extent. However, the addition of PMA significantly weakened the restoration of GPX4 induced by SSK1 (Fig. 5J and K). Taken together, these results demonstrate that SSK1 alleviates IL-1β-induced ferroptosis-related injury in NPCs by suppressing NF-κB signaling.

PPARγ is required for the protective effects of SSK1 against IL-1β-induced ferroptotic and degenerative injury in NPCs

RNA-seq analysis and differential gene visualization highlighted PPARγ as a potential mediator of the protective effects of SSK1. Given the established role of PPARγ in suppressing inflammatory signaling and ferroptosis during IDD [30], we next investigated whether SSK1 exerted its effects through a PPARγ-dependent regulation of NF-κB and ferroptosis. To further determine whether PPARγ is involved in the protective effects of SSK1, loss-of-function experiments were performed in NPCs using sh-PPARγ, and the effects of the PPARγ agonist pioglitazone were also evaluated. Western blot analysis showed that IL-1β reduced PPARγ expression and increased the p-IκBα/IκBα and p-P65/P65 ratios, indicating activation of NF-κB signaling. SSK1 restored PPARγ expression and suppressed NF-κB activation, whereas PPARγ knockdown weakened these effects. Pioglitazone partially reproduced the regulatory effects of SSK1 (Fig. 6A and B). Consistently, SSK1 restored COL2 and GPX4 expression and reduced MMP13, TF, P53, and P21 levels, while these effects were attenuated by PPARγ silencing and partially mimicked by pioglitazone. These findings suggest that PPARγ contributes to the protective effects of SSK1 against degenerative, ferroptotic, and senescence-associated changes in NPCs.

IL-1β treatment significantly reduced GPX4 fluorescence intensity and increased BODIPY fluorescence, indicating aggravated lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic injury. To improve the local retention and sustained delivery of SSK1, a PD hydrogel based on DAFM was further developed, characterizing its structural and physicochemical properties. H&E and DAPI staining showed that, compared with native AF tissue, decellularized tissue exhibited effective removal of cellular components and nuclei, indicating successful decellularization (Fig. 7A). Quantitative analysis further confirmed a marked reduction in DNA content after decellularization, whereas collagen content was largely preserved, although part of the GAG content was lost during the decellularization process (Fig. 7B–D). These findings suggest that DAFM retained major matrix components while effectively removing residual cellular material. The microstructure and functional properties of the hydrogel were then examined. SEM observation showed that both DAFM and PD hydrogels possessed porous structures (Fig. 7E). Release, degradation, swelling, and compressive testing further indicated that the PD hydrogel had sustained-release capability and suitable physicochemical properties for local drug delivery (Fig. 7F–I).

To further evaluate the cytocompatibility of the material system, live/dead staining was performed. Fluorescence imaging showed that cells cultured with DAFM or PD were predominantly viable, with only minimal dead cells observed, indicating favorable biocompatibility and no obvious cytotoxicity under the experimental conditions (Fig. 7J). To further determine whether hydrogel-based delivery could enhance the protective effects of SSK1, the biological activity of SSK1@PD in IL-1β-treated NPCs was then evaluated. RT-qPCR analysis showed that IL-1β markedly decreased the expression of the anabolic ECM marker COL2 and the ferroptosis-protective gene GPX4, while significantly increasing the expression of the catabolic marker MMP13, the iron transport-related gene TF, and the senescence-associated genes P53 and P21. Compared with PD alone, treatment with SSK1@PD more effectively reversed these transcriptional alterations, indicating that incorporation of SSK1 into the hydrogel further improved ECM metabolic balance and suppressed ferroptosis- and senescence-related gene expression in NPCs (Fig. 8A). Consistent with the mRNA results, western blot analysis showed that SSK1@PD restored COL2 and GPX4 protein expression and reduced MMP13, TF, P53, and P21 levels more effectively than PD alone (Fig. 8B and C). To further assess the effect of SSK1@PD on ferroptotic injury, biochemical assays were performed to examine lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation, and antioxidant capacity. IL-1β stimulation significantly increased MDA, Fe2+, and total iron levels and reduced GSH content, whereas SSK1@PD treatment effectively reversed these changes (Fig. 8D–G). In addition, IF analysis of GPX4 showed that IL-1β significantly weakened GPX4 expression, whereas SSK1@PD prominently restored GPX4 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 8J–L). Because cellular senescence is another major pathological feature of IDD, SA-β-Gal staining was further performed to assess the senescent phenotype. As expected, IL-1β markedly increased the proportion of SA-β-Gal-positive NPCs, whereas SSK1@PD treatment significantly reduced senescence-associated staining (Fig. 8K–M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SSK1-loaded PD hydrogel exerts stronger anti-degenerative, anti-ferroptotic, and anti-senescent effects than dose hydrogel treatment alone, further supporting its potential as an effective local therapeutic strategy for IDD.

SSK1@PD ameliorates IDD progression and suppresses ferroptosis- and senescence-related signaling in vivo

To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of SSK1@PD in vivo, a puncture-induced rat model of IDD was established, followed by local injection of PD, free SSK1, or SSK1@PD according to the experimental design (Fig. 9A). X-ray imaging showed that puncture injury led to progressive narrowing of the intervertebral disc space, whereas disc height was better preserved in the SSK1@PD-treated group than in the untreated IDD group (Fig. 9B). Quantitative analysis of the DHI further confirmed that SSK1@PD exerted a more pronounced protective effect than did PD treatment or free SSK1 treatment (Fig. 9D). H&E showed severe structural disruption, matrix loss, and disorganization of the AF architecture in the IDD group. PD or free SSK1 partially alleviated these pathological changes, whereas SSK1@PD more effectively preserved disc architecture and matrix-rich morphology at both 1 and 2 months after treatment (Fig. 9C). Consistently, histological scores were significantly improved in the SSK1@PD group (Fig. 9E and F). To further examine the effect of SSK1@PD on ECM metabolism in vivo, IHC staining was performed for COL2 and MMP13. The expression of COL2 was markedly reduced in degenerated discs, whereas SSK1@PD treatment significantly restored COL2-positive staining at both time points (Fig. 9G–I). In contrast, MMP13 expression was substantially increased in the IDD group but was effectively suppressed after SSK1@PD treatment (Fig. 9H–J). These findings indicate that SSK1@PD attenuates matrix degradation and promotes matrix preservation in vivo. We next investigated whether the therapeutic effects of SSK1@PD in vivo were associated with inhibition of ferroptosis- and senescence-related signaling. IHC staining showed that GPX4 expression was markedly reduced in the IDD group, whereas SSK1@PD treatment significantly restored GPX4-positive staining at both 1 and 2 months after treatment (Fig. 10A–D). In contrast, the senescence-associated marker P53 was strongly elevated in degenerated discs but was markedly suppressed following SSK1@PD administration (Fig. 10B–E). In addition, IF staining demonstrated that p-P65 expression was substantially increased in the IDD group, indicating activation of NF-κB signaling, whereas treatment with SSK1@PD effectively reduced p-P65-positive staining in vivo (Fig. 10C–F). Biochemical assays further supported these observations. Compared with the sham group, degenerated intervertebral disc tissues exhibited significantly increased ferrous iron and MDA levels, indicating enhanced iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation. Treatment with SSK1@PD significantly reduced both ferrous iron and MDA contents to a greater extent than did the other treatments and the untreated IDD condition (Fig. 10G and H). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that local injection of SSK1@PD effectively alleviates puncture-induced IDD, preserves disc structure and ECM homeostasis, and suppresses ferroptosis-, senescence-, and NF-κB-related signaling in vivo.

Discussion

IDD is a multifactorial and progressive disorder characterized by ECM disorganization, inflammatory amplification, oxidative stress imbalance, and loss of cellular homeostasis within the disc microenvironment [[31], [32], [33]]. Although increasing attention has been paid to biological intervention for IDD, currently available treatments remain largely palliative and fail to effectively reverse the hostile degenerative niche inside the disc. In the present study, an SSK1@PD hydrogel as a localized therapeutic platform for IDD was developed, demonstrating that this system exerts dual benefits at both the mechanistic and delivery levels. Mechanistically, SSK1 alleviated IL-1β-induced NPC injury, at least in part, through modulation of the PPARγ/NF-κB/ferroptosis axis and attenuation of senescence-related deterioration. From a biomaterials perspective, the PD hydrogel serves as a local delivery platform that helps retain SSK1 within the disc and prolong its therapeutic action. This may explain why SSK1@PD showed better protective effects than free SSK1 in vitro and in vivo.

Ferroptosis should be interpreted as an important component of the degenerative network rather than as an isolated endpoint. Inflammatory stress may weaken antioxidant defenses and increase susceptibility to lipid peroxidation, whereas ferroptotic injury may further aggravate matrix catabolism and cellular dysfunction [5,[34], [35], [36], [37], [38]]. Evidence from other tissue injury models further indicates that oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagy or mitophagy, and inflammatory forms of regulated cell death are closely interconnected. Astragaloside IV has been shown to attenuate endoplasmic reticulum stress and pyroptosis after spinal cord injury by enhancing AMPK-mediated autophagy, whereas 3,4-dimethoxychalcone promotes ischemic and diabetic flap survival through TFEB-mediated autophagy and the inhibition of PANoptosis [39,40]. In addition, MC4R activation and SGK1 inhibition have been reported to suppress oxidative stress-associated AIM2 activation and pyroptosis after spinal cord injury by preserving mitochondrial homeostasis or promoting FoxO1-mediated mitophagy [41,42]. Although these studies were conducted in different injury models, they support the close interaction between oxidative stress and regulated cell death. In our study, the rescue effect of Fer-1 and the attenuation of SSK1-mediated protection by erastin suggest that ferroptotic stress is a functionally relevant component of IL-1β-induced NPC degeneration [16,43,44].

PPARγ has been increasingly recognized as a protective transcriptional regulator in musculoskeletal degeneration, where it modulates inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress responses, and matrix metabolism [[45], [46], [47], [48], [49]]. In our study, PPARγ silencing weakened the protective effects of SSK1, whereas pioglitazone partially reproduced them. These findings suggest that restoration of PPARγ contributes to NF-κB suppression and ferroptosis inhibition. However, the partial effect of pioglitazone also indicates that additional mechanisms may be involved.

Beyond pathway-level regulation, the senolytic and cytoprotective effects of SSK1 may be biologically complementary. Selective removal of dysfunctional senescence-associated NPCs may reduce inflammatory and catabolic stress within the local microenvironment, thereby improving the functional state of the remaining cells [50,51]. These findings help reconcile the senolytic and cytoprotective effects of SSK1. SSK1 preferentially eliminates senescence-associated NPCs while largely sparing non-senescent cells, whereas the surviving cell population shows improved proliferation, ECM homeostasis, and resistance to ferroptosis-associated injury. Thus, the overall protective effect of SSK1 may involve both selective clearance of dysfunctional senescent cells and alleviation of degenerative stress in the remaining NPCs, although whether its anti-ferroptotic action is direct or secondary to senolytic activity requires further investigation. Simultaneously, one of the major translational challenges for IDD therapy is the avascular and structurally dense nature of the disc, which restricts drug penetration and local persistence [[52], [53], [54]]. The PD hydrogel serves as a local delivery platform that helps retain SSK1 within the disc and prolong its therapeutic action. This may explain why SSK1@PD showed better protective effects than free SSK1 in vitro and in vivo. In addition, the partial protective effect of PD alone suggests that the scaffold may also contribute to improving the local microenvironment.

Several limitations of the present work should be acknowledged. First, although our findings strongly support the involvement of the PPARγ/NF-κB/ferroptosis axis, the precise molecular basis by which SSK1 restores PPARγ remains to be fully elucidated, and whether this regulation is direct or secondary to modulation of senescence-associated cellular states requires further investigation. Second, although the rat caudal disc model is convenient and widely accepted for proof-of-concept studies, it cannot fully recapitulate the size, biomechanics, and chronic progression of human lumbar disc degeneration. Future studies in larger animal models will therefore be necessary to strengthen translational relevance. Third, our in vivo experiments focused on relatively short-term outcomes, and longer observation periods are required to determine whether the protective effects of SSK1@PD can be sustained and whether the material undergoes favorable remodeling over time. In addition, further optimization of the SSK1 dose and hydrogel formulation, comprehensive characterization of the in vivo release kinetics and degradation behavior, and evaluation of the long-term safety of repeated administration are warranted. Future studies should also assess the feasibility of minimally invasive, imaging-guided intradiscal injection to facilitate the clinical translation of this local delivery strategy.

In conclusion, an injectable SSK1-loaded PEGDA/DAFM hydrogel was developed that effectively attenuates disc degeneration at least in part through modulation of the PPARγ/NF-κB/ferroptosis axis and improvement of the local disc microenvironment. This platform combines favorable physicochemical and biomechanical properties with good cytocompatibility and sustained-release capability and exhibits robust anti-degenerative, anti-ferroptotic, and anti-senescent effects in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, our findings provide a strong preclinical rationale for the development of mechanism-informed, matrix-based localized therapies for IDD and support the further translational evaluation of SSK1@PD for degenerative disc disease.
