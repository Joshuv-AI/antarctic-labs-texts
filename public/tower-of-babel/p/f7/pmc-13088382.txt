Orbital MRI for thyroid eye disease activity staging: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shuyi Liu, Chuang Sun, Shuxin Liu, Tianyue Zhu, Don O. Kikkawa, Wei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for staging the activity of thyroid eye disease (TED).MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in the Medline, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science Core Collection, and EMBASE databases for relevant studies. The risk of bias and applicability of the included studies were assessed using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST). Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity, while publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test.ResultsThis meta-analysis included 16 studies involving a total of 2,317 eyes published between 2020 and 2025, with 15 studies conducted in mainland China. All studies were found to have a high risk of bias, primarily due to retrospective design and lack of validation. The pooled area under the curve (AUC) value for MRI in differentiating active and inactive TED was 0.84 [95% CI (0.81–0.86), I2 = 66.5%, p < 0.001], indicating fair diagnostic performance. T2-weighted MRI sequences showed superior diagnostic efficacy compared to T1-weighted sequences [AUC = 0.84, 95% CI (0.80–0.87), I2 = 63.4%, p = 0.001; AUC = 0.81, 95% CI (0.77–0.85), I2 = 0%, p = 0.642; respectively]. Subgroup analysis based on orbital tissue type revealed that the extraocular muscles provided the highest diagnostic efficacy compared to the lacrimal gland and orbital fat [AUC = 0.83, 95% CI (0.80–0.87), I2 = 71.5%, p < 0.001].ConclusionOrbital MRI demonstrates a valuable diagnostic efficacy for staging TED activity to assist the CAS, with extraocular muscles serving as the most promising indicator of disease activity. This non-invasive imaging modality offers a tool for assessing pathological changes in TED. Future studies should focus on the diagnostic efficacy models for MRI in TED staging in diverse populations for expanding its global generalizability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04714-y.

Introduction

Thyroid eye disease (TED), also known as Graves’ orbitopathy, is a complex autoimmune disorder of the orbit that is closely associated with thyroid dysfunction. It is the most common orbital disease in adults [1]. Approximately 40% of patients with Graves’ disease develop TED, which is the most prevalent extra-thyroidal manifestation of diffuse toxic goiter. TED is characterized by symptoms such as eyelid retraction, proptosis, diplopia, and progressive vision loss, all of which significantly impact patients’ quality of life [2, 3].

TED can be classified into two phases based on disease activity: active and inactive. The active phase is marked by inflammation and edema and is more responsive to pharmacological treatment. In contrast, the inactive phase is characterized by fibrosis and fatty infiltration and typically requires surgical intervention, such as orbital decompression [4]. The Clinical Activity Score (CAS) is the most widely used tool for staging TED activity since it is easy to operate. A CAS score of ≥ 3 indicates that the disease is in the active phase [5]. To improve the accuracy and consistency of CAS in TED staging, the European Group on Graves’ Orbitopathy (EUGOGO) has developed and recommended the use of a scoring map [6]. However, the CAS is a semi-quantitative scale that is influenced by the clinician’s experience and exhibits limited comparability across different ethnic populations due to anatomical variations in the orbit [7, 8]. Additionally, CAS primarily assesses anterior orbital manifestations and does not adequately reflect inflammation in the deep orbit [9].

Given these limitations, the development of objective biomarkers for TED activity staging is crucial, particularly those that can capture pathological changes in the deep orbit. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a valuable tool in this regard due to its excellent soft tissue resolution and non-invasive nature. MRI can be used to quantify inflammatory activity and provide detailed anatomical insights into conditions such as proptosis and extraocular muscle thickening [10]. Quantitative MRI parameters, including T1-mapping, T2-mapping, and water fraction, have been investigated for their ability to differentiate between active and inactive TED, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy and informing clinical decision-making [11, 12]. In light of this, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of MRI in staging TED activity. Through this review, we aim to consolidate existing research and assess the potential of MRI as a non-invasive, objective biomarker for TED activity.

Methods

A registered protocol for this study was published on the Open Science Framework before the study commenced (https://osf.io) (Supplementary Material 1) [13]. This systematic review and meta-analysis involved data extraction and synthesis of existing studies, and therefore did not require ethical approval, as informed consent was addressed by the original studies.

Data sources and searches

A comprehensive search was conducted across several databases, including Medline via PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and EMBASE, with a cutoff date of October 8, 2025. The search incorporated both medical subject headings (MeSH) and relevant keywords. Search terms included “Graves Ophthalmopathy,” “Magnetic Resonance Imaging,” and related synonyms (Supplementary Material 2). Only studies published in English were included. We also manually reviewed the reference lists of eligible studies and relevant meta-analyses to identify additional studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Studies with participants aged > 18 years. A confirmed diagnosis of thyroid eye disease (TED) with comprehensive clinical data. Orbital MRI performed before the patients received treatment. Adequate image quality for analysis. Reporting of the area under the curve (AUC) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI).

Exclusion criteria: History of glucocorticoid therapy, biologic therapy, radiotherapy, or orbital decompression surgery. Presence of dysthyroid optic neuropathy. Coexisting eye diseases, tumors, or trauma. Duplicate publications, study protocols, reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, or letters.

Study screening and data extraction

Two authors (SY Liu and C Sun) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies according to predefined eligibility criteria. Duplicates and irrelevant studies were removed after the initial assessment. Subsequently, full-text articles were downloaded and thoroughly reviewed to confirm study eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with W Lu.

Data extraction was performed independently by SY Liu and C Sun, who collected the following information: corresponding author, publication year, country or region, study design, sample size (eyes), study period, participant demographics (age, sex), TED diagnosis method, MRI protocol and CAS interval, MRI field strength, orbital tissue type analyzed, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and the corresponding 95% CI. W Lu reviewed the extracted data, and any discrepancies were discussed.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed by SY Liu and C Sun using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) (www.probast.org) [14]. The tool includes 20 signaling questions grouped into four domains: participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis [15]. Responses to each question were categorized as “yes,” “probably yes,” “no,” “probably no,” or “no information.” If any question in a domain was answered as “no” or “probably no,” that domain was considered at high risk of bias. Only studies judged as low risk in all four domains were classified as having low overall risk of bias.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Data were synthesized and analyzed using STATA software (version 14.0; Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA). We extracted the AUC and corresponding 95% CI from each study to evaluate the diagnostic performance of MRI in staging TED activity. Heterogeneity was assessed using the chi-square test and I² statistic, and a random-effects model was employed due to the clinical heterogeneity of the included studies. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the pooled effect size. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Subgroup analyses were performed based on MRI sequence type and orbital tissue type.

Results

Search results

A total of 4,248 records were identified through database searches. After removing 1,833 duplicate studies, 2,415 articles were excluded based on title and abstract review. Full-text screening led to the exclusion of 26 studies, leaving 16 studies for inclusion in this systematic review [16–31]. All studies were evaluated as having a high risk of bias based on the PROBAST tool, indicating potential methodological flaws in study design and validation. Specifically, ten studies were classified as having a high risk in the participants domain due to reliance on retrospective records, which may introduce recall and selection biases [16, 18, 20, 22, 23, 25–29]. In the predictor’s domain, two studies had a high risk due to lack of reported independent image analysis by multiple observers [24, 28]. In the outcome domain, nine studies were judged to have high risk because of inadequate time intervals between predictor assessment and outcome determination [19, 21, 22, 24, 25, 30, 31]. All 16 studies had a high risk in the analysis domain due to the absence of internal or external validation and failure to account for overfitting or underfitting of prediction models. Furthermore, eight studies did not provide coefficients for predictors in multivariate regression models [16, 20, 21, 23–26, 31], and three studies lacked intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) to assess inter- and intra-observer reliability for quantitative measurements [20, 24, 26, 28]. One study had an insufficient sample size, unable to meet the guideline of having more than 20 events per variable [18], and one study did not comprehensively assess model performance [31].

In terms of applicability risk, nine studies were assessed as having high risk, one study was classified as unclear risk, and six studies were categorized as low risk. The risk of applicability was high in the predictor and outcome domains for nine studies, primarily due to concerns regarding the timing of predictor measurements [16–18, 20, 23, 26–29].

Overall diagnostic efficacy of MRI for staging TED activity

Sixteen studies reported the diagnostic efficacy of MRI for staging TED activity [16–31]. Pooling analysis revealed an overall AUC of 0.84 [95% CI (0.81–0.86), I2 = 66.5%, p < 0.001; Fig. 2], indicating a fair ability to differentiate between active and inactive TED. Given the significant heterogeneity, we performed a sensitivity analysis to explore potential sources of variability. The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that none of the included studies significantly altered the pooled effect size, indicating the robustness of the overall findings, as shown in Supplementary Material 3. A visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested no substantial publication bias (Fig. 3), and Egger’s regression test (p = 0.757) confirmed that publication bias was not significant.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on MRI sequence type and orbital tissue type (Table 3). The analysis of MRI sequence types indicated that T2-related sequences [AUC = 0.84, 95% CI (0.80–0.87), I2 = 63.4%, p = 0.001] were more commonly used and demonstrated better diagnostic efficacy than T1-related sequences [AUC = 0.81, 95% CI (0.77–0.85), I2 = 0%, p = 0.642]. Additionally, subgroup analysis based on orbital tissue type revealed that extraocular muscles [AUC = 0.83, 95% CI (0.80–0.87), I2 = 71.5%, p < 0.001] provided superior diagnostic performance compared to lacrimal glands [AUC = 0.77, 95% CI (0.71–0.83), I2 = 44.5%, p = 0.125] and orbital fat [AUC = 0.77, 95% CI (0.67–0.88), I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.955].

Discussion

Main findings

This meta-analysis synthesizes data from 16 studies published between 2020 and 2025, encompassing 2,317 eyes, and demonstrates that orbital MRI offers a robust diagnostic capability for differentiating active and inactive TED with a diagnostic efficacy of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.81–0.86). These findings underscore the potential of orbital MRI as an effective, non-invasive tool to assess pathological changes associated with TED.

Findings interpretation

Accurate staging of TED is crucial for effective management, and the CAS remains the most widely used clinical tool for assessing disease activity [32]. However, CAS is a semi-quantitative scale based on subjective clinical observations, which makes it susceptible to inter-observer variability and dependent on the clinician’s expertise [33]. Notably, the Krippendorff alpha for inter-observer agreement in CAS scoring was only 0.332 when CAS ≥ 3 was used to define active disease [34]. Moreover, CAS primarily evaluates external and anterior manifestations, which overlooks deeper orbital pathologies [35].

In contrast, orbital MRI offers a more objective and comprehensive evaluation. It is non-invasive, free from ionizing radiation, and provides superior soft tissue contrast, enabling better visualization and quantification of deep orbital tissues, particularly those related to water content and collagen structure [36, 37]. These advantages make orbital MRI a powerful tool for TED activity staging. However, there is still limited research examining the diagnostic efficacy of MRI across different sequence types and orbital tissue types, which restricts its broader clinical adoption. This study includes only those studies that provided definitive diagnostic parameters (such as the area under the curve (AUC) and its corresponding 95% CI), ensuring the reliability of our conclusions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis offering a comprehensive, evidence-based evaluation of MRI’s diagnostic utility in TED staging.

Consistent with previous studies, our analysis found that MRI serves as an objective tool for activity staging, capable of capturing pathological changes in the deep orbit, thus overcoming the limitations of clinical metrics, which only assess anterior orbital manifestations [38]. The active stage of TED is characterized by inflammation and edema in the extraocular muscles, along with orbital fat expansion, while the inactive stage is marked by fibrosis and fatty degeneration in these muscles [39, 40]. Therefore, most of the included studies focused on quantitative parameters of extraocular muscles. Mechanistically, thyrotropin receptors on orbital fibroblasts induce the production of collagen fibrils and hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans, leading to differentiation into myofibroblasts and preadipocyte fibroblasts [41, 42]. Moreover, an increasing number of studies support the correlation between lacrimal gland dysfunction and TED progression, including lacrimal gland enlargement clinically, lacrimal gland inflammation pathologically, and proteomic changes in tear films molecularly [43–45]. Given the established role of the lacrimal gland in TED, quantitative MRI parameters related to this tissue have garnered attention. Compared with inactive TED patients, those with active TED exhibit larger lacrimal glands, suggesting that these parameters could serve as potential indicators for TED staging [46]. However, studies have also found that both structural and functional MRI parameters generally provide more significant diagnostic value in distinguishing TED from healthy controls than in differentiating between active and inactive TED patients [47]. Therefore, there is a need for the development of a new scoring system that incorporates multiple MRI parameters in future research.

Various MRI sequences have demonstrated diagnostic efficacy for TED. Specifically, conventional T2-weighted sequences, with their derived signal intensity or relaxation times, have been widely used to visualize and quantify water content and collagen structure, reflecting histopathological changes. These sequences are particularly effective in assisting TED staging due to their high sensitivity to inflammation and edema [48, 49]. In contrast, few studies in this review focused on T1-weighted sequences. However, research has indicated that T1-weighted sequences can serve as the first-line non-invasive examination for myocardial fibrosis [50]. Therefore, the role of T1-related sequences in understanding muscle fibrosis in TED warrants further investigation.

Implications and limitations

The results of this meta-analysis reinforce the diagnostic efficacy of orbital MRI for TED activity staging. The application of orbital MRI provides a non-invasive and effective method for assessing pathological changes in TED, particularly in deep orbital tissues. Given its potential, further efforts should be directed toward exploring clinically accessible methods for assessing disease activity in the deep orbit and integrating MRI parameters to guide treatment decisions for TED patients.

However, several limitations should be considered. Firstly, the majority of the studies included in this analysis were conducted in mainland China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to Western populations. This could be due to differences in the presentation of TED, with Chinese patients often showing fewer symptoms such as exophthalmos, upper eyelid retraction, and edema compared to Caucasian patients [51]. These regional differences may have influenced the adoption of alternative diagnostic approaches, such as imaging modalities, to facilitate earlier and more accurate diagnosis in Chinese populations. Therefore, future studies should aim to develop diagnostic efficacy models for orbital MRI in TED staging that are applicable across diverse populations. Secondly, differences in MRI slice thickness, different region of interest placement, and different MRI field strength across studies directly impacted the data points available for quantitative analysis. Therefore, standardizing MRI imaging protocols is essential to enhance the consistency and reproducibility of results in future research. Thirdly, many studies included in the analysis had relatively small sample sizes. The sample sizes of the included studies ranged from 72 to 280 eyes, which could affect the robustness of the findings. Therefore, future research with larger sample sizes is needed to validate and refine these results. Lastly, issues related to the transparency of reporting were noted, as eight studies did not provide information on the coefficients of predictors in multivariate regression models, and three studies did not report intraclass correlation coefficients for assessing inter- and intra-observer agreement on quantitative MRI parameters. These shortcomings may have contributed to heterogeneity between studies and limited the ability to detect publication bias. Therefore, rigorous study designs, more comprehensive reporting, and standardized methodologies are essential to strengthen the quality and reliability of future research.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that orbital MRI is a valuable diagnostic tool for TED activity staging to assist the CAS, providing an effective and non-invasive method to assess pathological changes in deep orbital tissues. Future research should focus on refining diagnostic models, incorporating larger sample sizes, exploring more specific MRI sequences, and conducting multicenter validation to further establish the role of orbital MRI in TED management.
