Mechanisms of intrinsic osmolality and sodium detection by magnocellular neurosecretory neurons
Authors: Sandra Salgado‐Mozo, Anzala Murtaz, Joshua C Wyrosdic, Julie O'Reilly‐Fong, Cristian Zaelzer, Mary P LaPierre, Charles W Bourque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe maintenance of extracellular fluid (ECF) osmolality and sodium concentration ([Na+]o) near optimal “set point” values sustains physiological functions and prevents pathological states such as hypo‐ and hypernatremia. The peptide hormones vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone) and oxytocin (a natriuretic hormone in rats) play key roles in this process. These hormones are synthesized by hypothalamic magnocellular neurosecretory cells (MNCs) that project to the neurohypophysis and are released into the systemic circulation in response to rises in ECF osmolality or [Na+]o. These homeostatic responses are highly sensitive. For example, vasopressin release is elicited by an increase in ECF osmolality as small as ≥1%. The osmotic and sodium‐dependent control of vasopressin and oxytocin release at the neurohypophysis is directly regulated by the electrical activity of MNCs. This regulation involves an array of mechanisms that include synaptic inputs from the brain and periphery, the effects of chemicals released by glial cells, and intrinsic sensory properties of MNCs. These overlapping mechanisms may offer an important degree of redundancy for the homeostatic control of vasopressin and oxytocin release and contribute to the high sensitivity of these responses. Recent work has shown that the intrinsic sodium sensitivity and osmosensitivity of MNCs play an important role in the control of these neurons in vivo. This review provides an update of our current understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms that contribute to the cell‐autonomous sensory properties of MNCs.Keywords: Na X , osmosensitivity, sodium sensitivity, supraoptic nucleus, Trpv1

INTRODUCTION

Regulation of the body's hydration status is essential for life and involves a complex array of interdependent homeostatic responses that collectively maintain extracellular fluid (ECF) volume, osmolality, and sodium concentration ([Na+]o) near optimal levels. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 A core requirement for any form of homeostatic regulation is the ability to measure the physiological parameter being controlled with a high degree of precision and specificity. Here, we review our understanding of the cell‐autonomous mechanisms by which magnocellular neurosecretory cells (MNCs) detect changes in ECF osmolality and [Na+]o.

Hydration status affects both natremia and osmolality

Mammals maintain the composition and volume of their ECF near constant values to sustain vascular perfusion and physiological function throughout the body. This regulation is achieved by the concerted action of numerous processes that each contribute to the stabilization of individual parameters. For example, regulated release of hormones such as insulin and glucagon serves to maintain circulating glucose concentration in the low millimolar range. Similarly, the concentration of calcium ions in the ECF is partly regulated by coordinated actions of parathyroid hormone and calcitonin. This control of individual solutes occurs in concert with a regulation of ECF osmolality (overall solute concentration) to preserve ECF volume and prevent the occurrence of osmotic insults. Indeed, most of the solutes present at millimolar concentrations in the ECF (e.g., Na+, Cl−, bicarbonate, glucose) do not freely cross cell membranes and can affect osmotic balance across cell membranes. Acute changes in ECF osmolality can be harmful because they can lead to significant changes in the volume of cells and organs due to osmosis. Being encased in a solid cranium, the brain is particularly vulnerable to shrinking and swelling; accordingly, neurological symptoms are among the first to emerge in response to such insults. 8 , 9

Mild fluctuations of ECF osmolality occur throughout the day due to the ingestion of food and liquids, and as a result of water loss incurred through breathing, sweating, and production of urine. For example, the consumption of food, 10 , 11 , 12 exercise, 13 or exposure to heat, 14 can significantly raise blood osmolality within minutes. Conversely, osmolality is rapidly reduced upon water ingestion. 15 Because sodium is the predominant ion in the ECF, changes in osmolality induced by water loss or accumulation are typically correlated with changes in [Na+]o. 16 As such, hyperosmolality is typically associated with hypernatremia, whereas hypoosmolality is associated with hyponatremia.

In healthy individuals, behavioral and physiological mechanisms are rapidly recruited to buffer these perturbations and adjust the intake and excretion of sodium and/or water to restore ECF osmolality near an ideal value (set point ~286 mosmol/kg in humans). 1 For example, hyperosmotic conditions stimulate thirst and reduce salt appetite, whereas hypoosmolality suppresses thirst and promotes appetite for salt. 1 Hyperosmolality increases water reabsorption and promotes sodium excretion (natriuresis) by the kidney. Conversely, hypoosmolality stimulates renal water excretion (diuresis) and promotes sodium reabsorption. When appropriately coordinated, these core “osmoregulatory” mechanisms dampen the dynamic changes in natremia and osmolality that occur throughout the day. 1

OSMORECEPTORS AND SODIUM DETECTORS

The modulation of behavioral and physiological homeostatic responses to oppose intermittent changes in water balance implies the existence of highly sensitive interoceptive sensory mechanisms. Classic experiments performed in dogs, 17 goats, 18 , 19 and sheep 20 first provided clear evidence showing that the brains of these animals house receptors which are individually specialized for the detection of ECF [Na+]o and osmolality. It is now well established that such receptors are present in the organum vasculosum lamina terminalis (OVLT) and subfornical organ (SFO), which are sensory circumventricular organs located in the anterior part of the third ventricle. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Moreover, it is known that a variety of mechanisms are involved in the detection of [Na+]o and osmolality at these sites, featuring the involvement of various types of cells (e.g., glial cells and neurons), ion channels, receptors, and transporters that each play important roles in converting changes in [Na+]o and/or osmolality into functionally relevant changes in the electrical activity of the output neurons within the SFO 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 and OVLT. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Interestingly, homeostatic effector neurons located in some of the downstream nuclei have also been shown to display sensitivity to [Na+]o and osmolality. For example, neurons in the median preoptic nucleus (an effector site for thirst) are sensitive to [Na+]o. 34 , 35 Moreover, MNCs in the supraoptic (SON) and paraventricular nuclei (PVN) which regulate diuresis and natriuresis through the release of vasopressin and oxytocin, are intrinsically sensitive to both [Na+]o and osmolality. 36 It is important to note that in addition to its classical role as a mediator of parturition 37 and lactation 38 , 39 in mammals, oxytocin release into the bloodstream of rats can stimulate natriuresis in rats. 40 , 41 Indeed, increases in blood osmolality can excite MNCs in this species and cause oxytocin release into the circulation. 42

Previous work has shown that the osmoresponsiveness of MNCs is regulated by the integration of intrinsically generated receptor potentials, 43 proximal taurinergic gliotransmission, 44 , 45 changes in excitatory and inhibitory synaptic drive 46 , 47 and bursting behavior promoted by NMDA receptors 48 , 49 , 50 (Figure 1). Interestingly, the synaptic regulation of these neurons is not limited to inputs arising from the OVLT 46 , 51 and SFO. 52 , 53 Previous work has shown that vagal and spinal pathways can modulate the activity of MNCs in response to the activation of receptors located in the gastrointestinal tract and hepatic portal vein. 12 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 Signals from these areas mediate powerful anticipatory (presystemic) effects that predict the future impact of ingested substances and thus dampen the osmoregulatory perturbations associated with feeding and drinking. 2 , 12 , 58 , 59 Similarly, inputs from the suprachiasmatic nucleus contribute to the synaptic regulation of vasopressin releasing MNCs to buffer the potential dehydrating effect of sleep. 51 , 60

Our understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms that mediate [Na+]o and osmotic detection at various central and peripheral sites is far from complete. Notably, important differences have been reported in the details of how these signals are sensed by different types of cells in the OVLT, 24 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 61 , 62 , 63 SFO, 25 , 26 , 27 , 29 SON 43 , 44 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 and PVN. 69 , 70 Even specific subtypes of ion channels appear to operate differently at various locations. 29 , 34 , 35 , 63 Moreover, the identity of osmoregulatory sensors located in the gastrointestinal and hepatic areas is currently unknown. By contrast, our understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms mediating [Na+] and osmotic detection in MNCs is extensive, and recent work has shown that the cell‐autonomous sensory properties of MNCs contribute significantly to their overall contribution to osmoregulation. In the remainder of this review, we provide an update on the specific mechanisms by which MNCs intrinsically detect changes in [Na+] and osmolality.

MNCs as intrinsic sensors of [Na+]o and osmolality

Extracellular recordings from coronal brain slices in vitro first showed that SON MNCs could be excited by local hyperosmotic solutions 71 and in vivo experiments in anesthetized rats later revealed that these neurons could be excited by the application of small quantities of hypertonic NaCl solution in close proximity to the recorded neuron. 72 The hypothesis that MNCs may be intrinsically osmosensitive received additional support when recordings from rat 65 , 73 , 74 and guinea pig 64 MNCs in hypothalamic slices and explants revealed that the excitatory effects of hyperosmotic solutions could be preserved under conditions blocking synaptic transmission. However, such approaches could not exclude the possible involvement of substances released from other types of adjacent cells (e.g., astrocytes or endothelial cells). A clear demonstration that MNCs are intrinsically osmosensitive was ultimately provided by experiments showing that cells acutely dissociated from the SON of adult rats can be depolarized and excited by hypertonic solutions and hyperpolarized/inhibited by hypoosmotic solutions. 43 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 Electrophysiological recordings later showed that MNCs acutely isolated from rat SON were responsive to increases in [Na+]o even in the absence of an accompanying change in osmolality. 66 , 79 Patch‐clamp analysis combined with imaging techniques applied to MNCs acutely isolated from rats and mice has provided a unique opportunity to explore core mechanisms underlying sodium sensitivity and osmosensitivity of these particular cells.

Osmosensitivity of MNCs is a mechanosensitive process

Ion substitution experiments performed on voltage‐clamped MNCs 43 , 66 , 76 , 77 revealed that hyperosmotic solutions increase a non‐selective cation conductance, whereas hypoosmotic solutions lower cation conductance. Combining confocal imaging with patch‐clamp recording further revealed that the increase in cation conductance induced by an acute (<30 min) hyperosmotic stimulus is time‐locked to a reduction of cell volume. 43 Although this observation suggested cell volume as a possible mediator of the osmotic effect, the possibility remained that an osmotically induced change in intracellular solute concentration associated with osmosis might play a role in osmosensitivity. However, the effects of acute hypertonicity and hypotonicity could be respectively mimicked by reducing or increasing cell volume by modifying the pressure applied to a recording pipette in the absence of osmotic stimulation. 43 These findings provided strong support for the involvement of mechanosensitive channels as mediators of the acute intrinsic osmosensitive response. Specifically, the observations imply involvement of shrinking‐activated and swelling‐inhibited cation channels. Indeed, MNCs were found to express stretch‐inhibited non‐selective cation channels that could be blocked by gadolinium (Gd3+) and exhibit macroscopic responses (whole cell voltage, current and excitatory effects) that could also be blocked by micromolar concentrations of Gd3+. 43 , 77 , 80

The identity of the osmotic transduction cation channel in MNCs was partly revealed by experiments showing that osmosensitivity was absent in cells derived from mice lacking the Trpv1 (transient receptor potential vanilloid) gene, 81 which encodes the capsaicin receptor. 82 However, unlike cells expressing full‐length TRPV1, MNCs do not respond to capsaicin and cannot be stained using antibodies directed against the protein's N‐terminus. 81 , 83 Indeed, DNA cloning experiments subsequently revealed that the SON of rats and mice expresses primarily a transcript of Trpv1 that lacks exons that code for the initial part of the cytoplasmic N‐terminus (ΔN‐Trpv1), including zones coding for amino acid residues required for capsaicin sensitivity. 84 Transfection of the ΔN‐Trpv1 variant into cultured heterologous cells or hypothalamic neurons from Trpv1−/− (knockout) mice is sufficient to confer osmosensitivity (Figure 2), as well as mechanosensitivity but not responsiveness to capsaicin. 81 , 83 Collectively, these data identify ΔN‐Trpv1 as a key mediator of osmosensitivity in MNCs.

Osmotic gating of ΔN‐Trpv1 reflects push‐activation by microtubules

Mechanical gating of ion channels can be either facilitated or impeded by elements of the cytoskeleton. 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 Super resolution imaging with structured illumination microscopy revealed that SON MNCs display a uniquely dense and intertwined array of microtubules (MTs) throughout their entire cytoplasm and that many of these MTs extend all the way to the plasma membrane. 89 This contrasts distinctly with other types of neurons which typically display dense expression of MTs in their dendrites, but sparse levels in their cytoplasm. Because the C‐terminal region of TRPV1 contains two binding sites for tubulin that can serve as attachment sites for tubulin and MTs in biochemical assays, 90 , 91 it was hypothesized that MTs might interact with ΔN‐Trpv1 in MNCs. 89 Indeed, immunoprecipitation assays on lysates of freshly microdissected rat SON showed that tubulin is part of a complex with TRPV1, and this interaction can be displaced competitively by short peptides mimicking the native tubulin binding sites of TRPV1. 89 Moreover, proximity ligation assay (PLA) with primary antibodies directed against TRPV1 and tubulin revealed that ΔN‐Trpv1 and MTs interact primarily along the cell surface, consistent with their role as putative sites of osmotic transduction (Figure 3A). 89 , 92 Displacing this interaction with peptides mimicking the tubulin binding sites eliminated osmotic and mechanical responses of the cells, indicating that this association is required for osmosensory transduction in MNC. 84 , 89 , 93

Taken together, these findings suggested that the activation of a cation current during osmotically induced cell shrinking could reflect either (i) an increase in the number of ΔN‐Trpv1 channels becoming associated with MTs during inward displacement of the membrane (where association confers activation), or (ii) that force applied to channels via pre‐attached MTs could mediate channel opening. Analysis of cells exposed to isotonic and hypertonic solutions with PLA showed no significant differences in the density of sites at which ΔN‐Trpv1 and MTs are associated, thereby contradicting the association model. Therefore, the current hypothesis is that inward displacement of the plasma membrane causes mechanical activation of ΔN‐Trpv1 channels via forces applied via MTs. 89 , 94

Cortical actin fenestrae may optimize osmosensory transduction

Although the results described above provide strong evidence supporting an active role for MTs during osmosensory transduction, it has been known for nearly two decades that actin filaments (F‐actin) also play a crucial role in this process. Specifically, depolymerization of F‐actin with cytochalasin‐D has been shown to abolish the mechanosensitivity and osmosensitivity of acutely isolated MNCs. 95 Conversely, rapid enhancement of submembrane F‐actin density upon acute exposure to angiotensin II, 96 or to the actin polymerizing drug jasplakinolide, 95 strongly amplifies the intrinsic osmosensitivity of MNCs. Interestingly, the enhanced osmosensitivity of MNCs observed following a 7‐day salt‐loading protocol in rats is also associated with an increase in submembrane F‐actin density. 97 , 98

A recent study showed that F‐actin forms a highly dense layer beneath the plasma membrane of MNCs and this layer is significantly thicker and denser in these cells than in non‐osmosensitive neurons. 97 However, the density of this F‐actin cortex is not homogeneous across the entire subcortical area of the cells and shows regions of low and high density. Indeed, three‐dimensional reconstruction of the F‐actin cortex in isolated MNCs stained with fluorescently labelled phalloidin revealed ovoid gaps (i.e., fenestrae) that punctured much of the F‐actin cortex at regular intervals (Figure 3B). 92 Interestingly, PLA revealed that F‐actin does not interact with ΔN‐TRPV1 (Figure 3A). Moreover, many sites at which MTs interact with ΔN‐TRPV1 are aligned with F‐actin fenestrae (Figure 3C), suggesting these may be preferred sites for osmosensory transduction.

Actin fenestrae amplify membrane displacement during mild hyperosmolality

Previous work combining imaging with measurements of cell capacitance in isolated MNCs has shown that the surface area of the plasma membrane of these cells exceeds the minimum amount that would be required to completely cover each cell by over 50%. 99 Because the lipid bilayer is not elastic, 100 this observation implies that MNCs feature a non‐uniform cell surface topology whereby membrane furrows, pits, and/or protrusions serve to store this membrane reserve. Moreover, changes in the dimension of these features have been shown to occur when transmembrane water movement is induced by small osmotic stimuli (Figure 4A). 92

Imaging techniques applied to isolated MNCs 43 , 76 , 80 , 81 , 95 , 98 , 99 and MNCs in brain slices 99 have shown that these cells undergo shrinking with significant inward displacement of the plasma membrane when exposed to strong hyperosmotic stimuli (e.g., +10%). Although this is compatible with a hypothetical push‐activation of ΔN‐TRPV1 channels, it has been difficult to explain how uniform shrinking could mediate significant channel activation when the cells are exposed to smaller, near‐threshold stimuli. It is well known that homeostatic responses such as thirst and VP secretion can occur in response to stimuli of +1% (i.e., +3 mosmol/kg), 17 , 101 which would barely affect cell volume. For example, assuming ideal osmometry geometry predicts the membrane of a 20‐μm diameter MNC would be displaced by a mere 48 nm in response to a +4.5 osmol/kg (+1.5%) stimulus. Actual displacement would be even less given that ~20% of cellular volume is osmotically inactive. 99 Yet activation of mechanosensitive ion channels such as homomeric Piezo1 commonly requires displacements exceeding 100 nm for activation. 102 A recent study showed that the presence of a fenestrated F‐actin cortex could provide an explanation for the high osmosensitivity of MNCs. 92 Our observations indicate that the F‐actin cortex of these cells can prevent an overall inward displacement of the membrane in response to mild hyperosmotic stimuli and that water efflux resulting from osmosis promotes the formation of membrane pits at fenestrae (Figure 4A). Indeed, confocal imaging of isolated MNCs in which the plasma membrane was fluorescently labeled revealed that membrane pits form dynamically over a period of a few seconds when MNCs are exposed to a 1.5% hyperosmotic stimulus. 92 Importantly, the depth of these pits was proportional to the diameter of the underlying fenestrae (Figure 4B) and ~10 times greater than the displacement predicted by homogenous osmometry (Figure 4B,C). The existence of fenestrae may therefore serve to amplify the magnitude of local membrane displacements induced by small, physiological osmotic stimuli (Figure 4D).

Osmotic detection during chronic stimuli: The hypertrophy paradox

Much of our understanding of osmosensitivity in MNCs was generated by electrophysiological studies in vitro examining the effects of osmotic stimuli typically lasting less than 10 minutes. Under these conditions, MNCs display sustained changes in cell volume that progress with a time constant of ~60s. 99 Although this time course is physiologically relevant, it is well known that MNCs exposed to prolonged changes in ECF osmolality (hours‐days) undergo significant morphological and genetic changes to adjust hormone synthesis with ongoing demand for release at the neurohypophysis. 97 , 103 , 104 , 105 , 106 , 107 , 108 , 109 Notably, MNCs display significant hypertrophy in response to sustained hypertonicity and hypotrophy in response to chronic hypotonicity. 110 These changes in soma size are opposite to those required for ΔN‐TRPV1 push‐activation (Figure 4D). Yet MNCs exposed to chronic hyperosmotic conditions such as those induced by 7 days of salt loading (2% NaCl as drinking fluid) continue to display strong excitatory responses to acute increases in osmolality and mechanically induced decreases in cell volume. 98 This suggests that other osmosensitive channels can become functionally expressed under these conditions and/or that MNCs can dynamically reconfigure interactions between MTs and ΔN‐Trpv1 channels during the hypertrophic response.

Studies on isolated MNCs have shown that increases in soma size associated with the hypertrophic response become apparent after 30–150 min of exposure to a sustained hyperosmotic stimulus. 111 The increased soma size is accompanied by increased input capacitance and can be prevented by blocking vesicular exocytosis. These observations indicate that hypertrophy reflects a significant increase in plasma membrane surface area, which results from the fusion of internal membranes. Interestingly, this phenomenon is associated with a reversible translocation of ΔN‐Trpv1 channels from internal stores to the plasma membrane that depends on the Ca2+‐dependent activation of phospholipase C (PLC) and on SNARE‐dependent exocytosis. 112 , 113 It is therefore possible that PLC activation somehow maintains the activity of ΔN‐Trpv1 channels independently of the initial MT‐mediated mechanical activation, 78 or that newly inserted ΔN‐Trpv1 channels maintain the cell's acute mechanosensitivity by becoming attached to MTs that have undergone polymerization and extended to the plasma membrane. The insertion of new ΔN‐Trpv1 channels and cytoskeletal remodeling represent powerful mechanisms for adaptation to sustained stimuli in MNCs. Further studies are required to examine these aspects.

INTRINSIC SODIUM DETECTION BY MNCs

Non‐cell‐autonomous mechanisms for detection of acute changes in [Na+]o have been described. 29 , 30 For example, it has been shown that the [Na+]o‐dependent modulation of action potential firing frequency in OVLT neurons is caused indirectly by the [Na+]o‐dependent release of protons from adjacent glial cells followed by paracrine activation of acid‐sensing ion channels expressed on the neurons themselves. 63 The [Na+]o dependency of this effect is mediated by Na X , a member of the sodium channel family which is expressed in glial cells within the OVLT 63 and also the SFO. 29 The sodium‐dependent activation of OVLT and SFO neurons is then relayed to the SON via axonal projections (Figure 1). Here, however, we focus our attention specifically on cell‐autonomous mechanisms by which acute changes in [Na+]o can affect action potential firing frequency in MNCs.

An important consideration is that under physiological conditions MNCs can be significantly excited by very small changes in [Na+]o (e.g., +4 mM 79 ) and therefore, the underlying mechanism must account for this high degree of sensitivity. In principle, at least four categories of mechanisms could mediate cell autonomous [Na+]o detection. First, [Na+]o sensitive receptors (e.g., GPCR) could engage a cascade of second messenger signaling that leads to depolarization and excitation. Although sodium ions can indeed allosterically modulate some GPCRs, such as the M2 muscarinic receptor, 114 the affinity of this effect is not compatible with a role in detecting physiological changes in [Na+]o. Second, the modulation of electrogenic transporters or pumps, such as the Na/K ATPase, could theoretically mediate a membrane current capable of modulating membrane potential and action potential firing. Although the Na/K pump is highly sensitive to changes in intracellular [Na+] and extracellular [K+], its sensitivity to changes in [Na+]o is insufficient to mediate physiological sodium detection. 115 A third possible mechanism for [Na+]o detection consists of the shift in steady‐state leak sodium current (I Na) caused by changing the chemical gradient across Na+‐permeable channels active at rest (see below). A fourth mechanism would consist of the activation of sodium or cation permeable channels caused by an increase in [Na+]o.

Sensitivity to [Na+]o reflects increased sodium conductance

Voltage clamp recordings from rat MNCs in brain slices have shown that under constant osmotic conditions, an increase in [Na+]o of +4 mM causes an average inward current of −2 pA at a holding voltage of −50 mV. Under current clamp, the same stimulus causes membrane depolarization and excitation (Figure 5A,B). 79 Remarkably, current clamp recordings confirm that stimuli as small as 1 pA are sufficient to change the action potential firing rate of MNCs (e.g., Figure 6A,B). This striking degree of responsiveness is made possible by a simple dendritic morphology, 116 , 117 high input resistance (~700 MΩ) and amplification of the depolarizing effect by ion channels showing voltage‐sensitivity at near threshold potentials. 118 , 119 However, can a current of 2 pA be generated by a simple change in leak I Na?

Assuming an intracellular [Na+] of 14 mM, the Nernst equation predicts that increasing [Na+]o from 140 to 144 mM would change the equilibrium potential for sodium (E Na) from +60 mV to +60.73 mV, an increase in driving force (DF) of 0.73 mV. If half the input conductance of a 700 MΩ MNC (1.43 nS) was mediated by sodium‐permeable channels (i.e., GNa = 0.72 nS), Ohm's law (I Na = GNa × DF) predicts that the change in leak I Na would be −0.53 pA. This value is an overestimate since K+ and Cl− conductances are likely greater than GNa at rest. Therefore, a simple DF‐mediated increase in leak current cannot account for the full I Na induced by a 4 mM increase in [Na+]o. Rather, Ohm's law indicates that GNa must somehow be increased by the stimulus. Indeed, current–voltage analysis of isolated MNCs revealed that the current induced by an isoosmotic increase in [Na+]o is accompanied by a significant increase of GNa, 79 indicating that sodium channel activation occurs in concert with a shift in E Na.

Intrinsic [Na+]o detection by MNCs requires Na X

Any increase in leak current associated with a rise of [Na+]o and mediated exclusively by increased DF would be fluxed by all the subtypes of sodium permeable channels that contribute to resting GNa. However, the accompanying increase in GNa which amplifies I Na may reflect a sodium‐dependent activation (or membrane insertion) of a limited subset of sodium permeable channels. Notably, MNCs express all three subunits (α, β, γ) of epithelial sodium channels (ENaCs), 120 , 121 a variety of non‐inactivating voltage‐sensitive sodium channels (VGSCs) 122 , 123 and non‐selective cation channels such as ΔN‐TRPV1. 84 Notably, bath application of the ENaC inhibitor benzamil has been shown to hyperpolarize MNCs, 120 indicating ENaC channels contribute to resting Na+ conductance and leak. However, as noted above, a change in leak current resulting from altered DF alone is not likely to mediate substantial excitation in response to small increases in [Na+]o. Moreover, a recent study showed that I Na induced by increasing [Na+]o in MNCs in rat hypothalamic slices is not significantly affected by benzamil or blockers of VGSCs (tetrodotoxin) and ΔN‐TRPV1 (SB‐366791) 79 (Figure 7A,B). These observations suggest such channels do not contribute significantly to the increase in GNa caused by an increase in [Na+]o under normal conditions. However, the impact of ENaCs and VGSCs may emerge in response to high dietary salt intake, which increases the expression of these channels by MNCs. 119 , 121 The contribution of ΔN‐TRPV1 to GNa also increases under acute hyperosmotic conditions 84 and these conditions are known to enhance [Na+]o sensitivity. 36 , 66 Although the activity or density of ENaCs, VGSCs, ΔN‐TRPV1 and other sodium permeable channels may contribute significantly to [Na+]o detection under various stimulated conditions, they do not appear to contribute to an acute increase in [Na+]o delivered under isotonic conditions.

Na X is an atypical member of the family of VGSCs which is encoded by the Scn7A gene. 124 Although it is homologous to other VGSCs, it is insensitive to tetrodotoxin. Moreover, it lacks charged amino acid residues within the S4 transmembrane segment that are required for voltage sensitivity and is therefore predicted to encode a voltage‐insensitive channel. In contrast to OVLT and SFO, where Na X is prominently expressed in glial cells, 29 , 63 previous work has shown that Na X is expressed in MNCs but not astrocytes in the SON 79 , 125 (Figure 8A,B). Demonstrating a functional role for Na X is not trivial because no drugs have been found that can selectively block this channel. However, recent RNA interference approaches have shown that I Na evoked by an increase in [Na+]o is significantly reduced in cells infected by adeno‐associated viruses (AAVs) designed to reduce the expression of Scn7a compared to cells infected by a control virus (Figure 9A–E). Moreover, knockdown of Scn7a expression in MNCs significantly lowers the fraction of these cells that express the activity dependent immediate early gene c‐Fos following acute NaCl injection in vivo. A potential confounding factor is that injection of NaCl increases both serum osmolality and [Na+]. However, no differences in cFOS expression were observed when animals were injected with hypertonic sucrose, which increased serum osmolality without increasing [Na+]o. 79 These results indicate that Na X channels expressed in MNCs contribute to the detection of increases in [Na+]o under physiological conditions in vivo.

Intrinsic responses to Na+ and osmolality set gain and bias

Concluding comments

This review has highlighted molecular and cellular mechanisms by which MNCs can intrinsically detect changes in ECF osmolality and [Na+]o. It is important to remember that these cell‐autonomous features are superimposed on a wide array of extrinsic mechanisms, including other ion channels, paracrine effects mediated by adjacent cells, and synaptic inputs from other osmosensitive areas, including the SFO, OVLT, and periphery (Figure 1). This remarkable degree of redundancy presumably explains why knocking out an individual gene, such as Trpv1, has failed to block c‐FOS responses or VP release induced by osmotic stimulation in vivo, 126 , 127 particularly in transgenic animals where gene expression has been prevented throughout the body and throughout development and adulthood. These conditions may thus allow compensatory changes in the expression of other genes that effectively minimize the absence of the targeted (knockout) gene. In this context, the AAV‐mediated knockdown experiments reviewed here are quite powerful and important. These experiments not only show that acute reduction of NaX expression impairs [Na+]o detection by adult MNCs in vitro and in vivo, they also provide clear evidence that the intrinsic sensory properties of MNCs contribute significantly to the regulation of these cells during dynamic systemic challenges to hydromineral homeostasis, as originally hypothesized by Leng et al. 128

Previous work has shown that the osmotic control of VP release can be modulated. 129 For example, osmotically induced VP release is sensitized by high salt intake, 98 hypovolemia 130 , 131 and during pregnancy. 132 , 133 Such effects could be mediated by changes in a variety of extrinsic and intrinsic mechanisms. Interestingly, it has been shown that the depth of membrane pits induced by mild osmotic stimulation increases as a function of the diameter of underlying F‐actin fenestra. One intriguing possibility, therefore, is that a modulation of the actin cytoskeleton could enhance intrinsic osmosensitivity by increasing the average diameter of fenestrae. Additional work is required to test this hypothesis.

Lastly, it is useful to remember that by altering the gap between resting voltage and action potential threshold (Figure 1), the intrinsic effects of [Na+] and osmolality on the membrane potential of MNCs can alter the magnitude of electrical responses synaptically promoted by other homeostatic signals (e.g., blood volume, arterial pressure and body temperature). For example, under hypoosmotic conditions, the relative hyperpolarization of the resting potential may impede the excitatory effects of inputs from thermosensitive areas, 134 diminishing water conservation under conditions that promote evaporative water loss. 83 As such, the intrinsic properties of MNCs do not only affect the relative gain of their osmoresponsiveness; they also impose a bias to the impact of other homeostatic signals according to the prevailing osmotic/natremic state.
