Pulmonary tuberculosis versus Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in children: A retrospective analysis of clinical and imaging characteristics
Authors: Renjie Qi, Qiushi Yang, Huan Li, Zong Wan, Shusong Ruan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to examine the differences in clinical and imaging characteristics between pulmonary tuberculosis and Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in pediatric patients. A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 37 cases of pulmonary tuberculosis and 34 cases of M pneumoniae pneumonia diagnosed at the Guiyang Public Health Treatment Center between January 2023 and October 2024. The analysis focused on comparing the epidemiological characteristics, clinical presentations, laboratory findings, and imaging features of the 2 groups. The age range of the pulmonary tuberculosis group was 10 to 15 years, which was higher compared to the 5 to 10 years age range of the M pneumoniae pneumonia group. The incidence rates of fever (50.00% vs 10.81%), cough (91.18% vs 64.86%), and expectoration (52.94% vs 29.73%) were greater in the M pneumoniae pneumonia group than in the pulmonary tuberculosis group. Conversely, the pulmonary tuberculosis group exhibited significantly higher positive rates for the γ-interferon release test (86.49% vs 20.59%) and the tuberculin skin test (75.68% vs 26.47%), as well as a greater incidence of hypoalbuminemia (13.51% vs 0). A 4-fold increase in the titer of mycoplasma antibody was observed in 32.43% of cases. Pulmonary tuberculosis was more frequently associated with cavity formation (10.8% vs 0) and calcification (16.2% vs 0), whereas M pneumoniae pneumonia was primarily characterized by lung consolidation (17.6% vs 2.7%) and bilateral upper lobe involvement (29.4% vs 10.3%). Significant differences were observed in age distribution, immunological markers, and imaging characteristics between children with tuberculosis and those with M pneumoniae pneumonia. However, there is a substantial overlap in clinical symptoms. It is recommended to implement immunological screening for tuberculosis in children diagnosed with M pneumoniae pneumonia.

1. Introduction

Tuberculosis represents a significant infectious disease that poses a substantial threat to pediatric health.[1,2] Current data indicate that annually, 7.5 million children globally are infected with mycobacterium tuberculosis, and 1.1 million of these children develop active tuberculosis, constituting approximately 12% of the global tuberculosis burden.[3] China is among the countries with the highest tuberculosis burden, exhibiting an incidence rate of 61 per 1,00,000 individuals. The prevalence of tuberculosis infection varies across the country, with notably higher rates in western regions, such as Guizhou Province, compared to eastern regions, such as Beijing.[4] The diverse clinical manifestations and low bacterial load characteristic of childhood tuberculosis complicate the acquisition of microbiological evaluation specimens, often leading to misdiagnosis, missed diagnosis, or delayed diagnosis. Consequently, the mortality rate associated with childhood tuberculosis is rising, with approximately 2,30,000 deaths occurring annually.[3,5] Furthermore, Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia is a prevalent etiology of respiratory tract infections in pediatric populations, with 10% to 40% of cases being self-limiting and presenting with symptoms such as fever and cough. The diagnostic criterion traditionally relies on a 4-fold increase in M pneumoniae antibody titer.[6] However, serological testing is often ineffective for early diagnosis due to the frequent absence of detectable serum antibodies in the initial stages of infection.[7] This limitation, coupled with issues such as inappropriate antibiotic use, bacterial resistance, and clinical misdiagnosis, can result in severe and potentially life-threatening pneumonia in some patients.[8–10] The demographic at risk for pulmonary tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia overlaps, and their radiographic presentations are similar, while the slow growth rate of the bacteria in vitro further complicates accurate diagnosis and treatment.[7,11,12] Currently, there is a paucity of comparative studies on these 2 conditions. This research seeks to establish a foundation for early differential diagnosis by conducting a comparative analysis of the clinical characteristics of pulmonary tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia in children.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Subjects of the study

The confirmed cases of children admitted to the Guiyang Public Health Treatment Center from January 2023 to October 2024 were retrospectively collected.

2.2. Inclusion criteria

M pneumoniae pneumonia group: the patients met the diagnostic criteria for M pneumoniae pneumonia set by the Respiratory Group of the Pediatrics Branch of the Chinese Medical Association,[13,14] and also met the following criteria: they met the diagnostic criteria for community-acquired pneumonia; the titer of serum mycoplasma antibodies increased by 4 times or the respiratory tract specimens were positive for PCR; and the pulmonary lesions improved significantly after anti-mycoplasma treatment.

Pulmonary tuberculosis group: the patients met the diagnostic criteria for pulmonary tuberculosis[15] and met the following conditions: chest CT showed new infiltrative shadows; sputum/bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was positive for acid-fast staining, Xpert MTB/RIF or mycobacterium tuberculosis culture; and there was no improvement in pulmonary lesions after anti-infective therapy.

2.3. Exclusion criteria

Patients who had primary pulmonary tuberculosis, clinically diagnosed pulmonary tuberculosis, infection with other pathogens, immunodeficiency diseases, congenital lung diseases and incomplete clinical data needed to be excluded. This study was reviewed and approved by the hospital ethics committee and informed consent is exempted.

2.4. Data collection

The following data were extracted through the medical record system: gender, age, time of onset, clinical manifestations, imaging features, and laboratory results.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Count data were presented as the number of cases (percentage) n (%), and comparisons between groups were made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method. SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk) was used for all statistical analyses. The significance level for statistical analysis was set at P < .05.

As illustrated in Table 1, the study cohort comprised 37 patients diagnosed with secondary pulmonary tuberculosis and 34 patients diagnosed with M pneumoniae pneumonia. The onset age for patients with pulmonary tuberculosis predominantly ranged from 11 to 15 years, whereas for those with M pneumoniae pneumonia, the onset age predominantly ranged from 5 to 10 years. Statistical analysis revealed that the onset age for the pulmonary tuberculosis group was significantly higher than that of the M pneumoniae pneumonia group. Additionally, the gender distribution between the 2 groups was relatively balanced, with no statistically significant difference observed.

3.1. Clinical characteristics

Upon admission, the clinical symptoms and signs of the 2 patient groups were comparable (Table 2). However, the incidences of fever (50.00% vs 10.81%), cough (91.18% vs 64.86%), and expectoration (52.94% vs 29.73%) were significantly higher in the group with M pneumoniae pneumonia compared to the pulmonary tuberculosis group (P < .001, P = .003, P = .035). A minority of patients exhibited hemoptysis and chest pain, with those in the pulmonary tuberculosis group more frequently presenting with an asymptomatic onset (13.51% vs 2.94%). The majority of patients in both groups experienced an onset time of 1 to 4 weeks (P = .629), and only a limited number of cases displayed pulmonary signs, which did not reach statistical significance.

3.2. Laboratory results

The data presented in Table 3 indicate that, in comparison to the M pneumoniae pneumonia cohort, the pulmonary tuberculosis group exhibited significantly elevated positive rates for the γ-interferon release assay (86.49% vs 20.59%) and the tuberculin skin test (75.68% vs 26.47%), with statistical significance (P < .001). Additionally, within the pulmonary tuberculosis group, 32.43% of the pediatric patients demonstrated a 4-fold increase in mycoplasma antibody titers. The reduction in albumin levels was notably more pronounced in the pulmonary tuberculosis group (P = .026), whereas the incidence of elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate was higher among patients with M pneumoniae pneumonia (P = .046). A minority of patients in both groups exhibited fluctuations in white blood cell counts, as well as mild elevations in C-reactive protein and d-dimer levels; however, procalcitonin levels remained within normal ranges, with no significant differences observed between the groups.

3.3. Imaging features

Table 4 indicates that intrapulmonary lesions in pulmonary tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia can occur in any lung lobe, presenting as either unilateral lung segment onset (67.6% vs 47.1%) or bilateral multi-segment onset (29.7% vs 52.9%). Lesions predominantly appear in the upper lobes, with middle lobe involvement being rare. Bilateral upper lobe involvement is more frequent in M pneumoniae pneumonia (P = .038). Both diseases commonly exhibit patchy shadows in imaging (P = .761), with occasional nodular opacities, ground-glass opacities, or pleural effusion. Pulmonary tuberculosis is more associated with cavitation and calcifications (P = .048, P = .014), whereas M pneumoniae pneumonia is more linked to lung consolidation (P = .035).

4. Discussion

Tuberculosis represents a significant respiratory disease contributing to health challenges among children, comprising approximately 12% of the global tuberculosis patient population and exhibiting a mortality rate of around 20.5%.[4,16] Similarly, M pneumoniae is a predominant pathogen responsible for respiratory tract infections in children, implicated in 10% to 40% of community-acquired pneumonia cases.[17] Beyond causing upper respiratory tract infections, M pneumoniae can lead to bronchitis, pneumonia, and severe extrapulmonary complications, which may pose life-threatening risks.[18]

In this study, it was observed that patients with pulmonary tuberculosis were more likely to develop the condition between the ages of 11 and 15 years, whereas patients with M pneumoniae pneumonia were more likely to develop the condition between the ages of 5 and 10 years. These findings are consistent with those reported in previous studies.[10,17,19] However, the onset duration for both groups was predominantly between 1 and 4 weeks, and the gender distribution was relatively balanced, with no statistically significant differences observed. Furthermore, patients with M pneumoniae pneumonia exhibited a higher propensity for symptoms such as fever, cough, wheezing, night sweats, and weight loss compared to those with tuberculosis.

In this study, patients with pulmonary tuberculosis demonstrated a higher likelihood of testing positive for both the Interferon Gamma Release Assay and the purified protein derivative test, aligning with findings reported by the World Health Organization.[20] Emaciation is frequently observed as a symptom of tuberculosis in children,[21] with a reported prevalence of 30%. Consequently, serum albumin levels were included in our statistical analysis, revealing a prevalence rate of 13.51%. Despite the diagnostic criterion for M pneumoniae pneumonia being a 4-fold increase in antibody titer, approximately 32.43% of patients in this study were coinfected with both mycoplasma tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia. A minority of patients in both cohorts exhibited variations in white blood cell count, mild elevations in C-reactive protein and d-dimer levels, while procalcitonin levels remained normal, showing no significant differences. Notably, an accelerated erythrocyte sedimentation rate was more prevalent among patients with M pneumoniae pneumonia.

In patients diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia, imaging studies frequently reveal involvement of the upper lobes of the lungs, which may be unilateral or bilateral. Mycobacterium tuberculosis preferentially colonizes regions of the upper lobes characterized by high partial pressures of oxygen, attributable to its aerobic nature. Conversely, M pneumoniae pneumonia is capable of disseminating along the bronchial tree due to its ciliary toxic effects, resulting in a significant incidence of peribronchial inflammation in the bilateral upper lobes. Lesions confined to the middle lobe are relatively uncommon, whereas bilateral upper lobe involvement is more prevalent among patients with M pneumoniae pneumonia. Both conditions predominantly present as patchy opacities on imaging; however, cavitary lesions and calcifications are more frequently observed in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. In this study, 4 children (representing 10.08%) exhibited cavities characterized by gas-containing spaces formed following the expulsion of caseous necrotic materials, indicative of active lesions. Additionally, 6 children (representing 16.2%) displayed calcified shadows, which suggest a history of infection. Furthermore, M pneumoniae pneumonia is more frequently associated with consolidation shadows and nodular shadows without structural damage, aligning with existing literature.[15,22] Consequently, in clinical practice, it is imperative to conduct a differential diagnosis by integrating the distribution characteristics and morphological details of the lesions. For instance, the presence of cavities in the upper lobes, accompanied by satellite lesions or calcification, should raise a strong suspicion of pulmonary tuberculosis.

For pediatric patients suspected of having pulmonary tuberculosis, it is advisable to employ molecular biological tests utilizing multiple specimen sources to enhance diagnostic accuracy, particularly in cases where high-quality sputum specimens are unobtainable. In pediatric patients with M pneumoniae pneumonia infection, it is imperative to rule out coinfection with pulmonary tuberculosis. Regarding treatment, antibiotics should be judiciously selected based on the pathogen type and drug sensitivity test results to prevent the escalation of antibiotic resistance due to misuse. For patients with pulmonary tuberculosis, adherence to a standardized treatment regimen is crucial to ensure comprehensive, consistent, appropriately dosed, and combination therapy. In the case of M pneumoniae pneumonia, it is essential to monitor drug resistance and adjust the treatment plan promptly.

Nonetheless, the study is subject to certain limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective analysis, it may be affected by selection bias. Secondly, the constraints of current detection technologies precluded the identification of other potential pathogens. Thirdly, the study population was exclusively composed of pediatric patients from a tuberculosis hospital. Future research should focus on multicenter studies with larger sample sizes to enhance the generalizability of the results.

5. Conclusion

The study demonstrated significant disparities in age distribution, immunological markers, and imaging characteristics between childhood pulmonary tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia. Nonetheless, there was a substantial overlap in clinical symptoms, and mixed infections were prevalent. Notably, in regions with a high tuberculosis burden, the coinfection rate of tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia was observed to be 17.6%.[23] In regions with a high prevalence of tuberculosis, it is advised that children diagnosed with M pneumoniae pneumonia undergo tuberculosis screening if they meet any of the following criteria: lack of significant improvement in clinical symptoms following 72 hours of antibiotic therapy; radiological findings indicating lesions in either the unilateral upper or lower lobes, or the presence of calcified lesions or cavities; concurrent hypoalbuminemia (albumin levels below 35 g/L). The recommended screening approach involves the combined use of the interferon gamma release assay and the tuberculin skin test. Whenever feasible, the rapid detection method endorsed by the World Health Organization (Xpert Ultra) should be employed alongside suitable specimens from pediatric patients – such as feces, nasopharyngeal aspirates, induced sputum, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or gastric juice – to achieve bacteriological confirmation. This study elucidated the age groups most susceptible to childhood pulmonary tuberculosis and M pneumoniae pneumonia, as well as the differences in clinical manifestations, laboratory test characteristics, and imaging features associated with these conditions. The findings can assist clinicians in making more precise differential diagnoses, thereby reducing the incidence of missed or misdiagnosed cases. Particularly in instances of mixed infections, the study offers a more comprehensive diagnostic framework. From a public health perspective, identifying the ages most at risk for these diseases facilitates the strategic allocation of prevention and control resources, enables targeted screening and prevention efforts in child-concentrated environments such as schools, and enhances the early detection rates of these diseases. Moreover, the study’s data on the prevalence of emaciation and the analysis of indicators, such as the titers of M pneumoniae pneumonia antibodies, provide foundational data for future in-depth research into the pathogenesis and influencing factors of these diseases. The findings of this study hold significant implications for the protection of pediatric respiratory health, the refinement of clinical treatment protocols, and the development of public health strategies.

Despite the relatively small sample size of this study, the findings possess a certain degree of generalizability. Firstly, the research participants exhibit a high level of homogeneity in key characteristics, as they are all children aged 0 to 15 years, demonstrating consistency in age, living environment, and basic health status. This homogeneity enhances the generalizability of the conclusions within this specific demographic. Secondly, the study’s conclusions align with those of numerous prior large-scale studies, thereby reinforcing the universality of the findings. Additionally, the study employed rigorous data analysis methods, effectively mitigating biases and confounding factors to ensure the reliability of the results. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that the limited sample size may constrain the broader applicability of the conclusions. Future research should focus on conducting larger-scale, multicenter studies to further validate and expand the generalizability of the study’s findings.
