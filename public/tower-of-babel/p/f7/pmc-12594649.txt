Fungal Infections – a Stealthy Enemy in Patients with Chronic Granulomatous Disease: a 28-years’ Experience from North India
Authors: Pandiarajan Vignesh, Sanjib Mondal, Ridhima Aggarwal, Sangeetha Siniah, Sathish Kumar Loganathan, Dharmagat Bhattarai, Jhumki Das, Sumit Goel, Kaushal Sharma, Harsimran Kaur, Aravind Sekar, Manpreet Dhaliwal, Saniya Sharma, Rakesh Kumar Pilania, Ankur Kumar Jindal, Deepti Suri, Kirti Gupta, Shivaprakash M Rudramurthy, Amit Rawat, Surjit Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungal infections contribute to a significant disease burden in patients with chronic granulomatous disease (CGD). While the presentation may differ depending on genotype and environmental exposure to fungus, associated mortality rates are universally high. This is a retrospective study wherein medical records of patients diagnosed with CGD from 1994 to 2022 at our center in North India were reviewed. We categorized patients into 2 groups: patients with proven invasive fungal infection and patients with probable invasive fungal infections. We analyzed demographic details, clinical details, and the overall outcome of these patients. We identified 40 patients (40.4%) with fungal infections in our cohort of 99 patients with CGD. Twenty-one patients had proven invasive fungal infection, and 19 had probable invasive fungal infection. Pneumonia was the most common presentation in our cohort (n = 35; 87.5%). Two patients had isolated osteomyelitis, 1 had brain abscess, and 2 had candidemia. Aspergillus sp. was isolated most frequently (n = 27), followed by Candida sp. (n = 5), Mucorales sp. (n = 2), Paecilomyces sp. (n = 1) and Fusarium dimerum (n = 1). Aspergillus fumigatus (n = 11) was the most identified species of Aspergillus. Eleven patients had bacterial co-infection. Twenty-one patients (52.5%) died in this cohort. Risk factors for mortality identified are X-linked CGD (p = 0.027), bacterial coinfection (p = 0.003) and infection in infancy (p = 0.045). Aspergillus sp. is the most common offending fungus, and pneumonia is the most common manifestation of fungal infection in our patients with CGD. Infants, males, and patients with bacterial coinfection should be treated more aggressively amongst patients with CGD with fungal infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-025-01940-9.Keywords: Chronic granulomatous disease, Fungal, Aspergillus sp., Pneumonia, Autopsy, Bacterial co-infection, X-linked CGD, Infants, Anti-tubercular therapy

Introduction

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is an inborn error of immunity (IEI) caused by a defective respiratory burst in phagocytes due to mutations in genes encoding protein subunits of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase. Patients present with recurrent infections, with pneumonia being the most common presentation [1, 2]. Fungal infections do occur in these patients, with Aspergillus sp. being frequently isolated [2]. These infections are often difficult to identify and treat, and are associated with high rates of mortality, especially if they progress to invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IA) [2]. Though the incidence and spectrum of fungal infections in cohorts of CGD patients have previously been reported, data on clinical manifestations, management, and long-term follow-up are scarce.

The clinical presentation of fungal infections in CGD differs depending on the genotype, degree of reduction of NADPH oxidase and environmental exposure to fungus. As India is a populous tropical country, most of our patients are exposed to moist, humid and overcrowded conditions. Many patients are routinely exposed to farmlands and a variety of animals by virtue of their agricultural practices. We expect the spectrum of fungal infections in India to be different compared to other countries. Here, we report the profile of fungal infections, clinical presentation, management and long-term outcome in a cohort of 99 patients with CGD from North India.

Methodology

A review of clinical records was carried out for patients registered at the Pediatric Immunodeficiency Clinic of the Department of Pediatrics, Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh, India between January,1994 and December, 2022. Diagnosis of CGD was based on nitroblue tetrazolium test and dihydrorhodamine assay in granulocytes [3]. Sanger sequencing or Next Generation Sequencing using a targeted gene panel for primary immunodeficiencies was performed for molecular confirmation whenever necessary [4].

We categorized patients into 2 groups – patients with proven invasive fungal infection and patients with probable invasive fungal infections, as per the 2020 revision and update of consensus definitions of invasive fungal diseases by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) [5]. A proven invasive fungal infection was diagnosed based on microscopic analysis (yeast, pseudo-hyphae, septate or aseptate hyphae in histology) or culture of sterile material (excluding sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid) or blood. Probable invasive fungal infection was considered based on clinical symptomology, radiological evidence, presence of elevated fungal markers (beta-D-glucan and galactomannan) either in serum or BAL fluid, positive fungal serology, or detection of fungal antigen by gel diffusion technology, all in the setting of an existing phagocytic immunodeficiency. Isolation of fungus from sputum and BAL fluid was also considered to be consistent with a probable invasive fungal infection. The yeast identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) while moulds were identified based on morphological characters in lactophenol cotton blue (LCB) mount. The isolates which were not identified by the above methods were subjected to PCR-sequencing targeting the internal transcribed spacer region (ITS region) of ribosomal DNA as described previously [6].

We followed the standard practice of antimicrobial prophylaxis (cotrimoxazole and itraconazole) in our patients [7]; however, we did not use interferon-gamma. Compliance to the antimicrobial prophylaxis was assessed at every outpatient follow-up visit. Therapeutic doses of antifungal medications were used whenever there was a suspected or confirmed fungal infection, and the duration of therapy was decided based on the assessment of clearance of infections.

We analyzed demographic details, clinical details, radiological findings, and the overall outcome of these patients. Statistical analysis was carried out with SPSS statistical software version 20.0. For comparison of two groups, the Mann-Whitney U test has been used (p < 0.05 considered to be significant). The study was approved by the Departmental Review Board of the Advanced Pediatric Centre of our institute (No. 03–24, dt. 22.03.2024).

Results

We identified 40 patients (40.4%) with fungal infections in our cohort of 99 patients with CGD (Table 1) [3, 8–20]. Twenty-one patients had proven invasive fungal infection, and 19 had probable invasive fungal infection. In the group of patients with proven invasive fungal infections, we identified 25 episodes of fungal infection (Three patients - P6, P16, P25 had 2 separate episodes of fungal infection). Fungus was isolated during hospital stay in 16 patients (28 episodes), with evidence of fungal infection being discovered post-mortem during autopsy in the remaining 5 patients (P33, P34, P36, P37, P38).

Fungal infection was documented in 16 patients with gp91-phox defect CGD, 23 patients with autosomal recessive CGD (p47-phox = 16, p67-phox = 6, unclassified = 1) and 1 patient with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency CGD. Males were predominantly affected (n = 33; 82.5%) in comparison to females (n = 7; 17.5%). Median age of onset of fungal infection was 24 months (Range: 0.5–204 months). Median delay in diagnosis of fungal infection was 30 days (Range: 0–107 days). The first episode of fungal infection was diagnosed at the time of diagnosis of CGD itself in 24 patients. Three patients had evidence of fungal infection prior to the diagnosis of CGD, and 13 patients who were already diagnosed with CGD on antifungal prophylaxis developed fungal infection in follow-up.

We identified significant differences in the age of diagnosis of CGD (median: 9 months vs. 60 months), age at diagnosis of fungal infections (median: 22.5 months vs. 66 months), and mortality due to fungal infections (75% vs. 37%) when we compared CYBB CGD with autosomal forms of CGD (p values 0.003, 0.003, and 0.027, respectively).

Microbiological Profile

Aspergillus sp. was most frequently isolated (n = 27), followed by Candida sp. (n = 5), Mucor sp. (n = 2), Paecilomyces sp. (n = 1) and Fusarium dimerum (n = 1) from various sites (Table 2). Aspergillus fumigatus (n = 11) was the most common species of Aspergillus, followed by Aspergillus flavus (n = 5). The second most common fungus isolated was Candida sp. (n = 5) (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Eleven patients had concurrent proven bacterial infections (Table 1).

Systemic Involvement and Mortality

The respiratory system (n = 35; 87.5%) was predominantly affected in our cohort. Two patients had isolated osteomyelitis (P21, P40), 1 had brain abscess (P12), and 2 (P35, P38) had candidemia. We observed a high mortality rate (n = 21; 52.5%) amongst patients with CGD with fungal infection.

Pneumonia

Almost all patients (n = 33/35; 94.28%) presented with low-grade fever. The majority (31/35; 88.5%) had a cough. Weight loss was present in 12 (34.28%) patients, and 17 (48.57%) had breathing difficulty. Clubbing was noted in 3 (8.57%) patients. However, 4 patients (P3, P23, P27, P34) did not have any respiratory symptoms. Median duration of symptoms was 30 days (range 4 days – 365 days). Seven (20%) patients (P7, P11, P14, P15, P18, P20, P34) had an acute onset (symptoms for < 2 weeks) of presentation, while most had a subacute to chronic presentation (n = 28, 80%). All patients had received multiple courses of antimicrobials before reaching our institute. A significant proportion of patients (9/28, 32.14%) with chronic symptoms had also received empirical anti-tubercular therapy (combination of isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol and pyrazinamide).

All 35 patients had radiological evidence of pneumonia. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) chest was done in 27 patients. Findings noted were lobar consolidation, nodules, ground glass opacity, parenchymal fibrosis, bronchiectasis, pleural effusion, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, calcified lesions and thickening of the fissures (Fig. 1). Two patients (P7, P36; Mucor sp. and Aspergillus sp., respectively) had a right upper lobe circumscribed mass. There was evidence of contiguous spread of infection from parenchyma to ribs in 5 patients (P7, P13, P16, P20, P31); P13, P20 also had vertebral osteomyelitis (Fig. 1).

Of the 7 patients with CGD for whom we have autopsy and post-mortem histopathology findings from our center, 5 (P33, P34, P36, P37, P38) were noted to have evidence of fungal infection in lung tissues. It is imperative to note that only 2 (P37, P38) of these 5 patients had ante-mortem evidence of fungal infection.

At presentation, all were initiated on broad-spectrum antimicrobial coverage. If there was an inadequate clinical response, antibiotics were escalated. Subsequently, all received antifungal therapy from the 35th (median) day of onset of symptoms. Median duration between hospital admission and initiation of antifungals was 5.5 days (range 2 days – 7 days). Most of the patients were treated with amphotericin B (n = 29) followed by voriconazole (n = 21), caspofungin (n = 2), micafungin (n = 1), posaconazole (n = 2), itraconazole (n = 1) and fluconazole (n = 1). Combined antifungal therapy, such as amphotericin with micafungin (n = 1), amphotericin with posaconazole (n = 2), and amphotericin with voriconazole (n = 5), was used in 8 patients. A combination of triple antifungal has been used with amphotericin, caspofungin and voriconazole in a single patient (P23) with Candida lusitaniae pneumonia. Three patients (P18, P26, P37) were switched over from amphotericin B to voriconazole due to nephrotoxicity and 2 (P2, P27) were changed from voriconazole to amphotericin B due to hepatotoxicity. After discharge, most of the patients were switched from amphotericin B to oral voriconazole. Two patients (P16, P29) with proven Aspergillus pneumonia who did not respond to amphotericin B and voriconazole had a good response to oral posaconazole (200 mg twice for 3 months). Median duration of antifungal therapy in cases with respiratory involvement was 6 weeks (Range: 4 to 12 weeks). Patients with pneumonia and bone involvement received prolonged antifungal therapy (Median: 24 weeks). One patient (P31) with pulmonary aspergillosis was treated with pioglitazone for 3 months in addition to antifungal therapy, however the patient did not improve and subsequently succumbed to the illness. Glucocorticoid was used in 3 patients (P17, P28, P32) with lung granuloma and aspergillosis (low dose oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day in tapering doses)) and in 2 (P2, P38) with evidence of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH).

Surgical interventions were needed in 4 patients (P7, P12, P20, P31). One child (P7) who underwent resection of right upper lobe lung mass had evidence of pulmonary mucor-mycosis on biopsy. Two patients (P20, P31) required rib resection for contiguous rib osteomyelitis. One child (P12) with brain abscess underwent decompressive hemicraniectomy. Twenty-one (52.5%) patients succumbed to illness. Lung abscess was noted in 3 patients (P33, P34, P38) on autopsy and 2 patients (P36, P38) had evidence of HLH in lymph node and splenic tissue.

Osteomyelitis (Other than ribs)

Two patients (P21, P40) had isolated osteomyelitis (Left humeral osteomyelitis and right frontal bone osteomyelitis). Both had fever, swelling, and pain over the local site at the time of presentation. The patient with left humeral shaft osteomyelitis underwent diagnostic bone biopsy, which revealed Aspergillus terreus on culture, and was treated with voriconazole for 48 weeks. The patient with right frontal bone osteomyelitis presented with scalp swelling, pain and pus discharge from the site. Pus culture revealed Aspergillus fumigatus. He also had fungal endocarditis and was treated with voriconazole for 48 weeks.

Brain Abscess

One patient (P12) had a fungal brain abscess. He presented with intermittent headache, fever and vomiting for a duration of more than 6 months. Imaging revealed bilateral nodular enhancing lesions. Culture from the biopsy revealed Aspergillus nidulans. He underwent hemicraniectomy with cerebral decompensation. However, he did not survive.

Bloodstream Infection (BSI)

Culture-proven bloodstream infection due to Candida albicans was noted in 2 patients [P35, P38]; one had associated pneumonia and HLH, while the other had concomitant pulmonary aspergillosis.

Complications due to Therapy

Adverse effects with amphotericin (n = 3) and voriconazole (n = 2) were noted. Two patients (P18, P37) on amphotericin developed nephrotoxicity, and one (P26) had hypokalemia-induced ileus. Similarly, transaminitis was noted in 2 patients (P2, P27) treated with voriconazole. Therapy was temporarily withheld and reinitiated once liver function tests normalized. Voriconazole-related phototoxicity was noted in the same 2 patients (P2, P27) requiring stoppage of medication.

Follow-up Course

Mortality due to fungal infection was noted in 21 patients (52.5%) (pneumonia: 19, brain abscess: 1, BSI: 1). Patients with X-linked CGD, male infants, bacterial coinfection and diffuse lung involvement were identified as risk factors for mortality. Four patients with CGD and fungal infections died after a mean duration of 1.25 years of follow-up due to other causes. Three patients were lost to follow-up. The remaining twelve patients are on regular follow-up with a mean follow-up duration of 4.5 years. Three patients (P6, P16, P39) underwent successful hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) (2 - haploidentical, 1 - matched sibling donor), and the remaining patients are being managed with antimicrobial prophylaxis.

Discussion

We analyzed the clinical profile, management, and outcome of patients with CGD with fungal infection in our cohort from North India. The incidence of fungal infection was 40.4%, with the most common manifestation being pneumonia. This is in line with previously published literature on the same [1, 21, 22]. Common clinical presentations include prolonged fever, respiratory symptoms, and weight loss. However, of note, 11% (4/35) of patients with fungal pneumonia had no respiratory symptoms at all. Similarly, Salvator et al. reported that in a cohort of adult CGD patients, 37% of patients with invasive fungal infection were asymptomatic and often required biopsies for diagnosis [23]. This highlights the need to screen patients with CGD for underlying fungal infection in the presence of unexplained constitutional symptoms at every visit.

Patients with CGD are in clinical practice initiated on empirical antifungal therapy in the presence of inadequate response to broad-spectrum antimicrobials. However, isolation of the fungus is crucial for better management and outcome based on the drug sensitivity pattern. To isolate an organism, invasive procedures such as BAL, image-guided fine needle aspiration of the lung, or even open biopsy were needed (Table 2). Likewise, Cohen et al. have previously reported tissue biopsy leading to the correct diagnosis in most of the patients, with even repeated bronchoscopies and lung biopsies performed for isolation of fungus [24]. In fact, Marciano et al. reported a higher positivity rate for organism isolation in lung biopsy (50%) in comparison to BAL (30%) [21]. Furthermore, we isolated fungi from lung tissue in 5 patients on autopsy. Interestingly, in 3 of these patients, there was no suspicion of fungal infection antemortem, one had blood culture positivity for fungus with no respiratory symptoms, and 1 had raised serum galactomannan; however, fungus could not be isolated antemortem. Hence, it is evident that the isolation of fungi can be difficult and often requires tissue analysis for diagnosis.

In line with previously published literature, Aspergillus sp. was the most isolated fungus, with A. fumigatus being the most common species [1, 2, 21, 25]. Of note, the incidence of A. flavus was higher (5/40; 12.5%) in our cohort, unlike other studies in which A. nidulans was more common [26–28]. A. flavus is epidemiologically prevalent in North India, likely because of favourable climate conditions [29–31]. Additionally, A. flavus has been reported as the most common pathogen causing fungal sinusitis and endophthalmitis in the North Indian population [29]. A. nidulans, a specific pathogen for patients with CGD, was isolated from a single patient with a brain abscess. In contrast to previous reports, we did not observe features of mulch pneumonitis in our cohort. None of our patients revealed a history of high levels of exposure to aerosolised fungi or had an acute fulminant presentation with rapid clinical and radiographic progression as seen in mulch pneumonitis [32]. Candida sp., Paecilomyces sp., and Mucor sp. were among the other fungi isolated. The incidence of pneumonia with Candida sp. was similar (3/99; 3.03%) to that reported in previous cohorts [1, 25]; however, the overall prevalence of Candida infections is 5/99 (5.05%). We also observed that none of the patients in our cohort developed pneumonia due to Candida following initiation of itraconazole prophylaxis.

Pulmonary aspergillosis in CGD is characterized by dense pyogranulomatous areas of consolidation in the absence of vascular hyphal invasion. These findings suggest that NADPH oxidase-independent pathways are usually sufficient to protect against hyphal angioinvasion, compared to neutropenic patients, where angioinvasion predominates [33–36]. Verweji et al. showed that circulating Aspergillus antigen might remain undetected in patients with CGD with IA [37]. We ordered fungal markers on limited occasions (n = 9). BAL galactomannan positivity (6/8; 75%) was found to be highly sensitive when compared to serum galactomannan (5/9; 55%) in the presence of pulmonary fungal infection. In addition to reduced hyphal angioinvasion seen in CGD patients, this finding may also be explained by higher sensitivity of BAL galactomannan in diagnosing invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in at risk patients [38, 39]. Thus, serum galactomannan remained an less sensitive marker to diagnose pulmonary aspergillosis in our CGD population.

Yao et al. demonstrated CT chest findings in 27 patients with CGD and fungal infection. These were mediastinal lymphadenopathy (88%), ground-glass-opacity (81.48%), nodules (77%), consolidation (62%) and pleural thickening (40%) [37]. A CT chest was done in 27 patients with fungal infection in our cohort, and we noted similar findings. As the findings mimic those of pulmonary tuberculosis, segregation of the organism is the cornerstone for appropriate management. With limited literature on fungal pneumonia in pediatric CGD patients regarding the approach to investigations, we attempted to design a workflow to aid clinicians in the assessment of a new-onset respiratory infection in a child with CGD. We considered the difficulties in diagnosis and the need for low radiation exposure in children, while balancing the need for accurate diagnosis for the best overall management (Fig. 2).

All patients were treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals. Amphotericin B was the most used antifungal, followed by oral voriconazole, which was similar to other cohorts [23, 24, 27]. However, the median duration of antifungal therapy for pulmonary infection reported by Salvator et al. was higher (median 307 days) when compared to our cohort (median 42 days). This difference is likely due to a higher frequency of virulent A. nidulans and a higher rate of contiguous bone involvement in other cohorts compared to ours, and earlier deaths occurring in our cohort compared to others.

Rib osteomyelitis, a well-known complication of fungal pneumonia in CGD, was seen in 5 patients in our cohort (with 2 having concomitant vertebral involvement). Dotis et al. previously reported that A. nidulans caused pneumonia with rib osteomyelitis more frequently (p = 0.001) than A. fumigatus in patients with CGD [40]. However, A. fumigatus was found to be the most common inciting fungus with this complication in our cohort. This may be due to the lower prevalence of A. nidulans in our country. The overall incidence of fungal osteomyelitis (including ribs) in our cohort (7%) is similar to the American (7.88%) and Italian cohorts (6.15%) [1, 25].

Mortality rate was remarkably higher (52.5%) in our cohort in comparison to the rate reported by Marciano et al. (16.41%) [21]. Prolonged disease duration before presentation, late recognition of CGD, as well as the fungal infection, could probably account for the higher mortality. We also did not use interferon-gamma in the prophylaxis or management of fungal infections in our patients. However, we noted that Aspergillus sp. remained the most common cause of death (16/21; 76.19%) in patients with CGD with fungal infection, in accordance with previous reports [1, 21, 22, 25]. In addition, we found X-linked CGD, infancy and the presence of co-infections to be significant risk factors for mortality in these patients (Table 3). A very high mortality due to fungal infections in CGD also highlights the unmet need for the development of novel and more potent anti-fungal therapies.

Analysis of mortality risk factors in patients with CGD and fungal infections

The strength of our study is that this is the first report focused on fungal infection in pediatric CGD patients from India. We have reported the clinical profile, diagnostic difficulties, therapeutic practice, and the outcome in patients with CGD with fungal infections. However, the retrospective nature of the study and limited access to polymerase chain reaction-based diagnostics for fungal infections are some of the limitations.

To conclude, patients with CGD are prone to fungal infection, with Aspergillus sp. being the most common followed by Candida sp. We report a uniquely high incidence of Aspergillus flavus in our cohort, a finding likely related to epidemiologic prevalence of this fungus in North India. Invasive fungal infection is one of the important causes of death in patients with CGD. Identification of the underlying microbial etiology and appropriate management with sensitive antimicrobials can reduce mortality. Infancy, X-linked CGD, and bacterial coinfection may be additional risk factors for mortality in these patients.
