Feasibility of shoulder joint visualization using the 0° Arthrex Nanoscope: a cadaveric study on twenty specimens
Authors: Jędrzej Lesman, Błażej Wójtowicz, Łukasz Gołek, Michał Szufnara, Marcin Domżalski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNanoscopic technology is increasingly employed in minimally invasive orthopedic procedures, offering enhanced access to tight compartments with reduced soft-tissue disruption. This study assessed the feasibility of visualizing key anatomical structures of the shoulder joint using a 0° nanoscope in cadaveric specimens.MethodsTwenty fresh-frozen human cadaveric shoulders (10 left, 10 right) were examined by two orthopedic surgeons with different experience levels using a 1.9 mm 0° nanoscope. Standard posterior, anterior, and lateral portals were used to assess glenohumeral and subacromial compartments. Predefined landmarks were graded on a 5-point Likert scale. The primary outcome was feasibility of identifying each structure; secondary outcomes included mean visibility scores and inter-observer variability.ResultsAll predefined landmarks were identified in all 20 specimens. The senior surgeon reported an overall mean visibility score of 4.45 (SD ± 0.18), and the resident 4.18 (SD ± 0.21). Highest scores were observed for the long head of the biceps tendon and superior labrum. Lower scores occurred in the subacromial space, particularly in bursal-side rotator cuff assessment, reflecting challenges inherent to the 0° field of view. Inter-observer agreement was good (mean differences ≤ 0.3).ConclusionNanoscopic visualization of the shoulder using a 0° device is feasible and yields high visibility scores for most landmarks. Surgeon experience may influence interpretation in anatomically complex regions such as the subacromial space. Introduction

Shoulder arthroscopy remains central to the diagnosis and treatment of intra- and peri-articular disorders. Conventional 30° arthroscopes provide a wide field of view and compatibility with standard surgical instrumentation, yet needle arthroscopy—also referred to as nanoscopy—offers a markedly smaller procedural footprint and improved access to anatomically constrained regions [1, 2]. These advantages have stimulated growing interest in nanoscopic platforms for both diagnostic and therapeutic use.

The Arthrex NanoScope system (1.9 mm, 0° optics) represents one of the most advanced and widely available needle arthroscopy devices. It allows direct visualization through miniportals with minimal capsular trauma and can be applied either in the operating theatre or in an in-office setting under local anesthesia. The system’s fully integrated design provides compact visualization without the need for a standard arthroscopy tower or separate fluid management units.

While nanoscopy has been increasingly described in other joints—particularly the knee, ankle, and wrist—for diagnostic arthroscopy, cartilage assessment, or minimally invasive procedures [2–5], its application to the shoulder joint remains comparatively underexplored. Only a limited number of cadaveric studies and technical notes have reported shoulder nanoscopy, most focusing on single procedures such as biceps tenotomy, subacromial decompression, or rotator cuff inspection [3–6]. To date, no comprehensive cadaveric analysis has systematically graded the feasibility of identifying key intra-articular and subacromial landmarks using a 0° nanoscope.

The unique anatomy of the shoulder—with its deep recesses, variable capsule tension, and wide range of motion—poses distinct visualization challenges for straight (0°) optics. Furthermore, the reduced inflow pressure and absence of angled viewing may limit the surgeon’s ability to assess peripheral structures, particularly in the subacromial compartment. These constraints highlight the importance of defining which anatomic regions can be consistently visualized with the 0° Arthrex NanoScope, and whether surgeon experience influences visualization quality.

Therefore, the present cadaveric study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of visualizing predefined key anatomical landmarks of the shoulder joint using the 0° Arthrex NanoScope system. We hypothesized that this platform would provide sufficient image quality to identify most clinically relevant intra-articular and subacromial structures, with visualization quality potentially influenced by the operator’s arthroscopic experience.

Materials and methods

Ethics

All methods followed the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki (latest revision, 2013). The study was conducted at the Orthopedic Clinic, Medical University of Łódź, with ethics approval RNN/60/25/KE for cadaveric research.

Specimens

Twenty fresh-frozen human cadaveric shoulders (10 left, 10 right) without prior surgery, significant trauma, or advanced arthritic change were thawed at room temperature for 24 h before dissection. All cadaveric shoulders were obtained from the institutional body donation program of the Department of Anatomy, Medical University of Łódź (Łódź, Poland). The specimens were used in accordance with national regulations governing the use of human remains for research and education. None of the specimens were privately owned or obtained from external sources.

Observers

Two independent evaluators participated: a senior attending (> 10 years’ arthroscopy experience) and a final-year orthopedic resident. Both had prior training with the nanoscope, including practice on at least two cadaveric shoulders.

Instrumentation and setup

A 1.9-mm 0° NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) with reusable handpiece, single-use cannula, and integrated fluid management was used. Shoulders were positioned in beach chair position.

Portals

Posterior, anterior, and lateral portals were established under spinal-needle localization with small skin incisions for the nanoscope cannula. Saline inflow was provided by a pressurized bag (no pump). Visualization commenced in the glenohumeral joint (posterior portal), with anterior and lateral views added as needed.

Anatomic landmarks and scoring

Assessed structures: long head of biceps tendon; bicipital groove; superior labrum; subscapularis tendon; glenoid surface/cartilage; rotator cuff (articular side); subacromial space/bursa; rotator cuff (bursal side); acromion undersurface. Visibility was graded: 1—not visible; 2—poor; 3—moderate; 4—good; 5—very good. Scores were recorded independently post-procedure. If discrepancies > 1 point occurred, they were discussed; original scores were retained.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized mean visibility per structure and observer. Inter-observer variability was evaluated via mean differences. Tabulation was performed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Visualization of all predefined anatomical landmarks was achieved in all 20 cadaveric shoulder specimens using the 0° Arthrex NanoScope system. The long head of the biceps tendon and superior labrum were the easiest structures to identify, with the highest visibility scores in both groups (Group 1—senior surgeon: 4.50 ± 0.18; Group 2—resident: 4.20 ± 0.21; medians = 5.0 and 4.0, respectively). Visualization of the glenoid cartilage, subscapularis tendon, and articular side of the rotator cuff was consistently good across all specimens, without statistically significant inter-group differences (p > 0.05).

The subacromial compartment and bursal side of the rotator cuff proved more challenging, particularly for the less experienced observer, with slightly lower mean scores (Group 1: 4.30 ± 0.24; Group 2: 4.00 ± 0.27). The acromion undersurface demonstrated the largest inter-observer difference, which reached statistical significance (p = 0.047). Despite these differences, overall visibility remained high, confirming that key anatomic structures can be reliably identified using the 0° Arthrex NanoScope.

Mean overall visualization scores were 4.45 ± 0.18 for Group 1 and 4.18 ± 0.21 for Group 2, with median scores of 5.0 and 4.0, respectively. Group 2 demonstrated slightly greater variability, suggesting that surgical experience may influence early spatial orientation during nanoscopic navigation; however, once the joint was entered, visualization of specific landmarks did not pose significant difficulty even for the less experienced surgeon.

Discussion

This cadaveric study demonstrates that the 0° Arthrex NanoScope system enables reliable visualization of key shoulder landmarks in both glenohumeral and subacromial compartments. Compared with standard 30° arthroscopy, the 0° optics of the Arthrex NanoScope provide a direct line of sight but inherently limit off-axis visualization and spatial orientation, necessitating thoughtful portal strategy and familiarity with the optical axis [5, 7–9]. Our highest scores for the long head of the biceps tendon and superior labrum align with prior needle-arthroscopy reports emphasizing straightforward intra-articular navigation and diagnostic utility [1–4, 10].

Position within current literature

Recent studies describe in-office needle arthroscopy protocols for the shoulder and other joints, citing faster diagnosis, minimal invasiveness, and good patient tolerance [2, 4, 10, 11]. Technical notes and early clinical series have demonstrated the feasibility of performing targeted procedures—such as biceps tenotomy or tenodesis, SLAP debridement, and subacromial decompression—using the Arthrex NanoScope or comparable 0° systems [3, 4, 12, 13]. Beyond the shoulder, in-office needle arthroscopy (IONA) has been validated for second-look assessments and cartilage evaluation, confirming the diagnostic potential of these platforms [11, 14].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically grade anatomic visibility across a broad panel of shoulder structures using the 0° Arthrex NanoScope in a controlled cadaveric model. The data presented here provide foundational insight into anatomic visualization capabilities and training potential for future clinical implementation [15–19].

Subacromial challenges with 0° optics

Lower ratings in the subacromial space reflect recognized limitations of straight 0° visualization, with reduced “look-around” capability compared with angled optics. Similar constraints have been reported in knee and shoulder needle-arthroscopy experiences, where precise portal placement and deliberate triangulation are recommended to mitigate blind zones [1, 5, 7, 8]. Nevertheless, the Arthrex NanoScope offers several advantages—rapid setup, minimal soft-tissue trauma, and the potential for in-office diagnostic use under local anesthesia [2, 10, 20]. The modest reduction in visibility within the bursal space must therefore be weighed against these procedural benefits.

Learning curve and training implications

Shoulder arthroscopy has a well-documented learning curve, with simulator-based and cadaveric training proven to improve performance metrics and confidence [21, 22]. Studies suggest that dozens of cases are required for safe independent arthroscopic practice, and many more to achieve subspecialty proficiency [23]. Our findings—particularly the minimal inter-observer variability between the senior surgeon and resident—suggest that the Arthrex NanoScope presents a manageable learning curve for visualization tasks. This aligns with recent reviews highlighting the simplicity of 0° optics and their suitability for training environments [4,24].

Integrating Arthrex NanoScope modules into cadaveric courses and virtual-reality simulation programs could enhance orientation skills, optimize portal strategy, and accelerate operator familiarity with this novel platform [21,22

Clinical translation

Beyond diagnostic applications, early reports indicate that selected therapeutic procedures—such as in-office subacromial bursectomy, acromioplasty, or biceps interventions—are feasible using the Arthrex NanoScope under local anesthesia in carefully chosen patients [3, 4, 12]. Although the role of subacromial decompression in impingement without cuff tear remains debated [9, 25], nanoscopic approaches may still be valuable for targeted assessment of bursal pathology or as low-burden adjuncts when MRI findings are equivocal.

Furthermore, the expanding use of IONA platforms for cartilage assessment, ACL repair surveillance, and second-look arthroscopy in other joints [11, 14] underscores the versatility of the Arthrex NanoScope. These features may be particularly advantageous in clinical scenarios where MRI is contraindicated, access to imaging is limited, or minimally invasive real-time visualization can enhance decision-making.

Strengths and novelty

This study provides structured, multi-landmark visibility data on 20 cadaveric shoulders assessed by two surgeons with different experience levels. The design directly informs training objectives, procedural planning, and educational applications for Arthrex NanoScope-based nanoscopy.

Limitations

Cadaveric conditions cannot reproduce intraoperative variables such as bleeding, synovial distension, or patient movement. Although the sample size of 20 specimens improves precision compared with earlier series, we did not stratify by body habitus or degenerative changes. Visibility scoring, while structured, remains inherently subjective. Only a single 0° system (the Arthrex NanoScope) was tested, and no direct comparison was made with standard 30° arthroscopes.

Conclusion

A 1.9 mm 0° nanoscope allows consistent identification of key shoulder landmarks in a cadaveric model (20 specimens) with good inter-observer agreement. Despite intrinsic off-axis limitations, high visibility scores in most regions support a diagnostic role and potential adjunctive therapeutic use. These data provide a foundation for training curricula and future clinical validation.
