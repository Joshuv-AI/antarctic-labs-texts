Delays in Tuberculosis Care in the Indian Population: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Janmejaya Samal, G. S. Preetha, Hari Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Being harmful to patients, delayed diagnosis and treatment can spread tuberculosis (TB) in the community, posing a significant challenge to TB elimination efforts. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the median delays of TB care and the proportion of the Indian population experiencing these delays.Methods:Data extraction and screening were conducted from August 1, 2024, to September 30, 2024, using PubMed, Scopus, and DOAJ search engines. The pooled proportion of the Indian population experiencing the total delay in TB care was estimated, at a 95% confidence interval (CI), using a random-effects model that assumed potential heterogeneity.Results:Of the 14 studies, 92.85% (n = 13) focused on drug-sensitive TB cases, and 7.15% (n = 1) focused on drug-resistant cases. Similarly, 64.28% (n = 9) of studies were cross-sectional, followed by 21.42% (n = 3) of retrospective studies. The estimated median patient delay is 20.5 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 15.3–25.0), the median diagnosis delay is 32.5 days (IQR: 30.0–37.0), the median treatment delay is 7.50 days (IQR: 2.75–12.8), and the median total delay is 45.5 days (IQR: 37.1–58.5). The pooled proportion of the Indian population experiencing total delay is estimated at 39% (95% CI: 17%–64%). With the random-effects model, high heterogeneity (I2 = 99%) was observed among the studies. The regression test for funnel plot asymmetry did not show statistical significance (z = 0.204, P = 0.838).Conclusion:The findings of this study can help program managers design strategies that can help in reducing delays in TB care in India.

INTRODUCTION

According to the World Health Organization’s estimates, tuberculosis (TB) incidence is 134 (95% UI: 125–145) cases per 100,000 population worldwide,[1] and 195 (95% UI: 164–228) cases per 100,000 people in India in 2023.[12] The Global TB Report 2024 states that India accounts for 26% of the global burden and 27% of multidrug-resistant and rifampin-resistant cases.[1] Approximately 29% of TB-related deaths among human immunodeficiency virus-negative individuals globally were attributed to India.[1] As part of the Union Budget 2017–2018, the Indian government announced its plan to end the TB pandemic by 2025, aiming to meet the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target.[3] The National TB Control Programme (NTCP), India’s longest-running initiative to combat TB, was launched in 1962 and was implemented in two phases by the Revised NTCP (RNTCP) between 1997 and 2019. The RNTCP was replaced by the National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP) on January 1, 2020.[4] Currently, NTEP supervises the technical and administrative aspects of the country’s national anti-TB efforts and functions as an essential part of the National Health Mission. The initial steps in TB care involve recognizing symptoms and seeking treatment at public or private healthcare facilities.[5] Any delays during these steps can hinder progress. It is therefore vital to identify presumptive TB cases at first contact and link them promptly to the most effective diagnostic procedures.[6] Improving case detection and ensuring early, accurate diagnosis of TB are also critical. This requires a contextual analysis of the health system’s supply and demand sides. Delayed diagnosis and treatment not only harm patients but also facilitate the spread of TB within the community.[7] This poses a significant challenge to TB elimination efforts. Consequently, it is essential to identify the causes of delays in TB diagnosis and treatment to develop strategies that will substantially reduce disease incidence. In this context, a systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to estimate median delays and the pooled proportion of the population affected by total delay.

Delays in tuberculosis care

The delays in TB care are understood in the light of the TB care cascade, in which the first step is the onset of symptoms and access to health facilities for treatment.[5] Similarly, delay can result in each of these steps; consultation delay is the delay from onset of symptoms to first consultation, diagnostic delay is the delay from initial consultation to diagnosis of the case, and the treatment delay is the delay from diagnosis to onset of treatment. Similarly, the total delay could be referred to as the delay from the onset of symptoms to the initiation of treatment [Figure 1].[6]

Objective

The main objectives of this systematic review and meta-analysis were to estimate the median delays of different types of delays faced by the Indian population and to estimate the pooled proportion of the Indian population facing delays in TB care in India.

Information sources and search strategy

Three databases, namely PUBMED, Scopus, and DOAJ, were utilized to identify the studies. The articles were extracted and screened during August and September 2024. Keywords including “total delays,” “health system delay,” “patient delay,” “treatment delay,” “diagnosis delay,” “tuberculosis care,” and “India” were employed to search for publications in these databases. The Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were employed to search the articles with the aforementioned keyword combinations. The search strings used are presented in Table 1.

Study selection

The publications were initially reviewed by their titles and abstracts to eliminate duplicates. Zotero 7.0.7 (Corporation for Digital Scholarship) was utilized for deduplication.[9] The remaining articles were individually evaluated by the first two authors, encompassing an examination of titles, abstracts, and methodologies, followed by a thorough analysis of the entire texts. The final inclusion of the articles was determined by a collective agreement among all authors.

Eligibility criteria

The studies were included based on the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design framework [Table 2]. In addition, English language articles reporting delays in TB care, primarily conducted in an Indian setting and published between 2004 and 2024, were incorporated into the review. The pediatric population was excluded because they follow different diagnostic and treatment protocols, and their health-seeking behavior is mediated by adults, mainly parents and caregivers. Furthermore, the factors linked to delays in TB care among adults are not directly applicable to children. Furthermore, including gray literature could have improved the study’s credibility; however, this was not feasible as gray literature is generally not indexed in the databases used for this systematic review. Moreover, the search strategy is heterogeneous and time-consuming, so only published literature was utilized for the review.

Statistical analysis

The pooled proportion of the Indian population experiencing delays was estimated at a 95% confidence interval (CI) utilizing a random-effects model. The model was deemed appropriate as it accounts for the inherent variability of the studies, allows the generalization of results to a broader population, and produces equivalent outcomes to the fixed-effect model when heterogeneity is minimal.[12] The forest plot, funnel diagram, and Galbraith plot were employed to visually represent the heterogeneity and bias present in the included studies. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and meta-regression were conducted as well. The threshold for statistical significance was established at P < 0.05. The analysis employed Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Study selection

Out of 1055 articles identified from three distinct databases, 205 were eliminated as duplicates, and 850 were screened, resulting in the exclusion of 824 articles. Twenty-six articles were sought for retrieval; three articles were inaccessible, and nine studies were excluded for failing to meet the eligibility criteria, resulting in the inclusion of 14 articles for the review and five articles for the meta-analysis that reported the proportion of Indian patients experiencing total delays in TB care. Study characteristics

Of the 14 studies, 92.85% (n = 13) of the studies focused on drug-sensitive TB cases, and 7.15% (n = 1) focused on drug-resistant cases [Table 4]. Similarly, 64.28% (n = 9) of the studies were cross-sectional studies, followed by 21.42% (n = 3) retrospective studies [Figure 3]. The highest number (21.42%, n = 3) of studies was found in 2017 [Figure 4]. Similarly, one pan-India study was found, which included samples from eight states, and two studies (14.28%) each were found to be carried out in Maharashtra, Himachal Pradesh, and Kerala [Figure 5].

Result synthesis

Characteristics of the studies

Of the 14 studies, eight reported the proportion of Indian patients experiencing any form of delay in accessing TB services, with five of these studies specifically reporting the proportion of the Indian population facing complete delay. Similarly, 11 studies documented different types of median delay. The total sample across all 14 studies was 5439, with the largest sample size of 2027 in a study from Andhra Pradesh and West Bengal. Eight studies reported the proportion of individuals experiencing one or other form of delay, while only five studies detailed the proportion of the Indian population facing total delay.

Median delays

The highest median total delay was reported by a study conducted in Shimla, Himachal Pradesh, at 85 days, while the lowest was 15 days, reported in studies from West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh. The highest median patient delay was also in Shimla, at 45 days, with the lowest at 7 days in a study from Ballabgarh, Haryana. The highest median diagnosis delay was observed in Nahan, Himachal Pradesh, at 45 days, whereas the lowest was 7 days, in a study from Ernakulam, Kerala. Similarly, the highest median treatment delay was reported in Mumbai, Maharashtra, at 31 days, with the shortest being 1 day in Ernakulam, Kerala [Figure 6]. The estimated median patient delay was 20.5 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 15.3–25.0), the median diagnosis delay was 32.5 days (IQR: 30.0–37.0), the median treatment delay was 7.50 days (IQR: 2.75–12.8), and the median total delay was 45.5 days (IQR: 37.1–58.5).

Pooled proportion of the Indian population experiencing delays

The pooled proportion (from five studies) of total delays in the Indian population was 39% (95% CI: 17%–64%). The studies showed varying effects, with Van Ness et al. having the largest effect size (0.74) and Balasubramanium A. et al. having the smallest effect size (0.08). The total effect size from the random-effects model (0.39) was significant despite this variation, lying between the lower and higher estimates of individual studies [Figure 7].

Forest plot for the pooled proportion of the Indian population facing total delays in tuberculosis care. CI: Confidence interval

Publication bias

Visually, the plot looked reasonably symmetrical [Figure 8], and studies were distributed on both sides of the mean effect size. The regression-based Egger’s test was found not to be statistically significant at P = 0.6670 (z = −0.43). To further assess the publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was employed. This was a nonparametric analysis of publication bias with linear estimation imputation on the right. This helped in evaluating the potential asymmetry in the funnel plot. The method found that no additional studies were imputed ([observed: theta = 1.356, 95% CI: 0.863–1.849], [observed + imputed: theta = 1.356, 95% CI: 0.863–1.849]), which indicates no strong evidence of missing studies due to publication bias. In this analysis, “theta” represents the overall Freeman–Turkey’s p.

Funnel plot for representing publication bias. CI: Confidence interval

Heterogeneity assessment

High heterogeneity (I2 = 99.00%) was observed among the studies [Figure 7]. Given the range of effect sizes, there appeared to be between-study heterogeneity, which the random-effects model accounts for. Overall, the findings were consistent with a statistically significant effect across these studies. Figure 9 depicts a Galbraith plot, which shows that almost four studies are closer to the regression line and fall within a 95% CI, with one study above the regression line, suggesting some degree of heterogeneity.

Galbraith plot showing the heterogeneity of the studies. CI: Confidence interval

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis [Figure 10] indicated that the effect size of the included studies is reported above 1.0, and all the studies showed a statistically significant effect size (P < 0.0001). The effect size by Balasubramanium et al. demonstrated the highest effect size of 1.55 (95% CI: 1.10–1.99), and the study by Vann Ness et al. demonstrated a moderate effect size of 1.18 (95% CI: 0.71–1.64); however, both support the overall trend, suggesting analytical robustness.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. CI: Confidence interval

Meta-regression analysis

A meta-regression using the prevalence and total sample size as variables indicated no residual heterogeneity with τ2 = 0, I2 = 0.00%, R2 = 100%, with a statistically significant Wald χ2 = 369.97 (P < 0.0001). The prevalence showed a statistically significant positive association (β =0.02176, z = 19.85, P < 0.001), whereas the total sample size was not statistically significant (β = 0.00006, z = 0.95, P = 0.343).

DISCUSSION

This study was instrumental in figuring out two important facets of delays in TB care among the Indian population. It estimated the median delays (in days) for the three types of delays and the total delays and estimated the pooled proportion of the Indian population facing delays while accessing TB services across several settings in India. Given the Government of India’s ambitious goal of eliminating TB by 2025, it becomes important to understand the nuanced facets of delays in TB care. Understanding these two facets would help the program managers devise targeted interventions to eliminate delays in diagnosis and treatment. This is further important as late diagnosis and treatment can perpetuate TB transmission in the community and are associated with a worse prognosis.[1327]

The review estimated the median delays (in days) for various types: total, patient, diagnosis, and treatment delays. This review estimated the median patient delay as 20.5 days (IQR: 15.3–25.0), the median diagnosis delay as 32.5 days (IQR: 30.0–37.0), the median treatment delay as 7.50 days (IQR: 2.75–12.8), and the median total delay as 45.5 days (IQR: 37.1–58.5). Other studies in India have also found a similar or higher range of patient delays across different studies, 20 days in Tamil Nadu[28] and 95 days in Maharashtra.[29] A systematic review published a decade before in 2014 estimated the median patient delay as 18.4 days (IQR: 14.3–27.0) among the Indian population, which is lower than the range of delays estimated in this review.[30] Compared to India, the patient delay in low TB-burden countries like France, the patient delay was estimated at 14 days (IQR: 0–53).[31] However, the same is found to be higher in some developed countries such as the USA and UK, which range between 22 and 54 days, similar to delays found in India.[3233] The median diagnosis delay estimated earlier by the systematic review was 31 days (IQR: 24.5–35.4), which is slightly lower than the current estimation of 32.5 days (IQR: 30.0–37.0).[30] Similarly, one of the other studies conducted in India has estimated a slightly higher median diagnosis delay of 36.7 days.[34] The estimated median treatment delay in this review was 7.5 days, which is quite higher than studies conducted elsewhere in India, such as 1.8 days in Wardha[29] and 2.5 days (IQR: 1.9–3.6) by the earlier systematic review.[30] The estimated total delay is 45.5 days, and other studies that have been carried out in India also found total delays, such as 37 days in Aligarh, Uttar Pradesh, in which the study estimated the range from 8 to 240 days.[35]

Several factors contributed to the delays in TB care in India. It is important to understand the consequences of all the contributing factors to delays in TB care to improve the TB care cascade.[6] Several studies carried out in different parts of India have identified factors that caused delays in TB care in India. The studies identified poor socioeconomic conditions,[1635363738] poor awareness about TB,[3637] myths and cultural beliefs about TB,[63738] and lack of family support [38394041]stigma,[42434445], and lack of family support.[384042] These factors, which are also referred to as the demand side factors of the health system, are associated with the patients’ economic, occupational, domestic, social, health-seeking, and TB-awareness status.[6] The other factors are called the supply-side factors of the health system, which are found in other studies in India, such as the attitude of healthcare staff[1636] and delays in referral for diagnosis.[1643] In addition, a recent systematic review has identified similar factors related to the TB care cascade among the Indian population.[46] These findings, based on the categories, can precisely help the program managers and researchers devise targeted strategies to aid in the elimination of TB among the Indian population.[47]

Given an understanding of these factors, the program managers should act by considering both the supply-side and demand-side factors contributing to delays in TB care in India. In addition, TB is not purely a clinical entity and is a social malady; hence, TB elimination efforts should address the complex mix of both biological and social factors.[48] The program managers in India further need to start taking the major determinants that are linked to TB and its mortality seriously, especially considering the National Strategic Plan (NSP) for TB elimination 2017–2025.[3] Intersectoral convergence among many sectors, including the community, will be necessary to address the issues of stigma and financial constraints.[35] It is crucial to take into account the inclusion of TB in social protection schemes that prioritize prevention and protect patients from impoverishment.[49] The primary causes of the delays in the health system’s supply side, such as repeated consultations or being bounced between the public and/or private sectors, must be addressed by the NTEP. The most crucial thing to remember in this context is the intricacy of the disease, its determinants, and accountability, which always raises the question of whether the TB elimination program can achieve this on its own or if a logical convergence with other public health programs could help.[50]

The review further estimated the pooled proportion of the Indian population facing delays in TB care based on five studies that reported the prevalence of delays among the Indian population across different study settings, which have not been estimated by other systematic reviews conducted on the said topic in India.[30646]

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study followed a systematic approach, adhering to the PRISMA guidelines for reporting. It used three databases that comprehensively captured evidence from 14 studies providing information on the patient, the health system, and the total delay. The study employed a robust statistical method involving the random-effects model and estimated the pooled proportion of Indian TB patients experiencing delays while accessing TB care. However, the study has some limitations. There was poor representation of studies reporting delays associated with drug-resistant TB. In addition, how the different types of delay have been defined across the included studies also affects the pooled estimate. High heterogeneity among the included studies limits the reliability of the pooled results. Heterogeneity is a fundamental and unavoidable feature of meta-analyses, indicating variances in study results that are beyond random chance. These variations arise from differences in study population, interventions, methodologies, and measurement tools, which can influence key meta-analytical outcomes, including pooled effect estimates, CIs, and overall findings.[51]

Implications of the study

There are several program-related implications to reduce delays in TB care by strengthening active case finding, improving laboratory turnaround times, and expanding access to molecular testing. Health education and social support measures can be developed to reduce patient-level delays in TB care. The findings also highlight that the median delay can serve as a metric to monitor all types of delays and to implement corrective actions at each level of the health system.

CONCLUSION

This review estimated median patient delay and the proportion of Indian patients facing delays in TB care, which the program managers should consider, given the Government of India’s goal to eliminate TB by 2025, 5 years ahead of the SDG targets. As the efforts are geared toward elimination, the program cannot afford such long median delays as the review has estimated. Furthermore, the pooled proportion also seems high compared to other estimations by Indian studies, which needs to be considered that delays can have disastrous outcomes at the individual level and can accentuate the transmission of the disease in the community. Although systematic reviews have been carried out on this subject, more extensive reviews and observational studies considering all these factors can further be studied to unravel the nuanced facets of delays in TB care in India.
