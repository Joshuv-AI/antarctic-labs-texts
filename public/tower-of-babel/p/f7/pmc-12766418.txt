Increasing physical activity and reducing sedentary behaviour in patients diagnosed with non-affective psychosis: a cross-sectional UK study identifying cognitions to target in treatment
Authors: Rowan Diamond, Bao Sheng Loe, Felicity Waite, Felicity Hudson, Sinéad Lambe, Daniel Freeman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundBetter interventions are needed to help individuals diagnosed with psychosis to be more physically active and less sedentary. Cognitions are likely to be crucial in blocking and initiating movement. Our aim was to identify cognitions likely to affect physical inactivity or sedentary behaviour in patients with psychosis in order to inform future research and intervention development.MethodsItem pools of 149 cognitions related to physical activity (46 promoting and 103 blocking) and 156 cognitions related to sedentary behaviour (105 promoting and 51 blocking) were developed with lived experience advisors and administered separately to patients with diagnoses of non-affective psychosis, aged 16 years or older, accessing secondary care mental health services in 43 National Health Service (NHS) providers in England and Wales. The study period was between 6th March 2023 and 19th February 2024. Measures were developed using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis. Internal reliability and test-retest reliability were assessed. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short form was used to validate the physical activity cognitions measures and the Sedentary Behaviour Questionnaire was used to validate the sedentary behaviour cognitions measure.FindingsNine hundred and seventeen participants with non-affective psychosis were enrolled. 563 patients completed the physical activity item pools and 523 patients completed the sedentary behaviour item pools, with 169 patients completing both questionnaires. 620 (67.6%) patients were men and 286 (31.2%) patients were women. 592 (64.6%) patients were White, 83 (9.1%) were mixed or multiple ethnic origin, 85 (9.3%) were Asian, 133 (14.5%) were Black, 4 (0.4%) were Arab, and 20 (2.2%) were other ethnic groups or preferred not to say. The mean age of the cohort was 42.9 (12.9 [18–81]) years.The Oxford Physical Activity Cognition (OPAC) (Promoting and Blocking) scales comprised eight factors: positive feelings with exercise, anticipation of reward, responsibility to others, negative social appraisal, physical health, hearing voices, preoccupation, and negative feelings towards exercise. The Oxford Sedentary Cognitions Assessments (OSCA) (Promoting and Blocking) comprised 11 factors: purpose, poor physical health, defeatist beliefs, unaware of time passing, low mood, television, sitting to steady mind, purpose, anticipation of reward, responsibility to others, and motivation from others.All four measures had excellent model fits. Internal reliability and test-retest reliability were good. The measures had small associations with self-reported physical activity and sedentary behaviour.InterpretationThe cognitive drivers of movement for patients with psychosis appear numerous. There are many different types of content of cognitions that could hinder or facilitate movement in patients diagnosed with psychosis, with a number overlapping across physical activity and sedentary behaviour and a number specific to each. These cognitions could be future plausible targets for interventions to increase movement. Required now is causal evidence of whether these cognitions are amenable to change and, if they are, whether this leads to behavioural change. Further research is also needed to replicate the findings.FundingNational Institute for Health and Care Research.Keywords: Exercise, Schizophrenia, Mental health, Beliefs, Behaviour, Sitting

Research in context

Evidence before this study

How to increase physical activity and lessen sedentary behaviour, especially in patients diagnosed with psychosis, is a clinical challenge. A better understanding of what prevents and promotes both types of movement could be a route to better intervention. The cognitions that prevent and promote physical activity and sedentary behaviour in patients diagnosed with psychosis are likely to be important determinants and need to be identified in order to facilitate the development of better treatment.

We searched Pubmed on 22nd April, 2024, with no date, language or study-type restrictions, in clinical populations, using the terms (“Physical activity” OR “Exercise” OR “Sedent∗” OR “Sitting” OR “Accelero∗”) AND (“Cogniti∗” OR “Beliefs” OR “Attitudes”) AND (“Scale” OR “Questionnaire”) AND (“Psychosis” OR “Schizophrenia” OR “Psychotic”). 73 papers were identified.

For sedentary behaviour there were no measures of cognitions specifically for people with psychosis, and only one potentially relevant measure that was developed for the general population that concerned motivation to rest.

There were several measures of beliefs about physical activity for the general population but no measures developed and tested specifically for patients with psychosis that assessed cognitions related to physical activity or sedentary behaviour.

Added value of this study

We conducted the first empirical study of cognitions relating to physical activity and sedentary behaviour in patients diagnosed with psychosis.

Illustrating the psychological complexity of promoting greater movement we found three factors likely to promote physical activity, five factors likely to limit physical activity, four factors likely to promote standing up, and seven factors likely to encourage sedentary behaviour. There were common factors that could influence both physical activity and sedentary behaviour, which were anticipation of reward, responsibilities to others, and physical health. But there were also many factors that differed between sedentary behaviour and physical activity. For example, negative social appraisals may discourage exercise, whilst defeatist beliefs may encourage sitting down. Furthermore, factors were identified that had not been included in existing measures of activity-related cognitions developed in the general population such as the impact of voices, defeatist beliefs, unawareness of time passing, and remaining sedentary as a way of managing overwhelming thoughts and experiences.

Implications of all the available evidence

It is now possible to assess a range of cognitions in patients with psychosis that may contribute to facilitating or preventing physical activity and sedentary behaviour. Many of these cognitions may be relevant across the general population but a number are specific to the difficulties of patients with psychosis.

There appear to be specific cognitions that underpin sedentary behaviour and specific cognitions that underpin physical activity in patients with psychosis. These could help to provide a better understanding of inactivity and may be a target for intervention in the future. The cognitive drivers of movement appear numerous. Longitudinal testing of the new measures against objective measurements of physical activity and sedentary time may be valuable. The new assessments could be useful in future research to further understanding but could also potentially contribute to developing better interventions.

Introduction

Increasing physical activity in patients with psychosis is a well-recognised treatment priority.1 Patients typically spend too little time engaged in physical activity, with negative consequences for physical and mental health.2 Time spent in sedentary behaviour is also independently associated with poor health outcomes and is a distinct treatment target.3 Increasing physical activity and decreasing time in sedentary behaviour can be considered together as increasing movement. A potentially important—but underexplored—factor that may affect movement is what the person is thinking: cognitions. We conducted interviews with 51 patients, carers, and staff who noted how cognitions affect both physical activity and sedentary behaviour (e.g. “I don't deserve to be doing these things”, “Exercise makes me feel humiliated”).4 In the current report we set out to identify and measure quantitatively the cognitions that may affect physical activity and sedentary behaviour in patients diagnosed with psychosis in order to lay the foundation for future efficacious treatment. Increasing movement is a challenge for many in the population but patients with psychosis face additional barriers, such as psychotic experiences (e.g. paranoia limiting a person leaving the home, hallucinations telling the person not to do activities), agoraphobia, worry, low self-confidence,5 depression,6 medication side effects,7 and physical health comorbidity.8 These factors may be partly responsible for the poor uptake, poor adherence, and high drop out from physical activity interventions for patients with psychosis.9,10 It is plausible that cognitions mediate the relationship of these factors to reduced movement (e.g. ‘I am not able to exercise because of my mental health’ or ‘My anxiety will get too much for me if I go out’).

Cognitions often drive behaviour, and are therefore a plausible intervention target for increasing physical activity and decreasing sedentary behaviour. The importance of beliefs and attitudes are identified in several theoretical models of behaviour change (e.g. the theory of planned behaviour and social cognitive theory).11,12 There are scales that measure general population beliefs specific to exercise, for example the Behavioural Regulations in Exercise Questionnaire (BREQ).13 However, these scales were not developed with patients with psychosis. Nor have any scales been developed to understand cognitions related to sedentary behaviour.

Our aim was to identify the cognitions that may limit and promote physical activity and sedentary behaviour in patients with psychosis. Our primary objective was to develop assessments with high face validity and excellent reliability that could be used for future research and intervention development. We also sought to assess validity of the scales in the first instance against self-report assessments of movement, though noting that correlations between self-report and actigraphy assessments of movement are typically weak.14,15

The hypothesis here was that positive thoughts were associated with increased movement and negative thoughts with decreased movement.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted to develop assessments of cognitions relevant to movement in patients with non-affective psychosis. Two samples were recruited (1) to develop an assessment of physical activity cognitions and (2) to develop an assessment of cognitions related to sedentary behaviour. Additional measures were administered in order to assess the potential validity of the assessments. The cognition items were re-administered to a subgroup of participants (69 for the physical activity questionnaires and 61 for the sedentary questionnaires) 1 week later to assess test-retest reliability. Participants were supported to complete questionnaires either in person, via telephone, or an online call by members of the NIHR Research Delivery Network in each NHS trust. This breadth of delivery methods was offered to facilitate participation to as broad a group as possible, serving to minimise selection bias. The study was designed and conducted in collaboration with a Lived Experience Advisory Panel including five members.

Participants

Participants were recruited from 43 NHS trusts in England and Wales between 6th March 2023 and 19th February 2024. Recruitment was by researchers in the NIHR Research Delivery Network (RDN) working with patients’ clinical teams. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same for both samples. The inclusion criteria were: aged 16 years or older, accessing adult NHS secondary care mental health services, and had a non-affective psychosis diagnosis. The exclusion criterion was insufficient understanding of English to complete the assessments. The sample size was selected based upon what would allow a large variety of cognition items, and on what would enable factor analysis and detect small associations.

Procedures

Four item pools were developed: two item pools measured cognitions relating to physical activity and exercise (one promoting and one blocking). Two item pools measured cognitions about sedentary behaviour (one promoting and one blocking). These item pools were generated using inductive and deductive methods. The items included were informed by accounts given by patients, carers, and staff in a preparatory earlier qualitative study by the current authors,4 clinical experience, and discussion with the Lived Experience Advisory Panel. Other cognitions were generated following a review of the literature, though no items were derived or adapted from pre-existing questionnaires.

Initial item pools included a total of 305 cognitions (46 cognitions encouraging exercise, 103 cognitions discouraging exercise, 105 cognitions encouraging sedentary behaviour, 51 cognitions discouraging sedentary behaviour). Items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = do not agree, 4 = totally agree). The full list of cognitions is provided in Supplementary materials.

The following scales were also administered:

Physical activity: International Physical Activity Questionnaire – short form (IPAQ-SF) 16

This is a widely used self-report measure of physical activity, which has been validated in people with psychosis.17 It assesses the number of days and hours/minutes per day participants have engaged in walking, moderate activity and vigorous activity over the past 7 days. An algorithm is used to calculate the total metabolic equivalent minutes over the week. The total was reported in metabolic equivalent tasks (METs) minutes per week. Higher scores indicate greater physical activity. The IPAQ-SF is recommended for population-based study due to feasibility and preferences over the long-form version.16 However, the IPAQ tends to over-estimate actual physical activity18,19 and correlations between total activity level and objective measurement can be poor.20,21

Sedentary behaviour: Sedentary Behaviour Questionnaire (SBQ) 22

This is a nine-item questionnaire, assessing the amount of time spent doing each of nine activities (e.g. watching TV, playing video games). Participants select from nine options ranging from ‘none’ to ‘6 h or more’. Sedentary time for an average day was calculated in minutes. For the purposes of this study, weekend days were not distinguished from weekdays due to the fact that the majority of patients do not work.23 The SBQ has acceptable measurement properties in a population of overweight adults and has been used in populations with severe mental health difficulties,24 though it has been found to be a poor predictor of objectively measured sedentary behaviour.25,26 Higher scores indicate more sedentary time.

Mobility and pain: Mobility and pain items of EuroQol (EQ-5D) 27

Two single items from the EuroQol-5D were used to assess mobility and pain. Mobility is rated on a 5-point scale from 1 = ‘I have no problems in walking about’ to 5 = ‘I am unable to walk about’. Higher scores indicate poorer mobility. Pain is rated on a 5-point scale from 1 = ‘I have no pain or discomfort’ to 5 = ‘I have extreme pain or discomfort’. Higher scores indicate greater pain.

Paranoia: Revised Green et al. Paranoid Thoughts Scale (R-GPTS) 28

The 10-item Part B scale was used for assessing ideas of persecution over the past fortnight. Items are rated on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all to 4 = totally) with higher scores indicating greater levels of paranoid thinking.

Negative symptoms: Self-evaluation of Negative Symptoms (SNS) 29

The 20-item scale assesses negative symptoms over the past week. Items are rated on a 3-point scale (0 = strongly disagree to 2 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater levels of negative symptoms.

Hallucinations: Psychosis Screening Questionnaire (PSQ) 30

The presence of hallucinations in the past 12 months was assessed using two items from the PSQ: ‘Have there been times when you saw/heard things that other people couldn't?’ (Yes/No); ‘Did you at any time hear voices saying quite a few words or sentences when there was no one around that might account for it?’ (Yes/No).

Mood: Patient Health Questionnaire – 2 (PHQ-2) 31

This 2-item measure assesses mood over the past 2 weeks on a 4-point scale (0 = not at all, 3 = nearly every day). Higher scores indicate lower mood.

Medication side-effects: Systematic Monitoring of Adverse events Related to TreatmentS (SMARTS) checklist.32

This is a 12-item checklist of common antipsychotic medication side-effects (such as sedation). A total number of side-effects are calculated.

Preferences for movement

A list of 27 activities such as walking, active housework, and cycling was used to assess the activities people had done at least twice in the past month, and the activities they would like to be doing in the next year. The number of activities was summed.

Outcomes

The key outcome was that the new scales—Oxford Physical Activity Cognitions (Promoting and Blocking) and Oxford Sedentary Cognitions Assessment (Promoting and Blocking) had good model fit, internal reliability, and test-retest reliability. We also tested initial construct validity using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form (IPAQ-SF), the Sedentary Behaviour Questionnaire (SBQ), and the other measures.

Ethics

All participants gave informed consent. The study was approved by the NHS West of Scotland Research Ethics Service—REC 4 (ref 23/WS/0010).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.2)33 with tidyverse packages (version 2.0.0),34 lavaan (version 0.6-19),35 semTools (version 0.5-6), and psych (version 2.4.6.26).36

Four separate analyses (one for each item pool) were conducted.

Prior to factor analysis, Bartlett's test of sphericity and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy were used to assess the suitability of factor analysis for the dataset.

The level of missingness in the data was less than 2%, and was random, allowing the use of full information maximum likelihood (FIML) rather than imputation for missing data.

The data for each item pool were randomly split into derivation and validation samples, and demographic and clinical significance was established using T-tests and Fisher's Exact test. Parallel analysis was used to identify the number of factors in the model, with the requirement for there to be at least three items per factor. Parallel analysis operates by comparing eigenvalues from the real data with eigenvalues from randomly generated datasets. Where the real eigenvalue is larger than the random one, this identifies a candidate factor. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted for each of the four measures using the derivation sample of 200. Items that correlated more than 0.90 were excluded prior to EFA. The item pools were refined during EFA by discarding items with cross-loadings across multiple factors or items that loaded poorly. Strong factor loadings were defined as ≥0.60, moderate loading was defined as between 0.40 and 0.59, and poor loading was defined as <0.30.

The remaining sub-samples (n = 363 for physical activity and n = 321 for sedentary behaviour) enabled validation of the factor structure of each measure using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Acceptable model fit was determined by a Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker Lewis Index (TLI) above 0.9, a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) below 0.08, and a Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) below 0.08. To evaluate the confirmatory factor structure of OSCA—Promoting Sedentary Behaviour we compared the correlated factors CFA model with the higher-order CFA model, where the factors are loaded onto an overall general factor, aligning with the theoretical conceptualisation of the OSCA—Promoting Sedentary Behaviour questionnaire. Given that the higher-order factor is nested within the correlated factor model by imposing additional constraints on the relationships among factors, we conducted a chi-square difference test to determine whether these constraints significantly worsen the model fit.

The psychometric properties of the scales were assessed using the omega reliability coefficient to determine internal consistency. One-week test-retest reliability was examined using a two-way, mixed effects, absolute agreement interclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Validity was examined using simple correlations.

Using data from the whole sample we assessed the extent to which the factors were associated with self-reported physical activity and sedentary behaviour respectively, using structural equation modelling (SEM). IPAQ and SBQ measures were log transformed to adjust for skewness.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analyses, data interpretation, or writing the report.

Oxford Physical Activity Cognitions (OPAC) scale—promoting physical activity

Bartlett's test of sphericity and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy indicated that factor analysis was appropriate (χ2 [990 df] 6038.74, p < 0.001, KMO 0.91).

Parallel analysis revealed that a three-factor solution was the most appropriate model. Items were excluded where factor loadings on more than one factor were greater than 0.3 and where there were only poor factor loadings. After confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) the three factors contained 10, 10, and 4 items. In order to produce a parsimonious model, and a resulting measure which was not too burdensome, the items in each factor were further reduced by retaining only the items which were most clinically helpful and had the best factor loadings. This resulted in a model with three factors and a total of 10 items. The resulting model had a good fit (robust χ2 = 66.39 [32 df], p < 0.001, comparative fit index CFI = 0.96, Tucker Lewis Index TLI = 0.95, root mean square error of approximation RMSEA = 0.06, standardised root mean square residual SRMR = 0.05). On examination of a higher order model a potential Heywood case37 between positive feelings and the higher order factor was identified, as the communality of greater than 1 is statistically impossible. This indicates that a higher order model would not be suitable (Positive feelings with exercise r = 1.01, Anticipation of reward r = 0.81, Responsibility to others r = 0.30). Fig. 1 shows the final model.

OPAC—promoting physical activity.

The OPAC scale—Promoting physical activity had good psychometric properties with strong internal consistency, with an overall scale reliability of ω = 0.86. The coefficients for the three factors were: Positive feelings with exercise ω = 0.81, Anticipation of reward ω = 0.88, Responsibility to others ω = 0.74. Participants provided retest data within 2–16 days (mean 7.14 days [SD 2.06]) and the intraclass correlation between scale scores was high (ICC = 0.94, n = 69).

Structural equation modelling was performed, regressing total IPAQ score on each of the OPAC (Promoting physical activity) factors, whilst also specifying the final structural equation model. Discussion

We believe this study can advance the understanding of physical movement in patients with psychosis. A varied and extensive range of cognitions were found that may affect whether a person remains sitting or gets up, and whether exercise is initiated or not. There are cognitions relevant to both physical activity and sedentary behaviour and a number specific to each type of movement. It is notable that many more cognitions were identified that may block movement than may promote it—which is a plausible reason why many patients are inactive. It is also clear that each patient endorsed numerous types of cognitions. For the first time we now have a method of assessing these thinking patterns. This provides optimism for new treatment development since these cognitions could be viewed as possible mechanistic intervention targets. A key step in future research is clear: show that the cognitions can be changed and test whether this leads to increases in movement.

The cognitions identified have very strong face validity—the item content very understandably links with whether a person may or may not engage with movement. The associations with self-reported physical activity and sedentary behaviour were largely in the expected direction but very modest in size. This is likely due to the inaccuracies in self-report of movement, and, further, that even after transformation there was high skewness in the data from the movement scales. The cognition scales would benefit from validation against objective measures, though there are challenges in interpretation in correlational studies. The key learning about the potential causal role of the cognitions, and their size of effect, will only be made from intervention tests in which they are altered. Use of objective measures would be practicable in such intervention work. There is also intriguing work to be conducted as to whether it is sufficient for change to only focus on lessening the cognitions that block movement or whether promoters of movement also need to be built up.

The importance of thinking separately about physical exercise and sedentary behaviour is reinforced by the findings of the study. There is overlap in potential cognitions underpinning them (anticipation of reward, responsibility to others, and poorer physical health) but also types of content unique to each. For example, physical activity, rather than sedentary behaviour, seemed to be especially inhibited by psychotic experiences. Defeatist beliefs and low mood appeared more related to sedentary behaviours than physical activity. In previous literature, defeatist beliefs have been implicated in functional outcome,40 and goal-directed behaviour,41 but they are less well-described in relation to specific measurements of sedentary behaviour and physical activity outcomes. Other symptoms emerging as potentially relevant in our factors, such as low mood and psychotic experiences, have been previously linked with physical activity2 and sedentary behaviour.21 We also note that previous research has highlighted the importance of social support to encourage physical activity.42 This was particularly seen in the current study for sedentary behaviour. Our findings indicate that there may be another influential interpersonal dynamic: responsibility to other people. This was seen for both types of movement.

In order to develop effective interventions for patients with psychosis, Freeman43 advocates precision in the articulation of outcome targets and mechanistic routes to change, extensive input from people with lived experience, building in measurement of outcomes during interventions, and personalisation of interventions. This approach was used to develop the Feeling Safe programme, leading to large treatment effects,23 and could be used to develop interventions for physical activity and sedentary behaviour, in which change to the identified cognitions could be mechanistically valuable. In such an intervention it is likely that any negative cognitions identified would be balanced by meaningful counterweight cognitions,44 and key cognitive behavioural approaches such as behavioural tests would be incorporated to build new cognitive learning. Future research into short-term behavioural testing around the identified cognitions could be extremely valuable.

There are a number of study limitations. Due to the cross-sectional design there can be no inferences about causality and future research is needed to test the causal hypothesis that targeting cognitions will change physical inactivity and sedentary behaviour. Although the socio-demographic and clinical information of the participants is similar to other studies of psychosis, we do not know that the group is representative of those attending clinical services. It is plausible that both the most inactive and most active patients would have been less likely to complete the study. The most active patients may also have been more likely to have been discharged to primary care services, limiting the assessment of cognitions that promote movement. We had insufficient participant group sizes to test whether the assessments are optimal within specific socio-demographic groups. Future research should be conducted with these issues of representativeness in mind. In our analysis we obtained generally high communalities and well-defined factors, supporting the stability of the solution within the sample size. However, external validation in independent samples would help to assess the generalisability of the findings. We also do not know if important cognitions have been missed from the original item pools. Of course, there are a range of factors other than cognitions, for example medication side-effects, low income, and poor provision of support4,45,46—that will also determine whether a person is active. The different administration methods of the questionnaire may have appealed to different groups of participants. For example, those choosing to participate by completing it in person may have been more physically able and may potentially be experiencing a less acute phase of their illness, which may have affected their responses. For measure development it is important to include participants with a range of physical activity levels and symptom severity, so different administration methods increase the accessibility of the study. Although the differences between missingness and cognition scores by administration methods were small (see Supplementary materials), these may represent a potential bias in the sample. This study is exploratory and represents the first stages of understanding whether interventions that target cognitions could be helpful in the future. Nevertheless, the plausibility of the cognitions identified, the wide coverage in content, and the reliability of the assessments indicate that targeting cognitions could be a promising area for future research into helping patients to become more active.
