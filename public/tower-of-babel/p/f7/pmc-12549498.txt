Aggressive natural killer cell leukemia therapy in the L-asparaginase era: why are we failing?
Authors: Sang Eun Yoon, Junhun Cho, Hyun-Young Kim, Seok Jin Kim, Won Seog Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAggressive natural killer cell leukemia (ANKL) is a rare malignancy with poor prognosis and unclear treatment strategies. Given the poor prognosis and lack of a standardized treatment for ANKL, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of L-asparaginase (L-ASNase)-based chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HCT).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 51 patients with ANKL (2000–2023) to assess the clinical outcomes and efficacy of L-ASNase-based chemotherapy.ResultsAmong the study patients, 18 (36.7%) had poor performance status (ECOG-PS ≥ 3) and 7 (13.7%) died before initiating chemotherapy. Of the 44 treated patients, 30 (58.9%) received L-ASNase-based regimens, showing higher response rates (46.7% vs. 21.4%) and a slightly longer median duration of response (4.5 vs 4.2 months) than non-L-ASNase regimens. Complete administration of L-ASNase in the first cycle improved response rates (p = 0.019), but poor performance led to premature discontinuation (p = 0.011). Among the responders, those who subsequently underwent HCT demonstrated prolonged survival compared to those who did not. Median progression-free survival and overall survival were 0.8 and 1.6 months, respectively.ConclusionHCT was associated with improved survival, particularly among patients who responded to initial therapy. However, given the high frequency of poor performance status at diagnosis, a stepwise approach, including early supportive care, careful chemotherapy administration, and timely HCT, is crucial for optimizing ANKL outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44313-025-00104-3.Keywords: Aggressive natural killer cell leukemia, Etoposide, L-asparaginase, Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

Introduction

The etiology and characteristics of aggressive natural killer (NK) cell leukemia (ANKL) remain poorly understood, primarily because of its rarity and the limited number of reports. [1] Both ANKL and extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) are strongly associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-induced oncogenesis. [2] ANKL is distinguished from ENKTL; the latter primarily invades the upper aerodigestive tract (nasal cavity, nasopharynx, paranasal sinuses, and palate). Unlike ENKTL, ANKL mainly presents with leukemic features and invades the peripheral blood, bone marrow (BM), liver, and spleen. Therefore, the diagnosis is confirmed primarily by BM aspiration or biopsy. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) shows that ANKL and ENKTL share CD3-, CD5-, CD56 +, and Epstein-Barr encoding region (EBER) in situ hybridization markers in most cases, but differ in the expression of CD16 +. [3–5] To diagnose ANKL, an integrated approach involving clinical course, pathological findings, and assessment of previous chronic active EBV infections is essential.

Most cases of ANKL present with an initial high fever, cytopenia, disseminated intravascular coagulopathy, elevated liver function, organomegaly, and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). [6, 7] The disease progresses rapidly, with a reported median overall survival (OS) of 1–2 months, necessitating focused treatment strategies. Anthracycline-based chemotherapy is recommended as the first-line treatment for T-cell lymphoid malignancies. However, ANKL and ENKTL, which are closely associated with EBV, have been reported to express P-glycoproteins in tumor cells, leading to multidrug resistance against chemotherapy. The combination of HLH and these diseases is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome. Therefore, etoposide, which significantly reduces T-lymphocyte activation, is recommended. [8] Consequently, regimens combining L-asparaginase (L-ASNase) and etoposide, such as SMILE and VIDL, are recommended for ENKTL and ANKL. Moreover, patients who respond to chemotherapy and undergo allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HCT) show improved survival. [9–11].

Despite the therapeutic parallels of ENKTL, ANKL remains poorly characterized, with inconsistent treatment responses and no established standards of care. Most patients present with a rapidly progressing disease and poor performance status, contributing to high early mortality. Given these unmet clinical challenges, this study hypothesized that L-ASNase combination chemotherapy followed by hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HCT) may improve outcomes in patients. We aimed to retrospectively evaluate the clinical outcomes, identify factors influencing treatment success, and explore the optimal timing and patient selection criteria for this therapeutic approach.

Methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a retrospective study to evaluate the survival outcomes of patients diagnosed with ANKL between 2000 and 2023. Treatment efficacy was compared between patients who received L-ASNase-containing regimens and those who did not. The primary objective was to assess the impact of L-ASNase on overall survival (OS), and the secondary objective was to evaluate the potential contribution of preemptive etoposide use to overall treatment outcomes. Clinical data were collected at diagnosis, including demographics (age and sex), presenting symptoms, laboratory findings (complete blood count, circulating EBV DNA titer (copies/mL)), BM involvement, organomegaly (splenomegaly, hepatomegaly), disease staging, and prognostic indices (Ann Arbor stage, PINK, and PINK-E) [12].

We assessed the previous treatment history, including prior etoposide administration, systemic chemotherapy with or without L-ASNase, autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (auto-HCT), and allo-HCT. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Samsung Medical Center (IRB 2016–11-040–025), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Korean Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The final update of the survival data was performed in June 2024.

Pathologic review of ANKL

ANKL was diagnosed according to the WHO 5th edition criteria, which require leukemic presentation, systemic NK-cell involvement, and the absence of clonal T-cell receptor (TCR) rearrangement. BM aspirates and biopsies were obtained from the patients with suspected ANKL (Fig. 1A). Because the presence of EBV-positive NK cells is considered pathological, the diagnosis was not solely dependent on the percentage of tumor burden. An experienced pathologist (J.C.) performed BM evaluation to ensure diagnostic consistency. The immunophenotypic analysis demonstrated surface CD3 (sCD3)-negative, CD3ε positive, and CD56-positive NK cells, and in situ hybridization (ISH) for EBER was conducted in all cases. When the tumor volume was insufficient, double staining, including EBV ISH, was performed to confirm the NK lineage of the EBV-positive cells (Fig. 1B–G). BM involvement was assessed qualitatively rather than relying on a strict percentage cut-off, as even a small number of EBV-positive NK cells can be indicative of ANKL.

Treatment and evaluation of clinical outcomes

Pre-phase treatment included the administration of high-dose steroids with or without etoposide (intravenous 150 mg/m2 infusion) before pathological diagnosis. The etoposide dose was reduced at the discretion of the investigator. One group of patients received L-ASNase-containing chemotherapeutic regimens, primarily steroids, methotrexate, ifosfamide, L-asparaginase, and etoposide (SMILE), etoposide, ifosfamide, dexamethasone, and L-asparaginase (VIDL), and methotrexate, ifosfamide, dexamethasone, and L-asparaginase (MIDL). The total number of L-ASNase doses was based on the recommended doses in each regimen (seven times in VIDL and SMILE and four times in MIDL). Cases in which more than 80% of the cytotoxic chemotherapy dose was administered were classified as full dose. In intensive chemotherapy regimens, a threshold of > 80% dose administration is used based on the concept of minimum effective dose [13]. However, this cutoff was set at the investigator's discretion rather than being pharmacologically validated; therefore, the statistical strength of this criterion may be limited. The other group received non-L-ASNase chemotherapeutic regimens, specifically cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (CHOP) and cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and prednisone (CVP).

We reviewed 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) scans to assess treatment outcomes according to the International Working Group (IWG) response criteria. [14–16] However, only 14 patients underwent PET/CT evaluation, and it was unclear whether BM involvement and its improvement could be reliably assessed without follow-up BM biopsy (Figure S1). Given the rapid clinical course and predominant BM involvement of ANKL, the utility of PET/CT is limited.

Therefore, the treatment response was primarily evaluated based on clinical symptoms and laboratory markers, in alignment with previous studies on HLH and ENKTL. [17] Clinical tumor response was defined as asymptomatic improvement accompanied by favorable laboratory changes. A patient was considered a responder if one or more of the following criteria were met: i) a decrease in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) to either normal or half of the baseline value; ii) an improved liver function test (LFT), defined as either a normal value or a bilirubin level below 3 mg/dL when the baseline was above 10 mg/dL; or iii) resolution of cytopenia in the subsequent treatment cycle without worsening of other laboratory parameters. Response assessments were based on clinical judgement, imaging findings when available, and serial laboratory evaluations.

Statistical analysis

Significant differences in categorical variables between groups were assessed using the chi-square test, with p < 0.05 considered significant. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to evaluate duration of response (DOR), progression-free survival (PFS), and OS. DOR was defined as the time from the onset of a response to progression or death for any reason in patients who achieved a response. PFS was defined as the time from chemotherapy initiation to disease progression or all-cause mortality. OS was measured from the initiation of chemotherapy to death or last follow-up. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed, and the log-rank test was used to compare the curves between groups. Cox regression analysis was performed to identify prognostic clinical values associated with PFS and OS. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 51 patients, 25 (49.0%) were Younger than 50 years and 34 (66.7%) were male. Poor performance status (ECOG-PS ≥ 3) was observed in 18 patients (36.7%), and fever at diagnosis was observed in 44 patients (89.9%). Laboratory tests revealed anemia in 36 (72.0%) patients, thrombocytopenia in 25 patients (50.0%), and elevated LFTs in 38 patients (77.6%). Serum EBV DNA was detected in 42 patients (84.0%), and BM EBV positivity was confirmed in 38 patients (82.6%). Except for serum EBV DNA, the other clinical features did not differ significantly between the BM EBV-positive and EBV-negative groups (Table S1). Diagnostic testing was incomplete in many cases, especially for NK cell activity and soluble CD25 levels. Among the cohort, 29 were confirmed by BM analysis, and one was diagnosed clinically (Table 1). Imaging revealed splenomegaly in 24 patients (48.0%) and hepatomegaly in 12 patients (24.0%). All patients had stage IV disease, and 47 patients (94.0%) had PINK-E scores ≥ 3, although most are not included in Table 1. Thirteen patients (25.5%) received prephase etoposide with dexamethasone. Thirty patients (58.9%) were treated with L-ASNase-containing regimens, 14 patients (27.5%) were treated with non-L-ASNase regimens, and seven patients (13.7%) died before chemotherapy. Clinical characteristics were broadly similar between the L-ASNase and non-L-ASNase groups, except for more frequent etoposide pretreatment in the former (p = 0.009).

Among 14 patients treated with non-L-ASNase regimens, the median number of cycles was 1 (range, 1–6), and three patients (21.4%) responded, with a median DOR of 4.2 months (95% CI, 0.52–7.88) (Fig. 2A, C, E). No responders underwent transplantation. In contrast, 30 patients received L-ASNase-containing regimens (median cycles, 2; range, 1–4). The response rate was 46.7% (n = 14/30), and the median DOR was 4.5 months (95% CI, 2.67–6.33) (Fig. 2A, C, E). Among the 14 responders, seven underwent transplantation (three auto-HCT and four allo-HCT) and the remaining seven did not. Of those who underwent transplantation, three received second-line chemotherapy before HCT, while four proceeded directly to HCT.

Seventeen patients received second-line chemotherapy: eight received L-ASNase-containing regimens and nine did not. The ORR was 23.5% (n = 4/17), with a median DOR of 1.8 months (95% CI, 1.57–2.03). Response rates (25.0% vs. 22.2%) and DOR (1.90 vs. 1.60 months, p = 0.985) were similar between the L-ASNase and non-L-ASNase groups (Fig. 2D, E). Among the second-line patients, 44.4% (4/9) of the initial responders also responded to second-line therapy, whereas none of the initial non-responders responded (Table S3), although this difference was not statistically significant.

Factors influencing L-ASNase combined treatment efficacy

Among the 30 patients treated with L-ASNase, 50% (n = 15/30) received cytotoxic chemotherapies that were reduced by more than 20% (< 80% dose), whereas 33.3% (n = 10/30) completed the total L-ASNase assigned per regimen. The patients treated with reduced-dose cytotoxic chemotherapy did not show significant differences in the proportion of responders (p = 0.715; Fig. 3A). The patients with a poor performance status (ECOG 3–4, p = 0.245; Fig. 3B) or elevated LFTs (p = 0.330; Fig. 3C) were not affected by dose reduction. In contrast, patients who received the total assigned L-ASNase in the first cycle demonstrated a significant improvement in response (p = 0.019; Fig. 3D). The patients with poor performance status (ECOG 3–4, p = 0.011; Fig. 3E) strongly influenced the number of L-ASNase administrations per patient, whereas elevated LFTs did not critically impact the dose reduction (p = 1.000; Fig. 3F).

Survival outcomes

The median follow-up duration was estimated at 108.2 months (95% CI 90.36–126.04). The median PFS and OS were 0.8 months (95% CI 0.21–1.39) and 1.6 months (95% CI 0.0–3.22; Fig. 4A), respectively. Patients with ECOG-PS 0–2 had significantly better PFS than those with poor performance (1.6 months vs. 0.3 months, p = 0.046; Fig. 4B). Among the patients who achieved a response, PFS was further improved compared to non-responders (4.5 months vs. 0.6 months, p < 0.001; Fig. 4C). However, the type of chemotherapy did not result in a significant difference in PFS (0.8 months vs. 1.3 months, p = 0.114; Fig. 4D). Although the L-ASNase-containing regimen did not demonstrate superiority to non-L-ASNase chemotherapy, responders among patients treated with L-ASNase showed extended PFS (4.5 months vs. 0.6 months, p < 0.001; Fig. 4E).

The patients with favorable performance status had a significantly longer OS (4.1 months vs. 0.3 months, p = 0.004; Fig. 4F). The patients who responded to treatment showed even more significant improvement in OS (7.2 months vs. 0.7 months, p < 0.001; Fig. 4G). Although there were no significant differences in OS based on the chemotherapy regimen (2.1 months vs. 2.9 months, p = 0.054; Fig. 4H), responders among patients who received L-ASNase showed extended OS (7.2 months vs. 0.6 months, p < 0.001; F ig. 4I). Among patients treated with L-ASNase-containing regimens, those receiving SMILE had numerically longer OS compared to those receiving VIDL or MILD (6.0 vs. 1.6 or 0.3 months, respectively), although the differences were not significant (p = 0.072; Fig. 4J). Furthermore, when comparing the PFS (6.60 months vs. 0.7 months, p = 0.001) and OS (17.5 months vs. 0.8 months, p = 0.003) between patients who received HCT and those who did not, the patients who underwent HCT presented remarkable results (Fig. 4K).

Univariate Cox analysis revealed a significant association between HCT and improved PFS (HR 4.155, 95% CI 1.692–10.205, p = 0.002). In the multivariate Cox analysis for OS, both good performance status (HR 4.179, 95% CI 1.562–11.181, p = 0.004) and HCT (HR 2.710, 95% CI 1.381–5.320, p = 0.004) remained significant independent prognostic factors (Table 3).

Comparison of the efficacy of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

Among the 17 patients who responded to first-line chemotherapy, seven underwent HCT, including four who underwent allo-HCT and three who underwent auto-HCT (Fig. 5A). Patients who underwent HCT showed better DOR (5.4 vs. 2.5 months, p = 0.033; Fig. 5B), PFS (6.6 vs. 2.5 months, p = 0.010; Fig. 5C), and a trend toward improved OS (17.5 vs. 2.9 months, p = 0.068; Fig. 5D) compared to those who did not. Subgroup analysis comparing allo-HCT and auto-HCT revealed no significant difference in OS (17.5 vs. 11.0 months, p = 0.810; Fig. 5E, F). Given that all HCT recipients were selected based on their response and good performance status, the potential survival advantage may reflect selection bias, and further validation is warranted.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of etoposide pretreatment and an L-ASNase-containing chemotherapy regimen followed by HCT in 51 patients with ANKL. Despite the incorporation of L-ASNase, the overall prognosis remained dismal, with a median PFS of 0.8 months and OS of 1.6 months, findings that are comparable to previous reports. Neither pre-phase etoposide administration nor the use of L-ASNase in the first-line setting significantly improved survival across the entire cohort. However, among patients with a good performance status (ECOG-PS 0–2), those who received full dose L-ASNase-containing chemotherapy and subsequently underwent HCT demonstrated improved survival. Thus, our findings suggest a potential role for L-ASNase-based therapy and HCT in carefully selected patients.

Despite reports supporting the efficacy of early etoposide- and L-ASNase-containing regimens, this study did not establish a clear benefit of their combined use. Thirteen patients received steroids and etoposide because of suspected severe HLH symptoms prior to diagnostic confirmation; however, no significant improvement in treatment response was observed. [18] Etoposide remains the cornerstone of pediatric HLH treatment, and is widely used to manage hyperinflammation in various clinical settings. [19, 20] However, the optimal dosing and timing in adult patients with malignancy-associated secondary HLH remain unclear. Although early etoposide administration may mitigate cytokine storm and prevent organ failure, its cytotoxicity can worsen cytopenia and exacerbate cytokine release syndrome (CRS), thereby amplifying organ dysfunction. Recent case reports have suggested that biological agents such as anakinra (a human interleukin-1 receptor antagonist) and tocilizumab (an anti-interleukin-6 receptor monoclonal antibody) may interrupt cytokine storms without compromising organ function. [21–23] While the current evidence is limited, these findings support the need to accumulate experience with the early use of cytokine-targeted biologics in ANKL as a potential adjunct to conventional therapies (Fig. 5G).

L-ASNase is a key drug for the treatment of EBV-associated lymphoid malignancies. [24] However, its use is frequently limited by adverse effects including hypersensitivity reactions, pancreatitis, hepatic dysfunction, nephrotoxicity, coagulopathy, and neurotoxicity. [25, 26] In this study, 10 of 30 patients treated with L-ASNase-containing regimens received 4–7 doses and showed superior outcomes compared to those who received fewer doses. This suggests that poor performance status at presentation may lead to early discontinuation or underdosing owing to clinician concerns (Fig. 3D, E). Patients with ANKL often present with critical conditions such as multi-organ dysfunction even before treatment initiation. Thus, implementing a stepwise treatment strategy is essential to stabilize patients, interrupt the cycle of inflammation and organ failure, and enable timely administration of scheduled therapies. Although L-ASNase-based chemotherapy has only been reported in limited ANKL reports, it remains a viable option for both first- and second-line therapies for EBV-associated NK/T-cell lymphomas. [27] There remains an unmet clinical need for structured pre-phase planning before intensive therapy with L-ASNase and HCT, particularly for patients presenting with compromised organ function and hyperinflammatory features (Fig. 5G).

A whole-exosome sequencing study of 14 ANKL cases identified recurrent alterations in STAT3, RAS-MAPK pathway genes, DDX3X, and epigenetic modifiers as key molecular drivers. [28] Although primarily conducted in vitro using NK cell lines, this study revealed pronounced drug sensitivity phenotypes, particularly JAK-STAT and BCL2 inhibition. Although further studies are warranted, these findings suggest that JAK and BCL2 inhibitors have therapeutic potential for ANKL. In a separate study, Huang et al. reported that 48% of 29 patients harbored mutations in the components of the JAK/STAT pathway. [29] Aberrant activation of this pathway appears to be driven by IL-10 overproduction through an undefined mechanism, and is associated with downstream MYC activation, implicating the MYC-IL10-JAK-STAT axis in ANKL lymphomagenesis. [30] This suggests that the dual inhibition of IL-10 signaling and JAK-STAT pathway may be a rational therapeutic strategy to suppress the hyperinflammatory status in ANKL. Notably, ruxolitinib, the JAK inhibitor that also modulates the IFN-γ signaling pathway, a key mediator of HLH, may serve a dual role in targeting malignant NK cells while mitigating CRS-like manifestations (Fig. 5G).

Treatment response to ANKL was assessed using both imaging and clinical evaluations. [17] However, as ANKL primarily presents with peripheral blood involvement, BM infiltration, and hepatosplenomegaly without nodal disease, radiographic remission may not fully reflect the clinical response. In several cases, discordance between PET/CT findings and clinical status was observed. Some patients demonstrated symptoms and laboratory improvement despite an incomplete metabolic response based on imaging, whereas others showed clinical deterioration despite radiologic remission. These discrepancies underscore the limitations of imaging-based assessment of ANKL and support a dynamic, clinically integrated response approach. Unlike other NHLs, where interim PET/CT is typically performed after two or more chemotherapy cycles, most patients with ANKL experience rapid disease progression with a median PFS of less than one month. Therefore, routine imaging-based evaluations may not be applicable to ANKL. Furthermore, in clinical trials, early mortality and lack of follow-up can lead to underestimation of response rates. Therefore, early and intuitive clinical improvement based on symptoms and laboratory markers may offer a more feasible and meaningful measure of the therapeutic benefits of ANKL.

A key limitation of this study is the potential for an immortal time bias. Patients who received higher doses of L-ASNase tended to have better ECOG performance status and prolonged survival, which may have allowed more treatment opportunities rather than indicating treatment efficacy. Thus, the survival benefit observed in this group may have been confounded by indications and survivor bias. While time-dependent covariate analysis could help adjust for these effects, our small sample size and high rate of early mortality within two months—especially in patients who did not receive L-ASNase or HCTlimited the feasibility of such modeling. Future studies should implement time-varying analyses to accurately assess the independent effects of treatment exposure on survival. Moreover, early death likely led to informative censoring and biased treatment allocation, disproportionately affecting patients with worse baseline clinical status. Addressing this issue requires prospective data collection and statistical frameworks that account for early attrition.

ANKL is associated with high early mortality, which may obscure the actual effect of L-ASNase- and etoposide-containing therapies. Although treatment strategies are often adapted from ENKTL, ANKL exhibits a distinct clinical course and more aggressive disease biology. For patients with a favorable performance status, a planned treatment strategy incorporating L-ASNase and timely HCT may offer survival benefits. Although our findings suggest a survival benefit with HCT, the limited number of patients and selection of only favorable responders introduce the possibility of selection bias. Moreover, given the frequent and overwhelming systemic inflammation, a stepwise approach is essential to enable treatment delivery. Thus, future research should focus on biological agents targeting hyperinflammation and the JAK/STAT pathway to improve the outcomes of this aggressive malignancy.
