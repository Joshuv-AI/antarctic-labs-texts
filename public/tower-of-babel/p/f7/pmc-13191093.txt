Beyond Threshold Prevalence: Reframing Treatment Use and Hidden Burden in Student Insomnia
Authors: Endah Fitriasari, Muhammad Taufan Umasugi
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the article by Martínez Lledín et al, on insomnia and its therapeutic approaches among university students in Madrid. Their estimate of insomnia prevalence is informative, but it may be more accurate to describe the reported 11.5% as the prevalence of moderate-to-severe insomnia according to the chosen Insomnia Severity Index threshold, rather than the total burden of clinically relevant insomnia symptoms. This distinction matters because 39.7% of respondents fell into the subclinical range and were analytically merged with the noninsomnia group. Recent meta-analytic evidence suggests that nearly half of undergraduate students worldwide experience insomnia symptoms, with prevalence varying materially by screening instrument. The Madrid study, therefore, appears to capture higher-threshold insomnia rather than a low-burden student population.1,2

A second issue is treatment-aware misclassification. The authors report that 25.5% of students classified as having no insomnia were nonetheless using medications or herbal products for sleep, and that 17.0% of those within the normal range were also doing so. Although this may indicate self-treatment of milder sleep difficulties, the converse explanation is equally plausible: some students may have scored below the insomnia threshold because ongoing treatment had already attenuated their symptoms at the time of survey completion. This possibility is clinically important because melatonin and benzodiazepines were among the most commonly reported agents, yet the survey did not record formulation, prescription status, treatment duration, indication, or the temporal relation between product use and symptom scoring. Future studies should therefore distinguish untreated insomnia, treated‑but-persistent insomnia, and treatment-masked insomnia.1

Third, the analytical strategy may have obscured meaningful gradients of risk. After collapsing the four Insomnia Severity Index categories into a binary outcome, the study relied on bivariate chi-square comparisons. Without multivariable modelling, it remains unclear whether the reported associations with sex, academic performance, stress, or neuropsychiatric morbidity are independent or confounded. An ordinal or multinomial model across the full symptom spectrum would have preserved clinical information and allowed a more rigorous assessment of dose-response patterns.1

Finally, the therapeutic frame remains largely product-centered. Current European guidance recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, including digital delivery, as first-line treatment, whereas fast-release melatonin, antihistamines, and phytotherapeutics are not recommended for insomnia treatment. Moreover, sleep hygiene education alone has limited efficacy as a stand-alone intervention, while digital sleep interventions in college-aged populations have shown significant reductions in insomnia severity and improvements in sleep quality. In university settings, this is not a peripheral issue but a practical opportunity: behavioral and digital approaches are scalable, lower-risk, and far better aligned with campus health delivery than informal self-medication.3, 4, 5

Accordingly, future student-insomnia research should move beyond asking who uses sleep products and begin identifying who lacks access to evidence-based behavioral care, who self-medicates outside clinical supervision, and who appears asymptomatic only because treatment is already in place. That shift would advance the field from threshold-based prevalence estimates to treatment-sensitive epidemiology with clearer implications for university policy and student health services.1,5
