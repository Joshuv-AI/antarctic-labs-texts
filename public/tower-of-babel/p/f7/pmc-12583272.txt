The antitumor mechanism of immuno-flap treatment in a rat model of head and neck cancer
Authors: Hiroaki Mori, Yasuto Akiyama, Chie Maeda, Akari Kanematsu, Tadashi Ashizawa, Kazue Yamashita, Haruo Miyata, Akira Iizuka, Tomoatsu Ikeya, Yoshichika Yasunaga, Jun Araki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLocal recurrence after surgical resection is a major clinical challenge in patients with advanced head and neck cancer (HNC). We previously reported using transfer of a flap treated with dendritic cells (DCs) (“immuno-flap”) as a novel cancer immunotherapy in a rat model of HNC. However, several problems, such as the improvement of DC yield and the verification of DC tracking, remained to be solved.MethodsThe initial DC production protocol was revisited, and the specific mechanism of the antitumor effect associated with functional DCs was investigated.ResultsThe yield and the purity of potent DCs were improved, and an in vivo tracking experiment revealed that, when injected into the skin flap, the DCs migrated to local lymph nodes within 24 h. In an in vivo long-term protection model in which DCs were injected into the skin flap weekly for 4 weeks, no strong antitumor effect on HNC tumor progression was observed, but a remarkable pathological effect was verified. Moreover, an enzyme-linked immunospot assay demonstrated that more CD8+ T cells positive for PD-1, CD40LIG, and TNF-α expression and producing interferon γ specific to SCC-158 tumor cells were identified in tumors and spleens from rats treated using immuno-flaps.ConclusionsThese results indicate that immuno-flap transfer to prevent local recurrence in patients with HNC has potential for clinical application given the improved yield and quality of antitumor DCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04208-8.

Introduction

Advances in cancer treatment, such as immune checkpoint blockade and combined neoadjuvant therapy, have led to significant improvements in overall survival [1–3]. However, despite intensive multi-disciplinary therapies including surgery and adjuvant chemoradiotherapy, locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) continues to be associated with relatively high rates of local recurrence, for which no appropriate therapeutic options have yet been established [4–6]. Among the long-term survivors of head and neck cancer of > 3 years, 15% die within 5 years and 41% die within 10 years of initial diagnosis. The leading causes of death in this group are late recurrence and second primary head and neck cancer (29%) [7].

Autologous flap transfer technology, wide local excision of the tumor, and immediate cosmetic restoration by reconstructive surgery are currently available [8, 9]. Recently, neoantigens—specific immuno-antigens whose expression is restricted to cancer tissues—have been used for vaccination-based cancer immunotherapy [10–13]. Combination therapy using neoantigen vaccines with chemotherapy after curative resection has proved to be efficient for the treatment and control of pancreatic cancer recurrences [14].

We previously reported using a skin flap augmented with antitumor immunity (the “immuno-flap”) as a novel cancer immunotherapy in a rat model of HNSCC. In this approach, a dendritic cell (DC) vaccine is locally administered in conjunction with a pedicled groin skin flap overlaying the tumor site [15]. Based on the study findings, we hypothesized that immuno-flap treatment combined with immune checkpoint blockade could be a promising therapeutic approach against local recurrence in advanced HNSCC (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Protocol for skin flap immunotherapy against recurrences of advanced head and neck cancer (HNC). Protocol for skin flap immunotherapy against recurrences of advanced head and neck cancer (HNC). Locally advanced HNCs have a high rate of local recurrence even after intensive chemoradiation combined with surgical ablation. In our protocol, dendritic cells (DCs) derived from the blood of cancer patients are developed into vaccines through blood monocyte culturing and coincubation with tumor lysates prepared from surgically resected tumors. Once a skin flap is transplanted, the tumor-specific DC vaccine is injected into the flap. The immunized skin flap (“immuno-flap”) is predicted to induce a tumor-specific T cell response in local lymph nodes, which might contribute to the regulation of local or systemic tumor recurrences

In the present study, we assessed the potential for the clinical application of this immuno-flap immunotherapy after the yield and quality of DCs obtained using the initial protocol had been improved, and we aimed to elucidate the specific mechanism of the antitumor effect of functional DCs.

Materials and methods

Animals and cell line

Pathogen-free male Fisher rats 5–8 weeks of age were purchased from the Clea Co., Ltd. (Shizuoka, Japan) and housed humanely in accordance with guidelines (Br J Cancer in 2010) concerning the welfare of animals used in cancer research. The experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shizuoka Cancer Center Research Institute. SCC-158 cells purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (Osaka, Japan) were cultured as previously reported [15].

Reagents and antibodies

Recombinant rat granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor and rat interleukin-4 were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) for the cultures required to generate mature rat DCs. Monoclonal antibodies against rat CD11b/c (clone OX-42: BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), rat CD103 (clone OX-62: BioLegend), and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II antigen RT1B (clone OX-6: BioLegend), rat CD80 (Clone 3H5: BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and rat CD86 (clone 24F: BD Biosciences) were obtained for FACS experiments to characterize rat DCs as previously reported [15]. Anti-rat CD3 monoclonal antibody (mAb)-BV421 (clone 1F4: BD Biosciences), anti-rat CD4 mAb-PE-Cy7 (clone OX-35: BD Biosciences), anti-rat CD8b mAb-BV605 (clone 341: BD Biosciences), anti-rat CD25 mAb-BV510 (clone OX-39: BD Biosciences), and anti-rat CD45 mAb-R718 (clone OX-1: BD Biosciences) were purchased for the analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) on a flow cytometer.

Bone marrow-derived DC production

Unfractionated rat bone marrow (BM) cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium containing 5% fetal bovine serum (cat. S1600-500, BioWest, Nuaillé, France) and incubated with 10 ng/mL recombinant rat granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor and 10 ng/mL rat interleukin-4 for 7 days. The method for producing SCC-158 tumor cell lysates has previously been described [15]. The initial protocol of coincubation of DCs with tumor lysates on day 7 and exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on day 8 was changed in terms of incubation time, but the concentrations of the tumor lysates and LPS were not changed. After the exposure of DC s to LPS, dead cells detected by Ghost Dye™ Red 780 (cat. #13–0865, TONBO biosciences, Seattle, WA) were deleted from DC cultures. Living single cell suspension was used for flow cytometry.

Immuno-flap elevation and grafting

Rats were anesthetized with vaporized isoflurane (DS Pharma Animal Health, Inc., Osaka, Japan) and shaved. Using scissors, a skin paddle of 2 × 1 cm spindle shape, together with its irrigating blood vessels (circumflex iliac artery and veins) and subcutaneous inguinal lymph nodes, was made in the right groin region and transferred to the tumor inoculation site in the flank. Sensitized mature DCs were then injected locally into the flap. Should the skin paddle extraction site be defective because of tumor resection, the flap was returned to its original location. The skin incision was sutured with nylon thread.

Rat models

Using the immuno-flaps, two different in vivo DC-based immunotherapy models were assessed. In the DC tracking model (supplementary Fig. 1), SCC-158 tumor cells were subcutaneously transplanted at the flank on day − 14. A skin flap was elevated and transferred to the tumor inoculation site in the flank, and injected with DCs on day 0. At 24, 48, and 72 h after injection with DCs, the resulting immuno-flaps were harvested, and the CD103+ DCs were tracked using immunohistochemistry (IHC). In the immuno-flap long protection model (n = 6 rats per group; supplementary Fig. 2), a skin flap was elevated and both SCC-158 tumors (2 × 105 cells) and DCs (1 × 106 cells) were injected into the skin flap on day 0. Thereafter, weekly injections of DCs were performed on days 7, 14, and 21. On day 28, the rats were euthanized, tumors and spleens were harvested, and antitumor effects and immunologic reactions were investigated using an enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISPOT: BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and IHC.

Isolation of rat TILs

SCC-158 tumors and spleens were harvested from untreated (control) and immuno-flap-treated rats on day 28 and were dissociated using a tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Cell debris was subsequently removed using a debris removal solution (Miltenyi Biotec) mediated by gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). Single-cell suspensions from tumors and spleens underwent flow cytometry analysis. Rat T cells isolated from resected tumor tissues using LS columns and rat pan–T cell microbeads on the QuadroMACS Separators System (Miltenyi Biotec) underwent ELISPOT assay and real-time PCR. Single spleen-cell suspensions isolated by physical dissociation from spleen tissues underwent ELISPOT assay.

Rat immune cell ELISPOT assays

First, 96-well microplates precoated with anti-interferon γ (IFN-γ) antibody specific to the ELISPOT assay (ELISpot Flex, Rat IFN-γ-ALP: MABTEC Inc., Nacka Strand, Sweden) were prepared after overnight incubation at 4 °C. In these ELISPOT microplates, 1 × 105 rat T cells or spleen cells were incubated overnight with irradiated rat SCC-158 tumor cells at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. After the removal of cells by pipetting, rat IFN-γ produced from immune cells was trapped on ELISPOT membranes and stained with biotinylated IFN-γ detection antibody and streptavidin–alkaline phosphatase conjugates. Positively stained spots on membranes were then measured using an AID EliSpot Reader (AID Autoimmun Diagnostika GmbH, Strassberg, Germany). Specifically, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, appropriate measurement conditions were set up as follows: spot count setting with a minimum intensity of 20, minimum gradient of 1.0 and minimum size of 30.

Immunohistochemistry

Immune flap and SCC-158 tumor tissues were fixed in 10% formalin neutral buffer solution and subjected to formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) preparation. The tissue blocks were sectioned so as to obtain the maximal tumor area. Hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections were assessed for tumor viability by well-trained pathologists. TILs were analyzed by applying an anti-CD4 mAb (clone CAL4: Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and an anti-CD8 mAb (clone OX-8: Abcam) to tumor sections from the control and immuno-flap-treated groups and then using an automatic WinROOF-based algorithm developed in-house to count the positive cells as previously described [16]. In the tracking experiment, rat DC localization was evaluated by IHC using anti-rat CD103 mAb (clone EPR22590-27: Abcam).

Real-time PCR for rat cytokines

Real-time PCR analyses of rat cytokines and chemokines such as CD3E, CD4, CD8B, PDCD1, CD40 LG, CD127, CXCR3, NCR1, IL2, IFNG, GZMB, TNFA, CCL4, CCL5, and CXCL10 were performed using the QuantStudio 12 K Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as previously described [17]. Total RNA was isolated from rat peripheral blood mononuclear cells using the High Pure RNA Paraffin Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA).

Statistical analysis

For in vivo studies, differences between the control and immuno-flap-treated groups were assessed by two-way analysis of variance with a Shirley–Williams test. Differences for other experiments, such as TIL-FACS analysis, ELISPOT assay, real-time PCR, and IHC analysis, including CD103+ cell counting, were evaluated for significance with the Student’s t test and Mann–Whitney U test. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant. Additionally, 10 fields of view in each section at high magnification (X200) were selected under a light microscope (IX71N-23TFL/DIC, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and positive cell numbers were assessed with an automatic WinROOF-based algorithm developed in-house for CD103+ cell counting.

Results

Improvement in the DC production protocol

Table 1 presents the improvement achieved in the DC culture protocol. The culture protocol was modified as follows: At step 1, 2-mercaptoethanol was added, and half the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium when the cytokines were added. At step 2, the incubation time with tumor lysate was reduced to overnight. At step 3, incubation with LPS was reduced to 6 h. At steps 4 and 5, the concentration of fetal bovine serum in the culture medium was increased to 10% from 5%, and nonessential amino acid and pyruvate acid were added to the cultures. Step 5 was performed in a larger scale than step 4. Subsequently, cell viability and DC purity increased to 68.5% and 80.8%, respectively, and the DC yield rate improved to 0.85% from 0.05% (Table 1). Supplementary Fig. 3 shows improved points in the context of comparing with the original DC culture protocol. DC characterization in vitro

The rat DCs in the living cells were characterized by flow cytometric analysis, with the rat DC gate (P1 gating, DC-enriched fraction) being illustrated as a large and widely distributed population (Fig. 2a). The mean percentage of those DCs that were determined to be CD103+Class II+ was 97.5% (Fig. 2a). The frequencies of cells expressing specific DC markers were as follows: CD103, 98.3%; MHC class II, 96.8%; CD80, 95.0%; and CD86, 96.3% (Fig. 2b). On the other hand, in the non-P1 gating population (lymphoid fraction) DC marker-positive cells were not much detected (data not shown). Effect of DC vaccination on SCC-158 tumor proliferation

Figure 4a presents the experimental schedule for the in vivo immuno-flap long protection model. The tumor volumes measured after tumor inoculation on day 0 revealed no significant growth suppression resulting from the weekly DC vaccine injections for 4 weeks (Fig. 4b). However, despite the lack of a difference in tumor size, pathology examination of tumors harvested on day 28 revealed massive necrosis and internal immune cell accumulations in the tumors from the group receiving the DC vaccine injections (Fig. 5). Moreover, tumor viability was reduced in tumors from DC-vaccinated rats compared with tumors from control rats (52% vs. 84%). Discussion

Advanced HNSCC has a high recurrence rate of approximately 30% and a 5-year survival rate between 50 and 60%. Thus, reducing the recurrence rate is a crucial clinical problem [4, 5].

We previously reported using a skin flap augmented with antitumor immunity through local administration of a DC vaccine, termed the “immuno-flap”, as a novel cancer immunotherapy in a rat model [14]. That approach has proven efficacy in inhibiting local recurrence after comprehensive combined therapy (surgery plus chemoradiation) for advanced head and neck cancers (Fig. 1).

In the present study, we improved our initial DC production protocol, boosting the DC yield and quality, and we also investigated the specific mechanism involved in the observed antitumor effect of the DC vaccine.

Table 1 presents data verifying a significant improvement in DC production (yield rate) after we revisited our initial protocol. The yield rate of rat BM-derived DCs has rarely been reported [18–21], but Chen-Woan et al. demonstrated that the specific yield rate of rat BM-derived DCs (MHC Class II+OX62+) was approximately 1% in the presence of mouse granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor [18]. We revised our culture protocol for rat BM-derived DCs using a 1% yield rate as a benchmark, and we improved our yield rate to 0.85% mainly by reducing the incubation time with tumor lysates and LPS.

Our DC tracking experiment revealed that migration of CD103+ DCs to regional lymph nodes after their injection into skin flaps was significant and that the cells remained for up to 72 h. Those results indicate that a tumor-specific T cell response could potentially be triggered by CD103+ DCs migrating into regional lymph nodes.

Our long-term protection model of weekly DC injections into the immuno-flap for 4 weeks revealed a significant antitumor effect on SCC-158 tumors and induction of tumor-specific TILs at the tumor site. Importantly, tumors harvested on day 28 seemed to be larger in the immuno-flap-treated group than in the control group. However, pathology examinations revealed a significant antitumor effect in the treated group because far fewer viable tumor cells were identified in that group than in the control group. Those results might indicate that DC vaccine treatment resulted in a phenomenon resembling pseudo progression [22–24].

This study successfully established a rat cytotoxic T lymphocyte ELISPOT assay. After DC vaccine injections, IFN-γ-producing effector T cells specific to SCC-158 tumor cells were induced and observed to contribute to an antitumor effect. We previously reported a phase II clinical trial of DC vaccine immunotherapy for newly diagnosed malignant gliomas in which the recruitment of IFN-γ-producing effector T cells positive for the CXCR3, PD-1, CD40LIG, and CD127 markers was crucial to the survival benefit observed in treated patients [25]. A similar Th1 response-associated antitumor effect is demonstrated in anti-PD-1 antibody therapy, which involves T cell–DC interactions mediated by IL-12 and INF-γ production [26, 27]. Another antitumor effect based on interactions mediated by IL-12 and INF-γ production was observed after direct intratumoral injection of IL-12 DNA vector or IL-12-producing DCs; potent antitumor effector T cells were induced as a result [28, 29]. In our immuno-flap model in rats, the DC vaccine induced similar effector T cells that then contributed to an antitumor effect. Those results indicate that cancer immunotherapy using the immuno-flap could potentially be a promising novel therapeutic regimen mediated by IL-12 production from CD103+ rat DCs.

CD103 (OX62 in the rat) is known to be expressed on intraepithelial lymphocytes and a subset of DCs in the gut mucosa [30]. Interestingly, CD103 can bind integrin beta 7 to form integrin molecule aEb7, which can bind cadherin E as the ligand [31]. CD103+ DCs producing IL-12 have also been reported to be involved in natural killer cell-based cancer metastasis suppression and anti-cancer immunosurveillance [32, 33]. Kuhn et al. recently reported that conventional CD103+ DCs are necessary for the promotion of an antitumor effect by CD40L-expressing chimeric antigen receptor T cells [34]. Our CD103+ DC tracking study revealed specific migration of CD103+ DCs into regional lymph nodes after their injection into skin flaps. Those observations indicate that CD103+ DCs might play an important role in homing to tumor tissues [35] and inducing activated tumor-specific TILs [36].

Few studies applying skin flap immunized with DCs as immunotherapeutic modality to advanced head and neck cancers have been reported to date. Recently, DC-based immunotherapies including neoantigen targeting combined with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy have been tried as phase I clinical trial for various solid cancers, such as melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and even pancreatic cancer. In some studies, significant beneficial effect on relapse-free survival is obviously reported [11–14]. Impressively, several researchers demonstrated that activated DCs resident in the draining lymphatics or lymph nodes adjacent to tumor tissues are crucial to antitumor response induction in head and neck cancers [37–39]. In the present study, as far as immune-flap is probed to be effective for the control of tumor relapse, ICB-combined approaches should be evaluated as the next step of specific immunotherapy against advanced head and neck cancers.

The following technical limitations remain unresolved in this study: (1) The experimental protocol is animal experiment level, which should be assessed in terms of quality, precision and reproducibility regarding the antitumor effect in vivo, (2) securing the safety of DC vaccine-given skin flap is important, and (3) several challenges on the way to clinical application, such as refining DC culture method, patient-derived tumor lysate preparation and quality check issues of DC vaccinations, are experienced.

Finally, based on these current results from immuno-flap DC vaccine immunotherapy in a rat head and neck cancer model, we are planning for a human clinical trial in the near future. Once refined, our improved DC vaccine protocol will be suitable for use in clinical trials for patients with HNSCC.
