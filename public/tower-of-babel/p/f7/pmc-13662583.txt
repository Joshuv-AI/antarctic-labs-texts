A non-bactericidal antimicrobial peptide provides protective effect against bacterial sepsis via regulation of antimicrobial immunity and vascular endothelial functions
Authors: Yan-Mei He, Ting Lin, Li-Xian Mu, Xiao-Yan Zhou, Yan Shen, Jia-Yi Yang, Hai-Yan Luo, Peng-Yao Liu, Lin Wei, Jing Wu, Hai-Long Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe anti-infective properties of bactericidal antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have been extensively studied, yet the functions and mechanisms of non-bactericidal AMPs in sepsis remain poorly understood. In this study, a novel β-sheet cathelicidin peptide, named Og-CATH, was identified from the skin of Odorrana grahami. Although Og-CATH showed no detectable direct antimicrobial activity in vitro, it provided both prophylactic and therapeutic protection in mouse models of sepsis induced by Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, or cecal ligation and puncture. This protection depended on neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages, but not T or B lymphocytes. Og-CATH did not directly chemoattract phagocytes. Instead, it stimulated P2X7 receptor-dependent chemokine release from macrophages, thereby promoting macrophage and neutrophil trafficking to sites of infection. Og-CATH also enhanced oxygen-independent bacterial clearance by increasing phagocytosis and neutrophil degranulation. In parallel, Og-CATH suppressed tissue factor production, a major trigger of the extrinsic coagulation cascade, and increased activated protein C expression in endothelial cells and mice, thereby reducing pulmonary fibrin deposition and thrombosis. Mechanistically, Og-CATH targeted myeloid differentiation protein 2 (MD2) and inhibited lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced recruitment of MyD88 to TLR4 in endothelial cells, thus preserving endothelial barrier integrity during sepsis. These findings identify Og-CATH as a non-bactericidal AMP that protects against sepsis by coordinating phagocyte recruitment, antibacterial effector activity, coagulation control, and endothelial stabilization. Og-CATH therefore represents a promising immunomodulatory candidate for further therapeutic development against sepsis.Keywords: Antimicrobial peptide, Antimicrobial immunity, Endothelial functions, P2X7 receptor, Myeloid differentiation protein 2 (MD2)

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and remains a global health burden, with an estimated 48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths each year (Nedeva, 2021). Timely and appropriate antibiotic therapy is central to sepsis management; however, the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria continues to compromise treatment efficacy and limit reductions in mortality from severe infections (Eurosurveillance Editorial Team, 2013; Strich et al., 2020). Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are gene-encoded molecules generated from precursor peptides through one or more proteolytic processing steps. These peptides have attracted considerable attention as potential agents against antimicrobial resistance, and AMPs with direct antimicrobial activity have been proposed as candidates for the treatment of bacterial sepsis (Mookherjee et al., 2007). However, accumulating evidence indicates that the in vivo anti-infective efficacy of many AMPs may depend more on immunomodulatory activity than on direct antimicrobial killing. This distinction is important because antimicrobial activity is often markedly reduced under physiological conditions, including exposure to monovalent and divalent salts and host factors such as negatively charged polysaccharides, glycosaminoglycans, F-actin, and apolipoproteins (Alford et al., 2020; Herb & Schramm, 2021; Mookherjee & Hancock, 2007; Segal, 2005; Teng et al., 2017). These findings have increased interest in new anti-infective strategies, with a shift in AMP development toward selective modulation of host innate immune functions. Cathelicidins are a major family of gene-encoded AMPs found across diverse animal species. As effector molecules of innate immunity, cathelicidins contribute to early host defense against invading pathogens and represent promising templates for anti-infective therapy. Although the antibacterial activities and therapeutic potential of bactericidal cathelicidins have been extensively characterized, the biological functions, mechanisms of action, and structure-activity relationships of non-bactericidal cathelicidins remain poorly defined.

Sepsis reflects a dynamic and often destructive interaction among invading pathogens, innate immune activation, inflammatory signaling, endothelial dysfunction, and coagulation imbalance. While several studies have examined the capacity of AMPs to modulate innate immune and inflammatory responses during sepsis (Cai et al., 2018; Li et al., 2013; Nagaoka et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021), far less is known about how these molecules influence the hemostatic system. Endothelial cells (ECs) occupy a central position at the interface of inflammation and coagulation, where they regulate vascular homeostasis, inflammatory tone, and barrier integrity. During sepsis, endotoxin exposure and systemic inflammatory injury disrupt endothelial barriers and induce endothelial tissue factor (TF) expression, thereby triggering the extrinsic coagulation cascade. Subsequent conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin promotes microvascular thrombosis and amplifies tissue damage (Aird, 2001; Iba et al., 2023; van der Poll & Parker, 2020). These endothelial alterations are now recognized as key determinants of sepsis progression and important therapeutic targets. However, whether AMPs, especially non-bactericidal AMPs, can restore or reprogram endothelial function during sepsis is still not fully understood.

In the present study, we identified Og-CATH, a cathelicidin AMP from the amphibian Odorrana grahami, and assessed its potential as a non-bactericidal immunomodulatory candidate against sepsis. We evaluated thermal, serum, and salt stability by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and characterized secondary structural features by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. Direct antimicrobial activity was tested in vitro, while prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy was assessed in experimental sepsis models in vivo. We then investigated the mechanisms underlying Og-CATH-mediated protection, with emphasis on phagocyte recruitment, phagocyte-mediated bacterial clearance, coagulation cascade activation, platelet function, thrombosis, and endothelial procoagulant-anticoagulant balance. Finally, we examined the regulatory effects of Og-CATH on endothelial inflammation, apoptosis, and barrier integrity and defined its molecular target and downstream signaling pathways.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Frog collection

Adult O. grahami specimens weighing 21–30 g were collected from Lijiang County, Yunnan Province, southwestern China. After capture, frogs were maintained in plastic containers until experimental use.

cDNA cloning and peptide synthesis

Total RNA extraction and cDNA library construction from O. grahami skin were performed according to a previously described procedure (Yang et al., 2017). An antisense degenerate primer (5′-TTGTCTGCCTCCTCGGCTTCC-3′) was designed based on the conserved amphibian cathelin domain sequence and paired with the 5′-PCR primer provided by the cDNA library construction kit. To obtain the full-length cDNA sequence encoding the Og-CATH precursor, a gene-specific sense primer (5′-ATGGCGCTCGCTGCTGCACTC-3′) was designed and used with the kit-provided 3′-PCR primer (5′-ATTCTAGAGGCCGAGGCGGCCG-3′). PCR amplification was performed with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 92°C for 10 s, annealing at 50°C for 30 s, extension at 72°C for 40 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min.

Og-CATH (AGRRRNRPPGRPRFPGLNNPIAGIPGRK) and a scrambled control peptide, sOg-CATH (FPRNRAKPGRIRPGRPNGARLNPIRGGP), were synthesized by Synpeptide Bio-Tech Co. (Nanjing, China). Peptide purity was >95%, as determined by HPLC.

Cells, bacteria, and mice

Mouse primary peritoneal macrophages (MPMs) and bone marrow-derived neutrophils (BMDNs) were isolated from male C57BL/6 mice as described previously (Kawasaki & Kawai, 2014). Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human monocytic leukemia THP-1 cells were obtained from the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Kunming, China), and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Microorganisms, including Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, and fungi, were propagated at 37°C in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth.

Male C57BL/6 mice and BALB/c mice weighing 18–22 g were acquired from the Laboratory Animal Department of Kunming Medical University (Kunming, China). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Yunnan Institutes of Health (China).

Bacterial infection models

Odorrana grahami frogs weighing 21–30 g (n=4) and male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) received Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection 4 h before or 4 h after challenge with S. aureus or E. coli. The bacterial inocula were 108 CFU per frog and 2×108 CFU per mouse. Peritoneal lavage fluid was collected 24 h after bacterial inoculation for bacterial counting.

For the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model, male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g received Og-CATH, sOg-CATH, or meropenem (10 mg/kg each) by intraperitoneal or intravenous injection 4 h before or 4 h after CLP. CLP was performed as described previously (Rittirsch et al., 2009). Survival was monitored for 7 days. For endpoint analyses, peripheral blood and organs, including lung, liver, and kidney tissues, were collected 24 h after CLP to determine bacterial burdens and concentrations of proinflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), using commercial assay kits (Multi Sciences, China). Lung, liver, and kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, sectioned, and stained with a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (Solarbio, China). Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and creatinine levels were quantified using commercial assay kits. Survival experiments included six mice per group, whereas bacterial burden, cytokine, and histological analyses included four mice per group.

Chemotaxis assay in vivo

Odorrana grahami frogs weighing 21–30 g (n=4) were intraperitoneally administered Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Peritoneal lavage cells were collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after injection and counted with a hemocytometer. Cell morphology was then examined by light microscopy after Wright-Giemsa staining.

Male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) received an intraperitoneal injection of Og-CATH, sOg-CATH, or meropenem at 10 mg/kg. An equal volume of PBS was used as the vehicle control. Peritoneal lavage cells were collected at 12, 24, and 48 h after peptide or meropenem administration to determine recruited cell populations. Myeloid cells were analyzed using FITC-conjugated anti-mouse Gr-1 and PE-conjugated anti-mouse F4/80 antibodies. Cells were incubated with the corresponding antibodies at room temperature for 30 min, washed with PBS, and analyzed using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer and FlowJo v7 software.

Chemotaxis assay in vitro

Direct chemotactic activity was assessed using Transwell chambers in 24-well plates. Neutrophils or macrophages were suspended at 7×106 cells/mL and added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 500 μL of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 2% FBS and Og-CATH at 25, 50, or 100 μg/mL (dissolved in PBS), with PBS used as the vehicle control. After 8 h of incubation, cells that migrated into the lower chamber were collected and counted using a hemocytometer. The number of cells recovered from the lower chamber was used as the migration index (Xue et al., 2023).

Indirect chemotaxis was examined using a macrophage co-culture system, as described earlier (Liu et al., 2023). Macrophages were suspended at 4×106 cells/mL and inoculated into the lower chamber 2 h prior to co-incubation. Og-CATH at 25, 50, or 100 μg/mL (dissolved in PBS), or PBS as the vehicle control, was then added to the lower chamber. Neutrophils or macrophages were suspended at 7×106 cells/mL, and 100 μL of the cell suspension was placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell insert. After 8 h of incubation, cells remaining in the upper chamber were counted using a hemocytometer. Migration was determined from the reduction in the number of cells retained in the upper chamber.

Myeloid cell-deficient sepsis models

For monocyte/macrophage depletion, male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) were intraperitoneally administered 200 μL of clodronate liposomes (FormuMax Scientific, CA, USA) 48 h before CLP (Cai et al., 2018). For neutrophil depletion, mice were intraperitoneally administered cyclophosphamide (Sigma, USA) at 200 mg/kg on day 0 and 150 mg/kg on day 3 (Lei et al., 2020). Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) was then administered intraperitoneally to myeloid cell-deficient mice, and peritoneal cells were harvested at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after injection for flow cytometric analysis of recruited cell populations. To assess the contribution of myeloid cells to the protective effect of Og-CATH during sepsis, CLP was performed in depleted mice, followed by intraperitoneal administration of Og-CATH 4 h before or 4 h after CLP. Bacterial burdens in peritoneal lavage fluid were determined 24 h after CLP.

Regulatory effects of Og-CATH on macrophages

MPMs were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with Og-CATH (100 µg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or meropenem (1 mg/mL). After 6 h of incubation, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA), and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time system to quantify CXCL and CCL chemokine expression, as described previously (Li et al., 2021). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

For in vivo chemokine analysis, male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) were intraperitoneally injected with Og-CATH (10 mg/kg). Peritoneal lavage fluid was collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after injection. Chemokine concentrations in macrophage culture supernatants and mouse peritoneal lavage fluid were analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Multi Sciences, China).

Pharmacological inhibition assays were performed to identify receptors involved in Og-CATH-induced chemokine production. Pertussis toxin, a Gi protein inhibitor (Invitrogen, USA), KN-62, a P2X7 receptor inhibitor (Invitrogen, USA), MTS510, a TLR4 inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and WRW4, a FLPR1 antagonist (GL Biochem, China), were used. All inhibitors were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). For receptor-blocking experiments, MPMs were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/well and allowed to adhere. Cells were pretreated with KN-62 (2 µmol/L), WRW4 (10 µg/mL), or MTS510 (10 μg/mL) for 1 h, followed by stimulation with Og-CATH (100 μg/mL) for 6 h. CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, and CCL2 concentrations in culture supernatants were then analyzed using ELISA kits (Multi Sciences, China).

Regulatory effects of Og-CATH on neutrophils

For phagocytosis assays, BMDNs were pretreated with Og-CATH (25, 50, or 100 µg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or PBS for 2 h. Both S. aureus and E. coli were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) fluorescent dye (10 μM in PBS) at 37°C for 30 min and then fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, China) for 1 h. After two washes with PBS, CFSE-labeled bacterial particles were incubated with BMDNs for 1 h. Cells were then washed, and extracellular fluorescence was quenched with trypan blue (15 mg/mL; Solarbio, China). Phagocytic uptake of bacterial particles was quantified by measuring CFSE fluorescence using flow cytometry.

Neutrophil bactericidal activity was evaluated using BMDNs seeded into 24-well plates at 1×106 cells/well. For reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection, cells were treated with Og-CATH (100 μg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or PBS for 20 min at 37°C, then collected and stained with dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; 5 µmol/L in PBS) at 37°C for 30 min. Fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry, and ROS levels were expressed as fold changes in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) relative to the PBS-treated control. For nitric oxide (NO) assays, cells were treated with Og-CATH (25, 50, or 100 μg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 6 h in a cell culture incubator. Nitrite concentrations in culture supernatants were measured using Griess reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, China). For degranulation assays, BMDNs were exposed to Og-CATH (25, 50, or 100 μg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or PBS for 6 h. Bactericidal/permeability-increasing protein (BPI), lactoferrin, and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) levels in culture supernatants were assessed using ELISA kits (Fine Biotech, China). For neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation assays, BMDNs were treated with Og-CATH (100 µg/mL), phorbol myristate acetate (PMA, 15 μg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or PBS, followed by staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) or anti-citrullinated histone H3 (Cit-H3) antibody (1:100). Nuclei and NET structures were visualized by confocal microscopy.

Regulatory effects of Og-CATH on HUVECs

To assess cytokine generation, HUVECs were seeded in 24-well plates, grown to 80% confluence, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h with or without LPS (10 μg/mL) in the presence of Og-CATH (25, 50, or 100 μg/mL). TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β concentrations in culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (Multi Sciences, China).

For apoptosis assays, HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 24 h with Og-CATH (25, 50, or 100 μg/mL) and LPS (10 μg/mL). After two washes with PBS, cells were collected using trypsinization and stained using an apoptosis detection kit (Biyuntian, China). Apoptosis rates were determined with a FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software. Total protein was extracted for western blot analysis of endothelial barrier-associated and inflammatory signaling proteins. The following primary antibodies were used: β-actin (CST, USA), E-cadherin (ZENBIO, China), occludin (ZENBIO, China), ZO-1 (ZENBIO, China), IκBα (CST, USA), phospho-IκBα (Ser32) (CST, USA), IKKβ (CST, USA), phospho-IKKα/β (CST, USA), phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (CST, USA), and NF-κB p65 (CST, USA). Quantitative analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

To assess procoagulant and anticoagulant activity, HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates, grown to 80% confluence, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h with or without LPS (10 μg/mL) in the presence of Og-CATH (25, 50, or 100 μg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or meropenem (1 mg/mL). In parallel, male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) received PBS or Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection 4 h before or 4 h after CLP. Lung tissues were collected 24 h after Og-CATH treatment. Tissue factor (TF; ZENBIO, China), thrombomodulin (TM; ZENBIO, China), endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR; Abcam, USA), and activated protein C (APC; Abcam, USA) levels in HUVECs and lung tissues were measured by western blotting as described previously (He et al., 2024).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

HUVECs were seeded in 6-well culture plates and grown to approximately 80% confluence. Cells were treated at 37°C for 1 h with LPS (10 μg/mL), Og-CATH (100 μg/mL), or the corresponding control conditions. After treatment, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in 1 mL Co-IP lysis buffer supplemented with complete protease inhibitor cocktail and 1 mmol/L phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12000 ×g for 12 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatants were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-TLR4 antibody (Proteintech, China). IgG was used in parallel as a negative immunoprecipitation control. Prewashed magnetic beads were then added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Beads were washed three times with Tris-buffered saline (TBS), and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer at 95°C for 5 min. Eluted samples were analyzed by western blotting. The antibodies used for immunoblotting included β-actin (CST, USA), TLR4 (Proteintech, China), MD2 (Abcam, USA), and MyD88 (Proteintech, China).

Affinity measurement

Interactions between Og-CATH and TLR4/MD2/LPS were investigated using the FortéBio Octet system (FortéBio, Inc., CA, USA). Og-CATH was dialyzed in PBST (PBS containing 0.01% Tween-20) and diluted to 25 μg/mL. AR2G biosensor tips (FortéBio, Inc., CA, USA) were prewetted in PBST for 10 min before use, and Og-CATH was immobilized on the sensors according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PBS-loaded sensors were used as controls. TLR4 (1 and 10 μmol/L; MCE), MD2 (0.046–0.733 mmol/L; Abcam), or LPS (1 and 10 μg/mL; Sigma) was added to 96-well microtiter plates (Millipore, MA, USA) at 150 μL per well. Binding assays were performed using the following sequence: baseline for 120 s, activation for 120 s, loading for 90 s, quenching for 120 s, washing for 120 s, association for 360 s, and dissociation for 360 s. Equilibrium dissociation constants were calculated using Octet Analysis software.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis

Binding affinity between Og-CATH and MD2 was quantified by direct titration using a PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern PANalytical Inc., MA, USA). Og-CATH was dissolved in ddH2O and diluted to 20 μmol/L, and MD2 was diluted to 2 μM. Og-CATH was loaded into the injection syringe, and MD2 was placed in the sample cell of the calorimeter. Each experiment consisted of 19 sequential injections of Og-CATH into the MD2-containing cell, with an injection interval of 150 s and constant stirring at 750 rpm. All measurements were carried out at 25°C.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

HUVECs were cultured in 6-well plates, harvested, and washed twice with cold PBS. Cells were resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a complete protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were centrifuged at 12000 ×g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. Equal volumes of lysates were incubated with Og-CATH (100 μg/mL) or PBS at room temperature for 1 h, aliquoted into 100 μL portions, and heated at 50°C, 55°C, 60°C, 65°C, 70°C, 75°C, or 80°C for 5 min. Samples were then cooled at room temperature for 3 min and centrifuged at 20000×g for 20 min at 4°C to separate soluble proteins from precipitated fractions. Supernatants were collected for immunoblot analysis using an anti-MD2 antibody (Abcam, USA).

Antithrombotic activity assays in mice

For fibrin detection, male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) received PBS, Og-CATH (10 mg/kg), sOg-CATH (10 mg/kg), or meropenem (10 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection 4 h before or 4 h after CLP. Lung tissues were collected after 24 h, and fibrin deposition was detected using an immunohistochemistry kit (Boster Biological Technology Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primary antibody used was anti-fibrinogen (Abcam, USA). In parallel, qPCR was performed to quantify expression of genes encoding TF, APC, urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA), and plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1) in mouse lung tissues. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

For the FeCl3-induced carotid artery thrombosis model, male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) were intraperitoneally injected with 0.9% saline, heparin (1000 U/kg), sOg-CATH (10 mg/kg), meropenem (10 mg/kg), or Og-CATH (10 mg/kg). One hour after treatment, mice were anesthetized and secured in a supine position. After shaving the neck region, the left common carotid artery was carefully exposed and isolated over a length of approximately 1 cm. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane-backed waterproof blue paper was placed underneath the artery to reduce background interference. Filter paper saturated with 10% (w/v) FeCl3 solution was then applied to the artery for 30–60 s, and residual FeCl3 was thoroughly removed with PBS. Blood flow was monitored for 30 min using a laser speckle imaging system, and the time to complete vascular occlusion was recorded. Data were analyzed using the dedicated software supplied with the system.

For the carrageenan-induced tail thrombosis model, male BALB/c mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) received 0.9% saline, heparin (1000 U/kg), Og-CATH (10 mg/kg), sOg-CATH (10 mg/kg), or meropenem (10 mg/kg) via tail-vein injection. After 1 h, tail thrombosis was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 0.25 mL of 1% carrageenan, with ambient temperature maintained at 17°C. Tail thrombus length was measured at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-induction.

Statistical analysis

Data were plotted using GraphPad Prism v.8. Bars indicate standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. Comparisons between two groups were carried out using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data are expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments or four mice per group, as indicated. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures or legends where appropriate. *: P<0.05 or #: P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Identification of a novel β-sheet cathelicidin from O. grahami with favorable stability under physiological and stress conditions

A cDNA sequence encoding a cathelicidin precursor was obtained from the skin cDNA library of O. grahami by PCR-based cloning and deposited in GenBank under accession number OQ870535. The inferred precursor contained 150 amino acid residues, comprising a predicted 20-residue signal peptide, a conserved 102-residue cathelin domain, and a 28-residue C-terminal mature peptide designated Og-CATH. The complete amino acid sequence of Og-CATH is shown in Supplementary Figure S1A. The mature peptide was highly cationic, containing eight strongly basic residues, including seven arginine residues and one lysine residue. Og-CATH had a calculated molecular mass of 3.08 kDa and a predicted isoelectric point (pI) of 12.78. BLAST analysis assigned the Og-CATH precursor to the cathelicidin family, characterized by a conserved cathelin domain and a variable C-terminal mature peptide region (Supplementary Figure S1B).

The secondary structure of Og-CATH was examined by CD spectroscopy under aqueous conditions and in membrane-mimetic environments containing 30–120 mmol/L SDS. Og-CATH adopted a predominantly β-sheet conformation in both H2O and SDS solutions, with β-sheet content ranging from 46.41% to 49.50% (Supplementary Figure S1C). Given that physicochemical stability is a critical determinant of peptide therapeutic potential, the thermal, serum, and salt stability of Og-CATH was further evaluated by HPLC. Og-CATH remained stable after exposure to 50–400 mmol/L NaCl, indicating strong salt tolerance (Supplementary Figure S1D). The peptide also retained structural integrity after incubation at 37°C for 7 days, 10 repeated freeze-thaw cycles at –80°C, and boiling at 100°C for 1 h. In fresh mouse serum, Og-CATH underwent gradual degradation, with 71.7% and 42.6% of the peptide remaining after 2 and 4 h of incubation, respectively. Together, these findings identify Og-CATH as a novel β-sheet cathelicidin with favorable stability across physiologically relevant and stress-exposure conditions.

Og-CATH lacks direct antimicrobial activity but protects O. grahami and mice against bacterial infection

Cathelicidins have been extensively investigated due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activities (Izadpanah & Gallo, 2005). To determine whether Og-CATH directly inhibits microbial growth, its in vitro antimicrobial activity was first evaluated by minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays. Og-CATH showed no detectable inhibitory activity at 200 µg/mL against 17 tested microbial strains, including standard and drug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria, and fungi (Supplementary Table S2). Consistent with this result, 200 μg/mL Og-CATH did not reduce CFU counts or metabolic activity of S. aureus, E. coli, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), or carbapenem-resistant E. coli (CREC) after 1, 2, 3, or 4 h of incubation (Figure 1A, B). Bacterial morphology also remained unchanged after exposure of S. aureus and E. coli to Og-CATH (200 µg/mL) for 1 h (Figure 1C). In contrast, LL-37, the sole human cathelicidin and used here as a positive control at 2×MIC (Nagaoka et al., 2020), markedly reduced bacterial CFU counts and metabolic activity and caused visible disruption of bacterial surface morphology (Figure 1A–C).

The in vivo anti-infective efficacy of Og-CATH was then examined in O. grahami and mouse bacterial infection models. Frogs weighing 21–30 g (n=4) and male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g (n=4) received Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection either 4 h before (−4 h) or 4 h after (+4 h) inoculation with S. aureus or E. coli. Bacterial burdens in peritoneal lavage fluid were quantified 24 h after challenge. Despite lacking direct antimicrobial activity in vitro, Og-CATH substantially reduced bacterial CFU counts in both frog and mouse infection models (Figure 1D).

Og-CATH protects against CLP-induced sepsis and sepsis-associated multiple organ injury in mice

Before investigating the mechanisms underlying the in vivo anti-infective activity of Og-CATH, we assessed its toxicity and immunogenicity both in vitro and in vivo. Og-CATH showed no detectable cytotoxicity or immunogenicity under the tested conditions, including concentrations up to 200 μg/mL in vitro (Supplementary Figure S2). Because CLP induces polymicrobial peritonitis that closely models severe bacterial sepsis, organ dysfunction, and lethality (Dejager et al., 2011), the protective efficacy of Og-CATH was then examined in CLP-induced septic mice (Figure 2A). Intraperitoneal administration of Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) either 4 h before or 4 h after CLP markedly enhanced survival in septic mice, with efficacy comparable to that of meropenem, used as a positive control at 10 mg/kg (Figure 2B). Og-CATH or meropenem treatment also significantly reduced the bacterial loads and decreased concentrations of proinflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, in kidney, liver, lung, and serum samples compared with PBS treatment (Figure 2C–F; Supplementary Figure S3). Histological analysis showed severe tissue injury in septic mice, characterized by hemorrhage, extensive edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration in kidney, liver, and lung tissues. These pathological changes were substantially alleviated by Og-CATH or meropenem treatment (Figure 2G). CLP also increased the lung wet-to-dry weight ratio and elevated serum markers of liver and kidney injury, including ALT and creatinine, relative to sham-operated mice (Figure 2H–J). Og-CATH and meropenem effectively reduced these injury indices. In contrast, sOg-CATH, the scrambled control peptide, did not confer significant protection in these assays.

To determine whether the protective activity of Og-CATH was influenced by administration route, prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy were further evaluated after intravenous delivery. Intravenous administration of Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) 4 h before or 4 h after CLP also enhanced survival in septic mice (Supplementary Figure S4), indicating that systemic delivery preserved anti-sepsis efficacy. This finding is consistent with the serum stability data, which demonstrated that Og-CATH retained at least 40% of its biological activity after 4 h of incubation in fresh mouse serum.

Og-CATH recruits phagocytes in frogs and mice, and protection in septic mice requires monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils

Effective pathogen clearance after infection depends on rapid recruitment of phagocytes to infected tissues (Bhuiyan et al., 2016). Results showed that Og-CATH administration (10 mg/kg) markedly increased leukocyte influx into the abdominal cavity of O. grahami frogs (Figure 3A, B). In mice, this response was dominated by neutrophils and inflammatory monocytes/macrophages, with recruitment peaking at 12 and 24 h post-injection (Figure 3C, D). In contrast, neither the scrambled control peptide sOg-CATH nor meropenem induced appreciable neutrophil or macrophage recruitment (Figure 3E).

To determine whether these phagocyte populations were required for Og-CATH-mediated protection, the in vivo anti-infective activity of Og-CATH was assessed in mice depleted of monocytes/macrophages or neutrophils using clodronate liposomes or cyclophosphamide, respectively. Results showed that depletion markedly reduced Og-CATH-induced recruitment of monocytes/macrophages (Figure 3F) or neutrophils (Figure 3G) into the abdominal cavity of mice. Functionally, Og-CATH administered intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg either 4 h before or 4 h after CLP failed to decrease bacterial burdens in peripheral blood from monocyte/macrophage-depleted or neutrophil-depleted septic mice (Figure 3H, I).

Og-CATH promotes phagocyte migration through P2X7 receptor-dependent chemokine release from macrophages

Given that Og-CATH demonstrated chemotactic effects on neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages in frogs and mice, its capacity to act as a direct chemoattractant was first examined. In Transwell assays, Og-CATH at 100 μg/mL did not induce migration of isolated macrophages or neutrophils, indicating that Og-CATH does not directly attract these phagocytes (Supplementary Figure S5). In contrast, when macrophages were included in the lower chamber, Og-CATH triggered dose-dependent migration of both macrophages and neutrophils (Figure 4A, B), indicating that Og-CATH promotes phagocyte migration indirectly through macrophage-dependent signals.

Macrophages can recruit immune cells to sites of infection by releasing chemokines. To determine whether Og-CATH-induced phagocyte migration depended on macrophage chemokine production, macrophages were treated with Og-CATH (100 μg/mL), sOg-CATH (100 µg/mL), or meropenem (1 mg/mL) for 6 h, followed by measurement of chemokine mRNA and protein expression. Og-CATH markedly increased macrophage expression of CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, and CCL2 by 40.0-, 19.0-, 18.0-, and 9.0-fold, respectively, compared with PBS treatment. Other chemokines, including CXCL5, CXCL7, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL14, CCL1, CCL3, CCL4, and CCL6, showed more modest increases of 1.0- to 5.0-fold (Figure 4C). Consistent with these transcriptional changes, Og-CATH markedly increased CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, and CCL2 protein levels in macrophage culture supernatants (Figure 4D). Intraperitoneal administration of Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) similarly increased CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, and CCL2 levels in mouse peritoneal lavage fluid (Figure 4E). A comparable response was observed in THP-1 cells, in which Og-CATH enhanced secretion of CXCL1, CXCL2, and CCL2 (Supplementary Figure S6). In contrast, neither sOg-CATH nor meropenem significantly increased these chemokines under the same conditions (Figure 4C).

To identify the receptor pathway responsible for Og-CATH-induced chemokine production, macrophages were pretreated with inhibitors or blocking reagents targeting chemotaxis-related receptors, including the P2X7 receptor, formyl peptide receptor-like 1, and TLR4/MD2. Pretreatment with the P2X7 receptor inhibitor KN-62 markedly suppressed Og-CATH-induced production of CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, and CCL2 in macrophages. In contrast, the formyl peptide receptor-like 1 antagonist WRW4 and the anti-mouse TLR4/MD2 antibody MTS510 had no significant effect (Figure 4F).

Og-CATH enhances neutrophil phagocytosis and degranulation

Neutrophils eliminate invading microbes through coordinated effector mechanisms, including phagocytosis, production of ROS and NO, formation of NETs, and release of granule-derived antimicrobial proteins (Andrés et al., 2022; Papayannopoulos, 2018). Og-CATH markedly increased neutrophil uptake of fluorescently labeled S. aureus and E. coli compared with PBS or sOg-CATH treatment (Figure 5A, B), indicating enhanced phagocytic activity. The effects of Og-CATH on oxidative and non-oxidative antimicrobial pathways were then examined. Og-CATH did not significantly alter ROS production, NO generation, or NET formation (Figure 5C–E). In contrast, Og-CATH dose-dependently promoted the release of the granule-associated antimicrobial proteins BPI and lactoferrin from neutrophils compared with sOg-CATH treatment (Figure 5F).

Og-CATH reduces thrombus formation without detectable bleeding liability in mice

Sepsis involves not only uncontrolled bacterial expansion and impaired phagocyte function, but also profound disruption of coagulation and fibrinolytic pathways within the circulation and alveolar compartment (Bastarache et al., 2006). Given the protective efficacy of Og-CATH in septic mice, its impact on sepsis-associated pulmonary fibrin deposition was examined. Extensive fibrin accumulation was detected in the lungs 24 h after CLP, whereas Og-CATH administration (10 mg/kg) before or after CLP markedly reduced pulmonary fibrin deposition (Figure 6A).

The antithrombotic activity of Og-CATH was further evaluated in FeCl3-induced carotid artery thrombosis and carrageenan-induced tail thrombosis models. In untreated mice, carotid blood flow was completely occluded within 10 min after application of 10% FeCl3. Conversely, intraperitoneal injection of heparin or Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) significantly delayed vascular occlusion, extending occlusion time to 23±2 and 28.5±1.5 min, respectively (Figure 6B, C). Consistent with these findings, heparin (1 000 U/kg) and Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) also reduced carrageenan-induced tail thrombus length (Figure 6D). In contrast, sOg-CATH and meropenem did not significantly affect carotid artery occlusion or tail thrombus formation (Figure 6B–D).

Potential bleeding liability was assessed using tail transection and skin bleeding models. Results showed that Og-CATH at 10 or 30 mg/kg did not prolong tail bleeding time (Figure 6E). Similarly, Og-CATH at 30 mg/kg did not increase visible skin bleeding (Figure 6F, G). In contrast, heparin (1000 U/kg) increased visible skin bleeding and markedly prolonged tail bleeding time (Figure 6E–G).

Og-CATH restores endothelial hemostatic balance by suppressing TF and enhancing the APC pathway

Platelets and coagulation factors contribute substantially to sepsis-associated coagulopathy, and their activation can promote fibrin formation through multiple pathways (Levi & van der Poll, 2017). To determine whether Og-CATH directly interferes with plasma coagulation or platelet activation, recalcification time, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and platelet aggregation were examined in vitro. Results indicated that Og-CATH (100 μg/mL) did not prolong plasma recalcification time, PT, or aPTT (Supplementary Figure S7A, B), nor did it inhibit platelet aggregation induced by ADP, collagen, or PMA (Supplementary Figure S7C).

Endothelial cells normally maintain an anticoagulant vascular surface by limiting thrombin generation and supporting endogenous anticoagulant pathways. During sepsis, however, endothelial dysfunction shifts this balance toward a prothrombotic state, contributing to thrombosis and organ failure (Pilard et al., 2022). The effect of Og-CATH on endothelial hemostatic regulation was therefore examined in LPS-injured HUVECs. LPS (10 μg/mL) induced endothelial injury, as indicated by a 30.85% reduction in cell viability (Supplementary Figure S8). Western blot analysis showed that LPS stimulation for 24 h increased TF, a key procoagulant initiator, and markedly reduced APC levels in HUVECs compared with control cells (Figure 7A, B). Og-CATH treatment significantly suppressed LPS-induced TF production and restored APC levels, whereas sOg-CATH and meropenem showed no comparable effect (Supplementary Figure S9).

Because EPCR and TM are central components of the endothelial anticoagulant system that support APC generation (Nan et al., 2005; Van de Wouwer et al., 2004), their regulation by Og-CATH was further examined. Og-CATH treatment markedly upregulated TM and EPCR protein expression compared with PBS treatment (Figure 7A, B). Similar effects were observed in lung tissues from septic mice, in which intraperitoneal administration of Og-CATH (10 mg/kg) either before or after CLP significantly reduced TF levels and increased APC, TM, and EPCR expression (Figure 7C, D). In contrast, Og-CATH did not significantly alter expression of uPA or PAI-1 (Figure 7E), indicating that its hemostatic effects are not mediated by direct modulation of the fibrinolytic system.

Og-CATH preserves endothelial barrier integrity in vitro and in vivo

Beyond impaired anticoagulant activity, sepsis-associated endothelial dysfunction is characterized by inflammatory activation, endothelial apoptosis, and disruption of intercellular junction proteins that maintain vascular barrier integrity (Giannotta et al., 2013; Kuo et al., 2022). The effect of Og-CATH on endothelial barrier protection was therefore examined in LPS-stimulated HUVECs. Results indicated that Og-CATH dose-dependently suppressed LPS-induced production of the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Figure 8A). Flow cytometric analysis further showed that LPS increased endothelial apoptosis to 19.4%, whereas Og-CATH reduced LPS-induced apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 8B). LPS exposure also decreased the levels of junction-associated proteins ZO-1, occludin, and E-cadherin in HUVECs compared with control cells (Figure 8C), whereas Og-CATH treatment significantly reversed these reductions. Consistent with the in vitro findings, Og-CATH administration (10 mg/kg) 4 h before or 4 h after CLP increased ZO-1, occludin, and E-cadherin levels in lung tissues from septic mice (Figure 8D).

Og-CATH directly targets MD2 and suppresses endothelial TLR4 signaling

LPS initiates inflammatory signaling primarily through TLR4, whereas MD2 functions as the ligand-recognition co-receptor of TLR4 required for LPS sensing. To define the molecular target of Og-CATH, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was first performed to examine direct interactions with LPS, TLR4, or MD2. Results indicated that Og-CATH bound MD2 with a KD of 1.23 μmol/L but showed no detectable direct interaction with LPS or TLR4 (Figure 9A). Independent ITC analysis confirmed Og-CATH-MD2 binding, with a KD of 0.142 μmol/L (Figure 9B). Although the binding affinities determined by SPR were slightly different from the ITC assay results, this is not surprising considering that the underlying principles of these two methods are different: SPR reflects the kinetics of binding and dissociation, whereas ITC directly measures the thermodynamics of the binding process. Molecular docking further supported a favorable interaction between Og-CATH and MD2 (Figure 9C). CETSA analysis provided additional evidence for target engagement in HUVECs, showing that Og-CATH increased MD2 thermal stability and shifted the MD2 melting temperature by 11.4°C (Figure 9D).

Given that Og-CATH significantly inhibited LPS-induced inflammatory responses in HUVECs, its effect on downstream TLR4 signaling was next examined. Co-IP analysis showed that Og-CATH inhibited LPS-induced recruitment of MyD88 to TLR4 and reduced formation of the TLR4/MD2/MyD88 signaling complex (Figure 9E, F). Consistently, LPS markedly increased phosphorylation of IKKα/β, p65, and IκBα, whereas Og-CATH suppressed phosphorylation of these TLR4 pathway components (Figure 9G, H). Because immune cells can mount strong LPS responses and contribute to endothelial injury through cytokine release, the effect of Og-CATH on the expression of inflammatory cytokines in LPS-induced mouse peritoneal macrophages and human THP-1 cells was also examined. Results showed that Og-CATH significantly reduced TNF-α production in both cell types (Supplementary Figure S10), suggesting that it may also attenuate endothelial damage indirectly by limiting immune cell-derived inflammatory signals. However, LPS also binds with endothelial cells and upregulates adhesion molecules and tissue factor in a MyD88-dependent fashion, which are critical for coagulation and vasodilation. Our results showed that Og-CATH does not act directly on LPS or its receptor TLR4, indicating that it can directly target endothelial cells to modulate their functional state, rather than merely providing indirect protection by reducing cytokine production from immune cells.

DISCUSSION

Despite major advances in our understanding of sepsis pathophysiology, clinical translation of new therapeutic strategies has remained limited, and many candidate treatments have failed in clinical trials (Gauer et al., 2020). This persistent therapeutic gap underscores the urgent need for anti-sepsis agents that protect the host through mechanisms beyond conventional bacterial killing. In this study, a previously uncharacterized cathelicidin host defense peptide, Og-CATH, was derived from the skin of O. grahami. Analysis identified Og-CATH as a short 28-amino acid cationic peptide with no detectable direct antimicrobial activity in vitro. Nevertheless, intraperitoneal administration of Og-CATH before or after bacterial challenge improved survival in mice subjected to lethal bacterial infection or CLP. In the CLP model of polymicrobial intra-abdominal sepsis, Og-CATH also reduced systemic inflammation and alleviated sepsis-associated injury in the lung, liver, and kidney. In contrast, the scrambled control peptide sOg-CATH failed to provide significant survival benefit, indicating that the anti-infective activity of Og-CATH depends on its specific peptide sequence.

Clinical and experimental studies have increasingly shown that prolonged immunosuppression after sepsis can increase susceptibility to secondary infection and death, shifting therapeutic interest from broad immunosuppression toward strategies that restore protective host immunity. Immunostimulatory approaches have shown encouraging effects in animal models and preclinical studies. Neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages are principal phagocytic effectors of innate immunity, and impaired activation or depletion of these cell populations is associated with increased mortality in sepsis (Zhang et al., 2023). In the present study, Og-CATH promoted leukocyte influx in O. grahami and induced neutrophil and monocyte/macrophage recruitment in mice. Depletion experiments further showed that monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils were required for Og-CATH-mediated protection, as its anti-infective efficacy was markedly reduced in macrophage-depleted and neutrophil-depleted septic mice. These findings indicate that Og-CATH acts primarily by strengthening host effector responses rather than by directly killing bacteria. This mechanism differs from that of bactericidal cationic immunomodulatory peptides, such as LL-37, which can directly recruit phagocytes to sites of infection (Tjabringa et al., 2006). Og-CATH did not act as a direct chemoattractant. Instead, it promoted neutrophil and monocyte/macrophage migration by stimulating P2X7 receptor-dependent release of CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, and CCL2 from macrophages. Neutrophils are professional phagocytes that eliminate microbes at infected sites (Herro & Grimes, 2024). Oxygen-dependent antimicrobial mechanisms, including NADPH oxidase-driven ROS production and NET formation, have been considered major pathways by which cationic host defense peptides combat invading pathogens (He et al., 2023, 2024; Shi et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). However, Og-CATH did not significantly alter oxidative neutrophil killing pathways. Instead, it enhanced neutrophil phagocytosis and promoted degranulation, leading to the release of the endogenous antimicrobial proteins BPI and lactoferrin, suggesting that Og-CATH indirectly facilitates bacterial clearance by mobilizing phagocytes and enhancing oxygen-independent neutrophil effector functions.

Although bacterial clearance is essential for improving sepsis outcomes, antibiotics alone often fail to resolve this syndrome because sepsis involves systemic inflammation, vascular injury, coagulation imbalance, and multiorgan dysfunction. Endothelial cells are central to this pathophysiology, as they regulate hemostatic balance and preserve barrier integrity across organs and tissues (Joffre et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023). However, most studies of cationic host defense peptides have focused on innate immune modulation and inflammatory control (Cai et al., 2018; Li et al., 2013; Nagaoka et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021), whereas their effects on the vascular endothelium during sepsis remain less well defined. In the present study, intraperitoneal administration of Og-CATH before or after septic challenge markedly reduced pulmonary fibrin deposition, indicating that this peptide can attenuate sepsis-associated hematological abnormalities. Its antithrombotic activity was further supported in FeCl3-induced carotid artery thrombosis and carrageenan-induced tail thrombosis models. Og-CATH did not prolong tail bleeding or increase visible skin bleeding, suggesting antithrombotic efficacy without detectable bleeding liability in the models tested. This profile is notable because bleeding risk remains a major limitation of anticoagulant therapy in vivo.

Several host defense peptides have been reported to suppress coagulation through inhibition of serine proteases or platelet aggregation (Chai et al., 2021; He et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2021). In contrast, Og-CATH did not prolong plasma recalcification time, PT, or aPTT and did not inhibit platelet aggregation in vitro. Instead, Og-CATH acted at the endothelial level. In LPS-stimulated HUVECs, Og-CATH effectively inhibited expression of the procoagulant initiator TF and increased expression of anticoagulant APC by upregulating TM and EPCR, two key components required for endothelial protein C pathway activation. Similar regulation was observed in lung tissues from septic mice. These findings indicate that Og-CATH rebalances sepsis-associated hemostatic dysfunction primarily by suppressing endothelial TF expression and enhancing endothelial APC production rather than by directly inhibiting plasma coagulation or platelet activation. Og-CATH also protected endothelial barrier function by inhibiting LPS-stimulated endothelial apoptosis and proinflammatory cytokine release, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, while restoring the barrier-associated proteins ZO-1, occludin, and E-cadherin.

The molecular target and endothelial signaling pathway regulated by Og-CATH were further investigated. Molecular docking, SPR, ITC, and CETSA analyses showed that Og-CATH selectively bound MD2, the TLR4 accessory protein required for LPS recognition, with strong affinity. In contrast, SPR analysis showed no detectable direct binding of Og-CATH to LPS or TLR4, indicating that MD2 is the most likely proximal target. During LPS sensing, CD14 facilitates LPS presentation to the TLR4-MD2 complex, promoting TLR4 dimerization and recruitment of adaptor proteins such as MyD88 to the cytoplasmic domain of TLR4, thereby initiating downstream inflammatory signaling. In this study, co-immunoprecipitation experiments in HUVECs confirmed that Og-CATH inhibited LPS-induced MyD88 recruitment to TLR4 and reduced formation of the TLR4/MD2/MyD88 signaling complex. Consistently, Og-CATH suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of IKKα/β, IκBα, and NF-κB p65, indicating inhibition of the endothelial TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB axis. Previous studies have shown that the human cathelicidin peptide LL-37 can act on endothelial cells to promote proliferation, preserve barrier permeability, and attenuate lethal sepsis, endotoxin shock, or liver injury; however, the intracellular targets and endothelial mechanisms underlying these effects remain incompletely defined (Suzuki et al., 2022; Zhai et al., 2020). The present findings identified MD2 as a direct target of Og-CATH and showed that MD2 engagement inhibited downstream TLR4 signaling, thereby reducing endothelial injury during sepsis. Thus, Og-CATH not only enhanced innate immune cell-mediated antimicrobial immunity but also alleviated endothelial injury during sepsis by targeting the MD2 protein. The comparison with meropenem further supported this mechanistic distinction. Although meropenem significantly reduced the bacterial burden in septic mice, it did not significantly regulate phagocyte function, endothelial function, carotid artery thrombosis, or tail thrombosis. Under the same conditions, Og-CATH prolonged vascular occlusion time and increased endothelial APC expression, indicating that MD2 targeting by Og-CATH promotes a shift in the endothelium from a prothrombotic state toward an antithrombotic state. These results demonstrate that the antithrombotic activity of Og-CATH is not merely a secondary consequence of reduced bacterial growth but reflects direct regulation of endothelial hemostatic balance. In recent years, non-antibiotic agents, including immunomodulatory host defense peptides, have gained attention as adjunctive approaches against drug-resistant infection, and their combination with antibiotics represents a promising strategy for multidrug-resistant bacteria. The results of the primary analysis of the study imply that Og-CATH may be a potentially promising candidate molecule that could be used in combination with antibiotics to combat microbial infections, which will be the focus of our future research.

In conclusion, this study identified Og-CATH as a novel cathelicidin AMP from the skin of O. grahami. Although Og-CATH lacked direct antimicrobial activity, it exerted both prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy against bacterial sepsis in mice. Beyond enhancement of innate antimicrobial immunity, Og-CATH also preserved endothelial barrier integrity and restored anticoagulant function by targeting MD2 and inhibiting TLR4 signaling, thereby reducing sepsis-associated hematological dysfunction and organ injury. Together with favorable serum and salt stability and low cytotoxicity, these properties support Og-CATH as a promising candidate for further development as an anti-sepsis therapeutic.
