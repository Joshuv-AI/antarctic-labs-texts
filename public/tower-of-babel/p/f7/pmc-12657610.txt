Oncoplastic surgery for cutaneous head and neck cancers: A novel technique combining wide excision, local flap reconstruction and sentinel node biopsy
Authors: Caroline A. Gjorup, Michael Chae, Terence Wong, David J. Coker, Thomas E. Pennington
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and purposesSentinel node biopsy (SNB) procedures in the head and neck (H&N) region can be technically demanding due to anatomic and size constraints. Often, node procurement is attempted through small incisions, making identification of crucial anatomy difficult. Given the primary site may be close to the sentinel nodes (SN), a local flap can be designed for the dual purpose of gaining access to the sentinel node field through raising the flap while facilitating repair of the primary site resulting in superior aesthetic outcomes. We detail our technique hereof.Subjects studied and methodsA review of consecutive patients undergoing surgery for cutaneous head and neck malignancies between July 2021 and December 2023 was undertaken. Patients, who had undergone a local flap reconstruction in the H&N region, through which the SNs were procured, were included in the study.Main findingsA total of 20 patients were identified, of whom eighteen had melanoma and two had Merkel cell carcinoma, located on the scalp, face and neck in which lymphoscintigraphy (LSG) identified unilateral drainage, with a median of three SNs (range 1–5), at a median of two levels (range 1–3) in the neck per patient. A median excision margin of 1.5 cm (range 1–2 cm) and a local flap alone was used in 19 (95 %) patients for SN procurement.ConclusionsThis novel technique of procuring the SNs through the local flap incisions offers an excellent anatomical visualization and safe procurement of SNs with low complication rates while offering good aesthetic reconstructive outcomes.

Introduction

Sentinel node biopsy (SNB) in the head & neck (H&N) region is routinely performed for intermediate and high-risk cutaneous melanoma, Merkel cell carcinomas (MCC) and occasionally, high-risk cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (SCC).1, 2, 3 SNB procedures in this region can be technically demanding due to the location of the lymph nodes and their small size. The average number of SNs and involvement of multiple node fields in the head and neck is higher than any other regional node field, and the discrepancy between mapped and procured SNs is also higher for H&N than any other region.4 Head and neck melanoma has a poorer prognosis than melanoma elsewhere, even when adjusting for Breslow thickness, age of the patient, mitotic rate and presence of ulceration.5

Technical factors contribute to a higher rate of false-negative sentinel node biopsies in the head and neck, with operator experience, anatomical constraints and morbidity risk-aversion contributing to this. Often, node procurement is attempted through small incisions, making identification of crucial anatomy more difficult. Sufficient access to the SNs in the H&N region is therefore of importance as a staging procedure and for a therapeutic benefit in regional disease control. Furthermore, reconstruction post-wide excision often requires tissue replacement rather than simple primary closure. Skin grafting can be a simple and safe option, but often creates an inferior aesthetic result in comparison to a well-designed and executed local flap reconstruction. Given the primary site is often anatomically close to the identified sentinel nodes, the opportunity exists to design the local flap repair such as to allow access to the sentinel node field in raising the flap. Such a technique not only provides excellent anatomic visualization and access to the sentinel node but also aligns with reconstructive principles, resulting in superior aesthetic otucomes.

In this paper, the senior authors’ experience with utilizing the incisions and raising of the local flap to obtain access to the SNs is reviewed.

Method

Patients

Patients with primary cutaneous H&N melanoma and MCC who underwent SNB between July 2021 and December 2023 by the senior authors (T.E.P. and D.J.C) at Melanoma Institute Australia (MIA), Sydney, Australia. The study was approved by the MIA Research Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Wide excision

Wide excisions were performed with 1 to 2 cm margins depending on the characteristics of the primary tumor or randomized arm of a clinical trial (MELMART II) if enrolled in this.6

Lymphoscintigraphy and SNB

A SNB was offered to patients according to the clinical practice guidelines developed by Cancer Council Australia recommending consideration of SNB for all patients with melanoma greater than 1 mm in Breslow tumor thickness, and for patients with melanoma greater than 0.8 mm with other high risk pathological features.7 All patients with MCC were offered SNB.

Details of the lymphatic mapping and SNB techniques used at MIA have been described previously.8 In short, using 20–30 MBq activity of 99m Tc-labelled antimony sulphide colloid preoperative dynamic and static lymphoscintigraphy, single photon emission computed tomography with integrated computerized tomography (SPECT/CT) and ultrasound was performed. If the primary site is near the regional node basin, fewer injections of radiotracer are given. The location of each SN was marked on the skin with an indelible marker pen and a pin-point permanent tattoo. Intradermal injections of Patent Blue V 2.5 % dye were given around the primary excision-biopsy site, from 0.2 ml to 0.8 ml with less volume used if the primary tumor site was near the SNs. The blue dye was injected 10 to 15 min prior to the first incision. Dual modality localization was utilized for SN detection and resection, using a hand-held gamma probe (NeoProbe®) in conjunction with the Patent Blue mapping technique. Sentinel nodes were serially sectioned and were examined using S100, Melan A and HMB-45 immunohistochemistry according to established standardized protocols.9

Local flap design

Based on the wide excision marking and the markings on the skin of the SNs, the local flap was designed with the incisions of the flap or the flap itself overlying the SNs. In the scalp, rotation flaps were designed to incorporate access to the occipital SNs as shown in Figures 1, 4. In the temple and cheeks, mobilizing inferolateral tissue, as in e.g., cervicofacial or hatchet flap, provided access to preauricular, intraparotid, and level II-III SNs as shown in Figures 2, 7, 8. These flaps were raised just above the superficial fasciae of the face and neck (parotid fascia, SMAS, platysma). Statistical

Median and ranges were used to describe continuous descriptive variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

A median excision margin of 1.5 cm (range 1–2 cm) and a local flap alone was used in 19 (95 %) patients with one patient requiring an additional incision for further SN procurement.

Rotation, hatchet and cervicofacial flaps covered 90 % of reconstructions and SN procurements with only one case requiring a further incision to procure a SN outside the reach of the flap as shown in Fig. 4.

A median of 2.5 (range 1–5) SNs and a median of 3 (range 1–6) total nodes (SNs + non-SNs) were retrieved. There was agreement between number of SNs on LSG and SNs retrieved in 15 (75 %) patients. Sentinel node positivity rate was 15 % (3/20).

Postoperative complications were seen in two patients (10 %): one patient developed a hypertrophic scar and one patient developed a clinically significant lymphocoele requiring drainage due to concurrent infection. No patients developed major complications (Clavien Dindo ≥3).

Median follow up was 7 months (range 0–28) at which time, all were recurrence-free. However, two patients underwent therapeutic lymph node dissection (TLND) due to regional recurrence.

Discussion

Approximately one in four cutaneous melanomas, one in three MCC and more than one in two cutaneous SCC are located in the H&N.2,10,11 A significant proportion will be offered SNB to improve staging accuracy, improve regional disease control and provide prognostic information. While providing optimal access for SN procurement through a local flap, the local flap reconstruction also restores skin contour, texture and color, thus balancing oncological and aesthetic factors.

In our series, two patients experienced isolated regional lymph node recurrence after six and eleven months respectively, requiring a TLND. These two patients had one and two SNs mapped, respectively. Both had one SN procured, which was without sign of metastasis. This false negative rate, defined as a recurrence in a patient with a negative SNB, of 10 % within a median follow-up of eight months is comparable to the literature with the false-negative rate in H&N SNB higher than SNB performed elsewhere on the body. False negative SNs, can occur because of deficiencies in nuclear medicine, surgery, or pathology.12

In a systematic review by de Rosa et al. the median false-negative rate within 31 months was 20 % (range 3 %–44 %).13 This is associated with the increased complexity of H&N SNB compared to SNB at other sites because of the anatomical proximity of the primary site to the SN in addition to more complex lymphatic drainage patterns in the H&N.13

Utilizing the local flap incisions for procurement of the SNs, which are often found close to branches of the facial nerve, the spinal accessory nerve (SAN), branches of the cervical plexus and major blood vessels including the internal jugular vein and carotid artery, also allows for good exposure to avoid damage to these structures as shown in Fig. 6. No patient in our series encountered nerve palsy. The facial nerve branches most often at risk of transient or permanent damage during head and neck cutaneous surgery is the temporal and marginal mandibular branches.14 In-depth knowledge of the course of the nerves, use of nerve stimulators when indicated, and good exposure of the surgical field is crucial to avoid iatrogenic neurovascular injury. The local flaps in our series were all raised superficial to the facial nerve branches, and utmost care was taken in the neck where the great auricular nerve and SAN are at risk when raising the flap. Once the flaps are raised, the exposure of the SNs and surrounding structures allow for careful dissection of the SNs.

The mean number of SNs harvested from patients with H&N melanoma is higher compared to other sites.1,4 In a study by Parrett et al. assessing SNB procedures in 365 patients, the authors found a mean of 3.7 nodes removed from 1.6 nodal basins per patient.15 In a study by de Wilt et al. analyzing the lymphatic drainage and SNB procures in patients, with melanoma of the H&N, the average number of SNs was 2.5.4 Our exact same finding regarding the number of SNs as our predecessors, is likely attributed to the same techniques utilized then and now with regard to both LSG (2- and 3D, standard + SPECT/CT), SNB and histopathological examination.

Discrepancies between mapped and resected nodes are common. In a study by de Wilt et al., 30 % of patients had less SN removed than identified as such on the LSG.4 In our study, 10 % and 15 % had more and less SNs resected, respectively, than identified on LSG. With the anatomical constraint, and limitations in distinguishing the radioactivity from the SNs and the primary injection site in the H&N region, this is to be expected. Optimizing the access to the SNs while simultaneously reconstructing the defect may increase the correlation between the SNs identified at LSG and at operation.

Two patients in our series relapsed in the neck and required a TLND. In the era before the MSLT-II and DeCog trials, a positive SN led to a completion lymph node dissection (CLND).16,17 In those times, limiting the incisions on the neck were sought to not compromise the vascularity of the flaps when performing a CLND, typically within a few weeks of the SNB procedure. Nowadays, a positive SN will not necessitate an immediate CLND due to the availability of effective adjuvant therapies. Instead, a TLND is reserved for when isolated nodal recurrence occurs. In a study by Gjorup et al. of SN positive patients, isolated nodal recurrence, in the SN field, was detected in only 5 % within two years.18 These patients may be considered for neoadjuvant immunotherapy, delaying surgery for between 2 and 3 months, allowing for 2–3 doses of immunotherapy.19 The disruption of the blood supply by local flaps in the neck is therefore less of a concern. The local flaps can, however, be designed with a possible later TLND in mind with linear incision lines preferable. This can be achieved with rotation flaps which are preferable to e.g. Keystone or transposition flaps on the neck. Even so, the incisions for a neck dissection are generally based on broad-based highly vascularized flaps. We encountered no flap necrosis, neither of the local flap used for the SNB or in the neck dissection flaps performed 6 and 11 months after the SNB.

In our series, rotation, hatchet and cervicofacial flaps were used in 18 patients (90 %).20 These flaps, which are all simple to mark overlying the SNs, easy to perform, rapid and safe to raise are also compatible with neck dissection and may be safer than e.g. transposition flaps when considering the potential for formal neck dissection in the future.

In our series, the complication rate was low, with two seromas, one of which required drainage, and one hypertrophic scar. There were no cases with flap necrosis, and no motor nerve palsies. The aesthetic outcome of flap reconstruction versus skin grafts, is superior regarding color and contour. In our series, one patient required steroid injection for a hypertrophic scar with none having required scar revision.

One disadvantage of local flap is the potential change of sensibility, yet most patients recover normal or near-normal sensation over time.

Limitations to this study include its retrospective design, relatively small number of patients, and the short length of follow-up. To fully assess oncologic outcomes, a prospective study with a larger sample size and longer follow up duration would be needed. A limitation of this oncoplastic technique is that, unlike SNB incisions directly overlying the sentinel node, local flap incisions do not pinpoint the exact site of the SNB. This distinction can be relevant for monitoring a SN positive lymph node basin. The precise location of the SN is, however, documented by the lymphoscintigraphy and in the medical notes, and in cases of a positive SN, the entire lymph node basin becomes crucial when evaluating for potential further regional metastases. Despite the small number of cases, employing our oncoplastic approach may lower the rate of false negative node procurement and iatrogenic nerve injury while providing an aesthetically pleasing reconstruction. The strength of this study includes sites of primary tumors throughout the H&N region, and various types of local flaps, showcasing the versatility of combining local flap reconstruction and procurement of SNs. It is acknowledged that not all patients with H&N cutaneous malignancies undergoing wide excision and SNB are candidates for this novel technique. The indication for combining wide excision, local flap reconstruction and SN procurement through the flap incisions are defects requiring local flap reconstruction where the flap can be planned in close proximity to the SN. This allows for safer access to the SN while the oncoplastic approach also may provide superior aesthetic outcomes.
