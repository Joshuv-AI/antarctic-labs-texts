Positive and Negative Cardiovascular Effects of JAK Inhibitors in Inflammation
Authors: Aliki Zavoriti, Pierre Miossec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Chronic inflammation, characteristic of many autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), or myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), is an independent driver of accelerated cardiovascular (CV) risk, primarily through proinflammatory cytokines that induce atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction. Although biologic therapies (tumor necrosis factor [TNF], interleukin [IL]‐6, or IL‐1 inhibitors) improve both clinical manifestations and CV outcomes, JAK inhibitors (JAKi) present a paradox. JAKi provide potent systemic inflammation control, sometimes superior to biologic therapies, this effect does not translate into a proportional CV risk reduction in all patients. Instead, JAKi have been linked to increased adverse CV events in high‐risk patient populations with RA, resulting in a Food and Drug Administration safety warning. Conversely, JAKi show therapeutic benefit in patients with MPNs. In MPNs, JAKi specifically target the JAK2 mutation, reducing blood cell proliferation and the associated prothrombotic state. This review compares the distinct biologic mechanisms underlying this JAKi CV paradox, contrasting the JAK2 mutation‐driven pathology in MPNs with the widespread cytokine‐driven alterations in inflammatory diseases. In the latter, the observed CV risk might stem from failure of JAKi to fully inhibit prothrombotic pathways induced by cytokines (TNF and IL‐17) and the potential dose‐related vascular toxicity. Understanding these binary effects will provide new insights into the divergent CV impact of JAKi.

Introduction

Any chronic inflammation, either obvious as in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and certain cancers, or subclinical as in aging, is linked to accelerated atherosclerosis and excess cardiovascular (CV) risk. 1 , 2 , 3 Chronic inflammation in these diseases is a direct and independent contributor to CV events (CVEs). The inflammatory process is initiated and maintained by the release of proinflammatory cytokines from damaged tissues, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)‐1, IL‐6, IL‐17, and acute‐phase proteins, notably C‐reactive protein (CRP), which all affect the CV system. 1 , 2 , 3 They promote vascular inflammation and endothelial cell (EC) dysfunction, lead to an imbalance in procoagulant versus anticoagulant activities, and make platelets more prone to aggregation thus favoring a prothrombotic state. 4 , 5 Therefore, targeted anti‐inflammatory therapies could be crucial to prevent or mitigate the progression of CVEs.

Anticytokine therapies, often with monoclonal antibodies (eg, TNF, IL‐6, or IL‐1 inhibitors) have shown promise in improving not only symptoms but also CV outcomes in various inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 More recently, JAK inhibitors (JAKi), targeted oral drugs that block the signaling of inflammatory cytokines (eg, IL‐6, IL‐23, and interferon‐γ [IFNγ] but not TNF or IL‐17), have demonstrated remarkable efficacy at suppressing inflammation, with some trial showing superiority over specific biologic agents. 10 , 11 However, and in contrast to biologic therapies, the CV risk remains elevated with JAKi even after controlling disease activity, and the highest doses are associated with increased incidence of thromboembolism, particularly in patients with high CV risk factors. 12 This has led the Food and Drug Administration to issue a boxed safety warning for all JAKi. Conversely, some JAKi used to treat myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), reduce the CV risk, suggesting that JAKi affect differently the vascular thrombotic status depending on the underlying disease. However, the biologic mechanisms behind such heterogeneity have remained poorly understood.

Although both RA and MPNs are inflammatory conditions, their etiologic factors are very distinct. In MPNs, the JAK2V617F mutation, activating the JAK/STAT pathway, leads to a high thrombotic risk. This is primarily due to abnormal expansion of blood cells that express elevated levels of inflammatory mediators, coupled chronic vascular activation. JAKi that specifically target the mutated JAK2 pathway are highly effective at reducing this disease‐driven hypercoagulability. In contrast, inflammation in RA and other inflammatory autoimmune diseases is more complex. There is massive production and spread of inflammatory cytokines, coming from activation of immune cells in damaged tissues, that directly target EC. The first approach investigated the effects of a single JAKi tofacitinib in a single disease RA, showing that this drug could block many inflammatory signals, but not the prothrombotic effects of cytokines 13 such as TNF and IL‐17. This review aims to expand these observations to other inflammatory diseases in comparison to cancer or MPNs in the presence of various JAKi acting on inflammation and cell proliferation.

Systemic inflammation contributes to CV risk

In the general population

Elevated inflammatory biomarkers like CRP independently predict future CVEs even in so‐called normal individuals. 14 A minimal increase in high‐sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) of ≥1.0 mg/L indicates intermediate CV risk and a level of ≥2.0 mg/L is the clinical threshold used to classify an individual as having increased CV risk. 14 CRP also serves as a reliable biomarker for the chronic, low‐grade inflammation associated with aging. 15 In older patients with a history of coronary artery disease, levels of hsCRP levels above 3 mg/L are associated with an increased incidence of future CVE. 16 Moreover, IL‐6, the cytokine that is primarily responsible for the production of CRP by the liver, is a strong biomarker for CV risk assessment in high‐risk patients, 17 and its predictive value is independent of traditional risk factors, including hypercholesterolemia. 17 Overall, both hsCRP and IL‐6 levels correlate with higher mortality and worse clinical outcomes. 17 , 18

The CANTOS trial was the first to demonstrate a dose‐response relationship where the degree of CV benefit by cytokine inhibition was directly associated with the extent of inflammation reduction. 18 The study enrolled 10,061 post‐myocardial infarction (MI) patients with elevated hsCRP (≥2 mg/L) and treated them with canakinumab, an antibody that inhibits IL‐1β activity. 18 Over 48 months, canakinumab, in addition to standard secondary CV prevention (statin therapy), significantly reduced the risk of major adverse CVEs (MACEs) by about 15% (nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, or CV death). This directly demonstrated that targeting inflammation alone could reduce CV risk, even beyond the benefits of lipid‐lowering therapies.

In autoimmune or inflammatory diseases

Patients with any of these diseases exhibit higher rates of CV morbidity and mortality compared to the general population. 19 The risk of CV disease (CVD) in RA and psoriatic arthritis is approximately 1.5 to 2.0 times higher than in the general population. 20 , 21 Individuals with ankylosing spondylitis exhibit a 25% to 60% higher incidence of CVD, depending on the type of CV or cerebrovascular condition (standardized incidence ratio ranged from 1.28 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.15–1.35] to 1.58 [95% CI 1.43–1.74]). 22 Outside arthritis, patients with inflammatory bowel disease, including Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis, have a 19% increased risk of acute arterial events compared to healthy controls. 23

This excess risk is largely driven by the prolonged release of proinflammatory cytokines, notably IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF, and IL‐17, from the inflamed tissue into the circulation, 24 , 25 inducing faster and more pronounced atherosclerosis. 4 CRP levels consistently rise with disease activity, and this elevation is associated with a subsequent increased risk for CVD, even when traditional risk factors are controlled. 26 , 27 A large prospective cohort study showed that for every 20 mg/L increase in CRP concentrations, the risk for CVE rises by 1% in patients with RA free of CVD over a 10‐year period. 28 Thus, a sustained elevated CRP level over a prolonged period will lead to a substantial cumulative increase in CV risk. Another study specifically investigating the association of time‐varying CRP levels with CVE in patients with psoriatic arthritis found a hazard ratio of 1.02 per unit increase in the CRP level, which suggests that for every 1 mg/L increase in CRP there is approximately a 2% increase in the risk of CVE. 29

In MPNs

MPNs are a group of blood cancers, including essential thrombocythemia, polycythemia vera, and primary myelofibrosis. The specific V617F mutation in the JAK2 gene (JAK2V617F) is the most frequent driver mutation in MPNs, found in about 95% of patients with polycythemia vera and half of patients with essential thrombocythemia and primary myelofibrosis. 30 The mutation causes the JAK2 protein to be constantly active, which in turn leads to the continuous activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. This results in the uncontrolled production of blood cells (platelets, leukocytes, and erythrocytes) and to chronic inflammation. 30 Increased levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF are found in patients with MPN. 30 , 31 In addition, cytokine expression, particularly of IL‐6, is more pronounced in the advanced phenotype of the disease, which is associated with worst prognosis and highest burden of systemic symptoms. 31 The overproduction of these cytokines is considered to be implicated in promoting a prothrombotic state. 30

All MPNs carry an increased risk of thrombosis compared to the normal population, 30 leading to death in approximately 40% of the cases. 32 At three months after diagnosis, the risk of having arterial thrombosis (eg, MI and stroke) is approximately 3 times higher and the risk of venous thrombosis (eg, deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism) is approximately 10 times higher. 33 Although CRP has not yet been formally incorporated into the standard adopted risk stratification models for MPNs, some studies have shown that high CRP level (>3 mg/L) is an independent risk factor for major thrombotic events. 34 Nevertheless, the link between CRP levels and the occurrence of CVD in patients with cancer, beyond MPNs, is complex and not yet fully established. 35 The elevated CRP level is far more likely to reflect tumor burden or poor prognosis in patients rather than ongoing inflammation. In addition, cancer treatments can directly damage the CV system in many ways, thereby predisposing patients to CVD, often without causing a specific elevation in CRP. 36

Vascular effects of inflammatory cytokines

Inflammatory diseases and tumors create a local proinflammatory microenvironment that extends systemically. Proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF, IL‐6, IL‐1β, or IL‐17, increase the CV risk through their effects on vessels, liver, and immune cells (Figure 1). It is important to understand that these cytokines do not act alone; their interactions and timing are crucial for the final effect. By acting on ECs, these cytokines trigger endothelial dysfunction, which is one of the starting points in atherosclerosis and thrombosis. They first induce expression of cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) on the surface of ECs, favoring leukocyte and platelet adhesion and recruitment. Further, they promote activation of the coagulation system by upregulating procoagulant factors (eg, tissue factor [TF]) in ECs and monocytes, while simultaneously inhibiting natural anticoagulant mechanisms (eg, thrombomodulin [TM]).

The prothrombotic role of key inflammatory cytokines. TNF directly damages ECs, making blood vessels more permeable, causing oxidative stress and cell death (apoptosis), all of which contribute to a higher risk of thrombosis. It also disrupts the balance of NO, a molecule that normally prevents coagulation. TNF interacts through synergy with IL‐17 and IL‐1β to amplify these effects. Combination of these cytokines acts on stromal cells in tissues, inducing the production of IL‐6. In turn, IL‐6 and IL‐1β promote Th17 differentiation and the release of even more IL‐17. Both IL‐6 and IL‐17 promote thrombosis through the liver, by triggering hepatocytes to increase the production of procoagulant proteins such as CRP and fibrinogen. TNF and IL‐17 synergize to directly cause strong prothrombotic effects on ECs, making them more receptive to adhesion for leukocytes and platelets by increasing cell adhesion molecules and procoagulation activity (tissue factor), while reducing protective anticoagulation (thrombomodulin). These defects lead to platelet activation and aggregation, which ultimately result in blood clots. TNF

TNF is the primary cytokine to be recognized in the pathogenesis of several immune‐mediated inflammatory diseases, and its targeting was the first to be tested in a growing list of diseases starting with RA, ankylosing spondylitis, and inflammatory bowel disease. TNF stimulates synoviocytes and other stromal cells to produce IL‐6 and chemokines (eg, IL‐8), in synergy with IL‐1β and IL‐17. 24 In ECs, TNF increases the permeability of the endothelial barrier and promotes expression of IL‐6, IL‐8, and CAMs (VCAM‐1, ICAM‐1, and E‐selectin), 37 leading to macrophage and lymphocyte migration and infiltration to the subendothelial layer. It stimulates ECs to release TF and promote oxidative stress, which in turn disrupts the EC barrier function and results in apoptosis. 37 TNF also impairs the production of nitric oxide, which is a potent inhibitor of platelet adhesion and vasoconstriction. 37

Several observational studies and meta‐analyses have shown that the use of TNF inhibitors (TNFi) reduce the incidence of CVE in patients with RA, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, and inflammatory bowel disease. 6 , 38 For instance, in patients with RA, a systematic review and meta‐analysis including 25 publications in total showed that TNFi reduce the risk of MI and stroke by 19% and the risk of all CVE by 54% compared to traditional disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs. 7 The positive effects of TNFi therapy on CV risk may result from slowing down atherosclerosis progression, 39 reducing vascular inflammation 38 and improving endothelial dysfunction. 38

IL‐6

IL‐6 is considered as the second crucial target in various inflammatory diseases, especially in RA. In the cytokine cascade, IL‐6 is produced through the synergistic interaction among TNF, IL‐1β, and IL‐17 acting on stromal cells such as synoviocytes, osteoblasts, myoblasts, and ECs. 24 IL‐6 is a direct driver of the inflammatory cascade implicated in CVD. It is the most potent inducer of CRP in hepatocytes 40 under the synergistic interaction of TNF and IL‐17.

As TNF, it causes EC damage by increasing inflammation and expression of CAMs. 41 It can exert an autoinflammatory response by inducing its own expression in ECs. 41 IL‐6 also acts on circulating leukocytes; it increases TF expression in monocytes, which triggers fibrin clot formation 41 ; and in combination with IL‐1β and IL‐23, it promotes the activation of IL‐17‐secreting Th17 cells. 42 IL‐6 might also exert antiatherogenic action by counteracting lipid dysregulation; it acts in macrophages to promote cholesterol efflux and efferocytosis, thereby attenuating foam cell formation within the arterial wall. 43 However, the overall contribution of IL‐6 to systemic inflammation leads to an atherogenic lipid profile, including hypertriglyceridemia and dysfunctional high‐density lipoprotein. 44

Clinical trials for IL‐6 inhibitors (IL‐6i), such as tocilizumab, the most widely used and studied IL‐6i, have generally not been designed with major CVE as a primary outcome. The evidence for their CV benefits is based on their powerful anti‐inflammatory effects controlling disease activity. 45 The few observational studies and meta‐analyses found that tocilizumab is associated with a similar 46 or even lower risk of CVE 8 , 9 when compared to other biologic or conventional therapies.

A notable aspect of IL‐6 inhibition, particularly with tocilizumab, is its influence on lipid profiles. Tocilizumab can increase total cholesterol and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels in patients with RA, 47 which initially raised concerns about a potential increase in CV risk. This is partly explained by the “lipid paradox” in RA, where active inflammation can reduce lipid serum levels, and effective anti‐inflammatory treatment will normalize them. 48 Major clinical data, including the ENTRACTE trial, has shown that this increase in lipids, does not appear to worsen CV risk, likely because IL‐6i overall benefit comes from its effective control on systemic inflammation. 9 , 47

IL‐1β

IL‐1β is another key proinflammatory cytokine that exerts prothrombotic effects. In ECs this cytokine induces a procoagulant and permeable phenotype via a TF‐dependent mechanism. 49 IL‐1β is an upstream mediator of IL‐6 in monocytes, 50 hepatocytes and ECs. 51 In combination with IL‐6, IL‐1β favors Th17 lineage commitment, therefore increasing IL‐17 production. 42 IL‐1β can also enhance platelet adhesion and aggregation, particularly in inflammatory settings. 52 In turn, activated platelets use IL‐1β to induce the expression of CAMs or integrins on ECs facilitating their attachment and the recruitment of leukocytes. 53

As discussed earlier, blocking IL‐1β has shown promise in reducing CVE. 18 This CV benefit observed in CANTOS trial was directly proportional to the magnitude of reduction in inflammatory biomarkers, particularly hsCRP and IL‐6. However, blocking IL‐1β activity in RA provides modest clinical improvements because inflammation in this disease is controlled by a redundant network of cytokines allowing other more dominant pathways to compensate. 54 In contrast, effects of IL‐1β inhibitors are massive in certain genetic autoinflammatory conditions where IL‐1 is a primary driver. 55

IL‐17A

IL‐17A (IL‐17) is a more recent treatment target. It is now a pivotal proinflammatory cytokine that plays a leading role in the pathogenesis of various inflammatory diseases. IL‐17 alone has modest effects on target cells but synergizes with other proinflammatory cytokines (eg, IL‐6, TNF, and IL‐1β), massively amplifying the overall inflammatory response. Co‐stimulation of IL‐17 with TNF or IL‐1β dramatically enhances the production of IL‐6 and IL‐8 in stromal cells, muscle cells, hepatocytes, and ECs. 5 , 24 , 51 IL‐17 enhances its own production, because the resulting IL‐6 acts as a signal to trigger the development of Th17 cells, establishing a self‐amplifying positive feedback loop, as discussed earlier. In addition, IL‐17 in concert with TNF produces a synergistic increase in the expression of CAMs (ICAM‐1 and E‐selectin), 5 promoting the enhanced rolling and adhesion of neutrophils to the vessel wall. 56 Moreover, in synergy with TNF, IL‐17 can initiate the extrinsic pathway of coagulation by increasing the expression of procoagulation genes (eg, TF) and decreasing anticoagulation gene expression (TM). 5 TM binds to thrombin generated by TF and converts it into activated protein C, which then inhibits the coagulation cascade. 57 These mechanisms result in potent activation and aggregation of platelets by IL‐17. 5

Clinical efficacy of IL‐17 inhibition has been extensively demonstrated and is a cornerstone of treatment for moderate‐to‐severe psoriasis. 58 Large real‐word studies for IL‐17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, and brodalumab) in their primary indications (plaque psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and ankylosing spondylitis) have generally reported a neutral impact on MACE 59 , 60 and some data point to a potential reduction. 61 , 62 Some research indicates that IL‐17i might offer greater protection than TNFi. Specifically, one study suggested an average 37% decrease in CVD risk for IL‐17i in patients with plaque psoriasis compared to a 22% decrease for TNFi users. 62

JAKi: Applications and unexpected CV outcomes

The JAK/STAT pathway and alternative cytokine signaling

The JAK/STAT pathway is key for signaling of a broad range of proinflammatory cytokines. It is important to differentiate cytokines that signal directly through the JAK/STAT pathway, including IFNs (IFNα/β and IFNγ), colony‐stimulating factors (G‐CSF and GM‐CSF), and interleukins such as IL‐5, IL‐6, and IL‐23. 63 whereas IL‐17, TNF, and IL‐1β do not. 64 Upon cytokine receptor binding, JAKs phosphorylate themselves and their receptors, leading to the recruitment and phosphorylation of STAT proteins (STAT1–STAT6). These activated STATs then move to the nucleus to regulate target gene expression, including inflammatory cytokines 63 , 64 (Figure 2).

Molecular mechanisms of the JAK/STAT signaling. Various cytokines, including IFNα/β, IFNγ, IL‐5, GM‐CSF, G‐CSF, IL‐6, and IL‐23, act as ligands to cytokine receptors associated with intercellular JAK molecules. Cytokine binding triggers the transphosphorylation of these JAK proteins, enhancing their catalytic activity. These activated JAKs then phosphorylate specific tyrosine residues on the cytoplasmic tail of the receptor, creating docking sites for the recruitment and subsequent phosphorylation of the seven‐member STAT family (STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5A, STAT5B, and STAT6). Once phosphorylated, these STATs, acting as both cytoplasmic signaling transducers and nuclear transcription activators, undergo dimerization and translocate to the nucleus to regulate target gene expression governing inflammation, chemotaxis, T cell proliferation, and hematopoiesis. The activation of specific JAK and STAT combinations is highly ligand dependent. For example, type I IFN (IFNα/β) utilize JAK1 and TYK2, whereas IFNγ (type II) signals via JAK1 and JAK2. In contrast, IL‐5, GM‐CSF, and G‐CSF signaling is mediated by JAK2, whereas IL‐6 and IL‐23 involve JAK1/JAK2/Tyk2 and JAK2/Tyk2, respectively. STAT1 is the primary mediator for interferons: IFNγ induces STAT1–STAT1 or STAT1–STAT3 dimers, whereas IFNα/β promote STAT1–STAT2–IRF9 complexes. Other factors show distinct profiles: IL‐5, GM‐CSF, and G‐CSF signal via STAT3 and STAT5A/B; IL‐6 preferentially induces STAT3; and IL‐23 utilizes STAT3 and STAT4. To terminate signaling, STATs induce suppressor of cytokine signaling proteins, which provide negative feedback by inhibiting activated JAKs and receptors. G‐CSF, granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor; GM‐CSF, granulocyte–macrophage colony‐stimulating factor; IFN, interferon; IL, interleukin.

In sharp contrast, IL‐17, TNF, and IL‐1β transmit signals mainly via the NF‐κB, MAPK, or CCAAT/Enhancer Binding Proteins (C/EBPs) pathways 65 , 66 , 67 ; therefore, the receptors of these cytokines do not interact with JAKs. Instead, IL‐17 receptors (IL‐17RA and IL‐17RC) are associated with NF‐κB activator 1 (Act1) and TNF receptor‐associated factor 6 (TRAF6). 65 TNF receptors (TNFR‐1 and TNFR‐2) interact mainly with TNF receptor type 1–associated DEATH domain (TRADD) and receptor‐interacting serine or threonine‐protein kinase 1 (RIPK1). 66 IL‐1β type I receptor (IL‐1R1) associates with receptor accessory protein and with the adaptor protein myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (MyD88). 67

Even though IL‐17 does not directly signal through the JAK/STAT pathway to exert its effect, its production is critically dependent on this pathway. IL‐6 potentiates IL‐1β‐mediated Th17 differentiation 42 by programming Th17‐related genes via JAK1 and STAT3. 68 Activated STAT3 induces genes, IL 17 A, IL 17F, IL 21, IL 22, and the transcription factor retinoic acid‐related orphan nuclear hormone receptor (ROR C). 69 RORc is the key transcription factor for Th17 lineage, 70 which in turn leads to the production of IL‐17. STAT3 also induces the expression of IL‐23R, making the nascent Th17 cells responsive to IL‐23, which further stabilizes and amplifies the pathogenicity of Th17 cells 69 (Figure 3). Although STAT3 is the main driver downstream of IL‐6, IL‐1β can also enhance STAT3 phosphorylation via NF‐κB signaling to facilitate Th17 differentiation and IL‐17 secretion. 71 In addition, IL‐1β‐activated AP‐1 and NF‐κB (specifically the c‐Rel subunit) bind to overlapping regulatory regions on the promoter of the RORC gene, ensuring robust Th17 cell commitment 72 (Figure 3). By contrast, STAT1 activated by IFNγ can negatively regulate Th17 development, 73 , 74 possibly through inhibition of STAT3 activity 75 , 76 (Figure 3).

Important inflammatory cytokines regulating Th17 differentiation and IL‐17 production via JAK/STAT and non‐JAK pathways. JAK‐dependent cytokines: IL‐6 and IL‐23 promote the differentiation and function of Th17 cells through the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway. When these cytokines bind to their cellular receptors, they activate associated JAK proteins (JAK1, JAK2, and TYK2), which then phosphorylate the receptors. This creates docking sites for STAT3. Once bound, STAT3 is also phosphorylated by JAKs, allowing it to form dimers and translocate to the nucleus. In the nucleus, STAT3 switches on genes for Th17 development such as RORc, IL‐17, and the IL‐23 receptor (IL‐23R). IL‐23 then binds to his receptor to ensure the Th17 lineage is sustained. Conversely, IFNγ acts as an inhibitor, activating STAT1 to competitively block Th17 differentiation. Non‐JAK‐dependent cytokines: IL‐17, TNF, and IL‐1β do not directly utilize JAKs for signaling. Instead, they activate adaptor proteins such as MyD88, TRAF6/Act1, and RIPK1/TRADD, which lead to the activation of the NF‐κB, MAPKs, and C/EBPs signaling pathways. IL‐1β‐activated NF‐κB and AP‐1 (from MAPKs), work together with STAT3 to drive the full Th17 differentiation program. They enhance the expression of genes like ROR C. IL‐17 and TNF often promote Th17 differentiation indirectly by stimulating other cells (such as monocytes or dendritic cells) to release the primary Th17 polarizing cytokines, IL‐6 and IL‐1β. These pathways are not directly inhibited by JAKi and therefore remain active even in the presence of these drugs. IFN, interferon; IL, interleukin; JAKi, JAK inhibitor.

JAKi

JAKi are a class of targeted small‐molecule drugs that work by directly interfering with the activity of JAKs. They competitively block ATP from binding within the JAK enzymes, thus blocking phosphorylation of JAKs. 64 JAKi are used in various inflammatory diseases and, to a more limited but growing extent, in certain cancers. In some trials, JAKi demonstrated superior clinical efficacy in achieving disease control and reducing inflammation (based on CRP levels) when compared with TNFi. 77 , 78

The main JAKi approved for inflammatory diseases typically target combinations of JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3 (eg, tofacitinib, baricitinib, and peficitinib) or are highly selective for a single JAK (eg, upadacitinib and filgotinib). Their primary goal is to reduce excessive inflammation driven by normal but overactive cytokine signaling via the JAK/STAT pathway (Table 1).

Although the JAK/STAT pathway can be aberrantly activated in many cancers, the use of JAKi is largely concentrated in MPNs. Ruxolitinib was the first JAKi approved for the treatment of myelofibrosis, 79 which is a potent and selective oral inhibitor of both JAK1 and JAK2. Fedratinib is primarily a selective oral JAK2 inhibitor. These JAKi target the constitutive activation of JAK2 (JAK2V617F) to block the resulting autonomous JAK/STAT activation and consequently control disease‐driving cell proliferation (Table 1).

JAKi in inflammatory diseases and related CV risk

Although biologic therapies targeting specific cytokines (IL‐6, TNF, and IL‐1β) often improve CV outcomes, JAKi do not follow this trend, can they even increase such risk at least in some patients? The ORAL Surveillance study, a phase IV safety trial of the JAKi tofacitinib in high‐risk patients with RA (>50 years old, preexisting CV history), revealed an increased risk of MACE and malignancies compared to TNFi. 12 In addition, the trial observed a dose‐dependent increase in venous thromboembolism with tofacitinib compared to TNFi. 12 , 80 This led to a protocol amendment mid‐trial to reduce the 10 mg twice daily dose of tofacitinib to 5 mg twice daily for most patients. Subsequent meta‐analyses and regulatory assessments have extended these warnings to other JAKi such as baricitinib and upadacitinib, suggesting a class effect. 81 Unlike tofacitinib, neither upadacitinib nor baricitinib was linked to a significantly higher rate of MACE in patients with RA compared to TNFi during their respective clinical development programs. 10 , 82 However, when focusing specifically on high‐risk patients (those ≥50 years old with at least one CV risk factor), some analyses showed a higher reporting for MACE for both agents 82 , 83 (Table 2). Despite differences in individual kinase selectivity profiles, a class‐wide boxed warning remains in place for all JAKi due to their shared mechanism of action. Unlike findings in RA, JAKi use in other inflammatory conditions, such as psoriatic arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis, or inflammatory bowel disease, has not yet been associated with an elevated risk of CVE 84 , 85 (Table 2). This discrepancy likely stems from the inherent CV risk associated with systemic inflammation in RA, alongside the older, more comorbid demographic of patients with RA compared to those with inflammatory bowel disease or psoriatic arthritis. Given that current evidence may not fully capture the long‐term risks for healthier or more heterogeneous cohorts, continued and prolonged investigation into real‐world data is warranted.

The link between JAKi and CVE is not believed to be due to a direct effect on the main components of the coagulation cascade. 86 , 87 Some studies hypothesize that selective JAK inhibition could block some cytokines that have both pro‐ and antithrombotic effects, priming to elevated thrombotic risk. 88 In RA, the persistent high systemic inflammatory burden causes accelerated atherosclerosis and dysfunction of ECs creating a prothrombotic environment in the vasculature. Because ECs are involved in the occurrence of CVE, some clinical trials for conditions like RA used flow‐mediated dilation (FMD) to evaluate whether JAKi improve EC function, and, by extension, reduce CV risk. One study found that tofacitinib significantly improved FMD after 12 weeks of treatment, and this improvement correlated negatively with disease activity markers (DAS28 and CRP). 89 Another study showed that FMD was not affected by one‐year tofacitinib treatment while artery thickness increased further despite treatment. 90 Further, a prospective study showed that three‐month treatment with JAKi did not affect coronary microcirculation, a measure that can reflect EC dysfunction, in a small cohort of patients with RA with at least one CV risk factor. 91

The progression from EC dysfunction to a symptomatic CVE is a long‐term process, establishing a direct correlation within these clinical trials of limited duration and sample size is difficult. 92 The most recent trials provide a follow‐up of only up to two years, which is fine for disease control but insufficient when considering CV risk. Therefore, larger clinical trials with longer follow‐up periods are necessary to fully understand if and how JAKi contribute to CVE.

JAKi in MPNs and related CV risk

Unlike in RA, thrombosis in MPNs stems from an acquired genetic defect. The presence of the JAK2V617F mutation results in the constitutive activation of the JAK/STAT pathway. This causes an overproduction and hyper‐reactivity of the MPN‐clone–derived blood cells (ie, erythrocytes, platelets, and leukocytes) making them inherently prothrombotic. 30 For instance, increased erythrocyte deformability promotes blood hyperviscosity, 93 whereas aberrant P‐selectin expression on platelets facilitates the formation of heterotypic platelet–leukocyte aggregates, 93 a clinical hallmark of the MPN thrombotic state. 94 These aggregates further reprogram monocytes to express TF and release TF‐bearing microparticles, 93 significantly amplifying thrombin generation throughout the circulation. Recent mechanistic studies have bridged the gap between this mutation and clinical thrombosis, establishing a direct causal link. Neutrophils harboring JAK2 mutation from patients with MPN exhibit a primed state for the release of extracellular traps (NETosis), which directly promotes thrombus formation. 95 Murine JAK2‐mutated ECs independently promote a prothrombotic state through increased P‐selectin expression, directly facilitating leukocyte and platelet recruitment even in the absence of elevated blood counts. 96

Parallel to these cellular defects, the mutation drives a robust, multifactorial inflammatory response. The malignant myeloid clones overproduce proinflammatory cytokines as in RA, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF, 97 , 98 creating a proinflammatory niche that recruits bystander healthy cells that further secrete inflammatory mediators, 97 As discussed earlier, these cytokines have a well‐documented systemic impact; they induce elevated CRP levels, which correlate strongly with the JAK2V617F allele burden. 99 Furthermore, elevated CRP is a robust clinical predictor of major thrombotic events in patients with MPN. 34 , 100 Collectively these processes alter blood flow and vascular integrity, leading to microcirculatory symptoms and an increased risk of arterial and venous thrombosis (Figure 4).

Contrasting mechanisms of thrombosis in myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) and chronic inflammatory diseases: a cell‐driven versus cytokine‐driven model. The primary cause of thrombosis in MPNs is clonal hematopoietic cell expansion and hyperactivation. By contrast, the primary cause of thrombosis in chronic inflammatory diseases is massive release of inflammatory cytokines from inflamed tissues. The key player in MPNs is the JAK2 mutation (JAK2V617F), that causes the JAK/STAT pathway to be constitutively active in newly forming blood cells (platelets, leukocytes, and erythrocytes), resulting in cell intrinsic abnormalities, in addition to the aberrant expansion. These abnormalities comprise increased deformability of erythrocytes, up‐regulation of thromboinflammatory genes such as P‐selectin, release of microparticles containing TF and excessive cytokine production (IL‐6 and TNF) establishing a proinflammatory state. These cytokines together with leukocyte: platelet aggregates activate ECs, inducing a proadhesive and procoagulant state. In chronic diseases, the key players are inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6, TNF, and IL‐17) leading to a hypercoagulation state by directly affecting ECs (the primary site of damage). The combination of IL‐17 and TNF affects these cells by inducing a proinflammatory state and by upregulating adhesion molecules (VCAM‐1 and ICAM‐1) and TF, whereas suppressing natural anticoagulants such as thrombomodulin. In MPNs, JAK2 inhibitors directly target the JAK2V617F mutation in blood‐forming cells, which in turn normalizes blood cell counts and reduces their prothrombotic properties. In inflammatory diseases such as in rheumatoid arthritis, JAKi can inhibit the downstream effects of some cytokines like IL‐6, but they do not directly inhibit the prothrombotic effects of TNF and IL‐17 on ECs, therefore JAKi might be less effective at preventing cardiovascular disease in this context. CAM, cell adhesion molecule; EC, endothelial cell; IL, interleukin; JAKi, JAK inhibitor; TF, tissue factor; TNF, tumor necrosis factor; TM, thrombomodulin.

Emerging evidence indicates the potential of the JAKi ruxolitinib to reduce the elevated risk of thrombotic events in MPNs, 101 , 102 , 103 possibly because it reduces the number of erythrocytes, platelets, and leukocytes. 104 , 105 Ruxolitinib also reduces chronic low‐grade inflammation, intrinsically linked to thrombotic risk, which might explain the favorable CV outcomes. 106 Patients treated with ruxolitinib show a substantial decrease in circulating levels of IL‐6 and CRP. 105 , 107 The reduction in these markers of inflammation is directly correlated with the improvement in MPN‐related symptoms. 107

Direct evidence for fedratinib impact on reducing thrombotic risk in patients with MPN is less established. Fedratinib effectively reduces the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, CRP, and others, and influences the immune cell profile, which are significant contributors to the prothrombotic state in MPNs. 108 Overall, these changes correlated with clinical response (eg, spleen volume reduction), suggesting that the therapeutic effects of fedratinib are mediated, in part, by its impact on inflammation. 108 Importantly, both ruxolitinib and fedratinib reduce the prothrombotic activation of ECs by decreasing proadhesive and procoagulant molecules and limiting IL‐6 signaling. 109 , 110 In addition, studies using microfluidics demonstrated that treatment of ECs with ruxolitinib prevents leukocyte adhesion from both healthy donors and those with JAK2V617F‐positive MPNs. 109 , 110

Unraveling the paradox: Mechanisms to explain the residual thrombotic risk despite the strong anti‐inflammatory effect of JAKi

JAKi (such as tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib) target JAK1, JAK2, or both, and therefore effectively block the downstream signaling inflammatory molecules like IL‐6. By blocking the IL‐6/JAK/STAT pathway, JAKi directly suppress the synthesis and secretion of CRP by the liver. 111 JAKi lead to a rapid and substantial decrease in CRP levels in RA and psoriatic arthritis. 112 , 113 , 114 However, studies have shown that inhibition of IL‐6 in humans reduces IL‐6 bioactivity and CRP but does not mitigate TNF levels, endothelial activation, TF mRNA induction, or other downstream coagulation markers (such as prothrombin fragment 1 + 2, TAT complexes, and D‐dimer). 115 By blocking the JAKs involved in IL‐6 and IL‐23 signaling, JAKi also interfere with the development of Th17 cells. 116 As a result, tofacitinib inhibits the production of IL‐17 by human T cells alone. 117 , 118 Although JAKi lower IL‐17 levels, 118 it rarely achieves complete and sustained elimination of all IL‐17. This incomplete inhibition of IL‐17 likely results from Th17 differentiation being partly sustained by IL‐1β, which acts independently of JAK/STAT pathway (as discussed previously).

JAKi are ineffective against TNF and IL‐17 activity because these cytokines do not signal through the JAK/STAT pathway. A key point is that both cytokines affect coagulation largely on their own, independently of IL‐6. As mentioned previously, TNF and IL‐17 act in synergy to exert major prothrombotic effects on ECs, including the induction of CAMs (VCAM‐1, ICAM‐1, and E‐selectin), increase in TF and decrease in TM expression. 5 Although recent studies confirmed that JAKi reduce vascular inflammation related to IL‐6 in vivo 119 and in vitro. 118 , 120 they paradoxically could not counteract the harmful effects of TNF and IL‐17 on ECs. 118 , 120 These effects comprise a persistence of CAMs induction and failure to restore the balance between TF and TM expression levels, implying that the aberrant coagulation mechanisms are still ongoing in the presence of JAKi (Figure 3).

In vitro treatment of EC with intermediate doses (1 μM) of tofacitinib decreases ICAM‐1 and E‐selectin expression, while at a higher dose (10 μM), this effect disappears and VCAM‐1 expression further increases. 120 Other JAKi (baricitinib, upadacitinib, peficitinib, and ruxolitinib) have little effect at 1 μM but worsen TNF+ IL‐17‐induced CAMs (VCAM‐1 and ICAM‐1) at 10 μM, suggesting a dose‐dependent effect of JAKi on CAMs. 120 Studies on thrombotic disorders such as the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) showed that the enhanced thrombosis is mediated by induction of CAMs (VCAM‐1, ICAM‐1, and P‐selectin) on ECs, and targeting these molecules in APS models reduces the size of thrombi. 121 Furthermore elevated plasma levels of soluble CAMs are considered molecular markers for atherosclerosis and predictors of incident coronary heart disease. 122 Therefore, the observed clinical increase in thrombotic risk with JAKi is a drug‐related adverse event 12 , 80 that may be mechanistically attributed to the up‐regulation of CAMs on ECs that becomes more pronounced at higher doses.

This could be due to off‐target effects within the cytokine network, where blocking the JAK/STAT pathway at high concentrations shifts the balance toward other CAMs‐inducing pathways, like TNF signaling (MAPK and NF‐κB), not fully inhibited. Induction of these alternative pathways following treatment with JAKi is a known and clinically relevant issue in cancer studies. 123 , 124

Another mechanism to explain the increase of CAMs with high‐dose treatment JAKi is cell toxicity. Indeed, peficitinib and fedratinib at high concentrations were shown to impair EC viability through increased apoptosis, even after reduction in IL‐6 levels. 120 High doses of JAKi could be cytotoxic for ECs through generation of oxidative stress. In this regard, the release of reactive oxygen species by cytotoxic agents, intricately linked with apoptosis, activates NF‐κB and MAPK pathways and to also amplify VCAM‐1 and ICAM‐1 expression. 125 , 126 , 127 Moreover, damaged or apoptotic ECs become procoagulant by increasing TF procoagulant activity and losing TM and TF pathway inhibitor effectiveness. 128 On this point, EC apoptosis at low blood flow sites within human atherosclerotic plaques was suggested to trigger plaque erosion and thrombosis. 129

The adverse profile of JAKi extends beyond CV and thrombotic concerns. By interfering with growth factors and IFN signaling, JAKi also predispose patients to significant hematologic and infectious risks. JAKi, specifically nonselective agents such as tofacitinib or baricitinib, disrupt GM‐CSF signaling, which depends exclusively on JAK2. This inhibition impairs the JAK2/STAT5 survival pathway in the myeloid lineage, 130 likely reducing mature neutrophil longevity and suppressing progenitor differentiation. Consequently, in patients with inflammatory conditions, JAKi therapy is frequently associated with transient blood cell abnormalities, including anemia, lymphopenia, and neutropenia, 10 , 131 , 132 , 133 often manifesting shortly after treatment initiation. Moreover, the inhibition of type I IFN (IFNα/β) via the JAK1/TYK2 pathway significantly compromises host antiviral surveillance. The clinical safety profile of JAKi in RA and ulcerative colitis is thus characterized by a notable frequency of Herpes Zoster reactivation, 12 , 134 representing one of the most prevalent complications observed in clinical practice.

Overall, JAKi are effective in MPNs because they block the overactive JAK/STAT pathway affected by a specific mutation. This is the central mechanism driving the disease, which leads to the overproduction of blood cells and a resulting hypercoagulable state. The situation is more complex in chronic inflammatory diseases, where the JAK/STAT pathway is activated by a wide range of inflammatory cytokines. Consequently, the prothrombotic mechanisms are more complex and not fully targeted by JAK inhibition because two of the main inflammatory cytokines, TNF and IL‐17, driving EC damage and thrombosis in these conditions use signaling pathways independent of JAK/STAT. At high doses, JAKi might even make ECs more prone to thrombosis through off‐target effects and cellular stress, increasing endothelial adhesiveness.

Conclusions

JAKi have demonstrated impressive therapeutic efficacy by directly targeting the hyperactive signaling in inflammation and MPNs. By inhibiting the JAK pathway, JAKi effectively reduce the systemic burden of inflammatory cytokines, notably decreasing circulating IL‐6 levels, which correlates with a marked improvement in constitutional symptoms and overall quality of life. This anti‐inflammatory action is widely considered a protective effect that should theoretically translate into reduced thrombotic and CV risk, a link that has been established for TNFi and IL‐6i. However, although JAKi are often highly effective at controlling the inflammatory components—sometimes achieving superior control than TNFi or IL‐6i—this potent reduction in inflammation does not translate into a proportional reduction in CV risk in chronic inflammation. Instead, the JAKi paradox suggests that their incomplete action on vascular cells and coagulation mechanisms introduces parallel thrombotic risk that override the anti‐inflammatory benefit, resulting in a residual or even increased CV burden.

It must be noted that in clinical practice, JAKi are frequently reserved for treatment‐refractory patients with aggressive disease; consequently, this cohort inherently presents with a more significant burden of baseline CV risk factors. To address this, an emerging trend in precision rheumatology emphasizes a personalized approach utilizing the venous thromboembolism risk stratification framework before JAKi therapy (VTE‐JAK) alongside the 2023 EULAR recommendations. Under these guidelines, patients should undergo formal CV screening using validated tools such as SCORE2 or the Framingham Risk Score before initiation. For high‐risk individuals, particularly those over age 65 or with a history of smoking, alternatives such as IL‐6i may represent more appropriate choices to mitigate safety concerns, as suggested by the ORAL Surveillance data. However, when patients must transition to a JAKi despite an elevated risk profile, a risk‐neutralization strategy must be essential. This approach should center on the intensive management of modifiable factors, including smoking cessation, rigorous blood pressure control, and the initiation of antiplatelet therapy where clinically indicated. Finally, experimental strategies co‐administering NF‐κB inhibitors could theoretically counteract CVEs by neutralizing the downstream effects of TNF and IL‐17; however, the safety and efficacy of these combinations require rigorous clinical validation.
