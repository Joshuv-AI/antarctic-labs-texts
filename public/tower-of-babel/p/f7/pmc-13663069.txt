Introduction

Depression is frequent among youth with HIV (YWH) and causes significant impairment. Adolescents and young adults bear a significant burden of the HIV epidemic in the United States. In 2019, youth ages 13–24 accounted for 21% of all new infections [1]. Research has also consistently found high rates of depression among YWH, with reported prevalence as high as 49% [2–11]. Depression has been shown to increase risk of disease progression, increase caregiver burden, increase healthcare costs, and decrease quality of life [12, 13]. YWH with depression are also likely to have other psychiatric symptoms [3, 14, 15], cognitive decline [16], and suicidality or self-harm [6]. One study found no differences in depression rates between youth who were newly diagnosed and those who had known about their HIV infection for more than a year, suggesting high rates of depression among YWH are not a transient phenomenon [3]. There is an urgent need to deliver effective treatments for depression among YWH to improve psychiatric and medical outcomes and their quality of life [17, 18].

There has been substantial research on the treatment of depression among youth, which indicates that a collaborative, combination treatment (psychotherapy and medication, tailored for the population) and measured care is the treatment of choice. However, it is seldom fully implemented in practice, leading to reduced effectiveness [18, 19]. We are not aware of any other published depression treatment trials for YWH in the U.S. other than our research, although interventions to address other outcomes (e.g. adherence) have assessed depression [20–22]. There have been more than a dozen projects to improve the mental health of YWH outside of the U.S., using a variety of modalities and most studies report improvements in depression [23–27]. In addition, two recent RCTs among YWH in Africa found that Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) was efficacious [24, 28]. In the U.S., practice guidelines, case reports, and clinical reviews of depression treatment for YWH suggest that a medication management algorithm (MMA) and an evidence-based psychotherapy, such as CBT (combination treatment, COMB) shows a more rapid reduction in depressive symptoms than using only a single treatment modality (i.e., either psychotropic medication or CBT) and that they are more effective when tailored to the population [29–39]. Tailored psychotherapy is essential to address the unique concerns of YWH such as chronic illness management, adherence to medications, transition to adult care, interrelated psychosocial factors, and personal issues [20, 21, 29, 40–46]. For example, as many as 89% of YWH endorse experienced social issues related to HIV, and 64% have experienced people avoiding them, rejecting them, or abusing them because of HIV infection [47]. Such issues have been associated with depression for those with HIV [48]. Those who have experienced such problems are at greater risk for psychiatric symptoms [9, 49, 50]. Therefore, CBT that can be tailored to these populations is essential. However, CBT is often not adequately tailored or is not delivered with fidelity [51].

Similarly, when antidepressant medication is needed for the treatment of depression, algorithms (a framework to decide if medications are needed, choice of meds, dosages, and adjustments) have been repeatedly found to be more effective than treatment as usual (TAU) for improving outcomes for youth with depression [52–57]. Algorithms provide flexible but structured decision points and recommendations based on available research and expert judgements in trials with youth with, and without, HIV [46–48, 50, 52, 53]. The algorithms base clinical decisions on regular measurement of depression symptoms and studies repeatedly show that measured-care treatment (care decisions guided by measures of symptomatology) is more effective than care not guided by measured assessments [38, 58–60]. Repeated measurement of symptoms and a scientifically based algorithm may not guide on-going clinical decisions. This results in clinicians continuing individuals on suboptimal doses or ineffective medications for too long or making medication changes too quickly. It may be difficult to sustain the necessary procedures in clinics due to a lack of ongoing supervision, time constraints, and other priorities [61–63].

We have demonstrated that a medication algorithm combined with CBT tailored for YWH and a collaborative measured-care treatment is efficacious for YWH and improves their quality of life significantly. In two prior studies, we have trained therapists and medication providers in this comprehensive, combined approach (COMB) and compared it to the routine psychotherapy and medication management at other clinics [64, 65]. Our larger, cluster-randomized RCT found at Week 24 statistically significant improvement in the site-level mean number of depressive symptoms, the proportion of YWH with a treatment response, and the proportion in remission at COMB sites compared to TAU sites (6.7 vs. 10.6, 62% vs. 18%, 48% vs.17%, ps\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\le\:$$\end{document}0.01, respectively) [64, 65]. However, by Week 48 depression indices in COMB had not continued to improve and differences were no longer significant between arms for two of our primary outcomes: mean Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (QIDS) scores (Fig. 1) and remission rates. Additionally, viral suppression did not improve in COMB compared to TAU. These findings indicate a “voltage drop” of COMB’s impact and program “drift,” known challenges to sustainability [51, 66]. The COMB clinics and clinicians reported that the most helpful elements of COMB were reminders to use the algorithms and treatment manuals, easy availability of patients’ depression scores, and the continued supervision calls, which reinforced skills learned during the initial training. However, implementation challenges reduced the sustainability of COMB. For example, after the formal intervention period, the depression rating scale was used less frequently because it became “less of a priority.” In addition, collaborative meetings became less frequent and there was less attention given to improving antiretroviral treatment (ART) adherence because supervision had stopped after 24 weeks. Because of the need to enhance sustainability, the combination treatment will be enhanced with additional implementation strategies (COMBEX), in addition to those strategies previously used.

This protocol paper describes a 5-year Hybrid Type 2 Cluster Randomized Trial in which COMBEX (with its additional implementation strategies) will be compared to COMB, with co-primary effectiveness and implementation aims guided by the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs in Healthcare [66, 67]. The trial will test the effectiveness of COMBEX compared to COMB on the implementation outcomes of adoption, fidelity, and sustainability. It will also evaluate the impact of COMBEX in reducing indices of depression (e.g., symptoms, treatment response, remission) and decreasing the viral load (VL), as compared to COMB. Randomization at the site levels was chosen to avoid loss of fidelity or cross-talk that could occur. Lastly, an explanatory, sequential, mixed-method approach will be used to evaluate the context of implementation, for both COMB and COMBEX, with data obtained from staff/clinicians. To our knowledge, this will be the first project to examine implementation outcomes in depression treatment among youth with HIV and among the few to test the effectiveness of treatment of depression in improving viral load suppression for YWH.

Methods and design

Overview

This project will compare an enhanced version of combination treatment (COMBEX) to our previously tested combination treatment (COMB) in a Hybrid Type 2 Cluster Randomized Trial. COMBEX will be enhanced by five implementation strategies as suggested in our post-trial interviews from our efficacy trial and it will also continue to use the implementation strategies used in COMB. It is hypothesized that these additional strategies will improve sustainability and depression outcomes at 48 and 72 weeks.

The co-primary aims focus on implementation outcomes and real-world effectiveness of reducing indices of depression and viral load. An explanatory aim will use data from staff/clinicians to explore the context of implementation for both COMB and COMBEX, as guided by the sustainability framework. To enhance our understanding of implementation context factors, we will also engage community partners to guide project development and implementation.

Setting

Sites are eligible if they have a medication prescriber, a therapist, and the ability to recruit at least 16 English-speaking participants starting, or in, treatment for depression with depressive symptoms and have not participated in COMB. All sites will be in the United States. A pre-study survey with interested HIV clinic directors will assess potential sites “readiness” for implementation by its desire to train its clinicians and the target clinic characteristics including population size and demographics, range of services, and location. We have not yet started recruitment, thus do not have a CONSORT flow diagram, however it will include the number of sites invited to participate, the number of sites that meet eligibility criteria and meet “readiness” for implementation, the number randomized to COMB and to COMBEX, the number of patients and staff enrolled at each site, and the retention over time of the sites.

Youth participants

Potentially eligible participants will be approached during regular clinic visits and information about the trial will be provided. For interested YWH, consent to contact will be completed and study staff will follow-up to screen for eligibility. Participants must be age 15–24 years (COMB has been tested with ages 15–24 and all materials are appropriate for youth), engaged in care at a participating HIV care site, have documented HIV-1 confirmed by medical records, a diagnosis as determined by a site clinician of nonpsychotic depression (either Major Depressive Disorder, Depression not otherwise specified, or Dysthymia as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-V)), significant symptomatology as defined by a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score ≥ 10, aware of their HIV infection as determined by site staff, English-speaking, and able and willing to provide written informed assent/consent and written parental or guardian permission (if required by state law and/or IRB policies). Youth will be excluded from the study if they: (1) have history of any psychotic disorders and/or Bipolar I or II disorder; (2) have a history of a severe alcohol or substance dependence according to DSM-V or had moderate symptoms and are experiencing withdrawal or dependence symptoms within the month prior to enrollment; (3) have depression and/or suicidal ideation requiring more intensive services; (4) intend to relocate from the study site; (5) are in therapy with a non-study therapist (unless willing to switch to study-trained therapist); and (6) if they are in imminent danger to themselves or others.

HIV care clinic staff

For pre- and post- trial interviews and surveys, clinic staff will be eligible if they are English speaking, have worked at their clinic for at least one year, and played a role in or are otherwise impacted by the trial at their site. Advertisement and recruitment will be done via staff meetings, flyers, and via the clinic’s staff email listserv. There will be representation from all relevant groups, including therapists, medication prescribers, support staff, and leadership.

Intervention

Understanding that frequently it is difficult for evidence-based interventions (EBI) to be adopted with fidelity we were attentive to several implementation factors throughout the efficacy trial of COMB. We utilized the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs in Healthcare to organize and address relevant factors [66, 67] This framework highlights core constructs (such as leadership support, training and supervision, staff and turnover, funding and resources, and more) of sustainability approaches in medical literature to allow for systematic identification of barriers and facilitators to sustainability, as well as comparison of approaches [67]. We used Expert Recommendation for Implementing Change (ERIC) strategies, which are a compendium of strategies, to enhance uptake and sustainment of EBIs in different settings [68]. Table 1 summarizes how the implementation determinants, identified by applying the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability, were addressed by COMB using four ERIC strategies [69]. We have also added a column on how the strategy was operationalized, in line with guidance from Proctor et al., 2013 [70]. Table 2 shows how the implementation determinants will be addressed by COMBEX via additional ERIC strategies and mechanisms, leading to improved implementation outcomes [69]. COMBEX includes five new ERIC strategies and four previously tested strategies in COMB. Operationalization of the new strategies will be determined based on lessons learned from our efficacy trial and site interviews and finalized in the first phase of our study. Because the platforms have common capabilities and features, another, similar platform and app could be substituted if this platform becomes obsolete [75].

YWH enrolled will complete the PHQ-9 when they arrive for a therapist or medication provider appointment at least once a month. The assessment will be administered by the clinic (paper/pencil) and given to the clinician or entered into the EMR as per the clinic’s usual routine. YWH will complete an online survey assessing depression and related factors quarterly for 72 weeks.

Randomization

Randomization is at the site level because the two arms will have different implementation strategies. Sites will be randomly assigned using a restricted randomization procedure to minimize imbalance in site characteristics between arms. We will use YWH factors to balance the arms such as sex at birth and number of YWH served. In our prior 13 site study, these factors best distinguished sites. Also, supplemental analyses of potential effect modification of eight baseline factors revealed that only sex at birth had a significant interaction with COMB treatment (females more likely to be “responders”, p = 0.01). With at least 11 possible sites (and the plan to only utilize 8 sites for the study), there will be many possible site combinations that will balance the arms. As done in our efficacy trial, a computer program will generate all possible site allocations that balance the three characteristics. One allocation will be randomly chosen. This will be done prior to enrolling individual participants.

Implementation measures, outcomes, and analysis

Multiple measures will be collected to assess outcomes related to the additional ERIC intervention strategies. The number of PHQ-9 scores for each enrolled YWH (reflecting use of the depression app vs. clinic administration) will be abstracted from the medical records at Weeks 24, 48, and 72. Therapist Session Checklist will document the number of sessions attended, topics covered, and any untoward events for each YWH. Medication Provider Checklist will document the YWH MMA stage (and rationale for deviations), PHQ-9 score, current antidepressant medications and side effects to antidepressants, Clinician Attendance at Supervision / Consultation Calls (a count reflecting incentive structures and period of access) will be monitored and counts tabulated. The Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) will be completed by enrolled YWH, therapist, and medication provider at the end of their study period and will reflect general reactions to the implementation of the interventions. These measures were developed for implementation research and each one contains 4 Likert items. The internal consistency alphas range from 0.85 to 0.91 and test-retest reliabilities range from 0.73 to 0.89 [79]. The Provider Report of Sustainment Scale (PRESS) is a three-item assessment of sustainability of EBIs. It was developed and validated using the Rasch measurement theory and it was tested with four different EBIs and across different settings. The reported Internal consistency alpha is 0.95 [80].

Outcomes by condition will be examined descriptively and evaluated in nonparametric analyses (e.g., Mann-Whitney U) with data from clinics (% of patient visits with a recorded PHQ-9 score) and clinicians. Adoption and fidelity will be determined from: [1] Therapist Checklists (number of topics covered and topics related to medication adherence); [2] Medication Provider Checklists (number of weeks on antidepressants that correspond to the MMA and deviations from the MMA); and [3] Clinician Attendance at Supervision Call records (counts of attendance). To assess feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, and sustainability, we will calculate mean total scores for the FIM, AIM, IAM, and PRESS among clinic staff. Mean scores that correspond to item anchors of “moderate or high” on the respective scales will be considered indication of intervention feasibility, acceptability and/or appropriateness. YWH also complete the FIM, AIM, and IAM and mean scores between conditions will be compared with t-tests.

YWH measures, effectiveness outcomes, and analysis

The primary effectiveness outcome will be the mean PHQ-9 score over the study period. Participants will complete the PHQ-9, a nine-item scale assessing depressive symptoms in the past 2 weeks. The PHQ-9 is a reliable and valid measure of depression in adults and adolescents [81, 82]. Total scores range from 0 to 27, whereby scores of 5–9 reflect mild symptoms, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, and ≥ 20 severe. Response to treatment (> 50% decrease from baseline) and remission from depression will also be analyzed as secondary outcomes. YWH participants at both COMBEX and COMB sites will complete assessments via REDCap at baseline and Weeks 12, 24, 36, 48 and 72.

The secondary effectiveness outcome will be the quantitative VL levels (copies/mL). This will be abstracted from the medical records at baseline and Weeks 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 (with their consent). Additional YWH outcomes include general quality of life (measured using the PROMIS Global-10 item scale [83–85]) and adherence to care, which will be measured by current prescription of any antidepressant medication, prescription of ART, and the number of psychotherapy and medication management sessions, and other HIV clinic visits attended. This will be abstracted from medical records at Weeks 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72. YWH will self-report their attendance to HIV medical / therapy appointments and use of ART and/or antidepressants at each quarterly assessment [86].

Other behaviors and variable of interest include substance use, sexual behavioral, and demographic data. Substance use will be measured using The Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST V2.0); this screens for all levels of problem substance use and demonstrates significant concurrent, construct, predictive and discriminative validity [87]. Sexual behavior will be assessed using commonly measured behaviors including lifetime history and past three-month frequency of occurrence of oral, vaginal and anal sex, number and sex of sexual partners, and frequency of condom use [3, 88]. Demographic data includes age, educational level, sex at birth, and stability of housing.

Intent-to-treat analyses of participants who completed the baseline assessment will be used to evaluate improvements from COMBEX vs. COMB in depression, log10 viral load, and treatment adherence. Given that both conditions are active interventions, we will evaluate treatment response from baseline for both conditions as well as the comparison between the conditions. We will use a linear mixed effect model to evaluate trajectory of PHQ-9 scores across the 72-week study period. The model will include site as a fixed effect along with linear slope across assessments, treatment condition, and the interaction between the two. Random effects will include participant-level intercept and slope. We will again use linear mixed effect model with a similar specification to evaluate viral load. For treatment adherence, we will use a generalized linear mixed effect model with log link and Poisson distribution including the same fixed and random effects as for depression and viral load. The model will also include the optimal number of components as an offset to account for between participant differences in treatment complexity. We will also use logistic regression to evaluate clinically important improvements by evaluating those who responded to the intervention and those who were virally suppressed at the 36, 48- and 72-week assessments. Exploratory analyses (effect moderation) will use site level characteristics (e.g., services available, clinic size, sex at birth) as possible site-level moderators to examine site factors associated with improved outcomes. Because power will be limited, effect sizes will be used to evaluate the potential importance of modifiers.

Sample size justification & power

Eight sites are available for randomization. This number was identified as being feasible given the scope of the project. It is expected that 16–17 patients will participate within each site for a total of 130 participants nested in 8 sites. Power for the project was estimated using simulations generated by the R package simstudy v0.6.0. Simulations assumed 10% of the variance in the outcome is at the level of the site, 40% at the level of the individual, with 15% attrition by the 48-week follow-up, and a two-tailed type-1 error rate of 0.05. For the primary effectiveness outcome, power is 0.80 to detect a standardized change from baseline (d) of 0.25 or greater across both groups and a difference of 0.51 or greater between groups. Assuming a PHQ-9 of 15 in the sample at baseline, with a standard deviation of 4.5, the study will be able to detect a change in PHQ-9 score of 1.13 or a 11% change over time which is lower than the minimal clinically important difference of 28.5% for the measure. We will be able to detect a between condition difference of 2.30 or a 23% improvement, assuming the COMB PHQ-9 at 48 weeks is 10 [89]. Power will be similar for log10 viral load.

Assessing the implementation context

To examine the implementation context of our study, we will conduct process (during the intervention [90] and summative (post-intervention) qualitative evaluations, consisting of in-depth interviews with site staff and clinicians (n = 64) to gather rich data on site-specific sustainability-related implementation strategies for both COMB and COMBEX. Data saturation typically occurs within twelve interviews, and major themes are present in as few as six [91].

The Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs in Healthcare (Table 2) will guide the interviews [67]. Process evaluation interviews will occur near the mid-point of the intervention period. Probes will focus on design and delivery, negotiating initiative processes, the people involved, and resources. Probes will also examine the acceptability and appropriateness of the additional ERIC strategies used in COMBEX such as the possible use of the depression rating app and clinician text system, and clinician reminders in the manuals and on calls. Summative evaluation interviews at the six-month, post-intervention period will focus on the same constructs but probes will focus on pros and cons to implementation sustainability post-intervention. For example, probes will explore issues related to continuing to provide clinicians with patient depression scale scores, use of the CBT and MMA protocols, sustainability of app use post-study, and ongoing collaboration with team members to ensure fidelity to and sustainment of the evidence-based intervention.

We will audio-record all interviews, using a HIPAA-compliant transcription service, and review transcripts to ensure accuracy. Analysis will employ a directed content analysis, a rapid, deductive qualitative coding method [92]. We will create an a priori coding scheme using codes based on domains from the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs in Healthcare [67]. Directed content analysis allows for inductive, emergent coding and codes will be added to the scheme as they are identified. The interview guide is constructed to code purposively for constructs relevant to outcomes, determined by our sustainability framework. We will conduct preliminary analyses of qualitative data concurrently to data collection so that we can incorporate emergent codes into the coding scheme. Two independent raters will code the interviews using NVivo to organize and manage the data. We will resolve discrepancies through consensus and discussion. Analyses will examine implementation sustainability constructs. In addition, the study design includes variation among study sites in number of YWH served, clinic size, and patient population (e.g., male / female). The restricted randomization will balance the eight sites on these factors for quantitative data, and the qualitative interviews will also assess relationships between these and other site factors on implementation outcomes. Using qualitative reflexive thematic analysis, we will examine codes to identify overarching themes using multiple, detailed steps of group-based analyses [93, 94].

Mixed methods analyses

Our team will conduct an explanatory, sequential, mixed-methods analysis. We will integrate the quantitative implementation and effectiveness data and the qualitative data, to form a more robust analysis of the implementation and sustainment contexts of COMBEX and COMB. Data integration will occur in two ways: (1) through a connecting method, as our survey and trial data links with the qualitative data through our purposive sampling of site staff and clinicians and (2) through a joint display approach to synthesize a correlation between our quantitative data (“low” or “high”) and responder sentiments described in the interview (“positive” or “negative”) aligning with the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs in Healthcare constructs [95, 96].

Two joint displays of integrated data will be produced: a between conditions and a within conditions matrix. Between conditions: We will examine differences in qualitative data on the implementation outcomes of adoption, fidelity, feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness according to condition. Based on initial efficacy trial outcomes, we hypothesize that both COMB and COMBEX will be considered feasible, acceptable, and appropriate. We hypothesize that enhancements in COMBEX will result in improved fidelity and sustainment for sites randomized to the COMBEX condition. Probes related to these constructs and specific to the new ERIC strategies in COMBEX will investigate this hypothesis. Within conditions: If quantitative measures indicate variability between study sites within condition in terms of adoption (e.g., high vs. low coverage of medication adherence-related content) and fidelity (e.g., high vs. low attendance at clinician supervision / consultation calls) or patient PHQ-9 score improvement, we will further examine qualitative data according to those differences. Or, if quantitative measures indicate differences in terms of implementation, we will analyze related qualitative data by site according to their level of implementation.

Discussion

Depression is a common psychiatric condition among YWH, with prevalence as high as 25% in the United States [2–4, 97, 98]. In two prior studies, we have successfully trained clinicians in the measured-care, tailored CBT and MMA approach for YWH (COMB). Despite the successful outcomes at Week 24 and use of implementation strategies, by Week 48 depression rates in COMB had not continued to improve. This protocol paper describes efforts to enhance sustainability of combination treatment via additional implementation strategies (COMBEX) as identified by our previous studies.

For YWH, depression not only reduces their quality of life but also increases their risk for non-adherence to ART. Studies with YWH have found that depression is associated with being less likely to take prescribed HIV medications [99, 100], poorer adherence [98, 99, 101, 102], and failure to maintain adherence [103]. Depression has also been associated with reduced likelihood of viral suppression [98, 104], which can lead to worse health outcomes [49, 105, 106]. Despite several depression treatment trials in adults with HIV, few have examined viral load as an outcome and data is even more limited among youth [20, 22, 107]. Notably, in our previous efficacy trail, viral suppression did not improve in COMB compared to TAU, even though there is a known association between depression, adherence to antiretroviral treatment (ART), and viral load. In an effort to address this, COMBEX consultation calls, manuals, and MMA will include ART adherence reminders and material. Aderence to ART and viral load will be abstracted from the medical records and examined throughout the study period.

Our study design will allow data from process and summative qualitative interviews to address and explore practical and operational issues involved in performing the study in real time. Despite the strengths of this proposed study, there are notable limitations. The sample will be recruited from HIV clinical care sites, thus results may not be generalizable to all YWH. The design of the site-randomized trial will balance participant demographics, however other site-specific characteristics of participants and staff, such as inherent differences in impact of clinicians, may contribute to outcomes.

Conclusions and future directions

We are applying the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs in Healthcare in a Hybrid Type 2 effectiveness-implementation trial to focus specifically on strategies that increase the adoption and sustainment of COMBEX, to create a long-term, effective intervention that results in health benefits for YWH with depression. To our knowledge, this will be the first project to examine implementation outcomes in depression treatment among people youth with HIV and among the few to test the effectiveness of treatment of depression in improving viral load suppression for YWH. This study will inform the broader implementation of this efficacious approach to treatment of depression for YWH by revealing strategies relevant to its sustainment.
