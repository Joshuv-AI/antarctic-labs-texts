From Radiation to Resonance: Ultrasound vs Traditional Imaging in Intensive Care Unit Lung Pathologies
Authors: Neeraj Pal Singh, Amol Trimbakrao Kothekar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Indian J Crit Care Med 2025;29(10):797-798.Keywords: Chest X-ray, Computed tomography, Consolidation, Diagnostic accuracy, Lung ultrasound, Mechanical ventilation, Pleural effusion, Pneumothorax

Lung ultrasound (LUS) has emerged as an essential diagnostic modality, providing rapid, bedside, real-time, accurate, reproducible, and radiation-free imaging for the evaluation of lung pathologies. Compared to chest radiography (CXR).1,2 Lung ultrasound demonstrates superior diagnostic sensitivity, and it also circumvents the logistical limitations of computed tomography (CT), particularly in hemodynamically unstable and mechanically ventilated patients.3

In a prospective study by Tayar et al. involving 110 mechanically ventilated intensive care unit (ICU) patients, LUS and CXR were compared against CT for the diagnosis of lung pathologies.4 Lung ultrasound demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, achieving 100% sensitivity and specificity for pleural effusion and consolidation, as well as 100% sensitivity with 97% specificity for pneumothorax. Agreement with CT was excellent for effusion (κ = 0.803) and pulmonary edema (κ = 0.902). In contrast, CXR exhibited limited sensitivity (55–67%), underscoring the superiority of LUS as a reliable bedside alternative to routine CXR.

This study focused on mechanically ventilated patients, in whom rapid and accurate diagnosis can be life-saving. It adds to the growing body of evidence supporting the diagnostic accuracy of LUS, as initially established by the bedside lung ultrasound in emergency (BLUE) protocol pioneered by Lichtenstein.1,5 Nonetheless, the authors appropriately acknowledge certain limitations, including the absence of a formal sample size calculation, which introduces statistical uncertainty, and the inherent challenges of subjectivity in image interpretation and operator-dependent variability, both of which may affect the study's reproducibility. A significant limitation of the study lies in its definition of consolidation and collapse: Consolidation was characterized by the presence of dynamic air bronchograms, whereas collapse was defined by the presence of static air bronchograms. In clinical practice, however, static air bronchograms—although typically associated with resorptive atelectasis—may also be observed in up to one-third of pneumonia cases, thereby complicating accurate differentiation.6 Finally, although imaging findings informed diagnostic decisions, the study did not systematically assess or report therapeutic interventions guided by LUS or CXR results. Longitudinal follow-up incorporating key clinical outcomes—such as survival, duration of mechanical ventilation, and treatment response—would represent an important direction for future research. Moreover, while all LUS examinations were performed by expert operators trained in accordance with international guidelines, such expertise may not be universally available, which could limit the broader applicability of the findings.

Overall, LUS demonstrates superior diagnostic accuracy compared with CXR in critically ill patients, underscoring its promise as a first-line imaging modality for pulmonary assessment in the ICU. Future efforts may focus on standardizing operator training and conducting high-quality outcome-based studies to determine the extent to which LUS-guided strategies can improve survival and optimize healthcare resource utilization.
