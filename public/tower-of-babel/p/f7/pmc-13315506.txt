Peri-implant soft tissue colonization after osteosynthesis in asymptomatic patients: a prospective study
Authors: Michaela Doležalová Hrubá, Tomáš Zídek, Martin Kloub, Karel Holub, Marek Peml, Jiří Urban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fracture-related infection (FRI) remains a serious complication in orthopedic trauma surgery, with microbiological confirmation based on positive cultures from deep tissue samples. However, the clinical interpretation of positive microbiological findings in asymptomatic patients remains unclear. This prospective study aimed to evaluate the incidence and characteristics of peri-implant soft tissue colonization in patients undergoing routine removal of osteosynthetic material without clinical or radiological signs of FRI. Indications for implant removal surgery included routine removal, prominence of osteosynthetic material, discomfort, removal prior to prosthetic joint implantation or patient request. It is a single-center prospective study. A total of 137 adult patients undergoing implant removal between 01/09/2023 and 30/11/2025 were included. For each patient, five deep tissue samples and the removed implant were collected for microbiological analysis, including sonication fluid culture. Histological evaluation was performed in a subset of cases. Peri-implant colonization was defined as the presence of identical pathogens in at least two tissue samples. All patients underwent routine postoperative clinical follow-up after implant removal surgery. Wound healing and local clinical status were assessed at the time of suture removal, typically 12–14 days postoperatively. In addition, all patients were instructed to return for further clinical evaluation in the event of any wound healing problems, local inflammatory signs or other symptoms involving the operated region. Peri-implant soft tissue colonization was detected in 32.8% of patients, while overall microbiological positivity (including sonication) reached 38.7%. Sonication and tissue cultures demonstrated comparable detection rates, with isolated positivity observed in 5.8% of cases. Histological positivity was rare (2.75%) and showed limited concordance with microbiological findings. Colonization rates varied significantly by anatomical location, with markedly higher prevalence in the shoulder region compared to other sites (70.0 vs. 20.7%, p < 0.001). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, shoulder region location was a strong independent predictor of culture positivity (OR 25.45, 95% CI 8.00–80.65), while male sex was also independently associated with positive cultures (adjusted OR 6.42, 95% Cl 2.11–19.51, p = 0.001). The microbiological spectrum was dominated by low virulence, predominantly polymicrobial flora, with Cutibacterium acnes as the most frequently identified organism. These findings demonstrate that peri-implant colonization is common in clinically asymptomatic patients, particularly in the shoulder region. Shoulder region localization and male sex were independently associated with microbiological positivity. Positive microbiological results should therefore be interpreted with caution and in the context of clinical, radiological and histopathological findings to avoid overdiagnosis of FRI and unnecessary treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00590-026-04858-z.

Introduction

Fracture-related infection (FRI) represents one of the most serious complications in orthopedic trauma surgery and is associated with prolonged antibiotic therapy, repeated revision procedures and impaired functional outcomes [1, 2]. Because clinical presentation may be subtle, particularly in low-grade infections, microbiological confirmation plays a central role in the diagnostic process. According to the international expert consensus definition published in 2018 [3], phenotypically indistinguishable pathogens identified by culture from at least two separate deep tissue samples are one of the confirmatory criteria of FRI [1, 3, 4]. This finding alone is considered sufficient to establish the diagnosis of FRI.

In patients with suspected FRI, microbiological examination is routinely based on collection of multiple deep tissue samples obtained using separate sterile instruments and “no-touch-technique” [4] early during revision surgery, prior to possible contamination [3]. In addition, removed implant should undergo sonication, a technique used to disrupt bacterial biofilm and release adherent microorganisms into sonication fluid for subsequent cultivation. These methods are intended to improve the diagnostic sensitivity of microbial testing, particularly in biofilm-associated infections.

However, several recent studies have demonstrated unexpectedly high rates of bacterial colonization of osteosynthetic material in patients without clinical signs of FRI [5–10]. Positive microbiological findings have been reported particularly in the shoulder region and are frequently associated with low-virulence microorganisms such as Cutibacterium acnes and coagulase-negative staphylococci [7–9, 11]. These observations raise important questions regarding the interpretations of positive microbiological cultures obtained during implant removal procedures, as positive findings may in some cases represent asymptomatic peri-implant colonization rather than clinically relevant infection. Distinguishing contamination, colonization and true low-grade infection therefore remains diagnostically challenging.

Biofilm formation may partially explain the persistence of microorganisms on implanted devices in the absence of clinically manifest infection. Biofilm can be defined as a multicellular microbial community attached to a living or non-living surface and surrounded by a matrix of extracellular polymeric substances produced by bacteria [12, 13].Bacteria embedded within a biofilm demonstrate reduced metabolic activity together with increased resistance to host immune response and antimicrobial therapy [14, 15]. This low-virulence biofilm-associated state may contribute to asymptomatic peri-implant colonization and complicate the interpretation of positive microbiological findings obtained during implant removal procedures.

Although previous studies have reported asymptomatic bacterial colonization of orthopedic implants, currently available evidence remains limited and heterogenous, while the relationship between tissue cultures, sonication findings and histopathological examination in clinically asymptomatic patients has not been sufficiently clarified. The present study was therefore designed not only to confirm previous observations of asymptomatic peri-implant colonization, but also to further characterize its anatomical distribution, microbiological profile, and concordance with histopathological findings in a prospective cohort of clinically asymptomatic patients undergoing routine implant removal.

Materials and methods

Patients

A total of 137 patients who underwent routine removal of osteosynthetic material were included in this prospective single-center study between 01/09/2023 and 30/11/2025. The study adhered to all ethical standards and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975). Approval was obtained from the hospital ethics committee (Ethics committee of Nemocnice České Budějovice, a.s., reference number 113/2023). All patients provided written informed consent for implant removal, tissue sampling and implant examination. The study was not registered.

Only patients older than 18 years with no clinical or radiographic signs of FRI were included in the study. As clinical confirmatory criteria of FRI were considered fistula, wound dehiscence and pus secretion, as clinical suggestive criteria were considered pain, redness, swelling, fever, new onset of join effusion, and persistent, increasing, or new wound secretion. As radiological suggestive criteria were considered osteolysis, implant loosening, sequestrum formation, malunion, nonunion and increased periosteal reaction. Inflammatory markers were not systematically obtained. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of wound healing issues, FRI or pseudoarthrosis, (2) minimally invasive removal (e.g. stab incision with no possibility to gain representative tissue samples) (3) inability/unwillingness to sign informed consent, (4) cases, where sample collection protocol was not fully followed. Study population

Indications for implant removal surgery included routine removal (e.g. hookplate after AC joint stabilization, temporary arthrodesis), prominence of osteosynthetic material under the skin, discomfort (e.g. distal fibula plate during footwear use), removal prior to prosthetic joint implantation or patient request.

Follow-up

All patients underwent routine postoperative clinical follow-up after implant removal surgery. Wound healing and local clinical status were assessed at the time of suture removal, typically 12–14 days postoperatively. In addition, all patients were instructed to return for further clinical evaluation in the event of any wound healing problems, local inflammatory signs or other symptoms involving the operated region. During the available postoperative follow-up period, no patient required further evaluation or treatment for clinically manifest FRI involving the operated region. The sampling set and sterile containers are shown in Supplementary material 1.

Material collection and tissue examination

For each patient, five deep tissue samples were collected during osteosynthetic material removal surgery. Each sample was obtained using a unique sterile set of instruments and a no-touch-technique from the site under or around the implant (preferably callus, periosteum, and surrounding scar tissue) and immediately placed into individual sterile specimen container before being transported to the Department of Microbiology laboratory. Tissue sampling was performed in accordance with the recommendations of the international society. There was no antibiotic prophylaxis administered before the removal surgery.

Upon arrival at the microbiology laboratory, all tissue specimens were inoculated onto culture media. Tissue samples were cultured on blood agar and incubated at 37 °C for aerobic cultures—incubation was performed under two different conditions—atmospheric air and an atmosphere with increased CO2 content. For anaerobic cultures, anaerobic blood agar (Bio-rad, “Schaedler type”) and selective anaerobic blood agar for Gram-negative pathogens (Bio-Rad, “Schaedler WS type”) were used. Enriched blood agar with broth (Viamar) was additionally used as an enrichment medium to promote bacterial growth. Routine culture examinations were evaluated after 48 h, whereas extended cultures were incubated for up to 14 days, with readings performed at 7 and 14 days.

Removed implants were placed intraoperatively into sterile containers of appropriate size and immediately sent to the microbiology laboratory for sonication analysis. Sonication was performed according to the Trampuz-type protocol. Sterile Ringer’s lactate solution was added to the implant in a minimum volume of 250 mL, in case of larger implants, additional solution was added to ensure complete implant immersion. Sonication was performed using an ultrasonic bath (KL K12, Fisher Scientific) at a frequency of 40 ± 2 kHz for 5 min. The resulting sonication fluid was subsequently centrifuged, and 0.5 mL aliquots were inoculated onto culture media. Five culture media were used for each implant: two aerobic agars, two anaerobic agars and one enrichment broth medium using the same culture media as for tissue samples cultivation and incubated as described for tissue cultures. Sonication fluid cultures were subsequently incubated for up to 14 days.

Following incubation, microbiological evaluation of all positive cultures was performed. Species-level identification was determined using matrix-assisted laser desorption-ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Further differentiation of coagulase-negative staphylococci was based on antibiotic susceptibility profiles (antibiograms). Clonal identity analysis of Cutibacterium acnes isolates was not performed.

In the majority of cases (79.6%), a corresponding sample for histological examination was obtained to assess the presence of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) per high-power field (HPF). The number of samples was limited by the lower availability of this method. Histopathological specimen was immediately placed in a sterile container and sent to the Department of Pathology laboratory. Tissue samples were fixed in formaldehyde for minimum of 24 h, followed by dehydration using ethanol, embedding in paraffin, and processing into sections. All samples were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Afterwards, all samples were examined microscopically by trained histopathologist, who was blinded to the microbiological results. According to standard procedures, the number of neutrophils was evaluated in five high-power fields at 400x magnification. Based on the number of neutrophils, PMNs infiltration was classified into 3 categories: (1) 0 PMNs/HPF, (2) 1–5 PMNs/HPF and 3) > 5 PMNs/HPF. The positivity cutoff was set > 5 PMN/HPF, according to the study published by Morgenstern in 2018 [16].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described using the median and interquartile range, while categorical variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies. Peri-implant soft tissue colonization was defined as the presence of bacteria in at least two of five tissue samples obtained during implant removal and was analyzed as a binary outcome. Differences in colonization rates between anatomical locations were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on expected cell counts. Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were performed using non-parametric tests.

Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate potential predictors of peri-implant soft tissue colonization, including shoulder region localization, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, diabetes mellitus, and time to implant removal. Variables considered clinically relevant were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model to assess independent predictors of colonization. Adjusted odds ratios (Ors) with 95% confidence intervals (Cls) were calculated.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF).

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.7.31.0 (The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). A two-sided-p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the exploratory nature of the study. The study cohort consisted of all consecutive eligible patient undergoing implant removal during the predefined study period.

Missing data

No significant missing data were present for the variables included in the statistical analyses.

Results

A total of 137 patients undergoing removal of osteosynthetic material were included in this prospective study. The median age of the study population was 48 years (interquartile range 36–60), and 72 patients (52.6%) were male. Osteosynthetic material was removed from various anatomical locations, most commonly from the acromioclavicular joint and the ankle region.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic, clinical and implant-related characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Patients with positive cultures were younger and more frequently male compared with culture-negative patients. The culture-positive group also demonstrated a markedly shorter median time to implant removal. No substantial differences were observed between groups with respect to BMI, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, chronic immunosuppression, or history of open fracture. Plate osteosynthesis represented the predominant implant type in both groups. Distinct differences in fracture pattern and anatomical localizations were observed between groups, with shoulder-associated injuries (particularly Rockwood type V injuries) being more frequent among culture-positive patients, whereas ankle fracture predominanted in the culture-negative cohort.

Sample positivity

Overall microbiological and histopathological findings are summarized in Table 2. Peri-implant soft tissue colonization, defined as the presence of bacteria in at least two of five tissue samples obtained during implant removal surgery, was detected in 45 patients (32.8%). Sonication fluid culture positivity was likewise observed in 45 patients (32.8%). Overall microbial findings are summarized in Table 2.

Concordant positivity of both tissue cultures and sonication fluid cultures was identified in 37 patients (27.0%). Discordant microbiological findings were observed in 8 patients (5.8%). In total, 53 patients (38.7%) were considered microbiologically positive based on either tissue culture or sonication positivity.

Histopathological examination was performed in 109 patients and demonstrated positivity in three cases (2.75%). The mean neutrophil count in positive histological specimen was 15.7 PMN/HPF. Only one patient demonstrated isolated histopathological positivity without concurrent microbiological positivity.

Distribution of anatomical localities

Detailed anatomical distribution of implant removal procedures and colonization rates are summarized in Table 3 and illustrated in Fig. 2. Osteosynthetic material was removed from a wide range of anatomical locations. The most frequently represented sites were the acromioclavicular joint (n = 33) and the ankle/distal tibia region (n = 33), followed by the proximal humerus (n = 11) and proximal femur (n = 10). Other locations included the clavicle, olecranon, forearm, wrist and hand, femoral diaphysis, patella, proximal tibia, tibial diaphysis, and foot.

Peri-implant soft tissue colonization exhibited marked variability across anatomical regions. High colonization rates were observed in the acromioclavicular joint (81.8%) and clavicle (83.3%), whereas no colonization was detected in the ankle region, patella, proximal tibia, hand, or wrist. Intermediate colonization rates were identified in the femoral diaphysis (25%) and tibial diaphysis (33%), as well as in the foot (11%) and forearm (25%).

Risk factor analysis

Results of the univariate logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 4. Shoulder localization and male sex were significantly associated with peri-implant soft tissue colonization. Patients undergoing implant removal in the shoulder region showed substantially higher odds of positive tissue cultures compared with non-shoulder localizations (OR 17.95, 95% Cl 7.35–43.86, p < 0.001). Similarly, male sex was associated with increased odds of culture positivity (OR 6.22, 95% Cl 2.68–14.44, < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for BMI, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, or time to implant removal.

Results of the multivariable logistic regression are summarized in Table 5. Shoulder localization remained independently associated with peri-implant colonization after adjustment for sex, BMI, smoking status, diabetes mellitus and time to implant removal. Microbiological spectrum of peri-implant colonization

The microbiological spectrum of positive samples is summarized in Table 6. Among colonized patients (n = 45), polymicrobial growth was observed in 66.2% of cases. Cutibacterium acnes was the most frequently detected microorganism, identified in 51.9% of cases, however, only 40% of cultures containing Cutibacterium acnes were polymicrobial.

The second most frequently isolated microorganism was Staphylococcus epidermidis (19.5%), predominantly detected in polymicrobial cultured (86.7%), followed by other Gram-positive, coagulase-negative staphylococci. In contrast, Staphylococcus aureus was identified only in one case, and exclusively as part of a polymicrobial culture.

Discussion

In this study, 137 patients underwent removal of osteosynthetic material for various indications, including routine removal, patient preference, or implant prominence. None of the included patients exhibited clinical or radiological symptoms of fracture-related infection (FRI) prior to implant removal surgery.

One of the most important findings of the present study is the substantial discrepancy between microbiological positivity and histopathological evidence of infection. Although 38.7% of patients demonstrated positive microbiological findings, histopathological positivity was identified only rarely and showed minimal concordance with tissue culture or sonication results. This observation raises the possibility that at least a proportion of positive microbiological findings may reflect asymptomatic colonization or perioperative contamination rather than clinically relevant infection.

The interpretation of positive cultures in clinically asymptomatic patients remains challenging, particularly when low-virulence microorganisms such as Cutibacterium acnes or coagulase-negative staphylococci are isolated. These microorganisms are well recognized component of normal skin flora [17] and may be introduced during implant insertion, persist within biofilm structures, or contaminate samples during removal and laboratory processing. At the same time, these organisms are also established causes of low-grade orthopedic implant-associated infections [18]. Distinguishing contamination, colonization and true low-grade infection therefore remains diagnostically difficult and likely cannot rely on microbiological findings alone.

The overall microbiological positivity observed in the present study is comparable to previously published cohorts of asymptomatic implant removal patients [5–9]. However, direct comparison with studies investigating confirmed or suspected FRI should be interpreted cautiously, as infected cohort generally demonstrate substantially higher bacterial burden and different pathogen profiles. Positive tissue cultures were detected in 32.8% ofpatient, which is comparable to previously published studies [5, 6]. The number of positive tissue samples and positive sonication fluid samples was identical, which is contrast to several studies reporting higher sensitivity of sonication fluid compared with conventional tissue cultures [19, 20].

Concordance between tissue culture and sonication positivity was observed in the majority of positive cases, however, isolated positivity of either tissue cultures or sonication fluid was identified in subset patients. These discordant findings may reflect differences in diagnostic sensitivity between methods [21, 22], low bacterial burden or contamination during sampling and implant handling.

In our study, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis was not performed, however, PCR has been reported to significantly increase bacterial detection [23], particularly for coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS), and Gram-negative bacilli [24]. In contrast, other authors have suggested that sonication fluid culture may be more sensitive than PCR in patients without clinical signs of infection [7].

The most colonized was the shoulder area—AC joint (81.7%), clavicle (83.3%) and proximal humerus (27%), which correspond with studies already performed [8, 9, 11].

In addition to the descriptive analysis, shoulder region localization emerged as the strongest predictor of peri-implant colonization in both univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. After adjustment for sex, BMI, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, and time to implant removal, patients undergoing implant removal in the shoulder region had more than 25-fold higher odds of culture positivity compared with non-shoulder localizations. Male sex was also independently associated with positive cultures, while higher BMI demonstrated a weak inverse association. These findings support the hypothesis that anatomical locations play a major role in the occurrence of asymptomatic peri-implant colonization and suggest that patient-related factors may additionally contribute to microbiological positivity.

The markedly higher colonization rates observed in the shoulder region may be explained by several anatomical and microbiological factors. First, the anatomical proximity to sebaceous-gland-rich skin, which are known reservoir of Cutibacterium acnes [8, 9, 11, 25]. This microorganism is capable of persisting in deeper dermal structure despite standard preoperative skin preparation [11], which may contribute to intraoperative contamination or long-term peri-implant colonization.

Second, Cutibacterium acnes demonstrated a strong affinity for implanted biomaterials as a pronounced ability to form biofilm on metallic surfaces [26–28]. Biofilm formation facilitates bacterial persistence in a low-virulence state without necessarily inducing clinically manifest FRI. This may partially explain the high proportion of asymptomatic positive cultures observed in the shoulder region in the present study.

A low incidence of tissue culture positivity was observed in the foot region (11%), including the calcaneus, which contrasts with the findings reported by Machuga [29], who described a 33% psitivity rate in calcaneal samples, frequently associated with polymicrobial flora.

No peri-implant soft tissue colonization was detected in the ankle region, representing a relatively large subgroup of patients. This finding is consistent with results reported by Both [9], who observed zero positivity in samples obtained from the fibula. Similarly, no positive tissue cultures were identified in the patella or proximal tibia, which contrasts with the study by Fuchs [5], but in agreement with findings reported by Nguyen [30], who observed no positive soft tissue cultures form the knee region following endoprosthesis removal. No positive cultures were detected in the wrist or hand region either.

The most frequently isolated microorganism in our cohort was Cutibacterium acnes (51.8% of detected pathogens), particularly in the area of the AC joint and clavicle. Akgun [8] reported 67.7% ample positivity for Cutibacterium acnes in shoulder region, obtained during revision surgeries for shoulder stiffness. Both [9] similarly described high Cutibacterium positivity in clavicular region from patients without clinical signs of infection. Torrens [31] further reported the presence of Cutibacterium acnes in the surgical field even at the end of primary surgery, although only a subset of patients subsequently developed FRI, potentially reflecting strain-specific differences in virulence and behavior [26].

Another frequently detected microorganism was Staphylococcus epidermidis (19.5%), predominantly identified in polymicrobial cultures (86.7% cases). Other coagulase-negative staphylococci were also detected. These findings are consistent with those reported by Previ [6], who identified CoNS in 47% f implant removal and sonication cases, and by Fuchs [5], who reported CoNS positivity in 46% of amples.

Staphylococcus aureus was identified only in one patient, which differs from findings reported by Esteban [32], who detected Staphylococcus aureus in 15% o sonicated intramedullary nail samples. No Pseudomonas species were isolated in our cohort, which contrasts with results reported by Previ [6].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although patients underwent routine postoperative clinical follow-up, the study was not designed to assess the long-term clinical significance of peri-implant colonization or the subsequent development of FRI. Therefore, the clinical relevance of asymptomatic microbiological positivity remains uncertain.

Second, owing to the observational single-center design and relatively limited number of positive culture events, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Although multivariable logistic regression was performed and no evidence of multicollinearity was observed, the number of outcome events remained relatively small in relation to the number of evaluated predictors. Therefore, the magnitude of the adjusted effect estimates, particularly for shoulder region, should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Third, molecular microbiological methods and strain typing were not performed. Clonal identity of Cutibacterium acnes isolates was therefore not assessed, while differentiation of coagulase-negative staphylococci was based solely on antibiotic susceptibility profiles. Consequently, it was not possible to distinguish true persistent colonization from intraoperative contamination to evaluate potential clonality or repeatedly isolated microorganisms.

Finally, the study cohort included heterogenous group of implants and anatomical localizations. Because implant type was strongly associated with anatomical region, particularly in shoulder procedures, implant-specific effects could not be reliably separated from localization-associated factors in the multivariable analysis.

Clinical implications and future research

The findings of the present study have important implications for the interpretation of microbiological results obtained during implant removal procedures. Positive tissue cultures or sonication findings in clinically asymptomatic patients, particularly in the shoulder region, do not necessarily indicate clinically relevant FRI. These results should therefore always be interpreted in the context of clinical presentation, radiological findings, and histopathological examination, in order to avoid overdiagnosis and unnecessary antimicrobial or surgical treatment.

At present, the findings of the present study should not be interpreted as evidence supporting modification of the current FRI diagnostic criteria. Rather, they highlight the need for more precise interpretation of positive microbiological findings in clinically asymptomatic patients, particularly when low-virulence microorganisms such as Cutibacterium acnes are isolated in the shoulder region. Long-term prospective studies with systematic clinical follow-up are required to determine whether asymptomatic peri-implant colonization represents a biologically inactive state, transient contamination, or a potential precursor of subsequent FRI.

The present findings also raise an important question regarding interpretation of the current 2018 FRI consensus, particularly the confirmatory criterion requiring identical pathogens in at least two deep tissue samples. In anatomical regions with high rates of asymptomatic colonization (especially shoulder and Cutibacterium acnes detection), this criterion may potentially demonstrate lower specificity when interpreted isolated. However, the present study was not designed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy or clinical outcomes associated with positive cultures, so that these findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting modification of the current FRI criteria. Instead, these findings highlight the need for future studies analyzing whether anatomical location, microorganism virulence and histopathological findings should be incorporated into more context-specific interpretation of microbiological findings in suspected FRI.

From a clinical perspective, management of asymptomatic patients with positive cultures after routine implant removal remains uncertain. In the absence of clinical, radiological, or histopathological evidence of infection, isolated microbiological positivity may not necessarily justify prolonged antibiotic treatment or repeated surgical revisions. However, careful postoperative surveillance may be appropriate, especially in patients with multiple positive cultures involving low-virulence bacteria.

The present findings may also support future development of risk-stratified sampling strategies during routine implant removal surgeries. Factors such as shoulder region localization and male sex were independently associated with higher microbiological positivity and may influence the pretest probability of asymptomatic colonization. Nevertheless, further confirmation studies are necessary before selective sampling approaches can be recommended in routine practice.

Finally, the potential implications of asymptomatic colonization for future reconstructive surgeries, for example joint replacement, remain unclear. In particular, the clinical significance of previous Cutibacterium acnes colonization prior to shoulder arthroplasty warrants further investigation, as persistent low-grade colonization may influence the risk of periprosthetic joint infection.

The high prevalence of Cutibacterium acnes and coagulase-negative staphylococci further highlight the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing contamination, colonization, and true low-grade infection in orthopedic trauma surgery. Future studies should focus on the long-term clinical significance of asymptomatic peri-implant colonization and its potential association with subsequent FRI. In addition, molecular microbiological techniques, including strain typing and biofilm characterization, may help clarify whether positive cultures represent persistent colonization, contamination, or clinically relevant infection. Larger multicenter studies with standardized sampling protocols are also needed to validate the present findings across different anatomical regions and implant types.

Conclusion

Peri-implant microbial positivity was frequently detected in clinically asymptomatic patients undergoing routine removal of osteosynthetic material, particularly in the shoulder region. Shoulder region localization and male sex were independently associated with microbiological positivity. Cutibacterium acnes and coagulase-negative staphylococci represented the predominant microorganisms, while histopathological evidence of infection remained rare and demonstrated limited concordance with microbiological findings.

The present findings raise the possibility that at least a proportion of positive microbiological findings obtained during osteosynthetic material removal surgeries may reflect asymptomatic colonization or contamination rather than clinically relevant infection. However, further prospective studies with long-term follow-up are required before these observations can influence current interpretation of FRI diagnostic criteria. Until then, positive microbiological findings in clinically asymptomatic patients should always be interpreted in the context of overall clinical presentation, imaging findings and histopathological examination, in order to avoid overdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment.
