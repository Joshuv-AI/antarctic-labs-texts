Effects of uncertainty stress on attentional control in college students: mediating effect of problematic mobile phone use and moderating effect of expressive suppression
Authors: Jingyu Zhao, Dongjun Li, Kun Li, Jinli Wang, Jiahui Zhao, Shouqi Li, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCollege students are susceptible to developing uncertainty stress. Uncertainty raises a range of issues that may affect attentional control. The aim of this study was to examine the pathways and moderators of the relationship between uncertainty stress and attentional control in college students.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 408 college students by completing questionnaires on problematic mobile phone use, expression repression, uncertainty stress, and attentional control. Moderated mediated effects were examined using the PROCESS plug-in integrated into SPSS.ResultsThere was a significantly negative correlation found between uncertainty stress and attentional control. This link may have been mediated by problematic mobile phone use. The effects of uncertainty stress on problematic mobile phone use and attentional control were mitigated by expressive suppression. Specifically, the direct impairing effect of uncertainty stress on attentional control and the indirect impairing effect through problematic mobile phone use were stronger for college students with higher levels of expressive suppression.ConclusionThis study enriches our understanding of how uncertainty stress impairs attentional control. To support college students in maintaining attentional control, educators should provide particular attention to those with greater levels of uncertainty stress, particularly those with higher levels of problematic mobile phone use and expressive suppression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04393-w.Keywords: College students, Uncertainty stress, Attentional control, Problematic mobile phone use, Expressive suppression

Background

The feeling of uncertainty that people have regarding circumstances or events is the root cause of uncertainty stress. Studies have shown that uncertainty is a primary stressor for college students [1, 2]. These stresses arise from both anxiety about academics, concerns about employment, and confusion about the direction of development [3], as well as from the tension between an awakening sense of self and a lack of social awareness [4]. Compared to stresses arising from relationships and family, college students are more sensitive to and vulnerable to uncertainty stress [5, 6]. Such stressors frequently have negative consequences for students’ mental health and emotional regulation [7, 8].

High psychological stress levels have been associated with attentional control disruptions, according to prior studies [9, 10]. Attention usually refers to the pointing and focusing of mental activity on certain objects. There are different understandings within the academic community regarding the concept of attentional control. Generally speaking, attentional control is the ability of an individual to consciously activate, focus, and maintain attention on a memorized representation in the face of irrelevant information distractions [11]. Combining results from neurobiology and cognitive psychology, attentional control is based on electrical activity in loops composed of neural tissues such as the frontal and parietal lobes [10], complete with competing planning mechanisms and attentional monitoring mechanisms operating together [12]. Attentional control is an important cognitive ability [13]. For college students, it directly supports academic achievement, career readiness, and daily adaptive functioning [14, 15]. Research shows that impairments in attentional control are significantly positively correlated with depression and sleep disorders [16], and are a cognitive mechanism underlying anxiety disorders in adolescents [17]. Therefore, paying attention to attentional control is of great significance for the personal growth, mental health, and long-term development of college students. Attention is a finite cognitive resource, according to Resource Limitation Theory [18, 19]. Chronic stress may cause overconsumption of resource, which would change attentional control [20]. Specifically, chronically stressed individuals exhibit longer response times, lower accuracy, and reduced attentional control than individuals who rarely experience stress. The evidence suggests that individuals under stress are more susceptible to the external environment and have less control over attention [21]. Another study has indicated that impaired attentional control may further exacerbate stress sensitivity [22]. These results suggest that there may be a reciprocal causation between stress and attentional control. This interdependent dynamic causal cycle often requires longitudinal studies that account for temporal dynamics. This study, based on the Resource Limitation Theory, considers uncertainty stress as a precursor to cognitive resource depletion and preliminarily verifies its pathway to maladaptive compensatory behaviors, providing a basis for subsequent longitudinal research [23]. This study hypothesized accordingly that the accumulation of uncertainty stress, a major stressor for college students, would impair their attentional control.

The accumulation of psychological stress is an important source of problematic mobile phone use (PMPU) [24, 25]. According to the resource limitation theory and previous research findings, PMPU is a common short-term coping strategy used to avoid the cognitive resource impairment caused by stress: College students often use their mobile phones to distract themselves from academic or employment anxiety, but this behavior can escalate into PMPU and further deplete the limited attention resources [26]. Psychological experiments have confirmed this finding: researchers have found that PMPU can lead to deficits in individuals’ cognitive and behavioral self-control [27]. These deficits, namely, include impaired attentional control. Neuroimaging evidence provides biological support for these results. It has been noted that PMPU users are less efficient at recruiting neural network resources for attentional control and exhibit greater distractibility during high attentional control tasks [28]. The same conclusions were reached from Observations of event-related potentials (ERPs) [29]. It is undeniable that PMPU impairs attentional control. These results support the detrimental effects of PMPU on attentional control. Based on this, we hypothesized that college students’ uncertainty stress may have impaired their attentional control through enhanced PMPU. That is, PMPU mediates the relationship between uncertainty stress and attentional control.

The mediating effects described above are likely to be moderated by college students’ expressive suppression. Expressive suppression, a common tactic for controlling emotional reactions, involves inhibiting emotional expressive behaviors that are occurring or about to occur. Unlike another emotion regulation strategy with the characteristic of antecedent-focused regulation, namely cognitive reappraisal, expressive suppression occurs after the emotional response has already been generated and belongs to response-focused regulation. It has clear observable behavioral manifestations and is easy to identify and evaluate in daily scenarios such as classroom interactions and peer relationships. Therefore, expressive suppression has broader practical applications [30]. According to Gross’s model of emotion regulation processes [31], expressive suppression reduces emotionally expressive behaviors but does not reduce the psychological experience of emotions, and may even enhance physiological responses to emotions. Expressive suppression builds on emotional self-monitoring, which occupies cognitive resources that are homologous to those of behavioral and attentional control [32]. When expression inhibition is weakened, the nervous system will remove part of the occupation of cognitive resources, and the resources for behavioral control, such as mobile phone use and attentional control, will be more abundant. In this sense, the reinforcing effects of uncertainty stress on PMPU and the impairing effects on attentional control may be less pronounced in college students with low expression suppression, and more pronounced in individuals with high expression suppression. This study hypothesizes that the mediating effect of uncertainty stress via PMPU on their attentional control is moderated by college students’ expression suppression (Fig. 1).

Moderated mediation model

In summary, this study constructed a moderated mediation model based on Resource Limitation Theory to examine the mediating role of PMPU in the relationship between uncertainty stress and attentional control in college students, and the moderating effect of expression inhibition on this mediation [19].

Methods

Participants

The study samples were obtained through convenience sampling. The surveys were conducted at a university in Jilin Province, China. We randomly selected 10 classrooms from the school’s classroom scheduling system, covering different majors and grades, and invited all students present in these classrooms to participate in the survey. Inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] enrolled college students [2], age ≥ 18 years, and [3] no diagnosed mental illness. The respondents received the questionnaire in paper form. They all signed an informed consent form before completing it. Using the following sample size calculation formula, the minimum sample size was calculated to be 385. Assuming an invalidity rate of 10%, the number of questionnaires to be distributed should exceed 424 [33].

Measure

Psychological stressor measurement questionnaire

It was adapted from the Social Stress Stimulus Events Scale developed by Yang et al. [34]. The uncertainty stress dimension of it was selected for this study. It contains 4 items, including “I don’t know how to achieve my goals”, and is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with the total score of the dimension being the sum of the item scores, with higher scores indicating higher levels of uncertainty stress. The Cronbach’s alpha for uncertainty stress in this study was .78, indicating acceptable reliability [35]. The validity was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and the model fit well (χ²/df = 3.73, RMSEA = .07, CFI = 0.99), indicating acceptable validity [36].

Self-rating questionnaire for adolescent problematic mobile phone use (SQAPMPU)

The SQAPMPU was developed by Tao et al. in 2013 [37]. The questionnaire contains 13 items, including “I always feel that I don’t spend enough time on my mobile phone”, which reflect adolescents’ desire for mobile phones, withdrawal symptoms, and physical and psychological effects. It is scored on a 5-point Likert scale. A higher overall score indicates a higher degree of PMPU. The questionnaire used in this investigation has an excellent reliability rate of 0.91 Cronbach’s alpha [35]. The CFA model fits well (χ²/df = 4.57, RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.91), indicating acceptable validity [36].

Emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ)

The ERQ was developed by Gross and John in 2003 [30]. Wang et al. translated it into Chinese, and the local revision was made in 2007 [38]. The dimension of “expression suppression” was included in this questionnaire, which consisted of 4 items, including “I don’t show my emotions”. A 7-point Likert scale was used, in which respondents were asked to answer each question from “completely disagree” [1] to “completely agree” [7] with respect to their own situation. The higher the total score, the higher the level of expression suppression. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.83, indicating good reliability [35]. The CFA model fits well (χ²/df = 4.12, RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.99), indicating acceptable validity [36].

Attentional control scale (ACS)

Derryberry and Reed et al. [39] created the Attention Control Scale (ACS), a self-report questionnaire used to measure attentional control. It is graded on a 4-point Likert scale and has 20 items, such as “It is difficult for me to carry on two conversations at the same time.” In this study, the questionnaire demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of. 98 [35]. The CFA model fits well (χ²/df = 2.85, RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 1.00), indicating acceptable validity [36].

Procedure

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (IRB: CCZYFYLL-SQ-2020-030) and conducted following the ethical standards for research on humans in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [40]. A paper questionnaire was distributed and collected by the researcher from college students. Incentive measures were not provided to the participants in this study. All students participated voluntarily and were fully informed of the research purpose, the commitment to confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time.

Statistical methods

SPSS 21.0 was used for data entry, descriptive statistics, and bivariate correlation analysis. The PROCESS 3.2 plug-in for SPSS was used to conduct tests of mediation and moderated effects. The indirect effect is calculated using the product method of path coefficients (a × b), where a is the coefficient of the independent variable (uncertainty stress) on the mediating variable (PMPU), and b is the coefficient of the mediating variable on the dependent variable (attentional control) after controlling for the independent variable. Accepted if the 95% bias-corrected Bootstrap CI does not include 0 based on 5,000 resamples, regardless of the significance of the direct effect [41]. For a detailed analysis of the code, please refer to Supplementary Material 1.

Descriptive statistics results for variables

In this study, 435 questionnaires were distributed, and all were returned. 408 respondents (307 females, 101 males) provided valid questionnaires. The sample size was verified for validity using G*Power 3.1. When the effect size f2 is greater than or equal to 0.05 and the Power (1 - β, type II error rate) is greater than 0.80, the sample has sufficient power [42]. They were 19.50 years old on average with a standard deviation of 1.07. Their grades were as follows: 131 freshmen, 122 sophomores, 113 juniors, and 42 seniors. Invalid questionnaires were defined as follows: (1) incomplete responses (n = 15); (2) selecting the same response for all items (n = 12).

Moderated mediation

According to the statistical method proposed by Wen et al. [43], in a test of mediating effects with moderation, the mediating effect is tested first, and then the moderating variable is added to test its moderating effect.

In the first step, a structural equation model was constructed with uncertainty stress as the independent variable, attentional control as the dependent variable, and PMPU use as the mediating variable. The model was tested using the PROCESS (Model 4).

The findings are presented in Table 2. In the separate paths, uncertainty stress positively predicted problematic PMPU use and negatively predicted attentional control. When both were included in the model, uncertainty stress and problematic PMPU use significantly and inversely predicted attentional control. All paths were significant, indicating that the mediating effect held.

To more clearly explain the nature of the interaction between uncertainty stress and expressive suppression, we grouped expressive suppression into high and low levels based on one standard deviation above and below the mean, conducted a simple slope test, and plotted the simple effect curves (Fig. 2a and 2b).

Figure 2a and 2b show, respectively, that when the level of expressive suppression was low, elevated uncertainty stress had no significant facilitating effect on PMPU (β = 0.11, t = 0.57, p = .566, 95% confidence interval [-0.26, 0.48]). Discussion

College students frequently experience a buildup of uncertainty stress [1]. Uncertainty stress may affect attentional control [21]. Dysregulation of attentional control can interfere with their learning process of knowledge and practical skills [14, 44]. The results of this study suggest that uncertainty stress impairs attentional control, and this impairment is further exacerbated by enhancing PMPU. The effects of uncertainty stress on both could be moderated by expressive suppression.

Relationship between uncertainty stress and attentional control

According to this study, college students’ attentional control was negatively predicted by uncertainty stress. This finding is consistent with earlier studies on how psychological stress reduces attentional control [45]. This finding supports the resource model of attentional control [19]. The accumulation of uncertainty stress in college students in antecedent situations is long-term. Neural circuits automatically allocate substantial attention to the processing of uncertainty stress in the experience, exploration, and resistance of ambiguity, thereby diminishing attentional control. Psychological stress alters humoral variables associated with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, disrupting frontal brain function and impairing an individual’s ability to regulate emotion and cognition [46]. These findings imply that college students’ attentional control can be improved by relieving their uncertainty stress.

Mediating role of PMPU

The present study identified PMPU as an exogenous mediating mechanism linking uncertainty stress and attentional control in college students. Previous research has demonstrated that psychological stress significantly and positively predicts an individual’s PMPU use [47]. This study focuses on uncertainty stress among college students. College students will use a variety of strategies to gather information and explore answers to cope with uncertainties in their lives, studies, and professional development. Mobile phones are the most frequently used tool due to their superior portability and resourcefulness, thus increasing the propensity for problematic use [48].

Simultaneously, the findings suggest that uncertainty stress in college students impairs attentional control by facilitating PMPU. This result is consistent with Resource Limitation Theory [24, 25, 27]. Attentional control requires necessary cognitive resources to support it. High PMPU behaviors triggered by high uncertainty stress further reduce the efficiency of neurological cognitive resource invocation, impairing the resources for attentional control and thus causing attentional control to diminish [28]. Thus, college students who are more affected by uncertainty stress are more likely to have PMPU behaviors and thus have a higher risk of impaired attentional control. From an intervention perspective, in addition to de-escalating uncertainty stress in college students, attention should be paid to their PMPU. More achievements have been made in PMPU interventions in recent years, such as exercise interventions, positive thinking, and personalized feedback [49, 50]. Relevant educators should select appropriate intervention methods for the specific situations of college students to avoid impairments in attentional control.

Moderating role of expressive suppression

The results of the present study support the hypothesis that expressive suppression acts as a moderating variable in the mediation model described above. That is, expression suppression moderated the pathways by which uncertainty stress affects PMPU and attentional control. Specifically, uncertainty stress facilitated PMPU more strongly and impaired attentional control more severely in individuals with higher levels of expression suppression relative to those with low expression suppression. Expressive suppression, while effective in controlling and reducing the expression of emotional behaviours, does not reduce the frequency of emotional experiences [30]. Also in the face of stress from uncertainty, high expressive suppression individuals tend to do their best to hide their emotional reactions and keep these waves deep inside, which in turn contributes to the accumulation of stress, which in turn enhances the depleting effects of stress on cognition and behaviour [51]. Therefore, they show increased PMPU and diminished attentional control. In addition, individuals with persistently high expressive suppression are more likely to be in distress with nowhere to express it, and are therefore more likely to resort to maladaptive coping styles, such as arousal and avoidance, to escape from it [52]. The diversionary entertainment provided by mobile phones in the virtual online world helps individuals to temporarily escape distress [53]. Therefore, PMPU is more prominent in highly expressive suppression individuals. This result suggests that concerned educators should pay attention to the style of emotion regulation strategies when correcting college students for PMPU and attentional control. Individuals who tend to expressive suppression should be helped to try more emotion regulation strategies to encourage them to express their emotions and improve their high expressive suppression.

Shortcomings and outlook

The present study incorporated PMPU into a model of attentional control among college students under the influence of uncertain psychological factors and found that expressive suppression moderates the model. The findings provide a possible new perspective for understanding the underlying mechanisms of attentional control. Yet some shortcomings remain here. Although the cross-sectional research design of this study is based on sound theoretical constructs, it is insufficient to establish causal relationships between variables. Especially, there may be reciprocal causation between variables that are difficult to verify through cross-sectional data. Future research should adopt longitudinal tracking designs with three waves or more, construct cross-lagged mediation models, and simultaneously test bidirectional causal paths, to more comprehensively reveal the dynamic interaction mechanisms between variables. The study used a self-reported ACS, which may be affected by social expectation bias or subjective perception differences. Future research plans to combine ACS with objective attention tasks when conditions permit, to comprehensively evaluate attentional control, reduce reliance on self-reported data, and enhance the universality of the results. Additionally, given the study’s practical application considerations, only the observable, interventionable expressive suppression in the path was analyzed among emotion regulation strategies, whereas the implicit strategies of cognitive reappraisal were not examined. Future research can examine expressive suppression and cognitive reappraisal, compare their differential mechanisms of action, and, in particular, explore how the explicitness of expressive suppression and the implicitness of cognitive reappraisal, respectively, regulate stress-related pathways, thereby further expanding the theoretical framework. At the same time, future research is prepared to incorporate actual outcome indicators such as performance, mental health, and cognitive processes as remote results, thereby further expanding the practical applicability of the research.

The range of problems caused by uncertainty stress should not be underestimated. The present study found a moderating effect of expressive suppression; however, the lack of intervention results warrants further exploration. This study identified a regulatory role for expressive suppression, but did not really apply it in an intervention. These efforts will be followed up on.

Conclusion

This study found that (1) college students’ uncertainty stress was a significant negative predictor of their attentional control; (2) PMPU mediated the relationship between uncertainty stress and attentional control; and (3) expressive suppression moderated the relationship between uncertainty stress and PMPU and attentional control. Specifically, high levels of uncertainty stress facilitated PMPU and more severely impaired attentional control among college students who tended to adopt expressive suppression as an emotion regulation strategy.
