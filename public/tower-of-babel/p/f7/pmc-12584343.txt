Career process experiences of male nurse managers: a qualitative research
Authors: Şehriban Yöndem, Osman Bilgin, Nazmiye Köseoğlu, Handan Alan, Serap Torun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMale nurses remain underrepresented in the global nursing workforce. Despite a growing need for gender diversity in healthcare, the career trajectories of male nurses, particularly those in managerial roles, have been underexplored.AimThe aim of this study was to determine the career process experiences of male nurse managers.MethodDescriptive phenomenological design, one of the qualitative research methods, was used in the study. Data were collected via video conferencing between September 2021 and February 2022 from 11 male nurse managers employed in university hospitals, Ministry of Health hospitals, and private healthcare facilities in Istanbul, Türkiye. Semi-structured interviews were conducted and analyzed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological analysis method. The form consisted of open-ended questions designed to gather participants’ personal and professional information, explore their experiences with career.FindingsCareer processes of male nurse managers findings were organized into four primary themes: “Pre-Employment Experiences”, “Entering the Workforce”, “Professional Experiences”, “Career Development and Future Plans”. These themes were further explored through the identification of 7 subthemes (Choice of Profession, Studentship, Interactions with Patients/Patient relatives, Interactions with Managers, Interactions with doctors, Being selected as a manager, Future plan) and corresponding codes.ConclusionMale nurse managers experienced both positive and negative situations related to their gender throughout their educational and professional journey, impacting their managerialprogression.

Introduction

The World Health Organization anticipates a global nursing workforce shortage in the upcoming years, which poses significant challenges for healthcare systems worldwide [1]. In response to this projected shortage, there is a growing consensus on the importance of enhancing workforce diversity in healthcare settings [2]. One critical dimension of this diversity is gender, especially considering that nursing remains one of the most gender-segregated professions globally, traditionally dominated by women [2, 3]. The WHO’s Gender Equity in the Health Workforce: Analysis of 104 Countries report highlights that men are still significantly underrepresented in the nursing profession [4].

Historically, the participation of men in nursing has fluctuated in response to broader socio-economic and political factors. A pivotal point was the global nursing shortage that emerged in the 1970s, which led to renewed efforts to recruit men into the profession [5]. These efforts were not only driven by workforce needs but also by calls to challenge the deeply embedded gender stereotypes and to professionalize nursing as a field inclusive of all genders [3]. In more recent years, campaigns by institutions such as the Australian College of Nursing and the UK National Health Service have aimed to normalize male presence in nursing and to shift public perceptions around gender roles in caregiving professions [6].

Despite such efforts, men still constitute a small minority in nursing. According to the WHO’s 2020 report, only about 10% of the global nursing workforce is male [7]. While the number of male nurses worldwide increased from 333,350 in 2019 to an estimated 735,000 in 2024 [8], this numerical growth has not yet resulted in significant structural change in the gender dynamics of the profession. Country-specific data underscore the persistence of this gender imbalance: men represent about 10% of nurses in the U.S [9]. 10.7% in the UK [10], 5.8% in Canada [11], 4.9% in Japan [12], and less than 1% in China [13]. In Türkiye, the precise national proportion is not officially published. However, a 2023 report by the Turkish Nurses Association (TNA) found that 22.4% of their sample (n = 8274) were male nurses [14], indicating a slightly higher but still minority presence in the Turkish context.

The underrepresentation of men in nursing is not solely a matter of numbers—it also reflects deeper structural and cultural barriers. Literature shows that men often encounter stigma, stereotypes, and discrimination when choosing a career in nursing [15]. Yet paradoxically, men who enter nursing may experience certain systemic advantages in their professional advancement. For example, studies indicate that male nurses are more rapidly promoted into managerial roles, partially because they are perceived as more flexible due to not taking maternity leave or because they more frequently volunteer for less desirable shifts such as nights [16]. This phenomenon, often referred to as the “glass escalator,” suggests that gender dynamics in nursing are complex and may produce both disadvantages and unearned advantages for male nurses depending on context [3, 16].

Most existing research on male nurses tends to focus on two main areas: the experiences of male nursing students and patients’ perceptions of male nurses [5, 17]. These studies have contributed valuable insights into motivations for entering the profession and societal attitudes towards male caregivers. However, there remains a significant gap in the literature concerning the lived experiences of male nurse managers—those who have not only entered the field but have also progressed into leadership roles. As such, our study addresses this gap by focusing on male nurse managers in Türkiye, a country where nursing has long been regarded as a female-dominated profession and where broader societal structures are influenced by traditional patriarchal norms.

This research is situated within a growing body of literature that calls for a critical and balanced examination of gender dynamics in nursing. Gauci et al. emphasize the need for honest and inclusive dialogue around increasing male representation in the profession, warning that such efforts must avoid reinforcing patriarchal structures that privilege men’s advancement over women’s [2]. Likewise, Watson et al. argue that nursing cannot afford internal divisions and must instead embrace gender diversity as a source of collective strength, advocating for respectful collaboration among all nurses regardless of gender identity [18].

Therefore, this study’s examination of male nurse managers’ career trajectories is both timely and necessary. It aims to contribute to strategies that promote gender diversity without compromising equity. Understanding the career experiences of male nurse managers in Türkiye—within the context of global trends and local cultural dynamics—can provide important insights into how gender continues to shape professional identity, leadership opportunities, and organizational dynamics in nursing.

In light of these considerations, the aim of this study is to explore the career process experiences of male nurse managers. Specifically, the study seeks to answer the following researchquestions:

(1) What are the career experiences of male nurse managers in Türkiye?

(2) How do gender-related factors influence their professional advancement?

Method

Study design

A descriptive phenomenological design was chosen because it allows for an in-depth examination of individuals’ lived experiences and the meanings they attribute to these experiences [19]. Descriptive phenomenology enables researchers to explore human experiences without imposing theoretical interpretations but instead focusing on participants’ own words and meanings [19].

Given the limited representation of male nurse managers globally and particularly within the Turkish context, qualitative research provides a valuable framework for capturing the nuanced and context-dependent experiences of this understudied group. Quantitative approaches are insufficient for exploring the deeper social, cultural, and professional meanings attached to male nurse managers’ career trajectories. Therefore, a phenomenological design was deemed appropriate to uncover shared themes and lived meanings within this specific population.

Setting and participants

The data were collected via video conferencing between September 2021 and February 2022, involving male nurses working in managerial positions in Istanbul, the most populous and metropolitan city of Türkiye.

In Türkiye, men entered the nursing profession with the amendment made in the Nursing Law in 2007. Prior to this, health officers with high school and/or undergraduate education were trained to work in inpatient health centres, which were opened to serve villages for the protection and treatment of public health. These individuals consisted of mostly male individuals with education equivalent to that of nurses. In 2007, with the amendment made in the nursing law, these schools were closed and these individuals were given the title of nurse [20].

This study includes two types of male nurse managers: (1) those who transitioned from the former role of “health officer” to professional nurse status post-2007 and (2) those who entered directly into the nursing profession after the legal amendment. The sampling strategy was based on purposive maximum variation sampling, aimed at capturing a broad range of professional backgrounds, management levels, and institutional types [21]. This method allows the identification of both commonalities and contrasts across diverse participants. To operationalize this, participants were selected from different hospital types (Ministry of Health, university, and private hospitals) and management levels (lower, middle, and upper management). However, as all participants were from a single urban center (Istanbul), the findings may not reflect the experiences of nurse managers in other geographical regions of Türkiye.

Additionally, the snowball sampling technique, a non-probability method, was employed to reach participants through referral chains within professional networks [22]. Initial contact was made through a male nurse manager known to one of the researchers. This method was particularly useful due to the limited visibility and accessibility of male nurse managers in Türkiye’s nursing workforce. While there are varying recommendations regarding the sample size in qualitative research, the emphasis is placed on gaining a detailed understanding through an in-depth exploration of the phenomenon within a small, focused sample. The goal is not to generalize the findings to a larger population, but rather to provide rich, contextual insights into the experiences and perspectives of the participants. Therefore, “theoretical saturation” serves as the key indicator of sample size adequacy in qualitative research. Data saturation is reached when no new insights or themes emerge from participants’ responses, or when the same information is repeated across interviews. At this point, the data collection process is concluded [23]. In this study, a total of 11 participants were interviewed. The sample size was determined by the principle of data saturation, which was reached when no new themes or variations in responses emerged after the 11th interview [23, 24].

Prior to applying the interview questions, expert feedback was received from two faculty members with expertise in qualitative research. Based on their input, the questions were refined and revised to ensure clarity, relevance, and alignment with the study’s objectives. To assess the appropriateness and clarity of the interview questions, the first author (R1), a doctoral student in nursing management with 10 years of clinical experience and 5 years in academia, and the second author (R2), a faculty member in nursing management with 18 years of clinical experience and 6 years of academic experience, both trained in qualitative data collection and analysis, conducted three pilot interviews. The feedback from these pilot interviews was used to refine and adjust the questions for clarity and relevance. The feedback obtained from these pilot interviews informed revisions to specific questions and helped validate the interview protocol, ensuring that the questions were clear, understandable, and capable of eliciting in-depth, meaningful responses from participants.

In-depth interviews were conducted by video and audio video conferencing method and recorded. The data were recorded using a digital voice recorder and transcribed verbatim. To ensure participant confidentiality, the transcripts were anonymized, and codes were assigned to each participant (e.g., Male Nurse Manager 1 = MNM 1). The audio recordings of the qualitative data were transcribed immediately after each interview, before proceeding to the next one, ensuring that the data was accurately captured and ready for analysis.

Immediately after each interview, a summary was provided to the participant for validation. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological analysis method [25], which involved identifying significant statements, formulating meanings, clustering themes, and verifying the findings with participants. The interviews lasted between 25 and 45 min.

Two researchers independently coded the data and compared their results to ensure inter-rater consistency. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third expert in qualitative nursing research. Coding and theme development were done manually on the day of each interview. The analysis followed a deductive approach supported by literature while remaining open to emergent themes.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, the following Lincoln and Guba’s criteria were applied:

Credibility

Interviews were conducted by researchers with experience in qualitative interviewing. While one researcher led the interview, the second observed and took notes without interrupting. The observing researcher did not intervene in the interview process. Member checking was conducted after each interview to confirm the accuracy of the data.

Dependability

A transparent audit trail was maintained, documenting all steps in data collection, coding, theme generation, and interpretation. Two independent coders evaluated the data, and a third researcher reviewed coding consistency. In this way, it was confirmed that the results obtained were based on the data obtained rather than the researcher’s own opinion.

Confirmability

Efforts were made to minimize researcher bias. The research team engaged in reflexivity by discussing their own positions and interpretations throughout the analysis. Participant quotes were used verbatim to support theme development without researcher commentary.

Transferability

While the sample was limited to male nurse managers in Istanbul, maximum variation sampling was employed to include a diverse range of management levels and institutional types. Nonetheless, the study acknowledges that results may not be generalizable to rural or underrepresented regions of Türkiye.

Regular peer debriefing sessions were held during data analysis to test the robustness of emerging interpretations and reduce bias.

Ethical considerations

Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Ethics committee of a university before the data collection phase (Date: 9 July 2021 No: 113/16). Before the interviews, the purpose of the study was explained to the participants and their consent was obtained for recording. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. It was stated that the recordings would be used only for scientific purposes and the identities of the participants would be kept confidential. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Theme 1. Pre-employment experiences

The majority of the male nurse managers talked about the reasons for choosing this profession and their experiences related to their student life before they started to work. The experiences of the participants in this period include two sub-themes consisting of " Choice of profession” and " Studentship “.

Subtheme 1.1. Choice of profession

Some of the participants emphasised that they switched to nursing from the health officer department. In addition, there were those who preferred nursing because of more job opportunities or with the guidance of their families.

Subtheme 1.2. Studentship

The participants stated that they had various experiences in their studentship due to their gender. Especially the participants who received undergraduate nursing education stated that they were a minority in the class and therefore they were treated differently from female students. On the other hand, the participants who were educated in the health officer department stated that men were in the majority in the class. In addition, the majority of the participants emphasised that they were exposed to the attitude and negative comments of the social environment.

Our quota was low in those years. We were 6 men out of 80 people. We were a minority. There were women to our right, to our left, all around us … (MNM1)

Our quota was low in those years. We were 6 men out of 80 people. We were a minority. There were women to our right, to our left, all around us … (MNM1)

Theme 2. Entering the workforce

Participants stated that they did not have any problems when they started to work in public hospitals after their student life because they were recruited through an examination. However, some of the participants who worked in private hospitals stated that they were not hired because they were male, while others said that they were hired because they did not use maternity leave and they were hired to work in places that require strength such as intensive care and operating theatres.

Since I was appointed directly through the civil service exam and since there was a serious shortage of nurses at that time and the number of graduates was low, there was no gender discrimination there. (MNM4)

Since I was appointed directly through the civil service exam and since there was a serious shortage of nurses at that time and the number of graduates was low, there was no gender discrimination there. (MNM4)

Theme 3. Professional experiences

While sharing their experiences in their working life, the participants talked about patients and their relatives, administrators and attitudes and behaviours of physicians. It was noteworthy that the participants did not use any positive/negative statements about their colleagues. Under this main theme, 3 sub-themes were analysed under the titles of " Interactions with patient/patient relatives “, " Interactions with manager " and " Interactions with doctor “.

Subtheme 3.1. Interactions with patients/patient relatives

Almost all of the participants talked about their positive/negative experiences about this sub-theme. They mostly stated that they had problems in terms of privacy and that they were not preferred because they were men. They emphasised that they were often mistaken for doctors in the working environment. At the same time, it was stated that patients/patient relatives who are prone to violence sometimes behave politely towards male nurses, and sometimes they show aggressive behaviour on the contrary.

There is no privacy in health, but in terms of privacy, we were arranging female nurses for female patients and male nurses for male patients (if needed). (MNM1)

There is no privacy in health, but in terms of privacy, we were arranging female nurses for female patients and male nurses for male patients (if needed). (MNM1)

Subtheme 3.2. Interactions with managers

The participants stated that they did not see any negative approach from their managers while working as a nurse, on the contrary, they encountered positive approaches.

There are ten female nurses and one male nurse. Naturally, it has always been a plus. (MNM1)

There are ten female nurses and one male nurse. Naturally, it has always been a plus. (MNM1)

Sub-theme 3.3. Interactions with doctors

Participants stated that doctors were distant to them and that they did not want to work with male nurses, especially when they were managers.

When I took over the management, some department heads said that they did not want to work with male nurses. (MNM5)

When I took over the management, some department heads said that they did not want to work with male nurses. (MNM5)

Theme 4. Career development and future plans

While talking about their working lives, male nurse managers also mentioned the process of being selected for the managerial position and what they would like to do in the future. This main theme was analysed with the sub-themes of “being selected as a manager” and “future plans”.

Subtheme 4.1. Being selected as a manager

Participants mostly stated that their communication skills were at the forefront in their appointment process. Another factor was their seniority in the unit they worked in, their experience and knowledge, and their compatibility with teamwork. There was also a participant who went to the senior management and said that he was a candidate for this position.

My bilateral relations were very strong. I think this came to the fore in my being preferred as a manager. (MNM6)

My bilateral relations were very strong. I think this came to the fore in my being preferred as a manager. (MNM6)

Subtheme 4.2. Future plan

While some of the participants stated that they were satisfied with their current position, it was observed that some of them had different future plans such as becoming a higher level manager, reaching a position that would carry nursing to better places, doing postgraduate education, and leaving the profession.

Discussion

This study explored the career experiences of male nurse managers in Türkiye, a country where nursing has traditionally been viewed as a female profession and where patriarchal cultural norms continue to influence occupational roles. The findings revealed a complex interplay of gendered experiences throughout various stages of participants’ professional trajectories, from their initial entry into nursing education to their current managerial roles. The themes identified in this research reflect both global patterns and context-specific dynamics that shape male nurse managers’ careers.

Pre-employment experiences: professional choice and studentship

The decision-making process surrounding the choice of nursing as a profession emerged as a crucial theme in participants’ narratives. Many male nurse managers reported transitioning from the health officer department or being guided by family members into nursing. Their choice was often shaped by pragmatic considerations, such as job security and employment opportunities. These motivations align with existing literature that identifies similar drivers among male nursing students, including economic stability and family encouragement [26–28].

However, their experiences during studentship were marked by gendered perceptions and social marginalisation. Participants frequently mentioned being in the minority within predominantly female cohorts and encountering both subtle and overt forms of bias. They described social isolation, being treated differently by instructors, and facing ridicule or curiosity from peers and the wider community. These findings mirror global research indicating that male nursing students often face discrimination and are perceived as deviating from gender norms [29–31]. Such experiences may influence their professional identity formation and impact their long-term commitment to the profession [32].

Traditional gender roles and patriarchal values continue to dominate in many sectors, including healthcare. Male nursing students in Türkiye may therefore face stronger societal resistance than their counterparts in more gender-progressive environments. Nevertheless, some participants observed a shift in attitudes over time, suggesting a gradual erosion of the stereotype that nursing is exclusively a women’s profession. This reflects national survey data indicating that the view of nursing as a “female-only” career has declined in recent years [5, 17, 33].

Entering the workforce: barriers and facilitators

The transition from education to employment presented mixed experiences for the participants. Those who entered public hospitals through state examination processes generally did not report gender-based hiring discrimination, highlighting the role of merit-based recruitment systems in promoting fairness. In contrast, participants who sought employment in private hospitals reported both discriminatory hiring practices and preferential treatment based on gender. Some were not hired because they were male, while others were chosen because of physical strength or the perception that men were less likely to request leave for caregiving responsibilities.

These findings underscore the duality of gendered assumptions in the workplace. On one hand, men are disadvantaged due to the perception that nursing is inherently feminine; on the other hand, they are advantaged when masculine traits (such as physical strength or resilience) are deemed valuable. Prior research confirms this dynamic, showing that male nurses are often steered towards roles that require physical effort or emotional detachment [34–36].

Patriarchal norms that associate caregiving with femininity likely contribute to initial resistance against male nurses, while utilitarian views of men as physically capable may simultaneously facilitate their employment in specific departments like intensive care or emergency units. This gendered division of labour reflects deeper cultural scripts that continue to shape organisational practices in healthcare.

Professional experiences: interactions with patients, managers, and doctors

During their professional careers, male nurse managers reported nuanced interactions with patients and their relatives. While some patients showed deference to male nurses, others questioned their presence in caregiving roles. Privacy concerns emerged as a prominent issue, with participants noting that gender discordance between nurse and patient often led to discomfort or resistance. Similar findings have been reported globally, with privacy frequently cited as a reason for patient preference regarding nurse gender [33, 34]. In Türkiye, where conservative values often inform gender interactions, such privacy concerns may be amplified.

Interestingly, participants observed that aggressive patients or their families were sometimes politer to male nurses, suggesting that masculine presence can serve as a buffer against workplace violence. Conversely, some patients or families reacted with heightened aggression. These divergent reactions suggest that male nurses constantly navigate fluctuating expectations and must manage the tension between perceived authority and mistrust.

In contrast to their interactions with patients, male nurse managers generally described positive experiences with their administrative superiors. Many believed they were supported and sometimes even favoured in promotion decisions. Some participants suggested that being the only male in a unit made them more visible and gave them an advantage in managerial appointments. This phenomenon, sometimes described as the “glass escalator,” suggests that men in female-dominated professions may experience faster upward mobility [37].

While this dynamic may seem advantageous, it raises ethical concerns about equity. Preferential treatment of male nurses in promotions—especially in a profession striving for gender balance—can unintentionally perpetuate new forms of inequality. As Gauci et al. have argued, efforts to recruit more men into nursing must be carefully designed to avoid reinforcing patriarchal advantage [2]. Thus, any perceived career benefits must be examined through a lens of fairness and inclusivity.

Relationships with physicians were more problematic. Several participants stated that doctors were hesitant to work with male nurses, particularly in leadership roles. Some attributed this to interprofessional hierarchy, while others felt that their gender intensified resistance from doctors. These findings echo previous research suggesting that male nurses may face suspicion or hostility from physicians, particularly when they challenge traditional gender or authority structures [17, 34]. In highly hierarchical and gendered medical systems like Türkiye’s, these dynamics can hinder collaborative practice and undermine managerial legitimacy.

Career development and future plans

Participants reported that their advancement into managerial positions was influenced by communication skills, team compatibility, and professional competence rather than gender alone. This finding is consistent with prior research that highlights the importance of personal attributes and work performance in nursing leadership development [38, 39]. It suggests that male nurse managers can gain legitimacy and respect when their promotion is grounded in merit.

Nonetheless, the long-term career aspirations of male nurse managers varied considerably. Some expressed satisfaction with their current roles, while others aspired to higher positions, academic advancement, or even leaving the profession. This range of perspectives highlights the complexity of career trajectories among male nurses. Some studies suggest that being male can be a risk factor for attrition, with many men viewing nursing as a temporary or transitional career [17, 40, 41]. Others see it as a stepping stone to broader leadership roles or sectors [42, 43].

Cultural and institutional factors in Türkiye play a significant role in shaping these aspirations. Patriarchal expectations often equate masculinity with ambition and progression, which may pressure male nurses to pursue higher positions or leave clinical roles. Moreover, societal perceptions of male nurses as “unusual” may drive them to overperform to gain recognition and legitimacy. These dynamics can result in burnout or dissatisfaction, particularly when advancement opportunities are limited or inequitable.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all participants were based in Istanbul, Türkiye, which may limit the transferability of findings to other geographical or cultural contexts. Although Istanbul offers access to a wide variety of healthcare institutions (e.g., public, university, and private hospitals), the geographic and institutional scope of the study was confined to this metropolitan setting.

Second, the relatively small sample size, though appropriate for qualitative research, may not fully capture the diversity of experiences among all male nurse managers across the country. Finally, as with all self-reported data, participants’ responses may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the growing literature on gender diversity in nursing by offering an in-depth examination of the career experiences of male nurse managers in Türkiye. It shows that while men may benefit from certain structural advantages, they also face unique challenges rooted in gender stereotypes, societal expectations, and institutional norms. In a country where patriarchal culture remains influential, these dynamics are especially pronounced. These results suggest that male nurse managers in Türkiye both benefit from and contend with gendered expectations at different career stages.

The findings suggest the need for comprehensive policies that both support the inclusion of men in nursing and promote gender equity at all levels of the profession. Efforts to recruit and retain male nurses should not inadvertently marginalise women or reinforce existing hierarchies. Instead, nursing must embrace a holistic vision of diversity that values all experiences and identities. Creating such an inclusive environment will require sustained institutional commitment, cultural sensitivity, and critical engagement with gendered power structures.
