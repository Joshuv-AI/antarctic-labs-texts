Protocol for continuous intravenous drug delivery with implantable iPrecio pump in free-moving rats and mice
Authors: Tusar Giri, Arvind Palanisamy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryVariable-rate delivery of intravenous drugs is difficult to achieve with a tethered infusion system in a freely moving animal. Here, we present a protocol for continuous intravenous delivery of oxytocin in pregnant rats and mice. We describe steps for using an implantable, preprogrammed, microprocessor-controlled infusion pump connected to the jugular vein to induce labor. This protocol can be adapted to a variety of experimental paradigms in non-pregnant animals where precise intravenous pharmacological manipulation is desired.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Giri et al.1,2

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the specific steps for programming, implanting, and connecting an iPrecio pump to the jugular vein to induce labor in pregnant rats as recently described by us.1,2 Furthermore, we have successfully adapted this protocol to mice with modifications. Because the programming steps are unique to the type of pump used (for rats vs. mice), we have described the approach for rats in detail and modified it for mice where necessary.

Institutional permissions

All animal experiments and methods reported here were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Washington University in St. Louis (#23-0021 for rats; #21-0001 for mice) and conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines. Please note that these procedures should only be undertaken by individuals with a high level of experience in aseptic rodent surgery after acquiring appropriate permissions at their institutions.

The programming and equipment set up is distinct from that for rats (UCD-X10R the data communication device, and SMP-310R ON device for magnetic activation of the pumps) and is shown in Figure 3. For installation of the software and the overall setup, please refer to the iPrecio SMP310-R User Manual. The infusion protocol is specific for labor induction with oxytocin in pregnant mice. Pump programming is flexible and can be adapted for any experimental paradigm.10.First step for mouse pump programming is to create an Infusion Profile, based on experimental design (for our experiments, we use the 96 h infusion protocol in Step 2 of Figure 4).a.Click File and open New (Step 3) and fill in the experimental details. This procedure requires administration of analgesics and antibiotics. Step-by-step method details

Implantation of an iPrecio pump requires strict adherence to sterile surgical technique. IACUC policies, procedures, and guidelines regarding rodent surgery should be strictly adhered to when conducting surgery. We recommend the following sequential multi-step process to ensure experimental success: (i) coupling of the venous catheter to the iPrecio pump, (ii) preparation of all the procedural medications (buprenorphine, enrofloxacin etc.,), and (iii) anesthesia induction followed by positioning and preparation of the animal for surgery. After the animal is initially anesthetized, a stable level of anesthesia is maintained for all subsequent steps of the surgical procedures.

Coupling of venous catheter to pump tubing

Timing: 3 min 1.Follow steps below to couple the venous catheter to the pump.a.Fill the catheter with heparinized catheter lock solution using a sterile 1 mL syringe and a 23G blunt needle.b.Remove the iPrecio pump from the incubator and place on a sterile drape.c.Attach the catheter to the iPrecio pump with a 22G steel coupler (Figure 6).Figure 6Coupling of pump tubing with venous catheter(A) Coupling of rat pump tubing with the venous catheter using a 22G coupler (black rectangle). Arrowed inset showing a magnified view of the coupling.(B) Coupling of mouse pump tubing with the venous catheter using a 22G coupler (black rectangle).d.Trim the iPrecio outlet tubing down to 2–2.5 cm from the base and trim the receiving end of the catheter length down to approximately 10 cm.e.Total length after coupling both tubes from the base of the pump should be around 12–12.5 cm.Note: This length has been determined for 12 week-old female pregnant dam with the pump implanted approximately 2.5 cm away from scapula. This could be modified based on the surgeon’s choice.f.Using a pair of forceps, grab and insert the tip of the steel coupler into the catheter supported with another pair of forceps.g.Slowly slide in the catheter over the coupler until 2/3rd of the coupler is inside the cannula.h.Similarly, insert the tip of the outlet tubing of the pump over the free end of the steel coupler already attached to the catheter and slide it over the coupler with the help of a pair of forceps.Note: The pump tubing is made from flexible polyurethane material, making it easy to insert with less resistance.i.For the coupling of mice femoral vein catheter (Instech Laboratories Inc.), the above steps were followed with modifications to account for the smaller size of mice.j.The iPrecio SMP-310R outlet tubing was trimmed to 4 cm from the base while the receiving end of the catheter was trimmed down to approximately 6–7 cm.k.The total length after coupling both tubes from the base of the pump should be between 10 and 11 cm.Note: This length has been determined for 12 week-old pregnant mouse (CD-1 IGS strain) with the pump implanted approximately 1.5 cm away from the scapula. This could be modified based on the surgeon’s choice.Pause point: We used a commercially available femoral vein catheter (Instech Laboratories Inc.) instead of the jugular vein catheter. Anesthesia induction

Timing: 3–5 min 2.Record the body weight of the animal.3.Place the animal into the pre-anesthesia induction chamber.4.Initiate isoflurane flow at 5% and turn the oxygen flow meter to 3 L/min rate. Note: Within 3–5 minutes the animal will become unconscious and the breathing will slow down. 5.Pinch the tail or paws to confirm loss of consciousness.6.Gently transfer the anesthetized animal from the chamber to the pre-warmed surgical platform.7.Switch the anesthesia tubing from the chamber to the nose cone while doing so.8.Apply ointment to both eyes.9.Place the animal’s nose inside the nose cone connected to the vaporizer to ensure adequate anesthesia for the duration of this procedure. Decrease the isoflurane concentration to a maintenance concentration of 2% after ensuring that the breathing is regular. CRITICAL: It is important to reduce the isoflurane concentration from 5% to 2% immediately after confirming optimal conditions for surgery (no response to tail pinch or skin incision) to decrease the risk of anesthetic overdose.

Expected outcomes

This protocol has thoroughly demonstrated the surgical procedures necessary for the creation of a long-term continuous intravenous drug delivery system with jugular venous cannulation. The workflow comprises of microprocessor-controlled infusion pump programming, followed by pump implantation, jugular vein cannulation, and subsequent refilling of drug via subcutaneous access of the refill port. Through this work, we have provided a viable and realistic pregnant rat model for labor induction and augmentation with oxytocin that enables new lines of investigation related to the impact of perinatal oxytocin exposure on the mother-infant dyad. More importantly, the surgical steps outlined in detail will be helpful in other experimental settings where precise intravenous delivery of any pharmacological agent is desired. Mastering the technique of jugular vein cannulation requires extensive practice and iterative learning from each implantation. Adhering to our protocol would increase the success rate of jugular vein cannulation with shortened anesthesia exposure times (approximately 35–50 min) and facilitate enhanced post-procedure recovery of the animals.

Limitations

These surgical protocols are comprehensive and should work seamlessly once adequate experience is gained. This is vindicated by the carefully managed oxytocin induction protocols to facilitate live birth in pregnant rodents. The biggest limitation is the possibility of pump malfunction or catheter occlusion which, despite adequate precautions, is always a possibility. Should this occur, the animal should be excluded from the experiment and the pump and the catheter must be removed for evaluation to identify ideas for refinement of the procedural technique.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1: Animal death

In our experience, pregnant mice are more sensitive to anesthesia than pregnant rats. Death can occur primarily due to higher dose/duration of anesthesia, hypothermia from heat loss during surgery, and from excessive bleeding.

Potential solution

To minimize death due to anesthesia it is best to perform the procedure as quickly as possible (< 1 h). We have observed that maintaining anesthesia with 2% in 100% oxygen (Step 9) minimizes this concern. To manage heat loss, it is absolutely essential to maintain a warm operating environment using standard equipment (a tunable heat source such as a digitally controlled warming pad in Step 14). We recommend monitoring the rectal temperature of the animal every 10 min for the first few procedures to ensure that the set temperature is working well to maintain the body temperature at 98.4°F (± 1°F). To minimize blood loss, we recommend meticulous dissection under an operating microscope using blunt-tipped instruments. For minor bleeding, exert gentle pressure on the bleeding area with absorbable sponge point or sterile gauze until the bleeding stops. If the bleeding point is clearly visible, use a fine-tip cautery to stop bleeding. In our experience, if the bleeding does not restart before skin closure, it is unlikely to be a post-operative concern. If the animal manifest signs of hypovolemia following a bleeding episode (such as delayed recovery, inexplicable hypothermia etc.,), inject sterile warm isotonic fluid (3–5% of body weight) subcutaneously at the end of surgery. Because of the possibility of animal death, risk of infection, and pump malfunction (problem 4 below), we recommend at least a 20% increase in planned sample size to account for the fallouts.

Problem 2: Difficulty in locating and cannulating the jugular vein

For investigators without experience in animal surgery, identifying the jugular vein could be a problem because of the delicate and variable anatomy.

Potential solution

The key point is the identification of the initial incision area at the neck (Step 20) depending on the side of the jugular vein that needs to be cannulated. For example, for right jugular vein cannulation, the incision should be above the right clavicle slightly lateral to the midline. We recommend practicing with a dissecting microscope with sample animals before initiating the experiment. Following blunt dissection of soft tissues, the jugular vein should be readily visible as a purple line. The vein is typically wider in diameter above the clavicle and then narrows down to bifurcate into two branches at the proximal end just below the mandible. Preparation of a clean segment of the vein at the cannulation site is a critical step (Figure 8B). Keeping the vein moist with drops of saline, supporting the vein with steady traction, and use of a needle dilator greatly improves the success rate of cannulation.

Problem 3: Delayed postoperative recovery

Potential solution

Prolonged duration of surgery, hypovolemia and hypothermia could delay postoperative recovery and should be avoided as described above. In the absence of the above, delayed recovery could be a random event. Regardless, we recommend at least 5 min of 100% oxygen at the end of the procedure until respirations are fast and regular. The animal should then be moved to its home cage and placed in a left lateral position for ease of respiratory monitoring. During the recovery phase, we routinely use a heat source over the home cage to keep the cage warm until the animal is fully awake.

Problem 4: Pump malfunction

As with all microprocessor-controlled pump experiments involving delicate moving parts malfunction may occur. However, in our experience, this is mostly due to kinking or blockage of the catheter rather than true pump failure.

Potential solution

The possibility of pump malfunction can be minimized by pre-warming the pump to 37°C for at least 3 days (Step 1). The optimal approach to ensure that the pump is functioning well is to palpate the reservoir over the skin to detect collapse of the reservoir with continuous discharge of the solution. This, however, is not a reliable strategy with low infusion rates. If the pump is occluded by a blood clot, for example, there is no mechanism in SMP-200 pumps (rats) to confirm if the infusion rate is delivered as programmed. However, in SMP-310R (mice), because of continuous wireless communication, this can be more reliably detected by monitoring infusion logs. Regardless, we advise visually inspecting the pumps upon removal, and aspirating and measuring the volume of leftover solution in the reservoir (Step 77) to ensure that the experimental drug was successfully administered as programmed.

Problem 5: Wound dehiscence

Potential solution

This is one of the most common problems we encounter during the execution of this protocol. This can be minimized by locating the pump away from the suture line (Step 21). Should dehiscence occur despite these precautions, remove all the granulation tissue (if formed) and re-stitch the incision with sterile suture. Tighten the knots just enough to close the incision but not too tight to obstruct the blood flow to the skin at the edges of the incision. Apply Neosporin ointment once daily for a week to enhance healing and control local irritation such as to prevent the animal from scratching at the incision area. If the animal still continues scratching, then consider trimming the nails.
