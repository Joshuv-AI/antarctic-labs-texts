Efficacy of topically administered progesterone for the treatment of retinitis pigmentosa in vivo
Authors: Yethindra Vityala, Afreen Begum Kaggallu, Sameena Sultana, Krishna Priya Kanteti, Tugolbai Tagaev
Published: 2023

Sir,

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a type of hereditary retinal degenerative disorders that cause the loss of photoreceptor cells and are currently partially treatable.[1] There are no conventional therapies available for patients with RP. Supplementing with Vitamin A has been the most often advised therapy for many years, since a few studies suggest that it can delay the development of retinal degeneration.[2] Vitamin A treatment may decrease RP development; however, long-term, high-dose treatment may have side effects.[34]

Effective treatments for RP are currently being investigated, and novel insights into the genetic variables that contribute to the disease have emerged in recent years. Gene-specific or mutation-specific studies indicate that gene replacement treatments are being developed to achieve common gene function in photoreceptors.

It has been demonstrated that oral administration of progesterone (PG) in high concentrations acts on multiple levels to delay the death of photoreceptors during the RP.[5] The purpose of the work presented is to investigate the efficacy of topically administered PG for the treatment of RP in vivo. According to the Declaration of Helsinki, the study’s protocols were followed.

An aqueous solution of PG in cyclodextrins at a concentration of 1 mg/mL was administered to the retinal degeneration slow mice between days 11 and 21 postnatally. Every 12 h, one drop of the PG solution was injected into the oculus sinister (OS), or left eye, of the mice in the treatment group (TG) that had received PG therapy. The OS of the mice in the control group (CG) was given saline. To investigate the possibility of a systemic effect, the oculus dexter, or right eye, was not treated.

The eyeballs were sectioned using a cryostat (Leica CM 1850) following standard processing. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) double-label immunofluorescence were among the techniques used. After mounting the sections with VECTASHIELD® Hardset™ Antifade Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, California, United States), 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole was used.

It was determined how many positive cells there were in each location following the TUNEL method. Quantitative analysis of GFAP involved measuring the percentage of tissue that was positively stained. To determine how many rows of nuclei were present in the nuclear membrane, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole counterstaining was used. The marginal mean comparisons were carried out using the Bonferroni correction, and the statistical analysis was carried out using a two-way ANOVA. The level of statistical significance used in these analyses was P < 0.05.

Eyes that have undergone treatment (OS-TG) and those that have not (OS-CG) vary statistically significantly in terms of the number of cells identified with TUNEL staining [Figure 1a]. In mice treated with PG, there is a reduction in the quantity of TUNEL-positive cells. The GFAP staining revealed a similar difference [Figure 1b]. However, there were no statistically significant changes found between the treated eyes of the two groups (OS-TG and OS-CG) in the outer nuclear layer row count [Figure 1c]. When compared to the other eyes, the TG exhibits statistically significant changes using the TUNEL and GFAP methods but not when using the outer nuclear layer row count.

Cell counts in Oculus dexter (pink) and Oculus sinister (blue) in the control group and retinitis pigmentosa in (a) terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling, (b) GFAP, and (c) cell rows of nuclear membrane staining. GFAP: Glial fibrillary acidic protein

This study showed that topical administration of a PG-cyclodextrin solution to the eyes of mice prevented the loss of photoreceptor cells in the RP animal model studied without affecting the condition of the contralateral eye systemically.
