An exploratory study on the potential of angiogenic genes in early HNSCC detection
Authors: Mao-Ling Huang, Fa-Zhang Lan, Lei Xu, Bin He, Long Huang, Zheng Tang, Zhen-Dong Jiang, Tao Liu, Wen-jun Shan, Cheng Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe early diagnosis and prognosis of head and neck tumors are crucial for enhancing patients’ quality of life. This study focuses on angiogenic genes to develop prognostic and diagnostic models, offering a novel strategy to improve patient outcomes and increase the early diagnosis rate of HNSCC.MethodsSamples were clustered based on MSI, SCNA, and mTMB. A vascularization-related diagnostic and prognostic scoring model was constructed through the analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The model was validated using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards analyses with data from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC). Subsequently, samples were grouped according to model scores, and immune infiltration analysis was conducted. Immune profiling, combined with narrow band imaging (NBI), was employed to verify the diagnostic model.ResultsCluster analysis based on MSI, SCNA, and mTMB burden values effectively stratified HNSCC into three distinct types. An angiogenesis-related prognostic model was developed from differential gene analysis to assess patient outcomes. Validation using an external database (ICGC dataset) confirmed the model’s robust performance. Following the stratification of TCGA samples into high-risk and low-risk groups based on risk scores, significant differences were observed in expression data and immune cell infiltration between the two cohorts. Furthermore, after adjustments using both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, the risk score index emerged as an independent prognostic factor. Subsequent validation using Narrow Band Imaging (NBI) combined with immunohistochemistry confirmed the model’s utility in diagnosing early-stage head and neck tumors.ConclusionThe model holds potential for application in the diagnostic and prognostic evaluation of early-stage head and neck tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03799-z.

Background

Annually, approximately 5 million cases of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) are diagnosed globally, representing about 6% of all cancer cases [1].Over the past two decades, advancements in diagnostic techniques, surgical procedures, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy have contributed to a decline in mortality rates for HNSCC patients. However, the 5-year survival rate remains at a modest 40–50%. The high rates of postoperative recurrence and metastasis are the two primary factors adversely impacting the survival outcomes of patients with HNSCC [2]. The main reason is the low diagnosis rate of early tumor. Therefore, the study of related molecular typing in the oncogenesis and progression of HNSCC will be helpful to improve the treatment of this disease.

The primary challenge in addressing HNSCC is the low rate of early tumor diagnosis. Consequently, investigating the molecular subtypes involved in the oncogenesis and progression of HNSCC is crucial for enhancing therapeutic strategies. Recent advancements in sequencing technologies, such as transcriptome sequencing and single-cell sequencing, have provided novel avenues for tumor diagnosis and prognosis. Transcriptome sequencing, which involves the sequencing of RNA transcripts, facilitates the identification of gene expression, alternative splicing, and previously unrecognized noncoding RNAs [3]. Utilizing the hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) dataset from TCGA, Long et al. developed a prognostic model based on four genes that effectively predicted overall survival in HCC patients, thereby demonstrating the potential of next-generation sequencing methods and data to identify clinical cancer biomarkers [4]. In a similar vein, we have developed a prognostic model based on genomic characterization to forecast the prognosis of patients with HNSCC.

In this study, we differentiate our approach from previous research by developing a novel clustering method. Utilizing this method, we classified head and neck tumors into three distinct categories. Subsequently, we constructed a prognostic model based on four genes—LMX1B, PRKAA2, SPINK6, and TRIML2—derived from our transcriptome sequencing survival analysis. The validity of this risk-coefficient model was confirmed using data from the TCGA and ICGC. Overall, this genomic characterization-based prognostic model has the potential to inform the prognostic assessment of patients with HNSCC (Fig. 1).

Methods and materials

Data download and preprocessing

The expression spectrum, mutation data, and clinical information for HNSC-US were sourced from the ICGC database (https://dcc.icgc.org/) and the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). Normal samples were excluded from these datasets, and they were combined with clinical data, excluding samples lacking overall survival (OS) data. Subsequently, three primary tumor genomic mutation features were calculated.

MSI burden, SCNA burden and mTMB

The gene expression data is preprocessed using the data_pre function from the PREMSIM package, and MSI status is predicted with the msi_pre function, resulting in a 0/1 output. In the TCGA CNV dataset, each row’s value is calculated as log2 of the sample CNV intensity over the normal baseline, adjusted for segment length. Synonymous variants are removed from the mutation dataset, and the total non-synonymous mutations per sample are calculated to construct a mutation feature matrix. A total of 488 samples are used for unsupervised cluster analysis. The sample count for each feature is detailed in Table 1, with “common feature” referring to three mutation features. (The matrix is presented in Supplementary Material 1)

Cluster typing and PCA analysis

The iCluster package divides samples into three types and PCA analysis is conducted on the mutation feature matrix to assess differentiation among these types.Then the number of samples was counted and the differences of characteristic values among the types were analyzed.

Analysis of SNV and CNV differences among typing types

The MafTools package’s oncoplot function analyzed SNV mutation differences across classifications, creating a waterfall diagram to display their distribution. CNV frequency distributions under various staining conditions were compared between subtypes.

Difference analysis of immune infiltration between different types

Using TCGA dataset expression profiles, the CiberSort algorithm calculated immune cell infiltration proportions, visualized with stacking diagrams and boxplots.

Differential expression analysis of immune genes among subtypes

Based on the expression profile data of TCGA dataset, the differential expression of genes at the immunoexamination sites (PD-L1, PD-1, and CTLA4) was analyzed respectively, and then demonstrated with boxplot.

Prediction of therapeutic effect difference of type immunotherapy

Based on the TCGA data sets of gene expression profile data, using TIDE software (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) prediction of head and neck squamous cell carcinomas different parting immune treatment curative effect.

Univariate Cox regression analysis

Deseq2 was employed for differential expression analysis among genotypes, using a corrected P value < 0.05 to identify differential genes. The Survfit function from the Survival package conducted Cox univariate regression analysis on these genes, with P < 0.05 as the threshold for identifying prognostic factors for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. The forestplot function was used to create a forest plot.

Lasso regression analysis and model construction

Based on Cox univariate results, Lasso regression via the CV. GLMnet function from the GLMnet package was used to eliminate redundant prognostic factors. Lasso regression coefficient was used to construct risk score = (0.236*LMX1b)+(-0.149*PRKAA2)+(-0.137*SPINK6)+(0.339*TRIML2).

Forecast performance analysis and external data verification

Using the Timeroc function from the Timeroc package, an ROC curve and AUC value for the Lasso Signature were generated. TCGA and ICGC expression profiles were split into high and low-risk groups using the median risk score, and the GGSurvPlot function from the SurvMiner package created Kaplan-Meier curves to assess survival differences between these groups.

Differential analysis of model robustness and immune infiltration

Clinical data from the TCGA dataset were analyzed across various factors like N0, N1-3, T1-3, T4, Smoking, Non-Smoking, Alcohol, and Non-Alcohol. Kaplan-Meier curves evaluated survival differences between high/low risk groups. The CiberSort algorithm calculated immune cell infiltration proportions for these groups, displayed via a boxplot.

Validation of independent prognostic factors for risk score

Based on the expression profiles and clinically relevant data of TCGA and ICGC dataset, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed using Survival package. Age, stage, perineural invasion, grade and risk score were selected for TCGA, and gender and risk score were selected for ICGC, and forest plots were plotted using Forestplot.

Build a line graph

The RMS package was used to build the histogram model based on stage and risk score. The clinical OS endpoints of 1 year, 3 years and 5 years were displayed respectively, and the histogram correction curves were drawn.

Immunohistochemical experiments

Refer to previous research [5, 6].

Results

Three types were obtained by cluster typing analysis

Utilizing the mutation feature matrix, a clustering analysis was conducted using the iCluster packages, resulting in the identification of three distinct genotyping subsets. Subsequent principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to these subsets. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the three genotyping subsets (iC1, iC2, and iC3) exhibited clear demarcations, suggesting that the unsupervised clustering approach yielded effective classification. The proportional distribution of these subsets is depicted in the pie chart (Fig. 2B), representing 37.5%, 35.04%, and 27.46%, respectively. Further analysis involved generating a cluster heatmap of the copy number variation (CNV) mutation data using the pheatmap package, with annotations for the clinical variables ‘Event’ and ‘Subtype’ included, as shown in Fig. 2C. The figure reveals that each genotyping subset possesses distinct CNV mutation patterns, with no overlapping mutation characteristics among them, nor any similar distribution patterns in the clinical annotations of ‘Event’ and ‘Subtype’.

Analysis of SNV and CNV differences among three types

Based on the results of clustering typing, the difference distribution of the three mutations in MSI burden, SCNA burden and mTMB in each typing was investigated, as shown in Fig. 2 below. The analysis of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma samples revealed distinct distribution patterns in the 3D scatter plot (Fig. 3A), utilizing three features as dimensions. Furthermore, the boxplots of mTMB and SCNA burden (Fig. 3B, C) demonstrate that the P-values obtained from the Kruskal-Wallis test for both variables are less than 0.01, indicating statistically significant differences in the distribution of mTMB and SCNA burden eigenvalues across the three groups. Similarly, the accumulation diagram of MSI burden (Fig. 3D) illustrates a significantly different distribution of MSI burden eigenvalues among the three groups. The maftools package was subsequently employed to analyze mutation differences across classifications. In Fig. 3E, mutation data were available for 183 samples in the iC1 category (Figure shows 180 samples), 134 samples in the iC2 category (Figure shows 133 samples), and 171 samples in the iC3 category (Figure shows 152 samples). Figure 3F highlights notable differences in the frequency of CNV among the three classification types, underscoring the diversity of CNV mutations. Additionally, there were significant statistical differences in the distribution of CNV types. The P values of Kruskal-Wallis test were all less than 0.01, and most of the CNV mutations were distributed in a low frequency range.

Immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint were different in three types

Utilizing the TCGA dataset, the CIBERSORT software was employed to estimate the proportion of immune cell infiltration across various subtypes. As depicted in Fig. 4A, there were significant differences in the infiltration ratios of immune cells among the different subtypes. Figure 4B further illustrates that the relative abundance of immune cells is statistically significant, as determined by the Kruskal-Wallis test. Specifically, native B cells, native CD4 T cells, monocytes, resting mast cells, and activated mast cells exhibited significant differences (P < 0.05). Subsequently, the gene expression profile data were analyzed. Figure 4C demonstrates that the expression levels of immune checkpoints, including PD-L1, PD-1, and CTLA4, vary across subtypes. The expression levels of the PDCD1 and CTLA4 genes, which correspond to PD-1, were significantly different among the subtypes, as determined by the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test (P < 0.05). In contrast, the expression of the CD274 gene, corresponding to PD-L1, did not show significant differences across subtypes. Finally, the TIDE algorithm was utilized to assess tumor immune dysfunction and rejection scores. The results, presented in Fig. 4D, indicate that samples with high TIDE scores were associated with poor immunotherapy outcomes, whereas those with low scores exhibited better outcomes.The TIDE value distribution of each subtype did not show significant differences, but the number of samples with TIDE value less than 0 in iC3 subtype was relatively large, indicating that the immune effect of iC3 subtype was better than other subtypes.

LMX1B, PRKAA2, SPINK6 and TRIML2 were combined to construct the risk model

Utilizing expression profile data from TCGA, we employed the likelihood ratio test (LRT) from the DESeq2 package to identify differentially expressed genes across various types, resulting in a total of 529 differential genes (P < 0.01). From these, the 30 most significant genes, based on P-value ranking, were selected to construct a cluster heat map, depicted in Fig. 5A. Subsequently, a Cox univariate regression analysis was conducted on the 529 differential genes using the survival package. The survminer package facilitated the generation of Kaplan-Meier curves, with overall survival serving as the clinical endpoint. The median expression level was utilized as the cutoff for grouping in the Kaplan-Meier analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves for selected genes are presented in Fig. 5B, where the P-values for PRKAA2, LMX1B, SPINK6, and TRIML2 were all below 0.05, indicating significant prognostic differences between groups. Furthermore, the forestplot package in R was employed to create a forest plot for the top 30 significant genes, as illustrated in Fig. 5C. To further refine the prognostic model by minimizing redundant factors, lasso regression analysis was conducted utilizing the glmnet package. Lambda and coefficient plots derived from the lasso regression are presented in Fig. 5D and E.The lambda.min value was selected to compute the regression coefficients for each gene. The risk model was constructed according to the regression coefficient and corresponding gene expression level, risk score = (0.236*LMX1B)+(0.149*PRKAA2)+(-0.137*SPINK6)+(0.339*TRIML2).

The external validation yielded results that indicate a modest performance of the model

Using the constructed risk model, the Timeroc package was employed to generate ROC curves for the TCGA and ICGC datasets, thereby assessing the predictive accuracy of the risk scores. The Kaplan-Meier analysis yielded a P-value of less than 0.01 for both the TCGA (Fig. 6A) and ICGC (Fig. 6B) datasets, indicating a statistically significant difference in prognostic outcomes between the high-risk and low-risk groups. The ROC curve for the TCGA dataset (Fig. 6C) revealed AUC values of 0.654, 0.651, and 0.687 for 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, demonstrating robust predictive performance. Similarly, the ROC curve for the ICGC dataset (Fig. 6D) exhibited AUC values of 0.593, 0.672, and 0.743 for 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, also indicating strong predictive capability. Finally, a clustering heat map was generated based on the identified prognostic genes, which showed effective clustering.

Significant differences in clinical data based on risk model

Based on clinically relevant data of TCGA dataset, the Kaplan-Meier curves of high and low risk groups under clinical categories (such as N0, N1-3, T1-3, T4, Smoking, No Smoking, Alcohol and No Alcohol) are shown in Fig. 7A below. Clinical data classification also has a good prediction effect. The proportion of immune cell infiltration in the high/low risk groups was calculated using the CIBERSORT. In Fig. 7B, there are a total of 22 types of immunoinfiltrating cells. In the high/low risk group, the proportion of immune cells among Plasma cells, T cells gamma delta, Dendritic cells resting and Neutrophils has significant statistical difference (P < 0.05).

Risk score was an independent prognostic factor

Utilizing the expression profiles from the TCGA and ICGC datasets alongside clinical data, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted, integrating all clinical factors and risk scores. In the TCGA dataset, the forest plots for univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses are presented in Fig. 8A and B, respectively. The analyses indicate that both perineural invasion and risk score demonstrate significant statistical relevance in both univariate and multivariate contexts (P < 0.05). Notably, the P-value for gender shifted from 0.048 in the univariate analysis to 0.58 following multivariate adjustment, suggesting that the risk score serves as an independent prognostic factor. Similarly, in the ICGC dataset, the forest plots for univariate and multivariate Cox regression are depicted in Fig. 8C and D. Here, the risk score alone maintains statistical significance across both analyses (P < 0.05), whereas other clinical factors do not exhibit a strong prognostic capability. Consequently, the risk score is affirmed as an independent prognostic factor in both datasets.

Preliminary analysis demonstrates the potential of the combined risk model and NBI approach to augment the diagnostic yield for early head and neck malignancies

We further analyzed the signaling pathways associated with the risk model, and we found that the risk model was significantly associated with the tumor angiogenesis pathway (Fig. 9A).Then, It was determined by immunohistochemistry that carcinoma expressed LMX1B, PRKAA2 and TRIML2 with a high level of expression. Conversely, SPINK6 expression levels tended to decline in carcinoma (Fig. 9B). Within the limited sample size of this study, the combined NBI and risk model approach showed perfect discrimination; however, this requires validation in larger cohorts to confirm its superiority over standard NBI (94.1%) (Fig. 9C).

Discussion

The high rates of postoperative recurrence and metastasis are the two most critical factors influencing the survival rate of patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [7]. Consequently, early diagnosis has emerged as a crucial element in enhancing the quality of life for these patients.

Although numerous RNAs have been demonstrated to improve outcomes in patients with head and neck cancer [8], they constitute only a small fraction, indicating the need for further research to elucidate the role of RNA. In our study, we employed an innovative approach by utilizing MSI, SCNA, and MTMB eigenvalues to classify head and neck tumors, resulting in the identification of three distinct subtypes. Incorporating clinical data into our analysis, we observed significant differences in immune cell infiltration among these subtypes, including variations in T cell and B cell populations. Additionally, immune checkpoints PD-1 and CTLA4 exhibited notable differences. These findings suggest that this classification system can be instrumental in further exploring the relationship between head and neck tumors and immune infiltration.

Subsequently, we conducted a differential gene expression analysis for the three identified types and developed a risk assessment model. Through this analysis, we identified four key genes: LMX1B, PRKAA2, SPINK6, and TRIML2. The model’s validity was tested using datasets from TCGA and ICGC. The AUC values for TCGA at 1, 3, and 5 years were 0.654, 0.651, and 0.687, respectively, while the corresponding AUC values for ICGC were 0.593, 0.672, and 0.743. Utilizing this mRNA-based prognostic risk scoring model, patients with HNSCC were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves derived from both TCGA and ICGC datasets demonstrated that the low-risk group exhibited a significantly better prognosis compared to the high-risk group. This model facilitates oncologists in formulating a comprehensive treatment strategy that considers both the clinical stage and genomic characteristics of the patient.

Narrow band imaging (NBI) is capable of clearly delineating the morphology and lesion boundaries of intrapapillary capillary loops (IPCL). However, its diagnostic accuracy for high-risk lesions is heavily reliant on the clinician’s experience, potentially leading to misdiagnoses among less experienced practitioners [9]. The integration of NBI with the risk model may contribute to improved accuracy in the early diagnosis of head and neck tumors, although further validation is required to confirm its clinical utility. Some genes within the risk model also showed associations with the angiogenesis pathway, suggesting a potential biological relevance that merits deeper investigation.

LMX1B encodes a member of LIM-homeodomain family of proteins [10]. And LMX1B is involved in neuronal maintenance and determines cell fate [11]. LMX1B is involved in angiogenesis through the WNT signaling pathway [12].

PRKAA2, encoding α2 subunit in the heterotrimer of AMPK (AMPKα2), is involved in the metabolism of a variety of tissues and diseases [13]. AMPK pathway is one of the important pathways involved in angiogenesis [14]. Vila et al. found the UBE20-AMPKα2 axis promotes the genesis and progression of tumors [15]. However, PRKAA2 is currently not being studied in HNSCC.

SPINK6, a Kazal-type serine protease inhibitor, has been controversial in cancer. Zheng et al. found that SPINK6 promoted nasopharyngeal carcinoma metastasis [16]. However, Ge et al. found that SPINK6 inhibited tumorigenesis of human hepatocellular carcinoma cells [17]. And Tian et al. also confirmed that SPINK6 was involved in the prognosis of HNSCC by using a different clustering method from ours [18]. Although SPINK6 plays a different role in tumors, it does appear to be associated with tumor prognosis, and more studies are needed to confirm this.

TRIML2, a member of the tri-partite motif (TRIM) family of proteins, may be regulated by the tumor suppressor p53. P53 was also a key upstream gene in the angiogenesis pathway [19]. TRIML2 may regulate p53 through the enhancement of p53 SUMOylation [20]. Song et al. found TRIML2 was a potential prognostic biomarker in triple-negative breast cancer [21]. Therefore, TRIML2 can be used as an angiogenic marker gene to diagnose early head and neck tumors.

While the protein-level overexpression of these genes (LMX1B, SPINK6, PRKAA2, TRIML2) in tumor tissues, as confirmed by IHC, supports their association with HNSCC, our study does not provide direct functional evidence for their role in tumor angiogenesis or progression. The mechanistic contributions of these genes to HNSCC pathogenesis remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies employing in vitro and in vivo models, such as gene knockdown/knockout experiments, are essential to validate their functional impact on key processes like cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation of these four genetic biomarkers as a collective entity, offering potential clinical insights for the development of novel prognostic factors in HNSCC. A notable advantage of our methodology is its independence from direct patient involvement, coupled with its cost-effectiveness compared to traditional sequencing techniques. Future research will employ single-cell sequencing of circulating tumor cells to examine the expression of these four genes in patients deemed unsuitable for surgical intervention. Accurate prognostic evaluation is crucial for selecting appropriate treatment modalities. In clinical practice, tumor staging plays a pivotal role in determining the prognosis for oncologists and patients with HNSCC. However, current staging systems, which are predominantly based on the extent of tumor invasion, fail to adequately capture the biological heterogeneity inherent in HNSCC patients. Each patient possesses a unique genomic profile. Our findings introduce the first model that integrates genomic characteristics with clinical data to predict patient outcomes in HNSCC. However, There are many limitations to the current study. First of all, TCGA-HNSC includes all head and neck tumors, including nasopharyngeal cancer and laryngeal cancer, but the stage and treatment plan of each type of tumor are different. Second, the majority of the races in TGCA are white and black, but head and neck tumors are more common in the yellow race. Therefore, our results need to be further validated in each type of tumor. In the future, we will also explore the possibility of more prognostic variables to further improve performance.

Conclusions

our research results indicate we have divided head and neck tumors into three subtypes, and the four-mRNA prognostic model is a reliable tool for predicting the OS of HNSCC patients. In the future, it can assist clinicians in selecting personalized treatment for patients with HNSCC.
