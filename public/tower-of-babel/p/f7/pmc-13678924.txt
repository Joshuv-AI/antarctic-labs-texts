Preliminary construction and application of the AFSP risk stratification framework for anesthetic management in airway tumors: a case report
Authors: Qingxiang Cai, Hongzhen Dong, Caineng Wu, Qingbiao He, Yong Wang, Wuhua Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo initially construct the Anatomy-Function-Surgery-Patient (AFSP) four-dimensional risk stratification framework, establish a dynamic anesthetic decision-making framework based on this framework, explore its application value in the anesthetic management of patients with airway neoplasms. This is a retrospective analysis of a single patient’s multistage treatment course.MethodsThe AFSP scoring tool was constructed based on relevant clinical guidelines and literature. A retrospective case of a 77-year-old patient with complex tracheal neoplasms was selected. The AFSP framework was applied to dynamically evaluate perioperative risks in three sequential treatment phases (tumor ablation combined with stent implantation, stent removal, and granulation tissue resection), guide the adjustment of anesthetic strategies, and observe the clinical outcomes.ResultsDuring Stage I (tumor ablation + stent implantation), the patient’s AFSP score reached 11 points, categorized as Grade IV (extremely high risk). We adopted general anesthesia supported by extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), which effectively prevented the life-threatening cannot intubate cannot oxygenate (CICO) crisis during anesthetic induction. In Stage II (stent removal), the AFSP score decreased to 8 points, falling into Grade III (high risk). The anesthetic strategy was adjusted to rapid sequence induction combined with high‑frequency jet ventilation (HFJV), which precisely targeted key risks including stent adhesion and intraoperative bleeding. For Stage III (granulation tissue resection), the AFSP score remained at 9 points (Grade III, high risk). On the basis of continuing the ventilation strategy used in Stage II, we added preoperative psychological intervention. No severe anesthesia-related complications occurred throughout the three treatment phases, and the patient completed the entire therapeutic course successfully, suggesting the application value of the AFSP framework in the anesthetic management of airway neoplasms.ConclusionThe initially constructed AFSP four-dimensional risk stratification framework may facilitate the evaluation of perioperative risks in patients with airway neoplasms and help guide individualized anesthetic management strategies for those undergoing multistage treatment, potentially improving perioperative safety. This study is based on a single patient with a limited sample size; the universality of the conclusions needs to be further verified in larger cohorts, and the AFSP scoring tool requires optimization with more clinical data.Keywords: Tracheal neoplasms, Complex airway, Risk stratification, Stage evolution, AFSP framework, Anesthetic management, Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, High-frequency jet ventilation

Introduction

Central Airway Obstruction (CAO) is a critical emergency from lesions affecting the trachea and bronchi. Malignant tracheal tumors, highly vascularized and fragile, pose great anesthetic challenges, with shedding and hemorrhage risking perioperative CICO events [1–3].Complex tracheal neoplasm treatment requires a multistage process (diagnosis, tumor debulking, stenting, complication management, recurrence retreatment) [4–6], with the ultimate goals of restoring airway patency, helping patients wean off ventilation support, and improving their quality of life [7].

As patients progress through sequential treatment phases, airway anatomy and pulmonary function undergo dynamic changes characterized by a cyclical pattern of treatment–repair–reinjury–retreatment. This evolution creates a shifting risk profile that complicates perioperative management across successive interventions, contributing to the elevated complication rates observed in multistage therapy [8, 9].

The 2022 ASA Difficult Airway Guidelines [10]focus on single-event emergency management, lacking a framework for dynamic airway changes. This limitation proves particularly consequential in multistage therapy for tracheal tumors. Current studies on complex airway anesthetic management mainly focus on single high-risk surgery emergency protocols [2, 11], with a lack of standardized risk stratification and dynamic decision-making systems covering the entire multistage course.

The AFSP system is a static scoring tool—it evaluates four dimensions at a single time point. Its “dynamic” nature derives not from the algorithm, but from its serial application across treatment stages. By reapplying the same framework, clinicians can track risk evolution and adapt anesthetic strategies accordingly.

This study constructed the four-dimensional AFSP risk stratification framework, established a dynamic anesthetic decision-making framework, and explored its potential clinical value in airway tumor management.

Clinical data and research methods

Basic case information

A 77-year-old male patient was admitted to our hospital on October 24, 2024, due to “recurrent cough and expectoration for more than 5 months, aggravated with hemoptysis for more than 1 month”. Transnasal bronchoscopy performed in another hospital revealed: a mucosal mass protruding in the lower trachea about 1 cm away from the carina, invading the carina and bilateral main bronchi, with severe luminal stenosis (the endoscope could not pass through) (Fig. 1). The surface of the mass was congested and rough with submucosal vasodilation. Pathological diagnosis was not completed due to the risk of massive hemorrhage and asphyxia, and the patient was transferred to our hospital for further treatment. The patient had no history of chronic diseases such as hypertension or diabetes, and was classified as ASA grade Ⅲ. Physical examination: The patient was in a forced semi-recumbent position, tachypnea. Breath sounds were extremely weak in the left lung, and rhonchi and moist rales were audible in the right lung. Significant chest tightness and shortness of breath occurred after activity, and the patient could not lie flat at night with frequent dry cough when supine. Arterial blood gas analysis (at rest): PaO2 73 mmHg, PaCO2 48 mmHg, pH 7.32. Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 2) showed: luminal stenosis of the tracheal carina and bilateral main bronchi, with a 51 mm × 34 mm soft tissue mass protruding outside the lumen. The left main bronchus was completely occluded, and the diameter of the stenotic segment of the right main bronchus was only 2.04 mm (normal adult diameter: 12–15 mm, stenosis degree: 83.0%).

The AFSP framework was developed for this study and has not been previously published. We reviewed the literature on difficult airways in central airway obstruction and selected the most relevant risk factors, including the Freitag system for grading stenosis [12], the ASA classification, and the surgical complexity grades from pulmonology literature [13, 14]. A group of anesthesiologists and interventional pulmonologists reached consensus on four core domains: Anatomy, Function, Surgery, and Patient.

For each domain we set a 0–3 scale. For Anatomy, we looked at how much the airway was narrowed (≤ 25%, 26%–75%, or ≥ 76%) and whether the lesion was at a tricky spot like the carina or glottis. For Function, we used SpO₂ levels and how symptomatic the patient was. For Surgery, we classified procedures by how invasive they were. For Patient, we used the ASA grades. We gave each domain equal weight, We added up the scores (total 0–12) and split them into four risk levels (I–IV).

The AFSP framework is therefore a preliminary, unvalidated assessment tool intended for hypothesis generation and proof-of-concept application.

Implementation path of the dynamic decision-making framework

The dynamic decision-making framework established in this study centered on the core path: stage risk assessment → targeted prediction of collapse points → individualized strategy formulation → whole-process dynamic monitoring. The specific steps were: ① Preoperative AFSP risk stratification was repeated at each stage, with risk grade updated according to prior treatment response and pathophysiological changes; ② Targeted anesthetic strategies were formulated based on updated risk grade and predicted collapse points, defining induction method, ventilation mode and emergency plan; ③ Core intraoperative indicators including airway pressure, SpO₂and hemodynamics were monitored in real time, with anesthesia depth and ventilation parameters dynamically adjusted; ④ An airway health record was established and regular follow-up performed to monitor airway stenosis and pulmonary function changes.

Formulation and implementation of anesthetic strategy

Preoperative preparation

A multidisciplinary emergency team (anesthesiology, respiratory endoscopy, thoracic surgery, ICU) was established. Prepared equipment included 12#/14# rigid bronchoscopes, transnasal high-flow oxygenation devices, HFJV, VV-ECMO, and various reinforced endotracheal tubes. Perioperative risks were fully explained to the patient’s family with signed informed consent. Multidisciplinary collaboration has been shown to significantly reduce perioperative adverse events in complex airway surgery, particularly for the whole-process management of extremely high-risk airway patients [15].

Intraoperative management

① After entering the operating room, preoxygenation was performed with HFNO, and vital signs were monitored: BP 151/70 mmHg, SpO₂ 96%, HR 74 bpm, RR 24 bpm. Sufentanil 5 µg was intravenously administered in divided doses with spontaneous breathing preserved.② VV-ECMO cannulation was performed under local anesthesia via the right femoral vein (drainage) and right internal jugular vein (infusion). Heparin anticoagulation maintained ACT at 180–220 s. Initial pump speed was 4160 r/min with blood flow 4.25 L/min, and vital signs remained stable after ECMO initiation.③ Anesthetic induction: Propofol 100 mg and rocuronium 50 mg were given intravenously, and the patient was repositioned from semi-recumbent to supine. SpO₂ dropped to 87%. Rigid bronchoscopy was inserted immediately and manual HFJV was started through the side port (Fig. 3, frequency 100–150 bpm, driving pressure 15–20 psi, I:E ratio 1:2). Inspection of the ECMO circuit revealed that the drainage catheter had migrated upward after repositioning, reducing the flow. The catheter was pushed back, pump speed was briefly increased, and HFJV was continued. SpO₂ returned to 99% within 90 s. Surgical operation lasted 80 min. ④ Surgical operation: Rigid bronchoscopy showed a hypervascular mucosal mass 1 cm proximal to the carina, with 85% stenosis of the right main bronchus and 95% stenosis of the left main bronchus. Biopsy, cryoablation, and APC hemostasis were performed on the right-side tumor; balloon dilation reduced stenosis to 40%. The left main bronchus tumor was treated with cryoablation and electrosnare resection, followed by implantation of a 12 mm × 40 mm fully covered metal stent under wire guidance with satisfactory positioning and patency. Estimated blood loss was 20 mL.Pathological examination of the biopsy later confirmed small cell lung carcinoma.

Intraoperative view of manual high-frequency jet ventilation (HFJV) administered via the rigid bronchoscope port

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) postoperatively and remained on ECMO support, for a total ECMO duration of 365 min (from successful cannulation ECMO initiation at 16:55 to decannulation at 23:00). ICU stay was 3 days. The procedure was successful, and the patient was subsequently discharged uneventfully.

The patient was followed up regularly thereafter. Serial chest CT and bronchoscopic examinations were arranged to monitor for disease recurrence, granulation regrowth, or stent-related complications, enabling timely detection and management of any deterioration in airway status.

StageⅡ: stent removal (December16, 2024)

Dynamic risk changes

Following chemotherapy (gemcitabine + carboplatin for 4 cycles, with partial response), the patient developed stent-related infection with increased endoluminal secretions(Fig. 4). As an important palliative treatment for tracheal tumors, stent implantation is associated with an increased risk of respiratory infections [16, 17]. Timely stent removal combined with anti-infective therapy is the key to improving prognosis.Compared with Stage Ⅰ, the patient’s airway status showed dual characteristics of positive changes and new risks.

De-escalated anaesthetic management

ECMO support was discontinued. Rapid sequence induction was performed with superimposed high-frequency jet ventilation (SHFJV: low frequency 10–20 bpm with tidal volume 8–10 mL/kg, high frequency 100–200 bpm, driving pressure 12–18 psi) maintained throughout the procedure, PaCO2 maintained 35–45 mmHg. A predefined haemorrhage emergency protocol was available. Stent removal and airway toilet were completed smoothly. There was almost no bleeding during the entire procedure. Sugammadex was administered for reversal to ensure smooth emergence. The patient was returned to the general ward postoperatively.

Stage Ⅲ: granulation tissue resection ( October 31, 2025)

Risk re-stratification

Post-interventional granulation hyperplasia caused recurrent left bronchial stenosis. Chest CT (Fig. 5) reexamination showed 85% stenosis of the left main bronchus, and bronchoscopy (Fig. 6 ) indicated granulation tissue hyperplasia in the left main bronchus (45 mm × 27 mm). For patients with advanced tracheal tumors, combined chemoradiotherapy and bronchoscopic interventional therapy can prolong survival time, but granulation tissue hyperplasia after treatment is a common cause of recurrent airway stenosis, requiring re-interventional treatment [17].

Discussion

The core anesthetic challenge in multistage complex tracheal neoplasm treatment is the dynamic evolution of airway status. Traditional static decision-making is prone to severe perioperative complications due to insufficient full-process risk prediction and dynamic adaptation. Recent shifts to multidisciplinary comprehensive treatment (interventional surgery, chemoradiotherapy, supportive care) and treatment stage subdivision have further emphasized the need for dynamic anesthetic management adaptation [18].The preliminary AFSP-based dynamic decision-making framework enables accurate risk quantification via four-dimensional assessment and adaptive anesthetic strategy adjustment with disease progression, addressing key challenges in complex airway multistage management.

The core advantage of the AFSP four-dimensional risk stratification framework lies in full-dimensional coverage and quantitative accuracy. The anatomical dimension focuses on airway stenosis degree and lesions at key sites, the functional dimension pays attention to oxygenation status and ventilatory reserve, the surgical dimension evaluates operation stimulation intensity and hemostatic requirements, and the patient dimension considers basic status and comorbidities. The collaboration of the four dimensions can comprehensively avoid the limitations of single-dimensional assessment. In Stage Ⅰ of this case, due to severe bilateral main bronchus stenosis (right main bronchus only 2.04 mm), resting hypoxia, high-stimulation surgery, and advanced age with ASA grade Ⅲ, the AFSP score reached 11 points (Grade Ⅳ), indicating an extremely high risk of routine anesthetic induction, which is the core premise for the preoperative decision to apply ECMO. The necessity of preoperative ECMO application can be elaborated from three aspects: First, the extremely severe airway anatomical stenosis leads to the collapse of oxygenation reserve. The patient’s right main bronchus stenosis was 83.0% (luminal diameter only 2.04 mm), and the left main bronchus was completely occluded. Oxygenation was only maintained by a small amount of ventilation through the right main bronchus. Anesthetic induction is very likely to induce tumor displacement and obstruction of the remaining right main bronchus, resulting in an immediate CICO crisis. ECMO can directly ensure the body’s oxygen supply through extracorporeal circulation, independent of the patient’s own airway ventilation, providing a “safe oxygenation buffer” for anesthetic induction and surgical operation [10, 11]. Second, the high invasiveness of the surgical operation increases the risk of airway loss of control. Stage Ⅰ requires rigid bronchoscopy-guided tumor ablation + stent implantation. Stimulation of the airway mucosa and touching of the tumor during the operation may lead to tumor shedding and massive hemorrhage, which in turn block the airway. The early establishment of ECMO can avoid acute respiratory and circulatory failure caused by airway obstruction, and gain time for intraoperative hemostasis and airway clearance. Third, evidence-based medical evidence supports the prophylactic application of ECMO in extremely high-risk airways. Patients with extremely severe central airway obstruction (luminal diameter < 5 mm) are at markedly elevated risk of CICO during routine anesthetic induction, and prophylactic ECMO has been recommended as a rescue strategy in such high-risk scenarios [19, 20], while prophylactic application of VV-ECMO can substantially reduce such risks, which is consistent with the recommendation in the 2022 ASA Difficult Airway Guidelines that prophylactic ECMO can be used in extremely high-risk airways [10]. Case reports have demonstrated that prophylactic ECMO application in anesthesia for patients with extremely severe central airway obstruction can significantly reduce perioperative mortality [1]. Therefore, preoperative transnasal high-flow preoxygenation and prophylactic establishment of VV-ECMO in this case are essentially based on the comprehensive prediction of the patient’s extreme airway conditions and high surgical risk characteristics, and the construction of a perioperative oxygenation safety barrier through extracorporeal life support technology, fundamentally avoiding fatal airway risks during induction and surgery.

In this case, the transient desaturation during induction did not indicate failure of the ECMO strategy. The drop was caused by catheter migration after repositioning, which temporarily reduced flow, a mechanical issue, not a failure of the system itself. The problem was identified and corrected within 90 s, and oxygenation was restored without airway compromise. If ECMO had not been established, this same event could have escalated into a life-threatening CICO crisis. This experience supports the use of ECMO as a safety buffer in extremely high-risk airway cases, while also emphasizing that its effectiveness depends on vigilant intraoperative monitoring and prompt troubleshooting.

Stage evolution management is the core logic of the dynamic decision-making framework based on AFSP risk stratification. The patient’s airway status in the three stages showed an evolutionary characteristic of “extremely severe stenosis → improved stenosis with infection → granulation hyperplasia and recurrent stenosis”, and the AFSP score decreased from 11 points to 9 points and then remained at Grade Ⅲ. The anesthetic strategy was dynamically adjusted accordingly: Stage Ⅰ adopted an enhanced protection scheme of “ECMO assistance + spontaneous breathing maintenance + transnasal high-flow preoxygenation + HFJV” due to extremely high risk; Stage Ⅱ reduced the risk level and simplified the strategy to “rapid sequence induction + HFJV”, while adding an emergency process for massive hemorrhage to address the risk of stent adhesion; Stage Ⅲ added preoperative psychological intervention on the basis of continuing the core scheme of Stage Ⅱ to address the anxiety of the patient after multiple treatments. This precise matching of “risk grade-strategy intensity” not only avoids overtreatment (such as abandoning ECMO in Stage Ⅱ) but also ensures perioperative safety, fully reflecting the core principle of individualized anesthesia.

Rational application of multimodal ventilation technology supports the dynamic decision-making framework. HFJV in Stage Ⅰ achieves effective ventilation via small tidal volume high-frequency pulses, reducing barotrauma risk; SHFJV in Stage Ⅲ combines low- and high-frequency ventilation advantages, ensuring alveolar oxygenation and improving carbon dioxide elimination efficiency, which is particularly suitable for precise ventilation during granulation tissue resection [21, 22]. The research confirms that the ventilation efficiency and safety of SHFJV in complex airway surgeries are superior to traditional HFJV, which can significantly reduce the incidence of intraoperative hypoxemia and hypercapnia [23]. Notably, the combined application of HFNO and SHFJV has unique advantages in tracheal tumor treatment: HFNO enables effective preoxygenation, improves oxygenation reserve (especially for preoperative resting hypoxia), prolongs safe apnea time during induction [24, 25], and provides a buffer for anesthesia and airway operations. For advanced tracheal tumor patients unable to tolerate intubation or rigid bronchoscopy, SHFJV combined with HFNO serves as a minimally invasive ventilation support, reducing airway stimulation and the risk of tumor bleeding/shedding. For grassroots hospitals without ECMO, HFNO + SHFJV is an effective alternative for anesthetic management of moderate to high-risk tracheal tumor patients. Our previous research also shows HFNO prolongs elderly patients’ induction safe apnea time and reduces hypoxemia incidence [26]. In addition, SJOV is a non-invasive, low-cost ventilation technique that has been used successfully in difficult airway emergencies [27, 28]. Liang et al. [29]and Li et al. [30] both reported CICO scenarios where SJOV maintained oxygenation and ventilation, averting catastrophic outcomes. Therefore, SJOV can also serve as an alternative ventilation strategy in resource-limited settings.

The AFSP score is calculated from a static assessment at each time point. Its clinical value, however, derives not from any single score, but from the temporal trajectory of scores across the patient’s treatment journey. In this case, the AFSP score decreased from 11 (Grade IV) to 9 (Grade III) following successful Stage I treatment, remained stable at 9 during Stage II despite stent infection, and persisted at 9 during Stage III with granulation hyperplasia. This trajectory—not any individual score—guided the de-escalation from ECMO to HFJV and the addition of preoperative psychological intervention. Thus, the ‘dynamic’ claim refers to the serial application of a static tool, analogous to how serial blood pressure measurements enable dynamic hemodynamic monitoring.

This study has limitations: First, as a single-case study with a small sample size, the generalizability of conclusions needs further validation in larger cohorts, and the AFSP scoring system requires optimization with more clinical data. Second, the AFSP system’s assessment of stenosis degree and surgical stimulation intensity relies heavily on subjective clinical experience; integrating AI image measurement technology in the future can improve its quantitative accuracy. Third, the proposed framework is highly dependent on advanced medical resources (e.g., ECMO, rigid bronchoscopes), requiring further simplified procedure optimization to enhance applicability in primary hospitals. Fourth, the AFSP framework has not been externally validated. Future prospective studies are needed to externally validate the framework .
