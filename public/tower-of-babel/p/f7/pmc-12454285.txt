Core-shell engineered Col/Cs@ECM microspheres for macrophage-targeted intracellular drug release in RA therapy
Authors: Xiyue Duan, Kepeng Hu, Jiawei Wang, Xiaozhao Wang, Xiaojun Long, Weiming Lin, Chengwei Wu, Wenjian Weng, Zhangfa Song, Kui Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The imbalance of macrophage polarization between M1 and M2 phenotypes in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) results in a persistent inflammatory cascade. Activating M2 anti-inflammatory polarization, which remove excess extracellular matrix (ECM) via phagocytosis, represents a potential therapeutic target for RA. This study introduces Col/Cs@ECM microspheres, a novel drug delivery system designed for macrophage recognition via a tailored ECM surface, enhancing phagocytic efficiency and accumulation. Moreover, the Col/Cs@ECM microspheres are composed of biocompatible and fully degradable materials, ensuring their safety profile within the physiological environment. Following cell phagocytosis, the collagen/chitosan (Col/Cs) core release the drug (Dexamethasone, Dex) intracellularly to inhibit M1 polarization by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway and to facilitate M2 polarization. This macrophage targeted and intracellular release approach offers a significant advantage over traditional medications by reducing systemic side effects and improving the therapeutic index. The strategy prompts macrophages to express anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, thereby remodeling the immune microenvironment. Additionally, the specially engineered ECM shell of the microspheres extends the anti-inflammatory response by prolonging macrophage lifespan, a feature that is not present in conventional treatments. This results in improved treatment outcomes in an in vivo RA animal model. This research presents a possible intracellular anti-inflammatory treatment approach for rheumatoid arthritis injection therapy with the potential to outperform existing treatments in terms of efficacy and safety.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic inflammation of the joints, leading to pain, swelling, stiffness, and loss of function, severely leading to complications of the heart, lungs and other organs [[1], [2], [3]]. The immune system with RA mistakenly attacks the synovium, leading to inflammation and joint damage [4,5]. Current treatment strategies focus on reducing inflammation, alleviating symptoms, slowing disease progression, and improving quality of life [6]. And the following are treatment options following ACR and EULAR recommendations: NSAIDs like Ibuprofen, Naproxen [7]; DMARDs like Methotrexate, Leflunomide [8]; Biologics like Etanercept, Adalimumab, Tocilizumab [9]; Corticosteroids like Prednisone, Methylprednisolone [10]; but long-term use may result in osteoporosis, weight gain, diabetes, gastrointestinal discomfort, cardiovascular risks, and other side effects [11].

Despite the availability of various treatment options, many RA patients face ongoing disease progression [12]. Notably, the synovial lining macrophages possess high phagocytosis capacities [13,14], and the disruption of macrophage homeostasis is a precursory event to the clinical onset of RA [15]. Consequently, there is a critical need for novel therapeutic strategies. This has led to a surge in interest in macrophage-targeted therapies [16], particularly those focusing on intracellular mechanisms, as they offer promising avenues for the treatment of RA [17]. However, many existing targeted systems face challenges in achieving both efficient recognition and effective intracellular delivery, often relying on passive uptake or targeting ligands that may not inherently couple recognition with the desired immunomodulatory outcome.

The pattern recognition of macrophages, their ability to identify and interact with specific molecular patterns like PAMPs, DAMPs and PRR [18]. The extracellular matrix (ECM) is not only highly biocompatible but also specifically recognizable by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on macrophages [19], which intrinsically linked to the activation of anti-inflammatory pathways, offering a unique opportunity to directly initiate immunomodulation upon recognition [20]. The phagocytosis of ECM components by macrophages during the M2 anti-inflammatory phase presents an opportunity for intracellular anti-inflammatory therapy [21,22].

Recent advances in injectable microspheres for the treatment of RA and other forms of arthritis, such as osteoarthritis, have shown great promise in addressing the dysregulated M1-M2 balance. These microspheres can be engineered to encapsulate therapeutic agents and release them in a controlled manner, targeting the inflamed joints [23]. By modulating the M1-M2 polarization, these microspheres aim to reduce synovial inflammation and promote tissue repair, providing a potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of RA and other inflammatory diseases [24]. Nevertheless, achieving precise intracellular delivery with minimal extracellular leakage remains a significant hurdle for many microsphere systems [25,26], potentially increasing the risk of off-target effects.

In this study, we introduce a novel biocompatible Dex-loaded Col/Cs@ECM microsphere system, fabricated via a simple and cost-effective process. The core innovation lies in leveraging the ECM shell's PRR recognition capability. This enhances phagocytosis and, importantly, directly engages the macrophage's anti-inflammatory polarization pathway upon interaction, potentially initiating an earlier therapeutic response. Efficient endocytosis via PRR recognition delivers the microspheres to the acidic phagolysosomal environment within 24 h, where the Col/Cs core swells and degrades, facilitating rapid and targeted intracellular drug Dex release. This design minimizes extracellular drug exposure, thereby reducing the required dosage and potential systemic side effects.

Finally, our system enhanced the anti-inflammatory capabilities of macrophages in the M2 state, leading to a favorable prognosis via intracellular way precisely. By combining the ECM shell's multiple role in targeted recognition and immunomodulation with the controlled intracellular release from the Col/Cs core, our microspheres offer a distinct drug delivery advantage over conventional systems. Meanwhile, the injection of microspheres could be used as an effective intracellular RA therapy in vivo. Furthermore, this study aims to provide a novel and effective approach for the treatment of RA and other inflammatory diseases by regulating the polarization state of macrophages through the precise delivery of drugs using ECM-shell microspheres.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chitosan, 4-Phenylbutyric acid, and Thimerosal were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). The Collagen I gel was purchased from Beijing Yierkang Bioengineering Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Span 80 and Cytochalasin D were purchased from Sigma Co. (Shanghai, China). The Petroleum ether was purchased from Titan Co. (Shanghai, China). 1-Methoxy-2-propanol, Genipin, and Dexamethasone 21-phosphate disodium salt were purchased from Aladdin Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Liquid paraffin, Ethanol, Acetic acid, and NaOH were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The PBS buffer was purchased from Cellmax Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). The CCK-8 test Kit was purchased from Dojindo Laboratories (Kumamoto, Japan). The 0.4 % Trypan blue was purchased from Yeasen Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The staining antibody were purchase from Abcam and Thermofish Co. (Shanghai, China). The ELISA kits were purchased from Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co. Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

Preparation of dex loaded chitosan-collagen copolymerized microspheres (MS)

The principle of preparation was using less polymerization steps and more elution steps as possible to attenuate the toxic effects of extraneous chemicals. Fortunately, collagen I and chitosan, both soluble in acidic conditions and polymerizable in alkaline media, were copolymerized in a single step by microemulsion preparation method. Chitosan, known for its widespread use as a drug carrier, offers a significant advantage through its pH-sensitive nature, which enables controlled release of encapsulated drugs [27]. Incorporating collagen type I into chitosan microspheres improved macrophage biocompatibility and altered the internal structure [28]. Genipin, a natural and biodegradable cross-linker [29], was used for shaping the Col/Cs microspheres.

Microspheres (MS) were prepared according to a W/O emulsion, and solvent evaporation/extraction method [30]. The external organic phase was composed by 20 mL liquid paraffin and 0.4 mL Span80. The aqueous phase was consisted of chitosan (Cs), Collagen I (Col) and dexamethasone (Dex), magnetic stirring for dissolution in deionized water, and adjusting pH to 4.0 by acetic acid. Notably, the molecular weight of chitosan was about 25,000 Da, and the degree of deacetylation was 85 %. Afterwards, keep the organic phase mechanical stirring at 360 rpm in triple-necked flask and adjust pH to 6.0 by 0.1M NaOH. 1 mL aqueous was dropped in flask and 3 % w/w Genipin was dropped after 30 min for solidification by amino. This emulsion keeps stirring at the room temperature with 550 CMH wind speed of fume hood for 20 h. Subsequently, the obtained suspension was centrifuged and the sediment washed with Petroleum ether, methyl acrylate, ethanol in succession for 2 times each. After all, the MSs were sterilized by 75 % ethanol and stored in −20 °C ultrapure water.

Preparation of ECM micro-sheets

The ECM was obtained from NIH3T3 cells, which were cultured in the environment of 37 °C and 5 % CO2, with the DMEM high glucose medium (Gibco) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (CellMax, SA211.02, Beijing, China) and 1 % antibiotic solution (penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco)). NIH3T3 cells were seeded in a sterile 10 cm-diameter Petri dish (Corning) at a density of 1 × 106 cells within 10 mL medium every dish.

Based on the protocol of a previous study [31,32], the ECM sheet was extracted through freezing and thawing process for 4 times within renewed ultrapure water. During the thawing process, the ECM was fragmented on the vortex in ice-water mixture systems for 10 min until completely fragmented, and collected with 10 mL ultrapure water in centrifuge tubes. Finally, the ECM micro-sheet was stored at −20 °C refrigerator for the next step.

MS surface crosslinked by ECM micro-sheets

2 mL of above MS suspension was added with 100 μL ECM micro-sheet suspension, and magnetic stirring 10min for mixing thoroughly. Then, 20uL of 10 mg/mL Genipin was mixed in and stirred for 5 h at 30 °C thermostatic water bath. Subsequently, the ECM hybrid MS (EMS) were collected by centrifugation at a speed of 6000g at 4 °C for 4 min, washed by ultrapure water for 3 times and finally stored at −20 °C for future use.

It is worth mentioning that, MSs and EMSs all had spontaneous fluorescence emission spectrum from about 400 nm to 700 nm (Fig. S1), possibly attributed to the n–π∗ transition of the C=N, C=O band and the π–π∗ transition of the conjugated C=C band [33,34]. However, further detections about fluorescence were interrupted by MSs’ unique behavior, like flow cytometry, immunofluorescence staining and so on. Subsequently, 0.04 % Trypan Blue exhibited the capacity to diminish spontaneous fluorescence emission spectrum of microspheres by immersion for 10 min [35]. And upon binding to proteins or crosslinked chitosan, the resulting complexes were capable of emitting red fluorescence around 600–720 nm with a maximum emission at 700 nm for following labeling and tracking the location and dynamics of microspheres.

Characterization of MS and EMS

The surface morphology of MSs and EMSs was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, GEMINI-300, Zeiss, Germany). The size of MSs were measured in Fiji software by trainable Weka segmentation function [36]. And the statistical analysis of Feret diameters, which represented the average of the longest and shortest axes of ellipsoids fitted to the particle outlines, initially involved a frequency distribution analysis, followed by a Lorentzian fitting to refine the results.

The FTIR spectrum was analyzed via infrared spectrometer (Nicolet 5700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at a range of 500∼2000 cm−1. The fits of the three area peaks of the protein secondary structure identified in the amide I region by Peakfit software.

Zeta potential was analyzed using a surface Zeta potential tester by electrophoretic light scattering measurement (Zetasizer Nano-ZS, Malvern, UK) at pH = 7.4 PBS buffer.

The hydrophilicity of samples was determined by a contact angle meter (OCA20, Dataphysics, Germany).

Drug release of MS

Pepsin was solved in pH = 4 buffer to simulate lysosome acidic environment. The MSs and EMSs were immersed in above buffer at 37 °C lasting 24 h, and replaced with fresh buffer at the time of each collection. The leaching solution was collected by centrifugation at a speed of 6000g at 4 °C for 4 min in order to measure the amplitude at 245 nm peak position of dexamethasone with UV–VIS. Meanwhile, the dexamethasone standard curve measures by UV–VIS in same condition for concentration calculation and analysis.

Cell isolation and culture of BMDMs

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Zhejiang University and ethically approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Research of the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Ethics Code: 2019-903).

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mice (male, 6 weeks old, Zhejiang Laboratory Animal Center, China) according to previous literature [37]. Briefly, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation to minimize animal suffering. The cells were extracted from the femur and tibia, and were treated with ACK lysis buffer (Boster) (1:10 dilution with ultrapure water) at 4 °C for 3 min to lysis red blood cells, centrifuged and collected with 1300 g for 3 min. Then the cell suspension was cultured in alpha minimum essential media (Gibco) contained 10 % fetal bovine serum (Sigma), 1 % penicillin (10000 mg/mL, Gibco) and streptomycin (10000 units/mL, Gibco), and 30 ng/mL mouse macrophage-colony stimulating factor (M-CSF, R&D Systems) at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 atmosphere. After 5 days culture, more than 85 % pure BMDMs were obtained.

Cell vitality assays

BMDMs were cultured in complete medium and treated under different concentration of MSs in 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well). Then, the CCK-8 solution (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was diluted in medium as 1:10 ratio to culture above treated BMDMs for 2 h. The cell viability was measured with the optical density (OD) of the solution at the wavelength of 450 nm with a microplate reader (MultiskanMK3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Flow cytometry for phagocytosis

Given the problem of autofluorescence of the microspheres at 400–800 nm within the detection ranges, the microspheres were stained with Trypan blue (0.04 % Trypan blue for 10 min at room temperature) to be measured by AF-700 channel on the flow cytometry machine. Thereafter, these MSs were engulfed by BMDMs in different treatments for further detection. The BMDMs were gated based on forward- and side-scatter, and dead cells and doublets were removed. Then cells were marked by PE-F4/80 (Thermo Fisher) to be distinguished with other inessential miscellaneous cells. Eventually, the data of phagocytosis were measured from the frequency of AF-700-positive cells of PE-positive group, and acquired by flow cytometry (Beckman CytoFLEX, America).

Immunofluorescence staining for phagocytosis

BMDMs (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on the 6-well plates and engulfed Trypan blue stained microspheres. The DiO green dye marked the cell membrane in phenol red-free medium (2 μg/mL) and the cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 atmosphere for 60 min. The LysoSensor showed active lysosome of BMDMs in FITC channel and was added in phenol red-free medium (1 μg/mL) lasting 5–60 min at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 atmosphere. The hoechst 33342 dye marked DNA in live cells and was observed in DAPI channel by confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 780, ZEISS, Germany). Especially, 37 °C and 5 % CO2 atmosphere was maintained by the microscope ancillary supporting devices.

On the other hand, BMDMs were fixed in 4 % PFA for 15 min, permeabilized by Triton X-100 after treatment, and blocked by BSA in PBS for 1 h. Alexa-Fluor 488 phalloidin and DAPI (Sigma, USA) were used to stain F-actin and nuclei, respectively. The confocal laser scanning microscope was used to captured pictures of cells. The quantitative analysis of cell phagocytosis was performed by ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry for the polarization of BMDMs

Upon different treatment, the cells were harvested with 0.25 % trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Cellmax), and repeat digestion once to harvested entirely. Single-cell suspensions were blocked with CD16/CD32 (Thermo Fisher) for 10 min at 4 °C prior to surface staining. The following anti-mouse antibodies were used: PE-F4/80 (Thermo Fisher) for distinguishing BMDMs, FITC-MERTK (Biolegend) for phagocytosis intensity, APC-CD86 for macrophage M1 polarization, FITC-CD206 for macrophage M2 polarization [38]. These cells were washed using cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Cellmax) and stored in PBS buffer added thimerosal (Macklin). All flow cytometry data was acquired on Cytoflex (Beckman) and analyzed by FlowJo V10 (TreeStar, Ashland, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining for the polarization of BMDMs

The cell marker expression was marked by rabbit anti-mouse iNOS (Abcam) and mouse anti-mouse CD206 (Abcam) primary antibodies, respectively, represented for M1 polarization and M2 polarization. Then the cells were washed by 0.05 % Tween 20 for 3 times and incubated with Alexa Fluor-555 donkey antirabbit, Alexa Fluor-488 goat anti-mouse fluorescent secondary antibodies (Abcam) and DAPI (Sigma) to counterstain the cells. After staining, the cells were stored in PBS dropped in anti-fluorescence quenching agent (Solarbio) at 4 °C and observed by a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM780, ZEISS, Germany). The ImageJ software was used for the semi-quantitative analysis of the immunofluorescence intensity, and the M2/M1 ratio represented the polarization of BMDMs.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for cytokines secretion

The fresh medium was changed with microspheres adding, and the supernatant after cell treated was collected by centrifuging at the speed of 12000g for 15min. The concentration of cytokines produced by mouse macrophages was detected by IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-10 ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co. Ltd), respectively, in accordance with manufacturer's guidelines, and the absorbance was tested at 450 nm.

ROS expression level measurement with flow cytometer

The ROS expression level of the BMDM after MSs treatment was validated as follows. BMDM cells were co-cultured with MSs for 0–24 h. The control group was untreated. The positive group was exposed to H2O2 (100 μM) for 3 h. Subsequently, the cells were stained with DCFH-DA (10 μM, Sigma–Aldrich, China) for 30 min in 37 °C and 5 % CO2 incubator. Then, the BMDMs were collected in 5 mL flow tubes after DCFH-DA staining. The intracellular ROS levels were measured using a flow cytometer (Beckman) and data analysis was performed using FlowJo.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assay

The BMDMs were seeded in six-well plates (1 × 106 cells per well) and the microspheres were added in. Trizol (Invitrogen) was used to extract the total RNA of cells on the ice box, 600 μL/well, after washed by PBS buffer for 2 min, and stored at −80 °C for future detection. RT-PCR assays were performed using a qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Vazyme) on the fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument QuantStudio 3 (ABI). The detected genes and their primer sequences was shown in Table 1. All the results were finally normalized to the expression of GAPDH. Collagen antibody induced arthritis (CAIA) in C57BL/6

CAIA protocol was used to induce arthritis in C57BL/6 mice, and the arthritis levels were measured to evaluate anti-inflammation response of above microspheres in vivo [39]. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Zhejiang Laboratory Animal Center (Zhejiang, China) and maintained under pathogen-free conditions, and arthritis was induced when mice were 8 weeks old. Mice were housed in groups and maintained in an environment-controlled room at 22 °C. All mice were fully anesthetized under gas anesthesia using isoflurane (2–2.5 %) and to minimize animal suffering. Mice were injected through tail vein with a cocktail of monoclonal antibodies specific for epitopes on type II collagen (500 μg; Chondrex, WA, USA). 4 days after injection of LPS, mice received 50 μg of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Chondrex, WA, USA) through intraperitoneal injection [40]. CAIA mice received an ankle joint injection of 200 μg microspheres 5 days later. All animal experiments were ethically approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Research of the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Ethics Code: 2019-903).

Development of RA was monitored every day for 7 days post-injection of the monoclonal antibody cocktail. The severity of arthritis was scored on scale of 0–4 in a blinded manner as follows: 0 = normal; 1 = mild swelling confined to the ankle joint or tarsal joints; 2 = mild swelling extending to the mid-foot; 3 = moderate swelling extending to the mid-foot; 4 = moderate swelling extending to the metatarsal joints; or 5 = severe swelling encompassing the ankle, foot and digits. The scores for all four paws were averaged to generate the final arthritis score. Histological scoring was performed by three independent investigators [41]. The weight data was measured by scales to evaluate mice growth and inflammation level. The inflammation cytokines were measured by ELISA including RF, anti-CCP, IL-6, TNF-α in serum extracted from blood. The Masson stain of ankle joint or tarsal joints indicated inflammatory cell status. The immunohistochemical staining of CCR7 and CD206 described the M1 and M2 polarization of macrophages.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and each experiment was repeated three times or more independently. The RT-PCR results were performed as the mean ± error. Student's t-test was used for a comparison between the two groups by using SPSS software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Dunnett's test were used to test statistical significance, multiple comparisons and post-hoc test, after data tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. Statistically significances were assigned as follows: when ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. The group of BLK indicates the blank negative control group.

Results

Preparation and characterization of microspheres

Collagen type I and chitosan were copolymerized in a single step by pH-controlled microemulsion preparation method (Fig. 1a). Genipin, a natural and biodegradable cross-linker [42], was used for shaping the Col/Cs microspheres (MSs) and the ECM-shelled Col/Cs microspheres (Col/Cs@ECM microspheres, EMSs). The entire system could be completely degraded and metabolized within a biological organism. Six microsphere formulations were synthesized, including pure Col, and Col: Cs ratios of 1:1, 1:3, 1:5, 1:10, and pure Cs. The MSs’ surface roughness decreased and the internal network architecture compacted with the proportion array above (Fig. 1h). The Feret diameters were 5.00 ± 0.09 μm (Col), 5.51 ± 0.81 μm (1:1), 5.70 ± 0.27 μm (1:3), 5.90 ± 0.16 μm (1:5), 7.35 ± 0.17 μm (1:10), and 6.24 ± 0.18 μm (Cs), (Fig. 1b, Fig. S2). They were controlled in 5–8 μm, a conducive dimension for phagocytosis through the formation of a phagosome, and then fused with lysosome. It is noteworthy that, this process is distinct from pinocytosis, where the cell engulfs fluid and small solutes in small vesicles, as phagocytosis specifically targets larger particles for internalization and degradation. The triple-helix structure of collagen I was maintained in MSs' preparation. The absorption intensity ratios of 1243 cm−1 (AIII) and 1449 cm−1 (AII) was AIII/AII = 0.1042/0.1195 = 0.87 (Fig. 1d), and the high secondary structure percentage of α-helix, 85.52 % (Fig. 1e), approximated the natural collagen [43,44]. The amide A band shifted from 3363 cm−1 in Col MS to 3444 cm−1 in Cs MS gradually (Fig. 1f), which exposed the N–H bond intensity growth. The amide I band shifted to 1655 cm−1, indicating more hydrogen bond coupled with COO− in copolymerized MSs compared to Col MS [45]. Moreover, the C-O-H and C-O-C glycosidic bonds peak at 1075 cm−1, was crucial for chitosan's swelling behavior in acidic conditions [46]. In summary, effective MS copolymerization resulted in a compact structure, maintaining swelling and drug delivery properties.

Dexamethasone (Dex), a potent anti-inflammatory, was encapsulated in MSs at 1 %, 3 %, 5 %, and 10 % weight percentages (Fig. 1i). The acidic swelling of chitosan, coupled with collagen's vulnerability to acidic hydrolysis, aligns with the lysosome's acidic environment, enabling precise and controlled drug release within cells following phagocytosis [47,48]. And the controlled Dex release from 10 mg MS in pH = 4.0 phosphate buffer (Fig. 1k). This encapsulation strategy shielded Dex from extracellular degradation and ensured sustained release within cells over 24 h. The final concentration of released Dex were 146.6 μg (1 wt%), 210.2 μg (3 wt%), 335.5 μg (5 wt%), 361.2 μg (10 wt%). The measured Dex loading capacity of 10 % weight group was 36.12 μg/mg. Furthermore, the 1:5 ratio loading 5 % Dex group were measured the highest drug release efficiency among 1:1, 1:3, 1:5, 1:10, four Col/Cs ratio microsphere groups (Fig. S3).

ECM micro-sheets encapsulated the MSs with a harmonizing scale (Fig. 1j) and were identified by FT-IR (Fig. 1l and Fig. S4a). The ECM shell coverage rate was 88.2 %, examining a total of 190 microspheres’ SEM images analysis. During the coating process, SEM imaging suggested a tendency towards monolayer coverage of the ECM on the microspheres. Therefore, the shell thickness was estimated as the thickness of ECM micro-sheets, which was around 100 nm. The ECM secondary protein structures, with α-helix, β-sheets, and β-turn contents were 51.73 %, 41.19 %, and 7.08 %, respectively (Figure S4bc). The enhanced absorbance occurred at 3444 cm−1 (amide A band) and 1655 cm−1 (amide I band), indicated the amide bond (CO-NH) formed (Fig. 1l). The middle absorbance occurred at 2922 cm−1 (amide B band), 1449 cm−1 (amide Ⅱ band), 1075 cm−1 (amide Ⅲ band) between ECM and MS.

Earlier studies demonstrated that negative Zeta potential microspheres were in advantage of macrophage phagocytosis [28]. MSs was about −10 to −20 mV in PBS buffer, and the lowest was 1:5 Col/Cs ratios (Fig. 1g). The Zeta potential decreased following ECM combined (Fig. 1m), might resulted from surface carboxyl groups being masked in ECM [49]. As for 4 h, 8 h and 24 h treatment, the EMS enhanced BMDMs’ biocompatibility significantly (Fig. 1n). Within 4 h, the ECM surface brought additional cell viability and no significant cell damage upon treatment with the inhibitor (Fig. S5a). Moreover, the addition dose of microspheres had minimal impact towards the macrophage cell viability (Fig. S5b). The phagocytosis of the microspheres by cells does not induce significant cell death evaluated by Annexin V/PI flow cytometry analyses (Fig. S6).

As for the long-time security consideration, the degradation of microspheres was monitored over 1, 3, 7, and 10 days, separately (Fig. S7). The 1:1, 1:5, 1:10 three ratio microspheres were immersed in simulated physiological fluid (SPF), and the 1:5 group exhibited the fast degradation speed. Furthermore, the degradation kinetics of these microspheres over 10 days fit a single first-order kinetic model, predicting that complete degradation will occur within 1–3 months.

The ECM shell of MS improved BMDM phagocytic capacity

To better visualize the alterations in phagocytosis, we employed cytochalasin D, an inhibitor that disrupts f-actin dynamics. This disruption resulted in a significant impairment of the phagocytosis process, and the EMSs were endocytosed completely with inhibitor but MSs scattered outside the cells (Fig. 2e and f). The extracellular matrix (ECM) shell was derived from the culture of mouse fibroblasts. The ECM, being a complex network of proteins and carbohydrates, contains various molecular patterns that can be recognized by macrophages. These patterns act as signals that can trigger phagocytic responses and facilitate the phagocytosis of macrophages.

To investigate the optimal concentration of EMSs, a series of experiments were conducted with 5 μg/mL (L), 10 μg/mL (M), 20 μg/mL (H) (Fig. 2g). MERTK, a membrane receptor, correlated with the phagocytic efficacy. Both MS and EMS treatments enhanced MERTK expression, and EMS outperformed the MS group especially with inhibitor. In comparison, the ECM group showed lower than BLK group after 12 h, which suggests that the phagocytosis may be completed at the 12 h.

With increasing of ECM shell, the percentage of cells containing microspheres significantly improved (Fig. 2h). The phagocytic capacity decreased across all groups with inhibitor, and the accumulation in the H group was higher than L group. The same conclusion in Q2 gate's percentage with inhibitor, the M group exhibited lower phagocytic accumulation than both the L and MS groups (Fig. 2i), which due to the disruptiveness by the inhibitor.

As for microspheres with varying compositions, ECM consistently enhanced phagocytosis, particularly under inhabitation, effectively restoring their phagocytic activity (Fig. S9). Furthermore, the higher EMS concentration, the more phagocytosis outcome. So, the H group concentration (20 μg/mL) was adopted for subsequent studies.

When the inflammatory response is intense, macrophages typically exhibit elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing the inflammatory status. In this study, the ECM shell significantly reduced intracellular FITC-ROS levels for all component ratios of MSs, and the 1:5 EMS was the best (Fig. 3b). This finding suggests that the ECM shell can effectively modulate the inflammatory response in macrophages. Actually, during the resolution of inflammation and tissue repair stages in RA, the ECM components interact with the pattern receptors of macrophages, aiding in the regulation of the anti-inflammatory response.

Meanwhile, the ECM shell enhanced phagocytic accumulative capacity across the three different time periods (Fig. 3d). And a higher content of chitosan correlated with an increased phagocytic uptake. Similarly, the Q2 percentages treated by EMSs were all larger than the MSs group (Fig. 3c). Meanwhile, the variability in MERTK expression among different microsphere groups was notable at 12 h (Fig. S10), and it was mitigated with the ECM shell. The 1:3 and 1:5 EMS groups exhibiting comparatively elevated expression levels. So, the ECM shell improved the cellular recognition and phagocytic process.

Upon ECM shell, the higher collagen content in the microspheres was more conducive to the CD206 expression at 3 h (Fig. S11). By 24 h, the differences among the groups tended to converge. The ratio of M2/M1 with EMS treatment were significantly enhanced compared to MSs (Fig. 3e and f), and the 1:5 EMS group was the highest. However, the anti-inflammatory effects showed minimal difference after 24 h (Fig. S12), suggested the degradation aligning with the earlier results. The higher MS concentration induced more M2 shift, and the EMS concentration influenced less (Fig. S13). These all indicated that the ECM shell was the key determinant in BMDM M2 polarization, overshadowing concentration.

As a result, the choice of a 1:5 ratio EMS was based on a comprehensive analysis of several key factors. First of all, the ECM shell was recognized by BMDM to promote phagocytosis and M2 polarization. Structurally, this ratio had the most negative zeta potential for the best stability and potential for effective encapsulation of Dex drugs [51]. From a drug loading perspective, the looser network facilitated drug encapsulation, while its faster degradation rate ensured controlled and timely drug release. Finally, in terms of biological effects, although the 1:5 ratio was not the best for phagocytosis, but exhibited the best anti-inflammatory polarization effect.

The distinct trends by Dex loaded microspheres treatment were shown in MERTK expression, MS accumulation in BMDM (Fig. 4f), and MERTK detail histogram (Fig. S15). Then the analysis as followed (Fig. 4g), the 6 h MERTK expression was highest in MS group, followed by EMS group, and the lowest was EMS + Dex group. Then, all groups decreased at 12 h. The accumulation in EMS groups increased from 6 h to 12 h, suggested that ECM accelerates the phagocytic process. Whereas the accumulation of MS group decreased due to degradation. As a result, Dex prolonged the duration of phagocytosis, and translated into a sustained subsequent immune polarization process.

The Dex treatment effectively cleared ROS, and the optimal Dex concentration with CD206 expression levels was 5 μL/mL (Fig. 4h). At 3h, Significant CD206 expression differences were among the groups, and by 24 h, the expression levels stabilized and increased. So Dex could prolonged the anti-inflammatory properties in the microspheres, and the best was 5 % Dex loaded EMS group (Fig. 4i). Similarly, the 3 % and 5 % Dex MS and EMS groups demonstrated the best anti-inflammatory outcomes (Fig. 4j at 9 h and Fig. S16 at 24 h). Additionally, the anti-inflammatory effected by MS + Dex dosages, all groups gradually increased, only the 10 % group exhibited decreased trend (Fig. S17). So, the 5 % Dex was the best, and the 10 % was excessively high.

The optimal M2/M1 polarization ratio was at the 9 h following EMS and EMS + Dex group. Moreover, the Dex released completely and promoted anti-inflammatory process at 24h (Fig. 4k). The 48 h timepoint was even better, which may due to the activity loss of untreated macrophages. Consequently, the depiction of polarization timeline was not straightforward but rather full of twists and turns, due to the complexity of phagocytosis, drug release and polarization activities. Furthermore, the delivery of Dex into the cells (MS + Dex) resulted in a more effective anti-inflammatory response compared to extracellular way (MS++Dex), and the best timepoint was 9 h (Fig. 4l). Both the intracellular and extracellular methods exhibited similar cellular viability changes over time (Fig. S18).

The TNF-α and IL-10 ELISA tests represented that the groups containing Dex showed a significant inhibition of M1 polarization and promotion of M2 polarization at 3h, with this effect diminishing by 12 h (Fig. 4m). Additionally, the MS + Dex group exhibited a more pronounced effect compared to the EMS + Dex group at 3 h, indicating that the ECM shell delayed the release of Dex, allowing for a sustained intracellular action of the drug.

The temporal dynamic process of phagocytosis and anti-inflammatory polarization were fluctuated under Dex loaded microspheres treatment (Fig. 4n). The MS group demonstrated complete phagocytosis by 12 h, while ECM modification enhanced the phagocytic process peak and augmenting cumulative phagocytosis. Dex treatment delayed the phagocytic peak and extended its duration. Assuming M1 polarization as negative and M2 polarization as positive. The MS group exhibited fluctuations around the zero value, whose polarization reached its trough at 3 h, then increased to its peak at 9 h before declining back to negative values, and eventually returned to near zero at 24 h. In comparison, the EMS group maintained a M2 polarization trend over the whole time. Dex significantly reduced the fluctuation amplitude and increased the M2 polarization trend beyond 24h.

To elaborate on the observed dynamic polarization pattern, a multifactorial mechanism was proposed to explain this phenomenon, particularly the peak at 9 h followed by a decline. The EMS + Dex group was taken as an example. The complex interplay of ECM recognition, drug release kinetics, microsphere metabolism, and cellular response characteristics was considered to contribute to this fluctuation. Initially, ECM recognition by macrophages pushed the polarization towards an M2 state temporarily, causing a peak. Then, a burst release of Dex led to a rapid induction of M2 polarization. However, as the drug concentration increased, cellular receptors were assumed to have reached saturation, and the response was considered to have become partially reversible. Around the 9 h mark, the combined effects of the initial drug burst, optimal microsphere swelling facilitating drug release, and the cell's capacity for M2 polarization were believed to have converged, resulting in the observed peak. Subsequently, the rate of Dex release slowed down, while the microspheres were observed to degrade actively. This degradation process was thought to have temporarily consumed cellular energy, potentially reducing the cell's capacity to sustain the polarization state, thus causing the decline observed after 9h. By 24 h, as the initial burst effect waned and the degradation process completed, the Dex or ECM-induced anti-inflammatory mechanism took over, allowing the M2 polarization effect to recover.

In summary, the Dex facilitated phagocytosis, and then the ECM shell further augmented and stabilized the effect, extending its therapeutic action beyond 24 h. The macrophage polarization process did not adhere to a linear progression, with Dex loading significantly amplifying the anti-inflammatory response. Notably, the 5 % Dex demonstrated the highest efficacy, while the ECM shell exerted its primary influence during the initial recognition. The 9 h time point emerged as the optimal moment for polarization, with the beneficial effects sustainable for at least 48 h post-treatment.

The specific transcriptions were compared by 3 h and 24 h time-sequential control (Fig. 5b) and the therapeutic process was divided into four main steps (Fig. 5c). Firstly, the ECM shell was identified by RANK, a membrane-bound protein, and upregulated this pattern recognition gene transcription, initiated the phagocytosis response. Secondly, the microspheres were engulfed by macrophages, consumed in phagolysosome and released the drug Dex, which leading to the downregulation of NF-κB pathway and an up-and-down regulation of STAT6 pathway from 3 h to 24 h. Finally, the polarization was regulated, with the downregulation of TNF-α (M1) and the upregulation of CD206 (M2), influencing the release of cytokines, thereby modulating the anti-inflammatory immune microenvironment.

To further elucidate the role of NF-κB and STAT6 signaling pathways, the immunofluorescence staining was in addition to qPCR analysis of mRNA. The p65 subunit of NF-κB was labeled with green fluorescence. The percentage of p65 localized within the nucleus was significantly decreased in the EMS + Dex group compared to the MS + Dex group (Fig. 5d). This observation suggested that NF-κB signaling is suppressed at 24 h. Concurrently, the increased phosphorylation of STAT6, forming p-STAT6, correlated with enhanced anti-inflammatory properties and M2 polarization. The expression pattern of p-STAT6 in the EMS + Dex group exhibited a temporal fluctuation that precisely mirrored the previously observed regulation of M2/M1 polarization timing (Fig. 5e). This congruence indicated that the STAT6 signaling pathway is absolutely a critical factor.

Furthermore, we analyzed the gene expression levels of lysosome-associated proteins, specifically TMEM175, CLCN7, TRPV5, and MMP9. As depicted in the first three panels of Fig. 5f, the expression of these relevant genes was significantly elevated in the groups treated with microspheres, suggesting a correlation between microsphere phagocytosis and lysosomal activity. Notably, as shown in the fourth panel, MMP9 expression was upregulated in the EMS + Dex group. Since MMP9 is an extracellular matrix metalloproteinase, this finding further corroborates the digestion of the ECM by lysosome.

Collectively, these findings provide evidence at both the genetic and protein levels, provided a comprehensive explanation of macrophages anti-inflammatory polarization shift upon phagocytosing microspheres, which focused in NF-κB/STAT6 signal pathway. Actually, macrophages have an intricate network of gene dynamic activation, and exhibit a complex interplay of genes, that cannot be simplistically categorized as pro-inflammatory genes or anti-inflammatory genes.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a novel Dex-loaded Col/Cs@ECM microsphere system with potent therapeutic effects on rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and the preparation and therapeutic process of the microsphere system was illustrated in Fig. 7. While current RA treatment strategies, including NSAIDs, DMARDs, biologics, and corticosteroids, have improved patient outcomes, they often face limitations such as systemic side effects, suboptimal targeting to the inflamed synovium, and the need for frequent administration [10]. For instance, corticosteroids, while effective, frequently lead to osteoporosis, weight gain, and cardiovascular risks upon long-term use [11]. Similarly, although biologics offer targeted therapy, they can be expensive and may lose efficacy over time or induce immunogenicity. Recent advances in drug delivery systems, particularly microspheres, offer promising avenues to address some of these limitations by enabling localized and sustained drug release [24]. However, even within this field, challenges remain, such as achieving precise targeting to specific cell populations within the inflamed joint and ensuring efficient intracellular drug delivery to modulate key cellular processes like macrophage polarization. The recognition that synovial macrophages play a central role in RA pathogenesis [13] has spurred interest in macrophage-targeted therapies. Our system builds upon these concepts but introduces distinct advantages.

The microspheres, with their distinctive ECM shell and Col/Cs core, are meticulously designed through a process that utilizes highly biocompatible and inherently anti-inflammatory materials, all of which are biodegradable within the human body [52]. This fabrication approach ensures the preservation of the materials' protein secondary structure, maintaining their desirable characteristics in the final product. Furthermore, the microspheres possess a negative surface charge, promoting phagocytosis by macrophages, and cytological assessments have verified their excellent biocompatibility. Additionally, in vitro degradation studies revealed the microspheres may exist for 1–3 months in SPF.

A key innovation of our Dex-loaded Col/Cs@ECM microspheres lies in the utilization of an ECM shell. As highlighted in our introduction section, the ECM is not only highly biocompatible but also specifically recognizable by macrophage pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). This allows for preferential targeting of macrophages, a mechanism distinct from the systemically administered drugs like corticosteroids or DMARDs. Upon interaction, the ECM shell engages PRRs, initiating a recognition process that avoids inflammatory pathway activation [3], unlike some DAMPs that can trigger inflammation. Furthermore, this engagement enhances phagocytosis via MERTK signaling, facilitating internalization. Simultaneously, the ECM shell contributes to prolonging macrophage viability, sustaining the anti-inflammatory response. This targeted engagement and internalization represent a significant advantage over strategies that rely solely on passive accumulation or surface modifications for targeting.

Once internalized, the microspheres leverage their hybrid porous Col/Cs core structure, with an optimized Col/Cs ratio of 1:5 for high drug loading capacity, phagocytosis enhancement and M2 polarization, to deliver Dex directly into the phagolysosome. The acidic environment triggers microspheres swelling and efficient Dex release, ensuring intracellular drug action. This intracellular delivery strategy contrasts sharply with conventional systemic administration, where much of the drug may remain extracellular or be cleared before reaching its target site within macrophages. Clinical trials and preclinical studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of Dex in RA therapy, and recent advancements in drug delivery systems have enhanced this potential [53]. With the best 5 % concentration, we achieved the highest CD206/CD86 (M2/M1) ratio at 9 h and optimized therapeutic efficacy within 24 h. Our internal delivery was better than external release by depressing NF-κB/STAT6 signal pathway and reducing the secretion of IL-1, TNF-α, and TGF-β. And the ECM shell further prolongs this effect. This innovative intracellular therapy system not only improves the effectiveness of Dex treatment but also reduces the frequency of dosing and minimizes systemic side effects.

Finally, the Dex-loaded Col/Cs@ECM microsphere system has enhanced the M2/M1 ratio, anti-inflammatory capabilities of macrophages via an intracellular route. The research demonstrated the therapy effectiveness of microspheres in a murine model of rheumatoid arthritis. Following intra-articular injection, the mouse body weight improved and histological recovery was observed in the joint tissue. The increased CD206 (M2) and decreased CCR7 (M1) through IHC staining indicated a shift towards a more anti-inflammatory environment. Additionally, serum ELISA tests revealed significant reductions in inflammatory markers such as RF, anti-CCP, IL-6, and TNF-α. These findings suggest that the microspheres could potentially offer both local and systemic therapeutic benefits in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis. Future detailed studies will aim to further validate observations and explore the underlying mechanisms of how the microspheres influence macrophage phenotype deeply.

All in all, the microspheres are composed entirely of biologically derived materials rather than synthetic polymers, as an effective therapy in vivo. The targeted delivery to macrophages, which play a crucial role in amplifying inflammatory responses, combined with the subsequent intracellular drug release, ensures a precise and sustained anti-inflammatory effect. Moreover, the versatility of our system allows for the adaptation to other diseases by adjusting the drug type and dosage encapsulated within the microspheres. In clinical applications, the microspheres offer the advantage of the lesion site injection, with a single administration providing efficient treatment, as validated in mouse studies. Moving forwards, the innovative strategy of Dex-loaded Col/Cs@ECM microspheres injection deserves further exploration for RA and potentially other immune-mediated disorders.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully developed a novel Dex-loaded Col/Cs@ECM microsphere system with significant therapeutic potential for RA. The unique design of the microspheres, featuring a biocompatible ECM shell and a Col/Cs core, ensures targeted macrophage therapy while maintaining excellent biocompatibility. The ECM shell recognition process activates microsphere engulfment, and prolongs macrophage viability, sustaining the anti-inflammatory response. The optimized Col/Cs ratio and acidic lysosome swelling facilitate precise and efficient intracellular Dex release, leading to improved therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, this internal delivery system outperforms external release by depressing the NF-κB/STAT6 signal pathway and reducing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The therapy system is administered through a single injection, reducing the frequency of dosing and side effects, eliminating the need for surgery and postoperative care. Overall, the Dex-loaded Col/Cs@ECM microsphere system represents a promising therapeutic strategy for RA both in vitro and in vivo and potentially other immune-mediated disorders, paving the way for future clinical applications.
