Application and clinical utility assessment of natural language processing-based software for copy-number variants interpretation
Authors: Songchang Chen, Chang Liu, Xiaorui Luan, Yuling Wang, Yuexin Xu, Yunshuang Li, Fenjiao Zhang, Weihui Shi, Xuanyou Zhou, Chenming Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManual interpretation of copy-number variant (CNV) according to the guideline published by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Clinical Genome Resources (ClinGen) in 2020 is labor-intensive and time-consuming. The application of natural language processing (NLP)-based software like CNVisi can reduce the burden of CNV interpretation, but its clinical utility needs to be further evaluated.MethodsWe firstly used 1000 CNVs which had been previously manually classified to assess the performance of CNVisi. To assess the clinical utility of CNVisi, we collected 5861 CNVs from 2443 next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based CNV sequencing (CNV-seq) samples. The CNVs were first classified by CNVisi and then reviewed by genetic experts. After removing duplicates, the remaining 3384 CNVs were used for assessment of classification consistency, and 154 CNVs that met the reporting rules were finally selected for further analysis.ResultsThe overall accuracy of CNVisi in distinguishing pCNVs (Pathogenic or Likely Pathogenic CNVs) was 97.7% (977/1000) in preliminary assessment of performance. And the accuracy of CNVisi in assessment of clinical utility was 99.6% (3370/3384). Among 154 CNVs that met clinical reporting rules, 23 CNVs were classified with disagreement between CNVisi and genetic experts. According to the reporting rules, total CNVs were classified with a high consistency of 98.6% (5781/5861) between genetic experts and CNVisi, and the CNV-seq results of 96.9% (2367/2443) samples could be accurately and efficiently interpreted by CNVisi. Background

Copy-number variants (CNVs) are prevalent throughout the genome and have been identified as significant contributors to a range of genetic disorders [1]. Evaluation of genomic CNVs is now recommended as a first-tier diagnostic test for fetuses with prenatal ultrasound anomalies, as well as for cases with autism, intellectual disability, developmental delay, and multiple congenital anomalies [2]. Several methodologies have been established for the identification of fetal CNVs in prenatal diagnosis, including karyotyping, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), and next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based CNV sequencing (CNV-seq), all of which are currently recommended for clinical application. Microarray-based methods and CNV-seq have enabled the identification of CNVs with increasing resolution, progressing from chromosomal-level analysis to single-gene and even single-exon precision. In a large prospective cohort of 3429 high-risk pregnant women referred for amniocentesis, CNV-Seq demonstrated a 1% higher yield of CNVs compared to what would have been expected from karyotyping alone [3]. As technological advancements have enhanced the resolution of CNV detection methods, more and more novel and rare CNVs have been discovered, complicating the interpretation of CNVs and genetic counseling. The rate of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) of fetuses referred for ultrasound anomalies, detected by high-resolution Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array, was 6.7% (7/104), which is significantly higher and more than double than detecting by lower resolution arrays [4]. Furthermore, the subjectivity inherent in clinical laboratory geneticists’ assessments may lead to disagreements in variant classifications and limit the consistency across laboratories. Correctly and accurately determining the clinical significance of CNVs is crucial for the option of prenatal diagnosis and can help medical management strategies.

The interpretation of CNVs is complex and exhibits variability between laboratories. To further refine the process for CNVs classification and promote consistency in interpretation and reporting, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Clinical Genome Resources (ClinGen) updated the guideline of CNVs interpretation and reporting in 2020 [5]. This guideline incorporates an evidence-based scoring framework to ensure precise classification into a five-tier system. The evidence categories include genomic content (Sect. 1); evaluation of dosage sensitivity (Sect. 2); gene number (Sect. 3); evidence based on cases in the published literature, public databases and/or internal lab data, and case–control and population evidence (Sect. 4); and inheritance pattern and family history for the patient under investigation (Sect. 5). The pathogenic classifications of CNVs are divided into five categories based on the sum of the evidence point values: pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), VUS, likely benign (LB), or benign (B). The ACMG-ClinGen guideline offers a comprehensive and authoritative classification system, but implementation in the process of CNV interpretation remains challenging. According to the guideline, gathering evidence for specific CNVs requires extensive use of various tools and databases, making the process of interpretation both complex and time-consuming. The use of different databases in the CNV interpretation process may also lead to inconsistent classification [6]. Additionally, some information may not be captured by current genetic databases but only exists in published papers, so clinical experts have to spend a long time searching literatures for CNV interpretation. The subjectivity of the clinician and differences in understanding of guideline may influence the score of some evidences, leading to conflicting CNV classifications. Due to the lack of a standardized pipeline, the consistency and reproducibility of CNV interpretation cannot be guaranteed within the same lab or across different labs. Given these limitations, there is an urgent need for a highly automated and standardized tool capable of streamlining the CNV interpretation process and ensuring consistency and reproducibility.

Several semi-automated tools have been developed to address these challenges, such as ClassifyCNV and AutoCNV [7, 8]. These tools automate the search for information related to CNV interpretation from various databases, simplifying the process of assigning scores for some evidences and achieving higher consistence with manually evaluated pathogenicity categories. However, some tools lack a web-based version, reducing their clinical availability. Alternatively, they still require the input of evidence values by searching and browsing published literatures [9], which are characteristic of time-consuming and low-automated. Moreover, most of these tools only use CNVs in public databases to evaluate the performance, but do not assess the clinical utility in a large scale. With the development of artificial intelligence (AI), the application of some algorithms can assist in interpreting CNVs. Yang et al. [10] developed a natural language processing (NLP) algorithm to extract disease diagnoses, copy number changes, and CNV locations from the published literature, then generated a database to display the relationship between CNVs and diseases. CNVisi is a software for automated CNV interpretation and generation of diagnostic reports, integrating multiple databases and applying NLP methods for analyzing historical clinical reports to develop knowledge bases for interpretation. This software can be integrated into NGS analysis pipelines, and significantly reduce the manual labor for CNV interpretation. In this study, we assessed the clinical utility of CNVisi and demonstrated that the automated CNV interpretation software was a good assistant for clinical experts by enhancing their overall work efficiency.

Methods

Automated CNV annotation and interpretation based on a three-step NLP method

CNVisi is a software designed to automatically interpret CNVs and generate reports following the 2020 ACMG-ClinGen guideline, which was developed on the basis of the previous study [11] with some modifications. It takes CNV region, gender, and variant type as input. Relevant CNV details are retrieved from six knowledge bases for annotation (Fig. 1A). If the CNV has similar genomic coordinates to a previously reported one, annotation information for this CNV will be displayed in the CNVisi. According to the scoring metric of the ACMG-ClinGen guideline, CNVisi evaluates and assigns suggested default point value for some evidence, which can be changed by user. Then the final score is automatically calculated and the corresponding CNV classification is generated. Finally, a clinical report is automatically directly generated for rapid review by clinical experts based on the annotation and interpretation.

Information from public databases and in-house historical Labeled Corpus were combined to build six knowledge bases for annotation (Fig. 1B, Table S1). In-house historical Labeled Corpus was developed by applying NLP methods to convert historical clinical reports. These reports often contain multiple CNVs and explanatory information within a single paragraph. Therefore, a three-step NLP approach was developed for paragraph segmentation, CNV-paragraph matching, and corpus classification.

Firstly, the original paragraph was segmented into several sub-paragraphs, so that each sub-paragraph referred to a result explanation of one specific CNV (Fig. 1B). The whole paragraph was first segmented by comma or period, and the first sentence of each report was chosen as positive training data. Other sentences from one-CNV reports (reports that only contain one CNV with related description) were chosen as negative training data. The testing dataset consisted of 11,611 prenatal and 18,624 products of conception diagnostic reports. Jieba with parameter HMM = True for Chinese text segmentation and TF-IDF for encoding were applied. After that, all sentences were classified as “begin” or “not begin” by the Naive Bayes Model, where “begin” referred to the first sentence of CNV explanation. Finally, all sub-paragraphs were generated according to the “begin” sentence.

where chr_score, type_score, cyto_score, and length_score were binary values determined by whether chromosome, variant type, cytoband, CNV length were identical in CNVs and sub-paragraphs. For different type of scores, these values were calculated according to whether related keywords appear in sub-paragraphs. In addition, empirical values were applied as the weights for different type of scores based on the data set. The sub-paragraph with the highest score was considered the most relevant for a given CNV. The performance of paragraph segmentation combined with CNV-paragraph matching had been estimated in previous study [11], which achieved an overall accuracy of 99.22%. In detail, 5646 out of 30,235 reports contain more than one CNV or aneuploidy. By excluding 44 reports with incorrect results, 37,175 sub-paragraphs were eventually obtained for corpus classification.

Finally, all sentences in sub-paragraphs were classified into six categories, namely, Basic, Aneuploid, Syndrome, Gene, Paper, and Patient, and these categories were integrated into Labeled Corpus (Fig. 1B). A pool-based active learning strategy was applied for corpus classification when no previous Labeled Corpus existed (Fig. 1A). And a keyword string matching method was used to produce the inaccurate label for sentences to generate programming training datasets. For example, if a key-word such as “literature” was in one sentence, this sentence would be classified as Paper. For classification, GloVe model was firstly applied to pretrain 200 dimension token-level vectors, and the vectors were trained on Chinese Wikipedia, in-house historical reports and Chinese Human Phenotype Ontology (CHPO) database. Next, sentence vectors were represented by the average of token-level vectors, and then, a bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory with Conditional Random Field (biLSTM + CRF) model was used for classification. The classification model was trained based on the inaccurate label and sub-paragraphs were chosen for labeling by clinical geneticists if any three consecutive sentences were classified as three different categories and if the number of different categories were greater than three in one sub-paragraph. The label for the final classification model was based on those labels from clinical geneticists as well as from programming training dataset generation. Only 815 sub-paragraphs among 37,175 were labeled, and the F1-scores for Syndrome, Gene, and Paper were 0.9381, 0.8645, and 0.9195. After manual corrections, 15,169, 17,575, 2260, 1304, 3173, and 1073 records were obtained for Basic, Aneuploid, Syndrome, Gene, Paper, and Patient, respectively. These records were further applied in developing knowledge bases. Moreover, all knowledge bases can be updated periodically. NLP methods were also applied to analyze historical clinical reports, and eventually biLSTM + CRF was applied in supervised learning for updating Labeled Corpus (Fig. 1A). The previous Labeled Corpus were used as a training data set, and new sub-paragraphs as a testing data set. The final classification results were further evaluated by clinical geneticists. The knowledge bases only contain evidences rather than classification at a given point in time, so classification in historical reports do not affect current variant classification.

In order to enhance its practicality and universally, we continuously update and improve CNVisi. For instance, we train the model based on more historical clinical reports, while routinely updating databases. In conclusion, CNVisi can provide structured information for genetic experts, which significantly reduces the time required for searching across multiple databases and streamlines the process of gathering evidences for CNV interpretation.

Preliminary assessment of CNVisi performance

This study has been reviewed and approved by the internal review board at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University (OGHFU) (2022-46). A total of 1000 CNVs were collected for preliminary evaluation of the performance on CNVs pathogenic classification. All CNVs were obtained from the historical reports and had been previously manually evaluated by clinical laboratory geneticists. Then, we used CNVisi to automatically classify these CNVs. The consistency of CNVs pathogenic classifications were compared between CNVisi and genetic experts. In this study, all CNVs with minimum length of 100 kb were identified on the CNV-seq platform, and the hg19/GRCh37 was used as reference for calling CNVs.

The performance of CNVisi on clinical utility

We collected 5861 CNVs from 2443 subjects referred for CNV-seq at OGHFU between January 2023 and September 2024. These CNVs were first automatically interpreted by CNVisi, then reviewed and revised by genetic experts at OGHFU to generate final clinical reports. After removing duplicate CNVs, 3384 CNVs were selected to assess the clinical utility of CNVisi. The accuracy was calculated by comparing the classification of CNVs by CNVisi and the classification by experts. Several CNVs were selected for further analysis of clinical utility based on the following rules. According to the consensus recommendation for the interpretation and reporting of copy number variation and regions of homozygosity in prenatal genetic diagnosis [12], reporting P and LP CNVs. For VUS, deletions ≥ 500 kb and duplications ≥ 1 Mb are generally reported. Therefore, a total of 154 CNVs were selected according to this rule for further analyzing the reason of inconsistent classification.

Results

The assessment of CNVisi performance on CNV pathogenic classification

We first estimated the performance of CNVisi for CNV pathogenic classification. The CNVs used to assess classification performance were obtained from the historical reports. So their correct classification had previously been manually evaluated by genetic experts following ACMG-ClinGen guidelines and was considered as the standards. To demonstrate the automatically annotation and interpretation of CNVisi, we introduced a CNV (chr4: 153,260,000-190,940,000_del, GRCh37/hg19) as an example. The genomic coordinate and variant type of this CNV were taken as input. Related details corresponding to the evidence were retrieved from six knowledge bases for annotation and finally automatically generated the classification of CNV (Fig. 2). According to the annotation result, this deletion spanned protein-encoding genes, and a point value of 0 (category 1 A) was automatically assigned by CNVisi. Thirteen genes in this interval such as GRIA2, NAF1, and FNIP2 were predicted to be haploinsufficient (HI), resulting in a point value of 0.15 (category 2 H). Additionally, the deletion contained a total of 101 protein-encoding genes, leading to a point value of 0.9 (category 3 C), and it overlapped with the CNVs of de novo cases in ClinVar and DECIPHER, adding another 0.9 point (category 4 C). Finally, CNVisi automatically calculated a point value of 1.95 and classified this deletion as P. We further compared this result with previous manual classification. Both genetic experts and CNVisi classified this CNV as P, though the scoring details differed. Experts did not refer to the cases from the public database, but referred to a case from published literature for Sect. 4 scoring (0.1). Furthermore, because this patient’s phenotype was consistent with similar cases, the Sect. 5 score of the manual classification was 0.1. Despite scoring differences, CNVisi provided a wealth of annotation information for CNV interpretation. The literature from the knowledge base can be used as a reference for genetic experts review and can also provide reference for the use of phenotype-related evidence. This saves time in searching multiple databases and enhancing the efficiency of CNV interpretation.

An example for demonstrating the result of CNV classification by CNVisi. For the convenience of display, this figure is a simplification of the CNVisi software interface. And all the information was originally in Chinese and was translated into English

Both B and LB CNVs were uniformly classified as B/LB in this study. Accuracy was calculated by comparing standards and classification of CNVs by CNVisi. Firstly, evaluating the ability of distinguishing pCNVs (P or LP CNVs) by CNVisi, and the overall accuracy of CNVisi achieved was 97.7% (977/1000) (Table 1, Table S2). For P/LP variants, CNVisi had 94.4% recall and 99.5% specificity. The precision and F1 score of CNVisi was 99.1% and 96.7%, respectively. We also evaluated the consistency of scores between CNVisi and experts for individual section (Fig. S1). The accuracy for both Sects. 1 and 3 reached 100%, while Sects. 2 and 4 achieved 95.6% and 79.0%, respectively. The inconsistency in Sect. 4 may be attributed to CNVisi’s inability to automatically evaluate criteria related to the literature. However, as CNVisi is capable of integrating information from multiple public databases, it may retrieve additional cases for scoring, which could also influence the score of this section. Genetic experts scored evidence from Sect. 5 in only three cases during manual evaluation, whereas CNVisi did not assign scores for this section, resulting in an overall accuracy of 99.7%. Despite inconsistency in individual section scores, the pathogenicity classification of only 23 CNVs was affected: 11 P CNVs were classified as VUS, 9 LP CNVs as VUS, and 1 VUS CNV as P, while 2 VUS CNVs were classified as LP. These differences were primarily due to CNVisi’s inability to automatically score literature-based evidence, resulting in lower scores of Sect. 4 in some cases, and leading to 34.8% (8/23) of pCNVs were classified as VUS. However, all literatures adopted by clinical laboratory geneticists were included in CNVisi. CNVisi was based on NLP methods for analyzing historical clinical reports and eventually developed literature knowledge bases for CNV interpretation. Therefore, CNV-related papers can be clearly presented in CNVisi if the CNVs to be evaluated have similar genome coordinates to previously reported CNVs. This is greatly convenient for clinical experts. Secondly, accuracy was calculated by comparing the exact variant classification. For the second approach, we also obtained high accuracy of 85.7% (857/1000), suggesting that CNVisi was capable of correctly classifying the majority of CNVs.

To further evaluate the clinical utility, we selected CNVs reviewed by genetic experts that met the reporting rules, including P, LP and some VUS CNVs. For VUS CNVs, deletions ≥ 500 kb and duplications ≥ 1 Mb were selected. A total of 154 CNVs were selected for further analysis. They were classified as 76 P, 4 LP, 72 VUS, and 2 B/LB CNVs by CNVisi (Fig. 3). However, 23 CNVs were not consistent with expert classification (Table S3). Among these, 1 P CNV was classified as LP CNV by CNVisi, and 3 P CNVs were as VUS. It is noted that 12 LP CNVs were classified as P CNVs, and 4 LP CNVs were as VUS. Most VUS CNVs were correctly classified, only 1 and 2 CNVs were misclassified as P and B/LB, respectively. These inconsistencies due to differences in sensitivity between CNVisi and experts, where CNVisi sometimes predicted LP as P or VUS as LP, potentially increasing the positive result yield for reviewers.

Among 23 CNVs with inconsistent classifications (Table S3), 5 were due to the differences in Sect. 4 scores, and 18 in Sect. 2 scores. Ten CNVs overlapped with low penetrance regions, including 15q11.2 recurrent region (BP1-BP2) (includes NIPA1) and 16p13.11 recurrent region (BP2-BP3) (includes MYH11). The uncertain relationship between these CNVs and the patients’ phenotype resulting in a lower manual score than the CNVisi score. Despite the differences, all low penetrance regions were specifically labeled in CNVisi. This can facilitate the laboratory geneticists to review the accurate classification of CNV at the end, and does not affect the accuracy of the classification. Three CNVs partially overlapped with established HI/ triplosensitive (TS) genomic regions. Genetic experts and CNVisi disagreed on whether this CNV contained the known causative gene, resulting in inconsistent classifications. The reason for the discordance in classification of 2 CNVs was that genetic experts referred cases from published literature, while CNVisi cannot automatically score the literature-related evidence, leading to different Sect. 4 scores. And the different scores of the 4O evidence resulted in inconsistent classification of 2 CNVs. Some CNVs were reported in the general population, but their frequency were lower to be considered a polymorphism (> 1%), causing different assigned scores between genetic experts and CNVisi. In conclusion, due to complication of practical clinical settings, CNVisi should be applied under the supervision of clinical experts.

We divided all 5861 CNVs into two groups according to the reporting rules. Two VUS CNVs were classified as B by CNVisi, 71 B/LB CNVs as VUS (Fig. 4A), 1 LP CNV as VUS, and 4 VUS CNVs as P, while 2 were classified as LP (Fig. 4B). Therefore, clinical reports for 76 samples associated with these 80 inconsistent CNVs may be erroneous. The remaining CNVs showed a high consistency of 98.6% (5781/5861) between genetic experts CNVisi. In other words, the CNV-seq results of 96.9% (2367/2443) samples could be accurately and efficiently interpreted by CNVisi following the reporting rules.

Moreover, we compared CNVisi’s performance to the performance of ClassifyCNV and AnnotSV [7, 9]. They are both comprehensive interpretation tools that implement the ACMG-ClinGen guideline, which is similar to CNVisi (Table S4). CNVisi is the only software that applies NLP methods for analyzing historical clinical reports to develop knowledge bases for interpretation, and provides literature related to the CNV. The other two software can only generate CNV classifications, while CNVisi can also automatically generate CNV descriptions and clinical reports. Furthermore, we analyzed the 3384 CNVs using ClassifyCNV and AnnotSV with default settings. Out of 3384 CNVs, AnnotSV determined 372 to be P/LP, compared to 86 calls by CNVisi, 62 calls by ClassifyCNV, and 86 calls by experts’ manual evaluation (Tables S5 and S6). CNVisi showed higher accuracy for P/LP CNVs (99.6%, 99.0% by ClassifyCNV, 91.5% by AnnotSV) (Table 3). Compared to CNVisi and ClassifyCNV, AnnotSV is less conservative when making P/LP calls. The recall of AnnotSV was higher than CNVisi and ClassifyCNV (98.8%, 91.9% by CNVisi, 66.3% by ClassifyCNV). For P/LP CNVs, CNVisi and ClassifyCNV both had 99.8% specificity and 91.9% precision, while AnnotSV’s values were 91.3% and 22.8%, respectively. CNVisi yielded the highest F1 score (91.9%), ClassifyCNV and AnnotSV achieved the F1 score of 77.0% and 37.1%, respectively. Overall, CNVisi was superior to previous tools for CNV interpretation and classification, and showed excellent clinical utility.

Discussion

Although ACMG and ClinGen presented more comprehensive criteria and a semi-quantitative scoring system, the lack of the standardized scoring pipeline and differences in understanding of evidence still lead to the inconsistencies among different clinical scientists within the same laboratory or across labs. Moreover, searching various databases and gathering the results for each evidence is complex and time-consuming. Therefore, there is an urgent need in the field for an automated software that has the ability to integrate multiple databases and accurately and efficiently interpret CNV. In this study, we assessed the accuracy of classification between CNVisi and clinical genetic experts, and demonstrated the clinical utility of tools like CNVisi for automated interpretation of CNVs.

The interpretation of the same CNV by multiple clinical laboratories may differ, even when using the ACMG-ClinGen standards. The National Center of Clinical Laboratories (NCCLs) in China distributed 234 CNVs to nine clinical laboratories [13]. The consistency of CNV classification was evaluated according to the results of independent classification across laboratories. As a result, the overall classification concordance rate was 76% (177/234). The reason of the inconsistency including differences in application of evidence categories, differences in the ability to scrutinize data in other data sources, and so on. Because the variability in tools and databases used during interpretation introduced discrepancies, the reproducibility of CNV interpretation results was also decreased. ACMG-ClinGen guideline recommend that only high-confidence data sets be used, such as gnomAD SV and DGV Gold Standard. Some laboratories may use other available population data sets, which leads to the differences in CNVs classification across labs [14]. These underscore the need for strengthening understanding of the ACMG-ClinGen technical standards, and establishing a standard pipeline.

The implementation of the ACMG-ClinGen guideline is time-consuming and need clinical and genetics knowledge. To address these challenges, many researchers are working to automate the scoring of evidence outlined in guidelines. Some tools based on ACMG-ClinGen guidelines have been developed to improve the efficiency and accuracy of CNV interpretation [7–9]. These tools can automate the interpretation of CNVs, but they have limited utility for time-consuming cases or those lacking sufficient data in public databases. Continuous searching and browsing of the published papers were required. Additionally, none of these tools provide related information of literature. Most evaluations of these tools rely on CNVs from public databases, with few assessing clinical utility using CNVs from actual clinical samples. CNVisi is a software that automatically interprets CNVs according to the 2020 ACMG-ClinGen guideline. It employs NLP methods to analyze historical reports and extract information, integrating data from public databases to develop knowledge bases for interpretation. These knowledge bases can provide genetic experts with information for CNVs interpretation, including related literatures. All CNVs used in this study were derived from the testing results of actual clinical samples. The overall accuracy of CNVisi for classification was 97.7% and 99.6% in preliminary testing and clinical utility testing, respectively. And it was superior to previous tools for CNV interpretation and classification. CNVisi assigns the suggested default point value for some evidence. To enable flexibility, the value can be changed by user. Sharing CNV interpretation data is crucial for resolving discordances [13]. CNVisi’s application of NLP to analyze in-house historical clinical reports helps build knowledge databases, contributing to this data-sharing effort. In comparison, CNVisi significantly reduces the burden of manual literature review for genetic experts and improves the efficiency of CNV interpretation.

The high accuracy of CNVisi underscores the stability and reliability. However, the differences for CNV interpretation in evidence and weights resulted in 23 inconsistent CNVs during the assessment of CNVisi on clinical utility. One key reason for these inconsistencies was the different score of evidence in criteria 4O (overlap with common population variant). ACMG-ClinGen guideline offer a possible point range of 0 to 1, which gives the interpreter freedom to determine the significance of the overlap with population frequency. The experts from OGHFU strictly follow the guideline’s default point value, but CNVisi sets a gradient of scores based on the evidence details. For CNVs that do not meet the population frequency cutoff of 1.0% are more prone to classification inconsistencies, even between manual evaluation by experts in different laboratories [13]. Ten inconsistent CNVs were classified as LP by OGHFU experts due to overlap with low-penetrance regions, but CNVisi classified them as P. There is inconsistency in classifying low-penetrance CNVs not only between software and experts, but also between experts in different laboratories [13]. The ClinGen Low Penetrance/Risk Allele Working Group and other researchers are working on standardizing the classification of these low-penetrance CNVs [5, 15, 16]. Although the classification between the software and the experts was inconsistent, the software clearly indicated the overlap between the CNV to be evaluated and the low-penetrance region, which facilitated the experts to check during the final manual review. A limitation of CNVisi is that the criteria relevant to literature cannot be scored automatically. This led to 2 P CNVs were classified as VUS. CNVisi applies NLP methods for analyzing historical clinical reports to develop knowledge bases for interpretation, such as paper database. If the CNV has similar genomic coordinates to a previously reported one, annotation information and related literature for this CNV will be displayed in CNVisi. Although CNVisi cannot automatically scoring literature-related criteria, genetic experts can still use these papers during the final review to perform manual evaluation. If the CNV is novel or rare, CNVisi may lack associated annotation information and literature, which compels genetic experts to search public databases for related literature. To address this limitation, we could develop AI-driven tools for literature retrieval and review, integrating it with CNVisi to further enhance the clinical utility. Overall, CNVisi should be used under expert supervision to allow for final manual adjustments based on literatures, saving time on literature searches.

Searching literature for CNV interpretation is labor-intensive and time-consuming, as some important information is buried in various literatures, such as the relations between CNV and human genetic diseases. However, information from paper is mostly presented as unstructured texts. Researchers can adopt text mining methods to identify potentially useful information in the literature and present it in a structured way, which enable automated and systematic analyses of literature [10, 17]. CNVisi adopted NLP methods for analyzing historical reports and developed knowledge bases for interpretation. The historical reports generated after manual evaluation contain many valuable information that can be used for CNV interpretation, especially some that are not available in public databases but only exist in the published literatures. By using NLP methods, this information can be extracted and combined with information from public databases, then eventually present in the form of the structured information in system. Previous study has shown that the NLP approach applied in CNVisi performs well in paragraph segmentation, CNV-paragraph matching, and corpus classification. This guarantees the establishment of knowledge bases for CNV interpretation. In the future, with the accumulation of historical reports in different clinical settings, CNVisi can be further trained to interpret CNVs more accurately and efficiently, allowing clinicians to make better decisions for the medical management.

Previous study discovered that utilization of the ACMG-ClinGen guideline for interpretation led to more CNVs were classified as VUS [14]. This limitation results in lengthy candidate CNV sets requiring review, with many benign CNVs being classified as VUS. The increasing number of VUS also increases the burden of genetic counseling and medical management. To address this problem, some CNV interpretation tools employ machine learning (ML) approaches such as random forest, gradient boosted trees, and XGBoost to estimate CNV pathogenicity [18]. These methods learn from data elements related to dosage sensitivity, overlapping genes, and population frequencies in public databases (ClinVar, DECIPHER, DGV, and gnomAD) to predict pathogenic [19–21]. ML methods enhance the accuracy, enabling rapid identification and interpretation of potentially pathogenic CNVs [19, 22], and can be used to find CNVs that should be reviewed first, and combined with algorithms based on ACMG standard, which can significantly reduce the burden of CNV interpretation. Although NGS has tremendously improved the resolution of CNV detection, it also poses a significant challenge to accurately discern the pathogenicity of the CNVs identified through NGS. The application of ML provides convenience for predicting the pathogenicity of CNVs and promotes the consistency of CNVs pathogenicity classification in different laboratories. Therefore, some studies have attempted to combine these two methods to further improve the efficiency of CNV interpretation [6]. Compared with the classification according to automated guidelines, the combined approach was able to reduce the number of VUS and reveal the incorrect classifications [20].

Periodic expansion of existing databases and publication of new papers add new information for CNV interpretation, which may change the pathogenicity classification of VUS CNVs [23]. The tools that can automate pathogenicity classification may be beneficial to reclassify VUS CNVs more quickly [24, 25]. The function of automatic monitor can be developed in these tools in the future for periodically update classifications of VUS CNVs. Tools based on AI are highly beneficial in predicting pathogenic classification of novel and complex CNVs which may not be well understood yet. Therefore, integrating AI-based prediction tools into ACMG-ClinGen guideline-based automated CNV interpretation software may lead to more accurate CNV classification in the future. Although AI is beneficial to CNV interpretation, further evaluation are recommended towards the insurance of secured and privacy-preserving for clinical application [26].

This study was not without limitations. All CNVs used for clinical utility assessment of CNVisi were derived from Chinese, so interpreting CNVs of other ethnic populations by CNVisi may result in classification concordance that differs from this study. Moreover, since these CNVs were obtained from prenatal diagnostic testing, they are predominantly associated with fetal structural anomalies. The classification consistency between CNVisi and manual evaluation for other CNVs, such as those associated with neurodevelopmental disorders in children, should be further studied. Additionally, the dataset in this study is derived from a single institution in China, and the CNV classification evaluated by genetic experts from this institution was considered as the standard. Due to the differences in the implementation of ACMG-ClinGen guidelines across different institutions, this may also influence the generalizability of results. In the future, the clinical utility of CNVisi should be evaluated in more institutions and more clinical scenarios. At the same time, CNVisi should increase the frequency of updating the knowledge base and incorporate more historical reports to expand the knowledge base to improve its consistency with the classification by genetic experts.

Conclusions

CNVisi is a software for CNVs interpretation, which apply NLP methods for analyzing historical clinical reports to develop knowledge bases for interpretation. Applying CNV interpretation software such as CNVisi in clinic can reduce the burden of genetic experts and improve the efficiency of CNV interpretation.
