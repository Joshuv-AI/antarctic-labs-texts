Electrical properties determine the liquid flow direction in plasma–liquid interactions
Authors: Calum T Ryan, Anton A Darhuber, Rudie P J Kunnen, Hanneke Gelderblom, Ana Sobota
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring atmospheric pressure plasma impingement, plasma induced liquid flow will influence the transport and distribution of plasma generated charged and reactive species in liquids. We use particle image velocimetry and supplementary pH, conductivity and temperature measurements to investigate electrical properties of an AC kHz plasma jet interacting with water and electrolytes. We observe that the dominant driving mechanism in low conductive solutions are surface forces such as shear stresses and stagnation-pressure induced dimpling. These give upwards flows beneath the plasma–liquid interaction point. In highly conductive solutions, such as water with dissolved salts, the dominant driving mechanism is electro-hydrodynamic forces, with flows directed downwards underneath the plasma jet in our system. We therefore demonstrate that the direction of initial plasma induced liquid flows can be controlled through the addition of salt ions. In electrically grounded salt solutions, we also observe time resolved flow direction switching, possibly due to modification of salt solutions via electrolytic and plasma induced reactions changing the dominant flow mechanism over time.Subject terms: Applied physics, Fluid dynamics, Plasma physics, Electrochemistry

Introduction

The effectiveness of cold atmospheric plasma applications has been well documented for some time now1–4. In some of these applications a layer of liquid, primarily water5,6, is present. When plasmas interact with water, charged and reactive species are deposited into the liquid, creating plasma treated water7,8. Once these species are deposited into the liquid, their transport through the liquid media can then govern the efficiency of the desired use9. Different species distributions in liquids are preferable in different applications, such as homogeneous in liquid activation, or localised to a wound area in plasma medicine. While much has been discussed about the conditions to generate different species in plasma applications, little is known about methods to influence the species transport, and thereby increase the application efficiency.

A potential method of controlling this species transport/distribution within liquids is through the plasma induced liquid flow (PILF)10–12. By creating or redirecting existing PILF within a system, the liquid can push and either spread or focus species through the medium, giving the desired distribution required for the application. Despite not much being known about the sources of PILF, we can make assumptions on the driving mechanisms based on related systems and past experimental observations. Plasmas can be described as fluids, so we can expect that some of the mechanisms driving the flow will be similar to that of gases impinging upon liquid surfaces13,14. In the configuration of an atmospheric pressure plasma jet (APPJ), where much of the source gas remains unionized, the relevancy of gas-liquid impingement increases compared to other plasma–liquid configurations. When a gas/plasma jet is directed towards a liquid interface, liquid vortexes are induced below the jet-liquid interaction point through a combination of surface forces driven by the gas/plasma interacting with the liquid, such as surface shear stresses and the impinging normal force, inducing surface flows and dimpling respectively. The resulting flow remains relatively localised to the interaction point and will be in an upward direction, (i.e.towards the liquid interface) directly below the interaction point. This upwards direction is due to the surface shear stresses being directed away from the interaction point, with mass conservation resulting in upward flows beneath the surface where the vortices meet. Within this study, this flow regime shall be called the ”upwards regime”, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. The upwards regime has been observed in several previous publications.15,16 Note that in previous work Kawasaki et al.17 had called this regime the “vortex regime”, however the name upwards regime will be used in this study for clarity, due to the relevance of vertical flow direction on our results. These names, for all intents and purposes, are interchangeable however.

The unique properties of atmospheric pressure plasmas modify the driving forces of the liquid flow compared to the impact of an unionized gas jet. These processes would then cause the system to either diverge away from or amplify the upwards regime, depending on plasma and liquid properties. Marangoni flows18 have been suggested as one such PILF effect15,19–21. Here, a localised surface tension change occurs due to a chemical reaction, such as surfactant removal or modification, which is a relevant technological application of plasmas2. A local increase of surface tension will induce surface flows directed towards the plasma–liquid interaction point, which is then redirected downwards into the solution bulk. This PILF regime is called the “downwards regime”17. A diagram of the downwards regime can be seen in Fig. 1b. The onset of Marangoni flows within PILF is debatable, however, as there are conflicting results of directly measured surface tension within the context of plasma–liquid interactions15,22–24, potentially due to the difficulty of quantifying surface tension in experiments25. The role of Marangoni flows during plasma–liquid interactions therefore remains unclear.

The last PILF effect to discuss is that of electro-hydrodynamic (EHD) forces within the liquid26,27 (In the remainder of this study, when EHD is mentioned we will be discussing the EHD in the liquid) . The transport of charged species in an electric field placed across a fluid will cause collisional momentum transfer between the charged species and the surrounding media, thereby inducing a flow. The strongest component to EHD forces is that of the Coulomb force, which is given as fe=ρE within the Navier-Stokes equation. Here, ρ is the free charge density and E is the electric field. Dickenson et al.28 discussed that in a pin-to-plate plasma configuration the primary governing influence on the PILF was related to the conductivity of the solution. They state that the dominating PILF mechanism within dielectric and conductive fluids are the surface stresses and EHD forces, respectively. The addition of salts to water produces the charge carriers required for liquid phase EHD flow to occur, and chemical modelling has shown that we can likely expect an interfacial imbalance between Na+ and Cl- in saline under plasma impingement29. This imbalance, alongside charged and reactive species injection from the plasma to the interface, produces a local micro-scale charge and ion density gradient required to induce liquid phase EHD30. This influence of electrical properties on PILF has not been extensively discussed previously, and combined with the complicated nature of directional PILF (as described above), further investigations are clearly necessary.

We investigate the effect of electrical properties on PILF within the context of an APPJ interacting with liquid water and electrolytes, with the goal to further the understanding of PILF directional mechanisms, and the dominant driving forces in different systems. The electrical properties we investigate are the solution grounding, applied AC voltage, solution conductivity and plasma power. We employ particle image velocimetry (PIV) to observe both time averaged and time resolved characteristic flow velocities, obtained from the peak vertical velocity magnitudes of the PILF. These results are combined with supplementary temperature and pH measurements to gain a better understanding of the flow results obtained.

Results

To investigate the effect of electrically grounding the solution, every time-averaged experiment is performed in two separate configurations; once in an electrically floating solution and once in an electrically grounded solution. Within this study, we state that our solution is grounded when the ground plate (see “Methods” section) is submerged at the bottom of our liquid container, and floating when it is removed. The effect of grounding will then be discussed with regards to the other investigated parameters in the respective subsection.

PILF velocity as a function of applied plasma voltage

The AC voltage amplitude was varied between 1.5 and 2.5 kV and characteristic flow velocities of the PILF (vz,30s) were obtained, as seen in Fig. 2a. The applied voltage was modified to investigate its effect on the APPJ induced surface stresses on de-mineralised water (DW), due to the effect of applied voltage and plasma power on the force exerted by an APPJ plume31–33. An additional control experiment with pure He gas/0 kV is also performed. The PILF regime observed in all voltage experiments was the upwards regime; see Fig. 2b. vz,30s increases with applied voltage. The observed value of vz,30s is roughly twice as high when the liquid is electrically grounded compared to the corresponding floating experiment.

PILF velocity as a function of initial solution conductivity

Conductivity and temperature measurements

To increase the conductivity as required to modify the dominant PILF processes, NaCl and KCl salts were added to DW. When the Maxwellian charge relaxation time of a solution is longer than the interaction time between the plasma and the solution (see “Methods” section), the dominant mechanism will be the surface stresses. Solutions with a lower relaxation time than the plasma–liquid interaction time will instead have EHD forces dominant28. The Maxwellian charge relaxation time of a solution can be approximated using its conductivity34 via τ≈ϵ0ϵσ-1, where ϵ0 is the permittivity of free space and ϵ and σ are the relative permittivity and conductivity of a material, respectively. To calculate the relaxation times for each solution, the initial conductivity, alongside the temperature, were measured; see Fig. 3a. Plasmas can induce desalination35 and are expected to modify the ion concentration ratio29, and so it is important to note that the conductivities described in this section are with respect to initial conditions. The relaxation time for DW is 3.6 μs28, and the relaxation time calculated for the electrolyte solutions were in the range of 0.1 ns. The electrolyte relaxation times are all smaller than the approximately 1 μs interaction time between the plasma and water, confirming quantitatively the dominant driving mechanism transition from contact to EHD. The temperature was taken due to the dissolution of KCl in water being an endothermic process, a parameter that can then influence our resultant flow from relations to surface tension, viscosity and conductivity.

(b) Characteristic PILF velocity for varied solution conductivity—the blue and red shaded areas represent the upwards and downwards regimes respectively.

Time averaged velocities

The results of the time averaged solution conductivity experiments can be observed in Fig. 3b. Negative values for vz,30s show the downwards regime. We observe that within the floating NaCl experiments vz,30s decreases as the concentration of salt increases towards saturation, with electrolyte conductivities greater than 10 S m-1 giving negative velocities away from the liquid surface. This behaviour is also observed in the floating KCl experiments. The difference in scaling between NaCl and KCl solution conductivities and the flow velocity is likely due to the temperature decrease from the endothermic KCl dissolution (see Fig. 3a) and variation in the physical properties of the two fluids (e.g. viscosity, surface tension)36. This would induce different scaling variations for all of the driving mechanisms occurring at once during PILF. Within grounded salt solution experiments, there is always a negative flow velocity bias compared to the floating experiments, implying an additional downward force in salt solutions when grounded. The added force is likely due to the large increase in electric field strength of the solution that grounding causes, increases the strength of any EHD flow that is present.

Time resolved velocities

During the 30 s of PIV recording for the time averaged salt solution experiments, we observed what appeared to be possible downwards velocity decreases when grounded salt solutions were used at larger concentrations, but this was too small to confirm purely visually. To observe if these fluctuations were simply random or the result of a temporal effect, PIV recordings were extended and time resolved PILF velocities (vz) were obtained; see Fig. 4a. For the first minute of plasma impingement on the 50 g L-1 KCl and NaCl solutions vz increases towards zero, before switching direction from the downwards regime to the upwards regime. This regime transition also occurs in the 100 g L-1 NaCl experiment, however over a much larger time scale. PILF in DW fluctuates somewhat around a constant velocity value. vz in the NaCl experiments seems to tend towards the same value occurring in the DW experiments. The KCl results, following the regime transition, maintains a lower velocity than both NaCl experiments. We observed that a white powder like substance would form on the ground electrode following the longer experiments, once said electrode had been removed from the PIV setup and enough time had passed for any leftover water to evaporate. To identify whether this substance was leftover PIV tracers, crystallized salt or NaOH and KOH (within the relevant salt experiment), the powder was dissolved in DW and the pH of the new solution was measured. These results can be seen in Fig. 4b. An increase in pH was observed, indicating the solution was alkaline, as NaOH and KOH solutions are expected to be37.

PILF velocity as a function of plasma power

The plasma power from every time averaged experiment is calculated to let us compare the PILF velocity when either the voltage or solution conductivity are varied, by P=f∫0TI(t)V(t)dt, where f is the frequency of the AC applied voltage (30 kHz), T is the period of one AC oscillation, and both I(t) and V(t) are the current and voltages as functions of time t respectively. As the parameter therefore changes with both voltage and the conductivity related current, this gives us a link to more easily discuss PILF with regards to all of our time averaged results. Grounding DW induces an increase in plasma power compared to floating DW; see Fig. 5a. When comparing the power to DW vz,30s a linear trend appears within the range of our results; see Fig. 5b. Low concentration floating NaCl solutions also appear to fit this linear relation between plasma power and vz,30s, however all other electrolyte experiments break this trend and form a more scattered set of results in comparison, showing a change in dominant flow mechanism.

Discussion

We investigated the role of electrical properties (specifically the solution grounding, applied voltage, solution conductivity and the plasma power) on PILF within the APPJ-liquid system. All DW and low NaCl concentrated floating experiments show an apparent linear relation between the plasma power and the flow velocity over our data range. The driving mechanism in these lower conductivity solutions are likely surface stresses28, shown by the relation between the flow velocity and plasma power31. All experiments within lower conductivity solutions are in the upwards regime. When salt is added such that the conductivity increases, there is no longer a linear relation between flow velocity and plasma power, and the downwards regime occurs within floating electrolytes when the concentration approaches saturation. When our electrolyte experiments were grounded, the large increase in electric field also caused an additional downwards flow bias. We expect this change from the upward to the downward flow regime in the electrolyte experiments to be due to the increased strength of EHD forces over surface stresses. The time averaged results therefore also validate the dominant force relations discussed by Dickenson et al.28 within the APPJ-liquid system, in addition to the pin-plate system described in their work. Our work therefore qualitatively shows that the direction of PILF can be controlled by increasing or reducing the salt content of a solution. As the goal of PILF research is to move towards methods of flow control, understanding how to determine the direction alone in certain setups is a big step forward.

Yang et al.12 have performed flow investigations using a non-thermal helium plasma jet similar to the one that we use, with it impinging on de-ionised water. Their PIV experiments, however, show that the downwards regime was present. The jet configuration here is slightly different, with a lower gas flow and a pulsed DC voltage source compared to the AC source we have used, which implies additional mechanisms when this power supply is used. This voltage source would be an interesting topic to explore within further research regarding directional PILF.

Grounded salt solution PILF transitions from the downwards regime to the upwards regime after a certain period of time, whereas this transition does not occur during grounded DW experiments. Within the NaCl experiments the flow velocity tends towards the same value occurring in grounded DW experiments. The KCl solution transitions regime but remains at a lower final flow velocity, likely due to the fact that KCl dissolution in water is an endothermic process (see Fig. 3a). This decrease in liquid temperature will then cause a resultant increase in the solution viscosity, causing an increased resistance to flows in the KCl experiments. As mentioned in the introduction, PILF can be used to influence the transmission of charged and reactive species through liquids. Therefore, regime transitions are important to consider due to the decrease in efficiency over time when a downwards regime is preferred, such as in plasma medicine where the desired species move across electrolyte solutions such as sweat38.

Flow regime transitions similar to the one we observed have been discussed in previous works11,15, and these have been thought to relate to Marangoni flow velocity reduction via surfactant removal. One exception is in the work of Kawasaki et al.17, where the regime transition was manually produced when the applied frequency of a plasma interacting with a surfactant solution. The downwards regime occurred when they use a low applied frequency before transitioning to the upwards regime with increased frequency. This transition is likely due to a force balance between Marangoni flows and surface stresses. The Marangoni flows would induce a downwards regime due to the water purification properties atmospheric plasmas can employ, increasing the surface tension at the plasma–liquid interaction point through surfactant removal. When the applied frequency is increased, the plasma power is also increased. As our results show, the upwards inducing surface stresses will also increase with plasma power, perhaps overcoming any Marangoni stresses. This may then cause the directional regime transition observed by Kawasaki et al.

The theories surrounding the PILF regime transitions described previously do not explain why this phenomenon occurs in our time resolved grounded salt solution experiments. There are no surfactants added to our solutions and the only chemical species added is the highly soluble salts for conductivity modification. Marangoni flows are therefore unlikely to play a role in the dynamic regime transition we observe. Instead, the regime transition is more likely to be caused by reaction-induced conductivity reduction. As our plasma liquid interface is anodic, the current travelling through the conductive solutions to the cathodic ground electrode could induce electrolytic reactions39,40. The presence of cathodic reactions seems to be validated through the production of NaOH and KOH on the ground electrode, both of which are products of aqueous NaCl and KCl electrolysis at the cathode respectively41,42. The production of the OH compounds, alongside the reactive chemistry induced by the plasma–liquid interactions29, may increase the process of salt modification at a higher rate, decreasing the systems conductivity over time. This conductivity reduction could explain in particular why the flow velocity for NaCl solutions tend towards that which is occurring in the DW experiments. If electrolysis is indeed occuring, the ions H+ and Cl- would produce H2 and Cl2 respectively, reducing the ion density and therefore the conductivity over time. However, to attempt conductivity measurements following experiments may be unreliable; plasma generated charged species entering the liquid should locally increase the conductivity at the surface, making it difficult to accurately confirm conductivity reduction in the bulk. Another explanation for the regime transition could occur via the OH compound buildup on the ground cathode, as this may be enough to limit the electric field propagation onto the metal itself, reducing the strength of the EHD forces present. To accurately consider the possible causes of the PILF regime transition, chemical analysis of the solution following the grounded plasma-electrolyte experiments would be useful to examine the different present hypotheses. Our work, however, does show that temporal regime transitions can occur when surfactants are not present, highlighting that further research in this area is required.

Conclusion

The influence of electrical properties on the dominant PILF mechanism is investigated. The upwards PILF regime is maintained when the conductivity of the system is low, such as when the liquid is DW. The flow velocity of this regime is shown to increase with the plasma power, as expected due to the relation between surface stresses and plasma power shown in previous literature. When the liquid conductivity is increased such that the Maxwellian charge relaxation time decreases below the plasma–liquid interaction time, the flow transitions towards the downwards regime. This regime change is likely due to EHD effects inducing a large downwards force away from the surface. Therefore, the direction of PILF can be changed in plasma–liquid systems with positive gas phase electric field when salts are added to the solution. However, when grounded salt solution experiments occur for a relatively large period of time, we observed that the downwards regime decreases in intensity before eventually transitioning to the upwards regime. We anticipate that this regime switching is possibly due to salt loss via electrochemical reactions at the ground electrode, decreasing the conductivity of the bulk liquid and transitioning the dominant forces from EHD to surface stresses. This implies an automatic time sensitivity to the direction of PILF when surfactants are absent.

Methods

Plasma jet

The plasma jet used in our experiments has been described in numerous previous studies43–46. It was chosen due to its high reproducibility and relatively low power output. The plasma jet is powered by an AC power supply, with a frequency of 30 kHz and an applied voltage of 2 kV, when the effect of applied voltage on PILF is not investigated. The plasma jet ignites in air at approximately 0.78 kV. The voltage is attached to an electrode made of a hollow metal pin with an inner/outer diameter of 1 mm/1.8 mm and length 64.5 mm. It is placed into a glass capillary of inner/outer diameters of 2 mm/4 mm and length 90 mm, the connection of which is sealed with Teflon tape. 5 mm below the end of the pin electrode, a grounded ring copper electrode of thickness 3 mm is placed around the glass capillary. The high voltage electrode is probed for both voltage and current, using a Tektronix P6015A voltage probe and a Magnelab CT-F1.0-BNC current transformer respectively. A 1 kΩ resistor is placed in series with the ring ground electrode and an Agilent N2683B voltage probe is used with the resistor to calculate the current. The waveforms obtained from the probing can be seen in Fig. 6a, alongside two shaded regions highlighting the generation of the plasma bullet (marked as i) and the interaction time between the plasma and the liquid (marked ii), the latter of which occurs over 1 μs. The waveforms of voltage and applied plasma current are integrated to calculate the plasma power. Helium 5.0 gas (purity ≥99.999%) is supplied to the plasma jet via PFA tubing and a flow controller, the flow rate of which was set to a value of 1 slm for all experiments. The pin electrode, gas tubing, flow controller and helium gas canister are all connected via Swagelok connections.

The time between these regions is due to the plasma’s propagation delay from the APPJ electrodes to the liquid surface. (b) Experimental setup diagram—the ground plate is removed from the water container completely when the experiment is electrically floating.

Particle image velocimetry

PIV experiments47 were undertaken using the setup described in Fig. 6b. The bottom orifice of the glass capillary was maintained a distance of 1 cm above the undisturbed liquid-gas interface. NaCl or KCl (obtained from Sigma Aldrich and used as is) was added when required. The solution is dosed with 55 μm diameter polyamide PIV tracer particles (obtained from LaVision). We believe the possibility of charging effects on the tracers disrupting the PIV to be negligible. as any electrostatic forces will be counteracted by stronger drag and added mass forces from the water. Solutions are prepared using a magnetic stirrer to dissolve first the salt (if present) into the DW, followed by mixing in the tracer particles. After mixing for 5 minutes, we assumed the seeding density of both tracers and salts to be homogeneous. The liquid depth is approximately 5.9 cm in a square-shaped container of side length 7.5 cm. This size is chosen to be much larger than the characteristic area of PILF to remove potential influences of the wall48,49. During grounded experiments, a thin copper plate is submerged into the solution, with a wire connecting to ground. The water container is made from PMMA and will therefore cause the solution to be electrically floating when the ground plate is not present.

Tracers are illuminated using a 1 mW red laser, expanded into a light sheet, and reflected against a mirror for alignment. Water solutions and tracers are not re-used due to the potential modifications of certain physical properties of both by the plasma23,50. The water is filtered after every experiment before draining to remove the tracer particles and avoid leaking micro plastics into the environment. Time averaged experiments are repeated 5 times each. The PIV images are recorded using a Fastcam Mini AX100 at 60 fps with a square recording window of sides 1024 px. The window is calibrated such that each side of the image is approximately 5 cm long in the measurement area. Time averaged experiments are recorded for the first 30 s of APPJ impingement to visualise flows in liquids with known initial properties, whereas time resolved experiments are performed for 6 minutes for long term visualisations.

PIV recordings are analysed using the open source MATLAB plugin, PIVlab51,52. PIV is performed using a 32px interrogation window and 50% window overlap. When vector fields are obtained for every frame, they are time averaged to reduce perturbations and noise. Subsequently, the characteristic velocity vz,30s obtained for each experiment is chosen to be the cell with the highest vertical velocity magnitude (positive values in the vertical velocity field represent upward directed flow and negative values represent downward directed flow). Large horizontal flow asymmetries were observed when the APPJ was angled relative to the surface normal of the liquid-air interface to such a small degree that it was not observable during experiment preparations. It was decided that to simply observe trends in PILF for varied parameters, only the vertical flow needed to be investigated, especially as later analysis and past studies have suggested the primary flow features characterising different regimes were in the vertical direction. Due to the difficulties in jet alignment, this can lead to the position of the cell in which vz,30s is obtained to not be fixed, or on axis with the jet. However, the vertical velocity being the focus of the investigation should make concerns with the position of vz,30s negligible. For time resolved experiments, the same analysis method was mostly used. However, the time resolved peak vertical velocity vz was instead taken every 1 s (60 frames) from t = 0 s to 20 s, and every 10 s (600 frames) from t = 20 s to 360 s.

Supplementary measurements

To fully characterise and gain a deeper understanding of the driving mechanisms of PILF, the conductivity, temperature and pH were measured at different stages of the experiments that the authors deemed relevant for the specific properties using a Mettler Toledo SevenExcellence pH/Cond meter S470.

Conductivity and temperature

The dissolution of KCl in water is an endothermic process. Temperature affects the viscosity, surface tension and the electrical conductivity of a solution. Therefore, to accurately compare the PILF in the DW, NaCl and KCl experiments, the conductivity was determined, alongside the temperature for further clarity and quantitative discussion on the fluid properties. Salinities used were increased to values close to saturation, and the decrease in temperature at 200 g L-1 of KCl was to such a degree that condensation started to form on the outside of the container during mixing. The conductivity values given by the sensors were modified as to the specifications of ISO-7888 for the data given in this study.53.

pH

The interaction of atmospheric plasmas and water is known to deposit and form OH and H species in water. In addition, the presence of the copper plate electrode in the grounded experiments may induce effects similar to plasma electrolysis39,40 in salt solutions. The AC APPJ used generates a slightly positive liquid surface potential. We can therefore assume that the plasma–liquid surface will act as an anode and the ground plate as a cathode within these systems, when the plasma interacts with the water. pH measurements can then be used to validate the presence of electrolysis in experiments, due to the production and distribution modification of OH and H species in electrolytic systems.
