Association of B-vitamin-derived dietary pattern with depressive symptoms: evidence from the NHANES
Authors: Peng Zhao, Mengyuan Zheng, Mengyao Wu, Yiquan Xiong, Fangyao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESDepressive symptoms are a multifactorial global mental health issue. B-vitamin intake is associated with mental and neurological health, but the association between B-vitamin-related dietary patterns and depressive symptoms remains unclear. This study examined the association between B-vitamin-related dietary patterns and depressive symptoms.SUBJECTS/METHODSData were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2005–2018. Reduced rank regression (RRR) was used to derive the dietary patterns, with 6 energy-adjusted B-vitamin intakes (B1, B2, B3, B6, B9, and B12) as response variables. Both predictor food groups and B-vitamin response variables were energy-adjusted before RRR using the residual method. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for the sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related confounders.RESULTSA total of 31,037 participants were included in the analytic sample for dietary pattern derivation and association analyses. The identified dietary pattern was characterized by the higher consumption of milk, soy products, whole grains, dark green vegetables, red and orange vegetables, and fruit, and lower consumption of added sugar, solid fat, and alcoholic drinks. In the fully adjusted model, each 1 SD increase in the dietary pattern score was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms (aOR, 0.856; 95% CI, 0.802–0.913). Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), participants in the highest quartile (Q4) had significantly lower odds (aOR, 0.668; 95% CI, 0.571–0.781; P for trend < 0.001).CONCLUSIONHigher scores for the B-vitamin-related dietary pattern were associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms among United States adults. These findings suggest that B-vitamin-related food patterns may be relevant to depressive symptoms, but causality cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional study. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these associations.Keywords: B vitamins, diet, depressive symptoms, nutrition surveys, cross-sectional studies

INTRODUCTION

Depressive symptoms remain a major global public health concern, affecting approximately 332 million individuals (5.7% of the global adult population), with a steadily increasing prevalence [1]. Beyond impairing the quality of life and social functioning, they often follow a recurrent and chronic course and are associated with an increased risk of premature mortality [2,3]. Although pharmacological and psychological interventions remain the cornerstone of treatment, their effectiveness is frequently limited by an incomplete response, adverse effects, high costs, and restricted accessibility, particularly at the population level [4,5]. Recently, diet has been increasingly recognized as a key modifiable lifestyle factor with important implications for the prevention and management of depressive symptoms [2,6,7,8].

Previous research on the association between diet and depressive symptoms has focused primarily on individual nutrients, specific food items, or simplified food categories [9,10]. On the other hand, such approaches may not adequately capture the complexity of the overall dietary structure because the intakes of nutrients and foods are often strongly correlated and may have health effects through synergistic mechanisms [11]. Dietary pattern analysis addresses these limitations by integrating the intakes of multiple foods and nutrients to characterize the overall dietary behavior and has been used widely to examine associations with a range of health outcomes [12,13,14,15]. At the same time, identifying the contribution of specific nutrients within broader dietary patterns remains essential for understanding the diet–disease relationships [16,17].

Among these nutrients, B-vitamins have attracted considerable attention because of their critical roles in the central nervous system [18,19,20]. Specifically, B-vitamins, including vitamin B6, folate, and vitamin B12, are involved in one-carbon metabolism, methylation processes, and the synthesis and regulation of neurotransmitters [21,22,23]. Insufficient intake of these nutrients can lead to elevated homocysteine levels, increased neurotoxicity, and dysregulated inflammatory responses, all of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of depressive symptoms [24,25,26,27,28]. Consistent with these mechanisms, epidemiological studies have suggested that inadequate B-vitamin intake is associated with a higher risk of depressive symptoms [29,30,31,32].

Despite growing evidence supporting the biological relevance of B-vitamins, most studies have focused on individual nutrient intake or supplement use, which may not adequately reflect how multiple B-vitamins are obtained together within real-world dietary contexts. From a dietary pattern perspective, it is unclear which combinations of commonly consumed foods best characterize the variations in B-vitamin intake and whether such food-based B-vitamin-related patterns are associated with depressive symptoms. To address this gap, 6 dietary B-vitamins were selected as response variables in reduced rank regression (RRR) to focus the analysis on B-vitamin-related dietary variations rather than on the general nutrient-based pattern. Therefore, this study aimed to derive a B-vitamin-related dietary pattern and evaluate its association with depressive symptoms, providing epidemiological evidence to inform future research on diet-related approaches to mental health.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study population and data sources

This cross-sectional study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), an ongoing, nationally representative program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. NHANES uses a complex, multistage, stratified probability sampling design to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized civilian population in the United States (U.S.) [33]. Data from 7 consecutive biennial cycles (2005–2006 through 2017–2018) were integrated to enhance statistical power and ensure the stability of dietary pattern estimates. Data integration and weighting were performed according to the NCHS analytical guidelines to account for the complex survey design [34,35].

The initial study population consisted of adults aged ≥ 20 yrs who had completed the 24-h dietary interviews and the depressive symptoms assessment. Pregnant women and individuals with implausible daily energy intakes were excluded to minimize the influence of atypical physiological states and improve the reliability of dietary data. Implausible energy intake was defined using prespecified sex-specific cutoffs: < 800 kcal/day or > 6,000 kcal/day for males and < 600 kcal/day or > 4,000 kcal/day for females. This definition was consistent with previous NHANES-based dietary pattern research using RRR [36]. The use of sex-specific cutoffs is also consistent with the methodological considerations for assessing the plausibility of self-reported dietary energy intake [37].

Participants who met all eligibility criteria after these exclusions constituted the analytic sample for deriving the B-vitamin-related dietary patterns and examining their associations with depressive symptoms. B-vitamin-related dietary pattern assessment

Dietary intake data were derived from the “What We Eat in America” component of NHANES. Data accuracy and methodological consistency were ensured by using only the first 24-h dietary recall, which was conducted in-person by trained interviewers at the Mobile Examination Center using the Automated Multiple-Pass Method of the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) [38,39]. The nutrient profiles were derived from the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) [40], and standardized food components (e.g., cup or ounce equivalents) were obtained from the Food Patterns Equivalents Database (FPED) [41]. For pattern derivation, the 29 FPED-defined categories were aggregated into 23 major food groups based on their nutritional profiles and culinary uses (Supplementary Table 1).

The RRR was applied to derive dietary patterns specifically associated with B-vitamin intake. This method identifies linear combinations of food groups (predictors) that maximize the explained variation in specific nutrients (responses) [42,43,44]. In the current analysis, the 23 aggregated food groups served as predictor variables, while the daily intakes of 6 B vitamins (B1, B2, B3, B6, B9, and B12) from the FNDDS served as the response variables. Multiple RRR-derived factors can be extracted. Therefore, all extracted factors were evaluated based on the proportion of variation explained in the B-vitamin response variables and the nutritional interpretability of their food group loading patterns. Food groups with absolute factor loadings ≥ 0.20 were identified as characteristic components [45]. For subsequent association analyses, the selected RRR-derived B-vitamin-related dietary pattern was defined as the factor that explained the largest proportion of the variation in the B-vitamin response variables and had an interpretable food group loading structure [42,46]. Other extracted factors were examined descriptively to determine if they represented additional interpretable RRR-derived dietary patterns.

The selected RRR-derived B-vitamin-related dietary pattern was determined by calculating individual scores as the weighted sum of standardized food group intakes, with weights corresponding to the factor loadings. Thus, higher scores indicated greater resemblance to the selected dietary pattern. For subsequent association analyses, the study population was stratified into quartiles based on these scores, with the highest quartile (Q4) representing the highest dietary pattern score category.

Depressive symptoms assessment

The depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a reliable and internationally validated screening tool with high diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity and specificity of 88%) [47]. The PHQ-9 evaluates 9 symptoms based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria, with participants reporting their frequency over the preceding 2 weeks. Each item was scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), resulting in a total score of 0 to 27. Consistent with the established clinical cutoffs and previous NHANES-based research [48], a total score of ≥ 10 was used to define clinically relevant depressive symptoms.

Covariate

Based on a directed acyclic graph (DAG, Supplementary Fig. 1), the covariates included in this study were categorized into the following 3 groups: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, poverty-income ratio (PIR), and employment status; (2) lifestyle factors, encompassing the smoking status, total daily energy intake, body mass index (BMI), sleep disorders, and physical activity (measured in MET-min/week) [49]; and (3) health conditions.

Specifically, cardiovascular and respiratory diseases were identified through self-reported physician diagnoses. Hypertension and diabetes were defined using a comprehensive approach that integrated the self-reported diagnoses, current medication use, and clinical or laboratory measurements (i.e., blood pressure for hypertension; fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and oral glucose tolerance tests for diabetes).

Statistical analyses

The statistical analysis followed a 2-stage approach: initially deriving and validating a B-vitamin-related dietary pattern through RRR, and examining its association with depressive symptoms using survey-weighted logistic regression models to ensure national representativeness.

Before conducting RRR, the predictor food groups and response B-vitamin variables were energy-adjusted using the residual method [50]. Specifically, each of the 23 aggregated food groups and each of the 6 B-vitamin intakes were regressed separately on the total energy intake, and the resulting standardized residuals were used in RRR analysis. The B-vitamin-related dietary pattern was then derived using RRR, with the energy-adjusted residuals of 23 food groups as the predictor variables and the energy-adjusted residuals of the daily intakes of 6 B-vitamins, including vitamins B1, B2, B3, B6, B9, and B12, as response variables. The sampling weights were not applied during this step to focus on the inherent dietary structure and ensure pattern stability [42,44]. Spearman rank correlation analyses were performed between the dietary pattern scores and individual B-vitamin variables to evaluate the ability of the derived dietary pattern to capture B-vitamin-related variability. The RRR-derived dietary pattern explaining the largest proportion of variation in the B-vitamin response variables was selected as the primary exposure for subsequent analyses according to the predefined factor-retention criteria.

Subsequent population-level analyses incorporated NHANES complex survey weights and design parameters [51]. The participants were categorized into quartiles based on the survey-weighted distribution of the dietary pattern score. The differences in baseline characteristics across these quartiles were evaluated using survey-weighted generalized linear models for the continuous variables and Rao–Scott χ2 tests for the categorical variables. The values are presented as the weighted means ± SEs for the continuous variables or weighted percentages (SE) for the categorical variables.

Missing data for the major covariates were reported, and the missing covariate values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. Five imputed datasets were generated, and their estimates were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between the B-vitamin-related dietary pattern score and depressive symptoms. The dietary pattern score was analyzed as a categorical variable based on survey-weighted quartiles and as a continuous variable. For the continuous analysis, the dietary pattern scores were standardized using a Z-score transformation, so that the ORs represent the risk change per one SD increase. Five successive models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity; Model 2 further adjusted for education, marital status, PIR, and employment status; Model 3 additionally incorporating BMI, smoking status, sleep disorders, and physical activity; Model 4 further accounting for comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory disease); and Model 5 additionally adjusted for total energy intake. The linear trends across quartiles were assessed by treating the median value of each quartile as a continuous variable.

Subgroup analyses based on the key sociodemographic characteristics were performed to evaluate the consistency of the associations and explore the potential effect of modification. Three sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. First, RRR was repeated without prior energy residual adjustment, and the primary association analyses were repeated using the dietary pattern score derived from this non-energy-adjusted RRR analysis. Second, the primary association analyses were repeated after excluding participants with antidepressant use identified from the prescription medication records. Third, complete-case analyses were performed by excluding the participants with missing covariate data and repeating the primary association analyses.

DAG was constructed using DAGitty version 3.1 (Johannes Textor, Radboud University Medical Center, Nijmegen, The Netherlands) to identify the minimal sufficient adjustment sets for confounding control. All subsequent statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All hypothesis tests were 2-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Compared with the participants in the lowest quartile (Q1), those in the highest quartile (Q4) were more likely to be male (57.3% vs. 50.0%), have higher levels of education (college graduate and above, 38.8% vs. 21.3%), have a higher family income-to-poverty ratio (≥ 4.00, 39.4% vs. 31.0%), and be never smokers (61.5% vs. 46.7%). The participants in Q4 also had lower proportions of current smoking, obesity, and sleep disorder, whereas the patterns for comorbidities were not entirely consistent across the specific conditions. Specifically, the prevalence of depressive symptoms was lower in Q4 than in Q1 (5.20% vs. 10.40%).

Characteristics of the derived B-vitamin-related dietary pattern

Six RRR-derived factors were extracted from energy-adjusted RRR analysis, corresponding to the 6 B-vitamin response variables. Among these factors, Factor 1 explained the largest proportion of the variation in the B-vitamin response variables, accounting for 15.15% of the total response variation and 4.50% of the variation in the 23 predictor food groups. The corresponding proportions of B-vitamin variation explained by Factors 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 were 6.35%, 4.30%, 1.56%, 1.31%, and 0.50%, respectively. Therefore, according to the predefined factor-retention criteria, Factor 1 was selected as the RRR-derived B-vitamin-related dietary pattern for subsequent association analyses. The selected RRR-derived B-vitamin-related dietary pattern was characterized by nine food groups with absolute factor loadings ≥ 0.20 (Fig. 2). This pattern had positive loadings for milk, soy products, whole grains, dark green vegetables, red and orange vegetables, and fruit, and negative loadings for added sugar, solid fat, and alcoholic drinks. Among the positive contributors, milk showed the highest factor loading, followed by soy products and whole grains. These food group loadings suggested a dietary pattern characterized by a higher consumption of nutrient-dense plant foods and milk, and lower consumption of added sugar, solid fat, and alcoholic drinks.

The relevance of the derived dietary pattern score to B-vitamin intake was further supported by its correlations with individual B vitamins. Spearman rank correlation analyses showed that the dietary pattern score was significantly and positively associated with all 6 B vitamins, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.34 to 0.57 (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2). The proportions of the variation in individual B vitamins explained by Factor 1 were 28.19%, 18.11%, 11.06%, 7.74%, 21.73%, and 4.06% for vitamins B1, B2, B3, B6, B9, and B12, respectively. Pairwise correlations among the 6 B-vitamin intakes were weak to moderate, with Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.19 to 0.68, suggesting that these nutrients shared related but non-identical dietary sources (Supplementary Table 2).

Supplementary Table 3 lists the distribution of standardized, energy-adjusted food group variables and B-vitamin values across quartiles of the dietary pattern score. Higher quartiles of the dietary pattern score were generally characterized by the higher standardized intakes of positively loaded food groups and B vitamins, and lower standardized intakes of negatively loaded food groups.

In sensitivity analyses using RRR without an energy residual adjustment, Factor 1 explained 35.32% of the variation in the B-vitamin response variables and 8.50% of the variation in the predictor food groups, which were higher than the corresponding proportions in the energy-adjusted RRR analysis. These results provided complementary information on the influence of the energy adjustment on the derived dietary pattern. Supplementary Table 4 lists the detailed factor loadings, explained variation, and correlations with B-vitamin intakes from the non-energy-adjusted RRR analysis.

B-vitamin-related dietary pattern and risk of depressive symptoms

Table 2 presents the associations between the B-vitamin-related dietary pattern score and depressive symptoms. In Model 1, adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, higher quartiles of the dietary pattern score were associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms. Compared with the participants in Q1, the aORs for depressive symptoms were 0.835 (95% CI, 0.716–0.974), 0.602 (95% CI, 0.515–0.704), and 0.478 (95% CI, 0.408–0.560) for Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively.

After further adjustments for the sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, BMI, sleep disorders, chronic comorbidities, and total energy intake, the inverse association remained statistically significant for Q3 and Q4. In the fully adjusted model, compared to Q1, the aORs for depressive symptoms were 0.949 (95% CI, 0.804–1.120), 0.738 (95% CI, 0.628–0.867), and 0.668 (95% CI, 0.571–0.781) for Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively. An inverse trend remained evident across increasing quartiles of the B-vitamin-related dietary pattern score (P for trend < 0.001). When the dietary pattern score was analyzed as a continuous variable, each one-SD increase in the score was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted model (aOR, 0.856; 95% CI, 0.802–0.913). Consistent results were observed across sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Supplementary Tables 5, 6, 7).

Subgroup analyses showed generally consistent associations between the B-vitamin-related dietary pattern score and depressive symptoms across major population subgroups. The inverse association was directionally consistent in males and females, and no statistically significant interaction by sex was observed (P for interaction = 0.662). No significant interaction was observed according to the BMI category (P for interaction = 0.822). Similarly, no significant interactions were observed according to age, race/ethnicity, education status, marital status, family income-to-poverty ratio, or employment status (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

In this nationally representative cross-sectional study of U.S. adults, a B-vitamin-related dietary pattern was derived using reduced-rank regression, with 6 energy-adjusted B-vitamin intakes as response variables. The identified pattern was characterized by a higher consumption of milk, soy products, whole grains, dark green vegetables, red and orange vegetables, and fruit, and a lower consumption of added sugar, solid fat, and alcoholic drinks. Higher scores for this dietary pattern were associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms after a multivariable adjustment.

These findings are generally consistent with previous studies linking healthier dietary patterns to more favorable mental health profiles. Prior observational studies and meta-analyses have suggested that dietary patterns rich in fruit, vegetables, whole grains, and other nutrient-dense foods are associated with lower odds of depression or depressive symptoms [10,52,53]. In contrast, Western-style dietary patterns and food groups characterized by higher intakes of added sugars, sugar-sweetened beverages, high-fat foods, and energy-dense processed foods have been associated with less favorable mental health outcomes [52,54,55,56]. The pattern identified in the present study shared several features with these healthier dietary patterns, including higher intakes of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, soy products, and milk, together with lower intakes of added sugar, solid fat, and alcoholic drinks.

An important methodological issue in this study is the role of total energy intake in deriving the B-vitamin-related dietary pattern. The absolute intakes of both food groups and micronutrients are closely related to the total energy intake. Therefore, dietary patterns derived from non-energy-adjusted variables may partly reflect the overall food quantity rather than dietary composition [57]. In the primary analysis, both predictor food groups and B-vitamin response variables were energy-adjusted before RRR to reduce the influence of the total energy intake. Sensitivity analysis using the dietary pattern scores derived without an energy residual adjustment yielded a different explanatory profile of the RRR solution, with a higher proportion of B-vitamin response variation explained by the factor selected as the primary dietary pattern and a somewhat different set of leading food contributors. This contrast suggests that the total energy intake may materially influence the dietary pattern identified by RRR. Therefore, the energy-adjusted pattern in the primary analysis may better reflect a B-vitamin-related dietary profile independent of the total energy intake.

A related consideration is excluding participants with implausible energy intakes. Self-reported dietary data are subject to measurement error, and extremely low or high reported energy intakes may reflect an atypical dietary intake, reporting error, or unusual physiological conditions [58]. Therefore, prespecified sex-specific cutoffs were applied to exclude individuals with implausible daily energy intakes, consistent with previous NHANES-based dietary pattern research aimed at improving the plausibility of dietary intake data [36]. Although this approach may reduce the influence of extreme dietary reports, it may also affect the distribution of food groups and nutrient intakes used for RRR. Therefore, the identified dietary pattern should be interpreted as applicable primarily to adults with plausible reported energy intakes.

Several neurobiological pathways may provide biological plausibility for the observed inverse association. Because the RRR-derived pattern explained the relatively larger variation in vitamins B1, B9, and B2, mechanisms involving these B vitamins may be particularly relevant. B vitamins are involved in brain energy metabolism, one-carbon metabolism, methylation reactions, homocysteine regulation, and neurotransmitter-related pathways [59]. Thiamine plays a vital role in glucose metabolism and neuronal function, which may be relevant given the high energy demand of the brain [60]. Folate and vitamin B12 are required for the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine and for the synthesis of S-adenosylmethionine, a methyl donor involved in methylation reactions and neurotransmitter metabolism [61]. Riboflavin, through its coenzyme forms, also participates in homocysteine metabolism and folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism [62]. Vitamin B6, although less strongly captured by the present pattern than vitamins B1, B9, and B2, is also involved in various neurotransmitter-related metabolic pathways, including pathways related to serotonin, dopamine, and γ-aminobutyric acid [63]. These pathways provide a plausible biological context for the observed association between the B-vitamin-related dietary pattern and depressive symptoms. These mechanistic considerations should be interpreted as the biological context for the observed dietary pattern association, rather than as evidence that any individual B vitamin independently accounts for the association or that synergistic interactions exist among B vitamins.

This study had several strengths. First, a large, nationally representative sample of U.S. adults was used, which enhances the population relevance of the findings within the U.S. Second, the use of RRR allowed the derivation of a dietary pattern informed by multiple B-vitamin response variables, rather than relying solely on predefined dietary indices or purely data-driven dietary patterns. Third, the predictor food groups and B-vitamin response variables were energy-adjusted before RRR, which helped reduce the influence of the total energy intake on the identified dietary pattern.

Nevertheless, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precluded causal inference and could not exclude reverse causation because depressive symptoms may influence appetite, food choices, and dietary reporting. Second, dietary intake was assessed using 24-h dietary recalls, which are prone to recall error and may not fully reflect usual long-term intake. Third, although this study adjusted for a wide range of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related covariates, residual confounding remains possible. In particular, important psychosocial factors such as psychological stress, psychiatric history, social support, and prior mental health treatment were not fully considered. Fourth, this study was based on the dietary intake rather than the circulating B-vitamin biomarkers. Therefore, this study could not directly assess the B-vitamin status, absorption, or metabolism. Fifth, this study did not examine the associations between individual B-vitamin intakes and depressive symptoms, nor did it conduct interaction or joint-effect analyses among the B vitamins. Therefore, this study cannot determine the independent contribution of each B vitamin or evaluate the potential synergistic effects among B vitamins. Sixth, the RRR-derived dietary pattern depends on the selected food group classification, response variables, energy-adjustment approach, and analytic decisions, which may influence pattern derivation and reproducibility. Finally, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings beyond U.S. adults. The identified B-vitamin-related dietary pattern may partly reflect the dietary structure, food availability, and nutrient fortification practices in the U.S. The major dietary sources of B vitamins may differ across countries because of variations in food fortification policies, staple food patterns, dietary culture, and the consumption of milk, soy products, whole grains, vegetables, and animal-source foods. Therefore, external validation will be needed in populations with different dietary structures and nutrient sources.

In conclusion, a B-vitamin-related dietary pattern characterized by higher consumption of milk, soy products, whole grains, vegetables, and fruit, and lower consumption of added sugar, solid fat, and alcoholic drinks was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms among U.S. adults. These findings suggest that B-vitamin-related food patterns may be relevant to depressive symptoms, but they do not establish causality. Future prospective cohort studies, biomarker-based analyses, and randomized dietary intervention studies will be needed to confirm these associations and clarify the potential role of B-vitamin-related dietary patterns in mental health.
