Junctophilin-2-orchestrated calcium signalosome regulates brown adipocyte thermogenesis and energy metabolism
Authors: Biyi Chen, Jinxi Wang, Qingwen Qian, Guangqin Zhang, Yihui Wang, Mark Li, Rubin Tan, Zhuoying Yang, Weiyang Zhao, Grace Ciampa, Daniela Sarahi Yang Bennett, Yixi Liu, Qi Zhu, Zhiyong Zhu, Sepiso K. Masenga, Zer Vue, Han Le, Qian Shi, Leonid V. Zingman, Antentor O. Hinton, Duane D. Hall, E. Dale Abel, Ling Yang, Long-Sheng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dysfunctional adipocyte calcium handling is implicated in obesity and thermogenesis. Junctophilins (JPs) stabilize calcium microdomain junctions between the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum, but whether JPs are required for adipocyte function is not known. We show that JP2 is enriched in thermogenic brown adipose tissue (BAT) relative to other fat depots and is downregulated under conditions of nutrient overload. Conditional knockdown of JP2 in adipocytes, and more selectively in BAT, exacerbates cold intolerance and susceptibility to diet induced obesity. Mechanistically, JP2-depleted brown adipocytes exhibit calcium handling dysfunction with elevated cytosolic calcium levels at baseline but diminished norepinephrine-induced calcium transients, reduced store-operated calcium entry. Basal cytosolic calcium overload accounts for an increase in calpain activation and ensuing downregulation of STIM1 and hormone-sensitive lipase in JP2-depleted cells. Furthermore, JP2 silencing in brown adipocytes reduced oxygen consumption rates and compromised mitochondrial structure and quality. Together, these findings demonstrate that JP2 is essential for normal calcium homeostasis in brown adipocytes and reveal a critical role for JP2 in thermogenesis and resistance to diet-induced metabolic dysregulation.

Brown adipose tissue (BAT) activation is essential for classical nonshivering thermogenesis by utilizing intracellular and systemic lipids and extracellular glucose as fuel sources (1–7). Acute increases in nutritional intake stimulate BAT activation and thermogenesis (2, 3). However, chronic excessive nutrient exposure results in “whitening” of BAT and reduced BAT metabolic capacity in mice and humans (8–10). Although BAT mass declines with age and obesity (11, 12), studies in both rodents and humans demonstrate that improvement of BAT function ameliorates hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia and reduces adipose tissue inflammation that characterizes obesity (13–16). Accumulating evidence also demonstrates the cardioprotective role of BAT in cardiovascular disease (17–20). Despite many advances in our understanding of adipocyte biology, obesity remains a challenging problem and a threat to human health and well-being.

Ca2+ ions are important for diverse cellular signaling events, including those involved in obesity. Regulation of intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis is initiated in most cells through intricate, integrated signaling between plasma membrane (PM) ion channels and Ca2+ release channels on intracellular organelles such as endo- or sarco-plasmic reticulum (ER/SR) (21). Disruption of such signaling in hepatocytes has been linked to the pathogenesis of obesity (22) and work in adipocytes has indicated a link between changes in intracellular Ca2+ concentrations and insulin resistance (23–25) but the molecular source of Ca2+ influx remains elusive (2, 26). A recent study, however, demonstrated that intracellular IP3 receptor-mediated aberrant Ca2+ signaling in adipocytes leads to immuno-metabolic imbalance in obesity (27).

It has been long appreciated that plasma membrane depolarization in brown adipocytes is triggered by cold and neurohormonal stimulation [e.g., norepinephrine (NE)] (28) that possibly involves both inward and outward membrane ion channel currents (29, 30). Furthermore, a store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) pathway is also present in brown adipocytes (31), a pathway known to replenish depleted ER/SR Ca2+ stores and mediated by Orai and STIM proteins (32–34). Nevertheless, it is unclear how different Ca2+ handling ion channels and transporters are integrated to regulate intracellular Ca2+ dynamics in brown adipocytes or if disturbed Ca2+ homeostasis in BAT contributes to obesity and obesity-associated pathologies, including insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

Brown adipocytes share a common lineage with skeletal muscle cells, the critical source of shivering thermogenesis (35, 36). Ca2+ cycling in cardiac muscle cells is well understood to be regulated by Junctophilin-2 (JP2), a critical structural protein that spatially organizes the SR–PM junctions essential for precise control of myocyte contractility (37, 38). However, whether JP2 participates in normal Ca2+ homeostasis in brown adipocytes or if it contributes to obesity and associated metabolic disorders is not known. In this study, we provide evidence that JP2 is an important regulator of intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis in brown adipocytes with JP2 loss of function contributing to obesity and impaired thermogenesis in vivo.

Results

JP2 Is Enriched in Brown Adipose Tissue and Is Downregulated by an Obesogenic Diet.

Publicly available data indicate Jph2, the gene encoding JP2, is the predominant junctophilin in BAT with Jph1 being expressed at threefold lower levels and Jph3 and Jph4 approximating background noise (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). We found a similar expression pattern in BAT isolated from adult mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Immunoblotting of isolated mouse fat depots revealed JP2 protein expression is sixfold more abundant in intrascapular brown adipose tissue (BAT) relative to inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) but is undetectable in epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) (Fig. 1 A and B). A similar relative enrichment of JP2 was observed in TERT immortalized human BAT vs. WAT adipocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). These results suggest JP2 may have unappreciated functions in adipocytes, particularly in BAT. Since BAT becomes less metabolically active and whitens in obesity, we asked whether the relatively high levels of JP2 in BAT are regulated in response to a high-fat diet (HFD). Immunoblotting and immunofluorescence analysis revealed JP2 becomes downregulated in BAT over a 20-wk HFD intervention as compared to regular diet (RD)-fed controls (Fig. 1 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1F).

In Vivo Knockdown of JP2 in Brown Adipocytes Exacerbates Diet Induced Obesity and Metabolic Dysfunction.

To discern whether JP2 downregulation in adipose depots in response to HFD is a consequence of diet or a contributing factor of adiposity, we generated an adipocyte restricted JP2 knockdown model (Adipo-shJP2, SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) by crossing Adipo-Cre mice with mice that conditionally express a JP2 short hairpin RNA (39). Immunoblotting confirmed JP2 is downregulated in BAT and iWAT of 2 to 3-month-old Adipo-shJP2 mice without altering the expression of the thermogenic marker UCP1, relative to their littermate controls (Fig. 1 E and F). Male Adipo-shJP2 mice gained significantly more weight on a 20-wk HFD than Adipo-Cre littermates beginning after 14 wk despite showing no growth differences on a regular diet (RD) (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). Body composition analyses by NMR and ex vivo analysis revealed that male HFD-fed Adipo-shJP2 mice have elevated fat mass but reduced lean mass when compared to HFD-fed Adipo-Cre controls (Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Figs. S1G and S2 D–G).

HFD feeding alone is sufficient to induce a prediabetes-like metabolic syndrome characterized by moderate hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular complications. RD-fed Adipo-shJP2 mice had similar fasting glucose levels and comparable glucose and insulin sensitivity as Adipo-Cre littermates. HFD feeding, however, exacerbated glucose intolerance and circulating insulin levels in Adipo-shJP2 mice relative to Adipo-Cre mice (Fig. 1 I–K and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 H and I). Histological staining of isolated BAT revealed HFD promoted significant vacuolization and hypertrophying of brown adipocytes after JP2 knockdown (Fig. 1 L and M). We reasoned that the increased adiposity, BAT cell size, and diabetic susceptibility after JP2 silencing may be due to limited adipocyte lipolysis. Indeed, serum glycerol levels of Adipo-shJP2 mice were significantly reduced relative to those of Adipo-Cre controls as was NE-induced glycerol release from BAT SVF cell cultures after JP2 silencing (Fig. 1 N and O).

We next sought to identify JP2-dependent mechanisms underlying HFD responses and thermoregulation. BAT differentiation and thermogenic capacity is dependent on PPARγ-mediated transcriptional regulation, particularly through the PPARγ2 isoform, and UCP1-dependent uncoupling (40–42). JP2 silencing had minimal effects on total protein levels of UCP1 and PPARγ when mice were given either a RD or a HFD (Fig. 1 P–R). HFD, however, promoted PPARγ isoform switching from adipocyte enriched PPARγ2 to the ubiquitous PPARγ1 isoform, a change we found to be significantly more robust in Adipo-shJP2 BAT. Interestingly, neither HFD nor JP2 silencing had a significant effect on UCP1 levels. To more specifically address JP2 function in BAT, we repeated HFD studies in UCP1-shJP2 mice that silence Jph2 expression in UCP1 expressing adipocytes. Consistent with global adipocyte JP2 knockdown mice, HFD-fed UCP1-shJP2 male mice gained more weight, had increased fat depot masses, and were more glucose intolerant than their UCP1-Cre littermate controls (Fig. 1 S–V and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 J–O). These results support the idea that localized expression of JP2 in BAT not only influences BAT function but also affects the mice to diet-induced metabolic dysregulation.

Adipocyte JP2 Silencing Attenuates Thermogenic Responses.

We next assessed the idea that JP2 has a physiological role in regulating thermogenesis. We recorded in vivo temperature changes in Adipo-Cre and Adipo-shJP2 mice in response to known thermogenic stresses. NE administration (1 mg/kg, IP) induced an increase in tissue temperatures, as expected (Fig. 2 A–C). Despite our finding that UCP1 expression is preserved in BAT of Adipo-shJP2 mice, the magnitude of temperature changes in BAT and core body was significantly blunted in Adipo-shJP2 animals, with a similar trend in iWAT. Muscle tissue temperature increases were not significantly different between genotypes indicating that JP2-dependent thermogenesis from adipocytes does not involve shivering thermogenesis. To confirm adipocyte JP2 knockdown impairs thermogenic capacity, we exposed Adipo-shJP2 mice to a cold challenge (Fig. 2 D and E). The average body temperature of Adipo-shJP2 mice was consistently lower than that of Adipo-Cre mice when housed at 4 °C. Core body temperature of Adipo-shJP2 mice, however, returned to that of control littermates after transitioning to a 30 °C environment indicating normothermia can be achieved under ideal conditions. Differences in cold-induced thermoregulation were not attributable to altered systemic metabolism, activity, food intake (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–E), cardiac function (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F–J), or blood pressure (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 K and L). Thermogenesis was also evaluated under HFD feeding in which we found the core body temperature of Adipo-Cre but not Adipo-shJP2 mice increased significantly (Fig. 2F). UCP1-shJP2 mice were found to have a similar attenuation of NE-stimulated tissue temperature increases as Adipo-shJP2 mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3M), ruling out the contribution of non–brown fat adipose tissues in JP2-dependent thermogenic responses. We also observed that the known sensitivity of UCP1 knockout animals to cold challenge (43) was not altered by additional JP2 knockdown in all adipocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3N), which supports a hypothesis in which JP2 operates within the UCP1 thermogenic pathway in BAT and critical for nonshivering thermogenesis.

JP2 Deficiency Promotes Mitochondrial Dysfunction in Brown Adipocytes.

The impaired glycerol release and thermogenic capacity of JP2-silenced brown adipocytes suggests a defect in mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism and uncoupling efficiency. We therefore evaluated potential differences in the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of differentiated BAT stromal vascular fraction (SVF) cells cultured from Adipo-Cre vs. Adipo-shJP2 animals using the Seahorse assay. Preadipocytes were isolated from the SVF of BAT and differentiated for 10 d. Under basal conditions, Adipo-shJP2 brown adipocytes demonstrated reduced OCR normalized to cell number (Fig. 3 A and B). Treatment with 1 μM NE increased basal respiration in both Adipo-Cre control and Adipo-shJP2 cells but this increase was blunted in Adipo-shJP2 cells to a level comparable to unstimulated Adipo-Cre basal respiration. A similar trend was found in proton leak, maximal, and mitochondrial independent respiration (Fig. 3 C–E).

Molecular analyses indicate Adipo-shJP2 brown adipocytes express canonical brown adipocyte markers but undergo subtle remodeling that likely contributes to impaired oxygen consumption and thermogenesis. We found no evidence that oxidative phosphorylation complex subunits are repressed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B) in Adipo-shJP2 cells. Quantitative PCR indicate Fapb4, Adipoq, Pparg, and Lipe (HSL) expressions are unaltered while Ucp1 expression is increased and Ppargc1a (PGC1a) is decreased. Immunoblotting revealed total UCP1 and PGC1α protein levels remain unaltered as did total PPARγ and ATGL (Fig. 3 F–H). However, consistent with in vivo Adipo-shJP2 BAT changes after HFD feeding, we found a significant PPARγ subtype switching from γ2 to γ1 in Adipo-shJP2 cultures. Interestingly, JP2-silenced cells also had reduced levels of total and phosphorylated hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), a key lipolytic enzyme in brown fat. Lower HSL activity would limit the availability of free fatty acids for UCP1-dependent thermogenesis and oxidative metabolism (43). The reduction in HSL is also sufficient to explain reduced plasma glycerol levels in Adipo-shJP2 mice and the decrease in NE-stimulated glycerol release from cultured Adipo-shJP2 BAT SVF adipocytes (Fig. 1 L and M).

As changes to OCR can also reflect changes in mitochondrial ultrastructure, we performed ultrastructural analysis of BAT samples using serial block-face scanning electron microscopy (SBF-SEM). Mitochondria of Adipo-shJP2 brown adipocytes are more abundant than in Adipo-Cre tissue, but smaller, indicating an increase in mitochondrial fission and/or fragmentation. In agreement with this, cristae within Adipo-shJP2 brown adipocyte mitochondria are fewer and have a lower quality index than those in control mitochondria (Fig. 3 I–M). Quantification of 3D reconstructed SEM images revealed that the average volume of mitochondria and lipid droplets is also reduced when JP2 is silenced (Fig. 3 N–R and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–M).

JP2 Is Required for Store-Operated Ca2+ Handling and Ca2+ Homeostasis in Brown Adipocytes.

Given the established role of JP2 is to maintain Ca2+ crosstalk between the plasma membrane and intracellular Ca2+ stores in cardiac myocytes and fibroblasts (37, 44), we assessed whether JP2 silencing in brown adipocytes affects intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis using live cell confocal Ca2+ imaging. JP2 knockdown did not affect the ability of adipocytes isolated from the SVF to differentiate and form clusters of lipid droplets (Fig. 4A). Perfusion of mature control Adipo-Cre adipocytes with NE induced frequent Ca2+ waves (or oscillations) that returned to baseline after NE washout (Fig. 4 A and B). Ca2+ dynamics in NE stimulated Adipo-shJP2 adipocytes, however, were strikingly different from those of Adipo-Cre cells, displaying lower average Ca2+ transient amplitudes that were slower, fewer, and tended to remain after NE washout (Fig. 4 C–F). Interestingly, the size of Adipo-JP2 BAT SVF cells that had undergone the differentiation process were significantly larger than that of differentiated WT BAT SVF adipocytes (Fig. 4G). These data indicate that JP2 is required for normal brown adipocyte Ca2+ handling and its response to catecholamines.

Since cold exposure and neurohumoral stimulation trigger plasma membrane depolarization in brown adipocytes (28), we sought to pharmacologically determine the molecular mechanisms by which JP2 regulates Ca2+ homeostasis in response to NE (Fig. 4H). Treatment with CL316243 and methoxamine elicited Ca2+ transients that were not significantly different from those treated with NE confirming the presence of β3- and α1-ARs, respectively, in brown adipocytes (45–47). Cotreatment with NE and prazosin, but not propranolol, prevented NE-induced Ca2+ transients, indicating α1-AR activation, but not that of β3-AR, is required for NE responses. We then evaluated the nature of Ca2+ transients and found BAPTA-AM, thapsigargin (TG), and 2-aminoethoxydiphenyl borate (2-APB) inhibited NE-induced transients revealing transients require highly localized Ca2+ microdomains, uptake by sarco-endoplasmic reticulum ATPases (SERCA), and ER Ca2+ efflux via inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate receptors (IP3Rs), respectively. The lack of a response to caffeine and a failure of ryanodine to attenuate NE effects indicate ryanodine receptors do not contribute to this pathway. A similar pharmacologic profile was observed in differentiated hTERT BAT cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) demonstrating NE responses in brown adipocytes operate through an α1/β3-AR–SERCA–IP3 receptor Ca2+ handling mechanism.

To rule out potential off-target effects of the JP2 shRNA, we performed rescue experiments in brown adipocytes derived from Cre-negative loxP-shJP2 mice. Differentiated adipocyte cultures were transduced with a negative control adenovirus (Ad-NC) or a Cre-expressing adenovirus (Ad-Cre) that allows for conditional expression of the shRNA hairpin. NE-stimulated Ca2+ transients were then recorded from differentiated adipocytes 72 to 96 h posttransduction (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B–E). The induction of JP2 shRNA expression with Ad-Cre successfully knocked down endogenous JP2 and attenuated Ca2+ transient amplitude and HSL expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F–H) to a similar extent as chronic expressing Adipo-shJP2 adipocytes (Figs. 4D and 3 G and H). The specificity of the JP2 shRNA was confirmed with adenoviral Flag-JP2 expression (Ad-JP2) that restored NE-induced Ca2+ amplitudes and HSL protein to control levels. During this investigation we developed a conditional floxed Jph2 mouse model (Jph2fl/fl) (44) which provided a second genetic model to test the consequences of JP2 deficiency. Repeating the above adenoviral studies in differentiated brown adipocytes from Jph2fl/fl mice demonstrated Cre-dependent abrogation of NE-stimulated Ca2+ transient amplitudes and HSL expression with JP2 overexpression normalizing these changes (Fig. 4 I–O).

To begin discerning mechanisms underlying altered Ca2+ responses, we immunoblotted lysates from the Jph2fl/fl and loxP-shJP2 rescue experiments and found that JP2 depletion blunted protein expression of the ER Ca2+ sensor STIM1, but not that of the Orai1 SOCE channel or IP3R (Fig. 4 N–O and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 G and H). As with HSL, STIM1 protein abundance was restored with Ad-JP2 and suggests ER Ca2+ sensing processes, such as SOCE, may be regulated by JP2 levels. Immunoblotting also demonstrated that JP1 levels remained unchanged in JP2-deficient and JP2-rescued adipocytes (Fig. 4 N–O), indicating JP1 is unlikely to compensate for JP2. Further immunoblotting of Adipo-shJP2 brown adipocyte lysates confirmed the downregulation of STIM1 after JP2 silencing and revealed a potential compensatory upregulation of Orai3 and SERCA2b without alterations to Orai1, IP3R, or the NCX1 Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 I and J).

Based on these results, we explored whether SOCE is in fact dependent on JP2. We first validated SOCE activity in wildtype brown adipocytes. Incubation of cells with the SOCE inhibitor 5J4 (1 μM) or the IP3R antagonist 2-APB (1 μM) effectively diminished TG-mediated Ca2+ transients under Ca2+ free conditions (Fig. 5 A and B). We next assayed whether JP2 physically interacts with Ca2+ handling proteins or regulators of mitochondrial fission and fusion. Flag immunoprecipitation from Ad-Flag-JP2 transduced adipocytes demonstrated a specific interaction only for STIM1 (Fig. 5C) suggesting JP2 may directly participate in SOCE regulation. In further support of this idea, Ca2+ transients in response to TG and after re-exposure to extracellular Ca2+ (1.8 mM) were significantly reduced in Adipo-shJP2 cells compared to Adipo-Cre cells (Fig. 5 D–G). Rescue experiments analogous to those described above demonstrated transients triggered by extracellular Ca2+ after ER Ca2+ depletion were reduced in JP2-deficient cells and rescued by heterologous JP2 expression (Fig. 5 H–M and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Taken together, JP2 deficiency in brown adipocytes perturbs ER Ca2+ sensing through the downregulation of STIM1 and the disruption of the JP2–STIM1 interaction while restoration of JP2 expression normalizes SOCE and the expression of STIM1 and HSL.

JP2 Loss-of-Function Promotes Calpain Activation and the Downregulation of STIM1 and Hormone-Sensitive Lipase.

We wondered whether the downregulation of STIM1 and HSL protein in Adipo-shJP2 adipocytes is due to transcriptional vs. posttranscriptional regulation. qPCR analysis revealed a significant decrease in Stim1 mRNA levels but not Lipe, the gene encoding HSL (Fig. 6 A and B). A possible posttranslational mechanism may be similar to that demonstrated for cardiomyocytes in which there is a reciprocal relationship between disease, JP2 downregulation, Ca2+ overload, and calpain activation (48–50). Indeed, we find that JP2-silenced adipocytes have induced calpain subunit expression, calpain activation, and elevated basal Ca2+ levels and demonstrate that JP2 rescue in JP2 knockdown cells normalizes both baseline Ca2+ overload and calpain activation (Fig. 6 C–G). We therefore assessed the potential that HSL and STIM1 are calpain substrates using in vitro calpain cleavage assays that showed micromolar Ca2+ is sufficient to induce calpain-1 (Capn1)-dependent proteolysis of both proteins, which were inhibited by the calpain inhibitor MDL-28170 (Fig. 6 H and I). Calpain-2 (Capn2) is activated by millimolar Ca2+ and showed only modest cleavage activity in these assays.

Last, we examined whether the changes we observed in differentiated adipocytes in response to JP2 depletion correlate with HFD-induced changes in vivo that were shown to repress JP2 (Fig. 1 C and D). Immunoblotting of BAT isolated from mice fed a RD or HFD revealed increased Capn2 and CapnS1 subunit expression, a trend of increase in Capn1 expression and decreased STIM1 and HSL expression (Fig. 6 J and K). In agreement with other HFD studies, (51) UCP1 protein levels increased by ~30% and likely reflects a compensatory response to nutrient overload.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates brown adipocyte mediated thermogenesis and metabolic function depends on uncompromised JP2 activity. JP2 is highly expressed in thermogenic brown adipocytes relative to other fat depots but is downregulated in the context of obesity. Mimicking this downregulation through JP2 silencing in all adipocytes or in UCP1 expressing brown adipocytes increases susceptibility to diet-induced metabolic dysfunction, as evidenced by exacerbated obesity, impaired glucose intolerance, and blunted nonshivering thermogenesis.

While BAT and WAT are regarded as the principal fat depots responsible for thermogenesis and lipid storage, respectively, both can exhibit characteristics of the other when adapting to the metabolic status of the organism (2, 3). During nutrient overloading conditions, BAT “whitens” through increased lipid accumulation and cellular hypertrophy. WAT, on the other hand, “beiges” during prolonged cold exposure through increased mitochondrial content, UCP1 expression, and respiratory uncoupling (8–10). We propose JP2 levels play a part in adipose tissue plasticity, at least in BAT. We find Adipo-shJP2 and UCP1-shJP2 mice have increased adiposity but attenuated thermogenesis when fed an HFD that correlates with increased brown adipocyte size and vacuolization and reduced glycerol secretion from lipolysis. While recent studies have raised concerns about the UCP1-Cre model for studying BAT function due to abnormal chromosome 1 integrity and off-target Cre expression (52, 53), the overlapping phenotypes we find for UCP1-shJP2 and Adipo-shJP2 mice argue against confounding secondary artifacts and instead favor a direct effect of JP2 silencing in BAT.

Studies in both rodents and humans demonstrate that improvement of BAT function ameliorates hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia during obesity (13, 15, 16, 54). β3-adrenergic receptor (AR) agonists, including mirabegron, stimulate BAT glucose uptake (55–57), increase human brown fat metabolic activity (56, 58) and improve glucose homeostasis in obese humans (59). Mechanistically, we find JP2 enables brown adipocytes to properly respond to NE by operating within an α1/β3-AR–Orai1/3–STIM1–IP3 receptor–SERCA2b Ca2+ handling pathway (Fig. 6L). β3-ARs have long been considered the primary β-AR subtype expressed in rodent brown adipocytes for mediating catecholamine responses (57, 60). However, there is also evidence that α1-AR signaling involving PLC-mediated IP3 release coexists with and synergizes with β3-ARs during thermogenesis (46). We confirmed brown adipocyte Ca2+ transients can be provoked by α1-AR and β3-AR agonists and their inhibition blunts NE induced transients with α1-ARs dominating. Studies performed in brown adipocyte cultures from rats indicated a similar synergism between α1- and β3-ARs whereby isoproterenol induced transients were found to be dependent on IP3R-dependent Ca2+ release and blocked by ER store depletion (45). We expand on these findings by demonstrating there is a requirement for highly localized BAPTA-sensitive Ca2+ nanodomains, SERCA activity, and SOCE in NE responses.

Junctophilins are required for stabilizing Ca2+ nanodomains that form between the plasma membrane and ER/SR (37). The canonical function of JP2 is based on its role in cardiac, skeletal, and smooth muscle myocytes where it couples plasmalemmal Ca2+ channel influx through voltage-dependent L-type Ca2+ channels with SR Ca2+ release through ryanodine receptors. Recently, the Spiegelman group reported Kcnk3-mediated voltage-dependent K+ efflux limits lipolysis and heat production in brown adipocytes by counteracting NE-inducible voltage-dependent Ca2+ influx through L- and P/Q-type Ca2+ channels (61). It is possible that JP2 also regulates voltage-dependent Ca2+ channels and compliments SOCE in brown adipocytes. Similarly, we recently demonstrated that JP2 interacts with STIM1 and regulates SOCE in cardiac fibroblasts (44). This phenomenon has been reported for the JP2 homolog JP4 in dorsal root ganglion sensory neurons and T-cells (62, 63) and may represent a common mechanism in non-excitable cells.

The observed reduction in Ca2+ transient amplitudes but increased cytosolic Ca2+ levels after JP2 knockdown and in response to NE suggests compromised ER Ca2+ reuptake, an ATP- and SERCA-dependent process. Despite finding an upregulation in SERCA2b, the accompanying reduction in oxidative phosphorylation likely limits ATP levels and its availability for SERCA activity. A role for SERCA2b in the thermogenesis of beige and white fat has been described in which a heat generating Ca2+ cycling process occurs between SERCA2b ATP hydrolysis and RyR2-dependent ER Ca2+ efflux (64, 65). Brown adipocytes are suggested to lack such a mechanism (64) and instead possess an alternative thermogenic, but UCP1 independent, futile creatine cycle (66). Our analyses indicate JP2-mediated BAT thermogenesis is likely UCP1-dependent as the cold intolerance of UCP1−/− Adipo-shJP2 mice is comparable to that of UCP1 knockout mice and brown adipocyte Ca2+ transients are unaffected by ryanodine receptor agonism and antagonism.

Prior studies in adult and human induced pluripotent stem cell derived cardiomyocytes have indicated JP2 deficiency leads to altered lipid homeostasis in addition to defects in mitochondrial structure and metabolism (38, 67–69). Here, we find adipocyte JP2 silencing similarly leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and smaller lipid droplets. Although Mfn2 has been reported to interact with JP2 (68), we were unable to detect an interaction between JP2 and Mfn2 or with other mitochondrial fission/fusion factors in adipocyte lysates. On the other hand, adipocyte identity, metabolism, and BAT-mediated thermogenesis are sensitive to PPARγ2 levels (41, 70) and we find that both HFD feeding and JP2 silencing leads to a downregulation of PPARγ2 in BAT in favor of the more ubiquitous PPARγ1 isoform. The consequences of PPARγ isoform switching in response to JP2 depletion appears to be modest as we do not see an apparent phenotypic difference in differentiated brown adipocytes with and without JP2 loss of function or a downregulation of other BAT markers including UCP1, Fabp4/aP2, Adipoq, or Ppargc1a/PGC1α.

The role for SOCE in differentiated mammalian adipocytes is also largely unknown, particularly for BAT, but may function in promoting preadipocyte differentiation while protecting against adiposity (71, 72). Here, we establish that SOCE inhibition and JP2 silencing blocks brown adipocyte NE-induced Ca2+ transients that parallels a downregulation in STIM1 expression. Mutations in STIM1 and ORAI1 cause Ca2+ release activated Ca2+ (CRAC) channelopathy that is associated with lipid droplet accumulation, decreased glycerol release, damaged mitochondria, and reduced fatty acid oxidation (73), observations consistent with BAT dysfunction from Adipo-shJP2 animals.

Since UCP1-dependent mitochondrial uncoupling is the primary source of BAT thermogenesis, it is perhaps not surprising JP2 knockdown caused significant decreases in basal and NE-stimulated OCR, mitochondrial structural defects, a reduction in total and phosphorylated HSL, and attenuated lipolysis. Uncoupling relies on the breakdown of triglycerides into free fatty acids by HSL which is phosphorylated and activated in response to NE and β3-AR signaling. We propose that the function of JP2 in regulating SOCE and intracellular Ca2+ handling regulates HSL abundance and activity. This idea is supported by an elevation in basal Ca2+ levels and increased abundance of calpain subunits and calpain activity in Adipo-shJP2 BAT in parallel with the identification of HSL and STIM1 as calpain substrates. Future studies could evaluate whether specific calpain proteolysis of these proteins contributes to BAT dysfunction, obesity, and metabolic syndrome.

In summary, we demonstrate that JP2-mediated intracellular Ca2+ handling in brown adipocytes plays a critical role in regulating BAT thermogenesis and susceptibility to diet-induced metabolic dysfunction. Our data support a working model in which a JP2-orchestrated Ca2+ signalosome operates through an Orai1/3–STIM1–IP3 R signaling axis. This pathway is essential for maintaining Ca2+ homeostasis in brown adipocytes and, in turn, for controlling BAT thermogenesis and overall energy metabolism.

Materials and Methods

Full description of the materials and methods are included in the SI Appendix. Experiments were performed in C57BL/6 J mice in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Iowa. Standard protocols were used for most assays or based on previously described methods including: isolation and culturing of adipocytes from BAT (61), in vivo metabolic analyses and temperature recordings (64, 74, 75), transthoracic echocardiograms (76), Seahorse assays (74), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with high-resolution serial block-face scanning electron microscopy (SBF-SEM) (77).
