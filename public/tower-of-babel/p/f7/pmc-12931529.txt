Impact of invasive coronary function testing on management of ANOCA patients and secondary care resource utilisation: insights from a multicentre retrospective study in the UK
Authors: Joanna Abramik, Hannah Withers, David Murphy, Jonathan CL Rodrigues, Ali Khavandi, Dylan Thompson, Ioannis Felekos, Kevin Carson, Mark Mariathas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstract:BackgroundInvasive coronary function testing (CFT) is indicated in patients with refractory angina with non-obstructed coronary arteries (ANOCA). Despite this, questions remain regarding patient selection for testing, safety and integration of CFT within clinical pathways and the impact that endotyping has on long term management of these patients. This study aims to investigate the safety of CFT in patients presenting with anginal chest pain, the prevalence of ANOCA endotypes in the tested population, and the impact of CFT on prescribed medical therapy and secondary care resource utilisation.MethodsA retrospective analysis of electronic case records of 159 consecutive patients who underwent complete invasive CFT at two UK centres between June 2022 and December 2024 was performed. All patients were tested for endothelium-independent coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) and coronary vasospasm (vasospastic angina (VSA)). 44 patients (27.6%) also underwent endothelial function assessment. The median length of follow-up was 9 months (IQR 4–16).ResultsAn ANOCA endotype was identified in 101 patients (63.5%) (CFT+ve). Of those, 24 (23.8%) were diagnosed with isolated CMD, 53 (52.4%) with isolated VSA and 24 (23.8%) with a mixed endotype. Five (3.1%) experienced intraprocedural adverse events. In 123 patients (77.3%), CFT led to a change in medical therapy. Re-hospitalisation for recurrent chest pain occurred in 21.7% of CFT+ve and 13.7% of CFT−ve patients, with the majority re-presenting within 6 months of CFT. VSA was linked to higher re-hospitalisation odds (OR 2.64 (1.10–6.33), p=0.03), and patients with VSA tended to re-present earlier than others (p=0.017). Higher antianginal therapy prescription and prior emergency presentations were also predictive of risk of re-presentation (OR 1.61, p=0.02 and OR 4.18, p=0.001, respectively).ConclusionsCFT testing had low intraprocedural risk and influenced onward management. Hospitalisation for chest pain post CFT testing was common. Further refinement of clinical pathways, including early follow-up for medical optimisation, is suggested.Keywords: Microvascular Angina, Cardiac Catheterization, Chest Pain

Introduction

Ischaemic heart disease is the leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide, particularly among patients with atherosclerotic coronary artery disease (CAD). However, more than half the patients presenting with angina have unobstructed coronary arteries.1 Traditionally, this group has been managed conservatively with empirical medical therapy, based on the assumption of a favourable prognosis.

Emerging evidence challenges this approach. Patients with coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) and/or vasospastic angina (VSA)—collectively referred to as angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA)—are now recognised to be at increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events.2 3 The CorMicA trial demonstrated that invasive coronary function testing (CFT), followed by stratified medical therapy based on ANOCA endotypes, leads to greater improvements in symptoms and quality of life compared with empirical treatment.4 These findings contributed to the recent European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Class I recommendation to perform invasive CFT in patients with persistent chest pain and non-obstructive CAD.5

Despite the growing evidence, the adoption of CFT in UK clinical practice remains limited. Logistical barriers, including restricted access to required equipment and pharmacological agents (eg, acetylcholine (ACh)), as well as limited clinical expertise, continue to hinder implementation. Concerns also persist regarding procedural safety across diverse hospital settings, integration into existing care pathways and the impact on healthcare resource utilisation.

In the West of England, only two cardiology centres currently offer complete invasive CFT to a combined population of nearly three million people. This paper outlines the experience of these centres in delivering CFT, with the aim of investigating the safety of CFT in patients presenting with anginal chest pain, the prevalence of ANOCA endotypes in the tested population and the impact of CFT on prescribed medical therapy and secondary care resource utilisation.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective electronic case notes analysis of all patients who underwent complete invasive CFT (n=159) at University Hospitals Bristol and Weston NHS Foundation Trust (UHBW) and Royal United Hospitals Bath NHS Foundation Trust (RUH) between June 2022 and December 2024 was performed by three independent assessors (JA, HW and DM). At both sites, CFT is performed solely by interventional cardiologists with specialist interest and expertise in ANOCA, and patients referred for this service would have usually had prior invasive coronary angiogram (ICA) or CT coronary angiogram confirming lack of significant obstructive epicardial CAD, unless presenting acutely with symptoms suggestive of unstable angina.

Study sites and diagnostic protocol

A total of 113 (71%) patients were identified at UHBW. UHBW is an inner-city tertiary cardiac and cardiothoracic referral centre with all-year-round primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) provision. CFT service has been in operation since June 2022 and is performed in accordance with consensus guidelines.6 Endothelium-independent CMD is tested primarily through bolus thermodilution method (with intravenous adenosine as hyperaemic agent), with seven (6.2%) cases also undergoing continuous thermodilution. Coronary vasospasm provocation is performed using incremental bolus doses of intracoronary ACh (2 mcg, 20 mcg, 100 mcg into left coronary artery).

Another 46 (29%) patients were identified at RUH. RUH is an acute, medium-sized district general hospital providing general cardiology services as well as in-hours primary PCI service. CFT service commenced in September 2022, with the majority of cases (69.5%) undergoing both bolus (with intravenous adenosine-induced hyperaemia) and continuous thermodilution for endothelium-independent coronary microvascular function, as well as coronary vasospasm provocation with intracoronary ACh in accordance with the consensus guidelines.6 In addition, since January 2023, endothelium-dependent microvascular function is tested using a novel continuous thermodilution method, using incremental doses of intracoronary ACh infusion delivered via a dedicated infusion microcatheter, with ACh flow reserve ≤1.5 indicating abnormal endothelial function.7 44 patients were tested in this manner.

In both centres, structural CMD is diagnosed if Coronary Flow Reserve (CFR) is <2.5 and Index of Microvascular Resistance (IMR) is ≥25, and functional CMD is diagnosed if CFR is <2.5 but IMR <25.6 VSA diagnosis is made according to standardised criteria.8 Clinician discretion has been applied for any grey-zone cases, for example, where there was a discrepancy between bolus and continuous thermodilution result for endothelium-independent microvascular function testing.

At both sites, an additional 92 patients underwent isolated bolus or continuous thermodilution testing for endothelium-independent coronary microvascular function, with 33 (35.8%) testing positive. As these patients did not undergo VSA provocation testing, their ANOCA endotyping is incomplete and were thus excluded from analysis. Due to a lack of validation of endothelial function testing with continuous thermodilution method, where endothelial dysfunction was the only abnormality determined on CFT testing, these patients were excluded from analysis (two patients).

Collected data

Collected data included patient baseline characteristics, including cardiovascular risk factors, antianginal therapy, number and type of pre-CFT secondary care healthcare encounters, including prior non-invasive and invasive testing and time to diagnosis from the initial presentation. ANOCA endotype classification following CFT, intraprocedural complication rates and the immediate outcome of the procedure, including changes to medical therapy and follow-up strategy, has been recorded. The patients have been followed up for a median of 9 months (IQR 4–16 months) and data regarding time to first re-hospitalisation due to myocardial infarction (MI), revascularisation or chest pain, outpatient clinic follow-up review and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) has been collected. MACE has been defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, cerebrovascular events and re-hospitalisation with either recurrent chest pain, or MI or revascularisation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software, V.29.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Categorical variables are presented as count and frequency in percentage. The continuous variables were tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean±SD, with non-normally distributed data reported as median (IQR). Variables were presented with ORs and 95% CIs. Between-group differences in continuous variables were evaluated with the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact test with a significance level of p<0.05. Cumulative event rates for re-presentation with chest pain post CFT were derived from the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test. Factors predictive of ANOCA diagnosis and re-hospitalisation risk were analysed using multivariate binary logistic regression method.

Results

ANOCA endotypes based on invasive CFT

An ANOCA endotype was identified in 101 patients (63.5%) (CFT+ve). Of those, 24 (23.8%) were diagnosed with isolated endothelium-independent CMD, 53 (52.4%) with isolated VSA and 24 (23.8%) with mixed endotype (figure 1). The mixed endotype included 17 patients with VSA and endothelium-independent CMD, four patients with endothelial dysfunction and VSA and three patients with endothelium-independent CMD, endothelial dysfunction and VSA. It is likely that the true prevalence of endothelial dysfunction in this study is greater than reported as only 27.7% of the sample underwent the test. Patients with no demonstrable ANOCA endotype on invasive CFT are termed CFT−ve.

There was no difference in the use of either CT coronary angiography (p=0.41) or functional ischaemia assessments (p=0.72) between CFT+ve and −ve groups. 28 patients (17.6%) had both CT coronary angiography and invasive coronary angiography separately to eventual invasive CFT, with no difference between the groups. 61 patients (38.4%) had both anatomical and functional ischaemia testing. Interestingly, there was no difference in prevalence of inducible ischaemia between the two groups (p=0.46). 30 patients (18.9%) underwent exercise stress testing (exercise treadmill test, exercise stress echocardiography or exercise stress myocardial perfusion imaging, all following Bruce exercise protocol), of whom a third had ECG evidence of ischaemia. There was no difference in incidence of ECG ischaemia between CFT+ve and −ve groups, and of three patients who had ECG changes in the absence of a visible perfusion defect or regional wall motion abnormality, only one was found to have an ANOCA endotype.

Factors predictive of detection of ANOCA endotype on CFT

Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression model was applied to identify factors predictive of a positive CFT in the studied cohort (online supplemental table 1). In multivariate analysis, only prior invasive coronary angiography was a factor predictive of the eventual diagnosis of an ANOCA endotype (OR 2.71 (1.34–5.50), p=0.005).

Periprocedural safety, postprocedural management and follow-up

Five adverse periprocedural events were observed, including one ad-hoc percutaneous coronary intervention due to coronary artery dissection, and four episodes of atrial arrhythmia secondary to intracoronary ACh infusion, treated either pharmacologically or with direct current cardioversion (n=2). No long-term sequelae of these events have been recorded. There was no difference in periprocedural events between the two study centres (3.7% (UHBW) vs 2.2% (RUH), p=0.65).

In 123 patients (77.3%), CFT led to a change in medical therapy, with 97 CFT+ve patients (96%) being established on targeted antianginal therapy according to ANOCA endotype, and 66.7% of patients with CMD established on a statin and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB). In the CFT−ve group, 14 patients (24.1%) had antianginal therapy discontinued. 62% of CFT−ve and 8.9% of CFT+ve patients were discharged from cardiology follow-up, with one patient in the CFT+ve and two patients in the CFT−ve arm being re-referred to cardiology services within the follow-up period (p=0.55). The median time to outpatient clinic follow-up was 7 months (IQR 4–11). Of the 60 patients who have already attended scheduled outpatient follow-up post-procedure, 21 (35%) were discharged after the first clinic appointment. Outpatient patient flow post invasive CFT is depicted in online supplemental figure 1.

There were 22 MACE events in CFT+ve patients and eight in CFT−ve patients during study follow-up (p=0.29) (table 2). Most frequently occurring MACE was re-hospitalisation for recurrent chest pain. No statistically significant difference in MACE was seen between CFT+ve and −ve groups either in the composite or individual end-points (table 2), and there was also no difference in MACE between patients testing positive for CMD or VSA (p=0.07).

Re-hospitalisation risk

Although no differences were noted between CFT +ve and −ve groups with regards to frequency of rehospitalisation, in a multivariate regression model (table 3), VSA has been found to be independently associated with an increased risk of rehospitalisation (OR 2.64 (95% CI 1.10 to 6.33), p=0.03). Patients with a history of hospitalisation for angina prior to CFT were also more likely to be re-hospitalised post CFT (OR 4.18 (95% CI 1.73 to 10.07), p=0.001). An increasing number of anti-anginal therapies was also predictive of risk of re-hospitalisation (OR 1.61 (1.08–2.41), p=0.02).

Re-hospitalisation timelines

Of the 30 patients who were rehospitalised following invasive CFT (18.9%), the majority presented early (60% by 3 months, 80% by 6 months). We have not identified any cases where chest pain symptoms leading to re-hospitalisation could be directly attributed to the index procedure. Patients with VSA were more likely to present earlier than other ANOCA endotypes, including mixed endotype (log rank 10.7, p=0.017) (figure 2).

Discussion

Invasive CFT is recommended in the ESC guidelines for investigation of patients with refractory chest pain and unobstructed coronary arteries. The provision of the service within UK practice is currently limited, but within the West of England, two centres, who are members of the British Heart Foundation - National Institute of Health Research (BHF-NIHR) Partnership CMD Workflow, have been undertaking invasive CFT for several years.

The main findings of our study suggest that CFT is a safe diagnostic method, with a low rate of periprocedural events, resulting in change in medical management. CFT+ve patients experienced higher frequency of MACEs, primarily contributed to by rehospitalisation for chest pain, although the observed differences in MACEs did not achieve statistical significance. In addition, VSA was an independent predictor of early rehospitalisation for chest pain, alongside higher antianginal therapy use and history of prior emergency admissions for unstable angina.

Over a period of 2.5 years, 251 patients were referred for invasive CFT, of whom 159 underwent a complete study, including an assessment of endothelium independent CMD and VSA. The remaining 92 were excluded from this analysis due to omission of vasospasm provocation—primarily early in the programme when ACh was unavailable or due to clinician preference or contraindications. Importantly though, occasionally, spasm provocation was not performed when a positive CMD diagnosis had been made, with the assumption that CMD was responsible for patient symptoms. As shown in the analysis of the cohort where both CMD and VSA testing was performed, mixed endotypes are common, consistent with a recent meta-analysis.9 As VSA and CMD management differs, VSA provocation should be performed as part of a complete CFT study.

Invasive endotyping for ANOCA is an evolving area, with many protocols currently in use among specialist centres, varying in the method for coronary flow reserve measurement, the sequence of tests, pharmacological agents used for induction of hyperaemia and spasm and their doses. This is of particular concern with regards to the dosing and method of delivery of ACh (bolus vs infusion), as the existing protocols result in extremely variable peak concentrations of ACh in the coronary artery, undoubtedly affecting the physiological effect exerted by the substance.10 There are also limited reports comparing ACh to ergonovine provocation testing, both of which may result in coronary spasm through different mechanisms, suggesting that complementary testing may be more appropriate.11 This variability impairs comparability of the results of various studies, as well as generalisability of the study results to the clinical environment, affecting widespread adoption of testing. The testing protocol used in our centres follows BHF-NIHR CMD Workstream clinical consensus guidelines6; however, even within the constraints of this document, some variability in practice will be present. Further international effort is required to develop a uniform testing protocol that is acceptable both to the clinicians and the patient, and ensures appropriate sensitivity and specificity to confirm or refute a diagnosis of specific ANOCA endotypes in patients presenting with chronic cardiac chest pain.

Although cardiovascular risk factors are associated with ANOCA,12 no significant differences in these were found between CFT+ve and −ve cohorts in this study. This is likely explained by population heterogeneity, as well as the retrospective data collection, where patient characteristics could not be confirmed. The only significant difference noted between the two groups was the number of hospitalisations for chest pain (with CFT−ve patients more likely to be managed solely in an elective setting), and completion of an ICA prior to invasive CFT, with the latter being the only factor predictive of a positive CFT (OR 2.71 (1.34–5.50), p=0.005) (online supplemental table 1). This suggests that refractory symptoms drove repeated investigation, often in an emergency setting. These experiences align with the existing literature,13 14 and the recent recommendation upgrade in the 2024 ESC Chronic Coronary Syndromes guidelines to class IB for invasive CFT in refractory angina in patients with ANOCA responds to the need for an earlier diagnosis, at least partly to minimise the impact of the delay and uncertainty on patients’ quality of life, functional capacity and healthcare resource utilisation.

Recently, ischaemia on exercise stress testing has been found to be highly specific for an ischaemic substrate in ANOCA.15 In our study population, there was no difference in incidence of ischaemia between CFT+ve and −ve groups, either on exercise stress testing or stress imaging with pharmacologically induced hyperaemia. Non-invasive ischaemia assessments routinely used in clinical practice currently lack sensitivity to detect all ANOCA endotypes.12 For now, invasive CFT remains the gold standard for ANOCA phenotyping and should be prioritised over repeated anatomical or functional tests with limited sensitivity for CMD and VSA. Similarly, the use of intracoronary imaging, while it may be indicated in MI with non-obstructed coronary arteries for delineation of acute plaque events or spontaneous coronary artery dissection,16 has a limited role in the diagnosis of ANOCA and is not used in routine clinical practice. However, some authors report an association between prominent vasa vasorum detected on optical frequency domain imaging, reflecting perivascular inflammation and coronary vasospasm,17 which may be an area for further investigation.

Phenotyping enables tailored antianginal therapy, as demonstrated by the CorMicA trial,4 although large randomised controlled studies showing improvement in symptoms where endotype-specific therapy has been applied are lacking.12 In our cohort, CFT altered management in 77.3% of patients, with 96% of CFT+ve patients receiving targeted treatment. Only 24.1% of CFT−ve patients had therapy withdrawn, likely reflecting comorbidities, residual diagnostic uncertainty or perceived benefit. Current guidelines offer no clear direction on discontinuing therapy in such circumstances. However, an antianginal washout period before CFT, recommended by experts in the field,18 might help assess individual treatment response.

As demonstrated previously, both CMD and VSA are associated with adverse long-term outcomes.2 19 20 In the present study, a difference in outcomes between CFT +ve and −ve patients was observed, driven by rehospitalisation for chest pain, although it did not reach statistical significance (table 2). This could be partially explained by the short follow-up period and the small sample size. The periprocedural event rate in this study was low, with one major complication of ad hoc revascularisation (0.6%) and four episodes of minor arrhythmia (2.5%), with no significant difference between study sites. These findings are reassuring, similar to those reported elsewhere21 22 and supportive of feasibility and safety of this procedure in both tertiary and secondary care centres.

Invasive CFT allowed early discharge from clinical follow-up of 62% of CFT−ve patients. Only two patients in this cohort were electively re-referred to cardiology, highlighting the benefit of reduced clinical ambiguity. On the other hand, nearly one in five patients has been rehospitalised for recurrent chest pain following CFT, finding similar to both the CorMicA and CorCTA trials.23 The majority of the patients were rehospitalised soon after invasive CFT (80% within 6 months), and most of those (81.5%) presented before their scheduled clinic follow-up. Rehospitalisation risk was the highest in patients with VSA (OR 4.18), high antianginal prescription burden (OR 1.61) or previous emergency admissions (OR 2.64) (table 3). This study shows that VSA patients were rehospitalised the earliest of all endotypes (p=0.017), which we hypothesise reflects the difficulty with symptom control, potentially exacerbated by the temporary discontinuation of vasodilating agents ahead of the CFT and the abrupt presentation of VSA, often mimicking that of MI. Importantly, symptoms of angina appear to inversely correlate with the dose of ACh triggering coronary spasm on provocation,24 although this study did not test for this association, nor for correlation with risk of recurrent chest pain. These findings highlight the need for close post-procedural follow-up with rapid medical therapy optimisation, with the potential benefit of reducing the risk of rehospitalisation. Although no cost analysis has been performed in this study, improved postprocedural care is likely to have significant implications on resource utilisation in healthcare systems.

Limitations

This study bears several limitations. First, it is a retrospective analysis, relying on interpretation of data available from electronic case records within two hospital centres, which is subject to bias. Second, the sample size is relatively small; however, it represents all patients who underwent invasive CFT within a large geographical area since the conception of the service at both centres, reflecting relatively poor uptake in CFT altogether. Third, the study found no significant differences in baseline characteristics between patients who tested positive or negative for ANOCA endotypes, which is at odds with the published literature, which may in turn affect generalisability of the findings of this study to other populations. Finally, the follow-up period is relatively short, which does not allow evaluation of long-term sequelae of ANOCA diagnosis or its impact on healthcare resource utilisation or MACEs.

Conclusion

Invasive CFT is a safe technique which impacts medical management of patients. Phenotyping of these patients not only allows personalised care with alterations in treatment strategies but also enables risk stratification. Patients with VSA, with high antianginal therapy requirements, and those who have had prior emergency admissions for unstable angina are more likely to be hospitalised post CFT. These patients may benefit from close post-procedural follow-up for symptom reassessment and medical therapy up-titration. Importantly, invasive CFT removes clinical ambiguity and allows early discharge of patients who test negative for ANOCA. Further multicentre prospective studies evaluating all ANOCA endotypes (including endothelial dysfunction), inclusive of cost effectiveness analysis, are required to evaluate the impact of invasive CFT on long term outcomes and healthcare resource utilisation in patients with ANOCA.
