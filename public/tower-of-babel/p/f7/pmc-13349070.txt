Lineage-specific type 1 fimbriae in hypervirulent ST23 Klebsiella pneumoniae target α3β1 integrin to promote intestinal epithelial invasion
Authors: Qian Zeng, Ying Zhang, Shujing Li, Xinyue Fan, Su Wang, Jiayuan Liu, Cui Tai, Meng Li, Yuanyuan Wei, Yunkun Huang, Yi Li, Lefu Lan, Xiaofei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp), particularly sequence type 23 (ST23), is a predominant global cause of pyogenic liver abscesses. While ST23 hvKp is strongly associated with this invasive pathology, the molecular basis for its enhanced gut translocation potential, a critical prerequisite for hematogenous dissemination to the liver, remains unclear. Phylogenetic analysis of type 1 fimbrial gene clusters revealed that ST23 isolates uniquely encode a distinct combination of fimbrial subunits. Using engineered strains, we demonstrated that ST23-derived type 1 fimbriae, but not those from the ST11 lineage of classical K. pneumoniae, specifically bind to host enterocyte α3β1 integrins. This specific interaction conferred superior invasion capacity across intestinal epithelial barriers to ST23 hvKp compared to strains expressing ST11 fimbriae (P < 0.05). Thus, ST23 hvKp exploits lineage-specific type 1 fimbriae to target α3β1 integrin, driving efficient gut epithelial invasion—a pivotal initial step in systemic dissemination leading to liver abscess formation. This work identifies a dedicated molecular invasion system intrinsic to ST23 hvKp, mechanistically explaining its enhanced invasive capacity and strong clinical association with pyogenic liver abscesses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05146-w.Keywords: ST23 hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae, Type 1 fimbriae, FimH-integrin interaction, Integrin-mediated invasion, Liver abscess pathogenesis

Introduction

Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) is a predominant cause of severe pyogenic liver abscesses (PLA), particularly in healthy individuals without comorbidities [1, 2]. Sequence type 23 (ST23) hvKp is the leading pathogenic subtype associated with PLA and is epidemiologically prevalent among Asian populations lacking predisposing conditions [3]. These infections carry high risks of fatal complications including sepsis and metastatic disease.

Currently, the mechanistic studies of PLA caused by ST23 hvKp are still limited by discrepancies between animal models and human pathology, and an incomplete understanding of the key host-pathogen interactions. Of particular concern is the compounded risk of severe infections and regional transmission driven by the convergence of hypervirulence and emerging antimicrobial resistance.

The pathogenesis of hvKp-associated PLA is hypothesized to initiate with gastrointestinal tract colonization, followed by translocation across the intestinal barrier and hematogenous dissemination to the liver via the portal circulation [4, 5]. While key pathogenesis mechanisms of hvKp, including capsule biosynthesis, siderophore-mediated iron acquisition, and various metabolic enzymes, have been well documented [6], the molecular mechanisms enabling ST23 hvKp to breach the intestinal epithelium remain poorly understood [7]. Crossing this barrier is a critical step preceding liver infection, yet the specific machinery by which ST23 hvKp accesses the portal circulation remains unclear.

Type 1 fimbriae, a class of chaperone-usher pathway pili ubiquitously expressed by Gram-negative pathogens [8], serve as a critical virulence determinant in hvKp [9]. The tip-localized adhesin FimH mediates initial attachment to host cells by recognizing mannosylated glycoproteins, an interaction that can subsequently trigger bacterial internalization into non-phagocytic cells [10, 11]. A well-characterized example is their mediation of bladder epithelial colonization by uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC), a key pathogenic driver of urinary tract infections [12]. Notably, naturally occurring, lineage-specific point mutations in the FimH lectin domain have been shown to fine-tune receptor binding specificity and affinity, thereby modulating tissue tropism and invasive capacity in UPEC [13, 14]. Similar to Escherichia coli, the type 1 pili of hvKp are encoded by the fim operon encompassing the structural genes from fimA to fimI with regulator gene fimK [15]. However, whether analogous lineage-specific variations exist in K. pneumoniae FimH and contribute to the enhanced invasiveness of ST23 hvKp has not been investigated.

In this study, phylogenetic analysis revealed that ST23 hvKp uniquely encodes lineage-specific type 1 fimbriae. To functionally dissect their role, we engineered isogenic strains expressing either ST23-derived or ST11 classical K. pneumoniae (cKp) type 1 fimbriae. We found that ST23 fimbriae significantly enhanced the invasion of intestinal epithelial cells compared to their ST11 counterparts. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that this phenotype is dependent on specific binding to host α3β1 integrins.

This lineage-specific fimbria-integrin engagement promotes efficient gut barrier breaching, providing a molecular basis for the enhanced systemic dissemination potential of ST23 hvKp. Our work thus identifies α3β1 integrin targeting as a critical invasion mechanism intrinsic to the ST23 lineage. Targeting this ST23-specific machinery may offer novel strategies to inhibit gut translocation and subsequent invasive infections.

Methods and materials

Phylogenetic analysis of Klebsiella pneumoniae fimbrial gene clusters

NCBI database

Klebsiella pneumoniae expresses two principal pilus types: type 1 and type 3 fimbriae [16]. We utilized the prototypic hypervirulent ST23 strain NTUH-K2044 (GenBank accession: NC_012731.1) as a reference model, with its type 1 and type 3 fimbrial operons retrieved from the NCBI Nucleotide database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). To assess fimbrial diversity, we analyzed 296 publicly available K. pneumoniae genomes through NCBI’s Nucleotide BLAST, extracting fimbrial sequences for phylogenetic reconstruction. Coding sequences were translated using ApE (v2.0.47), and phylogenetic trees were generated in MEGA (v11.0.13) with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The metadata of bacterial isolates from the NCBI database are comprehensively documented in Supplementary Table S1, including assembly accession numbers (e.g., GCF_000807395.2), geographic origin, specimen sources, sequence types (ST), and capsular serotypes (K-type).

Clinical strain

To investigate type 1 fimbrial variations between hypervirulent (hvKp) and classical (cKp) Klebsiella pneumoniae lineages, a total of 847 clinical strains were randomly collected from five tertiary hospitals across four Chinese provinces during June to July 2021. The participating institutions included Taizhou Municipal Hospital (Zhejiang Province), the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University (Guangxi Province), Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (Henan Province), Shandong Provincial Hospital (Shandong Province), and the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Guangxi Province). All strains were isolated from distinct clinical specimens obtained from individual patients, including but not limited to blood, sputum, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and liver abscess aspirates. Following collection, all isolates were stored at − 80 °C until further processing. Strain identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Daltonics GmbH, Germany), with standard reference strains included as controls throughout the study.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and capsular (K) typing were conducted using the Pasteur Institute’s BIGSDB-Kp platform (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr), with allelic profiles of seven MLST housekeeping genes (gapA, infB, mdh, pgi, phoE, rpoB, tonB) and the wzi locus determining K-types. After whole-genome sequencing, a subset of 224 strains comprising 69 ST23 strains and 155 ST11 strains was selected for phylogenetic analysis. Phylogenies were reconstructed for ST23 (hypervirulent) and ST11 (classical) lineages. The genomic metadata for ST23 and ST11 clinical isolates are comprehensively summarized in Supplementary Table S2, and the corresponding raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under BioProject accession number PRJNA1297889.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from bacterial cultures at mid-log phase using RNA Extraction Kit (Onrew, RNB481), followed by cDNA synthesis with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio) according to manufacturer protocols. Transcript levels of type 1 fimbrial genes were quantified on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using SYBR Green chemistry. The comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCT) method was applied for analysis, with rpoB serving as the endogenous control [20]. Experiments included three biological replicates per strain.

For eukaryotic cell analysis, total RNA was extracted from host cells using TRIzol Reagent, with GAPDH substituted as the reference gene. All subsequent qRT-PCR procedures (reverse transcription, amplification parameters, and data normalization) mirrored the bacterial workflow. Primer sequences are detailed in Table S4.

Bacterial growth kinetics assay

To evaluate potential metabolic fitness costs associated with plasmid-based complementation, growth kinetics were assessed for the wild-type NTUH-K2044, the ΔfimH mutant, and the plasmid-complemented strains. Briefly, single colonies of the respective strains were inoculated into LB broth (supplemented with 34 µg/mL chloramphenicol for the complemented strains to maintain plasmid selection) and grown overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. To ensure identical initial population densities, the optical density at 600 nm of each overnight culture was measured and standardized. The standardized cultures were subsequently inoculated into fresh LB broth at a 1:100 dilution ratio. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking (220 rpm). Bacterial growth was monitored by spectrophotometrically measuring the OD600 at the initial time point (0 h), at regular 1-hour intervals for the first 8 h to capture the exponential growth phase, and at a final 24-hour time point to assess the stationary phase. All growth curve assays were performed in independent biological triplicates.

Mannose-sensitive yeast agglutination (MSYA) assay

MSYA was performed as previously described [21]with modifications. Briefly, Klebsiella pneumoniae strains were cultured with shaking in Lennox Broth (LB; Sangon Biotech) at 37 °C overnight, subcultured (1:100) to mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8), and washed thrice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). Bacterial suspensions were normalized to OD600 = 1.0 in PBS. Saccharomyces cerevisiae ATCC 9763 was propagated in Yeast Extract-Peptone-Dextrose (YPD; Sangon Biotech) at 30 °C, harvested at stationary phase, and similarly resuspended to OD600 = 1.0.

For agglutination assays, 10 µL bacterial suspension and 10 µL yeast suspension were mixed on a glass slide (Sangon Biotech) at room temperature. Agglutination was assessed microscopically after 10 min using an Imaging System (Tanon) under bright-field illumination.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sample preparation and imaging

A single colony of K. pneumoniae was picked from a fresh LB agar plate, inoculated into 25 mL of LB broth, and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm) for 12 h. Subsequently, the overnight culture was diluted 1:100 (v/v) into fresh, pre-warmed LB broth and incubated under identical conditions. This subculturing process was repeated for three consecutive days to complete a total of three passages.

Resuspended K. pneumoniae cells were added to a 200-mesh copper grid and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The grid was negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 3 min and excess liquid was removed with filter paper. The grids were then observed using a JEM1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, JEM1400).

Host cell invasion assays

Invasion assays were conducted as described [22] with the following modifications. Human intestinal epithelial cell lines (Caco-2, NCM460) (QuiCell Biotechnology, Shanghai; authenticated by STR profiling and confirmed mycoplasma-negative) and ITGA3/ITGB1-knockdown derivatives were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; QuiCell Biotechnology) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

For infection, cells were seeded in 24-well plates (1 × 105/well) 24 h prior to assay. Monolayers were challenged with Klebsiella pneumoniae at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Following 4 h incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, extracellular bacteria were removed by triple PBS washing, followed by 1.5 h treatment with 1 mg/ml tigecycline (Meilunbio) to eliminate residual extracellular bacteria. Cells were lysed with 0.2% Triton X-100 (in PBS; Sangon Biotech) for 20 min at room temperature. Serial dilutions of lysates were plated on LB agar for colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration. Three biological replicates (each with technical triplicates) were performed per strain.

Molecular docking and dynamics simulations

The FimH lectin domain (ST23/ST11) and the human α3β1 integrin heterodimer were modeled using AlphaFold3 [24]. The α3β1 complex was reconstructed by rigid-body docking of ITGA3 and ITGB1 subunits via the HDOCK server (v1.1; http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn) [25]. FimH variants were docked to the α3β1 integrin using HDOCK’s hybrid protocol (rigid body docking followed by binding site refinement), sampling 100 conformations ranked by normalized docking scores. Used the Hdock algorithm for conformational sampling and scoring, and conformational sorting based on the docking scores. Selected the top 10 conformations with the highest scores, and the optimal conformation based on the binding site. The schematic diagram was drawn using PyMOL (v2.5.4) [26] and the interaction diagram was drawn using LigPlot+ (v2.2.5).

GROMACS 2022 [27] was selected as the dynamic simulation software, and Amber99sb-ildn was selected as the protein force field. The SPCE model was used to add water to the system, and a water box with a size of 20 × 20 × 20 nm3 was established (the edge of the water box was at least 1.2 nm away from the edge of the protein). Ions were added to automatically balance the system. Particle-mesh Ewald (PME) is used to handle electrostatic interactions, with the steepest descent method employed for energy minimization over a maximum of 50,000 steps. The Coulomb force cutoff distance and van der Waals radius cutoff distance are both set to 1 nm. The system is equilibrated using the normal-viscosity-transient (NVT) and isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensembles, followed by 100 ns of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations at room temperature and pressure. The cutoff value for non-bonding interactions is set to 10 Å. The V-rescale temperature coupling method is used to control the simulation temperature at 300 K, and the Berendsen method is used to control the pressure at 1 bar.

Data analysis of simulation trajectories was performed using the built-in analysis module of Gromacs 2022. The combined free energy was calculated using Gmx_MMPBSA. The combined free energy is typically negative, and a larger absolute value indicates stronger affinity between proteins.

Recombinant FimH protein production

The fimH sequence of the ST23 lineage was derived from K. pneumoniae NTUH-K2044 (locus tag: KP1_RS21355), while the ST11 fimH sequence was derived from K. pneumoniae HS11286 (gene ID: KPHS_43610). Recombinant FimH proteins were produced by Sino Biological Inc. (Beijing, China) using a prokaryotic expression system. Briefly, the respective coding sequences were synthesized and cloned into the pColdIII expression vector, followed by transformation into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3). Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at 15 °C overnight. Soluble proteins were purified via Ni-NTA affinity chromatography under native conditions. The column was washed with a buffer comprising 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 30 mM imidazole (pH 8.0), and the target proteins were eluted using a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 250 mM imidazole (pH 8.0). The purified proteins were verified by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting using an anti-His tag antibody. The theoretical molecular weight of the protein was ~ 29 kDa (excluding the C-terminal 6×His tag). The final protein purity exceeded 90%, as assessed by SDS-PAGE densitometry.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) binding kinetics

Human Integrin α3β1 protein (R&D Systems, 2840-A3) was coupled to CM5 chips (Cytiva, 29149603) through amino coupling (Cytiva, BR100050). After incubation, FimH-ST23 (FDH4-1) and FimH-ST11 (FDH4-2) was diluted using 1*HBS-EP+, and appropriate serial concentrations (at least 5 concentrations) were selected for Kinetic 1:1 Binding flow-through to the protein-CM5 system [28]. The binding was tested and analyzed using a Biacore 8 K (Cytiva) instrument.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data from all experiments are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on at least three independent biological replicates. Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0. Depending on the experimental design, specific statistical methods were applied as follows: (i) comparisons between two independent groups were evaluated using a two-tailed, unpaired Welch’s t-test; (ii) data normalized to a control group (set to 100%) were analyzed using a one-sample t-test compared against a theoretical mean of 100; (iii) multigroup comparisons involving a single independent variable were assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s post hoc tests; and (iv) time-course datasets involving repeated measurements (e.g., bacterial growth curves) were analyzed using a two-way repeated measures (RM) ANOVA with the Geisser-Greenhouse correction. Statistical significance is denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001; ns indicates not significant (P > 0.05).

Results

Phylogenetic divergence of type 1 fimbrial proteins distinguished hypervirulent ST23 Klebsiella pneumoniae

We analyzed 296 human-derived Klebsiella pneumoniae genomes from NCBI, including 76 sequence types (STs) with global representation: ST11 (21.9%), ST258 (9.4%), and ST23 (6.4%) (Fig. 1A). Clinical specimens accounted for > 80% of isolates, while the remaining 20% lacked precise origin metadata. Among 19 ST23 strains, six originated from pyogenic liver abscess patients (Table S1).

The color gradient (from yellow to red) represents the relative abundance of the isolates (from low to high). B Geographical composition of the 224 K. pneumoniae isolates collected from China. The pili of K. pneumoniae include type 1 fimbriae and type 3 fimbriae, which are encoded by different clusters of genes [16]. Using the liver abscess-derived ST23 strain NTUH-K2044 as a reference, we performed BLASTn alignment of type 1 and type 3 fimbrial genes across all strains. Phylogenetic construction of translated protein sequences was conducted using Neighbor-Joining methodology (MEGA v11, 1,000 bootstrap replicates). Strikingly, ST23 isolates formed monophyletic clusters for type 1 fimbrial subunits: FimB (18/20, 90%), FimC (19/20, 95%), FimF (95%), FimG (95%), FimH (18/20, 90%), and FimK (19/20, 95%) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure S1). The distinct sequence of the type 1 fimbrial protein in the German ST23 strain (ID 122), compared to other ST23 strains, might be associated with its geographical origin (Germany), differing from the predominant Asian origin of most isolates. Besides, the ST23 strain (ID 115) exhibited frameshift mutations in FimB and FimH, presumed sequencing errors. In contrast, type 3 fimbrial phylogeny showed no ST23-specific clustering (Supplementary Figure S2).

Clinical ST23 strains recapitulated lineage-specific fimbrial variation

To validate functional divergence between hypervirulent (ST23) and classical (ST11) lineages, we characterized type 1 fimbrial variants from 224 clinical Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates obtained across four Chinese provinces (Zhejiang, Henan, Shandong, Guangxi), with the source distribution mapped in Fig. 1B and clinical specimen types detailed in Fig. 1C (more details in Table S2). Phylogenetic analysis of ST23 (n = 69) versus ST11 (n = 155) strains revealed distinct clustering across type 1 fimbrial subunits (FimB, FimI, FimC, FimD, FimF, FimG, FimH, FimK; Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S3), and the vast majority of FimE proteins and all FimA proteins from ST23 and ST11 strains clustered on the same branch (Supplementary Figure S3). Based on the component of type 1 fimbriae (Fig. 2C), these data informed the construction of isogenic NTUH-K2044 strains expressing heterologous ST11-type fimbriae.

Genetic dissection of type 1 fimbrial biogenesis in ST23 hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae

To interrogate the functional specificity of ST23-associated type 1 fimbriae, we engineered isogenic mutants of the prototypic liver abscess strain NTUH-K2044 (ST23) through targeted deletion of key fimbrial genes (fimF, fimG, fimH, fimK, and fimFGH operon) using λ-Red recombination (Fig. 3A, primer sequences in Table S3). Mannose-sensitive yeast agglutination (MSYA) assays confirmed that ΔfimF, ΔfimG, and ΔfimH mutants—but not ΔfimK—abolished type 1 fimbrial assembly (Table 1; representative images in Figure S6), demonstrating essential roles for structural subunits (FimF/G/H) but not the regulatory FimK in pilus biogenesis.

Complementation with native ST23 fimbrial genes (pK2044fimF, fimG, fimH, fimFGH) restored MSYA activity in ΔfimF and ΔfimH backgrounds. However, neither complementation (pK2044fimG, pK2044fimFGH) nor heterologous substitution with ST11-derived genes (pHS11286fimG, pHS11286fimFGH) rescued agglutination in ΔfimG or ΔfimFGH mutants (Table 1, Figure S6), suggesting strict ST23-specific requirements for fimG-mediated subunit interactions.

Critically, while fimH restoration enabled pilus assembly (Table 1, Figure S6), its lectin domain—the primary mediator of host cell adhesion [30]—exhibited lineage-specific functional divergence. Consistent with these phylogenetic tree disparities in type 1 fimbriae proteins (Fig. 2B and Figure S3), heterologous reconstruction of functional ST11-type fimbriae in the hypervirulent NTUH-K2044 (ST23) backbone required coordinated expression of six divergent ST11 genes (fimI, fimC, fimD, fimF, fimG, fimH) in the ΔfimH genetic background (ΔfimH+pHS11286fimICDFGH). Importantly, growth curve analysis confirmed that heterologous expression of this multi-gene operon did not impose a detectable metabolic fitness cost, as all engineered strains exhibited indistinguishable growth kinetics compared to the wild-type strain (Figure S5).

Successful restoration of mannose-sensitive agglutination, as summarized in Table 1(with raw images provided in Supplementary Figure S6), confirmed the necessity of cross-lineage operon compatibility for pilus biogenesis, highlighting evolutionary constraints on fimbrial architecture between classical and hypervirulent lineages. To confirm the fidelity of genetic constructs, we analyzed transcriptional profiles of key fimbrial genes (fimI, fimC, fimD, fimF, fimG, fimH, fimK) across engineered strains by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Figure S4, primer sequences in Table S4).

Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provided visual confirmation of fimbrial architecture and successful engineering (Fig. 3B and C). The wild-type NTUH-K2044 strain displayed dense, rigid, rod-like appendages characteristic of type 1 fimbriae. In contrast, the ΔfimH mutant predominantly exhibited thinner, flexible, and wavy appendages consistent with the morphological features of type 3 fimbriae, confirming the successful ablation of type 1 fimbrial structures [16, 31]. Critically, the TEM images revealed comparable fimbrial densities between complemented (ΔfimH+pK2044fimH) and heterologously reconstituted (ΔfimH+pHS11286fimICDFGH) strains (Fig. 3D and E). Quantitative RT-PCR and immunoblotting confirmed equivalent fimA transcript and FimA protein levels (per 5 × 108/CFU) between ST23-native (ΔfimH+pK2044fimH) and ST11-reconstituted (ΔfimH+pHS11286fimICDFGH) strains (Fig. 3F and G), validating matched pilus density for functional comparisons [32, 33].

ST23-derived type 1 fimbriae drive intestinal epithelial invasion

To evaluate the functional contribution of type 1 fimbriae to host cell invasion, we compared four isogenic NTUH-K2044 variants: WT, ΔfimH, ΔfimH+pK2044fimH, and ΔfimH+pHS11286fimICDFGH. Given that bacterial colonization and invasion data are typically log-normally distributed, all colony-forming unit (CFU) counts were log10-transformed prior to analysis to stabilize variance and satisfy the assumptions of parametric testing (Fig. 4). Furthermore, because plasmid-based expression systems impose distinct physiological constraints compared to chromosomal regulation, we decoupled our analysis into two controlled comparisons: (1) WT versus ΔfimH to establish the fundamental requirement for type 1 fimbriae, and (2) ST23-complemented versus ST11-reconstituted strains to isolate lineage-specific functional differences under matched plasmid-expression conditions.

In Caco-2 cells, the WT strain invaded significantly more efficiently than the ΔfimH mutant (Fig. 4A; Welch’s t-test on log10​ values, P = 0.0377). Critically, for the lineage-specific comparison, the ST11-reconstituted strain exhibited a significant reduction in invasive capacity compared to the ST23-complemented strain (P = 0.0463; Fig. 4A). These results were strongly recapitulated in NCM460 cells. The ablation of fimH led to a significant decrease in invasion compared to the WT strain (P = 0.0327; Fig. 4B). Moreover, the ST23-type 1 fimbriae conferred a significant competitive advantage over the ST11-type 1 fimbriae, with the latter showing over 70% reduced invasion efficiency (P = 0.0248; Fig. 4B).

In summary, these data demonstrate that type 1 fimbriae are essential for K. pneumoniae to invade intestinal epithelial cells. Importantly, the lineage-specific fimbrial architecture of ST23 hvKp provides a superior capacity for epithelial breach compared to classical ST11 variants, likely contributing to its distinct gut translocation phenotype.

Integrin α3β1 mediated ST23-specific epithelial invasion via FimH recognition

To define the molecular basis of ST23-type 1 fimbriae tropism, we chose Caco-2 cells to further explore why type 1 pili assisted strains to invade enterocyte. Among the studies of type 1 fimbriae, the lectin FimH protein of Escherichia coli binds to integrins in the epithelial cells of the bladder. Considering the partial homology between Escherichia coli and K. pneumoniae in the type 1 fimbrial gene sequence, we hypothesized that FimH engages integrins—key epithelial adhesion receptors. In epithelial cells, the main integrin proteins are α3β1 and α6β1 [34], and it has also been mentioned in the literature that the integrin α2β1 in Caco-2 cells is also involved in bacterial invasion [35]. We used the AlphaFold 3 online website to predict the protein binding of these three integrins with lectin FimH-ST23 and FimH-ST11 proteins, and the prediction results are displayed in Table 2. Based on the prediction results, we concluded that all three integrins are likely to interact with type 1 pili in K. pneumoniae.

The ipTM and pTM scores represent the accuracy of the predicted subunit interface and global folding, respectively. A combined score (the combined ipTM and pTM value) > 0.75 indicates a high-confidence protein interaction [36].

To evaluate the functional role of specific integrins, we performed antibody-mediated blocking assays in Caco-2 cells (Fig. 5A, B). Neutralizing α3 integrin significantly reduced the invasion of the ST23-complemented strain (ΔfimH+pK2044fimH) to 78.5% of the untreated control. Strikingly, the ST11-reconstituted strain (ΔfimH+pHS11286fimICDFGH) was not inhibited; instead, its invasion increased 1.41-fold. Blocking α2 or α6 integrins increased invasion for both strains. Notably, blocking the common β1 subunit triggered a massive 1.89-fold increase in ST11 invasion, while ST23 invasion remained statistically unchanged. These data suggest that ST23 FimH specifically relies on α3 integrin, whereas ST11 readily utilizes compensatory alternative receptor pathways when primary integrins are blocked.

To rigorously confirm these receptor dependencies without the confounding effects of antibody steric hindrance, we generated stable ITGA3- and ITGB1-knockdown Caco-2 lines using lentiviral shRNAs. The qRT-PCR and Western blot confirmed highly specific silencing (Fig. 5C, D). In the ITGA3-KD1 line, ITGA3 mRNA was reduced by ~ 67% without affecting ITGB1. Interestingly, in the ITGB1-KD2 line (ITGB1 reduced by ~ 83%), we observed a significant compensatory upregulation of ITGA3 expression (1.76-fold relative to control).

Invasion assays in these knockdown lines perfectly mirrored the antibody blocking results (Fig. 5E, F). In ITGA3-KD1 cells, ST23-complemented strain invasion dropped to 75.0%, whereas ST11-reconstituted strain invasion increased to 118.5%. Conversely, in ITGB1-KD2 cells, invasion increased for both strains. The enhanced invasion of the ST23 strain in ITGB1-KD2 cells (122.0%) specifically aligns with the compensatory upregulation of its cognate receptor, α3 integrin, observed in this cell line. Together, these findings definitively establish α3β1 as the essential, specific receptor for ST23 FimH-mediated invasion, while highlighting the distinct, highly adaptive alternative-receptor tropism of ST11 FimH.

Structural and biophysical basis of ST23 FimH-α3β1 integrin interaction

To further explore why the interaction between ST23-type 1 pili and integrin protein α3β1 affects the number of invasive bacteria, we focused on analyzing the lectin on type 1 pili, the fimbrial tip-FimH protein, and its interaction with integrin dimer α3β1. Firstly, we compared the sequences of FimH-ST23 (NTUH-K2044) and FimH-ST11 (HS11286) proteins and found that the amino acids at position 165 were different. The position 165 of ST23-FimH is histidine (H) while position 165 of ST11-FimH is glutamine (Q) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our study identifies lineage-specific type 1 fimbriae as a critical virulence determinant enabling ST23 hvKp to breach intestinal epithelial barriers. Phylogenomic analysis revealed a conserved fimbrial operon in ST23 strains featuring the H165 residue in the adhesin FimH, which is replaced by Q165 in ST11 strains. Molecular dynamics simulations quantified that H165 stabilizes FimH-α3β1 integrin binding (ΔG = − 66.44 ± 15.49 kcal/mol), explaining ST23’s superior binding affinity (KD = 1.54 µM). This adaptation highlights ST23’s evolutionary fitness for gut invasion, while previous studies have demonstrated that lineage-specific wcaJ inactivation attenuates pathogenicity [38], further establishing the unique genetic profile of ST23 as a key determinant of high pathogenicity.

We constructed isogenic strains expressing ST23- or ST11-derived type 1 fimbriae in NTUH-K2044 (Fig. 3) and observed a functional incompatibility, extending prior observations that fimbrial deletion impairs pilus biogenesis [39, 40]. This incompatibility highlights a previously unrecognized requirement for subunit co-evolution within a lineage to maintain chaperone-usher pathway fidelity. Critically, plasmid-based complementation with pK2044fimH did not restore invasion above the level of the ΔfimH mutant (Fig. 4A–B). We attribute this to the inherent limitations of plasmid-based expression systems in K. pneumoniae. Constitutive plasmid-driven expression bypasses chromosomal phase-variation regulation and disrupts the stoichiometric balance required for optimal chaperone-usher pathway assembly, as evidenced by the dramatically reduced fimbrial density observed in both plasmid-complemented strains compared to WT (Fig. 3).

We therefore explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation of our genetic complementation approach and emphasize that the plasmid-complemented strains should not be directly benchmarked against the WT strain. Rather, our experimental design decouples the analysis into two controlled comparisons: (1) WT versus ΔfimH establishes the fundamental requirement for type 1 fimbriae in ST23 pathogenesis; (2) ST23-complemented (ΔfimH+pK2044fimH) versus ST11-reconstituted (ΔfimH+pHS11286fimICDFGH) strains serve as a matched experimental pair—both operating under identical plasmid-expression constraints, chaperone-usher assembly mechanics, and fimbrial density—allowing this comparison to isolate the lineage-specific functional divergence between ST23 and ST11 FimH variants. Under these matched conditions, the ST23 FimH variant confers significantly greater invasion capacity (Fig. 4A–B), consistent with our SPR data demonstrating higher α3β1-binding affinity (Fig. 9).

Functional validation confirms the necessity of FimH-ST23 and integrin interaction, as antibody blockade or lentiviral knockdown of α3β1 reduced invasion efficiency to 75–78% (P < 0.01). In contrast, surface plasmon resonance assays revealed no detectable binding between ST11 fimbriae and α3β1 (KD undetectable), which directly correlated with their attenuated invasive capacity. This functional divergence underscores ST23’s unique integrin-targeted invasion strategy. The abundance of α3β1 on intestinal epithelia makes it an ideal target for gut translocation [41]—a mechanism similar to uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), which exploits bladder integrins during urinary tract infections [42]. This exemplifies lineage-adapted tropism within Enterobacteriaceae, where ST23 HvKp uniquely targets intestinal α3β1 via H165-FimH.

Building upon the well-documented functional homology of FimH across Enterobacteriaceae, our identification of integrin α3β1 as a specific host receptor for Klebsiella pneumoniae type 1 fimbriae provides critical mechanistic insights into its distinct pathogenesis. Although α3β1 integrin is canonically localized to the basolateral surface of polarized intestinal epithelia, previous studies have established multiple mechanisms by which enteric pathogens can access these receptors. Transient apical exposure of basolateral integrins can occur during natural epithelial turnover at villus tips, through micro-lesions in the epithelial barrier, or via pathogen-induced disruption of tight junctions [43, 44]. Furthermore, active recruitment of integrins to the apical surface upon initial bacterial contact has been documented as a conserved mechanism for pathogen internalization, as exemplified by Yersinia and Listeria [45, 46]. Similar integrin-mediated invasion strategies have been demonstrated for Gram-positive pathogens, including Streptococcus agalactiae, which exploits fibrinogen-binding proteins to engage host integrins during epithelial and endothelial invasion [47, 48]. We propose that ST23 hvKp may exploit analogous mechanisms to access α3β1 during gut colonization, although the specific mechanism by which bacteria access basolateral integrins warrants further investigation using polarized epithelial models and live-cell imaging.

Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) is known to target integrins across multiple tissues [12, 49, 50]. However, ST23’s H165-dependent gut-specific invasion mechanism is unprecedented in this pathogen. Whether this FimH-mediated invasion strategy extends to other cell types expressing compatible receptors remains to be determined. This residue-centric adaptation exemplifies a universal principle of bacterial evolution, where targeted amino acid substitutions in adhesins dictate host tropism. As demonstrated in Salmonella [51], single-residue changes in FimH reconfigure adhesion landscapes without gene gain or loss—exemplified by position 56 substitutions that enable avian specificity through non-mannose receptors, and position 41 variants enhancing human enterocyte binding. Collectively, these parallels demonstrate that variations in critical amino acids of FimH determine host tropism—with ST23’s H165-α3β1 axis now established as a definitive model of niche-adapted invasion strategy evolution.

Although our in vitro models successfully delineated the molecular mechanism of ST23-specific intestinal invasion mediated by α3β1 integrin binding, several observations warrant further investigation. The effects of integrin blockade and knockdown on invasion were not as pronounced as those observed in Escherichia coli and viruses [52], which may be related to the concentration of anti-integrin antibodies added and the extent to which integrin α3 could be knocked down. Alternatively, these results could indicate the involvement of auxiliary adhesion factors in the invasion process, suggesting that α3β1 integrin functions as a critical but not exclusive mediator of ST23 gut translocation. Indeed, hypervirulent K. pneumoniae employs multiple redundant invasion pathways beyond type 1 fimbriae, including type 3 fimbriae (MrkD), outer membrane proteins (OmpK35/36, OmpA), and capsular polysaccharide [53–56]. In our preliminary antibody-blocking experiments with wild-type and ΔfimH strains, we observed highly variable and irreproducible results, likely reflecting compensatory invasion through these parallel pathways when α3β1 integrin is blocked. Therefore, murine studies using integrin-specific knockout models remain essential to validate the role of α3β1 in systemic dissemination in vivo.

Nevertheless, the H165-α3β1 interface represents a promising target for novel anti-infective strategies. Specifically, small molecules or monoclonal antibodies designed to disrupt this interaction could attenuate ST23 gut translocation while preserving commensal flora integrity—a critical advantage over broad-spectrum antibiotics. Furthermore, our integrated modular approach, spanning phylogenomic analysis, isogenic strain engineering, and functional validation assays, establishes a generalizable blueprint for dissecting lineage-specific virulence mechanisms in diverse bacterial pathogens.
