Special issue editorial: Menopause, andropause, and mental health
Authors: Veronika Engert, Christiane Wesarg-Menzel
Published: 2026

Nearly half of all humans will experience menopause—a universal yet profoundly underappreciated endocrine transition in women, defined by the permanent cessation of menstruation due to the loss of ovarian follicular function [1]. The resulting decline in estrogen and progesterone levels affects far more than reproductive capacity. These hormones play a central role in regulating stress response systems, immune function, metabolism, sleep, thermoregulation, and emotional well-being [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Menopause therefore represents not only a reproductive milestone, but also a critical psychoneuroendocrine transition with wide-ranging implications for mental health and daily functioning [6,7].

Symptoms often emerge during perimenopause, the transitional phase preceding the final menstrual period, and may persist for many years [1]. Epidemiological data suggest that up to 85% of women experience menopause-related symptoms of varying severity, with a substantial proportion reporting clinically relevant psychological distress, sleep disturbances, and reduced quality of life [8]. Importantly, even in the absence of overt symptoms, declining ovarian hormone levels are associated with increased risk for a range of cardiometabolic, skeletal, and neuropsychological conditions, including osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysregulation, and affective disorders [[9], [10], [11]]. From both an individual and societal perspective, the burden of menopause is considerable and growing, as middle-aged and older adults represent the fastest-expanding demographic segment worldwide.

Despite its prevalence and impact, menopause remains insufficiently addressed in medical training, mental health care, and research [12,13]. Unlike pregnancy—another major endocrine transition—menopause is rarely framed as a phase requiring proactive support. Instead, it is often culturally associated with loss: loss of fertility, femininity, vitality, and control over one's body. These meanings matter. They shape how symptoms are experienced, communicated, and treated, and they may amplify psychological vulnerability during a period already characterized by substantial biological change [14].

Given its prevalence and clinical relevance, one might therefore expect menopause to be a central focus of preventive and mental health care. Yet, paradoxically, it appears to represent one of the most striking gaps in female health care [15]. A major contributing factor is insufficient education and training during medical residency, resulting in limited knowledge, low confidence, and inconsistent practices in menopause management among primary care providers [13]. This lack of preparedness stands in sharp contrast to the duration and complexity of menopausal health concerns, which may persist for more than a decade.

An additional barrier to adequate care is the complex and often misleading history of hormone replacement therapy (HRT). When appropriately prescribed, HRT not only remains the most effective treatment for menopausal symptoms, but is increasingly recognized as conferring broader health benefits, including protective effects on cardiovascular and metabolic health [16]. Early concerns regarding severe adverse outcomes such as cancer and stroke—largely shaping clinical practice for decades—have been substantially revised or refuted in light of more recent evidence [17,18]. Nevertheless, clinical practice has been slow to reflect this shift in understanding, and hesitancy among clinicians persists [19]. As a result, many women continue to experience unnecessary symptom burden and potentially avoidable long-term health risks.

Comparable patterns of uncertainty and underrecognition can also be observed in male endocrine aging. Age-related declines in testosterone—often discussed under the term andropause or late-onset hypogonadism—have been associated with mood changes, reduced vitality, and impaired stress regulation [20,21]. Yet andropause remains inconsistently defined, irregularly assessed, and rarely embedded in mental health care frameworks [22]. Together, menopause and andropause exemplify how endocrine transitions in midlife continue to fall into a conceptual and clinical gap between aging, psychiatry, and endocrinology.

Psychoneuroendocrinology offers a uniquely integrative framework to close this gap. By linking hormonal change to stress physiology, immune processes, brain function, and mental health, the field is well positioned to advance a more coherent understanding of midlife endocrine transitions. Crucially, such an approach also highlights the need for improved education, evidence-based screening, and informed therapeutic decision-making across sexes.

The contributions to this special issue will underscore that menopause and andropause are neither niche topics nor inevitable declines to be endured without support. Rather, they are biologically meaningful life phases with substantial relevance for mental health and well-being. Addressing them adequately will require interdisciplinary research, revised clinical training, and a shift toward proactive, informed care for endocrine aging across the lifespan.
