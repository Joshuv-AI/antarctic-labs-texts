A Quality Improvement Initiative on Documentation of Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy Implementation Among Primary Prevention CRT-D Implant
Authors: Sadia Wasik, Yasbanoo Moayedi, Andrew C.T. Ha, Krishnakumar Nair, Ana Carolina Alba, Katherine V. Westcott, Stephen Chan, Barry Rubin, Heather Ross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The treatment for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction has advanced significantly in recent years. The 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure recommends 4 pillars of medication in its guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT): sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors. The implementation of GDMT at maximally tolerated doses has been proven effective in multiple trials. Rates of quadruple GDMT adoption among patients with cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D) remain suboptimal in the published literature. We explored challenges to documentation on GDMT use among patients with CRT-D and identified different approaches to overcome them. This article highlights our observations and findings.

Project Rationale

We conducted this quality improvement (QI) initiative at the Peter Munk Cardiac Centre in Toronto, Canada, between the first quarter (Q1) and fourth quarter (Q4) of 2023. The purpose was to understand the reasons why patients with HFrEF were not on documented maximally tolerated GDMT for 3 months before primary prevention CRT-D implant. The project sought to address and rectify these gaps to improve patient outcomes. Our goal was to improve GDMT documentation and implementation as much as possible, tracking progress using registry data and implementing steps as reasonable. To monitor the improvement, we used registry data points without additional data collection.

Project Design

Pre–QI intervention cohort: We conducted a retrospective review from April 2021 to December 2022, including all adults with HFrEF with either ischemic or nonischemic cardiomyopathy who received a CRT-D implant for primary prevention at initial implantation. We analyzed records where the GDMT status for heart failure medications was not mentioned in the chart. This included cases where GDMT was not at the target dose for 3 months and instances where GDMT was implemented but lacked documentation regarding its status, leading to a “not documented” designation. Our objective was to identify potential reasons for nondocumentation.

Post–QI intervention cohort (Q1-Q4 2023): We conducted a prospective review to assess progress. We evaluated GDMT documentation and compared the data between these 2 groups.

Ethical approval was waived by the Research Ethics Board at the University Health Network for participation in the EPDI registry, for which this project falls under. Under the Personal Health Information Protection Act of Ontario, we are unable make data available from this review.

Limitations

No additional data points were collected outside the EPDI registry form. The data collection form captured 4 GDMT designations: 1) maximally tolerated dose for 3 months; 2) inability to complete; 3) not documented; and 4) not attempted.

The specific reasons of not having optimum GDMT were not collected. Also, the database did not collect data on drug dosing, so we were unable to assess whether a given patient was receiving the target dose or maximal tolerated at suboptimal dose.

Project Description

The review of the registry data in the preintervention group appeared as inconsistent guideline adherence. Initially, improper charting and data entry errors were suspected as the main issue. However, the review process subsequently identified several potential causes for the lag in GDMT documentation.

The causes identified were lack of consulting provider awareness regarding the implications of inadequate documentation, workflow issues, and the absence of a structured GDMT protocol across various disciplines Additionally, some providers were focused on device-related issues and showed reluctance toward optimizing GDMT, viewing it as outside their scope of practice and a possible delay to CRT-D benefit. Several studies also support this school of thought.2

For patients from remote locations, waiting for GDMT titration was sometimes seen as a barrier to accessing CRT-D benefits. Other common issues included patient adherence, lack of drug insurance coverage, excessive out-of-pocket expenses, having other comorbidities, frailty, limited family support, and logistical convenience—for instance, when a patient was scheduled for a generator change or lead revision for a pacemaker/implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, where upgrading to CRT-D had potential benefits for heart failure management. Furthermore, some patients had contraindications for certain medications but lacked proper documentation of these contraindications. Intervention: Changes Made to Improve GDMT Implementation

The following changes were made after retrospective chart review:1.Data reconciliation meetings: Quarterly data reconciliation meetings were arranged to address gaps in GDMT documentation. These meetings also facilitated effective communication and ensured the clinical lead was informed of outstanding scenarios.2.Enhanced training of coders: Educational meetings were arranged with electrophysiologists and heart function specialists to understand each team's workflows, the context behind limited documentation, and the consideration process for CRT-D implant to broaden coders' understanding.3.Having the statement on GDMT status: The providers were encouraged to incorporate a statement regarding optimization status, whether in up-titration, optimized in maximum, or suboptimal doses. In the electronic medical record (EMR), we created smart phrases and statements to use in clinic notes more efficiently.4.Identify gaps to find solutions: We met with physicians in the heart function clinic to review charts to understand if there was a way to track optimization status when any statement regarding optimization was missing. It was concluded that without a statement or comment, it was not always obvious, even to another provider.5.Increase in communication and awareness: We encouraged a workflow to ensure the trial of GDMT medications before accepting referred patients.

In the preintervention group, 53% of patients had no documented GDMT status. In many of these cases, patients were on a beta-blocker and a renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitor, but the provider's comments on GDMT status were missing. According to NCDR definitions, those records lacked optimal GDMT documentation. Also, patients without all 4 pillars of GDMT (mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, beta-blockers and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors) were considered as lacking optimal documented status unless there was a contraindication mentioned in the note.

Discussion

We identified a gap in GDMT documentation in patients undergoing CRT-D implant. This documentation gap also encompassed a gap in implementation to some extent. Although a single approach cannot effectively resolve this matter for everyone, the strategies we have outlined offer a reliable framework for us to improve the documentation.

The strategies that contributed to this outcome include increased awareness among providers across different teams within our institution, having reconciliation meetings, a slight shift in workflow by prioritizing the GDMT trial from the community cardiologist before implant, the addition of smart phrases and statements in our EMR, better training of data coordinators, and improved charting.

Taking the data into account, we found 52% improvement in documentation in 3 months, with another 3 months of gray period when the situation was being assessed and strategies were being implemented. An important question is, How much of the GDMT documentation change is attributable to improved use of GDMT versus solely being due to improved documentation? Additionally, can we sustain this improvement and expect a cultural shift? Have we truly accomplished improvement, or was this merely a reflection of a concentrated effort in a target group? If it is the latter, how can we ensure that all patients benefit from the optimal GDMT? This includes heart failure patients receiving CRT-D who are under the care of electrophysiologists, as well as patients managed by heart function specialists, general internal medicine practitioners, and community cardiologists.

Future Directions

Although we have limited influence on patient-related barriers, we can do much more at the provider or infrastructure level. By addressing these, we can ensure more patients benefit from optimal medication.

Some proposed solutions include:1.Increasing awareness and facilitating ongoing discussions on this topic.2.Reducing provider hesitancy (eg, fear of complications) in managing patients with multiple comorbidities and side effects by implementing easy-to-use, structured protocols.33.Introducing user-friendly tools, such as EMR prompts, to provide guidance in navigating complex clinical scenarios and side effects.4.Engaging leaders who can set organizational priorities.5.Adopting an institutional preference for rapid sequencing (∼6 weeks) of GDMT rather than a longer up-titration approach.6.Involving all members, including pharmacists, nurse practitioners, and physician assistants, as applicable.

Conclusions

Evaluation of data and outcome metrics is essential for improvement in any area. Through this QI process, we have observed a measurable increase in documentation of GDMT, as well as documentation of reasons when GDMT was not employed. Ongoing efforts are essential to ensure optimal dose implementation and documentation for all patients after heart failure.
