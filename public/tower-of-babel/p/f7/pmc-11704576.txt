Medical students’ perceptions of mentorship in cardiothoracic surgery: A Thoracic Surgery Medical Student Association Nationwide Survey
Authors: Carlos E. Diaz-Castrillon, Cynthia Rangel, John A. Treffalls, Kristian Punu, Naima Alver, Lena Trager, Ravi K. Ghanta, Victor O. Morell, Ibrahim Sultan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe study objective was to explore medical students' perceptions of mentorship in cardiothoracic surgery in the United States.MethodsA voluntary, anonymous electronic survey was distributed to medical students through the Thoracic Surgery Medical Student Association. The survey included 28 questions across 4 domains: demographic information, current mentorship status, goals and qualities sought in mentorship, and self-reported barriers to mentorship.ResultsThere were 60 respondents to the survey. A total of 60% identified themselves as male and 35% as White. A total of 75% of students reported having reached out to potential mentors without receiving a response. Lack of access to a cardiothoracic surgery program/mentorship program (37%) or cardiothoracic surgery interest group at one's institution (42%) was the most frequent barrier to mentorship. Overall, 32% reported not having any mentors in cardiothoracic surgery. Students without mentors were more likely to report the lack of cardiothoracic surgery interest groups (53% vs 29%, P = .04). Female students were more likely to prefer a female mentor (48% vs 3%; P < .001). Female and non-White students reported the lack of a relatable mentor more frequently than counterparts. Empathy and commitment/attentiveness emerged as highly valued, with 54% (n = 32) rating them as “most important.”ConclusionsThere is a critical need to optimize the outreach of current mentorship programs for medical students, especially for those without access to cardiothoracic surgery departments. National organizations such as the Thoracic Surgery Medical Student Association can advocate for increased mentorship accessibility and the development of structured mentorship programs. Additionally, efforts should be made to attract more surgeons to serve as mentors and emphasize proactive engagement from students.

In the realm of cardiothoracic (CT) surgery, workforce projections spanning the past 2 decades have unveiled a challenging landscape.1 Changing demographics and shifts in clinical practice dynamics have pointed to a potential shortage of practitioners.2, 3, 4 This pressing concern has spurred the implementation of mechanisms aimed at expanding the pool of CT surgeons. These measures encompass modifications in training curricula and augmented exposure for medical students through initiatives such as surgical association scholarship programs and CT surgery interest groups.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Such collective endeavors epitomize the community's determination to safeguard a sustainable and proficient CT surgery workforce.

Amidst these endeavors, the role of mentorship stands out as a key factor in nurturing and guiding the next generation of CT surgeons. Mentorship holds a significant place in the development of medical professionals, particularly within surgical disciplines.10 In the context of CT surgery, mentorship plays a crucial role in not only imparting clinical and technical skills but also shaping the professional identities and aspirations of budding surgeons.11,12 Effective mentorship can bridge the gap between didactic learning and practical application, instilling confidence and fostering a sense of belonging.13 However, despite its acknowledged significance, an in-depth assessment of medical students' access to CT surgery mentorship remains elusive, with limited incorporation of their perceptions concerning past experiences, barriers to mentor access, and diversity considerations.

To gain deeper insights into the perceptions and barriers surrounding mentorship in CT surgery, the use of surveys emerges as a valuable tool. Surveys provide a structured approach to gathering firsthand accounts from medical students aspiring to embark on a career in CT surgery. These surveys offer a platform for students to articulate their preferences, challenges, and aspirations in the realm of mentorship. By leveraging survey data, this study aims to shed light on medical students' perceptions of mentorship in CT surgery, improving our understanding of the mentorship landscape within CT surgery and paving the way for targeted interventions that will ensure the attraction of the future generation of CT surgeons.

Material and Methods

In this cross-sectional study, a nonincentivized, anonymous survey was distributed directly via email to the 300 active medical students, members of the Thoracic Surgery Medical Student Association (TSMA). Reminders were sent through coordinators of CT surgery interest groups as well as through posts by the official TSMA Twitter account during December 2022. Only a single response to the questionnaire was permitted for each participant. This study was deemed exempt by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY22090172, October 12, 2022).

Questionnaire

A 27-question survey was designed to explore medical students’ perceptions and preferences on mentorship in CT surgery. Initially, the survey was structured to address 3 objectives: to assess mentorship status, to explore the objectives sought from mentorship as well as the preferred qualities in mentors, and to identify the perceived barriers to accessing mentors. We included 3 questions: “Have you ever reached out to potential mentors and not received a response?”; “Have you ever been told to reconsider a career in cardiothoracic surgery by any mentor or person of authority?”; and “Have you ever been directly exposed to a difficult/challenging interpersonal interaction with a CT-Surgeon?” to explore the experiences and challenges students face in their pursuit of mentorship. These questions were designed to uncover the prevalence of unresponsive mentorship, discouragement from pursuing CT surgery, and exposure to difficult interactions, which can impact students' career decisions and overall perception of the field. The questionnaire was crafted to include both multiple-selection and Likert-scale questions, ensuring a robust and nuanced understanding of the participants' perspectives. To refine the survey, 2 focus group discussions comprising medical students, residents, and faculty took place. This iterative process allowed for participant feedback to enhance the content and construct validity of the survey. The survey was hosted by the free platform Google Docs and was available from July 22, 2022, to August 6, 2022. The original survey document is included in the Appendix E1.

Statistical Analysis

Survey results were imported and analyzed using Stata 17 (StataCorp LP). Data are described as proportions (%) for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile range for continuous variables. We used the nonparametric Pearson chi-square test, Fisher exact test, and the Kruskal–Wallis test whenever appropriate. Pairwise post hoc comparison was conducted using a Wilcoxon rank test with a Bonferroni correction.

A total of 41.4% (n = 17) of mentors specialize in adult cardiac surgery, 31.7% (n = 13) in adult thoracic surgery, and 12.1% (n = 5) in congenital cardiothoracic surgery. The mentor's level of training reveals that 22% (n = 13) are chiefs of service or chairmen of their departments, 18% (n = 11) are junior faculty or assistant professors, and 20% (n = 12) are senior faculty or associate professors (Table 2).

Barriers to Mentorship

Medical students' perceptions about barriers to mentorship varied across respondents. Overall, mentor time constraints and the lack of access to a CT surgeon/CT surgery mentorship program were perceived as “most frequent/very frequent” barriers by 53% (n = 31) and 45% (n = 27) of students, respectively. Female students were more likely to report disappointing previous experiences with mentors (52.1%, n = 12) compared with male students (19.4%, n = 7), with these experiences being frequent, very frequent, or most frequent (P = .02). Likewise, 35% (n = 8) of female students reported the lack of a relatable mentor as a very or most frequent barrier compared with 18% (n = 6) of male students (P = .04). Moreover, 41% (n = 15) of non-White students ranked communication problems and not having a mentor whom they can relate to as a most or very frequent barrier in mentorship (P < .01) (Tables E4 and E5).

Discussion

CT surgery faces challenges posed by a workforce shortage and the necessity of nurturing the next generation of CT surgeons (Figure 5). Central to these challenges is the role of mentorship. Delving into medical students' perceptions regarding mentorship in this specialized field and understanding the potential obstacles they face are crucial for the sustainable development of the profession. Our study focuses on medical students who are members of the TSMA, a highly selective group with an inherent interest in CT surgery. This group's insights are particularly valuable because they represent motivated students who are actively pursuing a career in this field.Figure 5Graphical Abstract. In this cohort, 32% of the surveyed medical students have not yet established a mentorship relationship. Although it can be argued that most of these students (62%) were still MS1 and MS2, some difficulties in accessing mentorship seem to prevail. For example, although the majority of TSMA students met their mentors through their academic institutions, a considerable number reported a lack of access to CT surgeons or CT surgery mentorship at their institution as a common barrier. More important, 75% of the responders stated they had reached out to potential mentors without receiving a response, which corresponds to medical students identifying mentors’ time constraints as a frequent barrier.

Furthermore, the perception of mentorship barriers varied according to sex and race. Female and non-White students reported they perceived the lack of a relatable mentor, bad previous experiences, and lack of fluid communication as a frequent barrier more often than their male and White counterparts. Nevertheless, surveyed TSMA students showed high intrinsic motivation, as most of them stated that their interest in CT surgery has not been affected by these scenarios.

The implications of these findings stress the need for optimizing mentorship initiatives. The apparent limitation for some TSMA students in accessing CT surgeons or the low number of students reporting national meetings as the primary source for establishing mentor-mentee relationships signals a potential area for growth. This is where the outreach of established mentorship programs from professional organizations becomes crucial. Existing programs such as the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Looking to the Future Scholarship Program, the Women in Thoracic Surgery mentorship program, the American Association for Thoracic Surgery Member for a Day, Medical Student Diversity Scholarship, or Summer Intern Scholarship, as well as other notable mentorship awards championed by various cardiothoracic surgical societies such as the Eastern Cardiothoracic Surgical Society, Western Thoracic Surgical Association and The Southern Thoracic Surgical Association, among others, are excellent opportunities to bridge this gap.

Moreover, given the high representation of medical students who identify as Latino or Black within the surveyed population, organizations such as the Latino Surgical Society, Society of Black Academic Surgeons, Association of Black Cardiothoracic Surgeons, and Latinos in CT Surgery could play a pivotal role in further promoting mentorship through the TSMA and ensuring that these students have access to experienced mentors who can guide and support their career aspirations in CT surgery.

Our findings resonate with established literature about the transformative impact of mentorship in broadening professional affiliations and fostering career advancement,14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 consistent across different training levels. For example, CT surgery residents reported similar sought objectives such as career counseling (97%) and networking (93%).13 Furthermore, the factors influencing the success of a mentor-mentee relationship seem to be consistent. As expected, mentor qualities such as empathy and commitment are recognized as important by students. These qualities are instrumental in shaping the mentee's perception of the mentor as a role model, which has been shown to further amplify the mentor's impact.20 Additionally, clear communication and personal connection play a critical role in collaborative goal-setting,21 where receiving honest feedback from their mentors is highly valued by students and essential for professional identity.16,22

Despite the efforts made by academic institutions and societies, the primary challenge remains the absence of structured mentorship programs. A detailed investigation into mentorship within US surgical departments revealed that only half have formalized mentorship initiatives. Of note, most programs are unstructured and informal, and exhibit minimal engagement from key stakeholders.23 This, combined with the apparent limited access to CT surgery programs,24 emphasizes the pressing need for more structured mentorship frameworks within the field of CT surgery. It further accentuates the importance for organizations like TSMA for students without access to CT surgery departments. Of note, students without access to CT surgery programs or interest groups might be disproportionately represented in organizations like TSMA, which are perceived as valuable avenues for students to forge ties with CT surgery mentors.

To further support mentorship, we recommend the implementation of structured mentorship training for medical students starting in their MS1 years, facilitated through CT surgery interest groups and TSMA. This initiative would involve promoting a robust network of CT surgery interest groups to foster collaboration and share best practices. With the support of TSMA advisory board members, a multi-institutional initiative could be established, featuring a series of workshops designed to train students on how to effectively seek and use mentorship. These workshops would cover key topics such as effective communication, setting expectations, and utilizing feedback. Additionally, the initiative would include the development and distribution of career development plan templates for students and mentors to follow. These templates would help students set clear goals, track their progress, and improve communication between mentors and mentees, ensuring a structured and supportive mentorship experience. Finally, there is also a critical need to implement incentives at academic societies or institutions to attract more diverse CT surgeons to mentorship roles.

Study Limitations

This study, although offering valuable insights into mentorship perceptions and barriers in CT surgery, is not without limitations. First and foremost, the study is not representative of the broader population of medical students because the survey was distributed mainly to medical students affiliated with TSMA. Additionally, the survey's distribution via Twitter or CT surgery interest groups might further skew our participant pool to those more engaged in the online community or through those interest groups. Furthermore, our sampling method might not ensure a comprehensive representation of the entire population of medical students interested in CT surgery across various institutions, regions, and socio-demographic backgrounds. This potential lack of representativeness might impact the generalizability of the conclusions drawn from our results. Future studies should consider using stratified sampling techniques to gain a more diverse and representative sample. Finally, the low response rate of 20% further limits the generalizability of our findings, because it may not accurately reflect the broader experiences and perceptions of all medical students interested in CT surgery.

Conclusions

Mentorship in CT surgery is crucial given workforce challenges and the role of mentors in shaping future surgeons' careers. In this cross-sectional study, 32% of interested medical students still lack mentorship despite their presence in academic environments. This gap suggests we need to optimize the reach of existing programs, especially for students without access to CT surgery departments. Additionally, the diversity among our respondents suggests a significant opportunity to enhance inclusivity through targeted mentorship initiatives. To address these issues, academic societies should focus on extending mentorship to all medical students, leveraging organizations like TSMA and groups such as the Latino Surgical Society and Society of Black Academic Surgeons. Implementing structured mentorship training and fostering collaboration among CT surgery interest groups can provide a supportive environment for students. Future research should aim for more representative sampling and higher response rates to ensure broader applicability of findings and to develop innovative mentorship models that cater to diverse student needs.
