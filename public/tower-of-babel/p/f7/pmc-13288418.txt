Identification of Auditory Brainstem Paddle Migration Arising from Fat Atrophy Utilizing MRI
Authors: Justin Cottrell, Michael Bartellas, Sean McMenomey, John G. Golfinos, J. Thomas Roland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective To demonstrate magnetic resonance imaging’s (MRI’s) utility in investigating auditory brainstem implant (ABI) performance degradation. Design Case study. Setting Academic medical center. Participant This report presents a patient with neurofibromatosis type 2 and an ABI with durable, limited open-set speech who developed nonauditory side effects and a complete lack of auditory perception. ABI paddle placement was assessed with 1.5 Tesla (T) Siemens MRI, and device function was assessed utilizing integrity tests. Main Outcome Measure The etiology of ABI performance degradation utilizing 1.5 T Siemens MRI. Results On initial postoperative 1.5 T Siemens MRI, the ABI paddle approximated the cochlear nucleus. Approximately 1 year later, the patient experienced ABI performance degradation and dizziness. Although ABI device integrity testing was normal, follow-up 1.5 T Siemens MRI revealed lateral ABI paddle migration from the foramen of Luschka toward the flocculus, likely due to observed fat atrophy from the prior craniotomy reconstruction, suggesting a possible etiology for the observed ABI degradation. Conclusion This report presents MRI’s ability to successfully delineate postoperative fat atrophy, ABI paddle migration in relation to the brainstem, and residual tumor in a patient with ABI degradation.

Introduction

An auditory brainstem implant (ABI) applies current directly to the cochlear nucleus to facilitate auditory perception. 1 Patient performance with an ABI is variable, with a minority of patients achieving open-set speech perception. Although ABI performance outcomes lag behind cochlear implant technology, it still plays an important role in the management of sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) in patients lacking a viable cochlea nerve or implantable cochlea. 2

The intracranial space is dynamic in patients with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2), where multifocal tumors, and subsequent treatment, can result in progressive or abrupt changes to brainstem position. ABI performance over time in this patient population, and the implication of the evolving intracranial environment, is not well understood. In patient’s experiencing ABI performance degradation, integrity testing and imaging are useful investigations. 3 4

This case report presents a patient with NF2 who underwent an ABI with good result that subsequently developed nonauditory side effects (NASE) and cessation of auditory perception a year following surgery. A 1.5 Tesla (T) Siemens magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan demonstrated a lateralized right ABI stimulating paddle compared with the initial postoperative scan, possibly occurring from fatty atrophy at the craniotomy reconstruction, potentially explaining the patient’s NASE and loss of auditory perception. This case highlights MRI’s ability to analyze ABI paddle placement, and the potential to enhance clinical practice in evaluating ABI performance degradation over time.

Clinical Capsule

A 24-year-old patient with NF2 and bilateral SNHL underwent a left translabyrinthine tumor resection and ABI placement. The left ABI facilitated the patient’s ability to lip-read and improved environmental sound awareness. She had stable vestibular schwannoma tumor volumes on Avastin therapy for 2 years before additional growth was noted bilaterally. She underwent a right transcochlear approach for vestibular schwannoma removal and ABI placement with autologous fat graft. Immediate postoperative recovery and ABI programming went well, and the patient achieved open-set speech understanding with no NASE.

Approximately 1 year after the right ABI placement, the patient experienced progressive ABI performance degradation and increasing debilitating dizziness with right ABI device usage. Although ABI integrity testing was unremarkable, when evaluating immediate postoperative and 1-year postoperative T2-weighted CISS and T1-weighted gadolinium sequences on a 1.5 T Siemens MRI scanner, fat atrophy from the prior craniotomy reconstruction, slight cerebellar expansion, and right ABI paddle lateral migration from the foramen of Luschka toward the right flocculus were appreciated ( Fig. 1 ). Despite ABI program adjustments, the patient was unable to achieve right-sided auditory perception and had persistent NASE that resolved after discontinuation of the right ABI. She continues to use her left ABI to facilitate lip-reading and environmental sound awareness.

Discussion

The ABI stimulating paddle should ideally be placed to approximate the brainstem cochlear nucleus. 3 Potential complications of ABI include NASE, which is thought to arise from current spreading to additional brainstem regions. 4 Although NASE is common in the acute postoperative period, it tends to decrease and resolve within weeks of surgery, and can be further addressed with stimulation program adjustments. 4 This case describes a patient with an ABI that achieved stable open-set speech and no NASE for a year following surgery. The patient subsequently developed worsening auditory performance and dizziness with right-sided ABI device use. The ABI wire from the receiver stimulator to the stimulating paddle runs through the autologous fat graft utilized to reconstruct the transcochlear surgical defect. In the absence of other identifiable causes, it appears the lateral migration of the ABI paddle away from the cochlear nucleus toward the flocculus may have occurred due to traction forces resulting from postoperative fat atrophy. The cerebellar flocculus acts as a neural integrator, and current applied to the structure by the ABI paddle would explain the dizziness experienced by the patient. 5 This phenomenon of long-term stimulation paddle migration and performance change from fat atrophy has not been previously reported to the best of our knowledge.

The tumor burden in patients with NF2 is progressive, resulting in a dynamic intracranial environment for implanted devices. 6 Characterization of tumor growth and management strategies for long-term ABI performance are not well described in the literature. The ability to objectively assess ABI paddle placement to compare across individuals, and longitudinally, would be a useful adjunct in this space. Previous work to analyze ABI paddle position has utilized CT scans, measuring ABI paddle angulation in relation to the basion, with weak correlation to subsequent performance. 1 7 MRI may prove to be a more informative imaging modality for evaluating paddle placement, as it is superior to CT in resolving brainstem position and anatomic distortions resulting from coinciding tumors. The utility of MRI in this case to explain the clinical changes of the patient acts as a proof of concept for the development of a future objective MRI measurement system of ABI paddle position. Advancements in MRI-compatible ABI devices would further streamline such efforts. Acting in parallel with research to improve ABI paddle design and function can serve to improve ABI patient performance moving forward. 2
