Mechanisms of change in a community-based alcohol prevention framework among adolescents
Authors: Ina M Koning, Samuel Tomczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of the current study was to investigate the effects of a co-created, multi-component and community-based intervention on adolescent perceptions of social norms relating to alcohol, parental rules about alcohol, and the formal (outside home) and informal (inside home) perceived accessibility of alcohol. A quasi-experimental, longitudinal design was employed with one experimental municipality (52.6%) and one control municipality (47.4%). A sample of N = 2165 adolescents (Mage = 14.2, SD = 1.32, 51% girls) filled out two annual online questionnaires. Results demonstrated that adolescents in the intervention municipality displayed significantly lower levels of positive general norms towards alcohol than adolescents in the control municipality (β=-0.09, p<.001). Moreover, adolescents in the intervention municipality indicated perceiving alcohol as less accessible both within their own home (informal; β=-0.10, p<.001) and in formal settings (café, bar, etc.; (β=-0.11, p<.001). Additionally, they reported experiencing stricter rules from their parents about alcohol (β = 0.09, p<.001). This study supports a whole-community approach to alcohol prevention by reducing parental and community factors that influence adolescent alcohol use.Subject terms: Psychology and behaviour, Psychology

Introduction

During adolescence, most people have their first sip of alcohol. Early-onset alcohol use has been found to be an important risk factor for several health and social problems, such as academic problems, use of other substances, mental health problems and the obstruction of brain development1–4. The life-time prevalence of alcohol use among youth in the Netherlands aged between 12 and 16 years old has decreased from 71.9% in 1999 to 47.5% in 20215. This decline is most likely due to the raised awareness of the negative consequences of alcohol use, especially for adolescents’ development6. However, this decline occurred mainly until 2015, after which the decrease has come to a standstill. Furthermore, among drinkers, youth binge drinking (i.e. drink five glasses or more on the same occasion) is still high and increased from 64.1% in 2003 to 74.7% in 20215. Thus, it seems that new interventions are necessary to further decrease alcohol use among adolescents.

The current study evaluates a community-based intervention called LEF (Dutch translation of ‘courage’), designed to prevent adolescent alcohol use. A previous evaluation showed that the co-creation work for LEF (combined with publicity) contributed to changes in the targeted mechanisms and outcomes7. Specifically, involving stakeholders (e.g., policymakers, young people, schools) in the development of the intervention, while also disseminating media reports of the need for the intervention was associated with favorable outcomes, prior to any actual intervention being implemented. Adolescents (aged 15 and older) in the intervention condition perceived lower accessibility of alcohol in a formal setting and reported less positive rules and norms about adolescent alcohol use, compared to the control condition. However, among younger adolescents (< 15 years of age) in the intervention condition, more weekly drinking was reported compared to their peers in the control condition. This points to potential unintended effects of the LEF community-based intervention on youth drinking in younger age groups. This makes it imperative to investigate the effects of the intervention on the targeted mechanisms after the implementation of the intervention. The current report aimed to evaluate components of the LEF intervention to explore their impact on factors targeted by the intervention. The following factors within three domains (cf. socio-ecological model8;) were targeted by the intervention, i.e. (a) adolescents (e.g. social norms relating to alcohol), (b) parents (e.g. rules about alcohol), (c) community (underage alcohol sales compliance, e.g. lower accessibility of alcohol).

Positive alcohol attitudes can be a powerful predictor for greater alcohol consumption9,10 and earlier age of onset11–13. Attitudes reflect associations learned through direct and indirect experiences14. A longitudinal study on adolescents between 11 and 17 years old showed that positive attitudes towards alcohol were the strongest predictor of alcohol use compared to personality characteristics, drinking of peers and age10. A systematic review on alcohol prevention programs showed that adolescents’ attitudes towards alcohol can effectively be targeted by interventions15, making them a key target for intervention. Attitudes are determined by a person’s belief about the appeal and consequences of certain behavior (e.g. alcohol use) and they are shaped by their upbringing, socialization and their value system.

In addition to individual attitudes, social perceptions also influence behaviors. More specifically, social norms reflect a person’s perception of other people’s approval and performance of a behavior and their willingness to comply with those persons16. Thus, social norms are social factors that inform and guide the implementation of actions17. According to the social norms theory regarding alcohol use, people are inclined to misperceive the drinking behavior and attitudes of others18,19. That is, people tend to overestimate the (acceptance of) drinking behavior of others. This misperception of how others think and act influences their own drinking behavior19. There are two types of social norms, namely injunctive norms, which entail norms related to behavior which people approve or disapprove, and descriptive norms, which entails the perception of how other people behave20. Both injunctive and descriptive norms are predictive of adolescents’ alcohol use14,21,Pederson et al., 2017), with stronger relations for descriptive norms and adolescent drinking22. Similar to attitudes, norms are also malleable factors, which means that these are effective targets for the prevention of alcohol use7,10,23. Yet, injunctive norms are less amenable in comparison to descriptive norms24. The pre-intervention effects of the LEF intervention suggested that the typical increase in alcohol use and positive alcohol norms were already altered by the involvement of the public and the visibility of the intervention through publicity and local media7. Thus, it is expected that the implementation of intervention components will be able to even further change adolescents’ attitudes and norms regarding alcohol use, and consequently, adolescents’ drinking.

In addition to adolescents’ beliefs (i.e., perceived norms), parental rules about alcohol are also highly relevant in the prevention of adolescents’ drinking. There is an increased interest in the role of parents regarding adolescent alcohol use, as they are the most important socializing agents in the life of young adolescents25. Parental rules about alcohol refer to the extent parents approve of alcohol in several social settings (e.g. at a party, on Saturday evenings). Previous research has demonstrated the relevance of parents setting strict rules about drinking for the prevention of (regular) early drinking12,26–29. Even over time, with adolescents becoming older, those with parents who remained strict, were less likely to rapidly increase high levels of drinking30. Moreover, several studies showed that interventions targeting parents restrictive rule setting about alcohol was an effective method to decrease alcohol use of the adolescent30–32. In the pre-implementation phase of the LEF intervention, the decline in the strictness of parental rules was less steep in the experimental condition than in the control condition among adolescents aged 15 years or older, indicating additional pre-intervention effects7. Thus, it is expected that the implementation of the intervention components will be able to address the level of strictness of parental rules about alcohol.

Finally, next to parental and adolescents’ behavior and norms, the intervention targets communities. Communities play a pivotal role in influencing adolescent alcohol use via the availability of alcohol in formal settings (e.g., cafés). In a systematic review, Bryden et al33. revealed consistent evidence for the association between alcohol availability within the community and the level of alcohol use33–35. That is, higher outlet density in a local community was associated with an increase in alcohol use, particularly among adolescents. In fact, availability of alcohol in the community is one of the most important factors at a policy level to curb adolescents’ drinking36. A systematic review suggested that restricting the availability of alcohol would be an effective method to decrease alcohol consumption37. Thus, it is expected that the implementation of the LEF intervention, in which parents are informed about the importance of strict rules about alcohol and the parents and community stakeholders are encouraged to reduce the availability of alcohol inside and outside of the home, will be able to decrease the drinking behaviors of adolescents.

The current study

In sum, the co-created multi-component and community-based LEF intervention aims to reduce adolescents’ alcohol use by targeting three domains: (a) adolescents (norms about alcohol), (b) parents (rules about alcohol), (c) community (underage alcohol sales compliance; lower availability of alcohol). Since the report on the pre-intervention effects7, the community intervention component was implemented targeting the reduction of access to alcohol for adolescents. This study aims to test the extent to which the LEF intervention, compared to a control group, will lower adolescents’ perceptions of norms favourable to alcohol, increase parental rules about alcohol, and decrease the availability of alcohol inside and outside the home. More specifically, the intervention component ‘underage alcohol sales compliance’ was implemented through measures to reduce selling alcohol to minors in alcohol outlets. It is expected that this intervention strategy is expected to change the perceived accessibility outside home (formal accessibility).

Method

Design and procedure

The LEF program is a community-based intervention in the municipality of Edam-Volendam in The Netherlands that aims to delay the onset of alcohol use and reduce weekly drinking among youth (see7 for a more detailed description of the study). A quasi-experimental design is used including an experimental and a control municipality. One public secondary school within each municipality was willing to participate. The risk of spill-over effects between municipalities is highly unlikely as the control school is situated more than 43 km away. Though the experimental municipality is twice the size of the control municipality (36,000 vs. 18,600 inhabitants respectively in 2022), in many other ways, the municipalities are comparable in terms of the percentage using alcohol at of 12 to 18 years (9% vs. 8%), 18 + years (29% vs. 32%) and 18 + years old excessive alcohol users (7% in both municipalities).

Data were collected by trained research assistants in classrooms using online questionnaires available on a secured website. Parents received a letter of consent, which informed them about the participation of the school in the program, and they were given the opportunity to refuse participation of their child (1.13% refusal). The first data were collected in May/June 2018 (TN) and utilized for a needs assessment in which an explanatory model was designed (see7. A formal baseline assessment was conducted in November/December 2018 (T0) before any actual intervention was implemented, and data were again gathered in February/March 2019 (T1) when implementation of the intervention had started. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Faculty of Behavioral & Social Sciences at Utrecht University (FETC18-060).

Participants

All classes in all secondary schools except for the exam classes (classes at the end of their study) participated in the study. A total of 2893 students were asked to participate in the study. Of these, 524 students did not participate due to their parents’ refusal or their absence from school on the day of data collection (individual or whole class due to scheduling problems). In addition, students of a second school in the experimental municipality were only allowed to fill out the questionnaire outside school hours in their own time; 63 students out of 286 participated. Before the start of the formal baseline assessment, an additional 2 students dropped out. This resulted in a total sample of N = 2165 (Mage = 14.2, SD = 1.32, 51% girls), with 30% following lower secondary education. Somewhat more students were in the experimental condition (52.6%) than in the control condition (47.4%).

Attrition analyses showed that the participants who did not complete both questionnaires (at T0 and T1) were somewhat older (F(1,2163) = 835, p = < 0.001), enrolled in a lower level of education ((F(1,2163) = 52.93, p = < 0.001), had a higher perceived formal accessibility of alcohol ((F(1,2128) = 161.71, p = < 0.001), reported less strict rules about alcohol ((F(1,2146) = 378.40, p = < 0.001) and more positive norms about drinking ((F(1,2128) = 161.02, p = < 0.001) compared to the adolescents who completed both questionnaires.

Intervention

In 2018 the community-based LEF intervention was developed by applying co-creation in three steps of the developmental phase; needs assessment, explanatory model (Fig. 1), intervention strategies7. In a previous paper, we have demonstrated pre-intervention effects of the developmental phase (co-creation and media attention) on determinants of behavior (i.e. norms and rules about alcohol and perceived accessibility at home). In January 2019, the first intervention component ‘underage alcohol sales compliance’ was implemented by increased measures to reduce selling alcohol to minors in alcohol outlets. Three specific measures were implemented: (1) use of a narcotics dog to test for drug use, (2) training of outlet personnel in responsible alcohol serving, (3) underage alcohol sales test purchasing. Stakeholders representing these alcohol outlets were informed about the increase controls in a kick-off meeting before implementation of these intervention strategies. This intervention strategy is expected to change the perceived accessibility outside home (formal accessibility).

Measures

Outcome measures

The main outcome measures were norms about youth drinking (general, injunctive, and descriptive norms), rules about alcohol, and perceived accessibility of alcohol (home and outside the home).

General norms about alcohol use reflect the acceptability of adolescents consuming alcohol in various situations (for example at home, or at a party with friends). Participants were asked to what degree they thought it is acceptable for a person of the same age to drink alcohol in various situations on a 5-point scale (1 = ‘not acceptable at all’ to 5 = ‘very acceptable’). The instrument is based on a Dutch translation of the ‘Alcohol Use Norms Scale’38, which was used in earlier research in the Netherlands13. Originally it contained seven items, in this study we used five items. A mean score was calculated, where a higher score indicated more positive general norms about youth drinking. Cronbach’s alphas were 0.93 (T0) and 0.94 (T1).

Injunctive norms about alcohol use reflect the extent to which adolescents perceive their peers as approving or disapproving of various frequencies of alcohol consumption (for example every weekend, every day, or so much you go knock out). Participants were asked to indicate if their friends would approve or disapprove to drink alcohol at various frequencies on a 7-point scale (1= ‘strong disapproval’ to 7 = ‘strong approval’). The instrument is based on a Dutch translation of items previously used in research on social drinking39. In this study we used three items. A mean score was calculated, where a higher score indicated more positive injunctive norms. Cronbach’s alphas were 0.75 (T0) and 0.78 (T1).

Descriptive norms about alcohol use assess adolescent perceptions of the prevalence of alcohol use among their peers. Participants were asked to indicate how many peers drink alcohol at least once a week on a 5-point scale (1 = ‘no one’ to 5 = ‘all of them’). The instrument is based on a Dutch translation of items previously used in research on descriptive norms about substance use (Elek et al., 2006). A higher score indicated higher descriptive norms.

Rules about alcohol reflect the degree of parental rule-setting regarding alcohol use as experienced by the adolescent28. Participants were asked to what extent their parents would approve of their use of alcohol in various situations on a 5-point scale (1 = ‘never’ to 5 = ‘always’). Originally, it contained ten items, in this study we used five items (for example at home with parents present, and at a party with friends). All items were recoded and a mean score was calculated, where a higher score indicated stricter parental rules about alcohol use. Cronbach’s alphas were 0.93 (T0) and 0.94 (T1).

Perceived accessibility of alcohol reflect the perceived ease of getting alcohol in various situations. Participants rated various situations on a 5-point scale (1 = ‘very easy’ to 5 = ‘impossible’). Perceived accessibility was categorized into informal and formal. Informal perceived accessibility of alcohol was measured by calculating a mean score for three different home situations: at home from their parents, at a friend’s home, and at a home of relatives. Formal perceived accessibility was measured by calculating a mean score for five different situations outside the home: a liquor store, sports canteen, pub or café, and a club or community center. For formal perceived accessibility, Cronbach’s alphas were 0.86 (T0) and 0.88 (T1), and for informal perceived accessibility 0.78 (T0) and 0.82 (T1).

Demographics were measured using items that asked participants for their age in years, gender (boy or girl), and level of education (lower secondary education, general and pre-university secondary education).

Analytic strategy

Preliminary and missing data analyses were performed in SPSS IBM (version 28.0.0, IBM Corp., 2020). These preliminary analyses were employed for the total group and for each measurement occasion. Little’s Missing Completely at Random Test was significant, with χ2(81) = 211.08, p = < 0.001. After evaluating the patterns of missing data using the IBM SPSS Missing Values module, it was determined that the missing data most likely resembled missing at random. Accordingly, missing data were handled using Full Information Maximum Likelihood. To examine the effect of the intervention at T0 on the mechanisms at T1, a regression analysis was performed in MPlus (version 8.8, Muthén & Muthén, 2017), using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors and chi-square to non-normality. The dichotomous intervention variable (0 = control, 1 = experimental) was included as predictor and all direct (perceived accessibility of alcohol; formal and at home) and indirect (norms about alcohol: general, injunctive and descriptive; rules about alcohol) target mechanisms as outcomes. In addition, we controlled for age, gender, educational level, and all outcome variables at T0. Effect sizes of the relevant (standardized) regression coefficients were evaluated according to the benchmarks by Nieminen et al.40 with values of 0.20 or lower considered as small, values between 0.20 and 0.50 considered as medium, and values of 0.50 or higher considered as large.

Results

Tables 1 and 2 present the descriptive statistics and correlations for all study variables. Adolescents’ age was significantly related to all determinants of behavior. For example, higher age related significantly with more positive general and injunctive norms at T0 (r = .56, p < .001; r = .48, p < .001 respectively) and T1 (r = .53, p < .001; r = .42, p < .001). Higher age was also significantly related to higher perceived accessibility, less positive descriptive norms and less strict rules about alcohol. In line with the (indirect) relations depicted in Fig. 1, higher perceived formal and informal accessibility to alcohol were significantly related with less strict rules about alcohol at T0 (r = -.31, p < .001; r = -.57, p < .001 respectively) and T1 (r = -.22, p < .001; r = -.46, p < .001). In addition, more positive general and injunctive norms related significantly with a higher perceived formal and informal accessibility of alcohol.

Furthermore, the intervention condition significantly predicted both lower perceived accessibility of alcohol formally (outside home b = -0.27, p = < 0.001, β = -0.11) and informally (inside home b = -0.25, p = .027, β = -0.10), as well as stricter rules regarding alcohol (b = 0.22 p = < 0.001, β = 0.09). Discussion

The present study provides initial evidence regarding the effectiveness of the first component of the community-based LEF intervention, namely underage alcohol sales compliance, which aimed to enhance control over alcohol sales to minors in alcohol outlets. The results demonstrate a significant impact of the intervention on various mechanisms across three domains, including: (a) adolescents (norms about alcohol), (b) parents (rules about alcohol), and (c) community (lower availability of alcohol). Specifically, adolescents in the intervention condition displayed significantly lower levels of positive general norms towards alcohol than adolescents in the control condition. Moreover, adolescents in the intervention condition indicated perceiving alcohol as less accessible both within their own households and in external settings. Additionally, they reported experiencing stricter rules from their parents about alcohol.

This study builds on the results reported in a previous study that showed that the LEF-intervention7 already changed e.g. norms and rules about alcohol due to co-creation and publicity, and without any actual intervention component being implemented. The current findings point at the sustained effect of this environmental community-based intervention after the implementation of a component targeting underage alcohol sales compliance, at one-year follow-up. That is, adolescents in the LEF-intervention reported fewer positive norms and more rules about alcohol at follow-up compared to adolescents in the control group. Although part of this change in norms and rules may have already been initiated before (see7, in the current study there is an impact of the LEF intervention on norms and rules about alcohol in the whole group and not only among the 15 + year-olds as in the previous wave. This implies that the implementation of the underage alcohol sales compliance component and/or the co-creation process further strengthened the change in norms and rules in the LEF intervention.

Since the component that targets underage alcohol sales compliance equally affects all adolescents (e.g., training of outlet personnel to reduce the availability of alcohol), it is expected that it has similar effects across all age groups. The findings are consistent, with perceived accessibility being lower in the intervention group. This is in line with our expectation that this intervention strategy would lower the perceived accessibility outside home (formal accessibility; see also Fig. 1). The potential visibility of the compliance of the underage alcohol sales component may have contributed to the change in adolescents’ general norms about alcohol. Moreover, it seems that lower perceived ease of access to alcohol in the formal setting due to the intervention, may also relate to the decrease in perceived accessibility at home as well as the rules parents set about alcohol. Supported by social norms theories, it is suggested that parents’ rule-setting does not occur in isolation but is shaped by the broader regulatory environment. When parents perceive their surroundings, including underage sales, to be supportive of preventing underage drinking, they may feel more empowered and confident in setting stricter household rules. Strong external norms, such as the visible enforcement of underage sales laws, can reinforce parental boundary-setting by signalling that efforts to limit youth access to alcohol are shared and supported at the community level.

The intervention did not affect injunctive or descriptive norms. This is not surprising, since these norms were not directly addressed by the intervention component. In the next step, the impact of these mechanisms on actual alcohol use patterns should be examined, since lower perceived availability and lower positive norms are associated with reduced alcohol consumption32–35. In addition, further components of the intervention will be tailored to individual norms and address specific contexts of alcohol use (i.e., different formal and informal settings), which should lead to changes in setting-specific norms. This will also provide the opportunity to examine interactions between environmental and behavioral prevention, since community-based and parental rules and individual trainings will be tested in tandem.

Strengths and limitations

This study reports findings from a co-created community-based intervention (LEF) targeting predictors of adolescent alcohol use. The implementation of the underage alcohol sales compliance component of the intervention allowed us to examine changes in parental rules and community availability of alcohol with promising results. However, the component did not address individual norms and was not tailored to specific risk factors or patterns of alcohol use among Dutch youth. Therefore, the effects are small, and it is not clear how sustainable they are. It seems that effects of the pre-intervention phase persist during the intervention, but additional follow-up assessments are necessary to inspect the longevity of effects. Also, the study’s non-randomised selection of communities constitutes a limitation that may weaken causal imputation from the findings. Moreover, the sample is restricted to two Dutch municipalities which limits the generalizability of the findings. At the same time, it increases ecological validity because of the whole-community approach of the intervention. The study relied on self-reports of adolescents, which are affected by self-report bias. Additionally, other data sources (e.g., parents, store clerks) might provide a more complete picture of pre- and post-intervention levels of community availability or parental rules regarding alcohol. Alcohol use was also not analyzed as an outcome in this study, since it targeted proposed mechanisms of use and only examined two timepoints before and after the intervention. To examine mediation models of these mechanisms, additional timepoints are required to assess alcohol use. Nevertheless, the study used pretested measures with good psychometric properties and showed statistically significant changes in relevant outcomes in the expected directions.

Conclusion

This study shows that the first component of the community-based LEF intervention, namely targeting underage alcohol sales compliance, contributes to an increase in parental strictness regarding adolescent alcohol use and reduced availability of alcohol from the adolescents’ perspective. The findings are in line with previous theoretical and empirical work and support a whole-community approach to alcohol prevention. While the intervention did not change specific alcohol-related norms, it had an impact on other factors on a parental and community level. Overall, this is promising for complex, community-based prevention that addresses determinants of health and risk behavior (such as alcohol use) on multiple levels.
