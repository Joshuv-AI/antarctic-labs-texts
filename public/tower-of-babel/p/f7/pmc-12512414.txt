A rare case report: calcifying odontogenic cyst mimicking lateral periodontal cyst with 1-year Follow-up
Authors: Shimei Wang, Yutian Zhang, Yihang Zhong, Jiezhong Guan, Chuanjiang Zhao, Panpan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLateral periodontal cyst (LPC) and calcifying odontogenic cyst (COC) represent diagnostically challenging odontogenic lesions due to overlapping clinical and radiographic features. However, COC is exceptionally rare in periodontal tissues with only one case reported in the literature.Case presentationIn this case, a 35-year-old male presented with an asymptomatic gingival swelling between teeth #22 and #23. Initial clinical examination revealed a 1.5 cm x 1 cm firm, translucent swelling with deep periodontal probing depths (PD) (14–15 mm). Radiographic evaluation demonstrated a well-circumscribed radiolucency between vital tooth roots, initially suggestive of LPC. However, histopathological analysis identified characteristic ghost cells and dystrophic calcifications, confirming a final diagnosis of COC. The lesion was managed with conservative enucleation and guided tissue regeneration, omitting peripheral ostectomy. At one year follow-up, complete osseous regeneration was observed with no evidence of recurrence. PD normalized to ≤ 3 mm, demonstrating successful periodontal restoration.ConclusionsThis case highlights three critical clinical insights: the necessity of histopathological confirmation for definitive diagnosis of radiographically ambiguous odontogenic cysts; the potential for conservative management in select COC cases without aggressive features; the importance of Cone-Beam Computed Tomography in evaluating cortical integrity and lesion extent. These findings contribute to evolving understanding of COC management paradigms, suggesting tailored surgical approaches may be appropriate based on lesion characteristics and radiographic findings. Background

Odontogenic cysts represent the most prevalent pathological entities in the maxillofacial region, broadly categorized into developmental and inflammatory types based on their histogenesis, and categorized into periapical cysts, dentigerous cysts, and periodontal cysts based on common anatomical variants. Periodontal cysts are among the least common odontogenic cysts [1–3]. Among these, the lateral periodontal cyst (LPC) is the most frequently observed cysts within the periodontal tissues. LPC accounted for 0.4% of all odontogenic cysts collected in the adult population over a 30-year period [1].

Diagnosing periodontal cysts can be particularly challenging due to the overlapping clinical and radiographic features they share with more common odontogenic cysts. Differential diagnose of periodontal cysts usually include lateral radicular periodontal cysts, periodontal abscess, deep or complex periodontal pockets, radiolucent odontogenic tumors and benign mesenchymal tumors. Accurate diagnosis is crucial, as it directly influences treatment decisions and long-term prognosis.

LPC typically present as asymptomatic, well-circumscribed radiolucency between vital premolar roots [4]. They are generally considered to be either of a developmental odontogenic origin or of an infection origin [5] and always arise directly from the lateral periodontal ligament of an erupted tooth. Histologically, 17% of the cases of LPC presented with keratinizing epithelial lining [6]. However, these features may misleadingly resemble calcifying odontogenic cyst (COC) – a rare lesion accounting for only 0.9% of jaw cyst [1]. COC exhibit distinct histopathological hallmarks including: bilayered epithelial lining with palisaded basal cells; stellate reticulum-like intermediate layers; and ghost cell keratinization with dystrophic calcifications [7].

In the present case, a cyst located within the periodontal tissues was initially misdiagnosed as LPC based on clinical and radiographic features. However, histopathological examination ultimately revealed the lesion to be a COC. The lesion was successfully treated with periodontal therapy, demonstrating favorable outcomes at the one-year follow-up. COC in periodontal tissues remain exceptionally rare, with only one prior report since 1990 [8].

Case presentation

Clinical history

A 35-year-old male patient presented with a chief complaint of progressive gingival swelling in the upper left anterior region, which had been gradually increasing in size over the past year. The swelling was asymptomatic, with no reported spontaneous pain, sensitivity to hot or cold stimuli, or pain during mastication. The patient reported no symptoms from the affected teeth. His past medical history was noncontributory, including absence of systemic illnesses, drug allergies, or familial occurrence of analogous disorders. The patient reported smoking three cigarettes per day but had no other significant lifestyle factors. There was no documented history of any therapeutic measures being undertaken for this pathology prior to presentation.

Clinical examination

On extraoral examination, the patient’s face appeared symmetrical with no visible abnormalities. Intraoral examination revealed a 1.5 cm x 1 cm swelling on the buccal aspect of the gums between teeth #22 and #23(Fig. 1a-c). The swelling was firm, non-fluctuant, translucent, and painless on palpation. There were no signs of a sinus tract or drainage. Clinical examination showed no carious lesions or abnormal tooth mobility, and both teeth responded normally to the pulp vitality test, indicating healthy pulpal status. Gingival inflammation was observed around the affected area. Notably, a 14 mm and 15 mm deep pockets were detected on the mesial-buccal aspect of tooth #23 and distal-buccal of tooth #22(Fig. 1e). During probing, a clear fluid was observed to exude from the pocket, suggesting the presence of a cyst.

Radiographic examination revealed no periapical radiolucency in the roots of teeth #22 and #23(Fig. 1f). A Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) scan identified a low-density radiolucent area between the roots of these teeth, with partial loss of the buccal cortical bone. However, no evidence of root resorption or significant bone destruction was observed (Fig. 1g-j). Based on clinical and radiographic findings, a provisional diagnosis of LPC was made, pending histopathological confirmation. Periodontitis (Stage II, Generalized, Grade C) was classified using the 2018 World Workshop Classification System.

A treatment plan was devised, including supragingival and subgingival scaling and root planing (SRP), followed by periodontal surgery to excise the cyst and perform guided tissue regeneration (GTR). The possibility of postoperative cyst recurrence was discussed with the patient during informed consent.

After the initial SRP, the cyst significantly reduced in size (Fig. 1d), and the patient was satisfied with the results. As a result, the patient declined further surgical treatment at that time. However, six months later, the patient returned for a follow-up visit. At this time, the cyst had markedly enlarged. CBCT imaging revealed significant expansion and separation of the buccal cortical plate (Fig. 1g and i). The patient was counseled that the transient cyst reduction after SRP likely resulted from decreased inflammatory exudation secondary to biofilm removal, rather than true cyst resolution. His treatment delay had resulted in significant clinical deterioration. Further postponement of cyst enucleation risked irreversible complications including progressive cystic expansion causing unresectable bone defects, severe root resorption, and ultimately unavoidable tooth loss. After discussing the situation with the patient, a decision was made to proceed with surgical excision. A surgical excision was planned, and periodontal maintenance therapy was provided prior to the procedure.

Under local anesthesia, an intrasulcular incision with vertical releasing incisions was made (Fig. 2a). Full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap elevation revealed the cystic lesion occupying the alveolar bone directly subjacent to the reflected soft tissue (Fig. 2b, c). The cyst was enucleated in toto via blunt dissection (Fig. 2d). The specimen presented as a unilocular cystic structure containing turbid, cholesterol crystal-laden fluid with a semiflexible but non-friable wall. Notably, the buccal flap exhibited unexpected rigidity and thickening. Further exploration identified a distinct submucosal layer (Fig. 2e). Meticulous blunt dissection separated this layer from the mucosal epithelium (Fig. 2f), exposing the severely resorbed buccal cortical plate. The plate was complete encapsulation by dense fibrous connective tissue. Upon dissection, the buccal plate exhibited complete loss of cortical rigidity, presenting as a pliable, paper-thin fragment. No root resorption, root divergence, irregular bone destruction or marginal epithelial remnants was observed. Following thorough debridement and saline irrigation, the osseous defect was grafted with deproteinized bovine bone mineral (Bio-Oss®, Geistlich) (Fig. 2f). A collagen barrier membrane (Bio-Gide®, Geistlich) was passively adapted to cover the graft (Fig. 2g). Primary closure was achieved using 4 − 0 polyglycolic acid braided sutures (Vicryl®, Ethicon) (Fig. 2h). The excised tissue was immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathological analysis. The postoperative regimen comprised twice-daily oral rinsing with 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate for antimicrobial control, supplemented by ibuprofen 400 mg as needed for analgesic management.

Microscopic examination of the tissue revealed a cystic structure lined by non-keratinized squamous epithelium, with scattered ghost cells and areas of calcification, characteristic of a COC (Fig. 2i, j). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of COC.

The patient’s recovery was uneventful without any adverse and unanticipated events. Complete epithelialization of the surgical site was observed within two weeks. The patient received periodontal supportive care (SPC) every three months. One year later, no recurrence of the lesion and no periodontal pockets were observed during routine clinical and radiographic follow-up, with satisfactory healing (Fig. 3a-j).

Discussion and conclusions

Periodontal cysts constitute a clinically significant yet uncommon category of odontogenic cysts, representing approximately 0.5-2% of all oral cystic lesions [1–3]. Their rarity, coupled with the often-asymptomatic nature of these lesions [9], contributes to frequent misdiagnosis, particularly when clinical and radiographic features mimic more prevalent odontogenic pathologies. Current evidence suggests these cysts predominantly arise from epithelial remnants within the periodontal ligament or periapical tissues [10], with a distinct predilection for the anterior mandible as demonstrated in multiple case series [11–14].

In contrast, COC, although rare, exhibit distinct histological and clinical features that distinguish them from other odontogenic cysts (Table 1). COC are characterized by the presence of ghost cells within the cystic lining and often exhibit dystrophic calcifications [15–17]. These distinguishing features are crucial for differentiating COC from periodontal cysts, which typically show a conventional squamous epithelial lining without calcifications.

The difference between these cysts is particularly important in their developmental origins: LPC develop from epithelial remnants in the periodontal ligament [18–20], while COC originate from odontogenic epithelium, making them potentially associated with other odontogenic tumors like odontomas or ameloblastomas [21, 22].

Epidemiologically, LPC tend to be diagnosed with a peak age of presentation in the fifth decade [20]. In comparison, most COC individuals were in the second decade of life with a predilection for males (1.63:1) [23]. Both the intraosseous and extraosseous forms occur with about equal frequency in the maxilla and mandible, mainly in the incisor and canine areas [24]. Clinically, LPC are often asymptomatic and typically discovered incidentally during routine radiographic examinations. However, COC may cause localized swelling or discomfort when they grow significantly [25–27].

Radiographically, while both COC and LPC can appear as unilocular lesions, COC typically show mixed radiolucent-radiopaque features [17, 24] due to intracystic calcifications, while LPC are usually radiolucent without calcified structures [2, 28, 29]. Additionally, COC may also demonstrate root resorption, root divergence, or cortical expansion [16]. Radiographic examination may reveal COC associated with the impacted teeth [24, 30]. In contrast, LPC usually present as well-defined radiolucencies between the roots of vital teeth, without significant bone destruction or involvement of adjacent teeth.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges that clinicians face in distinguishing between these two lesions. Despite the availability of advanced diagnostic tools, such as CBCT and histopathological analysis, the overlapping clinical and radiographic features between COC and LPC necessitate careful interpretation. While the initial misdiagnosis was not uncommon, it underscores the importance of considering all diagnostic modalities, especially in cases where radiographic and clinical findings are ambiguous.

The differential diagnosis between LPC and COC is crucial due to the significant differences in their biological behavior and treatment strategies. LPC are generally considered benign and indolent, with a low recurrence rate following surgical enucleation [31]. COC is categorized as either a cyst or neoplasm (solid). COC is typically benign with a good prognosis. But, they can exhibit more aggressive behavior [15], particularly in cases of solid neoplastic COC (dentinogenic ghost cell tumor), recurrence is more prone to occur. When COC is present with other odontogenic tumors like odontomas or ameloblastomas [21, 22], the treatment is based on the associated tumor [24]. Misdiagnosis can lead to inadequate treatment strategies, which may affect long-term outcomes. For LPC the treatment typically involves conservative measures such as enucleation and curettage, which help preserve surrounding dental structures [32]. In the case of COC, as the recurrence rate is reported to be 5% [33], it is recommended to remove a 1–2 mm layer of bone around the lesion [24] to ensure complete removal of epithelial remnants and minimize recurrence risk [17].

In this case, despite the diagnostic discrepancy, careful CBCT evaluation and surgical exploration showed no evidence of root resorption, cortical perforation or no mixed radiolucent-radiopaque features. Besides, intraoperative gross examination revealed a fluid-filled cystic structure with smooth lining and absence of marginal epithelial remnants, provisionally confirming a benign cyst rather than a solid neoplasm based on characteristic macroscopic features. Consequently, we opted for conservative management involving complete enucleation and GTR, without performing peripheral ostectomy. At the 12-month follow-up, the patient exhibited excellent bone regeneration with no signs of recurrence and maintained normal periodontal health. This outcome suggests that selected COC cases without aggressive features may be effectively managed without extensive bone removal, though extended postoperative surveillance remains imperative to assess long-term outcomes. Future studies should investigate indications for frozen section analysis during surgery and optimal surgical margins for non-aggressive COC variants to establish evidence-based guidelines.

The potential for confusion between LPC and COC has been rare documented in the literature. Only one study described by Yeong-Lei Huang et. in 1990 have reported a case where COC were initially misdiagnosed as LPC due to their similar radiographic appearance [8]. However, compared to the similar case report from 1990, this case highlights key differences in the presentation and management of COC. While the 1990 case involved a mandibular lesion, our case presents a maxillary anterior cyst, emphasizing that the diagnosis of COC should not be based solely on its common location. Contemporary management, as utilized in this case, has progressed from simple excision (typical in 1990s-era approaches) to GTR, reflecting both technical advancements and heightened emphasis on functional-aesthetic rehabilitation. Lastly, both cases demonstrate that radiographic findings, a well-defined, round radiolucency between the roots of vital teeth, can be atypical, leading to the initial misdiagnosis. Hence, the two cases underscore the need for comprehensive diagnostic approach, especially histopathological examination, to establish a definitive diagnosis of COC.

This case report describes a diagnostically challenging COC initially misdiagnosed as a LPC in a 35-year-old male. This case provides three key contributions: (1) it represents an exceptionally rare periodontal-located COC, expanding differential diagnostic considerations; (2) demonstrates CBCT’s critical role in evaluating bony architecture for treatment planning; and (3) validates conservative enucleation without peripheral ostectomy in none-aggressive COC, with 12-month follow-up confirming complete healing. These findings challenge conventional radical resection approaches for non-aggressive COC, advocating for tissue-preserving strategies guided by advanced imaging such as CBCT with improved resolution and intraoperative frozen section analysis. However, our recommendation for conservative management applies exclusively to non-aggressive COC subtypes meeting strict criteria: radiographic confinement (intact boundaries on CBCT); absence of high-risk features (root resorption/mural proliferation); histopathological confirmation of simple cystic variant. In addition, this conservative approach is strictly constrained by single-case evidence, longer follow-up and larger cohort studies are needed to validate these criteria. This case also underscores the imperative of multidisciplinary diagnostic integration (clinical-radiographic-histopathological) for ambiguous odontogenic lesions, particularly emphasizing ghost cells as pathognomonic features. Our results support paradigm shifts toward minimally invasive COC management in selected cases, warranting further research to refine treatment protocols.
