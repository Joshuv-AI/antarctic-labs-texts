Disease outbreak preparedness: a baseline assessment of infection prevention and control practice in healthcare facilities in Lagos, Nigeria
Authors: Olorunfemi Olonire, Abiola Idowu, Kikelomo Wright, Olajide Sobande
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence-based infection prevention and control (IPC) measures in healthcare facilities are critical for preventing and containing outbreaks. However, studies have shown inadequate implementation of IPC practices among healthcare facilities in developing countries. To improve practice among healthcare facilities in Lagos, Nigeria, we conducted a baseline assessment of IPC practices in selected healthcare facilities, aimed at identifying strengths and gaps and giving recommendations to promote standard practices.MethodsWe conducted an analytical cross-sectional survey using a multistage random sampling technique to select 415 hospitals/primary healthcare centres, with 214 being private and 201 public, between July and October 2023. A structured questionnaire adapted from the WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) for acute healthcare facilities was used for data collection. Data were analysed using Stata BE 17 statistical software; p≤0.05 at 95% CI was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe total median IPCAF scores for all the facilities was 207.5 (IQR 132.5–415.0). IPC core components 7 and 8 were observed with the highest median scores of 70.0 (IQR 45.0–95.0) and 67.5 (IQR 47.5–82.5), while core components 1, 4 and 5 scored the least. For the levels of IPC promotion and practice, a total of 48.8% (202) of the surveyed facilities had an inadequate level of IPC practices, 25.5% (106) had a basic level, while the remaining 25.7% (107) had an intermediate level. More of the public 57.7% (116) facilities had an inadequate level of practice compared with 40.2% (86) of private facilities (p value ≤0.05).ConclusionThe study found that IPC practices across surveyed Lagos facilities were inadequate or basic, with none attaining advanced IPCAF standards. Consistent with findings from low-income countries, the results highlight the need for strengthened IPC programmes to improve compliance and sustained quality improvement in Lagos, Nigeria.

Introduction

Globally, millions of people acquire preventable infections in healthcare settings each year, with some leading to widespread outbreaks or epidemics.13 The emergence and re-emergence of infectious diseases pose significant risks to global health, security and socioeconomic stability.46 Over the past two decades, the world has witnessed the increasing frequency and impact of outbreaks such as severe acute respiratory syndrome in Asia, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus in the Middle East, Ebola virus disease (EVD) in West Africa (2014), and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 in China.710 These outbreaks illustrate that it is poor implementation of infection prevention and control (IPC) measures, and not the diseases themselves that heightens the risk of transmission within healthcare facilities and nearby communities. Evidence shows that weak IPC systems and low adherence to standard precautions increase healthcare-associated spread.11 Therefore, implementing robust, evidence-based IPC measures is critical to preventing and containing outbreaks while ensuring the delivery of safe and high-quality healthcare.1214

The WHO emphasises that strong IPC programmes in healthcare settings improve patient safety, enhance care quality and protect healthcare providers.5 12 Strengthening IPC capacity at the facility level can significantly reduce healthcare-associated infection (HAI) risks, enhance outbreak response and containment efforts, and prevent healthcare systems from becoming overwhelmed. Recognising the importance of standardised IPC practices, the WHO has defined a set of core IPC components that should be implemented at national, sub-national and facility levels.11 13 These components include the development of IPC policies and guidelines, education and training, surveillance, monitoring, auditing and feedback mechanisms, and ensuring an enabling environment with the necessary infrastructure.1517

To guide IPC implementation, the WHO proposes a five-step cycle designed to support IPC improvement interventions.18 A crucial component of this cycle is a baseline assessment of IPC measures, essential during the preparedness phase before an outbreak occurs. Outbreak management at the facility level unfolds in three distinct phases: (1) preparedness, when no outbreak is occurring, but IPC baselines are being established; (2) readiness, when a specific outbreak threat has been identified and (3) response when an outbreak is actively occurring, requiring swift and effective mitigation strategies.1922

Despite the existence of standardised IPC guidelines, many healthcare facilities in developing countries continue to struggle with their implementation, increasing the risk of HAIs among patients and healthcare personnel, as well as the broader spread of emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases into surrounding communities.2325 This underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions to strengthen IPC practices.26 27 Even in developed countries with more advanced IPC systems, the COVID-19 pandemic revealed vulnerabilities, demonstrating how rapidly emerging pathogens can breach containment efforts and cause devastating mortality among healthcare workers (HCWs).2830 Consequently, the need for effective IPC preparedness in healthcare settings cannot be overstated.

Poor IPC practices in healthcare facilities have profound health consequences in Nigeria and other low-income countries. Studies have shown recurrent infections and deaths among HCWs in Nigeria from viral haemorrhagic fevers including EVD and Lassa fever, highlighting the vulnerability of health systems where IPC structures are weak.31 32 For example, the death of a notable senior physician at First Cardiology Consultants Hospital in Nigeria during the 2014 EVD outbreak exemplifies how delayed detection and inadequate containment protocols can transform hospitals into amplification points for outbreaks. Similarly, the yearly resurgence of Lassa fever, often accompanied by HCW infections and fatalities, underscores persistent IPC gaps that allow highly transmissible pathogens to spread within clinical environments.

Furthermore, Nigeria, being a country with a low HCW-to-population ratio and escalating workforce migration (‘Japa syndrome’), these infections further deplete the already strained workforce, directly contributing to service disruption, burnout, morbidity and avoidable mortality among essential health personnel.33 34 Poor IPC also increases the incidence of HAIs among patients ranging from bloodstream infections and surgical site infections to neonatal sepsis conditions that significantly raise morbidity, length of hospital stay and treatment costs. Studies in Nigeria and across sub-Saharan Africa demonstrate that facilities with inadequate triage systems, poor environmental hygiene, limited access to clean water and insufficient personal protective equipment (PPE) experience higher rates of HAIs and nosocomial outbreaks compared with facilities with structured IPC programmes.35 36

Evidence from other low-income countries mirrors this pattern. During the West African EVD outbreak, weak hospital IPC systems in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone accelerated transmission, with over 800 HCW infections reported in 2014 alone.31 Similarly, in Uganda and the Democratic Republic of Congo, lapses in triage, improper waste management and inadequate isolation facilities have repeatedly contributed to the spread of viral haemorrhagic fevers within healthcare settings.37 Poor IPC also fuels antimicrobial resistance (AMR), as uncontrolled infections lead to inappropriate antibiotic use, compounding morbidity and mortality.36

By contrast, health facilities that maintain high IPC standards consistently demonstrate markedly reduced infection transmission, improved patient outcomes and enhanced protection for HCWs. Effective IPC systems with dedicated personnel, reliable PPE supply, trained staff, strong surveillance and sound hygiene programmes can reduce HAIs by up to 70% when implemented properly.38 For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, Nigerian hospitals with established IPC units and emergency preparedness protocols reported fewer HCW infections compared with facilities lacking structured IPC frameworks.34 39 International evidence further shows that hospitals with strong IPC programmes experience lower outbreak frequency, quicker containment of infectious threats, reduced patient mortality and overall system resilience.36 38

Thus, while poor IPC exposes HCWs, patients and communities to avoidable infectious hazards and accelerates outbreak spread, strong IPC systems serve as a critical line of defence, safeguarding health workers, enhancing patient safety, reducing HAIs and strengthening overall outbreak preparedness.

Therefore, in Nigeria, Lagos State, being the country’s commercial hub and most populous State, is particularly vulnerable to disease outbreaks, as witnessed during the 2014 Ebola outbreak and, more recently, COVID-19.4043 The 2014 Ebola outbreak in Lagos, introduced by a Liberian traveller seeking medical care, underscored the critical role of IPC measures in containing disease transmission.44 45 In response, the Lagos State Ministry of Health reviewed key lessons from the outbreak and initiated efforts to enhance public health preparedness against emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases. One notable initiative was the establishment of the Lagos Biobank, a Level 3 Bio-Safety Laboratory at the Infectious Disease Hospital in Yaba, to support infectious disease diagnosis and containment.46 Biobank facilitated rapid screening and research during the COVID-19 pandemic.4750

In addition to laboratory infrastructure, Lagos State has invested in monitoring and evaluation systems to ensure adherence to IPC standards across healthcare facilities. The Lagos State Health Facilities Monitoring and Accreditation Agency (HEFAMAA), established under the 2006 Health Sector Reform Law, is responsible for overseeing and improving the quality of healthcare services across the State.51 Since its inception, HEFAMAA has registered and monitored over 3900 public and private health facilities, ensuring compliance with quality assurance protocols, including IPC measures. The agency mandates healthcare facilities to align with international best practices in IPC to create safer healthcare environments and minimise the risk of outbreaks.

However, ongoing monitoring has revealed significant gaps in IPC implementation across many healthcare facilities, particularly in the adherence to standard precautions and other essential infection control measures. Deficiencies in IPC practices could leave facilities ill-equipped to respond effectively to outbreaks of emerging infectious diseases. This highlights the urgent need for enhanced IPC strategies to improve preparedness and compliance.

Recognising these gaps, HEFAMAA conducted this baseline assessment of IPC practices in primary and secondary acute healthcare facilities across Lagos State. This study aimed to determine and compare the baseline level of IPC practices in public and private healthcare facilities in Lagos by assessing the overall level of IPC practices in both settings and examining whether differences exist between them, guided by the corresponding null (no significant difference) and alternative (significant difference) hypotheses, to identify existing strengths and weaknesses, inform targeted interventions and support improved IPC implementation in healthcare facilities. Findings from this study will contribute to developing a sustainable IPC framework, ensuring that healthcare facilities in Lagos State are adequately prepared to prevent and respond to future outbreaks. In addition, the findings will guide future researchers and policymakers by highlighting specific IPC gaps, revealing priority areas that require intervention and providing evidence-based insights for informed decision making towards implementing strategies to strengthen IPC practices across healthcare facilities in Lagos State and in Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Study settings

The study took place in Lagos State, southwestern Nigeria. It is the commercial nerve centre of Nigeria. Lagos State consists of three Senatorial districts (West, East and Central), with 20 local government areas (LGAs) and 37 local council development areas.52 There were a total of 319 functional public (government-owned) primary healthcare centres (PHCs) with significant numbers operating 24-hour in-patient care, 28 public general secondary hospitals and one state government tertiary teaching hospital, plus four tertiary hospitals owned by the federal government and three Army services health facilities classified to operate at tertiary level.50 Lagos, being the commercial hub of Nigeria, has over 4000 private health facilities in the state, of various registration schedules including around 1600 hospitals.

Primary data obtained from the Lagos State Ministry of Health informed estimates of facility characteristics. These facilities varied widely in size, patient volume and service scope, with a public general hospital attending an estimated 500–900 outpatients daily and admitting 150–300 in-patients per month, while a busy private hospital served 100–200 outpatients daily with 50–100 in-patients per month depending on location and specialisation. The public PHCs used in this study, in addition to routine outpatient services, provided in-patient care, particularly for maternity and child health, and typically saw approximately 200 patients daily and admitting fewer than 50–100 patients per month for each PHC. Most public general hospitals had existed for decades, with well-established outpatient, inpatient, maternity, emergency, laboratory and surgical services, whereas private hospitals used for this study were long-standing HEFAMAA-registered facilities offering general medical, specialist and diagnostic services.

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional study was used to determine and compare the baseline assessment of the IPC measures and activities in private and public healthcare facilities in Lagos. The study identified the gaps and strengths in IPC measures for further improvement in the healthcare facilities in Lagos, Nigeria.

Study population

The study population consisted of primary and secondary public and private health facilities categorised as hospitals under the Lagos State HEFAMAA registration database. Data were collected on the characteristics of the facilities and IPC practices and from respondents who are the operating officers or persons in charge of clinical or IPC activities. As of 2022, there were around 1600 private primary and secondary-level hospitals, 28 public general (secondary-level) hospitals and 319 public PHCs in this category of healthcare facilities in Lagos State.

Inclusion criteria

All acute care primary and secondary-level hospitals in the public and private hospital registration schedules under HEFAMAA in Lagos State, including PHCs operating 24-hour in-patient care services, were included in this study. They comprised public secondary-level hospitals, and primary-level hospitals/PHCs, and private primary-level and secondary-level hospitals. These were healthcare facilities that operated 24-hour in-patient care.

Exclusion criteria

Tertiary healthcare facilities and other private health facility registration schedules under HEFAMAA in Lagos State were excluded from this study, including PHCs not operating 24-hour in-patient care.

Sample size determination

The minimum sample size required for comparing the baseline level of IPC practices in public and private healthcare facilities in Lagos was calculated using the formula for the comparison of two proportions, which is commonly used in comparative cross-sectional studies.53 54 The calculated sample size was n=200 (for each group), and a total of 400 for both the public and private healthcare facilities. However, making a 10% provision for non-response, the final calculated minimum sample size was 222 for each group. The total for both groups was 444 healthcare facilities, which compared the baseline level of IPC practices in the public and private healthcare facilities.

Sampling technique

A multistage random sampling technique was employed to ensure a representative selection of private hospitals and public PHCs across Lagos State’s 20 LGAs.55 Given their relatively smaller number, all public secondary healthcare facilities were included in the study to enhance comprehensive assessment.

Stage 1: selection of political wards

To ensure geographic representation, five political wards were randomly selected from each of the 20 LGAs using simple random sampling by balloting, resulting in a total of 100 wards. These wards served as the primary selection units identified by healthcare facilities.

Stage 2: selection of healthcare facilities

A sampling frame was developed, listing all private primary and secondary healthcare facilities and public PHCs within the selected wards. A stratified random sampling approach was then applied, ensuring proportional allocation based on the total number of healthcare facilities per ward and their classification as either primary or secondary level of care. This approach minimised selection bias, allowing a more balanced comparison of IPC practices across facility types and levels.

Study instruments and tool usage

A structured questionnaire, adapted from the WHO’s Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF), served as the primary data collection tool.56 The IPCAF tool, a globally recognised framework, was selected to provide a standardised and systematic assessment of IPC practices across healthcare facilities.56 57

To ensure local relevance and quality of data collection, the questionnaire was modified to accommodate relevant questions related to the primary and secondary levels of care, as different levels often require the minimum or full IPC requirements. Also, additional context-specific questions addressing key IPC challenges in Lagos State, such as medical waste management and resource availability, were included in the questionnaire. Before deployment, the questionnaire underwent a pre-test in 50 healthcare facilities that were not part of the main study. This pre-test helped evaluate clarity, reliability and applicability, leading to necessary adjustments to enhance comprehensibility and alignment with the local healthcare context.

Defined tool usage

The IPCAF tool was structured into eight sections, each corresponding to a core component of IPC as defined by the WHO. Within the original sections, 81 indicators are assessed and formulated based on evidence and expert consensus. Each indicator is presented as a question with predefined response options, allowing for a structured and objective evaluation of IPC practices. In our study, not all the questions in the IPCAF tool were used. The IPCAF tool was adapted to assess healthcare facilities operating mainly at the primary and secondary levels of care.

For scoring and classification, points were assigned to each response, with a maximum of 100 points per section and a total achievable score of 800. Based on the final cumulative score, healthcare facilities were categorised into one of four levels of IPC promotion and practice in accordance with the WHO IPCAF tool:

Inadequate (0–200 points): IPC core components were poorly implemented or absent, requiring significant intervention for improvement.

Data collection

Data collection for this study was conducted using the structured questionnaire administered electronically via Kobo Toolbox, an open-source platform designed for efficient and accurate data collection.58 59 To ensure the reliability of the process, 15 data collectors were recruited and trained over 2 days by IPC professionals on the use of the data collection tool and best practices for administering the questionnaire. The data collectors were nurses and doctors used for IPC during the COVID-19 outbreak in Lagos State, who had IPC certification from NCDC during the pandemic.

Data collection took place over 3 months, beginning in July 2023, with the trained data collectors working closely with designated focal persons in selected healthcare facilities. The questionnaire was designed to capture facility characteristics and IPC practices aligned with the eight core components of the WHO IPCAF. Data for this assessment were collected through face-to-face visits to the randomly selected public and private healthcare facilities. The trained data collectors administered the questionnaire directly to the relevant personnel, the operating officers, or persons in charge of clinical or IPC activities, and conducted on-site observations to objectively assess IPC structures, processes and practice. A pilot study was conducted in 50 healthcare facilities not included in the main study to validate the data collection instrument and assess its effectiveness. The feedback from this pre-test allowed for necessary refinements, ensuring that the questionnaire was comprehensive and contextually relevant.

Classification of variables

This study classified variables into dependent (outcome) and independent variables to facilitate a structured analysis of IPC practices across healthcare facilities. The dependent variables included the total IPCAF median score, the median baseline scores of IPC core components and the level of IPC practices, as determined by the WHO IPCAF tool.56 57 60 These variables provided a standardised measure of IPC implementation across different healthcare settings.

The independent variables consisted of key facility characteristics that could influence IPC performance. These included the type of healthcare facility, whether public or private, and whether it provided primary or secondary care. These classifications were selected because facility type and ownership can affect resource availability, staff capacity and the implementation of IPC measures, as shown in a previous study. Additionally, the study considered the availability of existing IPC programmes or quality improvement (QI) initiatives and monitoring and oversight mechanisms to ensure compliance with IPC standards. This was justified because HEFAMAA actively monitors these healthcare facilities, and some forms of QI programmes have been implemented in both private and public facilities by private organisations. The geographic location of each facility, categorised as urban (central), peri-urban (west) or rural (east), was also examined to assess its potential impact on IPC implementation. This classification reflects differences in the socioeconomic status, as facilities in the urban-like areas often have better access to resources and funding compared with peri-urban or rural facilities, which may face resource constraints that can affect IPC implementation.

Data analysis

The data collected in this study was analysed using Stata BE 17 statistical software. The data were imported from the downloaded Excel copy of the Kobo Collect tool into Stata, and it was cleaned prior to analysis. A univariate analysis was conducted to generate frequencies, percentages, means, medians and SD, providing a descriptive overview of the dataset. The χ2 test was used to compare differences or associations in IPC levels between public and private healthcare facilities, determining whether observed disparities were statistically significant.

A p value of ≤0.05 at a 95% CI was considered statistically significant, ensuring the findings were robust and reliable for informing IPC interventions and policy recommendations. Based on the hypothesis stated for this study, the p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant because it was <0.05. The null hypothesis was rejected in favour of the alternative hypothesis, indicating a significant association or differences in the baseline level of IPC practices between public and private healthcare facilities in Lagos.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

A total of 415 healthcare facilities, with respondents from 214 (51.6%) private and 201 (48.4%) public facilities, gave their consent to participate in this study over 3 months, giving a response rate of 93.5% from the sample size of 444 healthcare facilities. The majority, 338 (81.4%), of the surveyed healthcare facilities were of primary level of care, with 129 (31.1%) indicating having one form of QI programme and 227 (54.7%) provided services for the Lagos State Health Scheme. Level of IPC promotion and practice

Based on the overall score achieved in the eight sections of the IPCAF, a total of 48.8% (202) of the surveyed facilities had an inadequate level of IPC practices, 25.5% (106) had a basic level of IPC practices, while the remaining 25.7% (107) had an intermediate level of IPC practices (table 5). More of the public, 57.7% (116) facilities had an inadequate level of practice compared with 40.2% (86) of private facilities (p value ≤0.05) (table 5). Most of the facilities with inadequate practices in the public sector were of primary level of care. The total median IPCAF scores for the private and public primary healthcare facilities were 222.5 (IQR 147.5–427.5) and 153.8 (IQR 96.3–278.8) which correspond to a basic and inadequate level of IPC practices, respectively, while the total median IPCAF score for the private and public secondary healthcare facilities was 431.3 (IQR 200.0–542.5) and 472.5 (IQR 325.0–537.5) which correspond to an intermediate level of IPC practices, respectively.

Discussion

This study comprehensively assesses IPC practices across public and private healthcare facilities in Lagos, Nigeria, revealing significant gaps in IPC monitoring, training and programme implementation. The findings are consistent with previous studies from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) that have reported inadequate IPC compliance, particularly in resource-constrained settings.6165 Similar to research conducted in Ghana and Uganda, which found that a lack of structured IPC programmes contributed to high HAIs and AMR, our study highlights suboptimal IPC training and monitoring as key barriers to effective IPC implementation.6668

A critical finding was that 48.8% of healthcare facilities had inadequate IPC practices, with public primary healthcare facilities scoring the lowest on IPC preparedness. This aligns with studies in India, which found that primary healthcare centres often lack trained personnel, IPC infrastructure and sustainable IPC policies, leading to higher HAI transmission risks.69 70 In contrast, private healthcare facilities performed relatively better, a trend observed in Kenya studies, where better resource allocation, higher staff-to-patient ratios and independent monitoring mechanisms contributed to improved IPC compliance.71

Notably, this study revealed that facilities monitored by HEFAMAA had higher IPC scores, supporting previous findings that regular audits and enforcement improve IPC adherence.15 72 73 This is consistent with research in Ethiopia and Bangladesh, where government-led IPC monitoring significantly improved hospital compliance but had limited impact in primary care facilities due to systemic constraints.74

A major concern highlighted in this study was the low level of IPC training across healthcare facilities. Only 19.5% of facilities provided mandatory IPC training for HCWs, and even fewer extended training to cleaners (16.6%) and administrative staff (32.1%). These findings echo a study in Pakistan, where poor IPC knowledge among frontline staff and cleaners led to increased HAI incidence.75 Similarly, research from Brazil, Bangladesh and the Philippines found that IPC training is often deprioritised in overstretched healthcare systems, resulting in gaps in hand hygiene compliance, waste disposal and antimicrobial stewardship.7678 The association between higher IPC scores and facilities with structured training programmes suggests that investment in periodic, mandatory IPC training could significantly improve compliance.7981 This aligns with a systematic review of IPC interventions in Sub-Saharan Africa, which concluded that continuous training and refresher courses are critical for sustained IPC improvement.15 82

Our study found that only 23.4% of facilities had a structured IPC monitoring plan, and 77.1% lacked trained personnel for IPC audits. This reflects findings from Vietnam, where weak IPC surveillance systems contributed to delayed outbreak detection and poor infection control measures.83 Facilities that had undergone HEFAMAA monitoring scored significantly higher on IPC core components, reinforcing the need for routine external audits and corrective action plans. Notably, the lowest median IPC scores were observed in core components related to HAI surveillance, multimodal strategies and IPC programme implementation. This aligns with studies from Malawi and Indonesia, which found that gaps in HAI surveillance significantly undermine IPC effectiveness.84 85 In contrast, high-income countries with well-established IPC surveillance systems, such as Germany and Canada, have reported significantly lower HAI rates, demonstrating the critical role of structured surveillance in infection control.17

The disparity in IPC preparedness between public and private healthcare facilities is a significant concern. Our findings indicate that public healthcare facilities, particularly primary care centres, had the poorest IPC scores. This aligns with studies from Ghana and Uganda, where public healthcare centres often struggle with IPC due to resource limitations, overcrowding and poor waste management practices.8688 Furthermore, the overall IPCAF median score (207.5, IQR 132.5–415.0) suggests that IPC practices remain below WHO-recommended standards.8991 In comparison, studies from middle-income countries such as China and Thailand have reported median IPC scores above 400, indicating better adherence to IPC guidelines.92 93 This highlights an urgent need for policy-driven interventions to strengthen IPC programmes in Nigeria, particularly at the primary healthcare level.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, the large sample size (n=415) enhances the representativeness of the findings, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of IPC practices across healthcare facilities in Lagos. The WHO IPCAF tool also ensures global comparability, making it possible to benchmark IPC performance against international standards. The high response rate (93.5%) further strengthens the reliability of the study, minimising selection bias and ensuring that the findings reflect the broader healthcare landscape in the region.

However, the study is subject to certain limitations. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships, meaning that while associations between facility characteristics and IPC practices can be identified, these associations’ directionality or underlying causes cannot be fully determined.94 95 Additionally, self-reporting bias is possible, as healthcare facilities may have overestimated their IPC compliance when responding to the survey.96 Lastly, the study primarily relies on quantitative data, limiting the depth of insights into context-specific barriers to IPC implementation.24 97 In future research, incorporating qualitative approaches, such as in-depth interviews or facility observations, could provide a more nuanced understanding of IPC adherence challenges.

Recommendations for policy and practice

The findings of this study highlight critical gaps in IPC practices across healthcare facilities in Lagos, necessitating targeted policy and practice interventions. One of the most urgent recommendations is to mandate IPC training for all healthcare personnel, including cleaners and administrative staff, ensuring that all facility workers receive structured refresher courses to reinforce best practices and improve compliance.

Additionally, HEFAMAA monitoring efforts should be scaled up to ensure that all healthcare facilities undergo periodic IPC audits with structured feedback mechanisms. Strengthening regulatory oversight will help address existing deficiencies and promote adherence to IPC standards.

Since public primary healthcare facilities exhibited the highest IPC gaps, efforts should be directed toward strengthening IPC programmes at the primary healthcare level, with increased resource allocation, infrastructure development and staff capacity building.

Furthermore, integrating robust HAI surveillance systems will enhance early outbreak detection and response capabilities. This will require investments in digital reporting mechanisms, real-time monitoring tools and improved coordination between health facilities and public health authorities.

Finally, leveraging private sector engagement through public-private partnerships can improve IPC programme funding, training opportunities and access to essential IPC resources. Collaboration between government agencies, private healthcare providers and non-governmental organisations could drive sustainable improvements in IPC implementation and pandemic preparedness.

Conclusion

This study provides a critical baseline assessment of IPC practices in Lagos healthcare facilities, revealing widespread deficiencies in training, monitoring and programme implementation. Compared with other LMICs, Nigeria’s IPC preparedness remains suboptimal, particularly in public primary healthcare centres. The findings underscore the urgent need for government-led interventions to strengthen IPC capacity, enhance monitoring mechanisms and integrate standardised training programmes to mitigate HAIs and outbreak risks. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to assess the long-term impact of IPC interventions and explore barriers to IPC implementation using qualitative methodologies.
