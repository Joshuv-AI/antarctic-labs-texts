UHRF1 inhibits cuproptosis in lung adenocarcinoma via promoting FDX1 promoter methylation
Authors: Bin Shen, Yichen Luo, Zhangrui Huang, Ruyi Zhou, Shimin Pei, Siyuan Sun, Jianwei Huang, Chen Zhang, Jingwei Yan, Yifan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUHRF1 is a key epigenetic regulator implicated in the tumorigenesis of various cancers through DNA methylation; however, its specific mechanisms in the progression of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remain poorly understood. This study aims to elucidate the regulatory role of UHRF1 in LUAD, focusing on its impact on cuproptosis.MethodsUHRF1 expression and its correlation with patient prognosis were analyzed using the TCGA-LUAD dataset. Expression levels of UHRF1 and FDX1 in LUAD cell lines were verified via qPCR and Western blot. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed to explore UHRF1-associated pathways. The impact of UHRF1 on cuproptosis was assessed using CCK-8 assays, metabolite detection, and apoptosis analysis. Mechanistically, Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and Methylation-Specific PCR (MSP) were performed to investigate the binding and methylation status of the FDX1 promoter. Finally, the oncogenic role of UHRF1 was validated in vivo using a xenograft mouse model.ResultsClinical analysis revealed that elevated UHRF1 expression in LUAD tissues is significantly associated with poor prognosis. At the cellular level, UHRF1 overexpression downregulated FDX1 expression and inhibited DLAT oligomerization. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that UHRF1 is involved in metabolic reprogramming; specifically, its overexpression enhanced glycolysis while suppressing cuproptosis. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that UHRF1 binds directly to the FDX1 promoter, inducing hypermethylation and subsequent transcriptional silencing. Rescue experiments confirmed that restoring FDX1 expression reverses the cuproptosis-suppressive effects of UHRF1. In vivo, UHRF1 knockdown retarded tumor growth and promoted cell death, whereas concurrent FDX1 knockdown attenuated these tumor-suppressive effects.ConclusionUHRF1 negatively regulates FDX1 expression through DNA methylation, thereby inhibiting cuproptosis and driving LUAD progression. These findings clarify a novel epigenetic mechanism underlying LUAD and highlight the UHRF1/FDX1 axis as a potential therapeutic target. Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all diagnoses, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the most prevalent histological subtype, constituting about 40% of cases [1]. Although LUAD typically exhibits a slower progression rate than other subtypes, it is associated with a higher propensity for early distant metastasis. Clinical manifestations of LUAD commonly include cough, chest pain, dyspnea, and weight loss. Despite recent advances in diagnostic modalities (e.g., low-dose spiral CT screening) and therapeutic strategies (e.g., immunotherapy), the prognosis for LUAD patients remains unsatisfactory, with a suboptimal five-year survival rate [2–4]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms driving LUAD progression and identifying novel therapeutic targets are essential to improve patient outcomes.

Cuproptosis represents a distinct cell death pathway resulting from dysregulated intracellular copper metabolism [5]. Unlike other death modalities, cuproptosis occurs when excess copper binds to lipoylated proteins in the TCA cycle, inducing their abnormal oligomerization and destabilizing Fe-S cluster proteins. This process triggers proteotoxic stress, ultimately leading to cell death [5]. FDX1, from the ferredoxin family, is vital in the biological synthesis, heme synthesis, and electron transfer reactions of Fe-S clusters [6]. Emerging evidence suggests that FDX1 acts as a tumor suppressor in LUAD [7, 8]. For instance, Liu et al. [7]. reported that FDX1 is significantly downregulated in LUAD tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, and low FDX1 levels correlate strongly with unfavorable pathological characteristics and poor overall patient survival. While evidence increasingly underscores the significance of FDX1 as a tumor suppressor, the upstream mechanisms responsible for its downregulation in LUAD remain largely uncharacterized.

Epigenetic silencing of tumor suppressor genes is a hallmark of cancer progression. UHRF1(Ubiquitin-like with PHD and ring finger domains 1), also known as ICBP90 in humans and Np95 in mice, is a critical epigenetic regulator that recruits DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) to replication forks [9]. UHRF1 is frequently overexpressed in various malignancies, including bladder, prostate, lung, and cervical cancers [10–13]. Mechanistically, UHRF1 facilitates the hypermethylation of promoter regions, leading to the transcriptional silencing of tumor suppressor genes. For instance, in cervical cancer, UHRF1 represses TXNIP through DNMT1-mediated promoter methylation, enabling tumor cells to evade cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, ultimately driving their proliferation and survival [14]. In LUAD, UHRF1 overexpression has been linked to aberrant global DNA methylation [13], indicating its pathogenic role in lung carcinogenesis through epigenetic modification.

This study investigates the interplay between UHRF1 and the cuproptosis-related gene FDX1 in LUAD through bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation. Our findings reveal an inverse relationship between UHRF1 and FDX1, which was observed in bioinformatics and experimental analyses, mediated by UHRF1-induced hypermethylation of the FDX1 promoter. This study elucidates a novel epigenetic mechanism regulating cuproptosis in LUAD and highlights UHRF1 as a promising therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The mRNA expression profiles of LUAD (59 normal and 539 tumor samples) were obtained from the TCGA dataset. UHRF1 and FDX1 expression in LUAD patients was analyzed using the “Limma” R package and visualized via box plots. The prognostic value of UHRF1 in lung cancer was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. ssGSEA was performed to explore potential signaling pathways associated with UHRF1. The correlation between UHRF1 and FDX expression was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

The human normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B (CRL-3588) and LUAD cell lines A549 (CRM-CCL-185), HCC827 (CRL-2868), and Calu-3 (HTB-55) were purchased from ATCC (USA). BEAS-2B cells were cultured in specialized BEAS-2B medium (iCell-h023-001b, Icellverse, China). A549, HCC827, and Calu-3 cells were maintained in F12-K, RPMI-1640, and MEM (all from Gibco, USA), respectively. All culture media were supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, China). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. To induce cuproptosis or inhibit glycolysis, cells were treated with Elsm-Cu (60 nM Elesclomol + 1 µM CuCl₂; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) or 15 mM 2-DG (D408100, Aladdin, China) for 24 h, respectively.

Cell transfection

The overexpression constructs of UHRF1, FDX1, and their respective negative controls were all obtained from HonorGene (China). According to the manufacturer’s protocol, they were transfected into Calu-3 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen, USA). qPCR analysis was performed 24 h post-transfection, and Western blot analysis was conducted 48 h post-transfection. For the stable knockdown of A549 cells, lentiviruses carrying shRNA targeting UHRF1/FDX1 and their respective negative controls were obtained from HonorGene (China). A549 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in a 6-well plate and cultured for 24 h. The supernatant was then removed, and the diluted sh-UHRF1/sh-FDX1 lentivirus solution containing 8 µg/mL polybrene (Beyotime, China) was added. The transduction was performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10, followed by static incubation at 37 °C. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium without virus for further cultivation. The cells were cultured for an additional 72 h to achieve stable knockdown. Subsequently, 0.5–2 µg/mL puromycin (Beyotime, China) was added for positive selection and maintained for 7–10 days. During this period, the medium was replaced every 2 days until all non-transduced cells had died.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8. After counting and adjusting the concentration to 2 × 10⁴ cells/mL, transfected Calu-3 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 100 µL/well and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. At indicated time points, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to each well. Cells were incubated for an additional 2 h protected from light, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Detection of glucose uptake, ATP, and lactate

The levels of glucose uptake, lactate, and ATP were measured using specific assay kits (BC2505, BC0305, and BC2235; Solarbio, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was quantified using a flow cytometer (Agilent, USA) with an Annexin V-FITC Kit (C1062M, Beyotime, China). Briefly, transfected cells were harvested, washed, and gently resuspended in 195 µL of binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Cells were then stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL of propidium iodide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, and analyzed immediately.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

The interaction between UHRF1 and the FDX1 promoter was analyzed using the Magna ChIP A/G Kit (17-10085, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Briefly, A549 cells were fixed (1% formaldehyde, 10 min) to facilitate DNA-protein crosslinking, followed by lysis. Chromatin was sonicated on ice for 3 min to obtain 200-1,000 bp fragments, which were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-UHRF1 antibody (EM1707-01, Huabio, China) or a normal IgG control (ab109489, Abcam, UK). After reserving an input control, Protein Agarose/Sepharose (Beyotime, China) was used to precipitate DNA-protein complexes for qPCR analysis.

Methylation-specific PCR (MSP)

The methylation status of the FDX1 promoter was detected through MSP. Genomic DNA was extracted using the DP705 kit from Tiangen (China), bisulfite-modified using the DNA Bisulfite Conversion Kit (DP215-02, Tiangen, China), and amplified using AmpliTaq-Gold DNA Polymerase on an ABI 7500 qPCR system (both from Applied Biosystems, USA). Amplification products were electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel and detected by ethidium bromide staining.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA from BEAS-2B cells and LUAD cell lines was isolated using an RNA extraction kit (QIAGEN, Germany) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China). qPCR was performed using the QuantiNova SYBR Green PCR Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as an internal control. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blot (WB)

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and quantified for protein concentration using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Samples were denatured in SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Epizyme Biotech, China) at 98 °C. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-UHRF1 (87632, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-FDX1 (HA721329, Huabio, China), anti-GLUT1 (ab115730, Abcam, UK), and anti-HK2 (22029-1-AP), anti-LDHA (19987-1-AP), and anti-β-actin (20536-1-AP) from Proteintech (USA). After washing, membranes were then incubated with the HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab6721, Abcam, UK) or goat anti-mouse IgG (ab6789, Abcam, UK) for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence on a ChemiScope instrument (ChemiScope, China).

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

Bioenergetic profiles were measured using an XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, USA). Calu-3 cells were seeded at 1,000 cells/well and cultured to 90% confluency. Prior to analysis of cellular energy metabolism, cells were equilibrated in bicarbonate-free assay medium. For ECAR measurement, glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 µM), and 2-DG (50 mM) were sequentially injected. For OCR measurement, oligomycin (1.5 µM), FCCP (0.5 µM), and a mixture of antimycin A/rotenone (0.5 µM) were injected.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were fixed (4% PFA, 15 min), permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100, 10 min), and blocked (5% FBS, 30 min, room temperature) to ensure specific binding. Cells were incubated with an anti-DLAT antibody (ab110332, Abcam, UK) overnight at 4 °C. The following day, cells were incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG-Alexa Fluor® 594 (ab150116, Abcam, UK) in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Biosharp, China). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region with approval NO. KY-KJT-2024-46. Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from Hangzhou Hansi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China) and housed under SPF conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, at 25 ± 1 °C, and at 60%-70% humidity. To establish xenograft tumor models, A549 cells transfected with shRNA-UHRF1, shRNA-FDX1, or their respective controls were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of mice (n = 6 per group). Mice were administered 6 mg/kg Elsm-Cu to induce cuproptosis stress. Tumor dimensions were measured every 5 days, and volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume = (length × width²) / 2). On day 25 post-injection, experimental mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissues were fixed (4% PFA, 24 h), paraffin-embedded, and cut into 4 μm sections. After deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-UHRF1, anti-FDX1, or anti-Ki67 (HA721115, Huabio, China). After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab6721, Abcam, UK) at 37 °C for 30 min. Color was developed using DAB (Solarbio, China), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. After dehydration and clearing, neutral balsam-mounted sections were observed under a microscope.

TUNEL staining

Apoptosis in tumor tissues was detected using a one-step TUNEL Kit (C1086, Beyotime, China). Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and permeabilized with DNase-free proteinase K (Beyotime, China) for 30 min and washed with PBS three times. The TUNEL reaction mixture was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium (Beyotime, China). Sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope. The proportion of TUNEL-positive cells was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Data from three independent experiments were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc tests. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High UHRF1 expression in LUAD correlates with poor prognosis

Analysis of the TCGA data revealed upregulated UHRF1 in LUAD tissues, and its close association with poor prognosis (Fig. 1A, B). Consistent with clinical data, subsequent qPCR analysis demonstrated significantly elevated UHRF1 mRNA levels in three LUAD cell lines compared to the normal bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B cells. Among the cancer cell lines, Calu-3 cells exhibited lower UHRF1 expression, while A549 cells showed relatively higher levels (Fig. 1C). WB analysis confirmed consistent upregulation of UHRF1 protein in these cells (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that UHRF1 is aberrantly upregulated in LUAD and may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker.

UHRF1 inhibits exogenous copper ion-induced cuproptosis in LUAD cells

To investigate the role of UHRF1 in cuproptosis, we established a UHRF1-overexpressing (oe-UHRF1) model in Calu-3 cells and treated them with Elsm-Cu. Successful overexpression was verified by qPCR and Western blot (Fig. 2A, B). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that UHRF1 overexpression enhanced cell viability under copper stress conditions (Fig. 2C). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that UHRF1 overexpression significantly reduced the cell death rate from 10.48% to 3.65% (Fig. 2D). Mechanistically, Western blot analysis indicated that UHRF1 overexpression significantly downregulated the cuproptosis-related protein FDX1. Notably, under Elsm-Cu treatment, the oe-UHRF1 group maintained lower FDX1 levels, correlating with reduced sensitivity to cuproptosis in Calu-3 cells (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, IF assessment showed a significant decrease in DLAT oligomerization —a hallmark of cuproptosis—following UHRF1 overexpression (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results indicate that UHRF1 protects LUAD cells from cuproptosis induced by exogenous copper ions.

UHRF1 enhances glycolysis to suppress cuproptosis in LUAD cells

To explore the mechanism underlying UHRF1-mediated cuproptosis resistance, we performed GSEA, which identified a significant enrichment of UHRF1 in the glycolysis signaling pathway (Fig. 3A). To validate this, Calu-3 cells were treated with the glycolytic inhibitor 2-DG. As a result, UHRF1 overexpression significantly increased ECAR and decreased OCR compared to the vector control (Fig. 3B, C). Co-treatment with 2-DG attenuated these effects (Fig. 3B, C), indicating that 2-DG might inhibit the promotive effects of UHRF1 on glycolysis. Consistently, WB analysis revealed that UHRF1 overexpression significantly increased the protein levels of key glycolytic enzymes GLUT1, HK2, and LDHA, which were reversed upon co-treatment with 2-DG (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, glucose uptake, ATP, and lactate levels were significantly upregulated in oe-UHRF1 cells and diminished by 2-DG co-treatment (Fig. 3E-G).

We next analyzed whether this glycolytic enhancement contributes to cuproptosis resistance. In the presence of Elsm-Cu, 2-DG treatment abolished the survival advantage conferred by UHRF1 overexpression, leading to reduced cell viability and increased cell death rates (from 3.47% to 10.94%) (Fig. 3H-J). WB analysis showed that FDX1 was further downregulated in oe-UHRF1 cells upon Elsm-Cu treatment, confirming that UHRF1 expression lowered the sensitivity of cells to cuproptosis (Fig. 3K). Notably, co-treatment with 2-DG and UHRF1 overexpression led to an even greater reduction in FDX1 protein levels, suggesting that inhibiting glycolysis attenuates the cuproptosis tolerance conferred by UHRF1 (Fig. 3K). This also indicates that UHRF1 may inhibit cuproptosis through glycolysis. IF showed decreased DLAT oligomerization in oe-UHRF1 cells under cuproptosis induction, which was significantly re-promoted by 2-DG treatment (Fig. 3L). These data suggest that UHRF1 inhibits cuproptosis partially through the enhancement of glycolysis.

UHRF1 negatively regulates FDX1 via promoter methylation

TCGA data showed low FDX1 expression in LUAD tissues (Fig. 4A). FDX1 negatively correlated with UHRF1 expression, as revealed by Pearson’s correlation (Fig. 4B). Both mRNA and protein levels of FDX1 were significantly reduced in the three LUAD cell lines compared to BEAS-2B cells, confirmed by qPCR and WB (Fig. 4C, D). Given UHRF1’s role as an epigenetic regulator [11], we hypothesized that UHRF1 silences FDX1 via DNA methylation. MSP assays demonstrated significantly higher methylation levels in the FDX1 promoter region in LUAD cell lines (Fig. 4E). To further clarify the interaction between UHRF1 and FDX1, we selected A549 cells with high endogenous UHRF1 expression levels as the model for ChIP-qPCR analysis. ChIP-qPCR confirmed that UHRF1 directly binds to the FDX1 promoter region in A549 cells (Fig. 4F). Subsequently, we examined the expression changes of FDX1 in UHRF1-overexpressing Calu-3 cells. qPCR and WB analyses revealed that both mRNA and protein levels of FDX1 were markedly decreased upon UHRF1 overexpression (Fig. 4G, H). In addition, the methylation status of the FDX1 promoter in Calu-3 cells was detected by MSP. The results demonstrated that the methylation level of the FDX1 promoter was significantly higher in the UHRF1-overexpressing group relative to the empty-vector group. Moreover, treatment with the methylation inhibitor 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-Azacytidine, 5-Aza-CdR) markedly reduced the methylation level of the FDX1 promoter region in the UHRF1-overexpressing group (Fig. 4I). These results confirm that UHRF1 epigenetically silences FDX1 expression through promoter hypermethylation.

UHRF1 suppresses cuproptosis in LUAD cells by negatively regulating FDX1

To verify the UHRF1/FDX1 axis, we performed co-overexpression of UHRF1 and FDX1 to assess their interaction on cuproptosis in LUAD cells. qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that UHRF1 overexpression increased its own mRNA level while decreasing FDX1 mRNA, while FDX1 overexpression increased only its own mRNA without affecting UHRF1 levels (Fig. 5A-C). Functionally, FDX1 overexpression reversed the oe-UHRF1-induced resistance to Elsm-Cu, resulting in significantly reduced cell viability and increased cell death rates compared to the UHRF1-only group (Fig. 5D, E). Moreover, IF demonstrated reduced DLAT oligomerization in oe-UHRF1 cells under cuproptosis induction, which was significantly restored by FDX1 overexpression (Fig. 5F). These findings demonstrate that UHRF1 inhibits cuproptosis primarily by downregulating FDX1.

UHRF1 inhibits LUAD tumor growth by negatively regulating FDX1

To validate UHRF1’s regulatory role in vivo, xenograft models were established in nude mice. Under Elsm-Cu-treatment, UHRF1 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth (volume and weight) compared to the control group. Importantly, subsequent knockdown of FDX1 reversed this tumor-suppressive phenotype (Fig. 6A-C). IHC analysis showed that UHRF1 knockdown reduced the proliferation marker Ki67 and elevated FDX1 levels. However, co-knockdown of FDX1 restored the expression changes of Ki67 and reduced FDX1 expression but did not significantly alter UHRF1 levels (Fig. 6D). TUNEL staining further indicated that UHRF1 knockdown significantly increased tumor cell death, which was attenuated by subsequent FDX1 knockdown (Fig. 6E). In summary, UHRF1 promotes LUAD progression and resistance to copper stress in vivo by suppressing FDX1.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated bioinformatic analysis with experimental revalidation to unveil a novel epigenetic mechanism regulating LUAD progression. We demonstrated that UHRF1 is highly expressed in LUAD and is significantly associated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, UHRF1 acts as a transcriptional repressor of FDX1 by inducing hypermethylation of its promoter region. Furthermore, we found that UHRF1 confers resistance to cuproptosis by enhancing aerobic glycolysis, thereby reducing cellular reliance on mitochondrial respiration. These findings not only clarify the oncogenic role of the UHRF1/FDX1 axis but also highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for sensitizing LUAD tumors to copper-induced cell death.

Consistent with previous reports in various cancer types [15–17], our study confirmed that UHRF1 is upregulated in LUAD and functions as an oncogenic driver. While prior studies have linked UHRF1 to cell proliferation and metastasis [18], its specific role in regulated cell death remained underexplored. Here, we provide the first evidence linking UHRF1 to cuproptosis. Kostyrko et al. [12] characterized UHRF1 as a specific susceptibility factor downstream of oncogenic KRAS in LUAD, demonstrating that KRAS-driven oncogenic signaling cascades facilitate UHRF1 upregulation. In parallel, Li et al. [19] illustrated that activated MEK/ERK signaling synergistically elevates the expression of both UHRF1 and DNMT1 in carcinoma cells. In addition, Fu et al. [15] confirmed that AKT1-mediated phosphorylation markedly stabilizes UHRF1 protein by alleviating its ubiquitination-dependent degradation, thereby exerting vital oncogenic effects during tumor progression. This indicates that UHRF1 upregulation in LUAD may be driven by multiple factors. As a crucial epigenetic regulator, UHRF1 modulates DNA methylation by recruiting DNA methyltransferase DNMT1, thereby influencing gene expression [20]. Our ChIP and MSP assays confirmed that UHRF1 directly binds to the FDX1 promoter region, leading to its transcriptional silencing. This epigenetic suppression of FDX1 was reversible upon treatment with the demethylating agent 5-Aza-CdR, reinforcing the potential of targeting epigenetic machinery to restore FDX1 expression and sensitize tumors to cuproptosis.

The formation and progression of malignant tumors primarily result from the monoclonal proliferation of tumor cells [21]. Metabolic reprogramming, particularly the Warburg effect, is a hallmark of cancer that supports rapid proliferation [22–24]. Interestingly, our study identified a functional crosstalk between UHRF1-mediated metabolic rewiring and cuproptosis resistance. Cuproptosis is strictly dependent on mitochondrial respiration, as it involves the aggregation of lipoylated TCA cycle proteins [5]. Tumor cells often evade this fate by shifting their metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to glycolysis [25]. Our study revealed that UHRF1 suppresses cuproptosis by enhancing the glycolytic pathway. Specifically, UHRF1 overexpression upregulated glycolysis-related proteins (e.g., GLUT1, HK2, and LDHA), leading to increased glucose uptake and lactate production. This metabolic shift likely reduces mitochondrial TCA cycle activity, thereby diminishing the abundance of lipoylated targets for copper toxicity. This explains our observation that the glycolytic inhibitor 2-DG could reverse the protective effect of UHRF1, effectively re-sensitizing cells to copper stress.

Cuproptosis is a newly identified cell death form triggered by the toxic accumulation of intracellular copper ions [5]. FDX1, located on chromosome 11q22, encodes a low-molecular-weight protein containing a Fe-S cluster as its redox-active group [26]. Numerous studies have verified the tumor-suppressive function of FDX1 in LUAD. Zhang et al. [8] reported that low FDX1 expression is significantly associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes in LUAD patients; Zhao et al. [27] further validated that overexpression of FDX1 restrains malignant progression of LUAD cells. In the present study, analyses based on the TCGA-LUAD dataset revealed markedly downregulated FDX1 expression in LUAD tissues relative to normal tissues, alongside a significant negative correlation between UHRF1 and FDX1 expression levels. FDX1 is identified as a direct target of copper-dependent cell death and highlighted for its potential value in cancer therapy [28–30]. Chen et al. demonstrated that FDX1 knockdown in thyroid cancer alleviated cuproptosis by reducing DLAT and DLST lipoylation in thyroid cancer cells, and concurrently upregulated pyruvate and α-KG levels [30]. Consistent with these findings, we observed that restoring FDX1 expression in UHRF1-overexpressing cells significantly reversed cuproptosis resistance and promoted DLAT oligomerization. This confirms that FDX1 may thus be a key downstream target of UHRF1 in this context. In vivo, UHRF1 knockdown retarded tumor growth and promoted cell death, a phenotype that was rescued by concurrent FDX1 knockdown. These results solidly validate the therapeutic relevance of the UHRF1/FDX1 axis in a physiological context. Previous studies have indicated that FDX1 expression significantly decreases following Elesclomol-Cu (Elsm-Cu) treatment, which is not simply due to transcriptional inhibition but reflects the functional consumption of FDX1 during the execution of cuproptosis [31, 32]. FDX1 continuously catalyzes the reduction of Elesclomol-bound Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺; this redox reaction leads to its own sustained consumption. The generated Cu⁺ further induces aberrant aggregation of lipoylated proteins (e.g., DLAT), resulting in severe proteotoxic stress. This triggers a protective feedback mechanism where the cell downregulates FDX1 to mitigate copper reduction and alleviate toxic stress, which may inadvertently accelerate its degradation [33]. In this study, we observed that FDX1 levels showed a decreasing trend in the Elsm-Cu treatment group compared to the control group, consistent with previous literature. Notably, when combined with 2-DG treatment, FDX1 levels decreased further. We hypothesize that 2-DG, by blocking glycolysis, disrupts energy supply, forcing cells to rely on already impaired mitochondrial respiration. At this stage, despite the downregulation of FDX1 attempting to mitigate copper toxicity, the residual FDX1 may still participate in basal levels of copper reduction. Concurrently, glycolysis inhibition might be accompanied by restricted glutathione synthesis and compromised antioxidant capacity. This synergistic effect of energy metabolic dysfunction and oxidative stress, to some extent, undermines the protective effect conferred by FDX1 downregulation, ultimately leading to a significant exacerbation of cuproptosis.

Although previous studies have reported substantially higher copper levels in LUAD tissues relative to paracancerous normal tissues, and LUAD cell lines exhibit stronger copper uptake capacity than normal pulmonary epithelial BEAS-2B cells [34], tumor cells deploy diverse machineries to sustain robust copper detoxification capacity, such that endogenous copper concentrations remain insufficient to spontaneously trigger cuproptosis. For instance, tumor cells actively suppress cuproptosis pathways via metabolic reprogramming known as the Warburg effect [35]. Intact function of copper homeostasis proteins is essential for maintaining intracellular copper ion balance; targeted interference against these proteins can effectively induce cuproptosis and suppress tumor progression in lung cancer cells [36]. Existing evidence indicates that cuproptosis triggered by copper ionophores (e.g., elesclomol) is strictly dependent on exogenous Cu²⁺ [37]. We conclude that endogenous cuproptosis pathways do not prominently drive tumor biological phenotypes under basal conditions in LUAD, and the therapeutic potential of cuproptosis largely relies on intervention with exogenous copper ionophores or copper supplements. Through in vivo experiments with Elesclomol-Cu treatment, we found that UHRF1 knockdown markedly retarded tumor growth and induced cell death. This tumor-suppressive phenotype was fully rescued by simultaneous FDX1 knockdown. These results demonstrate that UHRF1 inhibits cuproptosis in LUAD cells via negative regulation of FDX1, supporting the UHRF1/FDX1 axis as a therapeutic target for LUAD.

Despite these promising findings, our study has limitations. While we confirmed that UHRF1 promotes glycolysis, the precise molecular intermediaries linking UHRF1 to glycolytic gene expression require further investigation. Additionally, future studies should explore whether small-molecule inhibitors targeting the UHRF1-DNMT1 complex can synergize with copper ionophores to treat LUAD clinically. Additionally, no genome-wide screening was performed in the present study, and thus our current dataset cannot systematically characterize all UHRF1-modulated target genes in LUAD. In future investigations, we will conduct genome-wide profiling of UHRF1 in LUAD models to systematically identify additional cuproptosis-related genes (e.g., DLAT, DLST, LIAS) controlled by UHRF1-dependent promoter methylation, to further dissect the regulatory impact of UHRF1 on cuproptosis in LUAD. Furthermore, the precise molecular mechanism underlying UHRF1 upregulation in LUAD remains unclear and requires investigation in future studies.

In conclusion, our study elucidates the mechanism of UHRF1 in LUAD and its interaction with FDX1: epigenetically silencing FDX1 via promoter hypermethylation and metabolically reprogramming cells toward glycolysis to evade cuproptosis. These insights suggest that targeting UHRF1 or restoring FDX1 expression could represent a viable strategy to overcome therapy resistance in lung adenocarcinoma.
