Identification and validation of reference genes for quantitative real-time PCR in Corydalis saxicola under various abiotic stresses
Authors: Liang Kang, Zhaodi Wen, Han Liu, Ying Lu, Mei Qin, Lirong Huang, Ming Lei, Cui Li, Dan Zhu, Li Li, Zhanjiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) is a commonly used method for measuring gene expression, but its accuracy depends on the use of stable reference genes for data normalization. In this study, we evaluated the expression stability of 11 candidate reference genes in Corydalis saxicola across different tissues and under three abiotic stress conditions (Ca²+ stress, Se²+ stress, and drought stress). Using four analytical algorithms (GeNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, and RefFinder), we identified the optimal reference genes: GAPDH2 and UBCE2-17 for different tissues; RPAP3 and UBCE2-5 A under Ca²+ stress; GAPDH8 and α-TUB under Se²+ stress; and ACT4 and RPAP3 under drought stress. Additionally, GAPDH2, TBP, and UBCE2-17 were recognized as the most suitable comprehensive reference genes applicable to both different tissues and various abiotic stress conditions. These results were validated by analyzing the expression patterns of CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 (key genes involved in benzylisoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis) as well as CsANN1 and CsANN9 (core genes regulating stress response). This study provides the first validated reference genes for C. saxicola, offering a more accurate and reproducible method for gene expression analysis in this rare medicinal plant. It will facilitate future research on secondary metabolism regulation and stress response mechanisms in C. saxicola.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54993-0.

Introduction

Corydalis saxicola, a member of the Corydalis genus within the Papaveraceae family, is extensively utilized in traditional Chinese medicine for the treatment of conditions such as hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and liver cancer1,2. Currently, proprietary Chinese medicinal formulations derived from C. saxicola have been developed specifically for addressing these hepatic disorders1. The therapeutic properties of C. saxicola are attributed to its bioactive compounds, which encompass alkaloids, steroids, triterpenoids, lignans, isoquinolinones, and flavonoids1,3. Notably, alkaloids constitute a significant category of pharmacologically active constituents, with 67 unique alkaloids identified thus far. This group includes berberines, protoberberines, benzophenanthridines, benzyltetrahydroisoquinolines, aporphines, protopines, and lignin amides1,4. Among these, berberine alkaloids, such as dehydrocavidine, dehydrochelerythrine, and tetradehydroglaucin, are the most commonly studied components found in C. saxicola1,2,4.

C. saxicola is a unique species that thrives exclusively in the crevices of rocks at altitudes ranging from 500 to 1200 m, as well as in the shaded and humid entrances of caves. This species is endemic to the karst regions of southern China5. The karst landscape is characterized by a distinct geomorphological formation resulting from the dissolution, precipitation, erosion, and other processes involving surface and groundwater interacting with soluble rocks, such as limestone and dolomite5,6. The soil in these areas is typically shallow, enriched with high levels of calcium, and exhibits poor water retention capabilities, contributing to a sensitive and fragile ecosystem7. C. saxicola demonstrates remarkable resilience to various environmental stresses, including calcium and drought, due to its adaptation to karst habitats8. Notably, selenium (Se) has been shown to alleviate heavy metal stress in crops by regulating antioxidant systems and metabolic genes9. In Plantago ovata, the application of low-concentration sodium selenate (< 50 µmol·L-¹) effectively induces the biosynthesis of various polyphenols, and exerts no inhibitory effects on plant growth10. Current research on C. saxicola primarily emphasizes its physiological traits11, chemical constituents12,13, and medicinal properties14–16; however, there is a notable lack of studies investigating the correlations between stress levels and the concentrations of bioactive compounds, as well as the molecular mechanisms underlying its stress tolerance. Investigating the relationship between the levels of pharmacologically active substances and the plant stress resistance is crucial for uncovering the genetic traits that contribute to the quality of authentic medicinal herbs, as well as for the conservation and genetic improvement of medicinal plant species.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) is an effective technique for analyzing gene expression profiles and has been extensively utilized in gene expression research17. This method offers notable advantages, including high sensitivity and excellent reproducibility. Nonetheless, the accuracy of the relative quantification results obtained from qRT-PCR can be influenced by several factors, including RNA integrity, cDNA quality, sample dilution factors, and experimental procedures18,19. To minimize potential errors during qRT-PCR analysis, it is essential to incorporate high-quality reference genes that exhibit specificity and stable expression for normalizing target gene expression20. Typically, the selection of appropriate reference genes involves identifying candidate genes followed by assessing their expression stability under specific experimental conditions. Housekeeping genes, which are crucial for maintaining fundamental cellular functions, such as actin, 18 S ribosomal RNA, tubulin, and ubiquitin, EF1α are commonly considered in this context21–23. Ideal reference genes should demonstrate stable expression across various tissues and developmental stages. However, recent research has indicated that no single reference gene consistently maintains high expression stability across all biological studies19,24.The transcription levels of housekeeping genes can differ across various species25, tissues, and organs26,27. Utilizing unvalidated reference genes may result in discrepancies in the expression outcomes of target genes, thereby raising concerns regarding the reliability of the experimental findings28. Consequently, it is essential to identify reference genes with relatively stable expression tailored to specific experimental conditions prior to assessing the relative expression levels of target genes. However, there is currently a lack of studies focused on the screening of reference genes in C. saxicola, which hampers the investigation into the mechanisms regulating gene expression in C. saxicola under varying conditions.

In this research, we selected 11 candidate reference genes—including actin 1 (ACT1), actin 4 (ACT4), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 2 (GAPDH2), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 8 (GAPDH8), α-tubulin (α-TUB), RNA polymerase II-associated protein 3 (RPAP3), TATA-box-binding protein (TBP), ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2-17 kDa (UBCE2-17), ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 5 A-like (UBCE2-5 A), ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 variant 1 C (UEV1C), β-tubulin (β-TUB)—based on the transcriptome data of C. saxicola. The expression stability of these candidate reference genes was assessed across various tissues and under abiotic stress conditions (including Ca2+ stresses, Se2+ stresses, and drought stresses) via real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and software tools such as geNorm29, NormFinder30, and BestKeeper31. Furthermore, the online analysis tool RefFinder32 was employed to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the results obtained from the aforementioned software. To validate the selected reference genes, we utilized the target genes berberine bridge enzyme-like 9 (CsBBEL9) and O-methyltransferase 9 (CsOMT9)—key enzymes involved in benzylisoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis—as well as annexins 1 (CsANN1) and annexins 9 (CsANN9)—core regulators of plant stress responses. This study represents the first systematic screening and validation of reference genes for normalizing real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) in different tissues of C. saxicola and under abiotic stress conditions, thereby establishing a solid foundation for accurate gene expression analysis in this medicinal plant species.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

On March 12, 2024, plants and seeds of C. saxicola were collected from Banla Village, Aidong Town, Donglan County, Hechi City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China. The collection process adhered strictly to the guidelines outlined in the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction, the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), and the Regulations on the Protection of Wild Plants of China. We have obtained the official Plant Collection Certificate for the national second-class protected wild plant C. saxicola (Certificate No.: Zhicai Zheng (Gui) 0030784) issued by the Forestry Bureau of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, which authorizes the collection of this species for scientific research purposes.

The collected specimens were identified as Corydalis saxicola Bunting (genus Corydalis, family Papaveraceae) by Senior Engineer Yude Peng from the Guangxi Botanical Garden of Medicinal Plants. The plant specimens were subsequently deposited in the Guangxi Chinese Medicinal Material Herbarium, assigned the specimen number 0317019 (Supplementary Figure. S1). The harvested C. saxicola plants were then transplanted into the greenhouse, and their seeds were collected later that same year.

Seedling culture conditions of Corydalis saxicola

The seeds of C. saxicola were soaked in clean water overnight and then sown in a seedling substrate (sand : vermiculite = 1:1). Subsequently, the seeds were cultured in an artificial climate chamber under the following conditions: a temperature of 25 °C, a humidity of 60%, a light intensity of 100 µmol·m-²·s-¹, and a photoperiod of 12 h light/12 h dark.

Tissue sample collection of Corydalis saxicola

Following 30 days of seedling growth, seven uniformly growing seedlings were transplanted into individual 25 cm (d) × 20 cm (h) plastic pots (one seedling per pot) and maintained under the same environmental conditions as during seedling culture. After transplantation, the seedlings were further cultured for 150 days, and seven different tissue samples (roots, stems, lateral stems, fruits, flowers, old leaves, and young leaves) were harvested between 9:00 and 10:00 a.m. and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent experiments. During the entire cultivation period, uniform watering was applied according to the growth status of the seedlings, and Hoagland nutrient solution was supplied to the plants every two weeks.

Ca2+ and Se2+ stress treatments

The seedling culture and growth conditions of C. saxicola were as previously described. After 30 days of cultivation, healthy seedlings were transplanted into hydroponic containers filled with 1/4-strength Hoagland nutrient solution for a 7-day acclimatization period. Thereafter, different concentrations of CaCl₂ (0, 5, 25, 50 mmol·L− 1) and Na₂SeO₃ (0, 10, 50 µmol·L− 1) were separately added to the 1/2-strength Hoagland nutrient solution to conduct Ca²+ and Se²+ stress treatments, respectively, with seven biological replicates per concentration. Following 30 days of treatment, roots (for Ca²+ stress) and leaves (for Se²+ stress) were excised as samples between 9:00 and 10:00 a.m. local time, and immediately stored at − 80 °C for subsequent use.

Drought stress treatment

Seedlings of C. saxicola were transplanted into plastic pots with seven biological replicates per treatment group, and maintained under standard water and nutrient conditions in a greenhouse. Before initiating the drought treatment, the seedlings were adequately irrigated, after which watering was discontinued. Samples were harvested between 9:00 and 10:00 a.m. local time at 0, 6, 12, and 18 days after irrigation was discontinued. Leaves from seedlings with uniform growth were selected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction and additional experimental analyses.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

RNA was extracted using the MolPure Plant RNA Kit (Cat. #19291ES50, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. In addition, the integrity of RNA was detected by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel(Biosharp, Anhui, China). Subsequently, the concentration and purity of RNA were measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

To generate the first-strand cDNA, the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat. #R312-01, Novozymes, Nanjing, China) was used in conjunction with 1 µg of total RNA, and the PCR reaction system was as follows: 2 µL of 5×gDNA wiper Mix was mixed with 1 µg of RNA template, incubated at 42 °C for 2 min, and then 2 µL of HiScript III Enzyme Mix, 1 µL of Oligo (dT)20VN, 1 µL of Random hexamers, and 4 µL of RNase-free ddH2O were added to a total volume of 20 µL. The samples were reverse-transcribed using a gradient PCR thermal cycler from Bio-Rad (T100™ Thermal Cycler, Bio-Rad, California, USA). The PCR reaction program was as follows: first, incubated at 37 °C for 120 min, and then heated at 85 °C for 5 s. After the reaction, the reverse-transcribed cDNA was stored at − 20 °C for subsequent experiments.

Screening of candidate reference genes and designing of primers

Housekeeping genes were initially selected from the complete transcriptome of C. saxicola based on quantitative criteria: FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) values ranging from 10 to 5000 across all 27 experimental samples, which included various tissues and CaCl₂-treated roots. Subsequently, genes representing five core biological processes were chosen: cell structure maintenance (ACT and TUB families), basic metabolism (GAPDH family), transcriptional regulation (TBP), protein modification (UBCE families), and transcriptional assistance (RPAP3). One to two representative genes were selected from each functional family, and the stability and functional conservation of these selected genes were further supported by published studies on the congeneric species Corydalis yanhusuo33. The final 11 candidate reference genes (see Supplementary Table S1 for their RNA-seq-derived FPKM values across C. saxicola tissues and CaCl₂-treated samples) are: ACT1, ACT4, GAPDH2, GAPDH8, α-TUB, RPAP3, TBP, UBCE2-17, UBCE2-5 A, UEV1C, and β-TUB.

Primers were designed using the online tool Primer3web (https://ginkgo.zju.edu.cn/genome/tools/primer3/) with the following parameters: PCR amplicon length of 80–300 bp, primer length of 18–25 bp, melting temperature (Tm) of 50–60 °C, and GC content of 45%–55%, with the aim of minimizing hairpin structure and primer-dimer formation to ensure high amplification specificity and efficiency. Primer specificity was further verified using NCBI Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). Prior to performing qRT-PCR experiments, three gene-specific primer pairs were designed for each of the candidate reference genes. The optimal primer pair for each gene was selected through conventional PCR followed by agarose gel electrophoresis analysis to confirm the absence of non-specific amplification and verify that the amplified band size was consistent with the expected target fragment size. The sequences of these primers, along with those for the target genes CsBBEL9, CsOMT9, CsANN1, and CsANN9, are provided in Table 1. All primers were synthesized by Tianyi Huiyuan Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China).

qRT-PCR reaction conditions

qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Green fluorescent dye method. The PCR reaction system was prepared according to the instructions of the 2×SYBR Green qPCR Mix Kit (Sikejie Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) as follows: 1 µL of cDNA template (approximately 10 ng), 0.8 µL of forward and reverse primers (10 µmol·L− 1 each), 0.4 µL of ROX Reference Dye II, 8.8 µL of ddH2O, and 10 µL of 2×SYBR qPCR Mix. All samples were run in triplicate. The samples were amplified using a real-time PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific Quant Studio™ 3, Waltham, MA, USA). The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed from 65 °C to 95 °C with a 0.5 °C increment per cycle and a 0.05-second hold to determine the melting temperature. Fluorescence signals were collected during the reaction. Three technical replications were incorporated for each sample. The amplification efficiency of the primers was set to 2.

The stability of candidate reference genes

After qRT-PCR, the cycle threshold (Ct) values of each sample were obtained by analyzing the data with the qRT-PCR instrument software (Quant Studio™ Design & Analysis Software). The expression stability of each candidate reference gene was comprehensively evaluated using five approaches: the comparative ΔCt method (ΔCt method), geNorm29, NormFinder30, BestKeeper31, and RefFinder32. Specifically, the R Package ctrlGene (version 1.0.1) was used for geNorm and BestKeeper analyses. RefFinder (http://blooge.cn/RefFinder/), a user-friendly web-based comprehensive tool, was employed for integrated analysis by incorporating geNorm, BestKeeper, NormFinder, and the ΔCt method.

GeNorm ranks gene expression stability by calculating the gene expression stability value (M), where a lower M value indicates higher stability; genes with M < 1.5 are considered relatively stable. The optimal number of reference genes was determined by the paired variation (Vn/Vn+1). Generally, if the Vn;/Vn+1 value is less than 0.15, no additional reference gene is required; conversely, if this value exceeds the threshold, the (n + 1)th reference gene must be included. NormFinder also evaluates gene expression stability based on a stability value, with smaller values corresponding to higher stability. BestKeeper assesses reference gene stability by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV) and standard deviation (SD), where lower SD and CV values indicate greater stability of the candidate reference genes. RefFinder assigns appropriate weights to each gene based on their rankings from the four algorithms (geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, and the comparative ΔCt method) and then calculates the geometric mean of these weighted ranks to generate an overall stability ranking.

Evaluation of target gene expression using stable and unstable reference genes

To validate the stability of the screened reference genes, four target genes with clear functional relevance to the experimental conditions were selected: CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 (key genes involved in benzylisoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis); and CsANN1 and CsANN9 (core genes regulating plant stress responses), which are responsive to different tissues and abiotic stresses. cDNA templates were prepared from various tissues of C. saxicola, roots under Ca²+ stress, leaves under Se²+ stress, and leaves under drought stress. Using qRT-PCR, the optimal candidate reference genes and their combinations were used as normalization factors. For the two-gene combination, joint normalization was performed by calculating the geometric mean29. Meanwhile, unstable reference genes were used for comparison. The relative expression levels of these target genes in C. saxicola were then analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

All experiments were repeated three times, following the reaction system and procedures described in Section “qRT-PCR Reaction Conditions”. The experimental data were analyzed with Excel 2021 and IBM SPSS Statistics 22, while Excel was employed for graph plotting. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) among variable treatments.

Results

Assessment of total RNA quality

Total RNA was extracted from various tissues and stress-treated samples of C. saxicola using the MolPure Plant RNA Kit, followed by analysis via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The agarose gel electrophoresis results (Supplementary Fig. S2) revealed distinct and sharp 28 S and 18 S rRNA bands across all samples, demonstrating that the extracted RNA retained excellent integrity without significant degradation. The nucleic acid purity ratios for all samples were provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Determination of primer specificity

PCR amplification was conducted using equal quantities of mixed cDNA as the template (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S3). Meanwhile, the expression abundance and stability of the 11 candidate reference genes were visualized using an RNA-seq-derived FPKM heatmap (Fig. 1B), which intuitively presented their expression profiles across different tissues and Ca²+ stress treatments. All primers successfully targeted fragments, producing distinct and prominent bands without the presence of primer dimers or nonspecific amplification products. The sizes of the bands were in accordance with the anticipated values. The specificity of the primers was confirmed through qRT-PCR technology (Fig. 2). The melting curves for each gene displayed a single melting peak, demonstrating that these primers are capable of specific amplification. Consequently, these primers demonstrate high specificity and efficiency in amplifying the candidate genes, indicating their suitability for use in subsequent experiments.

Expression stability analysis of selected reference genes under different conditions

The raw Ct values of the 11 candidate reference genes are presented in Supplementary Supplementary Table S3 and visualized in a boxplot (Fig. 3). Notably, some genes showed consistent expression across all experimental conditions, while others exhibited marked variability, highlighting the importance of validating reference gene stability in specific experimental contexts. Boxplot analysis revealed substantial differences in expression stability among the candidate genes across different tissues and under Ca²+, Se²+, and drought stresses, as indicated by variations in the interquartile ranges. A comprehensive preliminary stability assessment identified GAPDH2 and UBCE2-17 as the most stable reference genes across different tissues(Fig. 3B), RPAP3 and UBCE2-5 A as the top stable genes under Ca²+ stress(Fig. 3C), GAPDH8 and α-TUB under Se²+ stress(Fig. 3D), and ACT4 and RPAP3 under drought stress (Fig. 3E). For the global group (all samples), GAPDH2, TBP, and UBCE2-17 displayed superior overall stability(Fig. 3A).

In addition to using Ct values to assess the stability of gene expression, we applied established statistical tools such as BestKeeper, NormFinder, and GeNorm for a thorough data analysis. This approach was employed to validate our results and enhance our understanding of the dataset. By leveraging these statistical methods, we evaluated the stability of reference genes across different experimental conditions, offering crucial guidance for the selection of the most suitable reference genes to ensure precise gene expression analyses.

geNorm analysis

The expression stability values (M values) of candidate reference genes in different tissues and under different stresses were calculated using the geNorm program. This program uses a threshold of M = 1.5, where lower M values indicate higher stability of reference genes. The geNorm analyses are shown in Fig. 4. Comprehensive analysis of the expression levels of 11 candidate reference genes across all samples revealed that all genes, except GAPDH8 (M > 1.5), exhibited M values below 1.5. In various tissues, UBCE2-17 and UEV1C demonstrated the most stable expression. Under Ca2+ stress, GAPDH2 and UBCE2-5 A exhibited the highest stability. In response to Se2+ stress, α-TUB and GAPDH8 showed the greatest stability. During drought stress, ACT4 and RPAP3 were identified as the most consistently expressed genes.

Determining the optimal number of reference genes can reduce bias and fluctuations caused by a single reference gene. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the V2/3 for all samples of C. saxicola was 0.17, exceeding the critical threshold of 0.15, while the V3/4 values were below 0.15. This indicates that three reference genes are required for normalization across all samples to ensure stable and reliable results. Conversely, when examining different tissues, as well as Ca²+, Se²+, and drought stress conditions, the V2/3 values for C. saxicola were recorded at 0.12, 0.082, 0.031, and 0.087, respectively—all of which were below 0.15. These results imply that the selection of two reference genes is adequate for achieving stable and reliable normalization under these specific conditions.

BestKeeper analysis

BestKeeper evaluates gene stability primarily by comparing the standard deviation (SD) values among the Ct values of candidate reference genes. The results of the BestKeeper analysis are shown in Table 2. This indicates that GAPDH2 (0.979) is the most stable gene across all samples, while GAPDH8 (2.466) is the least stable. In various tissues, UBCE2-5 A emerged as the most stable reference gene, while ACT1 demonstrated the least stability. During Ca²+ stress, ACT4 displayed the greatest expression stability. To assess the reliability of the selected reference genes, the most stable and least stable reference genes identified by the RefFinder program were utilized as normalization factors. Notably, dehydrocavidine is the primary bioactive component of C. saxicola, and CsBBELs and CsOMTs are well-recognized as key enzyme families mediating dehydrocavidine biosynthesis in this species. The ANN genes encode a family of evolutionarily highly conserved, Ca²+-dependent water-soluble phospholipid-binding proteins known as annexins (ANNs), which act as critical regulators of plant responses to harsh environments—particularly well-suited to the karst high-calcium, arid, and nutrient-poor habitats where C. saxicola thrives. Therefore, CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 (representative members of the CsBBELs and CsOMTs families, respectively) were selected as target genes, alongside CsANN1 and CsANN9, to independently validate their expression patterns and comprehensively assess the impact of reference gene selection on qRT-PCR quantification results.

In various tissues, the most stable reference genes (GAPDH2 and UBCE2-17) revealed that CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 exhibited the highest expression levels in stems and flowers, respectively. In contrast, when the least stable reference gene (ACT1) was used for normalization, both CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 showed peak expression in stems, with a 10.5–31.5-fold difference in their expression levels (Fig. 6A, B). CsANN1 and CsANN9 also displayed the highest expression in stems; however, the use of stable versus unstable reference genes resulted in a 3.98–30.99-fold difference in their expression levels, leading to obvious quantitative biases (Fig. 6C, D).

Under Ca²+ stress, the expression levels of CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease with rising Ca²+ concentrations, peaking at Ca5 when normalized to the most stable reference genes (RPAP3 and UBCE2-5 A). In contrast, when the least stable reference gene (α-TUB) was used, both CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 showed peak expression at Ca25 (Fig. 6E, F). For CsANN1 and CsANN9, normalization to the most stable reference genes (RPAP3 and UBCE2-5 A) revealed a continuous increase in expression with rising Ca²+ concentrations, peaking at Ca50 (Fig. 6G, H). However, when the least stable reference gene (α-TUB) was employed, the expression levels of CsANN1 and CsANN9 displayed an initial increase followed by a decrease, peaking at Ca25—this pattern deviates from the actual trends of gene expression and metabolite accumulation.

Under Se²+ stress, normalization to the stable reference genes (GAPDH8 and α-TUB) showed that the relative expression of CsBBEL9 exhibited an initial decrease followed by an increase with rising Se²+ concentrations, peaking at Se50, while CsOMT9 displayed a continuous increase in expression, also peaking at Se50. In contrast, when the unstable reference gene (RPAP3) was used, both CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 showed a similar pattern of initial decrease followed by increase, but their expression levels remained consistently low (Fig. 6I, J). For CsANN1 and CsANN9, the expression patterns were consistent when using stable versus unstable reference genes, differing only in the magnitude of expression.

Under drought stress, normalization to the stable reference genes (ACT4 and RPAP3) showed that the expression levels of CsBBEL9 exhibited an initial decrease followed by an increase, peaking at 18 days of drought treatment. In contrast, when the unstable reference gene (GAPDH8) was used, a similar trend was observed, but no significant differences in expression levels were detected across time points. For CsOMT9, analysis with the stable reference genes (ACT4 and RPAP3) revealed an expression pattern of initial decline, subsequent increase, and final decrease over the drought treatment period; analysis with the unstable reference gene (GAPDH8) yielded a comparable trend (Fig. 6M, N). For CsANN1 and CsANN9, normalization to the stable reference genes (ACT4 and RPAP3) showed that their expression levels both exhibited an initial decrease followed by an increase, peaking at 12 days of drought treatment, indicating a clear response to drought stress. When the unstable reference gene (GAPDH8) was used, the expression patterns remained consistent with those observed using stable reference genes, differing only in the magnitude of expression.

Collectively, the selection of reference genes significantly impacts the quantification of target gene expression levels. Both the enzyme family genes mediating dehydrocavidine biosynthesis and the core ANNs family genes regulating this metabolic process are directly affected by the stability of the reference genes used for qRT-PCR normalization. The selection of appropriate reference genes is critical for obtaining accurate qRT-PCR results, whereas the use of unstable reference genes can lead to inconsistent findings and even deviations from the actual patterns of gene expression and metabolic regulation.

Discussion

Research into the biosynthesis and regulation of secondary metabolites in medicinal plants offers significant potential for generating genetic resources that can enhance the breeding of high-quality germplasm and support effective quality control measures for herbal medicines. It is commonly observed that the expression levels of key enzyme genes involved in secondary metabolic pathways positively correlate with the accumulation of secondary metabolites across various plant tissues34–37. Consequently, accurately quantifying target gene expression using techniques such as qRT-PCR is a crucial step in the identification and characterization of these genes. While the expression of reference genes typically remains stable under normal physiological conditions and is vital for essential cellular processes, their transcriptional levels can vary significantly under specific circumstances, such as environmental stress responses22,38,39 or different developmental stages22,40. Therefore, selecting reference genes with stable expression is critical for ensuring the reliability of target gene expression analyses. This study identified a set of putatively stable candidate reference genes through transcriptomic database mining. These genes, including GAPDH2, GAPDH8, TBP, UBCE2-17, UEV1C, UBCE2-5 A, ACT1, ACT4, β-TUB, α-TUB, and RPAP3, have been validated as stable reference genes in plants41–44. Their expression stability was systematically assessed across seven distinct tissue types and under specific abiotic stress conditions, including Ca2+ stress, Se2+ stress, and drought stress.

This study began by assessing the stability of housekeeping genes through descriptive statistical methods, including the calculation of mean Ct values and standard deviation analyses. The findings indicated that GAPDH2 demonstrated the highest expression stability across all samples, with a mean Ct value of 20.68 ± 1.33, while RPAP3 exhibited the lowest expression level (mean Ct = 28.56 ± 1.61). Although evaluating gene stability using mean Ct values and standard deviations is a straightforward approach, its utility is somewhat restricted due to inherent limitations in accounting for biological and technical variations45. Wang et al.44 identified GAPDH and TBP as the most reliable reference genes for qRT-PCR normalization in Nelumbo nucifera, showing consistency across various tissues and developmental stages. Similarly, in the traditional Chinese medicinal plant Dracocephalum moldavica, the GAPDH gene was recognized as the stable reference gene under PEG6000 treatment46. A prior meta-analysis indicated that GAPDH is the preferred reference gene in over 90% of high-impact journal publications47, a finding that aligns well with the results of the current study.

The ideal reference gene should exhibit minimal transcriptional variation across various experimental conditions. However, empirical investigations across different crop species have identified significant inconsistencies in reference gene expression profiles among distinct tissues, developmental stages, and stress conditions43. For instance, variations in the expression of standard reference genes have been observed in Solanum tuberosum under drought, cold, and salinity stress48; in Solanum lycopersicum experiencing nitrogen deficiency, cold, and light treatments49; and in Helianthus annuus throughout six leaf developmental stages50. These observations highlight the urgent need to validate reference gene stability for specific experimental contexts to ensure the reliability of plant gene expression analyses. The results of this study indicate that no universally stable reference genes exist across various tissues and abiotic stress conditions, emphasizing the lack of a one-size-fits-all reference gene panel for environmental stress research. This finding is crucial as it reinforces the importance of context-specific validation of reference genes. In light of the limited availability of validated reference genes in C. saxicola research, this study serves as a valuable resource for the scientific community. Our research provides essential insights into the selection of robust reference genes for C. saxicola under varying stress conditions, which is vital for accurate gene expression analysis and the development of high-yielding medicinal varieties.

Conventional normalization methods that depend on a single reference gene may undermine the precision of standardization outcomes29,51. This highlights the essential need for thorough validation of reference genes and the adoption of advanced statistical techniques to determine the optimal number of reference genes necessary for accurate gene expression normalization. Notably, single-gene normalization is inadequate for accurate qRT-PCR analysis. Thus, geNorm was used to determine the optimal number of reference genes for different tissues and abiotic stress conditions of C. saxicola (to develop context-specific reference gene panels), based on its pairwise variation values (Vn/Vn+1) with a general cutoff of 0.15—this also served as further quantitative validation of the screened stable reference genes. For different tissues and under Ca²+/Se²+ stress, the V2/3 values were all below 0.15, meaning normalization with two highly stable reference genes sufficed for quantification requirements. Under these conditions, plant gene expression fluctuated mildly, and a two-gene combination effectively offset normalization bias and avoided the inherent limitations of single-gene normalization52. In contrast, the V2/3 value under drought stress was slightly above 0.15, while V3/4 fell below the threshold after adding a third reference gene. This was attributable to drought as a persistent abiotic stress, which leads to a more complex plant gene expression regulatory network with more significant fluctuations53, thus requiring multiple reference genes to ensure quantification reliability and further confirming the condition specificity of the optimal reference gene number. The optimal reference gene combinations for all experimental conditions in this study were centered on highly stable genes including GAPDH2, TBP and UBCE2-17. This strategy clarified the rational number of reference genes to use, avoided experimental redundancy from excessive genes, and balanced quantification accuracy with experimental feasibility. In this investigation, three bioinformatics tools—geNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper—were utilized to evaluate the stability of candidate genes, revealing minor variations in stability rankings due to the differing computational algorithms and statistical methodologies employed by each program54,55. Specifically, geNorm identified TBP and UBCE2-17 as the most stable reference genes across all sample sets, while NormFinder ranked GAPDH2 and UEV1C as the leading candidates. BestKeeper confirmed GAPDH2 and UBCE2-17 as the most consistently expressed genes. Notably, GAPDH2 consistently ranked among the top three in stability across all analytical software, positioning it as a relatively optimal reference gene for qRT-PCR analysis in C. saxicola. The results of this study are consistent with previous research that emphasizes the importance of selecting reference genes based on specific contexts. The variability inherent in different algorithmic methods can lead to inconsistent results when analyzing the same datasets, as demonstrated by conflicting findings across various species such as Papaver somniferum56, Angelica dahurica57, Panax ginseng58,59, Glycyrrhiza60, and Ludisia discolor55, which were assessed using different analytical frameworks. To address the potential bias associated with using a single tool, we employed RefFinder, a well-established integrative platform for evaluating reference genes, to consolidate the findings from three distinct programs. Our thorough analysis identified GAPDH2, TBP, and UBCE2-17 as the most stable reference genes, while α-TUB, ACT1, and GAPDH8 showed the least expression consistency.

This study assessed the stability of candidate reference genes using four algorithms (geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, and RefFinder), thus offering multi-dimensional validation to ensure result reliability. Notably, minor inconsistencies existed in the stability rankings across algorithms: GAPDH2 ranked highly in RefFinder (Table 4) but relatively lower in geNorm alone (Fig. 4). The fundamental cause of these discrepancies stems from inherent differences in how each algorithm defines “gene expression stability” through its statistical weighting scheme61,62. GeNorm prioritizes the consistency of expression ratios between gene pairs, with a stronger focus on relative co-fluctuation across genes29. In this study, the expression ratio of UBCE2-17 to UEV1C remained stable across all tissues of C. saxicola, leading geNorm to classify them as highly stable (Fig. 4B). However, this algorithm assigns relatively low weight to systematic inter-group differences. In contrast, NormFinder assigns equal weight to intra-group variation and inter-group differences, enabling effective identification of “pseudo-stable genes” with substantial inter-group variation30. Specifically, under Ca²+ stress, TBP ranked moderately (6th place, Fig. 4C) in geNorm due to non-extreme relative fluctuations with other genes; NormFinder, however, detected significant expression differences between the control group and various Ca²+ concentration treatments—with inter-group variation being a major contributor—resulting in a much lower stability ranking for TBP (10th place, Table 3). BestKeeper relies exclusively on the absolute fluctuation of Ct values (assessed via SD or CV) as its weighting criterion, emphasizing the expression dispersion of individual genes31. For instance, under drought stress, GAPDH2 exhibited the smallest Ct value variation across all samples (Fig. 3E), making it the top-recommended stable gene by BestKeeper. This approach, however, disregards inter-gene correlations and systematic inter-group differences. RefFinder integrates the ranking results of the four aforementioned algorithms via geometric mean calculation, balancing their respective strengths and limitations to generate a more comprehensive overall ranking32. These differences in weighting schemes result in divergent stability assessments of the same gene across algorithms. When selecting a combination of multiple reference genes, geNorm’s V-value analysis is recommended as the priority; in cases where significant inter-group differences exist, NormFinder’s results are more informative.

Previous research has indicated that the concentration of dehydrocavidine in the roots of C. saxicola is greater than that in the stems8, with this variation linked to the stages of plant growth and development63. In the current study, the expression level of CsBBEL9 in the stems was found to be 1.32 times higher than in the roots, while the expression of CsOMT9 in the stems was reduced by 86.57% compared to the roots. However, when normalization was performed using unstable reference genes, the expression levels of CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9 appeared to be abnormally high. Additionally, it has been shown that appropriate levels of calcium and selenium ion stress can enhance dehydrocavidine accumulation in C. saxicola8. In line with these findings, treatments with Ca5 and Se50 were observed to upregulate the expression of CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9. Nevertheless, normalization with unstable genes resulted in lower expression levels of CsBBEL9 and CsOMT9, highlighting the significant influence of using unsuitable reference genes.

In conclusion, the reference genes identified in this study are suitable for gene expression analysis in C. saxicola across multiple organs and various abiotic stress conditions. We advise utilizing these reference genes in qRT-PCR experiments involving C. saxicola to prevent any potential inaccuracies.

Conclusion

Understanding the molecular mechanisms that regulate the biosynthesis of secondary metabolism in C. saxicola has increasingly relied on functional genomics, particularly transcriptomics. To date, although extensive transcriptomic data for C. saxicola exist, a thorough analysis of large-scale gene expression datasets to identify stable reference genes has not been performed. This study aims to identify potential housekeeping gene candidates that can act as reliable reference genes for quantitative gene expression analysis. To evaluate the validity and stability of these reference genes comprehensively, we employed four different tools to assess their expression stability across various tissues and under diverse abiotic stress conditions. We ranked the known reference genes based on their stability, revealing that the combination of GAPDH2, TBP, and UBCE2-17 is appropriate for normalizing gene expression data across all C. saxicola samples. Additionally, we found ACT1 and GAPDH8 to be the least stable among the candidate genes. Importantly, results from the same tool showed inconsistencies in identifying stable reference genes across samples subjected to different treatments. Therefore, we recommend selecting stable reference genes tailored to the specific treatment conditions of the samples for normalizing gene expression in C. saxicola, with the use of at least two highly stable reference genes. The reference genes identified in this study not only enhance the reliability of gene expression analyses but also support functional genomics and metabolomics research related to abiotic stress tolerance in C. saxicola.
