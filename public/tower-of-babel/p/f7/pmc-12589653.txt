Utility of clavicle cortex index as a screening tool for low bone mineral density
Authors: Yusuke Dodo, Ichiro Okano, Koki Tsuchiya, Kotaro Fujita, Joji Ogawa, Chikara Hayakawa, Ryo Yamamura, Yoshifumi Kudo, Katsunori Inagaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective study examined the relationship between the clavicle cortical index (CCI) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) bone mineral density (BMD). Data from patients undergoing elective spine and hip surgeries were reviewed, focusing on the bilateral clavicles on chest radiographs. Clavicles were divided into distal, middle-distal, middle-proximal, and proximal. Cortical and trabecular thicknesses were measured in these sections, and the CCI was calculated as (cortical + trabecular)/trabecular bone. The Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the CCI and BMDs in the proximal femur, lumbar spine, and distal radius. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the predictive value of the CCI for osteoporosis (BMD < 70%) and Hounsfield units. The study included 219 patients (mean age, 68.03 years). Mild but significant correlations were found between the bilateral middle-distal CCI and BMD in the radius (r = 0.409), with weaker correlations in the femur (r = 0.313) and spine (r = 0.209). ROC analyses for osteoporosis diagnosis were moderately accurate: 0.69 (0.55–0.83, femur), 0.716 (0.57–0.861, spine), and 0.834 (0.737–0.931, radius). The area under the curve for the Hounsfield units was 0.74 (0.55–0.89). The middle-distal CCI showed a correlation with BMDs, especially in the radius. Introduction

Low bone mineral density (BMD) is a risk factor for mechanical complications after spinal and hip surgery. Currently, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is the gold standard for evaluating BMD1,2. DXA measurements are simple, highly reproducible, and have a strong correlation with fracture risk; hence, they are widely recommended by clinical guidelines and have become the accepted standard for managing patients with osteoporosis3. However, DXA scanning is not always readily available, and mass screening for osteoporosis using DXA has yet to be implemented in most countries.

Recent studies have explored the retrospective use of computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and radiography to assess bone mineral density (BMD). Opportunistic screening for osteoporosis, using Hounsfield units (HU) calculated from abdominal CT scans obtained for other purposes, was first reported in 20134. Subsequent research has included CT-based HU measurements5–7, Quantitative CT7–9, MRI-based Vertebral Bone Quality10,11, and radiography12–14 to evaluate BMD. The deltoid tuberosity index (DTI), which is the ratio of the medullary part to the cortical bone of the humeral deltoid tuberosity, is also important. DTI can be easily calculated using only an anteroposterior radiograph and has demonstrated a strong correlation with local BMD in the humerus12. Additionally, various parameters of the humerus on anteroposterior radiographs are reportedly well-associated with BMD, as measured by DXA at different anatomical locations, including the distal radius, proximal femur, and lumbar spine14.

In this study, we focused on chest radiography, one of the most commonly performed imaging tests that is readily available in most medical facilities. We measured the cortical/trabecular ratio of the clavicles, which are less susceptible to age-related degeneration and are easily identifiable on chest radiography. We investigated the relationship between the clavicle cortical bone index (CCI) and BMD calculated using DXA and assessed the diagnostic performance of this index in predicting osteoporosis among patients undergoing spine and hip surgeries.

Methods

Study population and bone density measurement

We retrospectively reviewed the records of patients who underwent elective spine and hip surgeries at our center between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2021. According to the institutional protocol, all patients undergoing elective hip or spinal surgery underwent a preoperative DXA scan (Discovery; Toyo Medic Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) as part of the routine diagnostic workup. Additionally, nearly all patients who underwent elective spinal surgery also underwent lumbar CT (SOMATOM Force; Siemens, Munich, Germany) for preoperative surgical planning. HU values from lumbar CT were assessed using an elliptical region of interest (ROI) in the trabecular bone on the axial plane. For each vertebra, three axial slices (upper 1/4, middle, and lower 1/4) were selected, and the mean values of all three measurements were computed. An elliptical cylindrical ROI was used for all HU measurements, following the method described by Schreiber et al.7.

CCI measurement

Chest anteroposterior (AP) radiographs were used for clavicle cortex measurements. Because clavicle morphology is sensitive to patient posture, we carefully screened for conditions that could distort clavicle appearance. The positions of the proximal and distal clavicle were examined: normally, the proximal end overlies the fourth rib, and the distal end aligns with the level of the first rib. When either end deviated from these expected positions, additional imaging studies (e.g., spine radiographs) were reviewed to determine the cause. Patients in whom sagittal or coronal spinal imbalance or scoliosis was confirmed were excluded from the analysis.

The clavicle is composed of trabecular bone encased in cortical bone, and the middle three-fifths of the clavicle is much thicker than the outer and inner fifths15. The shape of the clavicle exhibits a double curvature, with a convex anterior curve in the medial half and a concave anterior curve in the lateral half16,17. The bilateral clavicles visible on standard anteroposterior chest radiographs were divided into four equal portions: A (distal), B (middle-distal), C (middle-proximal), and D (proximal). The measurement points using quintile points of the bone were described anatomically as follows: A is a concave curve where the medial side of the trapezoid ligament and the lateral edge of the trapezius and deltoid muscles are attached. B is the lateral junction of the convex and concave anterior curves where the conoid ligament and medial edge of the trapezius and deltoid muscles are attached. C is a convex anterior curve, where the lateral edge of the pectoralis major muscle is attached. D is a convex anterior curve where the costoclavicular ligament and sternocleidomastoid muscle are attached. All radiographic assessments were performed using a picture archiving and communication system (INFINITT PACS, INFINITT Healthcare Co., Ltd.) and the measurements were performed using command “R” functions to calculate the distances as described in the software manual.

To assess interobserver reliability, two trained researchers independently measured CCIs in 29 randomly selected patients. Both researchers were anonymized to the other measurements.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are shown as means ± standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables are summarized as counts (%). Comparisons between two continuous groups were made using the Student t test. For comparisons between two categorical variables, the chi-squared or the Fisher exact test was used, where appropriate. The Smirnov-Grubbs test was used to detect the outliers. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to compare the CCI at each point, unilateral and bilateral average values, and BMDs of the proximal femur, lumbar spine, and distal radius. Multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted using BMDs in each anatomical region as the outcome variable and CCI as the explanatory variable. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive value of CCI for diagnosing osteoporosis, defined as a BMD < 70% of the young adult mean value. Additionally, the predictive value of CCI was compared using ROC curve analysis with the HU cutoff value at the L1 vertebra, as defined by Schreiber et al.7 For assessing interobserver reliability, the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement (ICC 2.1) was calculated between two independent raters. According to the criteria set by Landis and Koch, ICC values of < 0.20, 0.20–0.40, 0.40–0.60, 0.60–0.80, and > 0.80 signify poor, fair, moderate, good, and excellent reliability, respectively18. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This retrospective study (Level of evidence: III) was approved by the Review Board of Showa University Hospital (No.22-304-B) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Because of the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for informed consent from each patient was waived by the Review Board of Showa University Hospital.

Results

Demographic data

A total of 219 adult patients were included in the final analysis (134 females and 85 males; mean age, 68.0 years). Seventy-four patients were excluded from the analysis for the following reasons: incomplete visualization of the full clavicle (n = 2); duplicate radiographs (n = 28); absence of DXA data (n = 36); use of mobile radiography systems (n = 36); sagittal imbalance of the spine identified on imaging (n = 6); and scoliosis confirmed on imaging (n = 1). Left clavicle measurements were unmeasurable due to invisibility (n = 18) and post-fracture changes (n = 1).

The proportion of females was higher among patients who underwent hip surgery was higher (83%). In patients who underwent spine surgery, lumbar BMD was the highest among the measured body regions (1.06 g/cm3). Conversely, BMD in the radius was the lowest in patients who underwent both hip (0.60 g/cm3) and spine (0.64 g/cm3) surgeries (Table 1).

Association between CCI and BMDs in various regions

CCIs were calculated at eight points (right clavicle, A–D; left clavicle, E–H). Bilateral middle-distal CCIs (B and G) showed high correlations with BMDs in the femur (B: r = 0.272, P < 0.001, G: r = 0.284, P < 0.001), spine (B: r = 0.171, P = 0.013, G: r = 0.194, P = 0.006), and radius (B: r = 0.292, P < 0.001, G: r = 0.390, P < 0.001) (Table 2). Positive correlations were observed between the average values of the middle-distal CCIs (B and G) and BMDs in the femur (r = 0.313, P < 0.001), spine (r = 0.209, P < 0.001), and radius (r = 0.409, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Among females, middle-distal CCIs showed the highest correlation with BMDs at all measured body regions: femur BMD (r = 0.333, P < 0.001), spine BMD (r = 0.280, P = 0.002), and radius BMD (r = 0.630, P < 0.001) (Table 2). The specific mean and standard deviation values for the CCI at the middle-distal points (B and G) for the overall population, and sex are shown (Table 3). No significant differences were seen at middle distal CCI (B and G) between females and males.

After adjusting for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and type of surgery (spine or hip), middle-distal CCI (B and G) was identified as an independent predictor of BMDs in the spine (β = 0.31, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.1–0.53, P < 0.001) and radius (β = 0.16, 95% CI 0.04–0.25, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Association between CCI and lumbar HU

Among the 219 patients, spine CT scans were available for 104 cases (52 females and 52 males), and the HU values were calculated. Lumbar HU showed significant correlations with femur and radius BMDs (r = 0.536, P < 0.001), while the correlation with lumbar BMD was relatively lower (r = 0.343, P < 0.001). In comparisons between HU values and CCIs, significant correlations were observed at nearly all sites, generally stronger than those with DXA BMDs. When analyzed by biological sex, female patients showed higher correlations (average CCI for B + G: r = 0.794, P < 0.001) than male patients (Table 4).

ROC analysis

ROC analyses were conducted to assess the diagnostic value of middle-distal CCI for osteoporosis screening. Regarding the sensitivity and specificity of CCI for the diagnosis of osteoporosis defined with the BMD < 70% of the young adult mean (YAM), the area under the curve (AUC) of the femur was 0.69 with a sensitivity of 64.6% and specificity of 75.0% at a cutoff CCI = 1.58. For the lumbar spine, the AUC was 0.72 with a sensitivity of 56.0% and specificity of 83.3% at a cutoff CCI of 1.60. For the radius, the AUC reached 0.834, with a sensitivity of 68.4% and specificity of 100% at the cutoff CCI = 1.54 (Fig. 3a). Further ROC analyses assessing the correlation with HU revealed an AUC of 0.74 at the cutoff CCI = 1.63 (sensitivity = 54.5%, specificity = 89.3%). (Fig. 3b).

Interobserver reliability

The ICCs varied across the measurement points, ranging from 0.4 to 0.8 (Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, the ICC for the averaged values at points B and G was 0.74 (95% CI 0.40–0.89), which is classified as ‘good’ according to Landis and Koch’s criteria18.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the CCI can be easily calculated using standard chest radiography. Notably, the middle-distal CCI was significantly correlated with BMD measured using DXA, with these associations being particularly pronounced in women. In addition, ROC analysis showed that the middle-distal CCI correlated well with the distal radius BMD in distinguishing osteoporosis, suggesting its potential as a screening tool. Additionally, multivariable analyses, performed by setting BMDs at each anatomical region as the outcome variables and adjusted for factors including age, sex, and BMI, showed that middle-distal CCI was an independent determinant of distal radius BMD.

The clavicle, with its transversal shape, contributes to the stability of the shoulder girdle and enhances the mobility of the upper extremity19. The clavicles are mostly visible on standard chest radiography, which is probably the most frequently performed imaging modality worldwide. In several hospitals, chest radiography is a routine part of the preoperative assessment of surgical candidates undergoing general anesthesia. According to a report from the Netherlands, chest radiographs were conducted at a rate of 130 per 1000 persons annually between 1999 and 200320. This high frequency of imaging presents numerous opportunities for screening osteoporosis.

In our study, CCI was correlated with BMDs at the femur, lumbar vertebrae, and distal radius. Interestingly, among all eight calculated points (A–H), the middle-distal CCI (B and G) showed the highest correlations. These middle-distal points typically do not overlap with other bones, such as the ribs and scapula, and are also free from lung interference. Furthermore, the cortical bone at these points is thicker than that at the distal ends, facilitating easier detection. A recent study explored the correlations between radiographic cortical/trabecular ratios and local BMD, introducing the DTI, which specifies a measurement location where the outer cortical lines of the humerus become parallel12,14,21,22. According to the DTI definition, two requirements should be fulfilled for good cortical index calculation: the cortex must be clearly visible, and the bilateral cortical bones must be parallel. Although this measurement was conducted on a different bone, the middle-distal points of the CCI measurement satisfied both conditions, unlike the other measurement points.

Another noteworthy observation was the better two-rater agreement for each measurement on the right side compared with that on the left. This finding suggests that differentiating between cortical and trabecular bone is more challenging on the left side in some cases. One potential explanation for this discrepancy is hand dominance; the clavicle on the dominant side, typically the right, tends to have a more visibly defined cortex than that on the left. Several studies have reported that hand dominance can influence the BMDs of the upper extremities. For instance, a small study demonstrated that BMD measurements on the dominant side of the forearm were higher than those on the nondominant side23. While information on hand dominance was not included in our study’s database, it is reasonable to assume that the right hand was the dominant side for most patients, which may have contributed to the higher agreement in measurements on the right side. Therefore, if the CCI measurement is to be conducted on only one side, the right side is recommended.

Our results revealed significant sex-related differences in several analyses. The clavicle, known for its high variability in shape, tends to be thicker and more curved in muscular male individuals compared to female ones17,19. This anatomical variation may have influenced the observed sex differences in the association between CCI and BMD. Additionally, osteoporosis is usually prevalent in old postmenopausal women24 and fewer male patients met the criteria for osteoporosis in this study. This discrepancy likely resulted in lower statistical power for the male group, preventing averaging out of individual variations as effectively as in female patients. Moreover, the current diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis, which were originally developed based on data from women, are still under debate regarding their applicability in assessing fracture risk in males. While our results support the use of CCI in female patients, further research is required to determine whether modifications are necessary to apply CCI to male patients accurately.

In our ROC analysis, the correlation coefficients for middle-distal CCI values with BMD were notably strong in the radius (AUC = 0.834), whereas correlations with spine and femur BMDs were comparatively weaker (spine: AUC = 0.716; femur: AUC = 0.690). Multiple indices have been used to assess bone strength. Tingard measurement is one of the indices, and studies demonstrated that diagnosis of osteoporosis defined with the BMD < 80% YAM, AUC was 0.87 with a sensitivity of 88.0% and specificity of 80.0% at the cutoff = 1.4412. Additionally, a study showed that the Deltoid Tuberosity Index (DTI) demonstrated that the diagnosis of osteoporosis defined with the BMD < 80% YAM, AUC was 0.83 with a sensitivity of 81.0% and specificity of 85.0% at the cutoff = 5.312. These studies were not the same as our study, showing that the AUC reached 0.834, with a sensitivity of 68.4% and specificity of 100% in ROC analysis. The CCI seems to be similar to these indices. However, the clavicle can be observed on chest radiographs, which are one of the most common and inexpensive radiographs worldwide. In this context, evaluation of the clavicle CCI may be a useful screening tool for osteoporosis. A previous study on opportunistic osteoporosis screening reported that CT attenuation values corresponded to DXA T scores. Patients with a normal DXA T-score (>-1) could still exhibit osteoporotic compression fractures4, highlighting the inherent limitations of DXA-BMD. Moreover, BMDs can be overestimated in certain body parts owing to degenerative changes. Given that our study population primarily consisted of patients who underwent hip or spine surgeries, most of whom were older than 65 years, degenerative bone changes or great vessel calcifications near the hip or spine25 might have contributed to greater measurement errors and weaker correlations with femur or spine BMD than with distal radius BMD. Conversely, the forearm, which rarely exhibited degenerative changes, may account for the stronger correlation observed. Additionally, the biomechanical environments of the radius and clavicle are similar, as both are upper extremity and non-weight-bearing bones, which might further explain the stronger correlation. Currently, spine and hip DXA scans are the standard measurements recommended in most guidelines26–28. However, a study has reported that HU obtained using CT could serve as an alternative method for assessing bone strength7. Our analysis showed that spine HU correlated better with middle-distal CCI (AUC = 0.740) than lumbar spine or hip DXA. This finding underscores the utility of vertebral trabecular HU in bone assessment, particularly in patients with degenerative changes. Vertebral trabecular HU is less affected by degenerative bony changes, such as osteophytes and facet degeneration, which likely contributes to its effectiveness in this context.

Recent advances in machine learning have propelled the field of artificial intelligence (AI) to achieve impressive breakthroughs in complex data environments where human capacities to identify high-dimensional relationships are limited. The diagnosis of osteoporosis has seen rapid developments in the application of machine learning methods, with several studies emerging in recent years. These studies report AUCs of 0.74–1.00 for osteoporosis diagnosis using radiographs, defined by DXA BMD, which appear to be higher than those achieved with CCIs (0.69–0.83). An AUC of 1.00 suggests that AI can perfectly discriminate the diagnosis of osteoporosis as determined by DXA; however, given the inherent inaccuracies of DXA itself4, one might question whether it is genuinely feasible to mimic these imperfections perfectly. It is widely recognized that AI models may overfit the data and lose generalizability. Therefore, the quality and quantity of data, extent of training, and use of a validation dataset should be carefully evaluated when considering the use of AI models29–32. A common criticism of AI-based diagnosis is that the learning process is often referred to as a “black box” since it is not intuitively understandable to ordinary people. By contrast, our results are straightforward and easily comprehensible. In addition, CCI measurement requires only a scale and radiograph, making it more universally applicable than AI-based tools.

Our results show that the potential advantages are that the calculation of the middle distal CCI is simple and easily calculated using one of the most standard and inexpensive AP chest X-rays used worldwide as a screening tool for osteoporosis. Osteoporosis is typically screened during routine health checkups using laboratory tests and imaging. The diagnosis is confirmed through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and anteroposterior (AP) and lateral spine radiographs33. However, an effective screening requires clinical awareness. Physicians must first suspect osteoporosis in a patient, as diagnostic tests, such as DXA, will not be ordered without prior suspicion. The CCI is a useful tool that can be easily calculated using routine chest radiography. Therefore, the CCI can be used for opportunistic pre-DXA screening. Because CCI is an opportunistic screening method, resource allocation specifically for CCI is only a small workload for radiologists or physicians. This is one of the strengths of the CCI. However, the CCI is not as accurate as other measurements, as discussed above, and cannot be measured in certain patients, such as those with severe spinal malalignment or those who had previously sustained fractures. In addition, the sex difference may also be a limitation in that the current diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis, which were originally developed based on data from women, are still under debate regarding their applicability in assessing fracture risk in males. The association between CCI and BMD might have been affected by other factors, such as age and BMI. However, we could not include such factors in the analysis because our study population mainly consisted of relatively homogeneous older patients (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, anatomical variations in the clavicle are likely to affect its reliability. Therefore, patients with deformities or previous fractures were excluded. CCI cannot be used in patients with spinal malalignment or clavicular deformity. The most important step should be to look at the local and surrounding anatomy, which potentially affects the CCI measurements. Lateral radiography should be performed to ensure normal spinal alignment, if available. In our study, we focused on the hips and spines of older Japanese patients. Therefore, CCI should be evaluated in terms of other ethnicities and physical activities in the future. A previous study has reported that ethnic differences in bone architecture34 and non-white populations (especially African Americans and Hispanics) are associated with increased risk of fractures35. Levels of physical activity also influence bone quality36. These points should be addressed in future research to enhance the robustness and applicability of our findings.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that middle-distal CCI is substantially associated with BMDs, especially those in the distal radius, among patients who underwent spine and hip surgeries. The CCI can be easily calculated using a routine chest radiograph, making it a practical and accessible tool for osteoporosis screening.
