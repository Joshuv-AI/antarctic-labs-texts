HSPA4 restrains transferrin in dopaminergic neurons to attenuate ferroptosis in a Parkinson’s disease model
Authors: Tong Gao, Huanhuan Wei, Qianqian Ju, Yongqi Lin, Xiang Yin, Xiaoyu Liu, Jianhong Shen, Qiuhong Ji, Cheng Sun, Lihua Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat shock protein A4 (HSPA4) is a molecular chaperone belonging to the heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) family. This study aims to investigate the antiferroptotic effects of HSPA4 in a Parkinson’s disease (PD) model and explore the underlying mechanisms. Here we show that HSPA4 overexpression reduces ferroptosis in erastin-treated SH-SY5Y cells and primary dopaminergic neurons, while HSPA4 knockdown exacerbates ferroptosis. In MPTP-induced PD model mice, HSPA4 rectifies behavioral defects, prevents the loss of dopaminergic neurons, and alleviates ferroptosis. Mechanistically, HSPA4 interacts with transferrin in the cytoplasm and inhibits its export from the cell. Consequently, extracellular iron cannot be transported into cells due to a lack of transferrin, thereby attenuating ferroptosis in dopaminergic neurons and slowing PD progression. By restraining transferrin in dopaminergic neurons, HSPA4 reduces ferroptosis and alleviates parkinsonism in a PD mouse model. Therefore, HSPA4 might present a potential therapeutic target for the development of PD treatments.Subject terms: Cell death in the nervous system, Parkinson's disease

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common aging-related neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer’s disease (AD). A clinical survey study revealed that approximately 6.1 million people suffered from PD worldwide in 20161. With the rapid increase in the aging population, an increasing number of people will be affected by this disease. Loss of dopaminergic neurons and the presence of Lewy bodies (LBs) in the midbrain are the hallmarks of PD2,3. The main PD phenotype is motor defects including rigidity, resting tremors, and bradykinesia4. Some nonmotor features, including cognitive decline, depression, and pain, often occur in PD patients4. Only 3-5% of PD cases are caused by genetic mutations, and most cases of PD are idiopathic4. Currently, no disease-modifying therapies are available that can slow down or arrest the progression of PD4,5. To date, the precise etiology of PD remains unclear; however, increasing evidence suggest that oxidative stress, toxic protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of PD6,7. Notably, ferroptosis, characterized by iron accumulation and excessive lipid peroxidation, has been implicated in the aggregation of α-synuclein, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation8. Therefore, ferroptosis has been identified as a key contributing factor to the pathogenesis of PD9,10.

HSPA4 is a molecular chaperone protein that belongs to the heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) family. Another key member of this family is HSPA5, also known as Bip or GRP78. A growing body of evidence has shown that HSPA5 exhibits antiferroptotic activity in various tumor cell types, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells, glioma cells, head and neck squamous carcinoma cells, and bladder cancer cells11–14. In contrast, the role of HSPA4 in ferroptosis has been much less explored, with only two bioinformatics studies suggesting its potential involvement in this process15,16. In addition to its antiferroptotic properties, HSPA5 has also been shown to play a beneficial role in PD. Several neuroprotective compounds, such as salidroside, luteolin, lithospermic acid, salubrinal, and ulinastatin, have been reported to confer their benefits in PD models through modulation of HSPA517–21. Moreover, a recent study showed that intranasal administration of HSPA5 provides neuroprotection in a lactacystin-induced rat model of PD22. Given the antiferroptotic and neuroprotective roles of HSPA5 in PD, along with our previous findings that HSPA4 is involved in neuroprotection in PD model mice23, we hypothesized that HSPA4 may also exert anti-PD effects by suppressing ferroptosis.

In the present study, we examined the potential role of HSPA4 in diseased PD cells and mice. Our data indicated that the overexpression of HSPA4 attenuated erastin-induced ferroptosis in SH-SY5Y cells, whereas the knockdown of HSPA4 further aggravated ferroptosis. In PD model mice, increased expression of HSPA4 in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) alleviated MPTP-induced parkinsonism. Our subsequent experiments revealed that HSPA4 interacts with transferrin in the cytoplasm and thereby prevents export of transferrin from dopaminergic neurons. As a result, the extracellular levels of transferrin were decreased, which impeded the import of iron and subsequent ferroptosis. In this way, HSPA4 prevented the loss of dopaminergic neurons in PD model mice and thereby alleviated PD symptoms.

Results

HSPA4 was reduced by erastin in SH-SY5Y cells

To examine the potential role of HSPA4 in ferroptosis, we first analyzed whether HSPA4 expression was affected in ferroptosis. To this end, SH-SY5Y cells were treated with erastin to induce ferroptosis. The results showed that cell viability gradually decreased with increasing concentrations of erastin (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Moreover, we observed that both the mRNA and protein levels of HSPA4 changed in a similar pattern (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). In addition, our immunofluorescence data further confirmed the reduced expression of HSPA4 in erastin-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Together, these data indicate that HSPA4 expression is decreased during ferroptosis, suggesting that it is involved in the progression of ferroptosis-related diseases.

Overexpression of HSPA4 attenuates erastin-induced insults in SH-SY5Y cells

Next, we examined whether HSPA4 affects ferroptosis. To this end, SH-SY5Y cells were transfected with a plasmid expressing Hspa4, and then, the cells were treated with erastin to induce ferroptosis. HSPA4 expression was markedly increased by transfection (Fig. 1A). The examination of cell morphology revealed that erastin induced bright- and round-shaped cells and reduced the number of cells (Fig. 1B), indicating that ferroptosis was occurred in erastin-treated cells. In accordance with the changes in cell morphology, cell viability was decreased by erastin; however, in HSPA4-overexpressing cells, this decline was partially counteracted (Fig. 1C). The expression of HSPA4 increased in the HSPA4-overexpressing cells (Fig. 1D). The erastin-induced release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was attenuated by HSPA4 (Fig. 1E). Because mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of ferroptosis24, we then examined the mitochondrial membrane potential and oxidative stress. Our data showed that the decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential in erastin-treated cells was largely prevented by HSPA4 (Fig. 1F). When newly synthesized, MitoTimer encodes a mitochondron-targeted green fluorescent protein, which shifts irreversibly to red fluorescence when oxidized25. Therefore, it is widely used to evaluate oxidative stress in mitochondria. As shown in Fig. 1G, erastin induced oxidative stress in mitochondria, whereas HSPA4 repressed this effect. To reinforce these findings, we measured lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) using the BODIPY (581/591) C11 probe. The results revealed a significant increase in green fluorescence in erastin-treated cells, which was largely prevented by HSPA4 overexpression, indicating that HSPA4 effectively attenuates erastin-induced lipid oxidation (Fig. 2A, B). To further confirm these findings, we prepared primary dopaminergic neurons and examined the protective effects of HSPA4. Similarly, overexpression of HSPA4 alleviated erastin-induced cell insults as evidenced by reduced lipid ROS generation and decreased LDH activity (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). The reduction in the level of intracellular glutathione (GSH) in erastin-treated cells was increased by HSPA4, whereas the increases in the levels malondialdehyde (MDA) and Fe2+ were largely counteracted by HSPA4 (Fig. 2C–E). The protein levels of GPX4, FTH1, and xCT were dramatically decreased by erastin, and these changes were partially reversed by HSPA4 (Fig. 2F). Together, these data clearly indicate that HSPA4 has the capacity to alleviate ferroptosis in SH-SY5Y cells and primary dopaminergic neurons.

Knockdown of HSPA4 aggravates erastin induced insults in SH-SY5Y cells

To further confirm the observed beneficial effects of HSPA4 on ferroptosis, we performed loss-of-function experiments using siRNA-mediated Hspa4 knockdown. Both the mRNA and protein levels of HSPA4 were markedly decreased by Hspa4 siRNA-1, -2, and -3 (Fig. 3A, B). Among these Hspa4 siRNAs, siRNA-1 exhibited the best knockdown efficiency; therefore, it was chosen for subsequent experiments. The examination of cell morphology revealed that HSPA4 knockdown further exacerbated the cell injury induced by erastin (Fig. 3C). In accordance with these changes, the viability of erastin-treated cells was further decreased by HSPA4 knockdown, while LDH release was further increased (Fig. 3D, E). The mitochondrial membrane potential was reduced by erastin, which was further increased by HSPA4 knockdown (Fig. 3F). Lipid ROS generation was increased in erastin-treated cells, and HSPA4 knockdown further strengthened this trend (Fig. 4A, B). The changes in GSH, MDA, and Fe2+ induced by erastin were further aggravated by HSPA4 knockdown (Fig. 4C–E). The protein levels of GPX4, FTH1, and xCT were reduced in erastin-treated cells, and these changes were further decreased by HSPA4 knockdown (Fig. 4F). Overall, these loss-of-function studies further confirmed that HSPA4 plays a beneficial role in combating ferroptosis.

HSPA4 plays a beneficial role in PD model mice

Our above data clearly indicate that HSPA4 attenuates ferroptosis in SH-SY5Y cells. Notably, ferroptosis is implicated in pathological cell death in neurodegenerative diseases such as PD26,27. Moreover, SH-SY5Y is a widely used cell line in PD because it functions as a dopaminergic neuron28. We therefore predicted that HSPA4 plays a beneficial role in PD. To address this prediction, we overexpressed HSPA4 in the SNpc by stereotaxic injection of adenovirus-associated virus carrying Hspa4 (AAV-HSPA4) and then administered MPTP to the mice to induce PD syndrome (Fig. 5A). The immunofluorescence and western blot data revealed that the expression of HSPA4 was increased in the SNpc of the AAV-HSPA4-treated mice (Fig. 5B, C). The results of the rotarod, pole, and traction tests revealed that MPTP induced motor defects in the mice; however, HSPA4 alleviated these defects (Fig. 5D–F). In addition, olfactory function in MPTP-treated mice was improved by HSPA4 (Fig. 5G). The reduction in tyrosine hydrolase (TH) expression in the SNpc induced by MPTP was largely prevented by HSPA4 (Fig. 6A, B). Similar changes were also observed in the striatum (Fig. 6C, D). These data indicate that the overexpression of HSPA4 in the SNpc is a useful strategy for alleviating the progression of PD.

Moreover, we analyzed whether ferroptosis in the SNpc was affected by HSPA4. To this end, we first examined ferroptosis associated proteins and found that the decreases in GPX4, xCT, and FTH1 in MPTP-treated mice were ameliorated by HSPA4 (Fig. 7A). Perls’ Prussian blue staining showed that ion deposition was reduced by HSPA4 both in the SNpc and striatum (Fig. 7B, C). Additionally, electron microscopy images showed that the integrity of the mitochondria was disrupted, as evidenced by the presence of crushed inner cristae, and these effects were largely alleviated by HSPA4 (Fig. 7D). Finally, we examined glial cell activation to evaluate neuroinflammation, because of its pivotal role in the pathophysiology of PD29. Our data showed that microglial activation was increased in the SNpc and striatum of PD model mice, as evidenced by increased ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba-1) expression (Fig. 8A, B). HSPA4 overexpression repressed MPTP-induced microglial activation (Fig. 8A, B). Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression in the SNpc and striatum was increased in PD model mice; however, these increases were largely prevented in AAV-HSPA4 treated mice (Fig. 8C, D). Accordingly, the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, including Ilb, Il6, and Il8, was markedly reduced by HSPA4 in the SNpc and striatum of PD model mice (Fig. 8E). These changes were further validated by ELISA assays (Fig. 8F). Hence, these data indicate that HSPA4 attenuates ferroptosis and represses neuroinflammation in PD model mice.

HSPA4 sequesters transferrin to repress iron transport into cells

In the subsequent experiments, we sought to elucidate the underlying mechanism of the HSPA4-mediated antiferroptosis effects. To this end, we transfected SH-SY5Y cells with a plasmid bearing S protein (SP)-tagged Hspa4, and performed an HSPA4 pull-down using SP tag. The resulting pull-down sample was analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). In total, 41 proteins were characterized in the pull-down sample. One protein, transferrin, attracted our attention because of its pivotal role in ferroptosis (Fig. 9A)24. Protein-protein docking analysis (https://hex.loria.fr/) revealed that HSPA4 has a high affinity for transferrin, with a binding energy of −449.6 kcal/mol and 4 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 9B). The protein pull-down results confirmed the interaction between HSPA4 and transferrin (Fig. 9C). This interaction was further confirmed by the coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) data (Fig. 9D). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that HSPA4 was colocalized with transferrin in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 10A). Furthermore, our immunofluorescence data showed that transferrin was sequestered by HSPA4 in cells (Fig. 10B, C). The western blot results showed that the level of intracellular transferrin was increased in the HSPA4-overexpressing cells, and in accordance with these findings, the level of extracellular transferrin was reduced (Fig. 10D). For this reason, the secreted transferrin in the cell culture media was reduced by HSPA4 overexpression (Fig. 10E). As a result, the level of intracellular Fe2+ was decreased in the HSPA4-overexpressing cells (Fig. 10F). Transferrin receptor plays a key role in the recycling of transferrin and subsequent Fe2+ import30. To determine whether transferrin receptor expression is involved in the reduction of intracellular Fe2+ induced by HSPA4, we assessed its expression levels. Both immunofluorescence and western blot analyses showed that transferrin receptor expression was increased by erastin, while HSPA4 had no effect on its levels (Supplementary Fig. 3A–D). Additionally, holo-transferrin-mediated iron uptake was not affected by HSPA4 (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Furthermore, the enhanced cell viability observed in HSPA4-overexpressing cells was abolished by holo-transferrin (Supplementary Fig. 3F), suggesting that HSPA4 does not influence extracellular transferrin (holo-transferrin)-mediated Fe2+ uptake or the resulting ferroptosis. These data indicate that HSPA4 restricts transferrin export by sequestering it in cells and thereby reduces iron import into cells. In this way, HSPA4 attenuates ferroptosis in SH-SY5Y cells.

Discussion

The loss of DAs in the SNpc is a defining hallmark of PD, which may deplete 80-85% of dopamine levels in the striatum31. Under these conditions, PD-related motor defects, including bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremors at rest, can occur31. Currently, the precise mechanisms responsible for the loss of DAs in the progression of PD are still unclear. In addition to α-synucleinopathy32, recent evidence has shown that dysregulated iron metabolism is also a contributing factor for the loss of DAs in PD33–35. Iron accumulation in the brain gradually increases with age36. Compared with those in controls, the iron levels in neuromelanin-positive neurons and surrounding tissue in PD patients are increased by 4-5-fold37. In the brain, accumulated iron deposited mainly in nigrostriatal tissue compared to other brain areas38,39. An ectopic increase in iron in neurons induces ferroptosis, as evidenced by increased lipid peroxidation and ROS, leading to the death of neurons33. Hence, in PD patient brains, iron levels in the SNpc are positively correlated with DA neuronal loss40,41.

Moreover, 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridium (MPP+) and 6-hydrodopamine (6-OHDA), two widely used neurotoxins in diseased PD cells, can induce ferroptosis in SH-SY5Y (human neuroblastoma) cells42,43. In accordance with these findings, the death of DAs in PD model mice was attenuated by ferroptosis inhibitors26. The reduction in iron caused by knockdown of the transferrin receptor is capable of slowing the cell senescence induced by α-syn-A53T overexpression44. Together, these findings strongly suggest that ferroptosis is involved in the pathological progression of PD45. In the present study, we treated SH-SY5Y cells with erastin, a potent inducer of ferroptosis, and observed that cell viability was decreased, which reciprocally supports the findings mentioned above. In line with our findings, erastin and RSL-3, another ferroptosis inducer, also decreased the viability of LUHMES cells46.

Because SH-SY5Y cells have many characteristics of DAs, they are widely used as DAs in PD studies28. We therefore predicted that HSPA4 might play a beneficial role in PD. As expected, overexpression of HSAP4 in the SNpc improved behavioral defects, prevented decreased TH expression, and maintained the integrity of mitochondria in PD model mice. Many studies have shown that HSPA5, another member of the HSP70 family, has anti-PD effects22,47–50. However, this is the first report showing direct evidence that HSPA4 attenuates PD-related syndromes. For the underlying mechanism, we performed HSPA4 pull-down experiments and found that transferrin was present in the pull-down samples. Protein-protein docking analysis further supported the interaction between HSPA4 and transferrin. Transferrin is a transport protein that binds with two atoms of iron and thereby favors iron transport in the body51. In peripheral blood, most transferrin is synthesized from the liver51; whereas in the brain, transferrin can be produced by the cerebellum, substantia nigra, frontal lobe, medulla oblongata, occipital lobe, etc.52. Our subsequent experiments revealed that HSPA4 interacts with transferrin in the cytoplasm and thereby prevents transferrin export from the cell. As a result, the extracellular iron cannot enter the cell due to a shortage of transferrin. In this way, HSPA4 alleviates ferroptosis in DAs and rescues the loss of DAs in PD. Notably, this mechanism responsible for the anti-PD effects of HSPA4 is different from that of HSPA5 in PD. As a key chaperone protein in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, HSPA5-mediated anti-PD effects are mainly due to the alleviation of abnormal protein misfolding and accumulation in DAs48,50.

In addition to the antiferroptotic activity of HSPA4 in PD, we also observed that HSPA4 attenuates the activation of microglia and astrocytes. In line with these findings, one of our previous studies revealed that HSPA4 plays a key role in repressing neuroinflammation in PD model mice23. Moreover, in this study, we found that HSPA4 directly suppresses the proinflammatory cytokine expression induced by LPS in microglia23. However, in the present study, AAV-HSPA4 was primarily expressed in DAs, not microglia. Therefore, the observed reduction in glial activation in AAV-HSPA4-treated PD model mice is likely an indirect effect of HSPA4, and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Recently, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) has been identified as a key mediator of inflammatory responses53. We hypothesize that HSPA4 may influence glial activation via mtDNA derived from DAs. Further evidence is needed to support this hypothesis.

xCT, also known as SLC7A11, is the functional subunit of the system Xc- cystine/glutamate antiporter. In cooperation with SLC3A2, SLC7A11 imports cystine into cells in exchange for exporting glutamate. Once inside the cell, cystine is reduced to cysteine, the rate-limiting precursor for glutathione (GSH) synthesis. Through this mechanism, SLC7A11 plays a crucial role in neutralizing lipid peroxides and preventing ferroptosis54. In the present study, we observed that erastin treatment reduced SLC7A11 expression, an effect that was prevented by HSPA4. Consistent with our findings, several studies have reported erastin-induced downregulation of SLC7A11 in various cell types, including SH-SY5Y cells55, primary cortical neurons56, TCMK-1 cells57, and LO2 cells58. In contrast, erastin was found to transcriptionally upregulate SLC7A11 in HT-1080 cells59. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in erastin dosage, treatment duration, and/or cell type-specific responses.

Notably, most of our in vitro experiments were conducted using SH-SY5Y cells. Although this cell line is widely employed as a dopaminergic neuron model in PD research, SH-SY5Y cells are not purely dopaminergic, as they are derived from a neuroblastoma lineage28. Consequently, their cancerous properties can affect cell viability, growth, differentiation, metabolism, and genomic stability. Therefore, the use of additional neuronal models, such as mesencephalic culture, primary neurons, PC12 cells, and Neuro-2a cells, and in vivo animal models, is necessary to validate findings obtained from SH-SY5Y cells28. In the present study, beyond SH-SY5Y cells, we also demonstrated that HSPA4 protects primary dopaminergic neurons from erastin-induced injury. Furthermore, using MPTP-induced PD model mice, we validated the neuroprotective effects of HSPA4 in vivo. In addition, patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which can be differentiated into dopaminergic neurons, represent a promising next-generation model for PD studies60,61. Most recently, organotypic brain slice cultures, neural organoids, and organ-on-a-chip systems have emerged as innovative models for studying PD pathogenesis and treatment responses62. The observed protective effects HSPA4 warrant further investigation in these advanced model systems.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in the present study, we observed that HSPA4, a member of the HSP70 family, attenuates ferroptosis induced by erastin in SH-SY5Y cells. In contrast, knockdown of HSPA4 further aggravates erastin-induced cell insults. In PD model mice, increased expression of HSPA4 in the SNpc ameliorates motor defects and olfactory function, prevents the loss of DAs in the SNpc and striatum, improves the integrity of mitochondria, and represses glial activation. All these anti-PD benefits conferred by HSPA4 are likely due to its potent antiferroptosis activity. Mechanistically, HSPA4 interacts with transferrin and thereby sequesters it in the cytoplasm. As a result, iron cannot be transported into the cell due to a shortage of transferrin. HSPA4 may play a beneficial role in ferroptosis and related neurodegenerative diseases, such as PD. HSPA4 may have therapeutic potential for treating ferroptosis-associated disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases (PD and AD), carcinogenesis, kidney fibrosis, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and ischemia-reperfusion injury.

Methods

Plasmids

The plasmid of pMito Timer was a gift from Zhen Yan (Addgene; plasmid: #52659; http://n2t.net/addgene:52659; RRID: Addgene_52659) (Laker et al., 2014REFERENCES). The HSPA4 plasmid was constructed as described in our previous study23. The nucleotide fragment for murine Hspa4 fused with the S protein tag at the N-terminus was synthesized and incorporated into the vector of pcDNA3.1(+) (Invitrogen) at the Nhe I and Xba I sites.

Cell culture, transfection and treatments

SH-SY5Y cells were obtained from the ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were cultured in DMEM (11965084; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099141C; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122; Gibco), and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. To overexpress HSPA4, SH-SY5Y cells were transfected with 1 μg of the plasmid expressing Hspa4. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the cells were treated with 5 μM erastin (HY-15763; MCE) for or 10 μM holo-transferrin (11096-37-0; Sigma-Aldrich) as indicated for additional 24 h. To knockdown HSPA4, three pairs of siRNAs targeting Hspa4 were synthesized at Obio Technology (Shanghai, China). The sequences of the siRNAs used as follows: CAU UGG AGC AGC AGC UAA GAT T (siRNA-1#); GCA AGU UUA UGU AGA CAA AUU TT (siRNA-2); GUC UCA AGA GUG UGA GAA ACU TT (siRNA-3). For gene knockdown, the siRNAs against Hspa4 were transfected into SH-SY5Y cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778030; ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were treated with 5 μM erastin for an additional 24 h to induce ferroptosis.

Culture of primary dopaminergic neurons

In this study, primary dopaminergic neurons were cultured using cerebral tissues obtained from SPF-grade Sprague-Dawley fetal rats at embryonic days 14–1863. Pregnant rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, and fetal brains were aseptically harvested via cesarean section. The collected brain tissues were rapidly rinsed, and then the meninges and vascular structures were meticulously separated under a dissection microscope. Target regions, including the SNpc and striatum, were microdissected and minced into tissue fragments. These fragments were transferred to trypsin solution (9002-07-7; Sigma-Aldrich) and digested at 37 °C for 7 min with gentle agitation. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding DMEM (11965048; Gibco) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (1009914C; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122; Gibco). After centrifugation, cell pellets were collected and washed to remove enzymatic residues. Mechanical dissociation was performed using pipettes to generate a homogeneous cell suspension, which was subsequently filtered through a 200-mesh (75 μm) cell strainer. Culture dishes pre-coated with 0.1 mg/mL poly-L-lysine (25988-63-0; Sigma-Aldrich) were UV-sterilized and air-dried prior to use. Cells were seeded at an optimal density and maintained in a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After 4 h of adhesion, the medium was replaced with Neurobasal Medium (2916968; Gibco) supplemented with 2% NeuroCult SM1 Neuronal Supplement (05711; STEMCELL) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and the medium was changed every two days thereafter. To increase HSPA4 expression, primary neurons were transfected with a plasmid carrying Hspa4 via electroporation using an electroporation apparatus (NEPA GENE, NEPA21, Japan).

RNA extraction and quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (15596018; Invitrogen). The RNA quantity and quality were assayed with a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, ND-1000). The RNA was then reversely transcribed into cDNA using a Prime Script RT Master Mix (RR036A; Bio-Rad). qRT-PCR was performed using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq II on a Lightcycler 96 system (Roche). The mRNA levels were normalized to the expression of 18S rRNA. The sequences of primers used were as follows, 18S rRNA: 5’-AGC TCC AAT AGC GTA TAT TAA AG-3’ (forward); 5’-CGG TCC TAT TCC ATT ATT CCT A-3’ (reverse). Hspa4: 5’-AAG TTT AGG TCA ACC CAT TAA AG-3’ (forward); 5’-TTG CAT TTC ATC CCA CTC CAT-3’ (reverse). Il1b: 5’-CAA CCA ACA AGT GAA TTC TCC ATG-3’ (forward); 5’-GAT CCA CAC TCT CCA GCT GCA-3’ (reverse). Il6: 5’-TCC AGT TGC CTT CTT GGG AC-3’ (forward); 5’-GTG TAA TTA AGC GCC GAC TTG-3’ (reverse). Il8: 5’-CAA GGC TGG TCC ATG CTC C-3’ (forward); 5’-TGC TAT CAC TTC CTT TCT GTT GC-3’ (reverse).

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Cells or tissues were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaF, 50 mM Na4P2O7, 10 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM EGTA, 10 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, 2 mM PMSF, 20 nM okadaic acid. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Cell debris was discarded and supernatant was kept on ice. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (23225; ThermoFisher Scientific). After boiling for 5 min in loading buffer, the samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010; Merck). The membranes were soaked in a solution of 10% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-FTH1 (1:1000; #4393; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-GPX4 (1:1000; #59735; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-xCT (1:1000; #29650; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-HSPA4 (1:1000; 21206-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-transferrin receptor (1:1000; #13113; Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-β-Actin (1:10000; 66009-1-1g; Proteintech). After incubation overnight, the membranes were washed 4 times in TBST and incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized with chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate detection kit (E-IR-R308; Elabscience). Relative protein levels were quantified by the ImageJ software.

Cell viability assay

MTT assay was used to evaluate cell viability. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 8 × 103 cells per well. At the end of the treatments, the cell culture medium was removed, and a final concentration of 500 μg/ml MTT was added to each well. After 4 h-incubation at 37 °C, the cells were treated with 20% SDS, and the cell lysates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 h. The absorbance at 570 nm was measured with a microplate reader (BioTek).

LDH assay

LDH activity was measured with an LDH cytotoxicity assay kit (C0016; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, at the end of the treatments, the cell culture medium was harvested and then incubated with the LDH-assay mixture. The mixture was incubated was performed for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The absorbance at 490 nm was detected with a microplate reader (BioTek). Cell death ratio was calculated by the following formula: The cell death ratio (%)=(Asample–Ablank)/(Amax–Ablank)×100.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was analyzed with a JC-1 staining kit (C2006; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5 μg/ml JC-1 was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed with cold PBS for three times. The absorbance at 590 nm represents a high membrane potential (red color), and the absorbance at 530 nm represents a low membrane potential (green color). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus). The fluorescence intensity was quantified by the Image J software.

Mitochondrial oxidative stress analysis

SH-SY5Y cells were cultured on glass coverslips in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were transfected with pMito Timer (Addgene; Plasmid: #52659) using Lipo8000 (C0533; Beyotime). Twenty-four hours-post transfection, the cells were treated with erastin as indicated in each figure legend. After the treatments, the cells were washed with PBS twice and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min on ice. Images were acquired with a confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM880) for both the green (excitation/emission wavelengths 488/518 nm) and red channels (excitation/emission wavelengths 543/572 nm). The fluorescence intensity was quantified using the ImageJ software.

ROS detection

ROS generation was determined using a BODIPY (581/591) C11 assay kit (S0043S; Beyotime). After the treatments, the cells were gently rinsed with serum-free medium, and then 2 μM BODIPY (581/591) C11 was added to the cells and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 20 min. Afterward, the cells were washed with serum-free medium three times to remove the unloaded BODIPY (581/591) C11 probe and then imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager M2). Oxidized BODIPY (O-BODIPY) was observed at an excitation/emission wavelength of 488/510 nm, while reduced BODIPY (R-BODIPY) was observed at 581/591 nm. The fluorescence intensity was quantified using the ImageJ software.

Glutathione assay

A glutathione (GSH) assay kit (A006-2-1; Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used to detect GSH levels. At the end of the treatments, the cells were washed with PBS and then harvested and centrifuged. The samples were homogenized in 300 μl of PBS or sonicated on ice and then centrifuged at 3500 × g for 10 min. A total of 25 μl of chromogenic agent was added to each well at room temperature for 5 min, and then the absorbance at 405 nm was subsequently measured with a microplate reader (BioTek).

Malondialdehyde assay

A cell malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (A003-4-1; Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, the cells were harvested and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. A total of 100 μl of the supernatant was added to 1000 μl of the MDA assay mixture. The total sample mixture was subsequently mixed and incubated at 95 °C for 40 min. Finally, the total samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The optical absorbance was measured at 530 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek).

Detection of Fe2+

An iron assay kit (E-BC-K881-M; Elabscience) was used to quantify the Fe2+ levels according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after the treatments, the cells were harvested, mixed with 120 μl of buffer solution, placed on ice for 10 min for cracking, and then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected and stored at room temperature. Afterward, the standards were diluted, according to the instructions. The same amount of chromogenic solution or an already diluted iron standard was added. After mixing, the supernatants were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The optical absorbance value was measured at 593 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek).

Mice

Ten-month-old male C57BL/6 mice were used in this study. After anesthetization, the mice were then immobilized in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD, Shenzhen, China). Adenovirus associated virus expressing Hspa4 (AAV-HSPA4) and control virus (AAV-CON) were generated at the Obio Technology Company (Shanghai, China). To increase HSPA4 in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc), AAV-HSPA4 or AAV-CON was stereotaxically injected into the right SNpc (AP: −3.2 mm, ML: −1.3 mm, DV: −4.3 mm from the bregma). A stainless-steel cannula (10 μl Hamilton syringe) was inserted and 1 µl of AAV was slowly injected over 5 min. At the end of the injection, the microinjector was left in place for 5 min before withdrawal. 28 days post-injection, the mice were treated with 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) (M0896; Sigma-Aldrich) at a dosage of 20 mg/kg/day via intraperitoneal injection for 7 consecutive days. The control mice were injected with saline. Mice were randomly assigned into 4 groups, Control (AAV-CON+saline), MPTP (AAV-CON+MPTP), HSPA4 (AAV-HSPA4+saline), and MPTP+HSPA4 (AAV-HSPA4+MPTP). Sample sizes for animal studies were determined based on established laboratory experience with PD model mice, with n = 6 mice per group providing consistent detection of biologically significant effects in prior experiments. All the animal protocols were approved by the Committee of Nantong University and the Administration Committee of Experimental Animals, Jiangsu Province, China (Approval ID: SYXK [SU] 2017- 0046). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Behavioral tests

The rotarod test was performed using an accelerating rotarod apparatus (model LE8500; Panlab)64. The mice were trained for 2 consecutive days before MPTP injection in acceleration mode (4–40 rpm) over 5 min. The training was repeated with a constant speed (16 rpm) until the mice were able to stay on the rod for at least 300 s. For the formal test, the mice were placed on a rotating drum, accelerated from 4 to 40 rpm over a 5 min period. The time spent moving on the rod was recorded before falling. Pole tests were conducted with the method described elsewhere64. Briefly, the mice were held on the top of a pole (diameter 16 mm; height 60 cm) with a rough surface. Mice were habituated to the task 1 d before testing. On the test day, the total time taken to descend was measured and considered locomotion activity. For the olfactory test, the mice were fed cheese pellets before the olfactory test to ensure that they were familiar with the odor of the pellet. The mice were fasted for 20 h prior to the test. One cheese pellet was buried 1.0 cm below the bedding on a corner of the cage. In each test, the mouse was initially placed at the center of the cage. The time at which the cheese pellet was detected and eaten by the mice was recorded. If a mouse failed to find the pellets within 5 min, the test stopped, and a score of 300 s was awarded. For the traction test, the mice were trained in advance for hanging to ensure that they were familiar with the device. In brief, the two forelimbs of the mice were hung on a horizontal wire. If the two hind limbs of a mouse could grasp the wire, 3 points were given; if only one hind limb grasped the wire, 2 points were given; if both hind limbs could not grasp the wire, 1 point was given.

Tissue collection and preparation

The mice were anesthetized with phenobarbital, and the brains were immediately extracted. A portion of the freshly obtained mouse brain was used for immunofluorescence, and the tissues were removed, fixed overnight in 4% PFA and then dehydrated in 10 and 30% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C. The frozen brains were sectioned at 12 μm and stored at −20 °C until use. The remaining brains were further dissected into the SNpc and striatum under stereomicroscopy according to the mouse brain atlas. For gene expression analysis, the collected SNpc and striatum samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence analysis of cultured cells, SH-SY5Y cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. The cells were then rinsed three times in PBS for 5 min each. For permeabilization, the cells were placed in ice-cold methanol and incubated for 10 min on ice. After three washes in PBS, the cells were transferred to blocking buffer for 60 min at room temperature. Then, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies, including an anti-HSPA4 antibody (1:500; 21206-1-AP; Proteintech), an anti-transferrin antibody (1:200; 17435-1-AP; Proteintech), and an anti-transferrin receptor antibody (1:200; #13113; Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C. After primary antibody incubation, the cells were rinsed three times in PBS and incubated with a fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (P0131; Beyotime). Fluorescence images were taken using a confocal microscopy (Leica, SP5). For the tissue sections, the SNpc and striatum slices were removed and placed in a 37 °C oven overnight. The slices were subsequently subjected to antigen retrieval by microwaving with citrate. After cooling to room temperature, the samples were incubated with anti-HSPA4 antibody (1:200; 21206-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-TH antibody (1:200; ab6211; Abcam), anti-GFAP antibody (1:500; ab7260; Abcam), and anti-Iba-1 antibody (1:500; ab5076; Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, and then with the corresponding secondary antibody (1:10000; SA00001-2; Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (P0131; Beyotime). Fluorescence images were taken using a fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss, Axio Imager M2). The fluorescence intensity was analyzed using the ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscope analysis

The SNpc samples were fixed in cacodylate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2.5% paraformaldehyde. After fixation, the samples were immersed in 1% osmium tetraoxide at 4 °C for 1 h, and then dehydrated using a graded alcohol (50, 70, 90, and 100%). Ultrathin sections were cut at 70 nm, contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined at 80 kV with a transmission electron microscope (JEO Ltd., Tokyo) by a blinded investigator.

Prussian blue iron staining

Iron staining analysis was conducted using a Prussian blue staining kit (G1428; Solarbio) according to its manufacturer instructions. Coronal brain sections containing target regions were dried at 37 °C and subjected to antigen retrieval. Prior to staining, reagents were equilibrated to room temperature, and incubation box were pre-warmed to 37 °C for 20 min. Tissue sections were initially incubated with Prussian blue working solution in pre-equilibrated incubation box at 37 °C for 20 min under light-protected conditions, followed by three 10 s washes in distilled water. Subsequently, sections were treated with incubation solution for 15 min at 37 °C and rinsed three times in PBS (2 min per wash). Then tissue sections were treated with enhanced working fluid at 37 °C for 15 min and rinsed three times in PBS (2 min per wash). Nuclear counterstaining solution was performed for 5 min, followed by a 10 min running water rinse to terminate the reaction. Sequential dehydration was carried out through an ethanol gradient (75, 80, 95, and 100%, 2 min each), followed by xylene clearing and neutral balsam mounting. Digital images were acquired using a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope, and iron-positive areas were quantified using the ImageJ software.

IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 assays

The levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 in the SNpc and striatum were measured using commercial ELISA kits, including mouse IL-1β ELISA kit (ml098415; mlBio, Shanghai), mouse IL-6 ELISA kit (ml066159; mlBio, Shanghai), and mouse IL-8 ELISA kit (ml063162; mlBio, Shanghai).

HSPA4 pull-down

SH-SY5Y cells were transfected with a plasmid expressing S protein-tagged Hspa4 by Lipofectamine (11668019; Invitrogen). Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaF, 50 mM Na4P2O7, 10 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM EGTA, 10 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, 2 mM PMSF, 20 nM okadaic acid. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatants were subjected to protein purification with S-protein agarose (3226253; Novagen). The resulting pull-down samples were denatured by adding 1 × Laemmli buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. The samples were then separated by SDS-PAGE. The gels were subjected to Coomassie blue dye (P0017; Beyotime) staining or western blot analysis. Alternatively, the pull-down samples were subjected to liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

For the pull-down protein samples, 200 μg of protein was added to 30 μl of SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM DTT, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). The detergent, DTT and other low-molecular-weight components were removed using UA buffer (8 M urea, 150 mM TrisHCl, pH 8.0) by repeated ultrafiltration (Microcon units, 10 kDa). Then, 100 μl of iodoacetamide (100 mM IAA in UA buffer) was added to block reduced cysteine residues and the samples were incubated for 30 min in darkness. The filters were washed with 100 μl of UA buffer three times and then with 100 μl of 25 mM NH4HCO3 buffer twice. Finally, the protein suspensions were digested with 4 μg of trypsin (V5111; Promega) in 40 μl of 25 mM NH4HCO3 buffer overnight at 37 °C, and the resulting peptides were collected as a filtrate. The tryptic digests of protein samples were then desalted on C18 cartridges (Empore™ SPE Cartridges C18 (standard density), bed I.D. 7 mm, volume 3 ml, Sigma), concentrated by vacuum centrifugation and reconstituted in 40 μl of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The peptide content was estimated from the UV light spectral density at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 1.1 for a 0.1% (g/l) solution, which was calculated on the basis of the frequency of tryptophan and tyrosine in vertebrate proteins. The resulting samples were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis (Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd.). The MS/MS spectra were searched using MASCOT engine (Matrix Science, London, UK; version 2.4) against the UniProtKB human (161584 total entries, downloaded 20180105).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed as previously describe, with slight modifications23. Briefly, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer, and lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was used to determine protein concentration using the Protein Assay Kit (23225; ThermoFisher Scientific). Lysates containing 1 mg total protein were incubated with 1 μg of anti-HSPA4 antibody (21206-1-AP; Proteintech) for 12 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, protein A agarose beads (16–156; Millipore) were added and incubated for an additional 1 h. The beads were collected by centrifugation, and the supernatant was discarded. After thoroughly washing, 2X loading buffer was added to the beads, and the samples were boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. The resulting immuno-complexes were then analyzed by western blotting, in which anti-light chain-specific antibody (115-035-174; Jackson ImmunoResearch) was used.

Transferrin assay

Transferrin in the cell culture medium was measured with a commercial ELISA kit (EK1201; MultiScience). Briefly, after the treatment, the cell culture media were harvested and concentrated by using an Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filter at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. All procedures were performed following the procedures provided by the manufacturer.

Protein-protein docking analysis

Molecular protein-protein docking of HSAP4 and transferrin was analyzed using the software of HEX8.0 (https://hex.loria.fr/). The PyMOL molecular graphics system (ver.2.0) was used to visualize the docking structures. The protein sequences of HSAP4 and transferrin were sourced from the NCBI protein database with UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot: P34932.4 and UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot: P02787.4, respectively.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as means ± SEM. All the experiments were performed in triplicate. No data points or outliers were excluded from the analyses, and no blinding was performed in this study. Statistical significance was calculated with one-way ANOVA analysis followed by Tukey’s Multiple Comparison Test. Significance was accepted at the level of p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (version 8.30; GraphPad Software).
