Management strategies of multinational corporations’ green supply chains by deep reinforcement learning
Authors: Dan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultinational corporations (MNCs) face the dual challenge of optimizing sustainability performance along with operational efficiency within complex global supply chains, particularly under emerging trade regulations such as the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism. This study aims to develop an intelligent decision-making framework to support both strategic and real-time optimization in green supply chain management (G-SCM). The proposed approach integrates an improved Transformer architecture with deep reinforcement learning (DRL) by modeling the dynamic supply chain environment as a Markov Decision Process. Environmental indicators, such as carbon emissions and energy consumption, are coupled with operational metrics, including inventory turnover and order fulfillment rates, into a unified reward function. A hierarchical attention mechanism is employed to capture the high-dimensional dependencies among supplier relationships, logistics pathways, and green practices. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed model significantly outperforms traditional DRL baselines in reducing carbon emissions, increasing renewable energy usage, and maintaining high order fulfillment rates. The inclusion of a supply chain complexity index further enhances robustness against disruptions. Additional analysis reveals that the model’s attention weights reflect the relative influence of key decision variables on overall optimization, providing interpretable pathways for multi-objective coordination in supply chains. Overall, this study presents an intelligent optimization method capable of real-time adaptation to complex supply chain dynamics while balancing environmental and operational performance, providing valuable insights for both practical G-SCM operations and policy formulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53821-9.Keywords: Deep reinforcement learning, Green supply chain management, Multi-objective optimization, Attention mechanism, Supply chain resilienceSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Amid the deepening global green development agenda, multinational corporations (MNCs) confront a critical period of comprehensive transformation within their supply chain systems1. On one hand, evolving green trade regulations, cross-border carbon accounting frameworks, and international regulatory mechanisms require firms to coordinate production and logistics across diverse institutional environments2. On the other hand, modern supply chain networks are characterized by extreme complexity, encompassing multi-tier supplier collaboration, cross-border transportation, energy structure adjustments, and green manufacturing practices. These networks exhibit high dimensionality, dynamic behavior, as well as strong interdependencies3. Under the multi-objective constraints of simultaneously managing carbon emissions, energy consumption, and operational efficiency, traditional management approaches—which frequently relying on static parameters, local information, or single-objective optimization—are increasingly inadequate4. This highlights the need for an intelligent decision-making framework capable of real-time response to disruptions, integration of heterogeneous multi-source data, and provision of interpretability, thereby addressing a central scientific challenge for advancing green supply chain management (G-SCM)5.

Recent studies have begun exploring the application of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) in supply chain scheduling and forecasting. For example, Kegenbekov and Jackson (2021) applied DRL for demand–supply synchronization6, while Yu et al. (2024) compared deep learning and traditional models in predicting supply chain costs7. Although these studies have provided preliminary insights into the application of intelligent algorithms in supply chain management, existing research still reveals four critical gaps for MNCs pursuing G-SCM under dynamic regulatory environments such as the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM).

First, most existing DRL models primarily focus on optimizing a single dimension of operational efficiency. Environmental externalities, such as real-time carbon emission intensity or energy structure, are rarely incorporated directly into the reward function. Even when environmental factors are considered, they are typically treated as static penalty terms or ex-post constraints rather than dynamic state variables.

Second, most existing approaches represent supply chains as flat or sequential structures. They lack the capability to capture high-dimensional dependencies among supplier networks, logistics pathways, and green investment decisions. This limitation becomes particularly pronounced when disruptions propagate across multi-tier and cross-border supply chains.

Third, conventional DRL models are generally regarded as “black-box” systems with limited interpretability. Although graph-based techniques such as Graph Attention Networks (GATs) can capture relational information, few studies link attention mechanisms to actionable green management decisions, such as identifying which supplier’s carbon intensity most significantly affects global optimization outcomes.

Fourth, supply chain resilience under high complexity and disruption scenarios is rarely modeled in a dynamic manner. Existing algorithms are often sensitive to parameter variations and struggle to adapt to policy shifts or unexpected shocks.

To systematically address these limitations, this study proposes a novel paradigm that integrates an improved Transformer architecture with DRL, formulating the multinational green supply chain optimization problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The specific contributions corresponding to the four gaps are as follows.

First, carbon emissions, energy consumption, inventory turnover, and order fulfillment rate are jointly embedded into a unified reward function. This design enables the DRL agent to dynamically balance environmental objectives and operational performance, rather than optimizing a single metric or applying static emission penalties.

Second, a multi-scale hierarchical attention mechanism is introduced to simultaneously capture interactions among material flow, information flow, and environmental flow across different supply chain levels. This design goes beyond single-layer analysis and effectively models cross-regional and multi-tier dependencies that are largely ignored in existing green supply chain DRL frameworks.

Third, the model provides interpretable attention weight visualizations that identify which environmental variables most strongly influence global decision-making. For instance, it can highlight the impact of a specific supplier’s carbon intensity or the energy efficiency of a particular logistics route. This transparency directly supports managerial decisions, such as prioritizing investment in green technologies, which is rarely achieved in conventional DRL-based frameworks.

Fourth, a supply chain complexity index is introduced, and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are employed to embed supplier relationships and extract topological features. This enables the model to identify critical nodes under disruptions and generate adaptive strategies. In addition, a temporal credit assignment mechanism within a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework aligns long-term carbon reduction objectives with short-term operational goals, ensuring consistency between strategic planning and operational execution, a capability typically absent in traditional DRL scheduling models.

In summary, unlike prior studies that address only one or two of these limitations in isolation, the proposed framework provides an integrated DRL-based solution that (i) endogenizes dynamic environmental externalities, (ii) captures multi-level structural dependencies through multi-scale attention, (iii) delivers attention-based interpretability for green managerial decision-making, and (iv) models supply chain resilience under complex and disruptive conditions.

Related work

In recent years, DRL has demonstrated significant potential in optimizing green supply chains. Existing studies have predominantly explored this topic from various perspectives, including policy constraints, green logistics, closed-loop supply chains, and complex network modeling. For example, Wang et al. (2023) analyzed the green development pathways of China’s digital industries under low-carbon pilot policies, underscoring the critical role of policy incentives in promoting green supply chain transformation8. Wang and Yang (2024) embedded carbon emissions trading mechanisms into a DRL framework and proposed a Constrained DRL (CDRL) model that incorporated carbon quotas into supply chain decision-making through hard constraints9. However, their work did not consider the impact of cross-border policy heterogeneity on carbon cost structures. Wu and Zuo (2023) developed a machine learning–based predictive system for green transformation, examining how corporate emission reduction behaviors affected supply chain performance10. However, their study did not address the temporal coordination between long-term environmental goals and short-term operational responses.

In logistics and transportation optimization, Xie et al. (2025) developed a DRL-based routing model that dynamically adjusted transportation paths to reduce fuel consumption11. EL Khalili et al. (2022) constructed a green distribution network using smart city traffic data12. However, their approach was limited to urban logistics and did not extend to global supply chains, nor did it account for carbon costs or cross-border tariffs. In the domain of closed-loop supply chains, Gao (2024) applied DRL to improve the utilization of recycled products13, while Wang et al. (2024) proposed an enhanced DRL algorithm to coordinate remanufacturing supply chain inventory and logistics resources14. However, these studies primarily focused on single-firm contexts, making it difficult to generalize their findings to multinational, multi-agent collaborative environments.

Regarding decision interpretability and complex network modeling, current literature exhibits notable limitations. Rolf et al. (2023) highlighted the difficulty of modeling the dynamic coupling between environmental flows and logistics operations in multi-tier supplier networks15. Hosseinnia Shavaki and Ebrahimi Ghahnavieh (2023) identified three major gaps: the lack of multi-objective collaborative optimization mechanisms, limited capability to represent complex network structures, and insufficient interpretability of decision processes16. Merneedi and Palisetty (2023) used deep learning to predict supplier green behavior but did not integrate their predictions into a real-time decision-making loop17. Paraschos et al. (2024) applied DRL to optimize lean production in Industry 4.0 settings18, yet their approach still failed to coordinate long-term environmental objectives with short-term operational responses and overlooked the impact of cross-border policy heterogeneity on carbon costs. Other studies, including Zou et al. (2024), Zhang et al. (2024), and Wang et al. (2025), focused on perishable goods supply chain time-window sensitivity, multi-tier inventory dynamic replenishment, and financial decision-making for green investments, respectively19–21. However, these works commonly neither integrated environmental costs into optimization objectives nor established dynamic links between carbon tariffs and financing costs.

Recent years witnessed several important advances in the application of Transformer-based DRL models, which further expanded the research domain. For example, Wang and Liang (2025) integrated GNNs with self-attention mechanisms and developed a reinforcement learning approach for supply-chain path optimization22. Zou et al. (2025) introduced multi-head attention and attention-over-attention techniques into a low-carbon multi-warehouse vehicle-routing problem, resulting in an improved Transformer architecture23. In addition, Wang et al. (2025) proposed a Transformer-enhanced distributed DRL method for IoT scheduling problems in edge–cloud computing environments24. Although these studies achieved notable progress in their respective areas, most of them focused on single-objective or single-level optimization. They offered limited support for multi-objective, multi-tier green supply-chain optimization and exhibited insufficient adaptability to environmental costs and policy fluctuations. In contrast, the improved Transformer-DRL framework developed in this study enables efficient multi-objective optimization under globally dynamic and uncertain supply-chain conditions. It also enhances decision interpretability, thereby addressing key gaps left by existing research.

Furthermore, G-SCM and reinforcement learning approaches provide a foundation for subsequent research. Early studies highlighted the trade-offs between environmental and economic performance along with the complexity of multi-objective optimization25,26. The emergence of DRL, including methods such as Deep Q-Network (DQN) and Monte Carlo Tree Search combined with neural networks, has provided a methodological basis for handling high-dimensional, dynamic supply chain environments27,28. Interdisciplinary research has also emphasized the potential of intelligent technologies for real-time monitoring, supply chain risk management, and environmental performance optimization29–31. However, a systematic DRL framework capable of simultaneously optimizing carbon emissions, resource efficiency, and operational performance is still lacking.

A synthesis of existing research reveals several key limitations: (1) environmental objectives and operational efficiency are often decoupled, with metrics such as carbon emissions treated as external constraints rather than endogenous decision variables; (2) models exhibit limited adaptability in a multinational context, failing to fully account for institutional differences, exchange rate fluctuations, as well as geopolitical risks; (3) supply chain topology modeling is insufficient, making it difficult to capture nonlinear risk propagation; (4) decision models lack interpretability, preventing a clear understanding of the causal relationships between input factors and outcomes. To address these challenges, this study proposes a DRL framework based on an enhanced Transformer architecture with multi-level attention mechanisms to enable dynamic optimization of cross-domain environmental costs. This approach achieves significant advancements in decision interpretability, adaptability, and sustainability-oriented intelligence, offering new theoretical and practical directions for research on multinational green supply chains.

Improved transformer-DRL hybrid architecture

Traditional DRL approaches face three fundamental limitations in optimizing multinational supply chains. First, standard neural networks lack the capacity to capture the complex topological dependencies inherent in multi-tier supplier networks. As a result, the coupled effects between environmental and operational factors at the node level—such as the influence of a supplier’s carbon emissions on overall supply chain performance—are often reduced to oversimplified linear aggregations. Second, strategic decisions with long-term horizons (e.g., investments in green technologies) coexist with short-term operational actions (e.g., transport route adjustments), operating on different temporal scales. A single policy network cannot effectively coordinate such asynchronous decision layers. Third, current DRL architectures lack structured mechanisms for responding to policy shocks, such as sudden changes in carbon border adjustment regulations.

To address these limitations, this study proposes an enhanced Transformer-DRL hybrid framework, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The hierarchical multi-scale attention mechanism offers substantial advantages over traditional single-scale mechanisms when addressing supply-chain management problems. Effective supply-chain decisions depend on recognizing short-term (e.g., daily), medium-term (e.g., weekly), and long-term (e.g., annual) spatiotemporal patterns. Conventional single-scale attention mechanisms struggle to balance these multi-scale dependencies, often resulting in myopic or overly rigid decision-making.

In contrast, the multi-scale attention mechanism embedded in the proposed framework executes parallelized processing of multiple temporal patterns (s = 1, 3, 7). This architecture enables the model to capture short-term fluctuations such as order delays, medium-term patterns such as seasonal inventory shifts, and long-term changes such as yearly carbon tax adjustments. Integrating these heterogeneous temporal signals improves both demand forecasting and risk anticipation. Consequently, the model can dynamically calibrate inventory strategies, accelerate turnover rates, and maintain high order fulfillment performance. The multi-scale integration also enhances routing decisions by reducing delivery times and lowering delay risk. Ultimately, the mechanism strengthens environmental performance while simultaneously improving operational efficiency.

Experimental setup

The empirical analysis in this study utilizes a consolidated dataset derived from the World Bank Logistics Performance Index (https://lpi.worldbank.org) and the macroeconomic panel data of BRICS nations. The selection of core indicators, as well as the construction of the research framework, is benchmarked against the theoretical model of green logistics determinants proposed by Aldakhil et al. (2018)43. This dataset systematically integrated operational data from MNCs across multiple industries, including manufacturing, retail, alongside logistics services. The coverage period spanned from 2015 to 2022, comprising approximately 1,200 firm-level records. The dataset recorded detailed management practice information, such as agile supply chain management and lean production, as well as key operational indicators, including the number of suppliers, inventory turnover, and order fulfillment rates. Furthermore, the dataset tracked records crucial environmental metrics, such as carbon emissions, the proportion of renewable energy usage, and the adoption of green packaging. Topological and risk-related features—including the supply chain complexity index, supplier relationship scores, and resilience indicators—were also included, alongside financial performance measures such as cost of sales and revenue growth. The preprocessing procedure involved the following steps: (1) excluding samples with significant missing values; (2) standardizing all continuous variables to mitigate dimensional differences; and (3) screening variables for the final model through a multicollinearity test (VIF < 5).

This study employed daily-granularity data, where each data point represented a single day of supply chain operations, including inventory turnover, order fulfillment rate, carbon emissions, and other operational indicators. This temporal resolution enabled the model to capture short-term fluctuations and supported optimization of day-to-day supply chain decisions. The dataset was partitioned chronologically into training, validation, and test sets. The training set consisted of data from 2015 to 2020, the validation set included data from 2021, and the test set comprised data from 2022. This temporal split ensured that both the validation and test sets contained out-of-sample data, thereby simulating realistic forecasting conditions in a continuously evolving operating environment.

Data preprocessing was executed in several steps. First, missing values for numerical variables were imputed using multiple imputation, while categorical variables were populated with their respective modes. Second, outliers exceeding the mean ± 3 standard deviations were either truncated or removed to reduce their impact on model training. Third, all continuous numerical variables were normalized to the [0,1] range using Min-Max scaling, and variables requiring a normal distribution were further standardized using Z-scores. Fourth, categorical variables such as industry, region, and supplier type were one-hot encoded. Finally, derived indicators, including the supply chain complexity index and the share of carbon-related costs, were calculated to enhance the model’s ability to capture both environmental and operational decision drivers.

In addition to the variables provided in the original dataset, several decision-relevant stochastic disturbance variables were constructed to support the modeling requirements of the reinforcement learning framework. These disturbances include demand fluctuations, transportation delays, and energy price variations. They were generated using bounded stochastic processes following either uniform or truncated normal distributions, with parameter ranges determined according to dataset documentation and industry statistical reports. All stochastic processes were synthesized using a fixed random seed (seed = 42) to ensure traceability of the data construction procedure and reproducibility of experimental results.

In the experimental design, the supply chain decision-making process was formalized as an MDP. The state space integrated both operational and environmental information, including the number of suppliers, inventory turnover, order fulfillment rate, carbon emissions, renewable energy usage, and a supply chain complexity index. The action space reflected managerial decisions such as supplier restructuring, logistics routing adjustments, and changes in green investment intensity. The reward function internalized both environmental and operational objectives by combining carbon reduction, energy-efficiency improvement, and service-level maintenance within a unified optimization framework. Penalty terms are introduced to constrain violations of resilience thresholds and risk exposure limits. Discussion

The results indicate several practical implications for multinational companies in supply chain management under the simulated framework. The model’s dynamic supplier selection and logistics routing recommendations can provide guidance for procurement and distribution decisions, while the attention weights help identify critical nodes that have the greatest impact on environmental performance and resilience, thereby enabling optimized allocation of green investments. Moreover, the model’s rapid adaptation to policy shocks supports a shift from passive compliance to proactive strategic optimization. The novelty of the proposed Transformer-DRL framework does not lie in applying multi-objective optimization to green supply chains, which has been explored in prior studies. For instance, Ferdous et al. (2024)45 systematically integrated sustainability dimensions using multi-criteria decision-making methods. Liu et al. (2020)46 and Zhao et al. (2019)47 applied evolutionary computation techniques, such as genetic algorithms, for regional resource optimization and supplier selection. The key advancement of the present study is the capability to execute sequential, real-time decisions in high-dimensional state spaces under highly dynamic and uncertain global supply chain environments. Compared with traditional methods that rely on precise mathematical models and static parameters, the framework combines DRL with Transformer architectures to enable a shift from “rule-driven optimization” to “data-driven adaptive decision intelligence.” The incorporation of attention mechanisms further enhances decision interpretability, addressing the “black-box” limitations of conventional approaches.

Building on these findings, the above results also carry several deeper theoretical implications.

First, in the theory of dynamic multi-objective decision-making, the experimental results challenge the conventional assumption that trade-off relationships among objectives can be predefined in a static manner. Classical environmental–economic trade-off models typically assume a fixed marginal rate of substitution between carbon emissions and cost. However, the Pareto front shown in Fig. 8 exhibits clear non-stationary evolutionary patterns across different training stages. The dynamic weighting coefficients adaptively adjust the relative importance of environmental and operational objectives according to policy conditions, supply chain structure, and real-time perturbations. This finding suggests that, in highly dynamic and high-dimensional multinational supply chain settings, decision theory should shift from “searching for a fixed optimal solution” toward “learning the dynamic trajectory of optimal solutions.” In addition, the temporal credit assignment mechanism within the hierarchical reinforcement learning framework enables stepwise alignment between long-term environmental objectives and short-term operational metrics, providing a computable theoretical paradigm for coordinated optimization across strategic, tactical, and operational levels.

Second, in supply chain resilience theory, the findings prompt a re-examination of the source of resilience. Traditional resilience theories emphasize passive buffering mechanisms, such as safety stock, backup suppliers, and contingency planning, to absorb disruptions. In contrast, the results of this study reveal a different resilience-generating mechanism. When supplier node disruptions occur, the model does not rely on pre-defined backup inventory. Instead, it reconstructs the graph structure in real time through a GNN. By dynamically adjusting the selection probabilities of critical suppliers, the cascading failure suppression rate is significantly improved, and recovery time is substantially reduced. Notably, in heterogeneous clean energy scenarios, the model actively increases the selection probability of high-availability nodes. This indicates that the model not only responds to disruptions but also perceives external resource constraints and proactively reconfigures the supply chain topology to mitigate risk.

Third, in the context of interpretability within multi-objective optimization theory, this study introduces attention-based visualization to establish a traceable linkage among decision variables, attention weights, and performance outcomes in DRL-driven green supply chain optimization. Interaction analysis shows that enabling the interpretability module improves carbon reduction performance while significantly enhancing decision traceability. This finding contradicts the common assumption that interpretability necessarily comes at the cost of performance. Instead, it suggests that interpretability and optimization performance can exhibit synergistic effects under certain conditions, as feedback from attention weight regularity can facilitate more stable gradient convergence. Moreover, disabling the interpretability module leads to only minor degradation in operational metrics but results in a substantial loss of decision traceability. This highlights the independent theoretical value of interpretability. Rather than directly maximizing rewards, it provides a justification mechanism for managerial decision-making.

Finally, it is necessary to clarify the relationship between these theoretical contributions and existing literature. The theoretical advancements of this study do not negate the validity of established methods. Traditional mixed-integer linear programming remains valuable as a baseline in static environments, and evolutionary algorithms continue to be effective for multi-objective exploration. The contribution of this study lies in delineating the boundary conditions of different theoretical frameworks. In highly dynamic, high-dimensional, and policy-disrupted multinational green supply chain environments, the static assumptions underlying traditional methods no longer hold. Under such conditions, the proposed Transformer–DRL framework extends existing theory by integrating dynamic adaptation, topological reconfiguration, and interpretability within a unified modeling paradigm.

Conclusion

This study proposed an intelligent decision support system that integrates an enhanced Transformer architecture with DRL for multi-objective optimization in G-SCM of MNCs. Experimental results obtained within a simulated decision-making environment demonstrate that the proposed system effectively mitigates carbon emissions and bolsters renewable energy utilization, while simultaneously maintaining high operational robustness across key operational metrics such as order fulfillment and supply chain resilience. Specifically, within the simulated scenarios—which incorporated stochastic shocks and perturbations—the model exhibited superior performance in balancing environmental sustainability with service-level maintenance. The intelligent decision-making framework developed in this study demonstrated the potential to jointly optimize carbon reduction and operational efficiency in simulated scenarios. The conclusion that the model could completely resolve risks in multinational trade should be interpreted with caution and within an appropriate context. The experimental results were constrained by the predefined simulation parameters. Therefore, the model’s performance in the dynamic and uncertain global trade environment should be adjusted according to regional differences in CBAM policies. The strategy recommendations generated by the model should be regarded as reference pathways for enterprises rather than definitive decision solutions.

The model’s core strength lies in its ability to dynamically perceive and respond to uncertainties—including policy changes and supply disruptions—while providing interpretable, model-driven decision insights that inform managerial analysis.

(1) Managerial and theoretical contributions.

The results indicate both theoretical innovation and potential managerial relevance within the proposed simulation framework. On the theoretical side, by integrating supply chain topology information with sequential decision paths, the model offers a novel approach for modeling high-dimensional dynamic systems and supports multi-objective optimization in complex supply chains. From a managerial perspective, the model enables scenario-based exploration of dynamic routing strategies under carbon tariff policies, allowing decision-makers to examine how to prioritize low-carbon logistics paths under CBAM and similar regulatory frameworks. Furthermore, through supplier-switching strategies and attention weight visualization, the model facilitates analysis of potential supply chain responses to policy shocks across different simulated scenarios, enhancing the reliability of resilience planning and green investment decisions. This integration links methodological innovation with model-driven insights, which can support scenario analysis and strategic reflection in G-SCM.

(2) Research limitations and future directions.

Despite its contributions, several limitations persist. Due to limitations in the breadth of data collection, the explanatory power of the research findings remained subject to certain boundaries. The current green logistics supply chain dataset primarily focused on a specific macroeconomic context. It did not fully capture the distinctive characteristics of all industrial sectors. When the model confronted extreme supply chain disruptions caused by severe geopolitical conflicts, fluctuations in prediction accuracy could occur. Although the hierarchical attention mechanism improved the interpretability of the decision-making process, the computational procedure relied heavily on hardware resources such as high-performance graphics processing units. This requirement increased the technical threshold for small and medium-sized enterprises when attempting to deploy the system. Future research should evolve in several directions. One is to enhance the model’s responsiveness to heterogeneous disruption events by designing adaptive and recovery strategies for raw material shortages, logistics hub closures, and sudden demand surges. Another is to integrate environmental costs with financial risks, constructing a unified state space and reward function encompassing exchange rate fluctuations, interest rate changes, and green credit constraints. Cross-industry and cross-region field validations should be conducted, leveraging privacy-preserving techniques such as federated learning to address multinational data-sharing challenges while iteratively optimizing the model.

In summary, the proposed intelligent decision support system attains a dynamic coordination of environmental performance and operational efficiency in multinational green supply chains. It provides actionable strategic guidance for firms navigating carbon policy pressures and seeking to fortify supply chain resilience in an increasingly volatile global landscape.

Data availability

All original statistical indicators involved in this study were obtained from publicly accessible sources. The Logistics Performance Index data of the World Bank can be downloaded from the official portal (https://lpi.worldbank.org). The relevant economic data for the BRICS countries were derived from the World Bank’s open data platform. The dataset compiled during the research process, the simulation parameter files, and the complete code scripts supporting the model operation have been deposited in the Zenodo repository and are openly accessible to the academic community. Researchers can retrieve these materials through the corresponding DOI and reproduce the technical workflow and experimental results reported in this study.
