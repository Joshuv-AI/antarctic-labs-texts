Spatiotemporal understanding of behaviors of laying hens using wearable inertial sensors
Authors: Tsuyoshi Shimmura, Itsufumi Sato, Ryo Takuno, Kaori Fujinami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomatic detection of behaviors and locations has been increasingly needed in the management of noncage systems where hen behaviors are highly diverse and active. Here we show a technology to spatiotemporally understand behaviors using a wearable inertial sensor, containing a 3-axis accelerometer and 3-axis angular velocity sensor (i.e., gyroscope), and a marker. Using supervised machine learning, we first developed a tool that automatically classified and counted 11 behaviors, including comfort behaviors such as head scratching and tail-wagging with small movements as well as dust-bathing with dynamic movements. As expected, these behaviors were observed more frequently in floor pens than in conventional cages (all P < 0.01). We also spatially mapped the behaviors in floor pens and visualized the behavioral frequency in each resource by detecting the colored markers on the sensor. Furthermore, using the time-series information included in the sensor data, we analyzed the behavioral transition from one behavior to another. The behavioral transitions were more complex in floor pens, and the number was higher in body shaking, tail-wagging, resting, litter scratching, dust-bathing, preening, and moving in floor pens than in conventional cages (all P < 0.05). Our tools presented deeper insights into where and what hens behaved and also suggested that connectivity between behaviors, as well as observing the frequency of a behavior, can be an important indicator for welfare assessment in laying hens.Key words: laying hen, behavior, welfare, sensor, engineering technology

INTRODUCTION

Animal welfare has progressed rapidly from a concept to laws or guidelines around the world. In the European Union (EU), conventional cages for laying hens have been banned since 2012. As alternatives, they introduce furnished cages and noncage systems including aviary and outdoor systems. The European Commission also intend to put forward a legislative proposal by the end of 2023 to prohibit the use of all cages including furnished cages for laying hens (European Commission, 2021). Not only the EU but also 10 states in the United States and New Zealand have banned the use of conventional cages, and Australia is in the process of mandating a phaseout (Bas Rodenburg et al., 2022). Therefore, the transition from cages to noncage systems has occurred all over the world. Compared with conventional cages, noncage systems are likely to improve the welfare of animals overall through the ability to perform natural behaviors that are driven by internal motivation (Nicol and Weeks, 2006; Janczak and Riber, 2015). Increasing the use space of hens also makes their behavior more active and variable. On the other hand, these improvements introduce unique problems in noncage systems. For example, it is difficult for caretakers to visually assess health and understand continuous changes in behavior in individual hens (Siegford et al., 2016). To solve such problems, the development of tools for automatic behavior recognition systems has been progressing.

As a tool for automatic behavior recognition, visual images and wearable sensors are mainly used. The technologies using visual images detect the shape of hens in movies and recognize some behavior automatically by labeling the detected shape with specific behavior (Wang et al., 2020). This can be achieved when a single hen is detected using a fixed-point camera. However, because the detected shape of hens changes when some hens overlap, the technologies have some difficulties when large numbers of hens are introduced in large areas such as cage-free systems. Recording and recognition using fixed-point cameras are also limited in aviaries consisting of complex structures: multiple tiers and resources such as nests, litter floor, and perches. In this point, the wearable sensors have some advantages for application at the actual farm level. The inertial sensors are representative wearable sensors that recognize the behaviors automatically by labeling the recorded acceleration with specific behaviors. More specifically, small triaxial accelerometers fitted on the back of hens can continuously record behavioral changes in velocity in the X, Y, and Z directions (Brown et al., 2013). The accelerometers are small in size and have only a very minor effect on their behaviors; therefore, it is suitable to attach monitoring equipment to hens with (Buijs et al., 2018). Although the number of recorded animals is limited due to wearables, the technology can recognize the behavior of specific hens precisely with time sequence information (Mishra et al., 2005). In addition, spatial information can be obtained by detecting the color-labeled sensor from the upper camera. By integrating the behavior with time and spatial information, we can understand when and where each hen did (Figure 1). Moreover, the precise data of the behavior of each hen have high scalability and therefore offer a better understanding of behavioral sequences in each individual and social interaction in a group, which may contribute to the proposal of a new indicator to assess the welfare of laying hens.

Outline of data collection and analysis.

Here we show a technology to understand the behaviors spatiotemporally using a wearable inertial sensor. First, we tested the behavior of hens automatically and precisely using a 6-axis inertial sensor containing an accelerometer. We then detected the spatio-temporal dynamics of behavior based on the detected data. We also compared the sequential organization of the behaviors of hens between conventional cage and floor pens. Finally, we suggest a new indicator to assess the welfare of laying hens.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethic Statement

Animals were treated in accordance with the guidelines of the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology (TUAT), Japan. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of TUAT, Japan.

Animals and Housing

Six mature laying hens (Rhode Island Red) at the age of 61 wk kept in cages were used. Two of the 6 hens were randomly selected, introduced into a conventional cage or a floor pen and used for data collection for classifier training and basic performance evaluation (STEP-1 in Figure 1). Then, 5 hens were used for the other data collection (STEP-2 in Figure 1) because 1 hen died. Since the hens adjust quickly to small housing for a few days (Shinmura et al., 2006), at least a week was set for habituation after the introduction into cage and floor pen, and then the data collections were started. After data collections, the hens were returned to their home cage and kept more than a week. These experimental operations were repeated, and finally the data from all hens were obtained in both conventional cage and floor pen.

A conventional cage was a commercial laying cage 45 × 38 × 46 cm (wide × deep × high) at the rear. The camera for behavioural observation of caged hens was set at the upper of the cage. The cage provided 855 cm2 floor space per hen with feeder and U-shaped drinker (23 cm per hen, respectively). A floor pens was performed in a small enclosure 100 × 80 × 120 cm (wide × deep × high), providing a total floor area of 4,000 cm2 per hen. The floor was covered with wood shavings. On 1 side was the nest box, which was 36 × 36 × 27 cm (wide × deep × high) on the litter, providing 648 cm2 nest space per hen. A wooden perch (4 cm deep and 3 cm high with a chamfered top edge) was fitted across the width of the cage with its center 30 cm from the litter floor and 50 cm from the rear, providing 50 cm perch space per hen. The feeder and U-shaped drinker were placed along the length of the litter floor (50 cm per hen, respectively).

All experiments were conducted in the same environmentally control room. The room temperature was set at 26°C. Lighting was provided by commercial LED ceiling bulbs, adjusted to give an intensity of 70 lux at the feeder. The illumination cycle was 14 h of light and 10 h of darkness, with the light period from 05:00 to 19:00 h. The birds had ad libitum access to water and feed. The feed contained approximately 185 g CP and 11.88 MJ ME per kg.

Data Collection and Analysis

The data collection was conducted in 2 steps with specific purposes: data collection for basic classification performance analysis and classifier training for the analysis in the subsequent step (STEP-1) and data collection for the analysis of spatial mapping of behaviors and behavioral transitions (STEP-2). The outline is shown in Figure 1. The data collection and analysis were performed according to our previous study (Fujinami et al., 2023).

In STEP-1, before data collection, to fix the position of the sensor, the hens were fitted with a harness for small dogs with a fake sensor (same weight and shape) on the back and kept in conventional cage and floor pens for at least a week as adjustment (Figure 1A). For the comparison of time spent on each behavior in conventional cage and floor pens by hens with no sensor, fake sensor, and actual sensor (Supplementary Table 2), direct visual scans at 5-min intervals were conducted on the behavior of individual hens in each treatment for 4 h (10:00-14:00). As a results, we confirmed no significant differences in behaviors between the situations that hens received the harness without sensor, with fake sensor, and with actual sensor (Supplementary Table 2). The data were collected after replacing the fake sensor with actual sensors covered with green-colored markers to detect the location (Figure 1A). The sensors were Bluetooth-based wireless inertial measurement units (TSDN151, ATR-Promotions) containing a 3-axis accelerometer (±19.62 m/s2 [±2 G]) and 3-axis angular velocity sensor (±500 degree/s) (i.e., gyroscope). The behavioral acceleration and angular velocity data were sampled at 100 Hz, which was shown to be the best in our previous study (Fujinami et al., 2023) and recorded between 10:00 and 14:00 in a day per housing condition. The sensor recordings were carried out on different days for each of the 2 paired hens. At the same time as sensor recording, the behaviors were video-recorded from the top using a webcam (C920 HD Pro, Logicool). The data from both the wireless sensor and video camera (at 30 frame per sec) were collected on the same computer running the software (ALTIMA, ATR-Promotions) for recording both data synchronously.

After recording in STEP-1 of data collection, the collected accelerometer and angular velocity data were labeled by the 2 human annotators who were professionally trained in behavioral observations. They first cross-checked that labeling was the same. In labelling, a software (SyncPlay, ATR-Promotions) was utilized to annotate the start and end periods of behaviors by observing the recorded video (Figure 1A). The average time per hen used for labeling between sensor data and behavior was 236.4 (±2.2) min in conventional cage and 239.7 (±5.5) min in floor pens, in which approximately 50% of the data were randomly chosen and labeled for training and testing the classifier based on supervised machine learning. As described in our previous study (Fujinami et al., 2023), the trained classifier using multi-layer perception recognized the behaviors by calculating the feature vectors from the accelerometer and angular velocity data in a 1.28-s window sampled at 100 Hz (Figure 1B). Thus, a window is a unit of behavior detection, where it is slid by 50% overlap between 2 consecutive windows. The classifier classified a total of 12 behaviors with comparatively unique and dynamic movement and importance for welfare assessment: eating, drinking, moving, standing, resting, litter scratching, preening, head-scratching, body shaking, tail-wagging, dust-bathing, and others (Table 1)(Fujinami et al., 2023). Dustbathing was recorded when 1 of 2 actions (vertical wing-shaking or scratching with 1 leg) was observed (van Liere, 1992).The analysis was performed by feeding sensor data obtained from the same hens that provided data for training the classifiers into the trained classifiers, in which 10-fold cross validation was used to understand the basic classification performance under the condition that the test data have an identical distribution with the training data. Note that the classifiers were trained for each housing method: conventional cage and floor pens (Figure 1B). The average accuracy of automatic detection of all behaviors was 77.5% in conventional cage and 96.3% in floor pen. The precision and specificity of each behavior and the confusion matrix was shown in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 1, respectively. The data in STEP-1 was not reused in STEP-2.

The data collection in STEP-2 was designated to collect data for the analysis of spatial mapping of behaviors and behavioral transitions, which was performed in the same manner with different number of days of data collection. The averaged total time of data collection was 22.5 d per housing system and 4.5 d per individual. The data recording was performed between 10:00 and 14:00. The collected data were not labeled and given to the classifier trained in step 1 for analyzing the spatial mapping and behavioral transitions (Figure 1C). The locations of the hens were also determined by detecting the colored marker on the sensor (Figure 1A). Using images obtained from the same movie recorded from the top floor pens, the outlines of the marker were detected on the basis of the RGB value. The location of the marker (hen) was detected by calculating the center of gravity of the outlines. By integrating the classified behaviors and detected locations, the behaviors can be spatially mapped (Figure 1D).

The acceleration and angular velocity data include time-series information; therefore, the behavioral transition, transition from 1 behavior to another, can be analyzed (Shimmura et al., 2015)(Figure 1E). In this study, behavioral transitions between the 11 classified behaviors were visualized.

To examine the correlation between the proportions of each behavior calculated by the classifier and by visual observation in floor pens (Supplementary Figure 2), direct visual scans at 1-s intervals were performed using the randomly selected 30-min video used for counting by the classifier.

Statistical Analysis

Since the recorded time (the number of count at 1.28-s intervals) was not completely same between individuals, the proportion of time spent by each individual hen in each behavior (the number of counts of a behavior / total number of counts × 100) were calculated using data counting at 1.28-s intervals by the trained classifier. The frequencies of each behavior and the number of transitions were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank sum test. to compare the housing systems. Behavioral transitions were analyzed by calculating the z-score and testing significance (Bakeman and Gottman, 1997; Shimmura et al., 2015). The number of significantly transited arrows that were in and out of each behavior was counted and compared between housing systems (Figure 4, Figure 5). More specifically, Figure 4 was generated by accumulating the transitions of each day into 1 transition per housing condition (the data of totally 22.5 d from 5 hens was pooled in each housing system). On the other hand, average transitions of 23 and 22 d for floor pens and conventional cage, respectively, were obtained to compare the behavioral transitions between housing conditions in a day, i.e., approx. 4 h.

RESULTS

Automatic Classification and Quantification of Behavior

The proportions of time spent by hens in each behavior automatically quantified by the trained classifier were compared between conventional cage and floor pens (Figure 2). More time was spent on eating, moving, and stopping in the conventional cage than in floor pens (all P < 0.05). On the other hand, the proportions of time spent on resting, litter scratching, head scratching, body-shaking, tail-wagging, and dust-bathing were higher in floor pens than in conventional cages (all P < 0.05).

Comparisons of average (+SD) proportion of time spent on each behavior between conventional cage (C) and floor pens (F) (*P < 0.05, ⁎⁎P < 0.01).

A significant correlation was found between the proportions of behaviors calculated by the classifier and visual scans (rs = 0.94, P < 0.01)(Supplementary Figure 1).

Spatial Mapping of Behaviors

The locations where each behavior was performed in floor pens were mapped (Figure 3). Overall, hens tended to express these behaviors outside the nest box. Specifically, hens tended to express eating and drinking more frequently near the feeder and drinker, litter scratching and dust-bathing on the litter floor, and stopping and preening on the perch.

Spatial mapping of behaviors. The blue dots indicate the location where the behavior was detected in floor pens with nest box, perch, feeder, and drinker.

Behavioral Transitions

Behavioral transitions in each housing system are shown in Figure 4. Complex transitions were found in floor pens compared with conventional cages. The average number of significant transitions for each behavior is shown in Figure 5. The number of transitions of moving, preening, dust-bathing, litter scratching, resting, tail-wagging, and body shaking was higher in floor pens than in conventional cages (all P < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

Our developed technology automatically classified and counted 11 behaviors using supervised machine learning of sensor data (Figure 2). The noncage system encourages litter scratching and dust-bathing (Shimmura et al., 2010), and a larger space is required for tail-wagging (Rodenburg et al., 2005). As expected, in this study, the behavior was more diverse in floor pens than in conventional cages (Figure 2). Specifically, litter scratching, dust-bathing, tail-wagging, and head-scratching were performed more frequently in floor pens than in conventional cages. In addition, a significant strong correlation was found between the proportion of behaviors by visual scans and that by classifier (Supplementary Figure 2). Therefore, these results indicated that our classifier detected the behaviors automatically. Although automatic detections of behavior were previously reported, this study successfully detected the highest number of behaviors: 3 behaviors of broilers by sensor (Abdoli et al., 2020), 6 behaviors of laying hens by image recognition (Wang et al., 2020), and 11 behaviors of laying hens by sensor (this study). While dust-bathing with dynamic and unique movements was detectable (Abdoli et al., 2020), this study also detected tail-wagging and head-scratching with small behavioral differences. Since these comfort behaviors are significant for assessing welfare (Albentosa et al., 2007; Shimmura et al., 2010; Campbell et al., 2016), our classifier is suggested to be useful for automatically evaluating the welfare of laying hens.

While resting was observed more frequently in floor pens, moving and stopping were more frequent in conventional cages (Figure 2). Laying hens need space for free expression of behavior (Savory et al., 2006). When the space for an individual is decreased, the distance to the neighbor is closer and disturbing behaviors by the neighbor are more frequent. As the results, the hens in conventional cages moved with small steps more frequently and appeared restless than the hens in furnished cages with larger space and resources (Pohle and Cheng, 2009). It was also reported that as the density per hen increases, the frequency of stopping with their eyes open also tended to increase (Carmichael et al., 1999; Rehman et al., 2018). Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that moving with small steps and stopping without sleeping was observed more frequently in conventional cages than in floor pens in this study. This also supports that our classifier could detect such behavior with small moving.

Eating was performed more frequently in the conventional cage than in floor pens (Figure 2). Shimmura et al. (Shimmura et al., 2008) compared the proportion of hen behaviors between 6 housing systems including cages and cage-frees and indicated that caged hens spend more time in explorative feed pecking, without actual intake, to compensate for their motivation for beak usage, resulting in an increased total frequency of eating and almost identical proportion of all pecking behaviors among the housing systems. Therefore, the hens’ pecking motivation in the conventional cage was suggested to be directed toward the feed, resulting in an increased total frequency of eating per day.

Hens tended to express 11 behaviors outside the nest box (Figure 3). Hens express their motivated behaviors at locations where adequate substrates and environments for expression are presented. For example, eating is observed at feeders, and dust-bathing and litter scratching are observed on litter floor (Channing et al., 2001; Shimmura et al., 2007; Campbell et al., 2016). As expected, these behaviors were also observed at the feeder and litter floor, which indicated that our technology well mapped the locations where each behavior was expressed. The locations where preening and stopping were performed more frequently were mapped nearer the perch compared with eating (Figure 3). This mapping is also consistent with previous reports that hens tended to increase their expression of preening and stopping when they were on a perch (Braastad, 1990; Appleby et al., 1992; Duncan et al., 1992). Since this study mapped location 2-dimensionally by the camera from the top, more precise mapping of the use of resources such as nest boxes and multi-tiered floors will be needed for further study. However, some technologies to detect the location have been reported, for example, passive radio frequency identification (RFID) track hen's visit, even if it is a multi-tiered aviary, by detecting the wearable RFID tag by detectors equipped with housing (Alindekon et al., 2023). The illuminance sensor is also a useful tool for detecting hens’ visits to the nest box. Therefore, the combination of our technology and these sensors has a high potential for better understanding their behavior and location in complex environments such as multi-tiered aviaries.

The number of significant transitions of moving, litter scratching, preening, body shaking, tail wagging, and dust-bathing was higher in floor pens than in conventional cage (Figure 4, Figure 5). As expected, an increased number of transitions of litter scratching and dust-bathing was observed in floor pens where litter is supplied. Also, as the transitions are expected to increase when the frequencies are higher, the number of transitions of preening, body shaking, tail wagging, and dust-bathing increased in floor pens with their increased behavioral frequency. However, the frequency of eating and stopping significantly increased in conventional cages compared with floor pens (Figure 2), while the number of transitions of these behaviors showed no significant differences between housings (Figure 4). The most striking results can be found in moving: The number of transitions more increased in floor pens while the frequency more decreased (Figure 2, Figure 4). As mentioned above, behavioral disturbance, such as contact by other individuals during resting, was increased in the conventional cage due to the shorter distance between hens, which resulted in significantly increased short movement in the conventional cage. The loose housing systems provide hens with more opportunities to express natural behaviors (Janczak and Riber, 2015), where hens tended to express exploratory moving in loose housing systems (Leone and Estevez, 2008). Therefore, it is suggested that hens actively performed exploratory moving in floor pens, which increased the continuous and connected moving while the frequency of moving itself is lower compared with conventional cage. It also suggests that not only observing the frequency of a behavior but also analyzing the continuity and connectivity of behaviors is important for behavioral assessment. Maria et al. (María et al., 2004) reported that increased complexity of behavioral transitions in less stressful environments occurs by hens’ attempts to adapt to their surroundings. Conversely, when behavioral transitions become simple, the hens’ ability to process information efficiently from their surroundings decreases (Alados et al., 1996; Alados and Huffman, 2000). Therefore, the complexity of behavioral transitions reflects adaptation to a complex environment and the hens’ conditions. Taken together, measuring behavioral transitions between behaviors, especially moving as “hub behavior,” as well as observing the behavioral frequency of each behavior can be an important indicator in welfare assessment.

While this study has potential as described above, further revision and development are needed. First, the sample size was small. Second, although the same 6 hens were used to improve the efficiency of data labeling, data independence was not ensured. Third, the precisions for automatic classification differed among behaviors and were lower in cages (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 1). In fact, some eating and drinking behaviors were plotted outside the feeder and drinker, which suggested mis-plotting (Figure 3). One effective method to solve these problems is further training the classifier. For example, more inputs of labeling data from various individuals, breeds, and housing environments will increase the precision of the classifier. More inputs contribute to better detection of behaviors with small and similar movements in cages. The inputs of more dynamic data from commercial cage-free environments (e.g. multi-tiered aviary) are also important to reflect the commercial conditions and apply at the farm level. On the other hand, inputs themselves should be highly precise. In this study, since only 1 camera was used for behavioral observations in the cage and floor pen, the annotators sometimes had difficulty recognizing the behaviors, which led to miss-labeling of behaviors (e.g. resting in cage). Taken together, the extension of the classifier by inputting of various and high-quality data is needed in future studies. To apply these methods at the farm level, further research is needed to establish long-term applicable sensors and simpler detectors in the near future.

In summary, we developed an automatic and spatiotemporal detector of 11 behaviors of laying hens using wearable inertial sensors and markers. The detector presented deeper insights into where and what hens behaved. The transition analysis between behaviors as a time series analysis also suggested that connectivity between behaviors can be a novel and important welfare indicator. The extension of classifier by input of various and high-quality data will be needed in future studies.
