Winter predatory efficiency and periodicity of the non‐native brown bullhead Ameiurus nebulosus
Authors: Alan Fritsch, Pavel Staněk, Miloš Buřič, Antonín Kouba, Lukáš Veselý, Paride Balzani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWinter is a challenge for fish in temperate regions as it is likely to create a bottleneck for small‐bodied fish due to starvation. Resource consumption could contribute substantially to the overwinter survival of fish. Nevertheless, fish feeding activity and related impacts at low temperatures are understudied. We assessed the hunting periodicity and functional response of a non‐native European brown bullhead Ameiurus nebulosus population under winter conditions. Our results provide evidence of predation at temperatures as low as 3 °C, while the hunting periodicity was clearly identified as nocturnal. Furthermore, a visible decrease in handling time was observed at 3 °C compared to 4.5 °C, suggesting that this temperature could be a thermal threshold in A. nebulosus' ecophysiology. Moreover, the potential predation impact calculated from the functional responses revealed a severe increase in resource consumption from 3 °C to 4.5 °C, suggesting that such slight temperature increase could accentuate A. nebulosus predation impact. Ultimately, our results suggest that such winter predation activity could harm other species' recruitment and play an important role in the establishment success of A. nebulosus non‐native populations.

INTRODUCTION

Freshwater fish communities have been undergoing considerable shifts since the dawn of the 21st century (Buisson et al., 2008; Dudgeon et al., 2006; Leroy et al., 2023; Su et al., 2021). One of the major drivers of those alterations is the introduction of non‐native fish (Cucherousset & Olden, 2011; Vitule et al., 2009), essentially motivated by aquaculture, sport fishing, biocontrol or even ornamental purposes (Bernery et al., 2022; Copp et al., 2005). Their spread and establishment can have dramatic consequences on local fish biodiversity as a result of direct predation, trophic cascades, deterioration of habitats, transmission of pathogens and hybridization (Britton, 2023; Cucherousset & Olden, 2011; Gozlan et al., 2010). Moreover, non‐native fish progressively displace native species, either through direct competition (Haubrock et al., 2021a; Su et al., 2021) or indirectly as successful invaders often thrive in degraded environments to which native species are more sensitive (Balzani et al., 2021; Dudgeon et al., 2006; Moyle & Light, 1996).

One of the main characteristics favouring the establishment and spread of non‐native fish is their tolerance to various abiotic conditions (Bernery et al., 2022; Haubrock, Copp, et al., 2021; Marchetti et al., 2004) because a species' range is primarily determined by how well its niche requirement fits the spatially varying environmental factors (Holt, 2003). Temperature is possibly the most important abiotic variable for ectotherms (Angilletta et al., 2002; Brown et al., 2004), hence biogeographical distribution of fish is determined by sufficient thermal conditions to maintain sustaining populations (Figueira & Booth, 2010; Shuter & Post, 1990). In temperate ecosystems, fish temperature tolerance is heavily challenged during winter because of associated episodic mortality events (i.e. ‘winterkill’; Hurst, 2007; Shuter et al., 2012). Although multifactorial (Hurst, 2007), this phenomenon remains highly associated with starvation, especially for small‐bodied fish, due to a lower lipid storage to body size ratio and faster reserve catabolization (Coulter et al., 2018; Fernandes & McMeans, 2019; Garvey et al., 2004). Thus, starvation is likely to create a competitive bottleneck for juveniles due to scarcity of resources (Coulter et al., 2018; Phelps et al., 2008; Shuter & Post, 1990). However, several factors tend to decrease active resource foraging in winter, such as lower metabolic cost that would favour dormancy (Garvey et al., 2004) or reduced mobility (Hurst, 2007; Wardle, 1980), which could enhance vulnerability to endothermic predators (Englund et al., 2011; Garvey et al., 2004). Despite associated risks, predation might be a necessary trade‐off for juveniles to survive winter (Rooke & Fox, 2020). Temperature can also strongly modulate both competitive and predation interactions (Dunson & Travis, 1991; Taniguchi et al., 1998; Ward & Morton‐Starner, 2002), suggesting that a better foraging capability during low temperatures could improve a non‐native fish's overwintering and facilitate its establishment.

Ictalurids (Siluriformes: Ictaluridae) are among the most successful invasive fish groups worldwide (Copp et al., 2005; Haubrock et al., 2021b; Tarkan et al., 2017). Native to North America, many species have been introduced beyond their natural range and are considered detrimental across America, Europe, Asia and Oceania (Burr et al., 2020; Copp et al., 2005; Matsuzaki et al., 2011; McDowall, 1984). Within this family, the brown bullhead Ameiurus nebulosus (Lesueur, 1819) was introduced to Europe for sport fishing and aquaculture during the 19th century (Aislabie et al., 2019) and now has stable and reproducing populations in most European countries (Copp et al., 2005; Rutkayová et al., 2013). Their detrimental ecological impacts have been underlined by risk assessments (Aislabie et al., 2019; Moore, 2010; Tarkan et al., 2017), field studies (Adamczuk, 2022; Kalinowska et al., 2023) and a laboratory experiment (Mieczan et al., 2022). Multiple reasons can be advocated for the brown bullhead's success outside of its native range, such as resistance to drastic environmental conditions (Burr et al., 2020; Cocan et al., 2018; Scott & Crossman, 1973), effective anti‐predatory defence (Cocan et al., 2020; Moldowan et al., 2015) and an opportunistic behaviour associated with a generalist diet (Collier et al., 2018; Rechulicz & Płaska, 2021). Ameiurus nebulosus has been observed to have crepuscular or nocturnal habits in its native (Toobaie et al., 2013) and introduced range (Dedual, 2002). Due to interest in its aquaculture, there is an extensive record of physiology and toxicology investigations on A. nebulosus (e.g. Brungs et al., 1973; Iwanowicz et al., 2009; McFarland et al., 1999), including studies covering its tolerance to low temperatures (e.g. Cocan et al., 2018; Crawshaw et al., 1982; Keast, 1985; Richards & Ibara, 1978). Despite being considered thermophilic (Scott & Crossman, 1973), this species is still capable of maintaining growth in a wide range of temperatures (Keast, 1985) and slowing down its metabolism in response to cold environments (Crawshaw et al., 1982). As A. nebulosus is still spreading throughout Europe toward northern latitudes (Okhremenko & Gajduchenko, 2024), assessing the winter predatory behaviour of this fish could provide insight into its foraging capability under low temperatures.

The predatory efficiency of an animal species in a specific context can be measured by its functional response (FR), defined as the relation between resource exploitation and resource availability in a controlled environment (Faria et al., 2023; Holling, 1959). This method allows direct comparison of a species' predatory efficiency under different ecological scenarios (e.g. sex, number of predator, varying environment), including different temperatures (Buba et al., 2022; Englund et al., 2011; Uiterwaal & DeLong, 2020). For example, testing different temperatures in the light of climate change predictions is an effective way of quantifying how these would affect species and assess whether the impacts of non‐native species increase or decrease (e.g. Kulessa et al., 2024). While much research effort has been concentrated toward higher temperatures in regard to climate change (Englund et al., 2011; Iacarella et al., 2015), studies analysing fish FR under 10 °C remain scarce, representing less than 7% of experiments in the FoRAGE database (Uiterwaal et al., 2022; update of 20/12/2024). Furthermore, only one FR study has been conducted on ictalurids, namely on the channel catfish Ictalurus punctatus (Rafinesque, 1818) (Faria et al., 2019). By analysing the FR of a non‐native population of A. nebulosus under low temperatures, we aimed to evaluate (i) their diel hunting activity, (ii) their predation efficiency under winter conditions (i.e. at different temperatures) and (iii) their in situ impact on small fish during this season. The non‐native topmouth gudgeon Pseudorasbora parva (Temminck & Schlegel, 1846) was used as a prey and acted as a proxy for native juvenile fish. We predicted predation to occur at night because of previous observations of its diel activity (e.g. Dedual, 2002; Toobaie et al., 2013). Based on the only FR study on ictalurids (Faria et al., 2019), we predicted type II FR, with higher attack rate and smaller handling time at higher temperature due to accelerating metabolism in ectotherms (e.g. Kulessa et al., 2024). Considering the small size of the individuals used in this study, we expected some degree of predation following the hypothesis that feeding could help small‐bodied fish to overwinter successfully. Subsequently, we anticipated a non‐negligible impact on the local fish fauna, especially considering the relative abundance that A. nebulosus can reach in invaded areas (Aislabie et al., 2019).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics approval and participation consent

The care and use of experimental animals complied with Czech Republic animal welfare laws, guidelines and policies as approved by Ministry of Environment of the Czech Republic (permission No. J4/01/22265/23).

Sampling

Fishes were caught during harvesting of the Drahkovský pond (49°19′12″N, 15°03′47″E, Czech Republic) on 30 October 2024. This pond is used for carp culture but also harbours other species (Table 1). Following identification, a minimum of 30 randomly selected individuals of each species were measured for their total length (TL) and weighed. All the animals were weighed together to measure the total biomass. For those species with less than 30 individuals, all of them were measured. To provide a more accurate depiction of the population, 80 A. nebulosus individuals were measured and weighed.

Ameiurus nebulosus and Pseudorasbora parva were brought to the facilities in Vodnaňy (49°09′15″N, 14°10′09″E, Czech Republic) and stocked outside in separate flowing‐water tanks for 2 weeks. Tanks were protected against animal escape with an outflow screen (≤2 mm). Ameiurus nebulosus were fed ad libitum every morning with P. parva.

Experimental setup

Two functional response experiments were performed outdoors in November 2024, following the same experimental setup. Thirty glass aquaria (25 experimental and five control) of 10 L (20 × 20 × 25 cm) were covered with black adhesive film to limit the visual stimuli for fish and equipped with a shelter consisting of half a terracotta pot (150 mm depth, 53 mm entry height) to further reduce animals' stress. Aquaria were then filled with 8 L of water and inserted into a tank filled with 15 cm of flowing water to buffer eventual water temperature fluctuations. The tank was covered with a net (4‐cm mesh) to provide some shadow and prevent predation from birds. A diagram of the experimental setup is available in Figure S1. Water temperature was recorded hourly using a datalogger MINIKIN (Environmental Measuring Systems) for the entire study duration.

Similar‐sized A. nebulosus individuals were then selected by their total length (TL mean ± standard deviation = 123 ± 4 mm) and inserted individually into each aquarium, where they were acclimated for 24 h without any food to standardize their hunger level. For each experiment and treatment (see below), a total of 25 A. nebulosus was used. After acclimation, 20–30 mm (TL) P. parva were added at different densities (1, 3, 6, 12, 20) into the aquaria, with five replicates for each prey density and treatment (see below). Five controls, i.e. aquaria with no predator and prey density of 20, were simultaneously set to measure the stochastic mortality of P. parva. At the end of the experimental time, the predator was removed from the aquaria, and the alive and dead prey counted. The number of consumed prey was calculated by subtraction from the original prey density. Dead prey that showed evidence of attack or digestion were also considered consumed. Animals were used only once, avoiding pseudoreplication.

Day vs. night experiment

In the first experiment, day‐ and night‐time prey consumption were assessed to compare predation rates under low temperatures at different periods. The experiments lasted 6 h, being performed between 11:00 and 17:00 (day‐time) and 17:00 and 23:00 (night‐time).

24‐hour experiment

In the second experiment, prey consumption of A. nebulosus was measured with an experimental time of 24 h on two separate runs (using a total of 25 A. nebulosus per run), starting at 14:00. Due to fluctuating weather conditions, temperature varied between the two runs, displaying temperatures (mean ± standard error) of 2.9 ± 0.1 and 4.5 ± 0.1 °C, respectively. Both runs were therefore analysed separately as different treatments and are henceforward referred to as ‘3 °C’ and ‘4.5 °C’ treatments, for simplicity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed on R v. 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024). Predation periodicity was assessed using a Kruskal–Wallis test on the interaction between period and prey density.

Following Juliano (2001), FR type was determined with a polynomial logistic regression between the proportion of resource consumption (i.e. prey consumed/original prey density) and the original resource density (original prey number). The fitted polynomial function allows the distinction of type II responses characterized by a negative first‐order term as opposed to type III characterized by a positive first‐order term (Pritchard et al., 2017). Type II responses were calculated based on Rogers's (1972) ‘random predator’ equation, which takes into consideration the decline of a resource induced by its consumption: (1) Ne=N01−eaN0qhNe−T where Ne is the resource consumption, N0 is the original resource density, a is the instantaneous resource capture rate, h is the handling time and T is the time of the experiment in days (Juliano, 2001; Pritchard et al., 2017; Rogers, 1972). To compute resource consumption without iterative methods, Equation (1) can be solved with the Lambert‐W function, denoted Wx (McCoy & Bolker, 2008): (2) Ne=N0WahN0e−aT−hN0ah

Parameters a and h are estimated by fitting a model via maximum likelihood estimation with the frair R package (Pritchard et al., 2017). Using the same package, differences between the parameters of experimental runs were tested with the frair_compare function. A 95% confidence interval was also estimated via bootstrapping (n = 2500) for each run. The theoretical maximum feeding rate is the asymptote of the FR function, calculated as 1hT.

The daily per capita impact of the predation of a species can be evaluated with the impact potential (IP) equation (Dick et al., 2017): (3) IP=AB×1hT where AB is abundance. When calculating the maximum feeding rate 1hT is not possible, the prey consumption estimate at maximum prey density has beenused as a proxy following Dickey et al. (2020). An estimation of the total abundance from the original pond was obtained by dividing total biomass by the median of 80 individuals' weight distribution. To provide an accuracy interval of this estimation, the previous calculation was also done by adding and subtracting the standard error to the median. Following Equation (3), the IP of A. nebulosus is: (4) IP=biomassmedian±SE×1hT

where SE is the standard error.

RESULTS

Only one P. parva death was observed in the control aquaria for each experiment, representing a 0.5% stochastic mortality for both experiments, therefore the presented values are not corrected for the natural mortality.

Day vs. night experiment

Prey consumption varied significantly between periods across prey densities (χ9,40 = 30.12, p value <0.001), being constant at day and increasing with prey density at night (Figure 1). Only one predation event (4% of total replicates) was recorded during the day, corresponding to an average predation of 0.04. In contrast, predation was observed for 56% of the brown bullheads during the night, with an average predation of 0.84, although the number of prey consumed remained nearly null at low prey density.

DISCUSSION

Predation under cold temperatures is an understudied albeit important driver of fish winter survival (Rooke & Fox, 2020). Here, we assessed the winter predatory behaviour of non‐native populations of A. nebulosus. Despite prior observations that A. nebulosus ceases feeding at 3.5 °C (Richards & Ibara, 1978), our results substantiate predation even below 3 °C. The overwintering behaviour of A. nebulosus was formerly categorized as ‘quiescent’ (Shuter et al., 2012) or ‘dormant’ (Reeve et al., 2022), defined by a severely decreased activity, sheltering and the absence of active hunting. While metabolism reduction and some degree of sheltering have been described in this species by Crawshaw et al. (1982), active feeding of A. nebulosus below 6.5 8°C was also observed by gut content analysis in a Canadian lake (Keast, 1968).

As expected, the first experiment revealed that predation occurred almost exclusively during the night, which is consistent with the diel activity pattern observed in the wild (Dedual, 2002). At night, predation increased with prey density, although prey consumption remained nearly null at low prey density. The predation periodicity could result from a perception advantage of A. nebulosus over the prey P. parva at night due to acute mechanical detection of this former (Biedenbach, 1973) and its electroreception capacity (Eeuwes et al., 2008; Peters et al., 2002). This tendency is consistent with the nocturnal diel activity of adult individuals of the congeneric yellow bullhead Ameiurus natalis (Lesueur, 1819) (Reynolds & Casterlin, 1977) and black bullhead Ameiurus melas (Rafinesque, 1820) (Darnell & Meierotto, 1965).

Difference in the FR type of a single species in similar conditions is unusual. While at 4.5 °C A. nebulosus displayed an unsaturated linear FR, a 1.5 °C colder temperature showed a type II response, thus implying a maximum predation rate under the given conditions. Despite prey consumption saturation at high densities being related to either handling time or satiation (Jeschke et al., 2002), it seems unlikely that the apparent maximum feeding rate of A. nebulosus at 3 °C is due to satiation because it had a substantially higher prey consumption in a slightly warmer environment for the same prey density. As Crawshaw et al. (1982) observed a consequent decrease in spontaneous activity of A. nebulosus from 5 °C to 3 °C, handling time could have therefore been a limiting factor at the lowest temperature. Although handling time difference at slightly different temperatures was not statistically significant, we found a marginal p value and a difference in FR type considering the modest temperature difference between treatments. A higher replication number could have shown a significant pattern, but this was not possible because of strong weather variation after running the experiments because they were carried out outdoors. This rigidity can limit the replicability of the study, thus we do not recommend such outside experiments without sufficient replication and solid knowledge of all components of the experiment (e.g. focal animals, relative stability of the weather).

Using the Jeschke et al. (2004) definition, the linear FR cannot be classified as a type I FR as it lacks the plateau at high prey density and consequently cannot be characterized properly. Although including a higher prey density may have solved this issue, linear functional responses have already been observed in predators in specific conditions (e.g. Benhadi‐Marín et al., 2018; Kushner & Hovel, 2006). In particular, Benhadi‐Marín et al. (2018) observed either linear or type II FR in different thermic conditions, which was interpreted as a temperature‐dependent prey capture efficiency. Although P. parva's activity in the experiment's thermal conditions is unknown, A. nebulosus have been observed to maintain relatively high spontaneous activity at 5 °C (Crawshaw et al., 1982). Better low‐temperature performance of a predatory fish over its prey can lead to a major advantage of the former in those specific conditions (Yocom & Edsall, 1974; Schmidt & O'Brien, 1982; Ward & Morton‐Starner, 2002; but see Morley & Buckel, 2014), which together with the aforementioned nighttime perception advantage could lead to a negligible handling time, implying the functional response would solely depend on the attack rate (Coblentz et al., 2023). As attack success depends on performance in the environmental conditions, prey search and attack rate can decline if the metabolic costs are too high in comparison to benefits (Englund et al., 2011). This generally causes the attack rate to decrease with temperature (Englund et al., 2011), which could in our case become too low for the fish to reach satiation in the experimental time.

Impact potential suggests that fish of similar size as the P. parva preys (i.e. TL < 30 mm) could undergo considerable predation pressure with the estimated population of A. nebulosus, highlighting its foraging capacity at low temperature and indicating a non‐negligible resource consumption in a small closed environment (i.e. a pond) at such predator population density. Although maximum feeding rate was not obtained at 4.5 °C, its IP estimate using a conservative proxy (i.e. using the prey consumption at maximal prey density) was more than the double than at 3 °C, suggesting that resource consumption increases severely despite such a small temperature difference. This observation is not without consequence as a several Celsius degree temperature increase is predicted to occur throughout the freshwater ecosystem of Europe (IPCC, 2023). While A. nebulosus' opportunistic omnivorous diet should moderate such results, Rechulicz and Płaska's (2021) observations emphasized the piscivorous tendency of this species as they reach bigger size. In our experiment, P. parva was used as a proxy for juvenile fish that could either overwinter or spawn in early spring, when water temperature is still low. However, this potential impact result must be taken only as an order of magnitude as experimental conditions do not fully reflect the environmental complexity. Regardless, our results suggest that, even with conservative population estimation, the daily potential predation impact of A. nebulosus during winter should not be overlooked as it could dramatically affect fish recruitment.

CONCLUSIONS

Seconding Keast's (1968) field observation of prey ingestion from A. nebulosus below 6.5 °C, this experiment is further evidence of its predation capability in winter conditions. Together with its resistance to thermal stress, an opportunistic hunting behaviour at low temperatures could allow survival of individuals at risk of starvation, such as juveniles, as has been observed in other fish species (Rooke & Fox, 2020). Although results on temperature difference are unsettled, a key threshold in A. nebulosus' metabolism might occur around 3 °C as its spontaneous activity drastically diminishes, consequently affecting its predatory efficiency. This study adds another layer to the understanding of A. nebulosus' behaviour relative to temperature, which could be a main asset to its success outside its native range. Our results underline the potential impacts of winter predation on juvenile fish that are already subject to an overwintering bottleneck. As this species has been observed to become predominant in specific fish communities (Aislabie et al., 2019), such feeding activity might suppress other species' recruitment and progressively lead to A. nebulosus dominance. Finally, our results suggest a seemingly inconsequential increase of winter temperatures could exacerbate the predation impact of certain non‐native species during that period, therefore highlighting another detrimental consequence of the global warming.
