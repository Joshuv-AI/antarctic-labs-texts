Sex-specific associations between insomnia symptoms and mental health among Chinese young adults and the modified effect of CRY2 gene DNA methylation
Authors: Meng Fan, Yaqian Niu, Wanyu Che, Yuming Chen, Yuxuan Cao, Tangjun Jiang, Meng Wang, Tingting Li, Shuman Tao, Yajuan Yang, Liwei Zou, Fangbiao Tao, Xiaoyan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of insomnia and mental illness varies by sex. The mRNA expression level of the CRY gene in patients with insomnia is significantly decreased, and the expression level of the CRY2 gene is closely related to depression. However, sex differences in the link between insomnia and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, as well as whether CRY2 gene DNA methylation can regulate this association, are not yet fully understood.ObjectiveInvestigate sex-specific differences in the associations between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress among young adults. Furthermore, we explored whether the CRY2 clock DNA methylation moderates these relationships.MethodsA total of 1100 participants from two Chinese universities provided baseline data on insomnia symptoms and follow-up symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress assessments 1 year later. Among them, 605 participants contributed DNA methylation data for the CRY2 gene. Binary logistic regression and restricted cubic spline models were employed to analyze both linear and nonlinear relationships. Moderation analyses examined the effect of CRY2 gene DNA methylation, complemented by stratified analyses for sex-specific differences.ResultsSignificant sex-specific differences emerged in recognizing symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, with insomnia symptoms notably increasing the risk of symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. Logistic regression revealed a significant association between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress exclusively in females. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a consistent nonlinear relationship in both males and females. Notably, CRY2 gene DNA methylation negatively moderated the relationship between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, and had sex-specific difference.ConclusionElevated insomnia symptoms at baseline correlated with an increased risk of symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress after 1 year, with significant sex differences observed. Logistic regression revealed that this association was significant only in females, although a consistent nonlinear pattern was seen in both sexes. Furthermore, CRY2 gene DNA methylation acted as a negative moderator of this relationship. These results highlight the necessity of an early monitoring strategy for females incorporating epigenetic factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01970-5.

Introduction

Both insomnia and mental health issues are critical public health concerns among young adults [1]. The college years, in particular, represent a peak period for the onset of many common mental disorders [2]. A mental health survey conducted by the World Health Organization across 21 countries reveals that 20.3% of college students are affected by mental disorders [3]. Insomnia and mental health may both be affected by sex hormones, with notable sex differences in their prevalence, which begin to emerge during adolescence [4, 5]. Based on DSM-IV diagnostic criteria, the prevalence of insomnia in adolescents has been reported to range from 4% to 23.8% [6–9]. Additionally, females have been found to have a 1.41 times higher risk of developing insomnia compared to males [10]. A recent meta-analysis indicates that biological sex plays a crucial role in influencing mental health, with females being a significant determinant of mental disorders among college athletes [11].

The presence of psychological disorders in childhood and adolescence can have profound and lasting effects on individuals [12]. Growing evidence suggests that insomnia in young adults can negatively impact their healthy development, contributing to various physical and mental health issues, particularly anxiety, depression, and stress [13, 14]. Statistics reveal that 78.2% of college students experiencing insomnia symptoms also report symptoms of depression [15]. Meta-analyses of longitudinal studies further indicate that baseline insomnia disorder serves as a significant predictor of newly onset mental disorders at follow-up [13, 16, 17]. Several randomized controlled trials have shown that actively addressing insomnia symptoms reduces the risk of both new and recurrent depression during follow-up, with insomnia treatment proving more effective than direct depression treatment alone [18, 19]. In conclusion, insomnia may be an independent risk factor for psychological problems. The prevalence of insomnia and mental health disorders varies by sex. However, to the best of our knowledge, sex differences in the link between insomnia and mental health have not been thoroughly investigated. Given the high incidence of both mental disorders and sleep disturbances during the university years, this period presents a crucial opportunity to explore the association between insomnia symptoms and mental health.

Sleep disorders and mental health issues are prevalent among young adults, and investigating the potential mechanisms underlying the interaction between these conditions may aid in their prevention and treatment. Biological clock genes influence the onset and progression of diseases by regulating the expression of genes involved in the cell cycle [20]. These genes are highly sensitive to social and environmental factors, which can lead to changes in their epigenetic expression, often through mechanisms such as DNA methylation [21]. At present, the role of DNA methylation in the relationship between sleep and mental health has become a key area of research. A review suggests that there are both genotypic and phenotypic overlaps between sleep and mood disorders, which could be leveraged in the future studies to explore potential epigenetic factors influencing mood changes through sleep [22]. A population-based study conducted in China revealed that the mRNA expression level of the CRY gene was significantly lower in individuals with insomnia compared to those with healthy sleep patterns [23]. Furthermore, numerous animal and human studies have demonstrated that the expression level of the CRY2 gene is closely associated with depression [24]. However, it remains unclear whether CRY2 gene DNA methylation moderates the relationship between insomnia and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in young adults, and whether this moderating effect differs by sex.

To investigate sex differences in the relationship between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress among young adults, we analyzed longitudinal data. Additionally, we sought to further validate the potential regulatory role of CRY2 gene DNA methylation in this context. Based on the above evidence, we propose the following three hypotheses:

H1: There is an association between insomnia symptoms and anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms, and this association is more significant in females.

H2: DNA methylation of the CRY2 gene moderates the relationship between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress with a negative effect.

H3: The moderating effect of CRY2 gene DNA methylation on the relationship between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress varies by sex.

Methods

Participants

A convenience sample of college students was recruited from two universities in Hefei, Anhui Province, and Shangrao, Jiangxi Province, China, between April and May 2019. A total of 1179 students from four majors participated in the study. After excluding the participants who failed to complete the questionnaire survey and those with incomplete or incorrectly filled responses, 1135 valid responses were retained, yielding an effective rate of 96.3%, along with 744 valid blood samples. One year later, 1135 students were followed up, with 1100 completing the follow-up survey and 35 lost to follow-up, resulting in a loss rate of 3.1%. Of the participants, 422 (38.4%) were male and 678 (61.6%) were female.

The inclusion criteria required that both baseline and follow-up questionnaires were complete, and CRY2 gene DNA methylation data were available. Ultimately, 1100 students met the criteria, with 605 providing blood samples, including 205 baseline blood samples from men and 400 from women. Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. 20170291), and all participants provided informed consent voluntarily.

Procedures

Prior to the main investigation, the researchers standardized the research protocol and conducted a pre-survey with a randomly selected group of students. Based on the pre-survey results, the implementation plan was refined and improved accordingly. In the formal survey, students completed an electronic questionnaire on their smartphones, which included demographic and health behavior information, taking approximately 10–20 min during class. The following morning, after completing the questionnaire, students were organized by Uni-President to visit the nearest Level 3A hospital for a physical examination and blood sample collection.

Measures

Sociodemographic data

Sociodemographic variables were obtained from a self-designed electronic questionnaires, including sex (1 = male and 2 = female), family finances (1 = Good, 2 = Medium, and 3 = Poor), study load (1 = Light, 2 = General, and 3 = Heavy), number of friends (1 = < 3, 2 = 3–5, and 3 = > 5), self-reported health status (1 = Good, 2 = Medium, and 3 = Poor), cigarette use (1 = No and 2 = Yes), alcohol use (1 = No and 2 = Yes), physical activity, and problematic mobile phone use.

Cigarette and alcohol use were assessed using an adapted question from the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System: ‘Have you smoked or consumed alcohol in the past month?’

Physical activity (PA) was evaluated using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) to assess the physical activity of college students over the past week [25]. The questionnaire classified physical activity levels into three categories: low, medium, and high, with corresponding metabolic equivalent (MET) values of 3.3, 4.0, and 8.0, respectively (αIPAQ-SF = 0.89).

Problematic mobile phone use (PMPU) was assessed using the Self-rating Questionnaire for Adolescent Problematic Mobile Phone Use (SQAPMPU) [26]. This questionnaire consists of 13 items across three dimensions, with each item rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (never) to 5 (always), yielding a total score ranging from 13 to 65. Higher scores indicate greater dependence on mobile phones, with a total score of 28 or higher defined as PMPU (αSQAPMPU = 0.92).

Insomnia symptoms

At baseline, the Chinese version of the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) was used to assess insomnia symptoms among college students over the past 2 weeks [27]. The total score ranges from 0 to 28, calculated by summing the scores of the seven items, each rated from 0 to 4. Higher scores indicate more severe insomnia symptoms. A total ISI score of less than 9 was classified as no insomnia symptoms, while a score of 9 or above indicated the presence of insomnia symptoms (αISI = 0.90).

Mental health

During the follow-up, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) [28] was administered to assess the anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms of college students over the past week. The scale comprises 21 self-report items, each containing seven items. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 'completely inconsistent' to 'completely consistent,' with scores from 0 to 3, reflecting the degree of agreement. The subscale scores are calculated by summing the item scores and multiplying by 2. For the depression subscale, scores between 0 and 9 were considered normal, while scores of 10 or above indicated depressive symptoms. For the anxiety subscale, scores of 0–7 were categorized as normal, and scores of 8 or higher indicated anxiety symptoms. Similarly, stress subscale scores ranging from 0 to 14 were regarded as normal, while scores of 15 or above signified stress symptoms (αDASS-21 = 0.96).

CRY2 gene DNA methylation detection

All blood samples were stored at − 80 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted using the TGuide cell/Tissue Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA methylation levels were assessed using MethylTarget™ target region methylation sequencing (Data processing unit: Genetic Center of Shanghai Tianhao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Bisulfite treatment allowed the differentiation of methylated and unmethylated cytosines, as methylated cytosine (C) remains unchanged, while unmethylated cytosine is converted to uracil (U), enabling sequencing-based detection of methylation in target fragments. The specific workflow included the following steps: 1) Prediction of CpG islands: CpG islands in candidate genes were predicted using EMBOSS Explorer software. 2) Sample quality control: Genomic DNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA quality was evaluated using the Nanodrop 2000. 3) Primer design and optimization of single-point PCR conditions, followed by the optimization of the multiplex PCR primer panel. 4) Bisulfite treatment: Genomic DNA underwent bisulfite conversion, converting unmethylated cytosine (C) to thymine (U). Multiplex PCR was performed on target regions of clock genes, with specific tag sequences added to the samples. 5) Sequencing: After sample quantification and accurate determination of library molar concentration, high-throughput sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq platform using 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing. FastQ data were generated and recorded to six decimal places. CRY2 gene DNA methylation was modeled as a continuous moderator in all analyses.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the young adults. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Chi-square test was employed to assess the detection rates of anxiety, depression, and stress across different covariates. We conducted a literature review and identified the following covariates using directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Supplementary Fig. 1): family finances, study load, number of friends, self-reported health status, cigarette use, alcohol use, physical activity, and problematic mobile phone use. Multicollinearity diagnosis was conducted, and it was found that there was severe multicollinearity in the study load (VIF > 10). To ensure model stability and mitigate bias from multicollinearity, we excluded study load from the final adjusted model. The remaining covariates all had VIFs < 5 (Supplementary Table 1).

To test Hypothesis 1 (H1), we first employed a binary logistic regression model to examine the association between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). An interaction term of insomnia × sex was constructed to test the interaction effect, with results reported as P-values. The potential covariates were adjusted for, including baseline self-reported health status (Good/Medium/Poor), cigarette use (No/Yes), alcohol use (No/Yes), physical activity (Low/Medium/High), problematic smartphone use (No/Yes), number of friends (< 3/3–5/ > 5), and family finances (Good/Medium/Poor). Next, a restricted cubic spline model was utilized to assess whether there was a nonlinear dose–response relationship between baseline insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress at the 1-year follow-up, after multivariable adjustment. Three nodes were chosen for the model, as they provided a robust test for linear deviations. Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex to explore potential sex-specific associations between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in young adults. In the binary logistic regression model, insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress are all binary categorical variables. In the restricted cubic spline model, insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress are all continuous variables.

To test Hypotheses 2 and 3 (H2 and H3), we used the PROCESS plug-in in SPSS software to examine whether CRY2 gene DNA methylation moderated the effect of insomnia symptoms and potential sex-specific differences. Mean centering was applied in the moderation regression analysis to avoid multicollinearity, with the significance level set at α = 0.05. A moderation analysis was conducted with insomnia symptoms as an independent variable, CRY2 gene DNA methylation as a moderator, symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, respectively, as a dependent variable, and the covariates were the same as above. When the interaction effect (insomnia × CRY2 gene DNA methylation) was significant, we further employed the Johnson–Neyman technique to explore sex differences in the moderation effects. In the mediation analysis, insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress were all continuous variables.

The rms package in R (version 4.3.1) was used to analyze the restricted cubic spline model, while the interactions package in R (version 4.3.1) was utilized for the Johnson–Neyman analysis. SPSS (version 23.0) was employed for the remainder of the data analyses.

Tests for interaction effects revealed that the insomnia × sex interaction term was significant for anxiety (P = 0.03) and stress symptoms (P = 0.012). The sex-stratified analysis further confirmed these patterns: After adjusting for covariates, insomnia symptoms were significantly associated with anxiety (OR = 3.961, 95% CI: 2.266–6.23) and stress (OR = 5.727, 95% CI: 2.672–12.274) among females, with the strength of associations substantially higher than those in the total sample. In contrast, as shown in Table 2, the associations were not statistically significant in males (P > 0.05). The insomnia × sex interaction term on depressive symptoms did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.141). However, the sex-stratified analysis showed a pattern similar to that of anxiety and stress symptoms: Among females, insomnia symptoms were a significant risk factor for depressive symptoms; females with insomnia symptoms had approximately 3.04 times the risk of reporting depressive symptoms compared to those without insomnia symptoms (OR = 3.044, 95% CI: 1.711–5.414). In males, the association between insomnia symptoms and an increased risk of depressive symptoms was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Moderating effect of CRY2 gene DNA methylation on the association between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in college students

In this study, the mean levels of CRY2 gene DNA methylation were 0.007 (SD = 0.002) among young adults, with 0.006 (SD = 0.002) in males and 0.007 (SD = 0.002) in females. Moderating analysis indicated that CRY2 gene DNA methylation negatively moderated the relationship between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in Chinese young adults. A 0.01-unit increment in CRY2 gene methylation was significantly associated with a 59.0% reduction in the impact of insomnia on anxiety symptoms, a 57.3% reduction in its effect on depression, and a 71.0% reduction in its effect on stress. Discussion

In this prospective, population-based longitudinal study of Chinese young adults, we investigated sex-specific differences in the associations between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, and for the first time, explored whether the clock gene CRY2 moderates this association. Our findings reveal that the association between insomnia symptoms and mental health symptoms exhibits significant sex differences, which are primarily observed in the females. Specifically, the risks of experiencing anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms among females with insomnia symptoms are approximately 4 times, 3 times, and 5.7 times those of females without insomnia symptoms, respectively, whereas no similar associations are observed in males. This finding supports the hypothesis in H1 that ‘the association is more significant in females,’ with particularly sufficient evidence in the context of anxiety and stress symptoms. Although the interaction effect for depressive symptoms did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.141), the stratified results—showing a significant association in females but not in males—suggest that potential sex differences may exist. Both males and females exhibited a nonlinear association between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress at follow-up. Importantly, the adverse effects of insomnia symptoms on symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress were to some extent moderated by CRY2 gene DNA methylation, with this moderating effect observed only in females, but not in males.

To assess symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in college students, we utilized the three subscales of the DASS-21, which allow for the differentiation of symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. Our findings revealed that 17.1% of the students exhibited symptoms of anxiety, 16.6% showed signs of depression, and 7.2% reported experiencing stress. Additionally, we observed significant sex differences in the prevalence of these conditions. These results are consistent with findings from prior studies [29–31]. However, not all studies support the notion that girls experience higher levels of depression and stress. For instance, a longitudinal study conducted in China found that female college students exhibited higher levels of anxiety compared to their male counterparts, but no significant sex differences were observed in depression or stress symptoms [32]. Despite this, most studies support the existence of sex-specific differences in the detection of anxiety symptoms among college students. These differences may be linked to biological factors (e.g., the influence of sex hormones on circadian rhythms) [33], as well as psychosocial stressors affecting young females, including academic pressure, societal expectations, and sex-based caregiving roles.

Our study investigated the association between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, revealing that insomnia was associated with an increased risk of anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms in young adults 1 year later. As young adults are in the peak period of mental disorders [34, 35]. This indicates that insomnia may be a precursor symptom of emerging symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. The sex-stratified models revealed that insomnia symptoms were associated with the highest risk of stress symptoms in females (OR = 5.727), followed by anxiety (OR = 3.961) and depression symptoms (OR = 3.044). In males, insomnia symptoms impacted depression symptoms most strongly (OR = 1.362). This granular analysis highlights that the association between insomnia and mental health is not uniform; its effects are modulated by both sex and the specific outcome. While binary logistic regression analysis revealed sex differences in this association, restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a significant nonlinear relationship between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in both males and females. The common binary screening threshold is ISI ≥ 10, but our study found that the risk began to increase significantly when ISI = 1.75 among young adults. This indicates that the onset of risk increase was much earlier than commonly believed, and the traditional cut-off point may have excluded a large group of people with significantly high risk (ISI: 1.75–9). Sex differences in the relationship between sleep and mental health have been a longstanding focus of research. While the link between insomnia and mental health has been extensively studied, findings on sex differences in this association remain inconsistent. Consistent with our results, a study conducted in the USA among young adults found that girls with insomnia symptoms were more likely to experience stress or develop stress symptoms in the future [36]. Some earlier studies have reported different outcomes. For instance, a longitudinal analysis of young Chinese men found that insomnia was a predictor of anxiety symptoms after 8 months, though it did not predict the onset of depression symptoms [37]. Although research findings on this association remain inconsistent, our study highlights the importance of insomnia as an early factor in monitoring the development of subsequent symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in females.

The genetic and epigenetic background of insomnia and mental health is still not fully elucidated. In our study, DNA methylation of the CRY2 gene had a negative moderating effect on the association between insomnia symptoms and anxiety, stress, and depression symptoms. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, the β values of the interaction terms are as follows: −59.040 for anxiety, −57.254 for depression, and −70.910 for stress, indicating a substantial moderating effect. This finding aligns with the previous research. Population-based studies have demonstrated that, relative to healthy controls, patients with bipolar depressive disorder show reduced CRY2 mRNA levels in blood monocytes following total sleep deprivation [38]. Animal studies have indicated that when inbred mice with inherently high anxiety levels undergo sleep deprivation, CRY2 mRNA expression becomes aberrant [39]. Furthermore, a study investigating common and distinct brain structural abnormalities in patients with bipolar or unipolar depression, as well as their associations with circadian rhythm gene expression, revealed that the CRY2 gene is pivotal in the right thalamic dysfunction observed in unipolar depression patients [24]. Additionally, animal studies using CRY2-deficient genetic mouse models have established a direct link between CRY2 and depression-like behaviors, suggesting that CRY2 plays a critical role in regulating depression-related emotional states and modulating the chronobiological gene expression profile in the mouse amygdala [40]. After stratification by sex, our study found that CRY2 gene DNA methylation in females could negatively moderate the association between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. These findings provide evidence that CRY2 gene may act as a link between insomnia and mental health. While many studies have explored the epigenetic mechanisms linking sleep and mental health, numerous circadian clock genes remain underexplored or not fully understood. Future research is necessary to further assess the role and impact of methylation in different biological clock genes on the connection between insomnia and mental health.

This study has several limitations. First, as this study did not collect the participants' previous history of mental illness and information on the use of psychotropic drugs, it is impossible to rule out the confounding influence of existing psychopathological states on the observation results. However, this study focused on the symptom dimension rather than the disease entity, which reduced the impact of clinical diagnostic heterogeneity. Second, due to its observational nature, the findings cannot establish a definitive causal relationship between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. Longitudinal and experimental researches are warranted to elucidate the causal pathways underlying this association. Nevertheless, despite these constraints, our results offer significant implications for the early prediction of symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, potentially informing preventive strategies and public health interventions. Third, both the independent and outcome variables were self-reported using scales, which may have led to an over- or underestimation of symptoms and introduced recall bias. Fourth, although we adjusted for key covariates such as family economic conditions, self-reported health status, number of friends, cigarette use, alcohol consumption, physical activity (PA), and problematic mobile phone use (PMPU), there may still be unmeasured confounding variables that could have influenced the results. Lastly, while our study assessed insomnia symptoms only at baseline, CRY2 gene DNA methylation was also measured solely at baseline. It limits our ability to account for biomarker fluctuations during follow-up that might independently affect mental health or mediate the insomnia–mental health relationship. Future studies with repeated biomarker measures could clarify these dynamics.

Despite its limitations, this study leveraged longitudinal data to examine sex differences in the temporal associations linking insomnia symptoms to subsequent symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. Most prior research has focused on clinical samples, which may have overestimated the correlation between insomnia and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress. In contrast, this study, conducted among college students, may better reflect the impact of insomnia on anxiety, depression, and stress in the general population. Furthermore, by exploring epigenetic changes in circadian clock genes, this study elucidated the biological mechanism of the CRY2 gene DNA methylation in the association between insomnia and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress, addressing gaps in both domestic and international researches. These findings provide a new perspective for the early warning signal identification of symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in females.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that insomnia symptoms are associated with an increased risk of anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms among young adults 1 year later, and there are a sex differences. In both males and females, insomnia symptoms exhibited a nonlinear relationship with symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress after 1 year. The detrimental effects were largely moderated by CRY2 gene DNA methylation, with this modulation being significant only in females, not in males. Overall our study reveals that insomnia symptoms are a potent, independent predictor of psychopathology, but its impact is epigenetically moderated by CRY2 gene methylation in a sex-specific manner.
