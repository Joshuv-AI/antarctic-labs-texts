Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)-Associated Secondary Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis Mimicking Sepsis and Multiorgan Dysfunction: A Case Report
Authors: Ajay Chauhan, Kapil Sharma, Aparna Valsan, Prajwal Gowda, Sumita Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome. It can closely mimic severe sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction. Intravesical bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), used for non-muscle invasive bladder cancer, is rarely associated with systemic infection or immune-mediated complications. We report a 50-year-old man who developed high-grade fever and progressive jaundice shortly after intravesical BCG therapy. He had hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, hepatitis, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury, marked hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, and hypertriglyceridemia. Urine polymerase chain reaction detected Mycobacterium bovis. Bone marrow examination revealed hemophagocytosis. He fulfilled six HLH-2004 criteria and had an HScore of 299, indicating a very high probability of HLH. After starting injection dexamethasone, there was transient clinical and biochemical improvement, but he deteriorated, and despite escalation to etoposide, he died on Day 19. This case highlights BCG-associated secondary HLH as a sepsis mimic and emphasizes the need for early ferritin testing, HLH scoring, and targeted mycobacterial evaluation in post-BCG patients with persistent fever, cytopenias, hepatitis, and organ dysfunction.

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a severe hyperinflammatory syndrome. It is caused by uncontrolled activation of macrophages and cytotoxic T cells and results in cytokine excess and rapid organ injury [1]. In adults, HLH is usually secondary to an identifiable trigger, such as infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease, or immune-modifying therapy [1]. Its clinical presentation often overlaps with severe sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction, making early recognition difficult in routine practice [1].

The Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis-2004 (HLH-2004) criteria are widely used for diagnosis, although they were developed primarily for pediatric HLH [2]. The HScore provides a practical probability-based tool for adults with suspected secondary HLH, especially when tests such as soluble interleukin-2 receptor levels or natural killer cell activity are unavailable [3].

Intravesical bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) is used as adjuvant therapy for selected patients with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer [4]. Although most adverse effects are local, systemic complications may occur and can include fever, hepatitis, pneumonitis, sepsis-like illness, and disseminated Mycobacterium bovis infection [5]. BCG-associated HLH is rare but clinically important because it may initially resemble sepsis. We report a case of secondary HLH following intravesical BCG therapy, presenting with fever, pancytopenia, hepatitis, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury, and multiorgan dysfunction.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old man was admitted with a one-week history of high-grade fever, up to 103°F, and progressive jaundice. The patient had no known history of diabetes mellitus or hypertension. He was a non-smoker with no previous history of tuberculosis or use of immunosuppressive drugs. There was no family history suggestive of HLH. Symptoms began two days after the third intravesical BCG instillation. It was given after transurethral resection of a bladder tumor for low-grade urothelial carcinoma of the urinary bladder. Details of the BCG strain, dose, and baseline investigations before BCG therapy were not available because the instillation had been performed at another hospital.

On admission, he was febrile but hemodynamically stable. Examination showed icterus and hepatosplenomegaly. There was no lymphadenopathy, ascites, or pedal edema. Cardiopulmonary and neurological examinations were otherwise unremarkable.

Baseline and serial investigations revealed pancytopenia and organ dysfunction (Table 1). He was managed in a high dependency unit and started on broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics for possible sepsis. Tests for dengue, malaria, scrub typhus, leptospirosis, enteric fever, acute viral hepatitis (A, B, C, and E), human immunodeficiency virus, brucellosis, Epstein-Barr virus, parvovirus B19, and cytomegalovirus were negative. Autoimmune workup, including antinuclear antibody, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody profile, serum complement levels, and autoimmune hepatitis panel, was also negative. Blood, urine, and sputum cultures were sterile. However, urine polymerase chain reaction testing detected M. bovis.

After steroid initiation, leukocyte and platelet counts improved transiently, renal function normalized, bilirubin declined, and ferritin decreased gradually (Table 1). Despite early improvement with dexamethasone, etoposide was initiated on Day 13 at 150 mg/m² twice weekly because of high-risk HLH with multiorgan dysfunction and recurrent cytopenia.

From Day 17 onward, he deteriorated again, with severe cytopenia and inflammatory rebound. On Day 18, total leukocyte count was 0.08 × 10³/µL, platelet count was 14 × 10³/µL, C-reactive protein was 187 mg/L, and procalcitonin was 8.2 ng/mL. This was accompanied by rising bilirubin and worsening renal function (Table 1). Despite supportive care, escalation of antimicrobials, and ongoing HLH-directed therapy, he developed sudden cardiorespiratory collapse and died on Day 19.

Discussion

This patient developed a sepsis-like illness with fever, pancytopenia, hepatitis, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury shortly after intravesical BCG therapy. This overlap with severe sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction can delay recognition of HLH, particularly in adults, where the early presentation is often non-specific [1]. In this case, the persistence of fever, worsening cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, marked hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, and hypertriglyceridemia prompted evaluation for secondary HLH.

The diagnosis was supported by both HLH-2004 criteria and the HScore. Although the HLH-2004 criteria were developed primarily for pediatric HLH, they remain widely used in clinical practice [2]. The HScore is useful in adults with suspected secondary HLH, especially when soluble interleukin-2 receptor levels and natural killer cell activity are unavailable [3]. Our patient fulfilled six of eight HLH-2004 criteria and had an HScore of 299, indicating a very high probability of HLH.

In the present case, the main differential diagnoses were severe sepsis with multiorgan dysfunction, tropical infections, acute viral or autoimmune hepatitis, hematological malignancy, and disseminated BCG infection. These were clinically relevant because the patient presented with fever, jaundice, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, renal dysfunction, and elevated inflammatory markers. Routine cultures were sterile, and evaluation for dengue, malaria, scrub typhus, leptospirosis, enteric fever, viral hepatitis, HIV, brucellosis, Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, parvovirus B19, and autoimmune liver disease was unrevealing.

Intravesical BCG is a live attenuated strain of M. bovis used as adjuvant therapy for selected patients with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer [4]. Most adverse effects are local, but systemic complications may occur and may reflect disseminated BCG infection, immune-mediated inflammation, or both [5]. Reported cases of BCG-associated HLH have described a similar pattern of persistent fever, cytopenias, hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, and multiorgan involvement after BCG instillation [6-8]. The pathogenesis of BCG-associated HLH is likely to involve both infectious and immune-mediated mechanisms. Intravesical BCG can rarely produce systemic immune activation through viable M. bovis infection or persistent BCG antigenic stimulation. In susceptible patients, this may trigger dysregulated macrophage and cytotoxic T-cell activation, leading to cytokine excess, hemophagocytosis, cytopenias, hepatitis, coagulopathy, and multiorgan dysfunction [1,5-8]. In our patient, urine polymerase chain reaction detected M. bovis, while blood, urine, and sputum cultures were sterile, and imaging did not show granulomas or lymphadenopathy. This finding supports BCG-related disease, but it does not by itself prove disseminated infection. Therefore, the diagnosis should be interpreted in the full clinical context, including the temporal relation to BCG, exclusion of common infectious and autoimmune triggers, and fulfilment of HLH criteria.

Previously reported cases of BCG-associated HLH have described a similar sepsis-like presentation after intravesical BCG, with persistent fever, cytopenias, hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, and multiorgan involvement [6-8]. Similar to those reports, our patient had rapid clinical deterioration despite HLH-directed therapy. However, microbiological confirmation has varied across published cases. In our case, urine PCR detected M. bovis, which supported BCG-related disease, but sterile-site confirmation was not available. Therefore, this case adds to the limited literature by highlighting the diagnostic overlap between post-BCG sepsis-like illness and secondary HLH while emphasizing the uncertainty that may remain when confirmatory mycobacterial testing from sterile sites is not performed.

The therapeutic course was consistent with HLH, contributing significantly to the illness. After dexamethasone was started on Day 7, leukocyte and platelet counts improved transiently, renal function normalized, bilirubin declined, and ferritin decreased. Etoposide was initiated on Day 13 at 150 mg/m² twice weekly because of high-risk HLH with multiorgan dysfunction syndrome and recurrent cytopenia. Despite this, the patient deteriorated after Day 17 with severe cytopenia, inflammatory rebound, worsening bilirubin, and renal dysfunction. This course may represent refractory HLH, superimposed infection during profound cytopenia, or both. Adult HLH remains associated with high mortality, particularly when diagnosis is delayed or organ dysfunction is already established [1,9].

This case highlights two important clinical messages. First, secondary HLH should be considered when a patient develops persistent fever, cytopenias, hepatitis, coagulopathy, and marked hyperferritinemia after intravesical BCG therapy. Second, a sepsis diagnosis should not exclude parallel evaluation for HLH when cytopenias and ferritin trends are disproportionate to routine bacterial sepsis. Early ferritin testing, HLH scoring, marrow evaluation, and targeted mycobacterial testing may help clinicians recognize this complication earlier.

A limitation of this report is the unavailability of details regarding the BCG strain, dose, and baseline investigations before instillation. Therefore, we could not assess whether a specific BCG preparation, dosing-related factor, or pre-existing hematological or biochemical abnormality influenced the development or severity of HLH in this patient.

Conclusions

BCG-associated secondary HLH should be considered in patients who develop persistent fever, cytopenias, hepatitis, coagulopathy, and organ dysfunction after intravesical BCG therapy. The presentation may closely mimic sepsis with multiorgan dysfunction, making early recognition difficult. Marked hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and progressive cytopenias should prompt early HLH scoring and marrow evaluation. In this case, transient improvement after dexamethasone did not prevent relapse, and the patient died despite escalation to etoposide. Early suspicion and parallel evaluation for HLH are important when post-BCG patients fail to follow the expected course of routine sepsis.
