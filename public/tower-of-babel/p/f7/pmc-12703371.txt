The disruption of COPII vesicles activates HSF-1 through SEC-23
Authors: Zhidong He, Na Tang, Hao Liu, Xueqing Wang, Yue Yin, Chao Peng, Yidong Shen, Yaming Jiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHSF-1 is a highly conserved transcription factor that plays a central role in protecting organisms from diverse cellular stresses. However, the mechanisms by which HSF-1 senses and responds to different types of stress remain incompletely understood. COPII-coated vesicles, responsible for transporting cargo from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus, are essential for protein secretion and cellular homeostasis. Disruption of these vesicles impairs protein secretion and triggers severe proteotoxic stress. Here, we show that HSF-1 directly monitors COPII vesicle dysfunction through interactions with the core COPII component SEC-23, in both Caenorhabditis elegans and NIH3T3 cells. Inhibition of SEC-23 or SAR-1 disrupts COPII vesicle formation, leading to the release of HSF-1 from the COPII complex. This release induces a specific transcriptomic change to restore protein homeostasis. Our findings reveal a conserved mechanism by which HSF-1 responds to COPII vesicle dysregulation, providing new insights into the HSF-1-centered proteostasis network.Keywords: HSF-1/HSF1, COPII, proteostasis

Introduction

Protein homeostasis, or proteostasis, refers to the maintenance of a stable and functional proteome, tightly regulated by a complex molecular machinery. Disruption of proteostasis is closely linked to a wide range of diseases. Every stage in a protein's life cycle, from synthesis to degradation, is critical to protein homeostasis (Jayaraj et al., 2020).

One essential process in proteostasis is the transport of newly synthesized proteins from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to other subcellular compartments. This intracellular trafficking ensures proteins to be delivered to their functional destinations and alleviates ER stress (Huh et al., 2003; Kakoi et al., 2013; Zacharogianni et al., 2014). The formation of coat protein complex II (COPII)-derived and coated vesicles (hereafter referred to as COPII vesicles) is a key step in this pathway. COPII vesicles, which bud from the ER, play a crucial role in protein trafficking. COPII consists of a group of conserved proteins, namely SAR1, SEC23, SEC24, SEC13, and SEC31. During COPII assembly, the small GTPase SAR1 is first activated at ER exit sites (ERES) by binding to GTP, which facilitates the insertion of its N-terminal region into the ER membrane. This is followed by the recruitment of the SEC23–SEC24 heterodimer through interactions with SAR1 to form the inner layer of the vesicle coat. Finally, the SEC13–SEC31 heterotetramer assembles to form the outer layer of the COPII coat (Jensen and Schekman, 2011). Given the critical role of COPII vesicles in protein secretion and intracellular trafficking, mutations in COPII components are associated with diseases characterized by impaired protein homeostasis (Routledge et al., 2010). Yet, how cells detect and respond to disruptions in COPII-mediated trafficking remains poorly understood.

Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) is a master transcription factor protecting protein homeostasis (Akerfelt et al., 2010; Gomez-Pastor et al., 2018). Initially identified for its role in inducing the expression of molecular chaperones, such as heat shock proteins (HSPs), in response to heat shock (Parker and Topol, 1984; Topol et al., 1985), HSF1 has since been shown to regulate protein quality control under a variety of cellular stress conditions, including translation errors and the accumulation of protein aggregates (Brandman et al., 2012; Li et al., 2016). However, the role of HSF1 in monitoring intracellular protein trafficking, a process essential for proteostasis, remains unexplored.

In this study, we demonstrate in the nematode C. elegans that HSF-1 interacts with the COPII component SEC-23. When COPII-mediated protein transport is disrupted, HSF-1 is activated to drive a distinctive transcriptomic change for maintaining proteostasis. Furthermore, we show that the interaction between HSF1 and SEC23 is well conserved in mammals. In NIH3T3 cells, perturbation of COPII similarly releases HSF1 from the COPII complex, leading to its nuclear translocation. These findings reveal a conserved mechanism by which HSF1 monitors COPII vesicle function to ensure protein homeostasis.

Results

HSF-1 interacts with the COPII component SEC-23 and responds to disruption of the COPII complex

Interacting proteins are essential for HSF-1/HSF1 to detect stress signals and orchestrate appropriate responses (Fritah et al., 2009; Neef et al., 2014; Burchfiel et al., 2021). Therefore, we performed mass spectrometry (MS) for unidentified HSF-1 interactors to explore how HSF-1 perceives various disruptions of proteostasis. To probe HSF-1 interactome with minimal intervention, we genetically tagged endogenous hsf-1 with gfp::3×FLAG by CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Zhou et al., 2019). hsf-1::gfp::3×FLAG and wild-type (WT) worms were grown to Day 1 of adulthood and subjected to anti-GFP immunoprecipitation (IP) (Figure 1A). The proteins specifically co-precipitated with HSF-1::GFP::3×FLAG were identified by MS (Figure 1B; Supplementary Table S1). Among these, a subset of proteins stood out: SAR-1, SEC-23, SEC-24.1, and NPP-20, which are subunits of the COPII complex (Figure 1C; Jensen and Schekman, 2011).

The well-conserved COPII complex mediates formation of the membrane vesicles that transport proteins from the ER to other organelles such as the Golgi apparatus. Its malfunction blocks protein secretion and results in the abnormal accumulation of secreted proteins in the ER (Jensen and Schekman, 2011). However, little is known about its interaction with the HSF-1-centered machinery for proteostasis. The interaction of COPII components with HSF-1 suggests that HSF-1 could monitor COPII and respond to its dysregulation. If this hypothesis is correct, inhibiting COPII components should activate HSF-1. Upon activation, HSF-1 is translocated to the nucleus and drives the expression of its target genes (e.g. hsp genes) (Li et al., 2017).

To test this hypothesis, we treated worms with RNA interference (RNAi) against the COPII components identified as HSF-1 interactors and examined HSF-1 activity (Supplementary Figure S1A and B). We observed that RNAi-mediated knockdown of sar-1 or sec-23 increased nuclear localization of HSF-1::GFP (Figure 1D). SEC-24, which forms a heterodimer with SEC-23, has two homolog genes in C. elegans, sec-24.1, and sec-24.2. While RNAi against either homolog alone had no effect potentially due to insufficient RNAi efficiency (Supplementary Figure S1A and C), simultaneous knockdown of both genes promoted nuclear localization of HSF-1::GFP, similar to the effect of RNAi against sar-1 or sec-23 (Supplementary Figure S1C). We also examined other COPII components by RNAi and observed little effect on HSF-1::GFP nuclear localization (Supplementary Figure S1C). RNAi against sar-1 or sec-23 also upregulated the protein level of HSF-1::GFP (Supplementary Figure S1D), suggesting a multi-layer activation of HSF-1 upon the disruption of COPII.

Consistent with these findings, RNAi against sar-1 or sec-23 induced the transcription of hsp genes (Figure 1E; Kovacs et al., 2019). However, there was no change in the mRNA levels of hsf-1 (Figure 1E), indicating that COPII components do not regulate hsf-1 through its transcription. SAR-1 initiates COPII formation, and SEC-23–SEC-24 heterodimer is the first to be recruited into the COPII complex (Jensen and Schekman, 2011). Therefore, HSF-1 could monitor COPII at the initial stages of its generation.

To further confirm the physical interaction between HSF-1 and COPII components, we performed co-IP experiments with purified proteins expressed in High Five insect cells. We found that HSF-1::EGFP co-immunoprecipitated with SEC-23::FLAG but not with SAR-1::FLAG (Figure 1F; Supplementary Figure S2). This indicates that HSF-1 interacts with the COPII complex specifically through SEC-23.

HSF-1 promotes proteostasis upon the disruption of COPII-mediated protein transport

Our findings that HSF-1 interacts with SEC-23 and is activated upon RNAi against sar-1 or sec-23 (Figure 1) strongly suggest that it monitors COPII to mitigate COPII-related disruption of protein homeostasis. We first examined VIT-2::GFP upon the inactivation of COPII and HSF-1. VIT-2 is a yolk protein expressed in the intestine and secreted into the body cavity to provide nutrients for the oocytes (Grant and Hirsh, 1999; Perez et al., 2017). When the formation of COPII vesicles was inhibited by suppressing sar-1 or sec-23, intestinal VIT-2::GFP level increased by 5 folds and punctate VIT-2::GFP aggregates also appeared in worm intestine (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figure S3A). These results indicate that the VIT-2::GFP accumulates and forms aggregates when its secretion is blocked. Interestingly, double RNAi against hsf-1 together with sar-1 or sec-23 did not further upregulate VIT-2::GFP but remarkably increased its aggregation (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figure S3A). In contrast, hsf-1 RNAi alone had no effect on either the level or the aggregation of VIT-2::GFP when its secretion was not interrupted (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figure S3A). Therefore, hsf-1 maintains the homeostasis of the accumulated VIT-2::GFP in the ER upon the disruption of COPII vesicles.

We next checked the fluorescent signal and protein level of DAF-28::GFP. DAF-28 is an insulin ligand, mainly secreted from ASI, ASJ, and PQR neurons and hinder gut but degraded in coelomocytes (Kao et al., 2007). DAF-28::GFP fluoresces only when properly folded (Safra et al., 2014; Safra and Henis-Korenblit, 2014), making it useful in tracking protein secretion and folding. Similarly, disrupting COPII through RNAi against sar-1 or sec-23 blocked the secretion of DAF-28::GFP and increased its GFP signal in the hinder gut (Figure 2B; Supplementary Figure S3B). The protein level of DAF-28::GFP was also mildly increased upon RNAi against sar-1 or sec-23 (Figure 2C; Supplementary Figure S3C), potentially due to impaired secretion and subsequent degradation in coelomocytes. hsf-1 RNAi alone did not change the GFP signal or protein level of DAF-28::GFP (Figure 2B and C; Supplementary Figure S3B and C), suggesting that hsf-1 does not regulate DAF-28 homeostasis in unstressed conditions. Moreover, when hsf-1 was suppressed together with sar-1 or sec-23, the fluorescence of DAF-28::GFP decreased, whereas its protein level further increased (Figure 2B and C; Supplementary Figure S3B and C). Therefore, hsf-1 is required for proper folding of DAF-28::GFP when its secretion is impaired. The misfolded DAF-28::GFP could form aggregates resistant to degradation, leading to the observed increase in its protein levels.

COPII defects swell the ER with un-transported cargos, potentially disrupting the homeostasis of non-secreted proteins as well. We then examined whether the activation of HSF-1 upon COPII disruption modulates general proteostasis. Polyglutamine (PolyQ), discovered in a group of neurodegenerative diseases, is prone to aggregation and widely used as markers of cytoplasmic protein quality control (Ross, 2002). Osmotic stress is known to induce cytoplasmic PolyQ aggregation (Moronetti Mazzeo et al., 2012). After 4 h induction with 500 mM NaCl, all the worms under luc2 RNAi showed the aggregates of PolyQ (Q44) as reported (Figure 2D). Suppressing sar-1 greatly delayed the formation of Q44 in an hsf-1-dependent manner, whereas hsf-1 RNAi alone had no effect (Figure 2D).

Heat resistance is another benchmark of general proteostasis (Akerfelt et al., 2010). Worms subjected to sar-1 RNAi exhibited a ∼2-fold improvement in recovery after heat shock compared to controls (Figure 2E). This enhanced thermotolerance was dependent on hsf-1, as it was abolished when hsf-1 was simultaneously knocked down (Figure 2E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HSF-1 controls the homeostasis of the secreted as well as other proteins upon the interruption of COPII vesicles.

Defective COPII induces specific HSF-1 transcription activity

HSF-1 is a master transcription factor that regulates proteostasis (Akerfelt et al., 2010; Gomez-Pastor et al., 2018). To investigate how it promotes proteostasis upon COPII disruption, we analyzed the transcriptomic changes in worms subjected to sar-1 RNAi, hsf-1 RNAi, and double RNAi targeting both sar-1 and hsf-1, respectively. As expected, sar-1 RNAi induced remarkable transcriptomic changes in worms (Figure 3A), with 2358 genes upregulated and 1912 genes downregulated (Figure 3B and C). Consistent with the activation of HSF-1 in response to COPII dysfunction (Figures 1 and 2), 386 (16.4%) of the upregulated and 319 (16.7%) of the downregulated genes upon sar-1 RNAi were controlled by HSF-1 (Figure 3B and C). To further characterize these HSF-1-regulated genes, we compared them with previously reported HSF-1 targets in worms (Kovacs et al., 2019). Indeed, disrupting COPII induced the expression of classical HSF-1-dependent genes involved in protein folding, such as hsp genes, consistent with earlier observations (Figures 1E and 3D, E; Akerfelt et al., 2010; Kovacs et al., 2019). However, HSF-1-regulated genes upon sar-1 RNAi were quite distinct from HSF-1 targets in the circumstances of heat shock, HSF-1 overexpression, and larval development (Figure 3D; Kovacs et al., 2019). The COPII-specific HSF-1 targets were enriched in pathways such as neuropeptide signalling, transcription regulation, and cell death (Figure 3E; Supplementary Table S2), implying a complex and tailored response of HSF-1 upon the impairment of COPII-mediated protein sorting and secretion.

Since RNAi against sec-23 or sar-1 induces hsp-4 transcription (Figure 1E), which is a marker of ERUPR (Calfon et al., 2002), we further compared the HSF-1-controlled genes upon COPII impairment with those induced upon ERUPR. Tunicamycin treatment is a well-established model to induce ERUPR (Heifetz et al., 1979; Denzel et al., 2014; Koh et al., 2018). We found that HSF-1-regulated genes upon sar-1 RNAi also remarkably differed from the reported targets of tunicamycin treatment (Supplementary Figure S4A; Koh et al., 2018), suggesting that the response of HSF-1 upon COPII disruption is distinct from ERUPR. Consistently, tunicamycin-treated worms did not show nuclear enrichment of HSF-1::GFP (Supplementary Figure S4B).

HSF1 monitors COPII vesicles in mammalian cells

Given the high conservation of HSF1 and the COPII complex across taxa, we investigated whether HSF1 regulates proteostasis in response to COPII dysfunction in mammalian cells. sec-23 has two mammalian orthologs, SEC23A and SEC23B, whereas the mammalian orthologs of sar-1 are SAR1A and SAR1B. We first tested their interactions with mammalian HSF1 by co-IP. HSF1-EGFP interacted with SEC23A-FLAG, but not SEC23B-FLAG, SAR1A-FLAG, or SAR1B-FLAG, in HEK293T cells (Figure 4A; Supplementary Figure S5). SAR1 initiates COPII formation at the ER (Jensen and Schekman, 2011). As expected, cytosolic HSF1 was enriched on the ER (Figure 4B). Moreover, we observed a clear colocalization of HSF1 with SEC23A on the ER (Figure 4B). Therefore, it is likely that SEC23A recruits HSF1 to the COPII complex, allowing HSF1 to monitor COPII vesicle assembly.

We next examined the colocalization of HSF1 and SEC23A on the ER when COPII was disrupted by Sar1b shRNA (Figure 4C). Sar1b shRNA suppressed the generation of COPII, causing a significant reduction of SEC23A puncta on the ER. Of note, HSF1 and its colocalization with SEC23A on the ER was also remarkably reduced by Sar1b shRNA (Figure 4D). Moreover, Sar1b shRNA significantly increased nuclear HSF1 level without changing total HSF1 level in the cell (Figure 4E). Therefore, disrupting COPII releases HSF1 from the COPII complex into the nucleus, indicating that mammalian HSF1 also monitors COPII through its interaction with SEC23A.

Discussion

As a master regulator of proteostasis, HSF1 responds to various alterations in protein metabolism (Akerfelt et al., 2010). In this study, we uncovered a well-conserved mechanism by which HSF1 monitors the proper formation of COPII that is crucial to protein sorting and secretion (Jensen and Schekman, 2011), through its interaction with the COPII component SEC23. When COPII formation is disrupted, HSF1 dissociates from the COPII complex and is translocated to the nucleus, driving gene expression to counteract the impaired protein homeostasis (Figure 4F).

While HSF1 is predominantly recognized as a nuclear transcription factor, our findings reveal that its ER-associated pool plays a pivotal role in regulating protein homeostasis. Notably, the enrichment of cytosolic HSF1 at the ER is significantly higher than expected by chance (Figure 4), suggesting a targeted localization. Given that the ER is the central hub for protein synthesis, folding, sorting, and transport, positioning HSF1 at this site enables it to directly monitor and respond to fluctuations in ER protein homeostasis.

Our data show a clear colocalization of HSF1 with the core COPII component SEC23A at the ER, potentially at ERES where COPII vesicles are assembled and released (Jensen and Schekman, 2011). This was supported by co-IP and binding assays using purified proteins, which confirm a direct physical association between HSF-1/HSF1 and SEC-23/SEC23A (Figures 1 and 4). The HSF-1–SEC-23 interaction appears to serve as a surveillance mechanism: under normal conditions, HSF-1 is tethered to the ER via SEC-23; when COPII vesicle formation is disrupted, this complex dissociates, rapidly mobilizing HSF-1 from the ER into the nucleus and facilitating an immediate transcriptional response (Figure 4F).

This activation mechanism could be an important complement to the conventional chaperone titration model, where HSF1 activation relies on the sequestration of inhibitory chaperones by accumulating misfolded proteins (Li et al., 2017). Our results indicate that HSF1 is poised to respond at the precise moment when protein trafficking is perturbed, thereby initiating a rapid corrective response before misfolded proteins accumulate to critical levels. Consistently, COPII is involved in worms’ response to protein aggregates and heat stress through HSF-1 (Figure 2). It will be interesting to investigate the detailed role of the HSF1–COPII interaction under physiological conditions in future studies. Besides, the HSF1 colocalized with SEC23 accounts for only 10% of its pool at the ER. Since HSF1 responds to various protein stresses, it is likely that the ER-associated HSF1 could also be important to sense other ER stresses, such as unfolded proteins (Kim et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2024).

Moreover, while both heat shock and COPII disruption enhance the transcription of protective genes, the gene expression profiles evoked by these stimuli are distinct (Figure 3). The COPII disruption causes a specialized transcriptional program that diverges substantially from the typical heat shock response. This suggests that HSF1 is not a one-dimensional responder to ‘stress’ but rather a versatile transcription factor that tailors its activity to the nature of the upstream insult. Similar context-specific transcriptional responses of HSF1 have been observed in other stress conditions (Ciccarelli et al., 2023; Rubio et al., 2024). These reports, together with our findings, highlight the need for HSF1 to employ distinct stress-sensing mechanisms for different types of cellular insults.

Because the null mutation of sar-1 or sec-23 causes severe developmental defects, the loss-of-function assays in this study were performed using RNAi. To minimize off-target effects, we set up rigorous negative controls of luc2 RNAi and scrambled shRNA in the assays of worms and NIH3T3 cells, respectively. Moreover, the genetic interaction of HSF-1 with COPII was concluded from independent RNAi assays against sar-1 and sec-23 (Figures 1 and 2). Nevertheless, it will be worth further consolidating these findings through inducible knockout assays in the future.

Materials and methods

C. elegans strains, transgenes, and culture

The strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Some strains were provided by the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center, which is funded by NIH Office of Research Infrastructure Programs (P40 OD010440). All strains were maintained at 20°C following standard worm culture protocol (Brenner, 1974).

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T and NIH3T3 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 10% bovine serum (Lonsera), respectively, and cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2. High Five cells were maintained in insect cell culture medium (Expression Systems) at 27°C without CO2 supplementation. All cell lines were tested free of mycoplasma contamination by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Cells were transfected at ∼70%–80% confluency using polyethlyenimine (Proteintech, PR40001) following manufacturer's instructions.

Plasmid construction

Primers used in plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

To generate pEGFP-HSF1-EGFP, the HSF1 cDNA was amplified from HEK293T cDNA and cloned into pEGFP.

To generate pEGFP-SEC23A/B-FLAG, pEGFP-FLAG was generated by replacing EGFP in pEGFP with FLAG tag sequence, and then the SEC23A/B cDNA was amplified from HEK293T cDNA and cloned into pEGFP-FLAG.

To generate pEGFP-SAR1A/B-FLAG, the SAR1A/B cDNA was amplified from HEK293T cDNA and cloned into pEGFP-FLAG.

To generate pIE-hsf-1::EGFP, the EGFP sequence and hsf-1 cDNA was amplified from pEGFP and N2 cDNA, respectively, and cloned into pIE.

To generate pIE-sec-23::FLAG, the sec-23 cDNA was amplified from N2 cDNA and FLAG tag sequence was added by primers, and then sec-23::FLAG sequence was cloned into pIE.

To generate pIE-sar-1::FLAG, the sar-1 cDNA was amplified from N2 cDNA and FLAG tag sequence was added by primers, and then sar-1::FLAG sequence was cloned into pIE.

To generate pLKO.1-CMV-Calr-N16-EGFP-KDEL plasmid, CMV-Calr-N16-EGFP-KDEL sequence was amplified from pEGFP-N16-EGFP-KDEL plasmid and inserted into pLKO.1-TRC-EGFP after the EGFP sequence was removed.

To generate pLKO.1-CMV-Calr-N16-EGFP-KDEL-SAR1B-shRNA or pLKO.1-CMV-Calr-N16-EGFP-KDEL-scramble-shRNA, Sar1b shRNA or scramble shRNA sequence was inserted into pLKO.1-CMV-Calr-N16-EGFP-KDEL plasmid using T4 DNA ligase.

RNAi

RNAi experiments in C. elegans were performed as reported (Kamath et al., 2001). Worms were fed from L4 on Escherichia coli HT115 bacteria expressing dsRNA against indicated genes until indicated ages. To minimize off-target phenotypes, luc2, a real but irrelevant gene to worm biology, was used as negative control. The strain of HT115 [L4440::luc2] was a gift from Adam Antebi lab in Max Planck Institute for Biology of Ageing.

To produce Sar1b-shRNA or scramble-shRNA lentiviral particles to infect NIH3T3 cells, HEK293T cells were transfected with the lentiviral plasmid (pLKO.1-CMV-Calr-N16-EGFP-KDEL-SAR1B-shRNA or pLKO.1-CMV-Calr-N16-EGFP-KDEL-scramble-shRNA), packaging plasmid (Delta 8.9), and envelope plasmid (VSV-G) at a ratio of 5:3:2 for 48 h. The harvested medium with lentiviral particles were mixed with fresh medium at a ratio of 2:1 and added to NIH3T3 for infection.

Microscopy and image analysis

For microscopy experiments, worms were anesthetized using 5 mM levamisole and mounted on 5% agar pads. The images were captured using an Olympus IXploreTM SpinSR or an Olympus BX53 microscope. Fluorescence intensities were measured by Adobe Photoshop. The mean nuclear and cytoplasmic fluorescent intensities were used to calculate the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio. NIH3T3 cells were imaged on an Olympus SpinSR or a HIS-SIM.

For the analysis of Q44 puncta, worms of indicated ages were transferred to NGM plates containing 500 mM NaCl as reported (Moronetti Mazzeo et al., 2012). Worms with Q44 puncta were counted every hour.

For colocalization analysis, deconvolution was performed using cellSens Dimension (Olympus). The puncta of HSF1 and SEC23A were marked by Imaris (Oxford Instruments). Cell area was calculated by ImageJ.

Co-IP

For MS of HSF-1::GFP interactors, ∼30000 synchronized Day 1 worms were collected in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 0.1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 10 mM Na4P2O7·10H2O, and protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)) and lysed by tissue homogenizer (Bertin). Worm lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 13000× g for 15 min. For co-IP in HEK293T cells, cells expressing indicated proteins were collected in lysis buffer 48 h post transfection. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing and cleared by centrifugation at 13000× g for 15 min.

The resultant supernatants from worm or cell lysate were incubated with anti-GFP beads (SMART) for 2 h at 4°C. Afterwards, the beads were washed three times with 1× lysis buffer. Proteins precipitated with beads were either eluted by 100 mM Glycine–HCl (pH 3.0) and neutralized with 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), snap-frozen, and stored at −80°C for MS or eluted using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer for western blot analysis.

To examine the interaction of purified worm proteins, each protein was expressed separately in High Five cells. First, the lysates with EGFP-tagged protein were incubated with anti-GFP beads for 2 h at 4°C. After three times of wash using lysis buffer, the beads bound with EGFP-tagged proteins were further incubated with cell lysates containing FLAG-tagged proteins for 2 h at 4°C. Proteins precipitated with beads were eluted using SDS loading buffer.

For co-IP of proteins expressed in HEK293T cells, cells expressing indicated proteins were collected in lysis buffer 48 h post transfection. Cell lysate was incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing and cleared by centrifugation at 13000× g for 15 min at 4°C. The resultant supernatant was incubated with anti-GFP beads for 2 h at 4°C. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with lysis buffer and boiled with SDS loading buffer for western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were separated by reducing SDS–PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were then blotted with antibodies against GFP (1:1000, Cat# sc-9996, Santa Cruz), FLAG (1:2000, Cat# F7425, Sigma–Aldrich), SAR1B (1:1000, Cat# 22292–1-AP, Proteintech), or α-tubulin (1:5000, Cat# T5168, Sigma). Signals of western blotting were captured by TanonTM 5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System and measured using Fiji (Schindelin et al., 2012).

MS

Proteins were pretreated with TCEP (Thermo Scientific) and N-ethylmaleimide (Sigma) for reduction and alkylation. The proteins were then digested overnight into peptides with trypsin (Promega). The resultant peptides were desalted using a MonoSpinTM C18 column (GL Science) and dried with a SpeedVac (Thermo Scientific). Dried peptides were separated by Easy-nLc 1000 nano HPLC (Thermo Scientific) and identified by a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The acquired MS/MS data were analyzed against UniProtKB Caenorhabditis elegans database (Proteome ID: UP000001940) by Sequest algorithm integrated in the Protein Discoverer software (Thermo Scientific). A decoy database containing the reversed sequences of all the proteins appended to the target database was used to estimate peptide probabilities and false discovery rates. Proteins with total peptide-spectrum match (PSM) > 2 in three independent experiments were considered reliable, otherwise removed from further analysis. Reliable proteins identified specifically in the experimental group are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

mRNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

To isolate mRNA from each sample, >100 synchronized worms of indicated ages or cells in a 6-cm dish were collected and subjected to mRNA isolation by RNeasy Mini (QIAGEN). cDNA was subsequently generated by iScriptTM Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad). qPCR was performed with Bestar® Sybr Green qPCR Master Mix (DBI Bioscience) or 2× NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein) on a QuantStudioTM 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) or a CFX384 TouchTM Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). ama-1 and cdc-42 were used as reference genes. Four technical replicates were performed in each reaction. At least three biological repeats were examined. qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3 (Zhu et al., 2015; Labbadia et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2019). dCt values were used in statistical tests.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

The paired-end mRNA-seq library was prepared and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform by Novogene China, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. mRNA-seq reads were mapped to WBcel235 by STAR (v2.6.0c) (Dobin et al., 2013). Mapped reads were quantified by RSEM (Li and Dewey, 2011) and normalized by fragments per kilobase million (FPKM). Genes with FPKM value > 2 in at least one sample were defined as expressed genes. Genes with a 1.5-fold change in expression compared to the control group were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology analysis of indicated subset of genes was conducted by Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) (Huang et al., 2009).

Thermorecovery assay

More than 30 worms of indicated ages of each group were heat-shocked for 4 h at 35°C and transferred to 20°C. After recovery at 20°C for 2 h, worms were prodded using a platinum pick at the tail, middle body, and head in turn. If the moving distance were more than half the length of its body, such worms were considered thermorecovered. Worms with vulval rupture or moving out of the plate were censored.

Immunofluorescence

NIH3T3 cells were fixed in 4% (w/v) formaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline for 15 min at room temperature and stained with anti-HSF1 (1:500, Cat# sc-13516, Santa Cruz) and anti-SEC23A (1:1000, Cat# PA5-28984, Invitrogen) antibodies overnight at 4°C. DNA was counterstained using DAPI (Cat# D8417, Sigma–Aldrich).

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise noted. Statistical tests were performed as indicated using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Detailed statistical information is shown in Supplementary Table S4.
