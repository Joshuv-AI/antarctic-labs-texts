Tuberculosis Development in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease: A Nationwide Population-Based Study
Authors: Haebeen Jeong, Eunyoung Lee, Ju Hyun Oh, Won-Il Choi, Bumhee Park, Joo Hun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary tuberculosis is a major comorbidity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, the risk factors for tuberculosis development in patients with COPD have not been thoroughly studied. Thus, this study investigated the development of tuberculosis according to comorbidities and inhaler prescriptions for COPD.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service database from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2020. This cohort included 139,589 COPD patients (≥ 40 years) with a new prescription of inhalers. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify significant risk factors for predicting the development of tuberculosis.ResultsMultivariate analysis demonstrated that a history of tuberculosis (hazard ratio [HR], 18.14; 95% confidence interval [CI], 16.44–20.02) and hospitalization during the screening period (HR for one hospitalization, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.17–1.44; HR for two or more hospitalizations, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.39–1.70), along with older age and male sex, were associated with the development of pulmonary tuberculosis.ConclusionOur data indicate that a history of tuberculosis and hospitalization during the screening period, along with old age and male sex, are independent risk factors for the development of pulmonary tuberculosis in patients with COPD.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is projected to become the third leading cause of death globally by 2030, and its burden continues to rise, particularly in developing countries.1234 The prognosis of COPD is closely linked to frequent exacerbations, primarily triggered by infections, including respiratory viruses and bacteria.5

According to the Lancet Commission report, infection-related COPD (COPD-I) is one of the five subtypes of COPD and is recognized as a significant contributing factor.126 The association between tuberculosis (TB) and COPD has been described in several studies.78 Structural lung damage caused by TB, including pleural changes, bronchial stenosis, and lung scarring, can predispose individuals to an increased risk of developing COPD.89 Conversely, increasing evidence suggests that patients with COPD are at a higher risk of developing pulmonary TB, mainly because of a compromised innate immune system and impaired pulmonary function.710 Furthermore, a previous study indicated that a TB diagnosis is associated with increased mortality in patients with COPD compared with that in patients with TB in the general population.11 Therefore, the co-existence of COPD and TB is a serious health concern that necessitates targeted prevention and management strategies. Some studies have explored the relationship between inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and TB risk in COPD.1213 However, the factors contributing to TB development in patients with COPD, beyond smoking, have not been sufficiently investigated. Hence, this study aimed to identify the risk factors for the development of TB in patients with COPD, with a particular focus on inhaler prescriptions and comorbidities, using data from a nationwide cohort in Korea.

METHODS

Study design

This study retrospectively analyzed data from the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) database, covering the period from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2020. The HIRA database contains comprehensive medical service claim records, including diagnostic codes based on the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Edition (ICD-10), as well as medication data. It encompasses healthcare services across all geographic regions and covers 98% of the Korean population, all of whom are insured under the country’s mandatory and universal national health insurance system.14 Key data fields, including drug names, quantities, dispensing dates, and durations, had minimal missing or out-of-range values, affecting less than 0.5% of the records.

The COPD cohort was constructed from the HIRA database based on the following criteria: 1) patients aged ≥ 40 years, 2) a primary diagnosis of COPD (J43–J44 of ICD-10 codes) recorded between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2020, 3) at least three prescriptions of one of the following respiratory medications within one year of observation period: long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA), long-acting β2-agonists (LABA), a combination of LAMA/LABA, a combination of ICS/LABA, triple therapy (LAMA + LABA + ICS), phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, mucolytics, or theophylline (Fig. 1).15

HIRA = Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service, DM = diabetes mellitus, COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, OS = osteoporosis.

Each patient underwent a one-year screening period without any inhaler prescriptions prior to the index date. The index date was defined as the date when the patient was first prescribed inhaler medication (Fig. 2). During the screening period, the following exclusion criteria were applied: patients with a history of cancer; patients who had been prescribed respiratory inhalers before the index date; patients who had inhaler prescriptions switched after the index date; patients with prescriptions of immunosuppressants; patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus without medications for diabetes mellitus; and patients diagnosed with osteoporosis without medications for osteoporosis. Subsequently, TB development during the observation period was assessed from the index date to December 31, 2020 (Fig. 2).

Case identification and comorbidities

Within the eligible cohort, TB cases that occurred after the index date were identified based on the following criteria: 1) a reported TB diagnosis with ICD-10 codes A15–A19, 2) treatment with two or more anti-TB drugs (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical code J01), including isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide, prothionamide, 4-aminosalicylic acid, and cycloserine; 3) at least two claims for TB-related medical expenses submitted to HIRA. A history of TB was defined as the diagnosis of TB before the index date.

All comorbidities including a history of TB were detected using the ICD-10 codes during the screening period (Table 1). Oral corticosteroid (OCS) usage was identified by a prescription of 420 mg (15 mg/day for four weeks) or more prednisolone for COPD during the observation period.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and medication prescriptions were summarized using descriptive statistics, including mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range] for continuous variables and number with percentage for categorical variables. A univariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to identify significant risk factors for the development of TB. Subsequently, a multivariate model was constructed, incorporating covariates that were significant in the univariate analysis at two significance levels of P value: Model 1 at 0.1 and Model 2 at 0.05. The proportional hazard assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The analysis was performed only on cases with complete data, and a threshold of P value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. All the analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ajou University Hospital (AJOUIRB-EXP-2021-582). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethical review board.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

This cohort included 139,589 patients with a mean age of 68.9 years, of whom 75.5% were male (Table 1). The inhaler treatment regimens included LAMA or LAMA/LABA in 77,505 (55.5%), LAMA/ICS in 30,417 (21.8%), triple therapy (LAMA/LABA/ICS) in 3,913 (2.8%), and LABA alone in 27,754 (19.9%) patients (Table 1). A history of TB was reported in 2,696 patients (1.9%), and 23,865 patients (17.1%) had diabetes mellitus, 2,509 (1.8%) had osteoporosis, 4,293 (3.1%) had chronic renal disease, and 7,304 (5.2%) had bronchiectasis (Table 1).

Hospital visits

During the screening period, 53,231 patients (38.1%) were hospitalized at least once for respiratory or cardiovascular conditions (Supplementary Table 1). Among them, 18,113 (13.0%) were hospitalized for respiratory conditions (Supplementary Table 1).

Prevalence of newly developed TB

While 2,399 patients (1.72%) developed TB, 2,276 (1.63%) were newly diagnosed with pulmonary TB during the study period, and 1,326 patients (0.95%) were bacteriologically and histologically confirmed (Supplementary Table 2). The remaining 128 patients (0.09%) were diagnosed with extrapulmonary TB, including those with nervous system involvement (A17), TB in other organs (A18), and miliary TB (A19) (Supplementary Table 2).

Risk factors, including inhaler prescription & comorbidities, for the development of pulmonary TB through univariate analyses

Significant risk factors for pulmonary TB identified in univariate analysis included age, sex, prior TB history, bronchiectasis, chronic renal disease, OCS use, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and hospitalization (Table 2). In contrast, ICS prescription was not associated with TB development (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.78–1.29) and was therefore excluded in the multivariate analysis. Among the comorbidities, only bronchiectasis showed a statistically significant association with TB risk (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.08–1.51) and was therefore included in the multivariate model (Table 2).

Risk factors for pulmonary TB identified through multivariate analyses

In multivariate analysis (Model 1) adjusted for factors with P value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis, the following factors were independently associated with an increased risk of developing pulmonary TB: previous history of TB (HR, 18.11; 95% CI, 16.42–19.99), age ≥ 80 years (HR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.53–2.40), male sex (HR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.26–1.55), and hospitalization for any reason (HR for ≥ 2 during screening, 1.55; 95% CI, 1.40–1.72) (Table 2). In multivariate analysis (Model 2) adjusted for factors with P value < 0.05, the same factors remained significant: previous TB history (HR, 18.14; 95% CI, 16.44–20.02), age ≥ 80 years (HR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.53–2.39), male sex (HR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.26–1.56), and hospitalization for any reason (HR for ≥ 2 during screening, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.39–1.70) (Table 2).

Risk factors for overall TB infections

In the univariate analysis, the risk factors for the development of overall TB included age, sex, previous history of TB, bronchiectasis, chronic renal disease, OCS use, CCI score, and hospitalization, consistent with the findings for pulmonary TB (Supplementary Table 3). The multivariate analysis (Model 1) adjusted for factors with P value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis identified the following independent risk factors: a history of TB (HR, 18.31; 95% CI, 16.51–20.31), age ≥ 80 years (HR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.53–2.40), and hospitalization for any reason (HR for ≥ 2 during screening, 1.63; 95% CI, 1.46–1.82) (Supplementary Table 3). In the multivariate analysis (Model 2) adjusted for factors with P value < 0.05, the same factors were confirmed as significant: history of TB (HR, 18.35; 95% CI, 16.54–20.35), age ≥ 80 years (HR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.27–2.05), and hospitalization for any reason (HR for ≥ 2 during screening, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.45–1.80) (Supplementary Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to evaluate the risk factors for the development of TB in patients with COPD, with a particular focus on inhaler prescriptions and comorbidities. Our findings demonstrate that a history of TB, advanced age, and hospitalization during the screening period were significant independent risk factors. However, inhaler therapy, including ICS, was not independently associated with the development of TB in patients with COPD. To minimize selection bias, we used a nationwide claims database. Moreover, we addressed the methodological limitations of previous studies by implementing a study design based on the initiation of a new inhaler therapy to avoid time-related biases. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest cohort study to investigate a comprehensive range of risk factors for the development of TB, including ICS therapy, in patients with COPD.

Our analyses demonstrated that inhaler prescription was not an independent risk factor for the development of TB in patients with COPD. Several studies have reported an association between ICS and an increased incidence of TB owing to the immunosuppressive effects of corticosteroids.1213 A nested case-control study in Korea that matched 4,139 patients on ICS with 20,583 controls identified a dose-dependent relationship between ICS use and TB risk.12 Another nested case-control study conducted in Quebec reported an increased risk of TB among ICS users who were not receiving OCSs.16 However, these studies often did not adequately adjust for key confounding factors such as TB history, underlying airway diseases, and comorbid conditions. In contrast, other studies found no association between ICS therapy and TB risk, consistent with our findings.1217 A large case-control study in Taiwan reported no increase in the incidence of TB among patients with COPD receiving ICS therapy, with no observed relationship between the duration or dosage of ICS and TB risk.12 The authors of that study highlighted the importance of addressing methodological issues and suggested that incorporating time-dependent covariates provides a more accurate representation of drug exposure.12 Therefore, our study included a history of TB, comorbidities of COPD, and hospitalization in the multivariate analysis to account for potential confounders.

Notably, our study found a significant association between a history of TB and the development of TB in patients with COPD. While a history of TB has been recognized as a risk factor for recurrent TB in high-prevalence regions, our study underscores its significance as a major risk factor for TB in COPD.1819 Survivors of TB often develop post-TB lung disease despite successful treatment, primarily due to residual structural lung damage and persistent respiratory abnormalities.920 Early studies have demonstrated that residual cavitation significantly increases the risk of TB relapse.1921 Moreover, immune dysregulation in COPD may further exacerbate the susceptibility to TB reactivation.22

The present finding of increased susceptibility to recurrent TB suggests that certain individuals may be intrinsically vulnerable to TB. For instance, the natural resistance-associated macrophage protein 1 (NRAMP1) gene, which regulates mycobacterial clearance by maintaining phagosome acidity, has been extensively studied as a key genetic determinant.2324 NRAMP1 variants have been linked to more severe manifestations of TB, including increased cavity formation and TB pleurisy.25 In addition, polymorphisms in the tumor necrosis factor-α gene have been implicated in both COPD pathogenesis and TB susceptibility.2728 These genetic factors may predispose patients with TB to severe post-TB lung damage, further increasing the risk of disease recurrence.2324252627

In our COPD cohort, older patients were found to be at a greater risk of TB. Although TB is highly prevalent among younger adults, numerous studies have emphasized increased susceptibility to TB in the older population.2829 This heightened vulnerability is largely attributable to immunosenescence, and the presence of comorbidities can further compromise immune function.30 As the global life expectancy continues to increase, TB in the older population is expected to become an increasingly significant public health concern. Moreover, TB in the older population often presents with atypical symptoms, and pre-existing chronic respiratory conditions such as COPD can obscure the diagnosis of TB due to overlapping clinical manifestations.31 This underscores the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion of TB in older patients with COPD. However, further research on the incidence of TB in relation to a history of TB in patients with COPD stratified by ICS therapy appears to be both necessary and meaningful.

This study identified male sex as an independent risk factor for TB in patients with COPD. This association is particularly relevant in Korea, where the smoking rate among men is three times higher than that among women.32 However, smoking status was not adjusted for in our analysis because of the lack of available data. Given that smoking is a well-established risk factor for TB, it is essential to determine whether the observed association between male sex and TB is influenced by differences in smoking rates.33

Our study demonstrated that patients with a higher frequency of hospitalization during the screening period had an increased risk of developing TB. This finding suggests that more severe COPD is associated with a higher risk of TB activation because hospitalizations in COPD, representing the overall burden of severe illness, are closely linked to severe acute exacerbations.5 Our analysis was limited to all-cause hospitalization; however, hospitalization due to respiratory diseases can serve as a more precise indicator of severe acute exacerbations of COPD. Although limited research exists on the relationship between COPD severity and TB activation, Benfield et al.7 reported that patients with moderate to severe COPD experienced more frequent hospitalizations due to infections, including TB. The authors attributed this vulnerability to impaired lung immunity, characterized by mucus hypersecretion and ciliary dysfunction, which predisposes patients to a higher incidence of respiratory infections.7 Moreover, a prolonged hospital stay may increase the risk of nosocomial infections, including TB transmission, further reinforcing this association.34

This study has important public health implications, underscoring the need for targeted TB screening and preventive measures in patients with COPD and a history of TB.35 Early identification and intervention among high-risk individuals can substantially reduce the burden of active TB, particularly in settings with limited healthcare resources. This issue is even more pressing in countries with a high prevalence of TB, where the combined burden of COPD and TB poses a major healthcare challenge.

This study had several limitations. First, this was not a prospective study, although the observational design reflects real-world clinical practice. Second, hospitalization for any cause was used as a covariate and interpreted as a proxy for COPD exacerbation; however, hospital admissions may have been attributed to non-COPD conditions. Consequently, the lack of data on the specific reasons for hospitalization limits the accurate assessment of exacerbation-related risks. Third, a history of TB was defined as any diagnosis before the index date, but stratification by treatment outcome was not possible. Fourth, the HIRA database does not include pulmonary function test results, which are essential for the accurate assessment of COPD severity. Fifth, smoking status and family history were not investigated because of a lack of information. Sixth, the precise ICS dosage was not measured. Finally, data on medication adherence and socioeconomic factors, both of which can influence TB risk, were excluded from the analysis.

In conclusion, our data demonstrated that older age, male sex, history of TB, and hospitalization during the screening period were independent risk factors for the development of pulmonary TB in patients with COPD, whereas ICS prescription was not. This study underscores the significant risk of TB reactivation in patients with COPD and a history of TB or frequent hospitalizations indicative of severe exacerbation of COPD. These findings emphasize the need for targeted screening and preventive measures to mitigate the risk of TB development in this vulnerable population.
