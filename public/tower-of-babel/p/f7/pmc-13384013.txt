Expression and localization of tight, adherens, and gap junction proteins in the chicken ovary during pause in laying induced by fasting
Authors: Karolina Frydrych, Marta H. Machura, Anna Hrabia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intercellular junctions are critical to normal functioning and remodeling of various tissues in the reproductive system; however, their importance in the avian ovary is poorly understood. The aim of this study was to examine the expression and localization of essential tight (claudin 1, occludin, zonula occludens 1 [ZO1 gene]), adherens (E-cadherin [CDH1], β-catenin [CTNNB1]), and gap (connexin 43 [GJA1]) junction proteins in the hen ovarian follicles during a pause in laying induced by fasting. 32-week-old Hy-Line Brown hens were randomly divided into two groups: the control group (fed ad libitum; n = 8) and the experimental group (fasted; n = 8), which was subjected to an induced pause in egg laying by complete feed withdrawal for five consecutive days. Tissue samples from prehierarchical (white and yellowish) and hierarchical (small yellow and the largest yellow preovulatory F3–F1) follicles were collected on day six of the experiment from the control hens (laying) and hens that had been fasted (non-laying). Quantitative RT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed differences in the expression of examined proteins on mRNA and/or protein levels between the white and yellowish follicles, as well as between the largest yellow preovulatory F3–F1 follicles and the theca and granulosa layers of these follicles. Immunofluorescence revealed differences in staining intensity between the layers of follicle wall, with localization of all proteins mainly in the granulosa layer. In fasted hens, the atresia of the largest yellow follicles was accompanied by: (1) no changes in mRNA transcript abundance of tight and adherens junction proteins in prehierarchical follicles, and a decrease in GJA1 mRNA level in the white follicles; (2) mostly a decrease in mRNA transcript levels of each type of molecules in the yellow atretic follicles; (3) a decrease, no changes, or an increase in examined protein abundances in the white and yellowish follicles; (4) a decrease in cell junction protein abundances in F3–F1 follicles; and (5) a marked reduction in immunoreactivity for occludin, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and connexin 43 in the wall of atretic follicles. Our results provide novel insights into the molecular composition of tight, adherens, and gap junctions and their contribution to the remodeling of the follicular wall during follicle development and fasting-induced atresia of yellow preovulatory follicles in hens.

Introduction

Ovarian follicle development and corresponding yolk accumulation are directly related to egg production efficiency in birds. These processes require structural integrity and effective communication between the oocyte and cells of the follicle wall. Disruption of these interactions leads to abnormal folliculogenesis and follicle atresia. The hen ovary consists of two main groups of follicles: (1) slow-growing, prehierarchical follicles (white [1–4 mm] and yellowish [> 4–8 mm]), which are highly susceptible to atresia, and (2) fast-growing, yellow preovulatory follicles (> 8–36 mm), which under normal physiological conditions are resistant to atresia. Their atresia occurs at the end of the reproductive season and is followed by cessation of egg laying. Moreover, atresia of these follicles can be induced under abnormal physiological conditions, including metabolic or environmental disturbances, such as day-length alterations, an imbalance in ovarian sex steroids, or fasting. Yellow follicles are arranged hierarchically, with the largest designated as F1, the next as F2, and so on (Johnson, 2015; Hrabia and Sechman, 2024). Following follicle recruitment from a group of 6–8 mm, the follicle undergoes rapid growth associated with intensive yolk accumulation, leading to a marked increase in follicle mass prior to ovulation. Yolk precursor proteins, synthesized in the liver in a process called vitellogenesis, are transported via the bloodstream to the oocyte, where they traverse the follicular wall and are internalized by endocytosis (Hrabia, 2022; Machura and Hrabia, 2024).

The development and function of the ovarian follicles is mainly orchestrated by the coordinated action of pituitary gonadotropins and ovarian steroid hormones. In addition to hormonal regulation, the specialized junctions between adjacent cells also play an essential role in the normal course of folliculogenesis and the maintenance of ovarian function. These connections are necessary to maintain tissue integrity and efficient transport of various substances between cells, including metabolites, ions, and signaling molecules. Moreover, communication between the oocyte and follicular cells, especially the granulosa cells, can impact the rate of yolk accumulation and, thus, facilitate fast growth of the yellow follicle in the avian ovary (Schuster et al., 2004; Stephens and Johnson, 2017; Frydrych et al., 2024). There are three key types of cell-cell junctions: tight, adherens, and gap. The tight junctions are specialized protein complexes that connect epithelial and endothelial cells, forming a selective intercellular barrier that regulates paracellular permeability to pathogens, ions, water, and other molecules. They are formed by transmembrane proteins, including occludin (OCLN gene) and claudins, which interact with cytoplasmic scaffold proteins, such as zonula occludens (ZOs, also known as tight junction protein [TJP]) (Fanning et al., 1998; Förster, 2008; Suzuki, 2013; Zihni et al., 2016). The adherens junctions regulate epithelial integrity, cytoskeletal organization, and cell signaling. The proteins that form these junctions include cadherins and catenins (Tsuchiya et al., 2006; Garcia et al., 2018). The gap junctions form intercellular channels, composed of connexins, that allow the transport of ions, metabolites, and small (< 1 kDa) signaling molecules between neighboring cells. Among the various connexin isoforms, connexin 43 (Cx43; GJA1 gene) is predominantly important in the reproductive tissues (Ackert et al., 2001; Krysko et al., 2004; Gershon et al., 2008; Winterhager and Kidder, 2015).

There are numerous studies indicating the involvement of cell-cell junctions and proteins that form these junctions in the maintenance of animal reproductive functions (Winterhager and Kidder, 2015; Zhang et al., 2018; Green et al., 2019; Kordowitzki et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024); however, limited data are available concerning the expression and role of intercellular junction proteins in the ovary of birds. First, Schuster et al. (2004) suggested that occludin functions in sealing the paracellular space between granulosa cells of chicken ovarian follicles, maintaining the transport of yolk precursors to the oocyte. Further, investigation of Stephens and Johnson (2017) confirmed the most abundant expression of occludin in the granulosa layer of small follicles, and the lowest in preovulatory follicles, as well as observed in vitro that occludin gene expression is regulated by epidermal growth factor, activin A, and activin B (Stephens and Johnson, 2017). Bae et al. (2013) suggested that β-catenin (CTNNB1 gene), as a structural component of adherens junctions and a key effector of the Wnt signaling pathway regulating genes involved in proliferation and differentiation, is an essential factor for normal development of avian gonads. Moreover, distribution of Cx43 in the granulosa layer of three of the largest preovulatory F3–F1 follicles (Farioli-Vecchioli et al., 2000) and the expression of Cx43 in cultured granulosa layer explants isolated from the quail F1 follicle (Krysko et al., 2004) were demonstrated. The presence of Cx43 in granulosa cells and inner perivitelline layer of the turkey F1 follicle (Słowińska et al., 2021) was also revealed. We recently strongly suggested the involvement of Cx43 in follicle development and its regulation by estrogen in the hen ovary (Frydrych et al., 2024).

Since cell-cell junction proteins have the potential to be implicated in the yolk accumulation in the oocyte and follicle development, processes affecting egg production in avian species, it seems necessary to characterize the expression profiles and localization of crucial proteins composing cell-cell junctions to clarify their participation in ovarian follicle development, as well as in follicle atresia that occurs during a pause in laying. In the present study, we examined the hypothesis that intracellular junction proteins are engaged in these processes in the hen ovary. Accordingly, the aim of the study was to examine mRNA transcript and protein abundances of crucial tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin 1, and ZO1), adherens junction proteins (E-cadherin [CDH1 gene] and β-catenin), and gap junction proteins (Cx43) in the hen ovarian follicles at various stages of development, and in follicles of hens subjected to a fasting-induced pause in laying. Immunofluorescent localization of proteins in the walls of normal and atretic follicles was also investigated.

Materials and methods

Animals and tissue sampling

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the protocol approved by the Local Animal Ethics Committee in Kraków, Poland (Approval No. 218/2015). Hy-Line Brown hens were obtained from a commercial breeder and housed individually under controlled environmental conditions (14 h light: 10 h dark), with ad libitum access to feed and water. Oviposition time was recorded daily for each hen to determine individual laying cycles. At 32 weeks of age, hens were randomly assigned to two groups based on previously established protocols (Leśniak-Walentyn et al., 2016; Socha et al., 2017; Socha and Hrabia, 2019; Wolak and Hrabia, 2021; Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025; Machura and Hrabia, 2026): a control group fed ad libitum (C; n = 8) and a fasted group subjected to a 5-day feed withdrawal to induce a pause in laying (F; n = 8). Hens in the fasted group stopped laying eggs on day 3.7 ± 0.31 of the experiment. All hens were euthanized on day 6 of the experiment, when all fasted hens stopped egg laying, by stunning followed by bleeding. Control hens that laid eggs were sacrificed ∼2 h after ovulation. Ovum location in the oviduct was always confirmed at the time of sampling. Animal health and welfare were monitored throughout the experiment to minimize stress and discomfort. Immediately after euthanasia, ovaries were excised, weighed, and all ovarian follicles from each ovary were isolated and characterized morphologically. Subsequently, according to previous studies (Wolak and Hrabia, 2021; Machura and Hrabia, 2026) and based on diameter and the degree of yolk accumulation (different stage of development), follicles were classified as follows: white (WFs; 1–4 mm), yellowish (YFs; 4–8 mm), small yellow (SYFs; 8–12 mm, when present), and the three largest yellow preovulatory (F3–F1; 26–36 mm). Granulosa (G) and theca (T) layers of F3–F1 follicles were separated according to Gilbert et al. (1977). In ovaries collected from fasted, non-laying hens, advanced atresia of the largest F3–F1 follicles made it impossible to separate the layers of the follicle wall. As a result, the entire wall of these follicles was set as theca (T+G) in the study. Tissues (entire wall) collected from pools of given follicle types (WFs, YFs, or SYFs), as well as the theca and granulosa layer from individual F3–F1 follicles were divided into two portions. One portion was placed in RNAlater (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and stored at −20°C for RNA isolation. The remaining tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for protein extraction and Western blot analysis. Follicles from additional laying hens (n = 3) and fragments of yellow atretic follicles of fasted hens (n = 3) were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (pH 7.6), processed using routine paraffin embedding, and sectioned (6 µm) for histological and immunofluorescence analyses.

Total RNA isolation, reverse transcription, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA extraction and reverse transcription (RT) procedures were performed as previously described (Hrabia et al., 2022; Frydrych et al., 2024; Machura and Hrabia, 2026). In summary, total RNA was isolated from collected tissue samples originated from 5 different birds of each group using TRI reagent (Sigma-Aldrich). Then, 2 μg of RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Rockford, IL, USA) with random primers. The manufacturer's protocol specified the following thermal cycling conditions: 10 min at 25°C, 120 min at 37°C, and 5 min at 85°C. The obtained cDNA, following 10x dilution, was used in a quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) with chicken-specific TaqMan Gene Expression Assays for ZO1, CDH1, CTNNB1, and GJA1 (Table 1) in a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), according to our previous studies (Frydrych et al., 2024; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025). The PCR conditions were as follows: 50°C for 2 min, followed by 95°C for 10 min; then, 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. The duplex qRT-PCR for the examined gene and 18S rRNA (reference gene) was performed in a 10 μL volume containing the following: 5 μL of TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher), 0.5 μL of TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher), which contains a specific TaqMan MGB probe and a pair of primers, 0.5 μL of Eukaryotic 18S rRNA Endogenous Control (a pair of primers and a TaqMan probe labeled VIC/TAMRA), 3 μL of water, and 1 μL of cDNA. Each sample was run in duplicate. A no-template control was included in each run. Relative gene quantification was calculated after normalization with the 18S rRNA transcript. Protein isolation and Western blot analysis

To assess the abundance of occludin, claudin 1, ZO1, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and Cx43 proteins, ovarian tissues originated from 6 different birds of each group were homogenized in in liquid nitrogen and dissolved in cold lysis buffer (BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA). The samples were then vortexed vigorously multiple times and subjected to sonication. The resulting lysates were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations in the supernatants were measured using the Bradford assay with the Pierce Detergent Compatible Bradford Assay Reagent (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA), using bovine serum albumin as the standard. Following a previously established protocol (Hrabia et al., 2022; Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025), tissue samples containing 60 μg of total protein were mixed with Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), denatured at 99°C for 7 min, and loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels optimized for each protein: occludin, claudin 1, and Cx43 on 12% gels, E-cadherin on 10% gels, and ZO1 and β-catenin on 8% gels. After electrophoretic separation under reducing conditions using a Mini-Protean Tetra Cell apparatus (Bio-Rad), proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes by semi-dry blotting in FLASHBlot transfer buffer (Advansta, San Jose, CA, USA) at a constant voltage of 25 V for 7 min. Membranes were then blocked for 1 h in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST, pH 7.6) and 5% nonfat dry milk. Following washing, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary polyclonal rabbit antibodies specific to occludin, claudin 1, ZO1, E-cadherin, β-catenin, or Cx43 (dilutions and sources listed in Table 2). The specificity of the antibodies was previously validated in avian tissues (Gumułka et al., 2022; Pardyak et al., 2020; Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025). After incubation, membranes were washed and incubated for 1 h with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies. Target proteins were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (WesternBright™ ECL; Advansta), and images were acquired using a ChemiDoc-It 410 imaging system with VisionWorks Life Science software, version 6.6a (Ultra-Violet Products Ltd., Cambridge, UK). Membranes were subsequently stripped for 30 min and reprobed with an anti-β-actin or anti-β-tubulin antibody for 1 h, which served as a loading control. Experiments were performed in quadruplicate. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess the structural details of the walls of ovarian follicles. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were stained with Harris' hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 1 M HCl for 1 s, and counterstained with eosin Y aqueous for 30 s. The slides were then dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with DPX mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), before being covered with a coverslip. Tissue sections were analyzed using an Axio Scope.A1 light microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany), and images were captured with an Axiocam 503 color camera using ZEN 2.3 Pro software (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Immunofluorescence

To localize occludin, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and Cx43 proteins in the walls of ovarian follicles, the immunofluorescent staining was performed following a previously established procedure (Hrabia et al., 2022; Frydrych et al., 2024; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025). Ovarian tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, rinsed in distilled water, and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwaving in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 3 min. To block nonspecific binding, sections were incubated with 5% normal goat serum in TBST at room temperature for 30 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with polyclonal rabbit antibodies against occludin, E-cadherin, β-catenin, or Cx43 (see Table 2). After washing with TBS, sections were incubated for 90 min with DyLight 594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG. Sections were washed again, mounted with VECTASHIELD® Vibrance™ Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories), and coverslipped. Negative controls were performed by replacing primary antibodies with normal rabbit serum or TBST. Fluorescent labeling was examined using an Axio Scope.A1 fluorescence microscope. Images were captured with an Axiocam 503 color camera and ZEN 2.3 Pro software (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Immunoreactivity was scored qualitatively as strong, moderate, weak, or very weak. Representative images show merged blue DAPI nuclear staining and red fluorescence indicating positive immunoreactivity for occludin, E-cadherin, β-catenin, or Cx43.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using a nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA on ranks, followed by Dunn's or Student-Newman-Keuls tests. To compare the means of two groups (fasted versus control), a Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used. Differences in values were considered significant at P < 0.05. The variables were examined for normality and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro-Wilk and Brown-Forsythe tests, respectively. All calculations were performed using SigmaPlot_V13 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Histological characterization of the wall of growing and atretic ovarian follicles

In agreement with previous reports (Wolak and Hrabia, 2021), in fasted hens, ovary weight was significantly reduced by approximately 61% compared with control hens. Ovaries from fasted hens contained hierarchical yellow follicles exhibiting advanced atresia, frequently accompanied by hemorrhagic changes. The walls of these follicles were characterized by pronounced atretic alterations, including connective tissue expansion, fragmentation and loss of the basement membrane, displacement of granulosa and theca cells toward the follicular lumen, and increased vascularity. Discussion

The present study demonstrated, in large part for the first time, the expression of genes encoding proteins involved in formation of tight (OCLN, ZO1), adherens (CDH1, CTNNB1), and gap (GJA1) junctions, together with the abundance and localization of occludin, ZO1, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and Cx43 proteins in the hen ovarian follicles at different stages of development, as well as under fasting-induced atresia. These findings represent a substantial extension of the observations previously reported by Schuster et al. (2004) and Stephens and Johnson (2017). The aforementioned authors demonstrated the expression of occludin primarily in small ovarian follicles of chickens and its decrease with follicle development, suggesting the involvement of this protein in the regulation of yolk accumulation. In a subsequent study, we revealed the Cx43 expression in the hen ovarian follicles during development (Frydrych et al., 2024). Furthermore, studies have demonstrated the expression and localization of several key cell-cell junction proteins in the hen oviduct, and their association with fasting-induced regression of the oviduct (Ariyadi et al., 2013; Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025). The novel findings presented herein unequivocally indicate the involvement of the examined molecules in tight, adherens, and gap junction assembly, as well as in the maintenance of homeostasis of ovarian follicle tissues in hens. Furthermore, our findings suggest that remodeling of intercellular junction composition is a prevalent molecular mechanism concomitant with structural and functional changes of the female reproductive system in birds.

In laying hens, we observed differential abundance of ZO1, CDH1, CTNNB1, and GJA1 transcripts, as well as of claudin 1, occludin, ZO1, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and Cx43 proteins in ovarian follicles at different stages of development. Additionally, we identified differences in transcript and protein abundances between the layers of the largest yellow follicle walls. A salient finding was the localization of all examined proteins predominantly in the granulosa layer and, to a lesser extent, within the theca layer. These results suggest that, in addition to occludin (Schuster et al., 2004; Stephens and Johnson, 2017) and Cx43 (Frydrych et al., 2024), which have been previously described, other proteins of tight and adherens junctions also play a role in the development of the hen ovarian follicles. The role of these proteins is likely attributable to their involvement in orchestrating follicle wall architecture, modulating cell signaling pathways, and facilitating egg yolk accumulation. The observed discrepancies between mRNA transcript levels and protein abundances of some examined molecules in hen ovarian follicles may be attributable to the post-transcriptional and post-translational regulations, as is characteristic of proteins forming cell-cell junctions in many tissues (Garcia et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018). It is important to note that, in addition to the regulation of gene expression of proteins that establish cell-cell junctions, the modulation of these proteins—including cleavage, phosphorylation, cellular relocation, and complex interactions with other proteins—determines the functionality of tight, adherens, and gap junctions. In this regard, the detection of two bands of β-catenin, with the smaller band predominating in the theca layer of the largest yellow follicles and the larger band, likely phosphorylated, primarily located in the granulosa layer of these follicles, is of particular interest. This observation suggests the potential for distinct roles of β-catenin in the layers of the follicular wall. Furthermore, the results may support the hypothesis that post-translational modifications of cell-cell junction proteins are crucial for the functionality of these junctions in the walls of chicken ovarian follicles.

In relation to occludin, our findings indicated its localization within the cell membrane of the granulosa layer, predominantly in small primordial follicles, and exhibited moderate signal intensity in yellow follicles. However, the Western blotting analysis revealed marked occludin abundance in the granulosa layer of the largest yellow follicles. The observed discrepancy may be attributable, at least in part, to factors such as tissue processing, the specificity and sensitivity of the utilized method, and the applicability of antibodies to a particular technique. These observations generally align with the findings of previous studies, which demonstrated that occludin is predominantly expressed in the granulosa layer of small follicles and its expression declines with follicle maturation (Schuster et al., 2004; Stephens and Johnson, 2017). In addition, the present findings corroborate earlier suggestions that occludin-mediated sealing of the paracellular space between granulosa cells may regulate the transfer of yolk precursors to the oocyte, thereby contributing to yolk accumulation and follicular growth in the hen ovary (Schuster et al., 2004).

Expression of the other tight junction protein, ZO1, was found at both the mRNA and protein levels in the granulosa and theca cells of growing follicles, as well as in the endothelial cells of blood vessels located in the theca layer and the ovarian stroma. To date, the demonstration of ZO1 has been confined to its detection in the egg coat of the turkey F1 follicle (Słowińska et al., 2021). Our novel results are consistent with the observations made in mice (Mora et al., 2012) and bovine (Zhang et al., 2017) ovaries, where the presence of ZO1 protein was observed in the granulosa cells, theca externa, and thecal vascular endothelial cells. ZO1, a peripheral membrane tight junction protein, has been shown to directly interact with transmembrane proteins, such as occludin, as well as cytoplasmic proteins, including F-actin. This interaction establishes a connection between membrane proteins and the cytoskeleton (Fanning et al., 2007). In non-epithelial cells, ZO1 has been shown to crosslink cadherin/catenin complexes and the actin cytoskeleton, playing a critical role in cadherin-based cell adhesion (Otani and Furuse, 2020). Consequently, in the hen ovarian follicles, ZO1 may also contribute to the structural integrity of the follicular wall. Alterations in ZO1 expression during chicken follicle development may indicate its involvement in processes related to follicle growth, including morphological tissue turnover (remodeling), as well as cell proliferation and differentiation (Zhang et al., 2018).

Furthermore, in this paper, we demonstrated different abundances of the transcript and protein of the key adherens junction proteins, i.e., E-cadherin and β-catenin, in the wall of chicken follicles at different stages of development. E-cadherin belongs to the class of calcium-dependent transmembrane proteins that bind to the actin cytoskeleton via the intracellular proteins α-catenin and β-catenin. In turn, β-catenin, in addition to its role as a molecular bridge between the cytoplasmic domain of E-cadherin and α-catenin, is a crucial actor in the Wnt signaling pathway. In addition to the previously found, abovementioned proteins in the egg coat of the F1 follicle of the turkey (Słowińska et al., 2021), the identification of these proteins in the follicles at different stages of development, suggests an essential role of adherens junctions for follicle growth and maturation in avian species. Of particular interest is the immunofluorescent localization of both adherens junction proteins, principally in the granulosa layer. Their localization at cell-to-cell contacts indicates that they form a barrier that regulates paracellular transport. Consistent with the findings of this study, previous research has localized cadherin and β-catenin to granulosa cells/cell membranes, thereby implicating these proteins in the follicular development and function in mammals (Sundfeldt, et al., 2000; Boyer et al., 2010). In birds, the intercellular space between granulosa cells appears to play a regulatory role in the movement of yolk precursors to the receptors on the oocyte surface, a process that may be influenced by adherens junction proteins. Consequently, E-cadherin and β-catenin have the potential to be implicated in the regulation of yolk accumulation, as it was strongly suggested for occludin (Schuster et al., 2004). The particularly robust immunostaining for E-cadherin observed in the WFs may indicate a potential role for this protein in the selection of follicles into the preovulatory hierarchy of fast-growing follicles. In addition to their role in cell adhesion and the maintenance of follicular integrity, adherens junction proteins may also be involved in signaling events primarily occurring in the granulosa layer of follicular wall in birds. For instance, they may regulate the fate of cells. As was found in ovarian tumor cells, E-cadherin can be engaged in promoting cell proliferation by activating Akt/MAPK signaling pathways (Reddy et al., 2005; Tiwari et al., 2013). Similarly, β-catenin, as a transcriptional regulator, controls cell proliferation (Ma et al., 2024) and differentiation by activating the Wnt signaling pathway (Garcia et al., 2018).

In avian species, a major gap junction protein, Cx43, has been identified during the process of follicle development in hens (Frydrych et al., 2024) and quails (Farioli-Vecchioli et al., 2000; Krysko et al., 2004). The Cx43 protein was predominantly localized to the granulosa layer. In this study, we sought to confirmed the expression and localization of this protein in ovarian follicles. Our findings provide further support for the assumption that Cx43 serves as a crucial player in intercellular communication, allowing for direct exchange or release of certain metabolites, ions, and small signaling molecules between granulosa cells, as well as between granulosa cells and the oocyte (Frydrych et al., 2024). Independently of Cx43 ability to form gap junctions, the role of Cx43 in the follicle wall may be attributed to the regulation of various processes, including steroidogenesis, regulation of cell volume, molecule secretion, and cell proliferation, migration, and survival. Such roles were also suggested in different cells of non-avian species (Ke et al., 2005; Francis et al., 2011; Winterhager and Kidder, 2015).

A body of research has previously been conducted on the participation of tight, adherens, and gap junctions in the tissue regression of the hen oviduct (Ariyadi et al., 2013; Frydrych et al., 2025; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025). This research, in addition to accumulating evidence that cell-cell junctions impact tissue remodeling under physiological and pathological conditions (Balda and Matter, 2016; Zhang et al., 2024), prompted us to address the following question: whether the molecular composition of cell-cell junctions in the ovarian follicles changes during a pause in laying induced by fasting in hens. We found that the effect of fasting on cell-cell junction protein expression varies depending on the stage of follicle development, emphasizing the developmental stage-specific nature of tissue architecture, cell interactions, and functions. In pre-recruited, slow-growing follicles, which are resistant to atresia during fasting (Proszkowiec et al., 2005; Socha and Hrabia, 2019), the abundance of mRNA transcripts remained mostly unchanged, while protein abundances altered depending on the specific protein. Conversely, in atretic yellow preovulatory follicles, advanced atresia was accompanied in most cases by decreased expression of examined genes and proteins in the follicle wall, suggesting decomposition of cell-cell junctions, leading to disruption in tissue structure, and eventually to follicle atresia. Taking into consideration that follicle atresia is initiated within the granulosa layer (Hrabia, 2022; Hrabia and Sechman, 2024), and that cell-cell junction proteins are primarily located in this layer of the follicle wall, we propose that follicle atresia is closely related to disturbance in expression and localization of cell-cell junction proteins. As shown previously, in cultured granulosa cells of quail the F1 follicle, Cx43 exhibits an inverse correlation with the rate of apoptosis, suggesting that Cx43 affects granulosa cell survival (Krysko et al., 2004). The hypothesis is further substantiated by the reduced and irregular immunoreactivity observed for occludin, ZO1, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and Cx43 proteins in the wall of atretic yellow follicles compared to normal/healthy follicles. Consequently, the disruption of intercellular junctions may represent a critical mechanism underlying ovarian follicle atresia. Subsequent studies that examine the expression and protein modifications occurring at various stages of follicle atresia are warranted.

Conclusions

The results obtained strongly suggest that tight, adherens, and gap junction proteins, including claudin 1, occludin, ZO1, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and Cx43, orchestrate follicle development and play a pivotal role in the transition of follicles from growth to atresia. The disorganization of intercellular junctions after fasting may be a key factor that initiates disturbances in the structure of the follicular wall, the cell-cell interaction and communication, and the accumulation of yolk, resulting in the atresia of hierarchical follicles. This phenomenon has the potential to impact the overall egg-laying performance of poultry. Furthermore, our findings provide novel insights into the molecular composition of tight, adherens, and gap junctions, and their contribution to the remodeling of the follicular wall during follicle development and fasting-induced atresia in hens.
