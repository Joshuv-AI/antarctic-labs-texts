Accuracy and Reduced Sleep Disruption of a Wearable Smartwatch for Nocturnal Blood Pressure Monitoring: A Validation Study
Authors: Yanbo Liu, Yang Lu, Jiabo Wu, Tian Zhang, Beibei Wang, Zhuang Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNocturnal blood pressure is a significant predictor of cardiovascular risk, but traditional ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) can disrupt sleep. This study aimed to validate the accuracy of the Huawei Watch D2 (WD2), a wrist‐worn oscillometric device, and assess its impact on sleep quality compared to conventional ABPM. This prospective, single‐center trial first validated the WD2 against a mercury sphygmomanometer in 85 participants according to ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 standards in both seated and supine positions. Subsequently, the device's nocturnal accuracy and sleep impact were compared against a traditional ABPM device in 46 participants over a three‐night protocol. The WD2 met all ISO accuracy standards. During nighttime monitoring, there was no significant difference between the WD2 and ABPM for mean systolic or diastolic blood pressure. However, the number of awakenings was significantly lower on nights with the WD2 alone compared to nights with the ABPM device (p = 0.016). In conclusion, the Huawei Watch D2 is a clinically validated device that provides nocturnal blood pressure readings comparable to traditional ABPM with the significant advantage of minimal sleep disruption, positioning it as a valuable alternative for nocturnal hypertension monitoring.

Background

Nocturnal blood pressure (BP) is increasingly recognized as a more significant predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality than daytime measurements [1]. Reflecting this, the most recent 2023 European Society of Hypertension (ESH) guidelines continue to strongly recommend out‐of‐office BP measurements, emphasizing that nocturnal BP provides superior prognostic information for cardiovascular events compared to office BP alone [2]. The guidelines, therefore, recommend nocturnal monitoring for a more accurate risk assessment in most hypertensive patients, noting that abnormal patterns like “non‐dipping” are linked to poorer outcomes.

The standard method for this measurement is 24‐hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM). However, its repetitive upper‐arm cuff inflations frequently disrupt sleep by causing brief arousals [3]. This sleep fragmentation can, in turn, trigger transient BP elevations, potentially leading to an overestimation of true nocturnal BP and affecting clinical interpretation.

Consequently, wrist‐worn smartwatches with oscillometric cuffs are emerging as a promising alternative. While numerous studies have validated the accuracy of various smartwatch models [4, 5], this technology has not yet achieved widespread clinical acceptance, potentially because each new device requires its own rigorous, independent validation to be trusted. For instance, although the predecessor, the HUAWEI WATCH D, has been successfully validated in several studies [6], a new model—the WATCH D2—was launched in 2025 with an enhanced air bladder and a more advanced BP algorithm. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether these devices can quantifiably reduce the sleep disturbances inherent to traditional ABPM while providing comparable nocturnal BP data.

Therefore, our primary hypothesis was that the Huawei Watch D2 is a clinically accurate device that meets the ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard. Furthermore, we hypothesized that when compared to conventional ABPM, the device could provide comparable nocturnal BP readings while causing significantly less sleep disruption.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective, single‐center, controlled trial aimed to evaluate the accuracy and comfort of the WD2 smartwatch against traditional mercury sphygmomanometers and ABPM. The study was conducted in three sequential phases: (1) Formal Validation (Seated Position): The primary phase involved validating the WD2's accuracy in the seated position, conducted in strict accordance with the ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard. (2) Supine Position Accuracy Assessment: As the ISO standard only requires validation in the seated position, we conducted an additional evaluation to establish the device's accuracy in the supine position. This step was a crucial prerequisite for assessing the device's suitability for nocturnal monitoring. All procedures and analysis criteria from the ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard were adhered to, with the only modification being the participant's posture. For this assessment, participants were positioned lying flat, with their hands relaxed and their palms facing their bodies. (3) Nocturnal Monitoring and Sleep Impact: The final phase compared the WD2's nocturnal BP accuracy and its impact on sleep quality against a conventional ABPM device.

Devices

The wearable device assessed was the HUAWEI Watch D2 (WD2), a wrist‐worn smartwatch that uses an oscillometric method for BP measurement, incorporating a miniature pneumatic pump and air bladder. While its predecessor has been validated in several studies, the manufacturer states that this new generation features enhancements to its air bladder and BP algorithm. For sleep tracking, the device utilizes its integrated TruSleep algorithm, which combines data from accelerometry (ACC) and photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors to monitor sleep stages, heart rate, and generate a sleep quality score. According to the manufacturer, this algorithm has been validated internally against polysomnography (PSG); however, these validation data have not been published. Reference devices included a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer (Jiangsu YuYue Medical Instruments Co. Ltd, Danyang, Jiangsu Province, China) for daytime validation and a conventional A&D TM2430 ABPM device for nighttime monitoring. For the sphygmomanometer, two cuffs with inflatable bladder dimensions (12 × 24 cm and 16 × 32 cm) were used, selected to ensure the bladder length covered 75%–100% and the width covered 37%–50% of each subject's upper arm circumference.

Participants

We recruited 109 volunteers aged between 18 and 80 years, encompassing both healthy individuals and patients with or without antihypertensive therapy. A final cohort of 85 participants was selected by the need to simultaneously satisfy two key requirements of the ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard: the distribution of reference BP measurements and the distribution of wrist circumferences. This ensured a robust and representative validation cohort. Key exclusion criteria, as per the ISO standard for auscultatory clinical investigations, included arrhythmias, pregnancy, secondary hypertension, and unclear Korotkoff sounds. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital.

Procedures

The study was conducted in three sequential phases. The first phase was the formal validation in the seated position, strictly adhering to the ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard. For this phase, the WD2 was compared against a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer, with readings taken by two trained and blinded observers. Proper fitting was ensured for both devices: an appropriately sized sphygmomanometer cuff was selected based on arm circumference, and the corresponding watch strap was chosen based on wrist circumference (M‐sized: 130–160 mm; L‐sized: 161–210 mm). Before the three official validation measurements, an initial measurement was performed and discarded from the analysis to mitigate alerting responses. During this validation, participants were instructed to place their left wrist on the right side of their chest to ensure the watch was held at heart level.

The second phase was an additional validation in the supine position, which followed all procedures from the first phase, with the only modification being the participant's posture. For this phase, participants lay supine with both hands placed at their sides and their legs kept flat, without crossing or bending them. The watch was worn on the left wrist, with the palm relaxed and facing the body. Participants were required to rest in this specific position for 5 min before the measurements began and to maintain this posture throughout the entire process.

The final phase involved nocturnal monitoring to assess both BP accuracy and sleep impact. This was conducted through a three‐night protocol: on Night 1, both the ABPM and WD2 measured BP; on Night 2, only the WD2 measured BP; and on Night 3, the WD2 only monitored sleep without BP measurements. For each monitoring night, participants fitted the devices after getting into bed and stopped the measurements upon waking in the morning. Measurements were taken automatically every 30 min, with ABPM readings scheduled 2 min prior to avoid interference. For these nighttime measurements, participants were not required to maintain any specific posture; according to the manufacturer, the device's BP reading algorithm is optimized using sensitive signal features extracted from various sleeping positions. To ensure the comparability of sleep data, which is closely linked to sleep duration in the TruSleep algorithm, participants with a sleep duration difference of more than 2 h between any two of the three monitoring nights were excluded from the final analysis (Figure 1).

Sample Size and Statistical Analysis

A minimum of 85 participants were required for the daytime validation to align with ANSI/AAMI/ISO standards, and a minimum of 35 participants were targeted for the nocturnal study per ESH guidelines. Agreement between devices was assessed using the mean difference (MD), the standard deviation of the differences (SDD), Student's t test, and Bland–Altman analysis. A repeated measures analysis of variance (RM‐ANOVA) was used to compare sleep quality parameters across the three nights. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Data

Initially, 109 volunteers were recruited for the study. However, in accordance with the BP distribution requirements outlined in the ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard, only 85 participants who met the necessary BP range stratification criteria were ultimately included in the final analysis. These 85 participants underwent both seated and supine BP validation measurements. The cohort consisted of 30 males and 55 females, with a mean age of 45.6 ± 14.1 years (range: 19–75 years). The average height and weight were 1.64 ± 0.08 m and 64.3 ± 12.5 kg, respectively, and the mean wrist circumference was 16.7 ± 1.9 cm. Among the participants, 17 (20.0%) had a known history of hypertension, and 11 (12.9%) were taking regular antihypertensive medication (Table 1).

A Bland–Altman analysis was conducted to evaluate the agreement between the WD2 and reference BP measurements in both seated and supine positions. In the seated position, the mean difference for SBP was 1.33 mmHg with 95% limits of agreement (LoA) ranging from –10.33 to +12.99 mmHg, and the mean difference for DBP was –0.22 mmHg with LoA from –9.04 to +8.60 mmHg. In the supine position, the mean difference for SBP was 1.69 mmHg with LoA from –12.54 to +15.91 mmHg, and for DBP the mean difference was –0.60 mmHg, with LoA from –10.47 to +9.26 mmHg. Across all conditions, the differences were centered around zero with no significant proportional bias, and over 95% of the data points lay within the LoA range. These findings support a high level of agreement between the test device and reference measurements, with acceptable variability for clinical validation (Figure 2).

The accuracy of the smartwatch sphygmomanometer was successfully validated against a mercury sphygmomanometer in 85 subjects, in accordance with the ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard. The device met the requirements for both primary criteria. For Criterion 1, which assesses overall measurement agreement from 255 paired readings, the mean difference (± standard deviation) was 1.33 ± 5.96 mmHg for SBP (p = 0.0230) and −0.22 ± 4.81 mmHg for DBP (p = 0.47). Validation of Blood Pressure Measurement in Supine Position

Building upon the previous validation in the seated position, we continued to evaluate the accuracy of the smartwatch sphygmomanometer (WD2) by conducting tests with 85 participants in the supine position to set the groundwork for nighttime BP measurement validation. Apart from the supine posture, all procedures strictly followed the AAMI/ESH/ISO Universal Standard (ISO 81060‐2:2018). In this supine validation, the device met the primary ISO requirements. For Criterion 1, the mean difference (± standard deviation) was 3.27 ± 6.03 mmHg for SBP and −2.37 ± 5.02 mmHg for DBP. For Criterion 2, the standard deviation of the per‐subject mean differences was 5.53 mmHg for SBP and 4.69 mmHg for DBP (Table 4). While these supine differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for both, paired t‐test), this finding likely reflects the methodological challenge of ensuring precise wrist‐to‐heart level alignment, where minor vertical deviations can introduce consistent hydrostatic effects on the pressure reading. Crucially, despite this statistical variance, the magnitude of the difference remained well within the tolerance limits for mean error required by the standard. The distribution of absolute differences further indicated good agreement: 86.3% of SBP readings and 91.8% of DBP readings were within ±10 mmHg of the reference, with only 0.4% of readings exceeding a difference of ±15 mmHg (Table 5). These findings confirm the device maintains acceptable accuracy in the supine position, providing a solid foundation for subsequent nighttime validation studies.

Accuracy of Nighttime Blood Pressure Monitoring

Of the 58 participants recruited for the nighttime validation phase, 12 were ultimately excluded from the final analysis. These exclusions were due to incomplete or insufficient data (n = 5), which occurred because participants, who were required to operate the devices themselves, forgot to properly activate the BP monitoring functions on some nights; ABPM malfunction (n = 1), one subject was excluded owing to premature battery exhaustion of the ABPM device; protocol deviations where sleep duration difference exceeded 2 h across monitoring nights (n = 2); and withdrawal due to severe sleep disturbance attributed to the ABPM device (n = 4) (Figure 1).

The analysis of nighttime BP accuracy was conducted on 46 participants (29 female, 17 male) with a mean age of 47.65 ± 13.91 years (Table 6). The mean difference between the devices (WD2 − ABPM) was small, at +0.98 mmHg for SBP and +1.52 mmHg for DBP. A paired t‐test confirmed that these differences were not statistically significant for either SBP (104.24 ± 13.46 mmHg for ABPM vs. 105.22 ± 9.98 mmHg for WD2; p = 0.347) or DBP (66.04 ± 9.08 mmHg for ABPM vs. 67.57 ± 6.68 mmHg for WD2; p = 0.073) (Table 7). To further evaluate the agreement between the devices, a Bland–Altman analysis was performed on these average nightly readings. The analysis demonstrated a low systematic bias for both systolic (+0.98 mmHg) and diastolic (+1.52 mmHg) pressures. The 95% LoA were −12.71 to 14.67 mmHg for SBP and −9.49 to 12.54 mmHg for DBP, providing a clinically evaluable range for individual measurement variability (Figure 3).

To provide a more granular comparison, we conducted a further analysis on all individual BP readings, resulting in 624 valid time‐matched pairs for analysis. The number of successful paired measurements per participant ranged from 8 to 19 (median: 14). This analysis showed a mean difference (WD2 − ABPM) of +1.20 ± 11.67 mmHg for SBP and +1.84 ± 8.48 mmHg for DBP. Unlike the analysis of nightly averages, a paired t‐test on this large dataset revealed that these small, positive biases were statistically significant (p = 0.0106 for SBP and p < 0.001 for DBP). To visualize the dynamic trend, we plotted the hourly average BP for both devices (Figure 4). The resulting graph clearly demonstrates that the BP curves recorded by the WD2 and the ABPM device track each other closely with nearly parallel patterns. The statistical significance of the small bias is likely attributable to the high statistical power of the large sample size. Given the small magnitude of the difference and the strong visual concordance in the hourly profiles, this statistical finding is unlikely to represent a clinically meaningful divergence. This confirms that the device provides a reliable estimate of a patient's moment‐to‐moment nocturnal BP profile.

To further probe the consistency of the devices and provide standardized data, we performed a subanalysis on three distinct fixed‐time windows based on a previously established methodology for segmenting nocturnal periods [7]. To ensure a representative assessment of BP within each window, only participants who provided more than seven valid recording pairs were included in the paired t‐test analysis for that specific segment. These windows included the 2 h‐early shifted nighttime (23:00–04:00), the Standard fixed‐clock nighttime (01:00–06:00), and the 2 h‐late shifted nighttime (03:00–08:00). The results from these segmented analyses were highly consistent with the findings for the entire nocturnal session, showing no statistically significant differences between the WD2 and ABPM for either mean systolic or mean DBP in any window. Specifically, the mean difference for SBP was 0.99 mmHg (p = 0.3167) for the 2 h‐early shifted window (n = 41), 1.00 mmHg (p = 0.3969) for the Standard fixed‐clock window (n = 40), and 1.07 mmHg (p = 0.5549) for the 2 h‐late shifted window (n = 21). Similarly, the mean difference for DBP was 1.04 mmHg (p = 0.1976), 1.85 mmHg (p = 0.0577), and 2.59 mmHg (p = 0.0509) for the respective windows. Sleep Quality and Measurement Comfort

To evaluate the impact on sleep, this study utilized a three‐night protocol where sleep quality was systematically assessed under different monitoring conditions. Night 1 involved simultaneous monitoring with both the conventional ABPM device and the WD2 smartwatch. On Night 2, participants used only the WD2 with BP measurements activated, and on Night 3, the WD2 was worn for sleep tracking alone, without any BP measurements. However, a more granular analysis of sleep fragmentation metrics revealed a clinically important distinction. The number of awakenings was significantly higher on the night participants wore both the conventional ABPM and the WD2 smartwatch (mean 1.30 awakenings) compared to the nights when the WD2 was used alone (Table 9). This suggests that while the TruSleep algorithm's multiparameter approach provides a stable, holistic measure of sleep architecture, it may be less sensitive to the brief, periodic disruptions caused by ABPM cuff inflations. Therefore, the significant increase in awakenings is a key finding that directly emphasizes the negative impact traditional ABPM devices can have on sleep quality and highlights the smartwatch's advantage in providing less intrusive nighttime monitoring.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive validation of the WD2,a wrist‐worn oscillometric smartwatch, for BP monitoring. Our findings demonstrate that the device meets the rigorous international accuracy standards (ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018) in both seated and supine validation tests. In a clinical nocturnal setting, the WD2's average nightly BP readings showed no statistically significant difference when compared to a traditional ABPM device. Critically, this comparable nocturnal accuracy was achieved with a significant improvement in sleep quality, as evidenced by a lower number of awakenings compared to monitoring with a standard ABPM device.

Establishing Clinical Grade Accuracy

A primary objective of this study was to validate the device's accuracy against the stringent ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 standard. The device successfully met the validation criteria, showing minimal mean differences and standard deviations when compared against a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer. This level of accuracy is critical for any device intended for clinical application, where precise BP management is essential for preventing cardiovascular events.

A noteworthy finding was the small but statistically significant mean difference for SBP in the seated position (1.33 mmHg, p = 0.023), as well as for both systolic and diastolic pressures in the supine position (p < 0.001). This should not be interpreted as a clinical failure of the device. Rather, it highlights the ability of a study with sufficient statistical power (n = 85 participants) to detect minor, systematic biases that are clinically negligible. The difference in the supine position is likely attributable to the well known methodological challenge of ensuring precise wrist‐to‐heart level alignment, where minor hydrostatic effects can cause a consistent offset. For both postures, the crucial conclusion is that the magnitude of these mean differences remained well within the ±5 mmHg tolerance limit required by the ISO standard, thus confirming the device's clinical grade accuracy.

The Bland–Altman analysis confirmed a low systematic bias, indicating that the WD2 is a reliable tool for tracking nocturnal BP trends in a clinical context. These findings are consistent with validation studies of other wearable oscillometric devices, such as the Omron HeartGuide, which also demonstrated a clinically acceptable level of agreement when compared to traditional ABPM [4]. While the comparison of average nightly BP showed no statistically significant difference, the analysis of all 624 individual measurements revealed a small but statistically significant bias. The interpretation of this finding is complex, as single, moment‐to‐moment readings can be influenced by multiple dynamic factors. These include constant, subtle shifts in sleeping posture that introduce variable hydrostatic effects, and the manufacturer's proprietary algorithmic adjustments, which are designed to compensate for these very changes. The small, systematic bias detected may, therefore, reflect the intricate interaction between these physical and computational factors. From a clinical perspective, however, the magnitude of this difference is minimal, and the strong visual concordance in the hourly trend analysis (Figure 4) confirms the device's reliability for tracking nocturnal BP patterns.

The Primary Advantage: Overcoming Sleep Disruption

The most significant clinical advantage of the WD2 lies in its ability to monitor nocturnal BP with minimal sleep disruption, directly addressing a major limitation of traditional ABPM [3]. Standard ABPM is known to cause discomfort and sleep fragmentation, which not only affects patient compliance but can also artifactually elevate BP readings during arousals. In this study, the impact of ABPM was so significant that several participants withdrew due to severe sleep disturbance, a powerful real‐world testament to the problem.

Our findings objectively quantify the WD2's benefit. While holistic sleep scores were similar, the number of awakenings was significantly lower on nights when the WD2 was used alone compared to the night with ABPM. This reduction in sleep fragmentation is a key clinical outcome, suggesting that the BP data captured by the WD2 more accurately reflect a patient's actual resting state.

Future Clinical Applications and Integration Into Practice

The potential clinical applications for this validated device are broad and significant. For patients who cannot tolerate traditional ABPM, the WD2 provides a viable method to obtain crucial nocturnal BP data, aiding in the identification of high‐risk phenotypes like non‐dipping hypertension. Its comfort enables multi‐night home monitoring, allowing clinicians to titrate antihypertensive medications based on nocturnal response more effectively. When integrated into telemedicine platforms, which are becoming central to chronic disease care [8], the device could revolutionize remote patient monitoring by providing longitudinal data for proactive interventions. Ultimately, by offering a more user‐friendly experience, the WD2 can enhance patient adherence and engagement in their long‐term hypertension management.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

A key strength of this study is its innovative methodological approach, recognizing that nocturnal BP is measured while patients are lying down. Yet, no specific validation standard exists for this posture [9]. We creatively adapted the rigorous ANSI/AAMI/ISO 81060‐2:2018 protocol for supine measurements. This provides a robust, clinically relevant accuracy assessment that goes beyond standard requirements and strengthens the foundation for the device's use in nocturnal monitoring. Furthermore, the study's rigorous, multiphase design and its dual focus on both accuracy and patient‐centric outcomes like sleep quality are additional strengths.

However, some limitations should be noted. Although the sample size was sufficient for device validation, future studies with larger, more diverse populations would further strengthen the findings. A key limitation is that the sleep quality assessment relied on the device's proprietary TruSleep algorithm. Although the manufacturer reports internal validation against PSG, the absence of peer‐reviewed, publicly available data makes independent verification of its accuracy challenging. Therefore, our findings regarding sleep parameters should be interpreted within this context, and we strongly support the need for future research to validate these results against the clinical gold standard of PSG. Future work should also include longitudinal studies to assess the long‐term accuracy and clinical impact of integrating such wearable data into routine patient care. Finally, while this study validates a cuff‐based wearable, the field is rapidly advancing toward cuffless BP monitoring technologies. Driven by the potential for continuous monitoring with minimal impact on sleep quality, these emerging devices, however, present their own unique and significant validation challenges that are yet to be fully addressed by current standards [10].

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Huawei Watch D2 is an accurate and clinically validated device that meets rigorous international standards. It provides nocturnal BP data comparable to traditional ABPM while causing significantly less sleep disruption. This positions the device as a promising tool to improve patient adherence and the quality of data in nocturnal hypertension management.
