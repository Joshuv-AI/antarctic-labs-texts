High-resolution assessment of wind energy resources over the Arabian Peninsula
Authors: Harikishan Gandham, Hari Prasad Dasari, Abdullah Alfadda, Ibrahim Hoteit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Arabian Peninsula (AP) is a climate change hotspot characterized by extreme climatic conditions that drive high energy demand and urgently require sustainable energy solutions. In this context, wind energy offers a reliable pathway for large-scale renewable energy development. This study presents a comprehensive, high-resolution assessment of wind resources across the AP using a 5-km reanalysis dataset (APR) generated with a regionally tuned Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model for the period 1980–2019. Validation of 100 m wind speeds (WS100) from APR against ground-based observations shows strong agreement, with statistically significant correlations and low biases across the region. WS100 magnitudes exhibit strong seasonality throughout the AP. Wind drought episodes (WS100 < 3.5 m/s) are prevalent along the western coast, while rated-wind conditions (WS100 between 11 and 25 m/s), critical for turbine performance, occur most frequently over the northern and central Red Sea. Extreme wind events (WS100 > 25 m/s) are rare across the AP. Long-term trends reveal a pronounced summer weakening of WS100 over the northern Red Sea and eastern AP, and a concurrent strengthening over the central Red Sea and southern Saudi Arabia. These changes are accompanied by increased wind droughts in the former and more frequent rated-wind conditions in the latter. Capacity factor estimates derived from modern turbine technologies highlight key wind energy hotspots in the Gulf of Aqaba, the Suez Canal, the Tokar Gap region, and central-western Saudi Arabia. These trends are analogous to the long-term evolution observed in WS100. Our analysis offers strategic insights for wind farm deployment and infrastructure planning, supporting sustainable energy development and strengthening energy security across the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44961-z.Keywords: High-resolution wind energy resources, 100 m wind speeds, Wind droughts, Weather research and forecasting model, Arabian Peninsula and Red SeaSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Climate change has undermined food and water security by altering precipitation and temperature patterns and by melting cryospheric elements, etc. Driven by increasing anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and unsustainable land use practices (e.g.,1), it has disrupted natural systems, amplifying vulnerabilities in already resource-stressed regions (e.g., Crowley2, Seneviratne3). It has significantly increased the frequency and intensity of climate extremes, thereby impeding progress toward achieving the global sustainable development goals4. Global efforts to combat climate change and global warming increasingly focus on accelerating the transition to green and renewable energy resources such as solar, wind, and hydropower (e.g., Pryor et al.5, Gernaat6, Olabi7, Pryor8). Wind energy, in particular, has emerged as a key pillar of the global shift toward clean energy due to its ability to generate large-scale electricity with zero carbon emissions, making it one of the most effective tools for decarbonizing the power sector (e.g., Pryor , Barthelmie9, Pryor10). With rapidly advancing technology, cost-effectiveness, and vast untapped potential, wind power offers a reliable and scalable solution to meet growing energy demands while supporting climate mitigation goals (e.g., Barthelmie et al.11, Evans et al.12). Following decades of development, the global wind industry surpassed the 1TW milestone in 2024 and is projected to exceed 2TW by 2030 (GWR13).

Within this global transition, the Arabian Peninsula (AP) represents a critical regional context where the deployment of renewable energy is particularly urgent and relevant. The AP has emerged as a critical climate change hotspot during recent decades (e.g., Gandham14, Zittis15), characterized by rapid increases in surface temperatures, thermal discomfort, chronic water scarcity, land degradation, and severe droughts (e.g., Dasari16, Olsson17, Perkins-Kirkpatrick18, Saharwardi19). These escalating climatic stresses have significantly surged energy demand across the region, particularly for cooling and desalination, placing added strain on already vulnerable energy systems (e.g.,20–22). Wind energy, already central to global decarbonization efforts, holds particular promise for the AP by addressing region-specific energy-climate challenges without compromising performance. Although the AP receives abundant insolation and has high solar potential (e.g., Dasari23), extreme dust loading, and high surface temperatures (e.g., Attada24) constrain solar energy use. Consequently, these harsh environmental conditions substantially degrade the efficiency of solar panels (e.g., Patt25, Salamah26). However, wind power remains relatively robust under such environmental stresses (e.g., Pryor and Barthelmie10, Richardson27).

Accurate evaluation of wind energy resources is the first and most essential step in wind farm development, optimal siting, and efficient operation (e.g., 8,28–30). Only limited attempts have been made to explore the wind energy potential of the region. The initial efforts have primarily focused on using in situ and ground-based observations to understand the variability (e.g., Al-Nassar31; Sultan et al32,33). However, these studies are spatially limited and fail to capture the broad-scale wind characteristics. Later studies employed coarse global reanalysis datasets to assess wind resources at the national scale. Yip34 indicated that the western mountain range of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) and the Red Sea (RS) coast are key zones of wind energy based on the MERRA (0.5° ⋅ 0.67° resolution) reanalysis data. Subsequently, Chen et al.35 denoted that the areas between Medina and the west coast of KSA exhibit favorable conditions during summer months for wind energy extraction. This finding is based on the regional climate simulations from the MENA-CORDEX ensembles and MERRA-2 reanalysis (0.5° ⋅ 0.625°). Hereher and El Kenawy36 further evaluated wind resources over Oman using the higher-resolution NCEP-CFSR reanalysis (0.25° × 0.25°). Notably, global studies based on ERA5 reanalysis (0.25° ⋅ 0.25°) imply that the AP can be considered one of the reliable wind power zones within the tropical belt due to the limited occurrences of extreme wind events (e.g.,10,37). However, these global datasets do not capture fine-scale atmospheric features, such as topographic flows, shallow jets, and localized wind patterns due to their coarse resolution. In fact, the high-resolution wind data are critical for capturing such wind attributes, particularly the extreme winds of the region (e.g., Pryor38).

Motivated by these limitations, several studies have adopted high-resolution dynamical model simulations to better characterize wind energy-relevant features over the Arabian Peninsula. Among these, Giani et al., (2020) represents one of the earliest high-resolution (6 km) regional efforts to assess wind energy resources over Saudi Arabia using WRF simulations. Their analysis included estimates of wind energy potential, capacity factors, and the identification of suitable locations for wind turbine deployment. Tagle39 combined high-resolution WRF simulations with large-ensemble outputs from the NCAR Large Ensemble (LENS) to examine wind variability and uncertainty. Crippa40 developed an improved method to extrapolate wind speeds from the surface to turbine hub height, addressing a key limitation in wind energy assessments that rely on simplified power-law approaches. Using hourly WRF simulations at 6 km resolution over Saudi Arabia, they demonstrated that accounting for boundary-layer dynamics and atmospheric stability substantially improves hub-height wind estimates. This approach reduces biases in capacity factor calculations compared to traditional fixed or stability-dependent power-law formulations. Chen et al.41 used 5 km WRF simulations to quantify turbine cut-out exceedance and extreme wind return levels over Saudi Arabia. Their Bayesian spatial extremes analysis revealed significant spatial variability in wind-energy operation risk. Li42 analyzed seasonal and annual characteristics of extreme wind speeds across the AP using convection-permitting WRF simulations, offering insights into wind variability and extremes relevant for energy system design. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the importance of high-resolution regional modeling for capturing mesoscale wind features over the AP.

However, several key aspects remain to be fully addressed over the AP. This includes characterization of diurnal and seasonal variability of winds, as well as the spatial distribution and persistence of wind events relative to turbine cut-in and cut-out thresholds. A consistent peninsula-wide assessment of hub-height winds and associated uncertainty is also required to represent the geographic complexity and multi-scale atmospheric variability governing wind resources across the region. Consequently, addressing these outstanding aspects requires long-term, high-resolution wind resource assessments that explicitly resolve the geographic complexity of the AP and multi-scale atmospheric processes governing its wind variability.

For the first time, this study employs a high-resolution (5 km) regional reanalysis specifically developed for the AP and adjoining regions (hereafter APR) for the period 1980–2019 to examine key aspects of the region’s wind resources. We first validated APR performance against the available wind observations at 100 m level. We then analyzed 100 m wind speeds (WS100) to characterize the spatial and temporal distribution of wind regimes across the AP. We categorized the wind events according to standard turbine operating thresholds and evaluated their occurrence frequencies in the AP. Using turbine-specific power curves, we estimate capacity factors and identify hitherto unexplored regions with strong wind energy potential. Finally, long-term trends in WS100/wind event frequencies are examined to reveal their variability and driving mechanisms. The study is structured as follows: Sect. 2 outlines the details of the high-resolution APR dataset. Section 3 presents the climatological characteristics of WS100, wind events, and capacity factors. Section 4 explores the long-term trends along with the physical mechanisms responsible for the observed changes in these variables. Finally, Sect. 5 summarizes the key findings of the study.

Data and methodology

The AP reanalysis

The APR is a high-resolution regional atmospheric dataset developed using the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model (e.g.,16,23,43) in an assimilative configuration. The model was configured at 5 km resolution, covering the AP and surrounding areas from 23°E to 81°E and 6°S to 36°N. This reanalysis was specifically generated to support regional climate studies over the AP by assimilating all available regional observations into the WRF model. The model configuration incorporates several advanced physical parameterizations, including the Rapid Radiative Transfer Model for Global Circulation Models44 for surface shortwave and longwave irradiance, Thompson microphysics for cloud-aerosol interactions45, the Kain-Fritsch scheme for cumulus convection (Kain and Fritsch46,47), the MYNN2.5 scheme for boundary layer processes, and the Noah land surface model for surface-atmosphere interactions48. Initial and boundary conditions were derived from ERA-Interim reanalysis data at a 0.75° resolution49. This in-house-generated APR datasets have been widely used to investigate regional atmospheric variability, extremes, and to support AI-driven weather forecasting systems (e.g., Dasari23, Dasari16, Dasari50, Hoteit51, Luong52, Sanikommu53, Sanjeev54, Thiruridathil55, Viswanadhapalli56, Viswanadhapalli57, Resifi58).

Empirical extrapolation techniques such as the logarithmic wind profile or power-law method (e.g.,59–62) are typically used to estimate wind speeds at hub height using surface or near-surface observations. These methods rely on simplified assumptions, such as idealized terrain, homogeneous land-type, neutral atmospheric stability, and horizontally homogeneous conditions. These assumptions often do not hold true in regions like the AP, where diverse landscapes and strong climatic variability introduce substantial uncertainty in estimating wind speeds at hub height (e.g., Giani et al., 2020). Critically, APR provides dynamically simulated wind fields at turbine hub height (e.g., 100 m). This offers significant advantages over conventional extrapolated wind estimates. Moreover, the high spatial resolution of APR resolves mesoscale features such as terrain-driven flows, thermal circulations, and localized low-level jets. As a result, the characterization of wind fields becomes more accurate and detailed. This enhances the reliability of wind energy assessments and supports optimal siting across the AP.

Wind observations

The King Abdullah City for Atomic and Renewable Energy (K.A.CARE) established the Renewable Resource Monitoring and Mapping (RRMM) network to collect high-quality solar and wind observations across Saudi Arabia. These measurements form the foundation for assessing the renewable energy potential of the country. To evaluate the model performance, wind speed profiles were obtained from ten sites that are part of the K.A.CARE network. Each site is equipped with a 100 m meteorological mast fitted with P2546A cup anemometers at 40, 60, 80, and 100 m, allowing multi-level measurements of wind speed (e.g.,63). The network follows uniform instrumentation and configuration standards, and data collection is aligned with international guidelines to ensure accuracy and reliability64. We collected these datasets from ten different locations for the period from September 2013 to November 2016. It is important to note that these independent datasets are not assimilated in the APR. Classification of wind events

Wind events are classified based on specific wind speed thresholds that govern standard turbine operating conditions (e.g.,27,65–67). Wind drought events, defined as WS100 < 3.5 m/s, represent conditions below the cut-in speed of an operational wind turbine. Sub-rated winds (SR), characterized by 3.5 m/s ≤ WS100 < 11 m/s, mark the threshold where turbines start functioning and generating power. Rated-wind episodes (RAW), defined as 11 m/s ≤ WS100 < 25 m/s, represent conditions at which turbines provide maximum output. Finally, extreme winds (EW) occur at speeds of WS100 ≥ 25 m/s, which exceed the operational limit, causing the turbines to automatically shut down to prevent damage and ensure safety.

Seasonal variations in WS100 and associated wind episodes are examined across four defined seasons, namely, winter (DJF; December to February), spring (MAM; March to May), summer (JJA; June to August), and autumn (SON; September to November) for the period between 1980 and 2019.

Capacity factor (CF)

The CF provides a measure of the efficiency of wind resource utilization and serves as a useful diagnostic for characterizing the temporal variability of wind power potential across the region (e.g.,8). The CF of a wind turbine is the ratio of the actual power generated to the theoretical rated power (e.g., Pryor et al., 2021). In general, CF depends on the wind speed, surface drag, land use, land type, and turbine technology (e.g.,8,68). We computed the CFs for different recent turbine technologies. We selected two high-end technologies: the Nordex 3.6 MW (https://www.nordex-online.com/en/product/n131-3600/), and the Vestas V150 4.0-4.2 MW (https://www.vestas.com/en/energy-solutions/onshore-wind-turbines/4-mw-platform/V150-4-2-MW). Details of each turbine technology and other relevant information are provided in Table 1. The CFs are estimated based on the methodology detailed in Pryor et al.38 which uses the company-provided synthetic power curve information. We have also utilized the ERA570 100 m winds, and atmospheric fields to explore the seasonal evolution in wind and large-scale circulation patterns. Seasonal trends were estimated at each grid point using ordinary least squares regression applied to the seasonal mean time series. Results

Validation of WRF simulated WS100

Figure 1 presents the validation of APR-derived WS100 against wind observations from the K.A.CARE network. These monitoring sites are well distributed across Saudi Arabia, providing comprehensive spatial coverage for validation. We broadly grouped the station locations into the following KSA regions: north Red Sea coast (Al Wajh, Jeddah, Yanbu), north (Al Jouf, Turaif), north-central (Riyadh), north-east (Hafar Al Batin), and south (Sharurah). Model skill was evaluated using correlation coefficients (r), mean bias, and root mean square errors. The APR winds demonstrate statistically robust performance across all stations. North Red Sea coastal sites, in particular Jeddah and Yanbu, exhibited particularly high correlation values (> 0.9, p < 0.01). These stations lie under the influence of Red Sea wind regimes, characterized by a dominance of northwesterly winds (e.g.,74). These are followed by the northeastern (r = 0.87) and northern (r = 0.85) stations. The mean absolute bias values were low at the northern stations such as Al Wajh (0.11 m s− 1), Turaif (0.2 m s− 1), and Al Jouf (0.24 m s− 1), while the other sites exhibit values above 0.3. These results confirm the robust skill of the APR wind fields.

Climatological features of wind resources

Seasonal WS100 distribution across the AP

Figure 2 presents the seasonal climatology of WS100 over the AP. The central-west region of Saudi Arabia, characterized by rugged terrain, consistently exhibits higher wind speeds year-round than other inland areas. Wind speeds display moderate magnitude of around 7 m/s during winters (Fig. 2a) and strengthen through the summer (Fig. 2c). The seasonal evolution of winds is largely regulated by atmospheric pressure gradients and Coriolis forces, which control the flow and intensity of air masses. These forces are driven by meso/synoptic scale processes embedded within the larger framework of global atmospheric circulation (e.g.,75,76). A clear manifestation of these dynamics is also observed over the AP. The winter season is characterized by the simultaneous evolution of the semi-permanent high pressure systems over North Africa and eastern AP (Figure S2a). The intensity and position of these concurrent pressure systems regulate the direction and intensity of the winter WS100. The high pressure over north Africa channels winds toward the southwestern AP, while the eastern AP system draws winds from the northern AS toward the same region. These winds interact with the orography, leading to increased wind speeds over the central-west AP. Meanwhile, the intensified winds over eastern Sudan arise from a steep pressure gradient between the east African high and the tropical low situated to its south, sustaining strong, persistent wind flow across the region. A moderate increase in wind speeds is observed over the northern AP during the spring (Fig. 2b).

As the season progresses into summer, the large-scale pressure systems over and around the broader eastern Mediterranean (EM) and AP undergo significant transitions. These prominent changes are driven by the development of the Indian summer monsoon. Intense solar heating over the land extends the monsoonal low into the eastern AP (e.g.,24,77). Meanwhile, westward propagation of monsoon-induced diabatic heating leads to subsidence over the EM, strengthening surface high pressure in EM region (e.g., Tyrlis et al., 2013). The establishment of these contrasting pressure centers generates a strong east–west pressure gradient. This plays a major role in initiating and sustaining the Shamal wind jets (e.g.,14,78,79). Further, these low-level northwesterly winds extend from the EM across the AP (Figure S2c). As a result, elevated WS100 magnitudes are observed across the AP (Fig. 2c), accompanied by the development of key high wind zones over the northern RS, eastern AP, and central KSA.

A closer examination of the summer WS100 climatology map reveals the emergence of a stronger jet-like wind pattern originating from the coastal regions of eastern Sudan and extending toward the western coast of KSA. This notable feature is associated with the development of the Tokar Gap jet, a semi-permanent, localized wind system linked to the dynamics of the South Asian monsoon (e.g.,80,81). In summer, the moisture-laden southwesterly monsoon flow converges with the northerlies associated with the EM high over the steep terrain of the Tokar Gap region. This interaction leads to the formation of strong westerly winds across the southern RS (e.g., Davis et al.82). This low-level system persists throughout the summer season and plays a pivotal role in dust transport and moisture dynamics.

Diurnal variability in WS100 magnitudes

Based on the discussion in the previous section, summer emerges as the critical period for wind energy resources, marked by the development of distinct high-wind areas across the AP. This section discusses the intraday variability of wind-resource availability during this peak season. The analysis highlights distinct intraday variability, with wind speeds intensifying during nighttime hours. The nocturnal enhancement is most prominent over north-central KSA, the northern Red Sea, and the Arabian Gulf (AG) (Fig. 3). Peak winds occur between 21 and 03 UTC (panels g, h, and a) across the land regions of west-central AP. This is in sharp contrast to daytime conditions (panels c–f), which exhibit widespread weakening.

Diurnal climatology of 100 m wind speed during summer over the Arabian Peninsula, derived from APR data for the period 1990–2019. The figure was created on Python-V3.11 (https://www.python.org/downloads/release/python-3110/).

The diurnal variability of WS100 is governed by the combined influence of atmospheric boundary-layer processes and terrain influences. During daytime, surface heating promotes a convective boundary layer characterized by strong turbulent mixing and enhanced frictional effects (Deepak83). This redistributes momentum vertically and tends to reduce mean wind speed at turbine hub height (e.g., Stull84, Wharton85). In contrast, nighttime conditions are associated with reduced turbulence and suppressed vertical mixing within a stable boundary layer, allowing stronger winds to persist near hub height. Over the northern AP, these diurnal contrasts can be further modulated by the evolution of the Shamal wind regime and associated low-level jets (e.g., Giannakopoulou et al., 2012; Francis et al., 2017). The intraday changes in the Shamal jet are governed by orographic forcing associated with mountain-plain circulations. During summer nights, rapid radiative cooling over the Zagros slopes relative to the adjacent plains generates northerly to northwesterly drainage winds directed toward the AP (e.g., Giannakopoulou and Toumi86, Mofidi87). Owing to their orientation, the Zagros act as an effective conduit, channeling Shamals and easterlies induced by the semi-permanent anticyclone over the eastern Iran into the AP interior14. In addition to that, strong nocturnal inversions across the AP suppress downward momentum transfer, confining jet maximum above the inversion layer. This reinforces elevated wind speeds aloft86,88. The nocturnal enhancement of the easterly component, in sharp contrast to the typical daytime pattern, further contributes to elevated WS100 magnitudes over western KSA.

A concurrent intensification of WS100 is also observed over the northern RS, synchronous with that over the AP interior. This tendency is associated with an increase in the meridional wind component over Egypt and northern AP (Figure not shown). It is driven by seesaw-like adjustment between subtropical highs and regional thermal lows. The EM high remains strong and extends southwestward into Egypt as the resident low weakens during nocturnal hours (Figure S3). Consequently, northerly flow intensifies over northern Africa, extending into Egypt. The complex terrain around the northern Red Sea outlets enhances wind channeling and thereby increasing WS100 magnitudes in the region. WS100 has also undergone significant diurnal variability over the central Red Sea in association with the evolution of the Tokar Gap jet. This funneling jet, likewise the Shamal jet, intensifies during late-night hours and peaks in the early hours (Fig. 3a-d). The jet begins to strengthen during late-night hours and peaks in the early morning (Fig. 3a−d), Its evolution is governed by the convergence between the southwesterly monsoon flow and northerlies from the eastern Mediterranean high, induced by the diurnal modulation of the monsoon low-level jet89. Furthermore, this gap jet frequently extends its influence across the central RS and can reach as far as the western coast of the KSA82,90.

Climatological features of wind events over the AP

Wind drought events

Wind power generation faces operational challenges during prolonged periods of weak winds, commonly referred to as wind energy droughts. No wind energy is produced during wind drought (WD) conditions, as wind speeds remain below the operational threshold of turbines. Figure 4 presents the seasonal distribution of WD hours across the AP. Critically, this analysis maps the severe WD zones across the AP due to the high spatial resolution of the APR. The overall spatial patterns remain broadly consistent throughout the year. The southwestern AP experiences the highest frequency of WD hours. Within this region, the west coast of the KSA stands out, with monthly totals surpassing 600 h. The majority of these events are present along the low-lying coastal and inland plains on the windward side of the western escarpment. The steep terrain acts as a formidable barrier, intercepting moist westerly and northwesterly winds. As the air mass is forced to ascend, its horizontal momentum is partially converted into vertical motion, triggering adiabatic cooling. This process suppresses buoyancy and attenuates the wind flow. Therefore, the combination of terrain-induced friction, turbulent dissipation, and orographic lifting results in a notable loss of wind speed. Furthermore, additional blocking and wave drag near the slope suppress wind magnitudes (e.g.,84). Further, the frequency of WD events diminishes noticeably toward the north.

Intriguingly, the southern RS is less affected by WD episodes during most of the year. However, it registers their highest frequency during autumn (Fig. 4d). A concurrent increase in WD hours is also evident over the southern AG. We also note an elevated WD occurrences over eastern Oman and southwestern Yemen. The persistent nature of high-frequency WD events along the western AP coast presents a considerable obstacle to the feasibility of offshore wind power in the region. This highlights a critical, inherent limitation for future renewable energy expansion.

Sub-rated wind events

Sub-rated winds (SR) support turbine operation by providing favorable operating speeds that allow turbines to generate stable power. SR hours are relatively uniform across the AP, with KSA emerging as a consistent hotspot (Fig. 5). Closer analysis, however, shows seasonal variations in hotspot intensity, particularly around the marginal seas. In boreal winter, SR events are particularly frequent over the northern AS and the Gulf of Aden (Fig. 5a). This intensification is dynamically linked to northeasterly winds flowing along the southern flank of the high-pressure system over the AP (Figure S2a). As these winds move southward toward the Gulf of Aden, the complex topography constrains the airflow through narrow passages. This terrain-induced channeling enhances wind acceleration, resulting in stronger winds during this period. A sharp decline in SR hours observed in these regions during the subsequent summers (Fig. 5c). This reduction is associated with the development of the monsoonal low-level jet, with wind speeds far exceeding those of the SR. These wind regimes belong to the category discussed in the following section. Notwithstanding, eastern Oman, UAE, and the southwestern coast of the AP record the lowest frequencies of these events.

Rated-wind events

Rated-wind (RAW) events are critical for wind-power generation. They mark the wind speeds at which turbines reach full operation capacity and deliver maximum power outputs. Figure 6 displays the spatial distribution of RAW events across the AP. The summer stands out with the highest frequency of the RAW events (Fig. 6c). Several prominent local hotspots emerge, including the northernmost part of the RS, central RS, and the Gulf of Aden. These regions were previously identified as having exceptionally high WS100. The seasonal mean RAW wind hours exceed 500 over these regions. Consequently, the same physical mechanisms that drive the higher WS100 in these locations are also responsible for generating the RAW hotspots. The southern coastal regions of Oman experience extremely high magnitudes of rated wind episodes. This intensity is particularly linked to the monsoon low-level winds, whose magnitudes are exceptionally high over the northern Arabian Sea (e.g.,57,79).

The AG region records a moderate frequency of RAW wind events throughout the year. However, a noticeable increase occurs during the boreal summer, particularly across its northern sector. This seasonal surge is driven by the evolution of the Shamal jets. These jets remain largely confined to the northeastern AP and the AG region during the core summer months. Inland, the Hijaz mountains record a moderate number of these episodes. Meanwhile, the north-central and southeastern AP exhibit a smaller number of such productive events.

Extreme wind events

Extreme wind (EW) events pose challenges for wind energy generation, often causing structural stress and potential damage to critical turbine components such as blades and gearboxes. To ensure operational safety, turbines automatically shut down when the wind speeds exceed the extreme threshold, resulting in a temporary halt in power generation. EW events occur very rarely across the AP (Fig. 7). As Fig. 7 illustrates, most areas record fewer than 10 h of extreme wind activity per season (the color bar is adjusted to highlight these low frequencies). Only a few localized patches show higher activity, primarily near complex terrain or coastal inlets. The spring season (Fig. 7b) exhibits slightly more prevalence of extreme wind activity compared to the other seasons. This marginal enhancement could be due to the frequent passage of Mediterranean depressions during this period (e.g.,24,91). The summer season (Fig. 7c) shows the lowest occurrence of such events, with almost the entire AP remaining unaffected by extremes. Nonetheless, minor, localized hotspots are occasionally observed near the border areas of Eritrea and Sudan. The sporadic and localized nature of EW events broadly indicates that the AP is largely unaffected by such extremes.

The annual climatology indicates that sub-rated winds dominate across the AP, accounting for more than 60% of all wind events (Figure S4). Interestingly, the west coast of AP is dominated by WD periods. This prevalence of moderate wind speeds reflects the typical background wind conditions over the region and underscores their importance in determining long-term wind energy availability.

High-resolution climatology of capacity factors across the AP

This section presents the climatological high-resolution maps of capacity-factor distribution across the AP for two representative turbine technologies (described in the Sect. 2.4). The spatial and seasonal evolution of CF reveals distinct and coherent patterns (Fig. 8). Both technologies yield comparable spatio-temporal patterns across the AP, though notable differences arise in specific regions. Between the two, the Vestas turbine technology showcases slightly higher CF magnitudes than the Nordex. This reflects its larger swept area and lower specific power, despite comparable cut-in wind speeds. On an intra-annual scale, variations in regional wind regimes shape the seasonal cycle of CF across the AP. CF attains its peak during summer (Fig. 8c and g) and declines to its lowest in autumn (Fig. 8d and h). Exceptionally high values appear over the Gulf of Aqaba and the Suez canal, while central Red Sea exhibits moderately high values during summer. Meanwhile, central KSA displays a relatively moderate CF. Conversely, the southwestern coast of the AP records low CF values. This could be largely due to the occurrence of frequent WD episodes that limit energy potential (Fig. 4). Likewise, the southern Arabian Gulf also records persistently weak CFs throughout the year. This underscores the dominance of the weaker and less sustained wind regimes in this sub-region. The spatial patterns here are broadly consistent with earlier high-resolution assessments over parts of the AP. In particular, Giani et al. (2020) similarly reported elevated CFs over regions such as the Gulf of Aqaba and the Suez Canal.

We further quantified the annual electricity generated by wind across each country in the AP (Figure S5). A turbine spacing criterion of 10 rotor diameters (10D), consistent with earlier literature (e.g.,8,69,92) was applied. Under this assumption, each model grid cell can accommodate approximately 11 turbines (Vestas turbine specifications). Under this configuration, the annual electricity production in KSA is estimated to be approximately 11,194 terawatt-hour (TWh). For the context, KSA’s annual electricity demand is approximately 400 TWh (https://www.eia.gov/international/analysis/country/SAU). The estimated wind electricity potential is therefore substantially larger. This estimate represents the theoretical upper-bound wind generated electricity potential under uniform spatial coverage. Distinct annual cycles of wind-generated electricity are observed among the AP countries. To characterize intra-annual variability, we computed the coefficient of variation (CV) from monthly production values. The results reveal a subdued annual cycle in KSA, with a CV of 10%, indicating modest seasonal fluctuations and a reliable supply of electricity throughout the year. In contrast, countries in the Arabian Gulf such as Qatar and Kuwait exhibit pronounced annual cycles, with CV values of 26.58% and 19.82% respectively. These regions record peak electricity yields in early spring, driven by favorable wind patterns, followed by steady decline through the summer months as conditions weaken.

Long-term trends in the WS100 and wind events

Trends in the WS100 and CF

Figure 9 presents the long-term seasonal trends in WS100, derived from four decades of APR. The trends exhibit strong seasonal dependence across the AP. During winter (Fig. 9a), a moderate yet statistically significant strengthening appears over the eastern AP, while spring (Fig. 9b) shows little to no change across most areas. In summer (Fig. 9c), the spatial distribution shifts markedly, showing a distinct decline over the northeastern AP and the northern Red Sea. In contrast, increasing trends emerge across the central to southern Red Sea and parts of southern KSA. Positive trends also extend into the Tokar Gap, the Gulf of Aden, and adjacent oceanic zones to the east. During autumn (Fig. 9d), the positive trends remain evident over the central to southern Red Sea and southern KSA. Additionally, strengthening of WS100 appears over the Arabian Gulf during this season. Supporting analysis using ERA5 100 m wind data (Figure S5) confirms these findings, with both datasets reproducing the pronounced summer decline in WS100. Moreover, the APR-derived trends demonstrate strong correspondence with the ERA5 results across all seasons. This inherent consistency reinforces the robustness of the APR product in accurately representing long-term wind-resource variability over the AP.

We also computed long-term seasonal trends in CF over the AP, considering both turbine technologies (Figure S7 & S8). The trends remain consistent across the two technologies. Critically, the CF tendencies closely mirror the WS100 distributions, highlighting their strong co-variability.

Trends in the wind events

We now investigate the long-term evolution of various wind event occurrences across the study period. The WD frequency trends show strong seasonal dependence (Fig. 10). During winter (Fig. 10a), a notable increase in WD frequency is observed across the south-western AP, extending into the southern RS region. By contrast, nearly two-thirds of the AP mainland show no discernible changes in WD episodes. No significant variations in WD frequency are observed across the region noticed during Spring (Fig. 10b). The summer season (Fig. 10c) presents a contrasting spatial pattern; an increase in WD occurrences is observed over the northern RS and eastern AP, while a concurrent decline is noted over the central RS and southern KSA. This anti-correlated spatial behavior aligns precisely with the trends in WS100 magnitudes (Fig. 9c). A pronounced decrease in WD episodes is evident along the west coast of the AP during the autumn (Fig. 10d). This indicates a localized recovery in wind activity during the post-summer transition. The UAE apparently exhibits a consistent decline in WD occurrences across most seasons.

Sub-rated wind events show an overall weak declining trend across the AP, with localized increases in a few areas (Fig. 11). Factors driving the observed trends in WS100 over the AP

This section investigates the physical mechanisms that underpin the temporal evolution of seasonal mean WS100 across the AP. As noted earlier, the development of pressure systems and the associated pressure gradients are the primary drivers of the changes in wind patterns (e.g.,93). To begin, long-term trends in mean sea level pressure (MSLP) are analyzed across the broader geographical domain encompassing both the EM and AP regions (Fig. 13). A widespread weakening of MSLP is evident across the geographical domain. During summer, this weakening becomes particularly pronounced, with reductions of up to −0.5 hPa per decade across the EM and eastern AP, extending into Egypt, Sudan, and the Red Sea region (Fig. 13c). This suggests a significant decline in the semi-permanent EM high. Concurrently, surface pressure over the AP displays a moderate decline. The persistent weakening of the EM high reduces the east-west pressure gradient, consistent with the findings of Gandham et al.14. This diminished gradient weakens northerly wind regimes, leading directly to lower WS100 magnitudes. The resulting decline explains the increase in WD episodes and the reduction in RAW events over the northern Red Sea. By contrast, nearly two-thirds of the Red Sea show enhanced WS100 magnitudes. This is associated with stronger westerlies, including the Tokar Gap jet, and could be driven by an intensified Indian summer monsoon in recent decades (e.g., Bryan94, Jin95).

During autumn, MSLP exhibits widespread declines, with the most pronounced reduction centered over the southern AP (Fig. 13d). Under these conditions, the north-south pressure gradient strengthens between the moderate low over the southern AP and the adjacent high to the north (figure not shown). This enhanced gradient likely intensified the easterly inflow into the southern AP, resulting in a moderate increase in WS100 magnitudes.

Summary and conclusion

Wind energy, a renewable and low-carbon alternative to fossil fuels, is pivotal in enhancing energy security and mitigating climate change. This study provides a comprehensive assessment of high-resolution wind resources and their long-term variability using a 40-year (1980–2019) AP regional reanalysis (APR) at 5 km resolution. This study estimates the annual production of wind-generated electricity across the AP. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia generates around 11,194 TWh owing to its large geographical domain and persistent wind regimes. Note that these values are sensitive to the turbine deployment assumptions and physical spacing imposed to limit wake interactions. These results offer a coherent regional perspective on the spatial distribution of wind energy resources across the Arabian Peninsula and provide valuable information for sustainable wind farm planning. Such insights are particularly relevant for supporting national clean-energy initiatives, including the Kingdom’s Vision 2030 goals, and for informing long-term renewable energy strategies aimed at enhancing regional energy security.
