Circadian and photoperiodic control of melatonin in Drosophila ananassae
Authors: Hannah K Dollish, Justin M Snider, Hannah Doskicz, Michael A Grandner, Fabian-Xosé Fernandez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelatonin is a widely conserved indoleamine with well-established roles in circadian timekeeping, sleep regulation, and physiological coordination across taxa. In Drosophila melanogaster, early studies reported daily melatonin rhythms, but little is known about melatonin dynamics in other Drosophila species. Here, we measured melatonin secretion in >14,000 Drosophila ananassae across 12 circadian timepoints under four simulated photoperiods: LD 8:16 (short day), LD 12:12 (equatorial), LD 16:8 (long day), and LD 20:4 (arctic summer). Using LC-MS/MS detection in pooled whole-fly samples, we identified robust, bimodal melatonin rhythms across all conditions, with conserved peaks at approximately ZT7 and ZT13 irrespective of daylength. Melatonin levels were significantly elevated under the short-day photoperiod compared to all others (p < 0.0005), indicating photoperiod-sensitive amplitude modulation. These findings confirm the presence of circadian melatonin rhythms in Drosophila, replicate a previously reported early-day peak observed in D. melanogaster, and reveal a second rhythm component not described in earlier studies. The data suggest that melatonin secretion in Drosophila is governed by an endogenous clock and phase-locked to lights-on, even in the absence of nighttime darkness. This work refines our understanding of melatonin rhythmicity in insects and underscores its potential role as a conserved circadian output across species.

Introduction

Melatonin (5-methoxy-N-acetyltryptamine) is a highly conserved indoleamine molecule found across animals, plants, fungi, and some bacteria [1]. First isolated from the bovine pineal gland [2], melatonin is best known for its role in regulating circadian rhythms and sleep in vertebrates [3]. In mammals, melatonin is secreted rhythmically by the pineal gland, with high levels occurring at night and low levels during the day [4]. This nocturnal signal provides internal temporal information to the body's tissues, synchronizing physiology with the external light-dark cycle [5]. In addition to its chronobiological role, melatonin modulates immune function [6], neuroendocrine signaling [7], seasonal reproductive behavior [8], and cellular redox status [9]. Across these roles, it acts both through high-affinity G-protein-coupled receptors (e.g., MT1 and MT2) [10,11] and receptor-independent antioxidant mechanisms [12].

Although melatonin's function has been most extensively characterized in vertebrates, there is growing recognition that melatonin is also present and functionally relevant in invertebrate species, including insects. In Drosophila melanogaster, early work by Finocchiaro et al. (1988) [13] and Hintermann et al. (1996) [14] confirmed the presence of endogenous melatonin and its biosynthetic machinery. The fly genome encodes arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (aaNAT1) [15], a homolog of the vertebrate enzyme responsible for converting serotonin to N-acetylserotonin, a key step in melatonin synthesis. This enzymatic conversion enables Drosophila to generate melatonin from dietary or endogenous tryptophan via the canonical serotonin–melatonin pathway [16,17]. In situ hybridization studies have shown that Drosophila aaNAT1 is broadly expressed across tissues, including the brain, ventral nerve cord, gut, and possibly oenocytes, suggesting that melatonin may be synthesized in both central and peripheral compartments [14].

Hintermann et al. (1996) further reported a circadian rhythm in whole-body melatonin levels in D. melanogaster, peaking during the subjective day. This was a surprising finding given the widely held assumption that melatonin is a hormone associated with darkness. Notably, their study did not find strong daily oscillations in aaNAT1 mRNA, suggesting that melatonin rhythms in Drosophila may arise through post-transcriptional regulation or through rhythms in downstream enzymes, such as hydroxyindole-O-methyltransferase (HIOMT), or from circadian modulation of substrate availability. Despite its low abundance and uncertain receptor targets in insects [18], melatonin in Drosophila has been shown to modulate sleep and circadian behaviors under certain conditions. For example, exogenous melatonin administration can shorten sleep latency [19] and restore behavioral rhythmicity in disease-model flies with disrupted circadian regulation [20,21], including those expressing Huntington's disease-associated genes [22]. These findings suggest that melatonin acts as a modulator of circadian output rather than as a core pacemaker component in the fly [23].

The question remains whether melatonin rhythms are robust, conserved features of Drosophila biology or whether their expression is conditional, strain-specific, or environmentally modulated. Importantly, nearly all previous studies have focused on D. melanogaster, leaving open whether melatonin rhythms are detectable or functionally significant in other Drosophila species. D. ananassae, a tropical species within the melanogaster subgroup, represents an ecologically and genetically distinct lineage that has evolved under relatively constant daylength and temperature conditions [24]. Studying melatonin rhythms in D. ananassae provides a valuable opportunity to assess how melatonin secretion patterns vary across Drosophila species and whether they are influenced by photoperiod, circadian phase, or evolutionary adaptation.

In the present study, we quantified whole-body melatonin secretion across 12 circadian timepoints in D. ananassae under four simulated seasonal photoperiods—short-day (LD 8:16), equatorial (LD 12:12), long-day (LD 16:8), and arctic summer (LD 20:4)—to determine whether melatonin output exhibits time-of-day- and season-dependent variation. By combining precise temporal sampling with high-specificity LC-MS/MS detection, we sought to evaluate the robustness of melatonin rhythmicity in this species, assess its phase relationship to light onset, and compare our findings to prior observations in D. melanogaster. In doing so, we address both the replicability and evolutionary generalizability of melatonin's circadian rhythm in Drosophila and provide new insights into how this ancient molecule may contribute to internal timekeeping across taxa.

Materials and methods

Fly stocks and rearing conditions

Drosophila ananassae were derived from an isofemale line maintained at the Drosophila Species Stock Center (DSSC) at Cornell University's College of Agriculture and Life Sciences (Stock #14024–0371.16). Flies were reared at 25 °C in DigiTherm® incubators (Tritech Research, Inc., Los Angeles, CA) under a 12:12 light-dark (LD) cycle. Illumination was provided by a broad-spectrum 4-W cold-cathode fluorescent light tube (model DT2-LB-F12IN/CIRC-L-INV), freely mounted with no fixture, yielding an illuminance of 887.7 lux at the level of the fly racks. Lights-on was set at 07:00 (Mountain Standard Time, MST). To maintain a steady supply of animals, stocks were transferred to fresh vials daily on a schedule that ranged between 11:00 and 16:00 during the light phase. Flies were cultured on a corn flour–nutritional yeast–agar medium (0.8 % agar, 3.5 % sucrose, 1.7 % glucose, 6 % fine-grained masa, and 1 % yeast), which was also used as the food source during all experimental phases [[25], [26], [27]].

Experimental cohorts and housing

Virgin male and female flies (n = 300; sex-balanced across timepoints, n = 150 females and n = 150 males per timepoint) were selected as late-stage, pharate-adult pupae, transferred to fresh food vials, and housed in same-sex groups of 25–50 individuals per vial. Upon eclosion, flies were maintained for 7 days under one of four seasonally relevant photoperiods in climate-controlled vivaria. Once placed into experimental photoperiod incubators, flies remained undisturbed for the full 7-day entrainment period, with no transfers or handling prior to sampling. On day 7, flies were harvested at 2-h intervals across the circadian cycle (ZT0–ZT24, 12 timepoints total), either during the light or dark phase depending on the programmed photoperiod. Dark phase collections were done with researchers operating under night vision headgear. At each timepoint, flies were rapidly collected into foil-wrapped tubes (n = 300), immediately flash-frozen at −80 °C, and subsequently transferred on crushed ice to the University of Arizona North Campus CORE facility at the BIO5 Institute.

Harvesting schedule and photoperiod design

To standardize environmental exposures while enabling continuous circadian sampling, each vivarium was independently programmed with time-staggered photoperiods, allowing for bi-hourly fly harvests to occur at three fixed local times daily (10:00, 12:00, or 14:00). At each collection point, animals were briefly co-housed (1–2 min) in light-protected tubes, combining males and females just prior to freezing. All collections were performed by the same experienced investigator (HKD) and involved a simple procedure: flipping a tube containing food into an empty tube, which was then placed immediately into a −80 °C freezer. Each transfer took less than 1 min per tube, ensuring that all samples collected in a given session were frozen within 4–5 min of harvest.

All light-dark transitions were verified using light sensors prior to experimentation, and vivaria remained isolated to prevent photic contamination across conditions.

Mass spectrometry

Frozen flies were added to flatbottom bead-beater tubes containing 200 μL of pure ethanol, 20–30 zirconia beads, and 25 μL of D4 melatonin (132 nmol/ml), an internal standard used widely for quantification of melatonin via mass spectrometry [28]. One ml of ethyl acetate was added to each tube. Samples were vortexed to ensure mixing, and a fast-prep bead-beater was used at 6.5 strokes/sec for 40 s to homogenize the flies within solution. Tubes were then centrifuged at 4 °C at 14,000 RPM for 10 min with high brake. Supernatant was transferred from sample tubes to 10-mm x 75-mm glass tubes. Extracts were then dried in a nitrogen dryer and resuspended in 100 μL of pure ethanol.

After vortexing and centrifugation at 3000 RPM for 15 min, the extracts were transferred to 2-ml acls vials with inserts and refrigerated. For melatonin quantification, 10 μL of sample was injected for LC/MS analysis on the Shimadzu Nexera XR UHPLC tandem Sciex 5500 Qtrap MS under conditions similar to those described by Zhao and colleagues [29], unless otherwise indicated. Chromatographic separation was achieved utilizing a Peeke Spectra C8 (3 μM, 150 × 3 mm) column with Methanol and Water mobile phases each containing 0.1 % formic acid. MS parameters and gradients were also taken from Zhao and colleagues [29]. A two-ANOVA with a post-hoc Tukey's HSD was applied to the data with significance set to a p-value of 0.05.

Results

Approximately 14,000 virgin male and female Drosophila ananassae were sampled to generate melatonin secretion profiles across 12 circadian timepoints under four simulated photoperiods: LD 8:16 (winter), LD 12:12 (equatorial), LD 16:8 (summer), and LD 20:4 (arctic summer). Data were collected over a single 24-h cycle and double-plotted across 48 h to aid in rhythm visualization (Fig. 1; values at each timepoint are available in supplementary materials). A two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of photoperiod (F3,33 = 15.27, p < 0.0001) and time-of-day (F11,33 = 2.13, p = 0.0459), accounting for 44.8 % and 22.9 % of the total variance, respectively. Post-hoc Tukey HSD tests revealed that melatonin secretion was significantly elevated under the winter photoperiod (LD 8:16) compared to all other conditions (vs. LD 12:12, p < 0.0001; vs. LD 16:8, p < 0.0001; vs. LD 20:4, p = 0.0005), while secretion levels did not differ significantly among the LD 12:12, LD 16:8, and LD 20:4 groups. Rhythmicity across all photoperiods was robust, with clear bimodal patterns evident in each condition. A conserved upswing at or near ZT7 appeared across all photoperiods, suggesting a circadian-controlled morning peak that is independent of daylength. A second upswing, occurring reliably at ZT13, was also observed in each photoperiod, including the winter-night LD 8:16 condition. Notably, the amplitude of the second peak at ZT13 relative to the first peak at ZT7 increased sequentially moving from shorter to longer days (Fig. 1). Lastly, melatonin secretion across all photoperiods appeared to be phase-locked to lights-on (ZT0), with both the ZT7 and ZT13 peaks aligning to this dawn anchor regardless of daylength (Fig. 2). This alignment suggests that while amplitude relationships vary with photoperiod, the underlying circadian control of peak timing remains stable across light–dark cycles, reinforcing the role of lights-on as a dominant phase reference in D. ananassae. This pattern complements previous findings from our lab showing that the seasonal phase response curve (PRC) to light in D. ananassae is anchored to lights-off (ZT12) [30]. Further studies will be necessary to determine the stability of this phase-locking effect under other possible environmental conditions, including those that systemically vary temperature [31].

Discussion

Across ecologically relevant photoperiods, melatonin in Drosophila ananassae shows a robust bimodal rhythm with phase-stable peaks near ZT7 and ZT13 that remain anchored to lights-on. This phase stability supports endogenous circadian control potentially involving the Drosophila morning oscillator [32]. As a separate observation, under summer-like long days (LD 16:8 and LD 20:4) both peaks occur entirely within the photophase, indicating further that light does not strongly suppress melatonin in this species, in contrast to mammals [33]. Considered together with previous findings, these data suggest a dual anchoring of circadian outputs in D. ananassae—one tied to dawn for melatonin secretion, and another tied to dusk for photic phase resetting—a relationship that mirrors key features of circadian organization in mammals, including humans.

These findings support and extend several key features of the foundational report by Hintermann et al. (1996), which characterized a circadian rhythm of melatonin in Drosophila melanogaster (Oregon-R strain) under 12:12 light-dark (LD) conditions. Most notably, both studies converge on the presence of a robust melatonin peak occurring approximately 6–7 h after lights-on (ZT6–ZT7). In Hintermann's data, melatonin levels rise during the early day and peak sharply at ZT6 before declining. Our results in D. ananassae reveal a similar upswing around ZT7 that is preserved across all four photoperiods examined, including winter (LD 8:16), equatorial (LD 12:12), summer (LD 16:8), and arctic summer (LD 20:4). This conserved phase relationship suggests that early-day melatonin secretion may be a circadian output that is shared across Drosophila species, rather than a species- or assay-specific artifact.

However, the current findings also reveal features that were not observed in the original D. melanogaster study. Specifically, our data show a second upswing in melatonin secretion occurring reliably at ZT13, approximately 13 h after lights-on. This second peak was not detected in Hintermann's work, which reported a unimodal profile with a single daytime maximum. The presence of this additional upswing in D. ananassae introduces the possibility that melatonin secretion in Drosophila may be more complex and potentially bimodal, with both peaks phase-locked to lights-on and modulated by photoperiod. That both ZT7 and ZT13 peaks persisted across long- and short-day conditions argues for their endogenous, clock-based regulation rather than a passive response to environmental light cues.

Several methodological and biological factors could account for the differences between studies. One possibility is species-specific divergence. D. ananassae and D. melanogaster are genetically and ecologically distinct: the former is a tropical species that evolved under relatively invariant photoperiods, while the latter is temperate and capable of seasonal adaptation [34]. Melatonin rhythms may therefore be tuned differently in these lineages. Alternatively, the absence of a second peak in D. melanogaster may reflect a narrower range of sampling or the absence of environmental variation in that earlier experiment.

Another important consideration is detection methodology. Hintermann et al. used biochemical assays available at the time (i.e., radioimmunoassay), which are techniques sensitive to low concentrations but potentially vulnerable to cross-reactivity with structurally similar indoles such as N-acetylserotonin. In contrast, our study employed LC-MS/MS, which provides greater molecular specificity. It is possible that the narrower peak observed by Hintermann was enhanced by assay cross-reactivity, while our detection method isolates true melatonin, revealing a broader or bimodal profile. Conversely, it is also possible that a second peak was present in the original study but undetected due to methodological limitations or insufficient temporal resolution.

Our experimental design also introduces several methodological improvements that may have clarified rhythmicity. We collected data across 12 circadian timepoints with precisely staggered photoperiods, allowing us to dissociate internal circadian time from local clock time. The resulting profiles were double-plotted to enhance visualization of temporal dynamics. All samples were handled using standardized freezing and extraction procedures known to minimize degradation — a particular concern for low-abundance molecules like melatonin. Furthermore, we sampled flies maintained in four different photoperiod regimes, revealing that while overall melatonin amplitude varied with season, the timing of peak secretion remained remarkably stable. This supports the hypothesis that melatonin output in Drosophila is governed primarily by an internal oscillator phase-locked to dawn, rather than by night length or darkness per se.

While D. ananassae lacks the well-characterized pineal organ found in vertebrates, melatonin synthesis in insects occurs across multiple tissues [35], including the brain, gut, and peripheral structures. Previous work has shown that the AANAT1 enzyme is expressed in these tissues in D. melanogaster and likely plays a similar role in D. ananassae. It is possible that differences in the relative contributions or phase relationships of these tissues account for the emergence of a second peak in one species but not the other. If, for example, gut-derived melatonin peaks early in the day while brain-derived melatonin peaks later, their sum in whole-fly extracts could produce a bimodal waveform. Dissecting tissue-specific melatonin rhythms will be crucial for resolving this question.

Although the melatonin secretion profile of D. ananassae resembles that reported for D. melanogaster, their temporal activity patterns diverge markedly. D. ananassae—a synanthropic species adapted to human-associated habitats—shows a distinctly diurnal, largely unimodal daytime activity pattern with consolidated nighttime sleep; this daytime bias persists in constant darkness, at elevated ambient temperatures (∼30 °C), and across illumination regimes that typically suppress daytime activity in other drosophilids [[36], [37], [38]]. By contrast, D. melanogaster exhibits a canonical crepuscular profile, with pronounced dawn and dusk peaks and comparatively greater nocturnal arousal [[36], [37], [38]].

Against this behavioral backdrop, the shared timing of melatonin secretion between the two suggests that circadian control is conserved, while its expression as overt activity is shaped by species-specific coupling and by masking [39]. Here, “positive photic and thermal masking” denotes the immediate, clock-independent promotion of activity by bright light or warmth, whereas darkness or cooler temperatures exert negative masking by acutely suppressing activity [40,41]. Because D. ananassae inhabits human-associated environments with bright, stable daytime illumination and relatively consistent warmth, these conditions are expected to amplify positive masking during the day and reinforce negative masking at night. Differences between D. ananassae and D. melanogaster in the magnitude or gating of these masking responses—therefore—offer a parsimonious explanation for how a broadly conserved melatonin phase can be read out as a diurnal activity architecture in the former versus a crepuscular profile in the latter.

One limitation of the current study involves maintaining flies under the experimental photoperiods for only seven days. While this amount of time may be insufficient for some seasonal endpoints, research in Drosophila suggests that endocrine reprogramming for seasonal phenotypes—most notably reproductive diapause—begins quickly, even if the fully stabilized state emerges more gradually. In D. melanogaster, low temperature (with photoperiod as a secondary cue [42]) triggers coordinated shifts across hormone axes within a week. During this early window, insulin/IGF signaling declines and lipid reserves accumulate; in parallel, juvenile hormone output from the corpora allata drops and ecdysteroid titers fall, accompanying an arrest of vitellogenesis [43,44].

Consistent with this rapid timescale, classic work by Pittendrigh and more recent studies by Vaze and Helfrich-Förster in D. melanogaster show that after only 5–6 days in a given light–dark cycle, flies released into constant darkness retain photoperiod-dependent phase relationships of eclosion and activity peaks [45,46]. These are circadian aftereffects indicating that daylength information was encoded within the past week (an even quicker timetable is observed with D. ananassae [30]). Although this behavioral evidence is indirect with respect to endocrine remodeling, it provides convergent support that seasonal entrainment/re-entrainment is achieved on a weekly timescale that aligns with early hormone-axis reorientation. Achieving peak diapause incidence and other stable seasonal traits typically requires 10–14 days of continued short-day, cool-temperature exposure in D. melanogaster [47]. By contrast, strongly photoperiodic species such as D. montana and D. littoralis can show clear hormonal reorientation and reproductive arrest in under a week [48,49]. In short, although complete seasonal remodeling takes longer to consolidate, the core hormone axes generally pivot on the timescale of about a week, suggesting that residual entrainment to the stock photoperiod was minimized in the present study and did not unduly affect melatonin levels.

In closing, our results confirm a key feature of Hintermann et al.‘s work—the presence of a daytime melatonin peak anchored to lights-on—while extending it by revealing a previously undescribed second peak and demonstrating that both features persist under varied photoperiods. This suggests that melatonin in Drosophila is an endogenous, phase-locked output of the circadian system, potentially encoding information about internal time even in the absence of darkness. Future studies comparing multiple Drosophila species, sampling specific tissues, and utilizing genetically modified lines will help determine whether this bimodal pattern is widespread, and whether melatonin in insects serves sleep/circadian roles analogous to those in vertebrates, or has evolved to fulfill species-specific physiological functions.
