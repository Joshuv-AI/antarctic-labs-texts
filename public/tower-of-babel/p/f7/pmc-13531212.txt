Chronic Total Occlusion After Kawasaki Disease
Authors: Juliana Gomez-Arostegui, Jane C Burns, Jiken Bhatt, Kirsten B Dummer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKawasaki disease (KD) is the most common acquired heart disease in children. Coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) occur in 25% of untreated and 5% of treated cases. Large CAAs increase the risk for thrombosis, myocardial ischemia, and chronic total occlusion (CTO). Patients with collateral formation may remain asymptomatic.MethodsAfter retrospective chart review, we describe 8 patients diagnosed with CTO between ages 2 to 50 years following childhood KD.ResultsAll patients developed giant CAAs after KD. Four pediatric patients presented with CTO between ages 2 to 16 years. Four adults had childhood KD that was untreated or managed before standardized treatment and were lost to follow-up until CTO diagnosis between ages 31 to 50 years. Three patients had surgical bypass.ConclusionsPatients with KD and large CAAs remain at lifelong risk for thrombosis and CTO. Collateralization from slow, chronic ischemia may allow for conservative management in asymptomatic patients.Key words: acute coronary syndrome, coronary artery bypass, coronary calcium score, perfusion

Kawasaki disease (KD), a coronary artery vasculitis of unknown etiology, is the most common acquired heart disease in children in the United States. Timely diagnosis and treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) within 10 days of fever onset significantly reduces the incidence of coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) from approximately 25% to 5%.1,2 Approximately 1% of patients develop giant CAA (≥8 mm or Z-score >+10).1

Complications related to giant aneurysms include myocardial ischemia, infarction, and death, primarily due to thrombus formation within the aneurysm.3 Although the risk of myocardial infarction decreases after 2 years, lifelong risk remains because of abnormal coronary artery structure and function.1 During the vascular remodeling phase, slowly progressive mural thrombus develops, which can lead to stenosis or chronic total occlusion (CTO). Concurrent low-grade ischemia stimulates coronary collateral development, often preserving normal myocardial perfusion.4,5 Many patients with CTO remain asymptomatic with normal ischemia testing given rich collateral networks.

In the absence of evidence-based guidelines for managing CTO in KD patients, treatment strategies have mirrored those for adults with coronary artery disease, despite differing underlying pathophysiology.6 As increasing numbers of KD patients survive into adulthood, there is a growing need to better understand the pathophysiology and natural history of the CAA to inform optimal management. Asymptomatic KD patients can be conservatively managed with medical therapy and ischemia testing at regular intervals. When symptoms or signs of ischemia occur, the risks and benefits of intervention must be carefully weighed in this high-risk population. We present a case series of 8 KD patients diagnosed with CTO.

Methods

This was a retrospective chart review of a case cohort. All subjects or their parents signed consents for participation in studies approved by the University of California–San Diego IRB (#140220 and #182122).

Case Reports

Case 1

This male infant with late KD diagnosis developed bilateral giant coronary aneurysms and thrombus in the right coronary artery (RCA) despite IVIG and aspirin therapy (Table 1). He was transferred to our institution for intensification of anti-inflammatory and anticoagulant treatment (bivalirudin, heparin, aspirin, and clopidogrel). Cardiac computed tomography angiography (CCTA) showed giant aneurysms of the RCA, left anterior descending artery (LAD), and moderate aneurysms in the circumflex with indistinct flow distally in the RCA and LAD suggesting thrombus. A repeat CCTA 2 years later revealed CTO of both the RCA and LAD with extensive collaterals from a remodeled circumflex coronary. Echocardiograms showed normal biventricular systolic function, and electrocardiograms were normal. The patient remained asymptomatic with normal exercise tolerance. The family moved out of the country and reported that he is doing well at 10 years of age, is asymptomatic during exertion, and is cared for by a multispecialty approach including conventional (aspirin) and functional medicine.

Case 2

This female infant was diagnosed late with KD, and an initial echocardiogram that showed bilateral giant aneurysms. She was treated with therapeutic low–molecular weight heparin and aspirin. Computed tomography (CT) angiography showed “no filling defects.” In early outpatient follow-up, there was echogenicity noted in the mid-RCA on echocardiogram and normal biventricular systolic function. CCTA confirmed nonocclusive thrombus in the RCA. Clopidogrel was added to her regimen. Later cardiac CCTAs showed CTO of the RCA, calcification, and the distal artery filling via collaterals. Routine biannual exercise stress echocardiogram (ESE) testing started at 8 years and has shown normal biventricular systolic function without regional wall motion abnormalities. She remains asymptomatic at 10 years of age on apixaban, aspirin, atorvastatin, and metoprolol.

Case 3

This male infant was diagnosed with KD at an outside hospital with bilateral giant aneurysms noted at the time of diagnosis. He was treated with warfarin and aspirin and had a normal ESE at 8 years. He remained asymptomatic. His first CCTA at 14 years showed CTO of the LAD and dense calcification of the remodeled aneurysms in the LAD and distal RCA (Video 1). A subsequent ESE 1 year later showed ST-segment changes consistent with ischemia (Figure 1) and wall motion abnormalities (Figure 2). He underwent coronary angiography and subsequent bypass surgery complicated postoperatively by Dressler's syndrome. His treatment plan includes apixaban and aspirin.

Case 4

This subject was diagnosed with KD at age 6 when an echocardiogram showed bilateral giant aneurysms and a small nonocclusive RCA thrombus. He was treated with warfarin and aspirin initially and then transitioned to apixaban and aspirin. Coronary angiography 1 year later showed patent but “sluggish flow” through the aneurysms. He reported intermittent chest pain with exertion. Exercise stress test at 11 years was normal, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) showed normal biventricular function without delayed enhancement. ESE at 12 and 14 years were normal. CCTA was first performed at age 17 years and showed CTO of the proximal RCA with reconstitution of flow from collaterals off marginal branches and a persistent giant LAD aneurysm (Figure 3). CMR at age 18 years showed normal biventricular systolic function and no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). He had issues with substance use and medical noncompliance. At age 20, he had an ESE with regional wall motion abnormalities and follow-up stress perfusion MRI showing a 6-mm region of LGE in the RCA distribution with fixed hypokinesis. The CCTA was unchanged. He is followed conservatively on apixaban, aspirin, atorvastatin, and metoprolol.

Case 5

This subject was diagnosed with KD at 7 weeks and treated with dipyridamole and aspirin before the advent of IVIG. He developed bilateral coronary artery, axillary, and iliac artery aneurysms. He experienced 1 episode of chest pain at age 6. Antiplatelet therapy was discontinued by 10 years when the echocardiogram was interpreted as normal. At age 31, he had a CT calcium score of 513 Agatston units, and CCTA revealed CTO of the LAD. His main medical issues were related to lower extremity claudication and need for vascular surgery secondary to stenosis of the iliofemoral arteries.7

Case 6

This subject self-referred as an adult with a history of KD and CAA in childhood with no medical follow-up since adolescence. A CT calcium score was 242 Agatston units, and CCTA showed CTO of the LAD with rich collaterals right to left. A coronary angiogram was performed and confirmed the CCTA findings. He subsequently had a normal CMR without LGE and normal ESE. He is asymptomatic, an avid runner, and regularly lifts weights. He is medically managed on aspirin and atorvastatin without intervention to date.

Case 7

This subject had KD at age 8 and was treated with aspirin alone before IVIG. He was followed up until 18 years of age and discharged from care. At age 50 years, a CT calcium score was 2,204 Agatston units. Subsequent CT angiography showed CTO of the LAD. The ESE was normal. He underwent left heart catheterization showing CTO of the LAD with extensive collateralization from the right and large aneurysm in the proximal RCA with faint opacification and swirling suggestive of subtotal occlusion from mural thrombus. Myocardial perfusion positron emission tomography/CT showed severe ischemia in the LAD territory but good myocardial reserve and normal function. Cardiac medications included rosuvastatin, rivaroxaban, aspirin, and metoprolol. Although he was asymptomatic, his local team recommended surgical bypass of the midsegment of the LAD and the distal RCA. Two-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was performed with in situ left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to the LAD and in situ right internal mammary artery to the RCA; the patient was doing well 6 months postsurgery and reported his functional capacity was back to his presurgical state.

Case 8

This subject had a KD-compatible illness in childhood in a Thai refugee camp. He presented in adulthood with a ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and was treated with unsuccessful percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty to the occluded proximal RCA aneurysm. He continued to have chest pain, and CCTA showed CTO of the RCA and reconstitution of diminutive posterior descending branch. ESE was normal with no evidence of ischemia. However, because of persistent symptoms at age 52, a triple CABG was performed at an outside hospital (LIMA-LAD, saphenous vein graft–obtuse marginal, saphenous vein graft–RCA). One week after bypass, an echocardiogram showed left ventricular ejection fraction of 60% to 65%, mildly decreased right ventricular systolic function, and mild mitral insufficiency. His medications include rivaroxaban, aspirin, rosuvastatin, ezetimibe, and metoprolol. He has had several emergency department visits after CABG for ongoing persistent chest pain.

Discussion

Management of CTO should be dictated by clinical presentation, with acute ischemic symptoms requiring prompt intervention regardless of underlying pathophysiology. While evidence-based guidelines exist for adult acute coronary syndromes caused by atherosclerosis, comparable guidance for KD patients is limited.6 Although both conditions involve inflammatory processes, their mechanisms differ substantially. In atherosclerotic CAD, chronic inflammation leads to progressive plaque accumulation within the arterial wall, resulting in luminal narrowing and restricted blood flow. Abrupt plaque rupture may occur, causing sudden and complete coronary occlusion. With KD however, acute inflammation of the coronary arteries in childhood leads to endothelial dysfunction, creating the risk for CAA. Aneurysms that are larger in size are associated with stasis of flow with eventual layering of thrombus against the wall leading to varying degrees of obstruction over time. Robust collaterals can form as the vessel slowly becomes occluded and results in chronic low-grade ischemic demand.

Interestingly, thrombus formation and total occlusion appear to differ between the right and left coronary arteries.8 A predominance of RCA CTO over LAD CTO was noted in our literature review. Anatomical and hemodynamic factors, including lower wall shear stress, degree of CAA surface area exposed to low wall shear stress, and longer residence times increase risk of subsequent thrombosis.9 Despite this predisposition, RCA CTO is associated with a more favorable clinical prognosis because of greater contralateral collateral development and involvement of a smaller myocardial territory, allowing for better preservation of systolic function.8 As a result, many patients remain asymptomatic into adulthood after their initial illness and may have delayed diagnosis of CTO.

Review of case reports, such as that by Tsuda,10 indicates that intervention for CTO after KD is rare, but has been reported. However, these procedures carry significant risks and have variable success rates, depending on operator experience, lesion complexity, and timing. For asymptomatic individuals, medical management is preferred over intervention.

In our series, 3 of the 8 patients underwent CABG. The decision to pursue surgical intervention for all 3 was made outside our institution. At the time of this report, clinical follow-up of the 3 patients was limited; all were alive and on medications, with 1 experiencing ongoing symptoms of chest pain.

Conclusions

Patients with KD and giant CAA are at lifelong risk for myocardial ischemia.1 Gradual mural thrombus formation may lead to asymptomatic CTO with preserved ventricular function, secondary to coronary collateralization.4 Although collateral flow may suffice in a rested state for years, eventual supply-vs-demand issues may present, especially during exertion.5 Although the optimal timing for intervention is unclear, the emergence of new ischemic changes on serial testing clearly warrants consideration for intervention, as was the case with patient 3.

For adult patients diagnosed retrospectively with KD and asymptomatic CTO on baseline imaging, routine follow-up with interval imaging to assess for myocardial viability and ischemia testing should be performed.5 Unless changes from baseline occur, conservative management with optimal medical therapy is preferred.
