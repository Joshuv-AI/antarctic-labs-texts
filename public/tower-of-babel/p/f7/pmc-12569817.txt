Zaotang-inspired pull-folding approach to fabricate oriented multifunctional scaffolds for bone repair
Authors: Zongying Zhang, Huitao Qi, Fei Xuan, Dan Wang, Qian Kong, Zhijuan Ai, Dongming Xing, Zhongmin Geng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bone defects caused by trauma, tumors, or infections impose significant clinical challenges due to donor scarcity and complications in traditional grafting. Current scaffold fabrication techniques like electrospinning and 3D printing face limitations in mechanical strength, process complexity, and production costs. Herein, we developed a cost-effective, solvent-free fabrication method inspired by traditional Chinese "Zaotang" candy-pulling techniques. By blending polycaprolactone (PCL) and collagen under controlled heating, a malleable composite was formed, which then mimicked Zaotang's honeycomb-forming process, repetitive pulling and folding of the semi-solid mixture induced structural orientation and porosity without solvents or specialized equipment. Subsequent in situ mineralization with strontium-apatite (SrAP) further augments bioactivity, leveraging Sr2+ ions' dual role in angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Systematic characterization confirmed the SrAP mineralization homogeneity and interfacial stability. In vitro studies demonstrated 1.37-fold enhanced osteoblast migration versus the PCL-collagen scaffold and 1.15-fold/121.26-fold upregulated VEGFA/Runx2 expression, respectively. Rat calvarial defect models revealed 50.85 % bone regeneration at 6 weeks, with micro-CT/histology showing mature vascularized bone formation. This Zaotang-inspired strategy established a scalable paradigm for functional bone scaffolds, merging cultural heritage with regenerative engineering to demonstrate significant translational potential for bone defect reconstruction.

Introduction

As the population ages, bone defects caused by accidental trauma and periosteal diseases remain a major health challenge [1]. Traditional treatment options focuse on autograft and allograft bone to repair the defect; however, these approaches were associated with certain limitations, such as infection risks, scarcity of available grafts, and potential immune rejection issues [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. As tissue engineering continues to advance, tissue repair scaffolds have been extensively employed as a highly promising strategy for addressing bone defects that cannot be effectively restored through natural healing processes. Scaffolds primarily serve to balance temporary mechanical support with efficient mass transport, facilitating biological delivery and promoting tissue regeneration [6,7]. For example, Xu et al. prepared diverse oriented degradable polylactic acid scaffolds via electrospinning technology, showing that scaffolds with concentrated orientation significantly enhanced bone regeneration in vivo and early vascularization [8]. Furthermore, the application of oriented scaffolds has demonstrated a marked increase in the expression of crucial tissue development proteins, including MyoD, when compared to non-oriented scaffolds [9]. Therefore, the application of oriented engineered scaffolds provides significant benefits that are indispensable for advancing bone regeneration strategies.

In particular, the structural orientation within scaffolds plays a critical role in mimicking native tissue architecture and enhancing functional outcomes such as cell guidance and tissue regeneration, as introduced earlier [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. The development of tissue engineering scaffolds requires careful consideration of key parameters including mechanical robustness, biocompatibility, controlled degradation kinetics, and tissue-specific property customization to ensure compatibility with host tissue requirements [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. The past few decades have witnessed remarkable progress in the development of advanced fabrication methodologies and associated technologies, driving substantial innovations in the design and production of high-performance oriented engineering scaffolds [18,19]. Recent advancements in oriented scaffold fabrication research have predominantly centered on four key technological approaches, including particulate leaching, electrospinning, freeze-drying, and 3D printing. The particulate leaching method offers a straight forward approach for fabricating scaffolds with tunable pore architectures through porogen size and morphology control. Nevertheless, it typically depends on organic solvents and yields limited pore interconnectivity [20]. Moreover, electrospinning enables scaffold fabrication with superior pore orientation and interconnectivity, effectively replicating the native natural extracellular matrix architecture. However, electrospun scaffolds typically exhibit limited mechanical performance, characterized by low strength and stiffness [21,22]. The freeze-drying process creates porous scaffolds through solvent sublimation, offering highly interconnected pores. However, precise control over pore size and morphology remains challenging. Recently, 3D printing has gained prominence in tissue engineering for creating scaffolds with precise micro-architecture. Yet, it faced challenges of limited mechanical strength and high production costs [23]. Thus, developing a simple, eco-friendly, and cost-effective approach to produce high mechanical properties, load-bearing oriented porous scaffolds remains a significant challenge in tissue engineering.

Strontium (Sr), a crucial trace element for human health, was recognized as a beneficial additive in biomaterials for angiogenesis and bone regeneration [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. Research has shown that the replacement of calcium ions by strontium ions in hydroxyapatite (HAP) successfully promoted the intracellular mineralization of collagen fibers, thereby accelerating bone formation [31]. Additionally, Zaotang, a traditional Chinese candy made from maltose, was prepared by repeatedly pulling and folding the sugar paste until a delicate honeycomb structure forms within. Inspired by this unique architecture and its fabrication process, a facile and cost-effective manufacturing method has been developed to construct oriented porous scaffolds. Specifically, collagen-known for its high osteogenic potential but limited mechanical strength-is uniformly blended with the biodegradable, high-strength polymer polycaprolactone (PCL) under controlled heating to form a homogeneous adhesive mixture. This is followed by a rapid, repetitive pulling and folding process, which is carefully executed before the mixture solidifies, to achieve the desired structural orientation and porosity. After in situ mineralization of strontium-apatite (SrAP), the prepared scaffold was systematically characterized through an array of physical, chemical, and mechanical analytical techniques. In vitro experiments were performed to examine how the SrAP mineralized scaffold modulates cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation. In addition, we evaluated the SrAP mineralized scaffold in vivo using a rat calvarial defect model (Scheme 1). Micro-CT imaging and histological analysis were performed at 4 and 6 weeks post-implantation to assess its performance. Synthesis of HAP and SrAP

Dissolve 1.2 M (NH4)2HPO4 and 2 M CaCl2 respectively in 10 mL deionized water. Stir rapidly, and drop (NH4)2HPO4 solution into CaCl2 solution dropwise. Finish the dropping within 40 min. Add NH3·H2O to the reaction system to adjust the pH to 10 and keep stirring for 1 h. Then, deposit the reaction product for 48 h. Wash the deposited product with several times of absolute ethanol and deionized water, and finally obtain the reaction product hydroxyapatite (HAP). Similarly, dissolve 1.2 M (NH4)2HPO4 and 2 M SrCl2·6H2O respectively in 10 mL deionized water. Drop (NH4)2HPO4 solution into the SrCl2 solution and adjust the pH of the reaction system to 10 with NH3·H2O, and continue stirring for 1 h. Deposit for 48 h. 2.Preparation of PCL scaffolds and mineralized scaffolds

Preparation of PCL scaffolds and mineralized scaffolds

A typical procedure for preparing Zaotang mainly involves four steps: (1) Boiling. Heat the maltose until it forms a uniform sticky semi-solid. (2) Cooling. Cool the semi-solid sugar to 80 °C. (3) Kneading. Knead the sugar quickly to make the paste uniform and dense, while preventing it from cooling too much. (4) Pulling. Repeatedly pull and fold the paste until a honeycomb structure forms inside. According to the procedure for making Zaotang, the PCL scaffolds were prepared as follows: 8.5 g of PCL particles (Mw = 30,000) and 5 mL of anhydrous ethanol were combined in a beaker and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was melted in an oil bath maintained at 70 °C under continuous stirring to facilitate dissolution and achieve a homogeneous sticky consistency. Once a uniform sticky mixture was obtained, it was transferred to another beaker for cooling. When the paste was below 60 °C, 1.5 g of type I collagen (a ratio according to previous reports [32,33]) was incorporated and vigorously kneaded into the mixture. Simultaneously, a 75 % ethanol solution was preheated in an incubator to 50 °C to prevent rapid cooling during the subsequent steps. After kneading made the mixture more uniform and dense, the mixture was repeatedly stretched back and forth in the above prepared 75 % ethanol solution simulating a sugar-pulling process-for hundreds of cycles. The stretched material was then immersed in deionized water to rapidly solidify. Finally, the solidified product was cut into blocks or sheets to form the PCL-collagen scaffold. PCL-collagen scaffold (PCLSC) samples subjected to 100, 200, and 300 pull-folding cycles were designated as PCLSC1, PCLSC2, and PCLSC3, respectively.

In situ mineralization of the PCLSC3. Weigh 0.79 g of (NH4)2HPO4 powder and dissolve it in 5 mL of deionized water. Separately, dissolve 1.1 g of CaCl2 solid in 5 mL of deionized water. At room temperature, slowly add the (NH4)2HPO4 solution dropwise to the CaCl2 solution while stirring rapidly with a magnetic stirrer. The addition of the (NH₄)₂HPO₄ solution was completed within 40 min. After the addition of the (NH4)2HPO4 solution, add NH3·H2O to adjust the pH of the reaction system to 10 and continue stirring vigorously for 1 h. Place the PCLSC3 in the stirred reaction system for mineralization deposition. After 7 days of deposition, remove the scaffold, wash it multiple times with anhydrous ethanol and deionized water to remove the undeposited solids. Then, freeze it in a −20 °C refrigerator and transfer it to a freeze dryer for freeze-drying to obtain the HAP mineralized PCLSC3 (CaSC). Similarly, weigh 0.79 g of (NH4)2HPO4 powder and 2.6 g of SrCl2·6H2O powder and dissolve them separately in 5 mL of deionized water. Under vigorous stirring, adding the (NH4)2HPO4 solution dropwise to the SrCl2 solution, then add NH3·H2O to adjust the pH to 10 and continue stirring for 1 h. Place the PCLSC3 in the reaction system for mineralization. After 7 days, clean it thoroughly with anhydrous ethanol and deionized water and freeze-dry it to obtain the SrAP mineralized PCLSC3 (SrSC). To evaluate ion release from the SrSC scaffolds, simulated body fluid (SBF) was prepared following Kokubo's protocol. The scaffolds were immersed in SBF at 37 °C for 4, 8, 20, 32, and 56 h, with a fixed SBF volume-to-scaffold mass ratio (VSBF/MSrSC) of 200 mL g−1, consistent with previous studies [34]. The concentration of Sr ions in the SBF was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

In vivo experiments with scaffolds

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the approval procedures of the Qingdao University Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethics Committee (laboratory approval number: NO. 20241122sd4420250120176176). A total of twenty 8-week-old female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were randomly assigned to four groups: a blank control group, a PCLSC3 group, a CaSC group, and an SrSC group. Under anesthesia, the skulls were exposed following depilation and disinfection with medical iodine, and two critical-sized full-thickness bone defects, each 6 mm in diameter, were created using a dental trephine, with continuous irrigation using normal saline for cooling. The scaffolds for the three experimental groups were longitudinally sectioned into 6-mm slices, weighed, and sterilized. No material was implanted in the blank control group, while the PCLSC3, CaSC, and SrSC groups received their respective aseptically prepared scaffolds. The incisions were closed with sutures and disinfected with iodophor. Postoperatively, each animal received 40,000 units/day of penicillin for three days. At 4 and 6 weeks post-surgery, the rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and their skulls were excised and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde.

Each sample was imaged using a Quantum GX2 high-resolution Micro-CT scanner with settings of 90 kV scanning voltage and 88 μA current. Following this, three-dimensional reconstruction of the specimen images was conducted to assess the repair efficacy of the rat skull defects. Bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and Trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) were quantitatively analyzed (n = 4).

The specimens in each group were decalcified using a commercially available ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution, with the decalcification solution being replaced every 48 h over a period of four weeks. Following decalcification, the samples were rinsed under running water for an extended period (overnight). Subsequently, the specimens underwent sequential dehydration through an alcohol gradient, followed by paraffin infiltration and embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 4 μm. The specimens were then subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson's trichrome staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, and immunofluorescence staining.7.Statistical Analysis

Results and discussion

1.Fabrication and characteristics of PCL-collagen scaffold (PCLSC)

1.Fabrication and characteristics of PCL-collagen scaffold (PCLSC)

Fabrication and characteristics of PCL-collagen scaffold (PCLSC)

In the first step of the fabrication process, PCL was heated under controlled temperature conditions until a homogeneous mixture was formed. Type I collagen was then incorporated and rapidly kneaded into the mixture. Subsequently, the mixture was repeatedly pulled, folded, and re-pulled in a cyclic manner until a honeycomb-like microstructure developed within the material. To elucidate the relationship between pore structure and pull-folding cycles, the number of repetitions was investigated. Fig. 1A presents the morphological and structural characteristics of the PCLSC processed with different pull-folding cycles (100, 200, 300; designated as PCLSC1, PCLSC2, PCLSC3, respectively). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that increasing the number of pull-folding cycles led to significant improvements in pore architecture, including enhanced pore size uniformity and interconnectivity. Pycnometric analysis revealed that the scaffold porosity of PCLSC1, PCLSC2 and PCLSC3 ranged from 41 % to 70 % (Fig. 1B). Considering that subsequent mineralization would reduce scaffold porosity, PCLSC3 was selected as the optimal scaffold. Moreover, PCLSC3 displayed pore diameters predominantly distributed within the optimal range of 80–120 μm, a critical dimension conducive to accelerated osteogenesis in bone regeneration applications. Following 7-day in vitro mineralization, the SrAP mineralized PCLSC3 (SrSC) and the HAP mineralized PCLSC3 (CaSC) scaffold derivatives were successfully fabricated from PCLSC3 precursors (Fig. 1C–S1). The proposed mechanism for HAP and SrAP mineralization suggests that anionic groups, such as -COO- on the surface of PCL polymers, facilitate the enrichment of Ca2+ or Sr2+ ions. Upon addition of an (NH4)2HPO4 aqueous solution, HPO42− readily interacts with these metal ions via electrostatic attraction, leading to the formation of nucleation sites and subsequent mineralization. Additionally, the incorporation of collagen enhances the hydrophilicity and introduces functional groups such as -COOH and -NH2, which may further promote the effective deposition of HAP or SrAP [35]. SrSC scaffolds demonstrated conformal SrAP mineralization layers along pore walls, exhibiting monodisperse particles with diameters smaller than 5 μm as shown in Fig. 1C. In contrast, CaSC scaffolds demonstrated heterogeneous HAP deposition patterns featuring polydisperse particles with irregular morphologies, as detailed in Fig. S1. Quantitative analysis revealed a 12.40 % reduction in scaffold porosity post-mineralization, as shown in Fig. 1D. Optimal osteoconductive scaffolds necessitate dual structural criteria: (1) macroporous networks (>100 μm) for cell migration, and (2) adequate porosity (>30 %) to sustain nutrient diffusion [36]. The PCLSC3 matrix exhibited more than 69.5 % interconnected porosity - exceeding the 60 % threshold critical for osteoblast infiltration. While mineralization reduced void volumes, both CaSC and SrSC maintained functional porosity above 50 %, enabling ECM remodeling during bone formation (Fig. 1D). The XPS analysis, as displayed in Fig. S2, confirms the detection of strontium within the SrSC sample. In addition, the inherent hydrophobicity of PCL limited its cellular adhesion capacity, as evidenced by the 82.7° water contact angle (Fig. 1E). Surface mineralization with phosphate-based coatings significantly enhanced hydrophilicity, with CaSC and SrSC demonstrating significantly reduced contact angles of 50.2° and 53.9°, respectively (Fig. S3). This transition from hydrophobic to hydrophilic regimes improved fibroblast adhesion. As a biodegradable polymer, PCL demonstrated gradual degradation characteristics in vitro. Fig. 1F shows that PCLSC3, CaSC and SrSC scaffolds maintained steady degradation over time. This slow degradation supports new cell growth by providing sufficient time for tissue regeneration. To characterize the mineralization components on SrSC scaffolds, we performed FT-IR spectroscopy and elemental mapping analysis. The characteristic PO43− absorption band of SrAP was observed at 1017 cm−1 in SrSC scaffolds (Fig. 1G and H), confirming successful phosphate incorporation. Elemental mapping revealed homogeneous distribution of P, O, N, and Sr throughout the scaffold architecture (Fig. 1I). These results confirmed successful deposition of SrAP mineralized particles on polymeric matrices, while HAP mineralization was simultaneously observed on CaSC (Fig. S4). Furthermore, the engineered channel-aligned scaffolds exhibited significant structural anisotropy, a critical feature mimicking native bone architecture. To systematically evaluate their mechanical properties, we tested their strength through uniaxial compression testing (transverse/longitudinal axes). The stress-strain curves demonstrated that SrSC scaffolds after mineralization maintained excellent compressive strength under bidirectional loading, showing favorable structural stability (Fig. 1J). Notably, the compressive modulus of SrSC (25.35 MPa transverse, 37.10 MPa longitudinal) showed no statistically significant differences compared with PCLSC3 and CaSC groups, indicating that strontium doping did not alter the intrinsic mechanical properties of the scaffold (Fig. 1K). Comprehensive physicochemical characterizations confirmed that SrSC met the engineering requirements for ideal bone regeneration materials in terms of mechanical performance, structural integrity, and compositional controllability. Moreover, regarding ion incorporation, cytotoxicity must be carefully considered. Previous studies have extensively explored the feasibility of incorporating Sr. For instance, Pontremoli et al. found that Sr release at concentrations of 1.6–4.4 μg mL−1 could stimulate osteogenic activity without adverse effects [37]. Similarly, Zhao et al. reported that a concentration range of 0.5–1.5 μg mL−1 released from a Sr-doped bioceramic was both safe and effective in promoting bone regeneration [38]. In this study, SrSC exhibited a sustained Sr release profile, with concentrations falling within these previously established ranges. SrSC scaffold promoted angiogenesis

Effective bone defect repair necessitates robust vascularization to ensure nutrient supply and metabolic waste removal. To evaluate this critical aspect, we first assessed scaffold biocompatibility using umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) cultured with different concentrations (0.1, 0.05, 0.025 and 0.0125 mg/mL) of CaSC/SrSC extracts. While CaSC maintained cytocompatibility up to 0.1 mg/mL, SrSC demonstrated optimal biocompatibility at 0.05 mg/mL (Fig. 3A), with live/dead staining confirming almost no dead cells even at the lowest concentration (Fig. 3B and Fig. S9). Given endothelial cell recruitment's pivotal role in osteogenesis, we next examined directional cell migration to the bone defect area. Transwell migration experiments and cell scratch healing experiments proved that the SrSC scaffold had a more obvious migration effect than the PCLSC3 group, and the mean number of cell migrations and closed areas were higher than those of the CaSC scaffold (Fig. 3C–F). Moreover, the angiogenic superiority of SrSC became particularly evident through tubulogenesis assays. At 0.0125 mg/mL, SrSC extracts induced 1.35-fold longer vascular network formation compared to CaSC, with 1.76-fold more master junctions, and 1.69-fold more junction points (Fig. 3G–J). The osteogenic differentiation potential of SrSC was systematically evaluated through in vitro assessments, using initial alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining of MC3T3-E1 cells as established methods for analysis. ALP staining demonstrated SrSC's superior capacity to promote MC3T3-E1 pre-osteoblast differentiation at 0.0125 mg/mL extract concentration, with 1.51-fold higher ALP activity versus CaSC on day 7 (Fig. 4A and B). Furthermore, ARS staining after 14 days demonstrated a significantly larger mineralized area in the SrSC group than in other groups, indicating markedly enhanced osteogenic differentiation (Fig. S10). To further verify the osteogenic potential of SrSC, we used immunofluorescence to test osteogenic-related proteins Bglap and Runx2. Complementary immunofluorescence analysis revealed significant upregulation of osteogenic markers Bglap and Runx2 in SrSC-treated cells compared to CaSC (Fig. 4C–F). Moreover, molecular profiling confirmed this trend, the expression levels of ALP, Runx2 and Col1a1 genes in the SrSC group were significantly higher than those in the CaSC group, which also indicated that SrSC had a more obvious osteogenic potential (Fig. 4G–I). To assess the microstructural characteristics of neoformed bone tissue, hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E staining) and Masson's trichrome staining techniques were systematically employed. At the 4-week postoperative interval, H&E staining images and Masson's trichrome staining images revealed nascent bone formation in the defect area of the SrSC group, demonstrating this group's unique osteogenic capacity, whereas no detectable mineralization was observed in control, PCLSC3, or CaSC groups (Fig. S13). At 6-week post-implantation, H&E staining images revealed progressive differences: Control defects remained fully occupied by fibrous tissue (labeled as "FT"), while the PCLSC3 group showed partial fibrous infiltration. The CaSC group exhibited nascent new bone (labeled as "NB"), whereas the SrSC group demonstrated substantially expanded mineralized bone deposition (Fig. 5H). Masson's trichrome results corroborated these findings, showing SrSC-induced collagen maturation and vascular infiltration (Fig. 5I). Moreover, the degradation of the scaffolds was assessed via H&E staining. At 4 weeks post-implantation, the SrSC group showed increased cellularity and distinct blood vessels containing erythrocytes, suggesting active host integration. The appearance of hollow areas indicated ongoing scaffold degradation. By 6 weeks, larger blood vessels and more intense collagen staining (pink) were evident, likely occupying sites left by degraded SrSC material, further supporting its biointegration.

In this study, osteocalcin (OCN) and osteopontin (OPN) were selected as specific osteogenic markers to systematically perform immunohistochemical analysis on four groups of calvarial tissue sections. While the SrSC group exhibited extensive new bone formation, these regions showed limited positive staining attributable to rapid matrix mineralization creating steric exclusion effects that impeded dye penetration (Fig. 5J). Notably, undifferentiated mesenchymal cells surrounding the scaffold demonstrated pronounced staining characteristics. The spatial distribution disparity in staining patterns suggested that the SrSC-implanted microenvironment not only effectively promoted the differentiation of undifferentiated mesenchymal cells into osteoblastic lineage, but also that the rapid deposition of mineralized components in the neo-bone matrix may have created steric hindrance effects against dye penetration. Collectively, these results demonstrate the superior osteoinductive capacity of the SrSC microenvironment.

In vivo experiments showed through immunofluorescence staining that the fluorescence intensity of angiogenesis markers α-SMA and CD31 was the highest in the SrSC group, while they were relatively low in other groups (Fig. 5K and S14). This was consistent with the results of the in vitro experiments. The in vivo experiments more convincingly verified the efficient guiding function of SrSC for cells and its promoting effect on cranial bone regeneration. This not only proved the beneficial influence of strontium element on bone regeneration but also provided a new idea for the development of directional pore scaffolds.

Conclusion

In summary, inspired by the traditional Chinese "Zaotang" candy-pulling techniques, we have successfully developed a biomimetic strategy to engineer a biodegradable bone repair scaffold with hierarchical architecture. Through the innovative integration of PCL with type I collagen and strategic incorporation of strontium through biomineralization, we successfully constructed a SrSC scaffold featuring uniaxially aligned microchannels. Systematic in vitro and in vivo experiments have proved that this material showed significant advantages in bone tissue regeneration, vascularization, and cell directional migration, providing a new solution to the problems of complex preparation process, insufficient biological activity, and low osteogenic efficiency of traditional bone regeneration materials. In this work, bone-vascular collaborative repair was achieved through structural biomimicry, element doping, and interface modification, and it showed broad application prospects in the field of bone defect treatment. This achievement lays an important foundation for the development of next-generation intelligent bone repair materials.
