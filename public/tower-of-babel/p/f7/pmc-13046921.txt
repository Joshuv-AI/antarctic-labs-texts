TROP-2 Is Not a Therapeutic Target in Melanoma: a Morphological Study

Introduction

Although cutaneous malignant melanoma represents only 1% of all skin cancers, it has become a leading cause of cancer death especially in young adults [1–3]. The worse outcome of melanoma is also favored by the high number of patients with disease progression and recurrence [4]. In fact, although new and innovative therapies significantly improved the overall survival of patients with melanoma, disease progression and recurrence are still important issues in treatment of this disease [5, 6]. Among the risk factors for melanoma, aging is increasingly recognized as a critical predisposing factor, primarily attributable to the accumulation of senescent cells, particularly fibroblasts, in the skin. Senescent cells undergo profound alterations in chromatin architecture and gene expression profiles, leading to the production and secretion of a broad spectrum of factors that influence melanoma initiation [7]. Trophoblast surface antigen-2 (TROP-2) is a transmembrane glycoprotein of 323 amino acids belonging to the epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) family. TROP-2 protein is organized in an extracellular domain, a transmembrane domain and a cytoplasmic tail [8]. TROP-2 plays a key role in promoting cell proliferation and invasion, and it is expressed in several normal and pathological tissues [9–12]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that TROP-2 expression is significantly increased in several human cancers favoring cancer cell proliferation and invasion, then promoting cancer progression and a poor prognosis [9, 13–15]. The role of TROP-2 is mainly related to cell proliferation and mobility owing to the interaction of its extracellular domain with cell adhesion molecules, making itself an adhesion molecule [16]. In our previous study, we demonstrated that TROP-2 is involved in cutaneous inflammatory processes, suggesting a role in maintaining epithelial integrity [17]. It has been demonstrated that TROP-2 is also a druggable target. In fact, anti-TROP-2 antibody–drug conjugate showed important beneficial effects in patients with metastatic and non-metastatic cancers [18, 19].

We hypothesized that TROP-2 could be expressed in melanoma and melanocytic nevi and that TROP-2 could be considered as a potential therapeutic target.

Methods

Patients

Our retrospective study included 60 formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens, obtained from the Department of Biomedical Sciences and Public Health (Section of Pathology) of the Università Politecnica delle Marche. The samples included 50 primary melanomas and 10 melanocytic nevi (used as control group). The study was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Data collection and management observed the applicable rules, regulations, and directives concerning patient protection, such as patient privacy. Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin sections (5 µm) were cut from FFPE tissue blocks, deparaffinized and rehydrated with xylene and a graded ethyl alcohol series. Sections were rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 40 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase. Thereafter, 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 98 °C for 10 min was used as antigen retrieval. Animal-Free Blocker (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) diluted 1:5 in PBS for 20 min was used to block nonspecific background. Subsequently, sections were incubated with mouse monoclonal anti-TROP-2 antibody (sc-376746; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA) diluted 1:300 and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Sections were incubated with anti-mouse biotinylated antibody diluted 1:200 (Vector Laboratories) for 30 min after washing. The avidin–biotin-peroxidase complex (ABC) method (Vector Laboratories) was performed using 3′,3′-diaminobenzidine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) as the chromogen. Sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted with Eukitt solution (Kindler GmbH and Co., Freiburg, Germany). Negative controls were performed by omitting the first or secondary antibody and using an isotype control antibody (ab18443; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at the same dilution of primary antibody for all the immunohistochemical reactions performed in this study.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Section rehydration and antigen retrieval were performed as reported in the immunohistochemistry section. After washing with PBS, tissue autofluorescence was blocked by incubating sections for 30 min with 0.1% Sudan Black B stain (SBB). Animal-Free Blocker (Vector laboratories) diluted 1:5 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature was used to block nonspecific binding sites. Subsequently, sections were incubated with the primary antibodies mouse anti-TROP-2 (1:300; sc-376746, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and rabbit anti-SOX10 (1:400; A700-080, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, sections were incubated with the secondary antibodies anti-mouse conjugated with Alexa Fluor-488 and anti-rabbit conjugated with Alexa Fluor-555 (both from Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) diluted 1:400 for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were visualized by using DAPI (Invitrogen). Sections were mounted with Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Negative controls were performed by omitting the first or secondary antibody or using an isotype control antibody (ab172730 and ab18443; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at the same dilution of primary antibody. Sections were visualized with the inverted microscope Eclipse Ti2-E supplied with an AX confocal system (Nikon, Japan).

Immunofluorescence Analysis

As the presence of melanin produced by melanocytes may alter the interpretation of TROP-2 staining obtained by immunohistochemistry, we performed immunofluorescence analysis using SOX10 nuclear protein as marker of melanocytes [20]. Interestingly, we found that epidermal melanocytes (Fig. 3a, red arrows) expressed TROP-2 protein (Fig. 3, in green). However, melanocytes of melanocytic nevi did not express TROP-2 protein (Fig. 3b). Discussion

Despite the low incidence of cutaneous malignant melanoma, the mortality rate of this type of cancer increased in the recent years [21]. In addition, disease progression and recurrence significantly affect the overall survival of these patients [1–3]. TROP-2 is highly expressed in several malignancies including breast, bladder, lung, and prostate cancers, where it plays a key role in cancer proliferation, migration, and invasion, making this protein a good therapeutic target for these malignancies [22–26].

TROP-2-targeted therapeutics mainly consist in antibody–drug conjugates (ADC), small-molecule cytotoxic drugs conjugated to monoclonal antibodies that recognize targeted protein expressed on the surface of cancer cells [27]. Several ADCs targeting TROP-2 have been approved for marketing by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) including sacituzumab govitecan (Trodelvy) and datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) [22]. In our study, we evaluated TROP-2 immunoreactivity in normal skin, melanocytic nevi, and melanoma tissues at different stages to evaluate TROP-2 as a potential therapeutic target in melanoma.

A recent study by He and colleagues demonstrated the absence of TROP-2 expression in melanoma (25 cases) [28], with their data in agreement with our findings. However, He and colleagues did not specify which stages were investigated. Moreover, they did not report whether TROP-2 was expressed in the nevi (which should be used as control to compare melanoma TROP-2 expression) and whether epidermal melanocytes express TROP-2.

Although it has been widely demonstrated the key role of TROP-2 in inhibiting cell adhesion (e.g., by interacting with integrin β-1 and RACK1) in cancer cells [29], its role in adhesion of normal cells is still poorly understood. Our study demonstrated TROP-2 expressions on the cell membranes of epidermal keratinocytes and epidermal melanocytes, suggesting a role of TROP-2 in maintaining epidermal melanocyte–keratinocyte interaction. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that TROP-2 can bind claudin-1 and claudin-7, two important tight junction proteins, potentially strengthening cell–cell junctions [30].

Interestingly, our findings also showed that melanocytic nevi and melanoma tissues (from the in situ to pT3b stage) did not express TROP-2 protein, suggesting that this protein may not be involved in melanoma pathogenesis. Thus, we suggest that TROP-2 could not be considered a therapeutic target for melanoma treatment because of the absence of detectable expression in the analyzed cohort. In addition, the latter is a relatively limited cohort and lacks very thick or metastatic melanomas that can represent the limitations of the current study. Furthermore, since very thick or metastatic melanomas represent the most advanced stages of the disease, they could theoretically present different molecular profiles.

Conclusions

While TROP-2 is a promising therapeutic target in skin malignancies such as cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) [31], sebaceous carcinoma, and sweat gland carcinoma [32], TROP-2-targeted therapy may not exert beneficial effects in Merkel cell carcinoma, atypical fibroxanthoma, and melanoma [28] as TROP-2 is not expressed in these malignancies. Our data also demonstrated that TROP-2-targeted therapy may not exert beneficial effects in patients with melanoma at early stages. Therefore, innovative therapies not focused on TROP-2 are necessary to improve the outcome of patients with melanoma.
