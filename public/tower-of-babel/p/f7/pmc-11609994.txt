The efficacy of expanded non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) in a high‐risk twin pregnancies cohort
Authors: Meng Meng, Jianping Chen, Yingjun Yang, Yun Zhang, Gang Zou, Fenhe Zhou, Xing Wei, Yuchun Ge, Jia Zhou, Luming Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOur objective was to evaluate the efficacy of expanded non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) that includes both trisomies and copy number variants (CNVs) in high‐risk twin pregnancies.Material and MethodsA prospective, double‐blinded cohort study was conducted, enrolling 73 high‐risk twin pregnancies characterized by increased risk of genetic disorders due to factors such as increased nuchal translucency, structural anomalies, fetal growth restriction, and other factors associated with chromosomal abnormality. Participants underwent invasive karyotyping and chromosomal microarray analysis, alongside separate expanded NIPT for research purposes. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of expanded NIPT were calculated.ResultsThe cohort included 24 monochorionic and 49 dichorionic twin pregnancies. The median cell‐free fetal DNA concentration in expanded NIPT was 16.7% (range 3.86%–49.1%), with a test failure rate of 1.4% (1/73). High‐risk findings for trisomy 21/13/18 were identified in five cases (6.8%), Turner syndrome in one case (1.4%), and CNVs indicative of high risk for clinically significant microdeletion/microduplication syndromes (MMS) in ten cases (13.7%). Of these, 56 cases (76.7%) tested NIPT negative, revealing one false‐negative for 45, X and five false‐negatives for CNVs. Expanded NIPT achieved a detection rate of 100% (5/5) for trisomy 21/13/18 with a false‐positive rate of 0% (0/5), a detection rate of 33.3% (1/3) for sex chromosome abnormalities with a false‐positive rate of 0% (0/3), and a detection rate of 66.7% (4/6) for MMS with a false‐positive rate of 3.0% (2/67). The positive predictive values for trisomy T21/13/18, sex chromosome abnormalities, and known MMS were 100% (5/5), 100% (1/1), and 66.7% (4/6) in the expanded NIPT, respectively.ConclusionsThe expanded NIPT demonstrated high detection rates for common trisomies and moderate detection rates for prenatal MMS in high‐risk twin pregnancies. Further studies with large sample sizes in low‐risk populations are needed.

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of twin pregnancies has been on the rise worldwide. Contributing factors include advanced maternal age, ovulation induction, and assisted reproductive technologies, which have intensified this trend. Compared to singleton pregnancies, twin pregnancies, both dichorionic (DC) and monochorionic (MC), present an increased risk of genetic disorders and structural anomalies for the whole pregnancy. 1 High‐risk twin pregnancies should be offered invasive prenatal diagnostic procedures. 2 , 3 However, due to concerns about the risk of fetal loss, 4 many women with high‐risk twin pregnancies opt against invasive testing, preferring the expanded non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), known for its advanced screening capabilities for genetic abnormalities. 4 , 5

Current evidence for NIPT in twin pregnancies is limited. While the efficacy of NIPT in detecting common aneuploidies (Trisomy 21/13/18) in twin pregnancies has been established and is widely recommended, 6 , 7 the performance of NIPT in identifying sex chromosomal aneuploidy (SCA) and microdeletion/microduplication syndrome (MMS) in these pregnancies remains uncertain. particularly for high‐risk patients. There is a lack of evidence regarding the residual risk when opting for expanded NIPT over invasive testing, especially concerning the risk of MMS, which is commonly attributed to the pathogenic or likely pathogenic copy number variations (CNVs).

The aim of this study was to assess the efficacy of the expanded NIPT in high‐risk twin pregnancies, targeting not only common aneuploidies but also SCA and MMS. The residual risk was also assessed.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and participants

A prospective, double‐blind study was conducted between June 2016 and December 2019 in Fetal Medicine Center of Shanghai First Maternity and Infant Hospital. Inclusion criteria included (1) twin pregnancies with both fetuses alive; (2) the presence of increased nuchal translucency (above the 99th percentile for gestational age), structure anomalies in either twin or other high‐risk factors for chromosome abnormalities, for example, the advanced maternal age or high risk of serum screening with the risk cut‐off point <1/270; and (3) acceptance of invasive testing for both twins. Exclusion criteria were (1) triplet pregnancies with one fetus demise and (2) refusal of invasive testing for both twins. Pretest counseling was provided for invasive procedures, while expanded NIPT was offered with informed consent obtained for scientific research purposes.

The prenatal diagnosis for high‐risk twin pregnancies

Chorionicity and amnionicity were determined, and fetal structure was evaluated by ultrasound. Following counseling, standard invasive prenatal diagnosis was offered. Amniotic fluid samples were sample from both sacs and cultured for chromosome karyotype analysis. Chorionic villi sampling was not offered to avoid the potential risk of confined placental mosaicism. Genomic DNA extracted from uncultured amniotic fluid underwent testing using the CytoScan 750 k array (Affymetrix Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Both sonographic and genetic findings were recorded. Pregnancy outcomes were monitored for all cases.

NIPT screening and bioinformatics analysis

Before intrauterine intervention, 5 mL of maternal peripheral blood was collected for NIPT testing. DNA extraction, library construction, sequencing, and analysis followed previously described methods. 8 The sequence from next‐generation sequence was compared with the human genome reference sequence map, and the percentage of each chromosome was calculated using Illumina Sequencing Analysis Viewer1.9.1 software. Z and T values were utilized to assess the samples' actual disease status 9 (Appendix S1, Figure S1). To obtain double blind, the NIPT and the bioinformatics analysts were kept blinded from each other's results (Appendix S2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 22, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the expanded NIPT were estimated. The exact confidence limits (Clopper–Pearson) were used to calculate confidence intervals using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

Patients characteristics

A total of 6251 twin pregnancies were observed at our Fetal Medicine Center during the study period. According to the inclusion criteria, 2580 twin pregnancies were identified as high‐risk pregnancies. Among which, 2507 cases were excluded; of these, 12.6% (317/2507) were with either twin of structural anomaly, 2.7% (68/2507) with increased nuchal translucency, 4.5% (112/2507) with either twin of fetal growth restriction, 20.8% (521/2507) with high risk of serum screen or advanced maternal age, and 59.4% (1489/2507) with assisted reproductive technology conception. These cases involved either opting for amniocentesis for one twin (n = 113, 4.5%) or directly choosing the NIPT test (n = 1955, 78.0%). In instances of severe structural anomalies, selective fetal reduction was chosen (n = 172, 6.9%). In addition, 6.3% (158/2507) declined the invitation, while 3.9% (97/2507) were lost to follow‐up. Only 0.5% (12/2507) opted for direct termination of pregnancy.

Finally, 73 women with high‐risk twin pregnancies were recruited (Figure 1). The median maternal age was 30.1 years (range: 22.9–40.2 years), with 16.4% (12/73) of the women being of advanced maternal age (≥35 years). 74.0% (54/73) of the cohort were nulliparous. 32.9% (24/73) were monochorionic diamniotic twin, while 67.1% (49/73) were dichorionic diamniotic twin twins. 56.2% (41/73) resulted from assisted reproductive technologies. Among these, 13 cases (17.8%) were included due to either or both fetuses presenting with an increased nuchal translucency, 52 cases (82.2%) were referred due to structural anomalies in either or both fetuses, increased risk factors such as advanced maternal age or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) (5/73, 6.8%), and MC complications (3/73, 4.1%). The median gestational age for NIPT testing was 21.9 weeks (range: 16.4–29.0 weeks) (Table 1).

The expanded NIPT findings

The median fetal fraction was 16.7% (range 3.86%–49.1%). Lower fetal fraction (<10%) was observed in 22.4% (11/49) of DC twins and 12.5% (3/24) of MC twins. A single NIPT detection failure occurred in an MC twin, resulting in a general test failure rate of 1.4%. This MC case with a failed cell‐free fetal DNA test, having a fetal fraction of 26.9%, was confirmed by karyotyping and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) as a monozygotic twin with one fetus of 45, X. The combined screen‐positive rate for common trisomy (trisomy 21/13/18), SCA, and clinically significant MMS was 16.4% (12/73, 95% confidence interval (CI): 7.9%−24.9%).

Five women (6.8%, 95% CI: 1.1%−12.6%) were identified at high risk for common autosomal trisomies: T21 (n = 2), T13 (n = 1), and T18 (n = 2), all confirmed by corresponding karyotyping and CMA. In one T21 case which was MC twin, both twins were affected. The remaining four cases involved one affected twin. Regarding SCA, one woman was identified with a 45, X twin (n = 1). No rare autosomal trisomies (RATs) were identified (Table 2).

Apart from the above positive findings from the expanded NIPT, CNVs uninvolved in the prenatal clinically significant MMS were further evaluated for research purposes. Four cases carrying these CNVs were identified: 1q21.1 deletion (n = 1), 16p13.1 duplication (n = 1), 5p12p13.1 duplication (n = 1), and 6q16.2 duplication (n = 1). All the CNVs were below 5 Mb. The genetic findings for negative expanded NIPT cases

In 56 twin pregnancies (76.7%) with negative expanded NIPT results, all were verified by invasive methods. There were six cases of discordant karyotyping or CMA results, including SCA (n = 1), pathogenic or likely pathogenic CNVs (n = 2), variant of uncertain significance CNVs (n = 2), and benign/likely benign CNVs (n = 1). The false‐negative SCA case involved one twin with 45, X. All the five MMS cases (pathogenic or likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance, and benign/likely benign CNVs) were indicated as CNVs below 5 Mb. The pathogenic CNV case involved a 22q11.2 deletion (2.5 Mb). The case involving the likely pathogenic CNV featured a 1p13.2 deletion (2.7 Mb), which was not previously listed. The remaining 50 cases demonstrated concordant negative CMA results (Table 4).

The performance for expanded NIPT

Overall, expanded NIPT achieved a detection rate (DR) of 100% (5/5, 95% CI: 47.8%−100.0%) for trisomy 21/13/18 with a false‐positive rate (FPR) of 0% (0/68, 95% CI: 0.0%−5.3%), a DR of 33.3% (1/3, 95% CI: 0.0%−86.7%) for sex chromosome abnormalities (SCA) with a FPR of 0% (0/70, 95% CI: 0.0%− 5.1%), and a DR of 66.7% (4/6, 95% CI: 29.0%−100.0%) for MMS with a FPR of 3.0% (2/67, 95% CI: 0.0%−7.1%). The PPVs for trisomy T21/13/18, SCA, and known MMS were 100% (5/5, 95% CI: 47.8%−100.0%), 100% (1/1, 95% CI: 2.5%−100.0%), and 66.7% (4/6, 95% CI: 29.0%−100.0%) in the expanded NIPT, respectively (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

This study represents the first prospective, double‐blind investigation into the efficacy of expanded NIPT for high‐risk twin pregnancies. In the 73 high‐risk twin pregnancies enrolled, the expanded NIPT obtained a relatively high DR and low FPR for trisomy 21/13/18, a moderate DR and FPR for SCA and MMS. The PPVs for T21/13/18, SCA, and known MMS were 100%, 100%, and 66.7%, respectively.

The sensitivity and specificity of the expanded NIPT reached 100% for detecting common trisomies (T21, T18, and T13), aligning with previous research. 3 Our high PPV (5/5) reflects the selected high‐risk population's increased prevalence of these trisomies. The findings suggest that expanded NIPT is capable of reliably identifying common trisomies, albeit with potential delays in subsequent management due to the NIPT process.

Given the scarcity of data, expanded NIPT exhibited similar accuracy for the SCAs in both singleton 10 , 11 and twin pregnancies, though the genetic mosaic condition for NIPT testing in dizygotic twin pregnancies might affect the accuracy. Literature indicates that, 12 , 13 in contrast to monosomy X (45, X), the expanded NIPT shows superior performance in identifying sex chromosomal trisomies (XXX, XXY, and XYY). In our research, all three cases were discordant MC twins, with one fetus exhibiting cystic hygroma/hydrops, yet invasive testing confirmed them as Normal/45, X, which implies that, if monozygotic twins with cystic hygroma or suspected discordant sex noted, the invasive testing rather than the NIPT should be suggested. Moreover, the expanded NIPT yielded varied outcomes: high risk, low risk, and test failure, with a DR of 33.3% (1/3), an NPV of 98.6 (70/71), and a false‐negative rate (FNR) of 50% (1/2). These inconsistent screening results suggest that the current algorithm for expanded NIPT is prone to inaccuracies in twin pregnancies. 14 Recent advancements in de novo paired‐end sequencing strategies have shown improved detection rates for SCAs in singletons, with a concordance rate exceeding 90%, which could enhance future NIPT performance in twin pregnancies. 15

Although there is ongoing debate regarding clinical implementation, 16 , 17 technical advancements such as increased sequencing depth have enhanced the detection of subtle signal variations in CNV segments. 18 Within our cohort of high‐risk twin pregnancies, the DR, PPV, NPV, and FNR for prenatal MMS screening were 66.7% (4/6), 66.7% (4/6), 98.5% (65/66), and 20.0% (1/5), respectively. Specifically, For the 22q11.2 (DGS) region, the screening effectiveness was comparable, 19 , 20 with DR, PPV, NPV, and FNR as 66.7% (2/3), 66.7% (2/3), 98.6% (68/69), and 33.3% (1/3), respectively. Our preliminary findings outperform those reported for expanded NIPT in twin pregnancies, demonstrating the feasibility of using expanded NIPT for identifying MMS in high‐risk twin pregnancies. 12 , 13 , 21 However, caution should be exercised regarding the utility of expanded NIPT as a secondary screening method prior to fetal CMA, especially for women resistant to amniocentesis.

In our study, we explore a scenario where expanded NIPT is offered to high‐risk twin pregnancies with a preference against invasive procedures, aiming to evaluate residual risk in low‐risk women. Our data indicate that expanded NIPT maintains similar efficacy in detecting the 22q11.2 deletion and SCAs in both singleton and twin pregnancies. Consequently, for true‐positive cases, a delay in prenatal diagnosis may postpone the subsequent clinical management in cases requiring selective termination, thereby increasing the risk of fetal loss. Furthermore, it is important to note that this cohort included false‐negative instances, such as one case of 45, X and one case of 22q11.2 deletion, with FNRs of 50% (1/2) and 33.3% (1/3), respectively. These results underscore that expanded NIPT cannot replace invasive prenatal diagnosis. Therefore, for high‐risk twin pregnancies, meticulous pre‐ and post‐test counseling, along with thorough follow‐up, is imperative, particularly to address the risk of false negatives. Finally, a cost‐effectiveness analysis is essential before expanded NIPT can be widely adopted in clinical practice.

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, to ensure accurate genetic diagnoses, we included only high‐risk twin pregnancies that underwent invasive testing, which restricted both our sample size and the diversity of our study population, thereby limiting the strength of our evidence. Second, the inability to collect placental samples from false‐positive cases (due to most participants delivering outside our center) prevented further genetic analysis, leaving confined placental mosaicism as an unaddressed variable. Future studies should aim to include large sample size, particularly from the low‐risk population.

CONCLUSION

Our investigation demonstrates that expanded NIPT achieves high detection rates for common trisomies in high‐risk twin pregnancies, though its effectiveness for prenatal MMS and SCAs is moderate. Thorough pre‐counseling and post‐counseling, counseling should be approached with caution. Further research with larger sample sizes, especially among low‐risk populations, is necessary prior to clinical adoption.
