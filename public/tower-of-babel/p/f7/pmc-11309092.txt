Phenotypic and Functional Outcomes in Macrophages Exposed to an Environmentally Relevant Mixture of Organophosphate Esters in Vitro
Authors: Braeden H. Giles, Nikola Kukolj, Koren K. Mann, Bernard Robaire
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Organophosphate esters (OPEs) are flame retardants and plasticizers used in consumer products. OPEs are found ubiquitously throughout the environment with high concentrations in indoor house dust. Exposure to individual OPEs is associated with immune dysfunction, particularly in macrophages. However, OPEs exist as complex mixtures and the effects of environmentally relevant mixtures on the immune system have not been investigated.Objectives:The objectives of this study were to evaluate the toxicity of an environmentally relevant mixture of OPEs that models Canadian house dust on macrophages using phenotypic and functional assessments in vitro.Methods:High-content live-cell fluorescent imaging for phenotypic biomarkers of toxicity in THP-1 macrophages treated with the OPE mixture was undertaken. We used confocal microscopy and cholesterol analysis to validate and expand on the observed OPE-induced lipid phenotype. Then, we used flow cytometry and live-cell imaging to conduct functional tests and uncover mechanisms of OPE-induced phagocytic suppression. Finally, we validated our THP-1 findings in human primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMC) derived macrophages.Results:Exposure to non-cytotoxic dilutions of the OPE mixture resulted in higher oxidative stress and disrupted lysosome and lipid homeostasis in THP-1 and primary macrophages. We further observed that phagocytosis of apoptotic cells in THP-1 and primary macrophages was lower in OPE-exposed cells vs. controls. In THP-1 macrophages, phagocytosis of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria was also lower in OPE-exposed cells vs. controls. Additionally, the OPE mixture altered the expression of phagocytic receptors linked to the recognition of phosphatidylserine and pathogen-associated molecular patterns.Discussion:The results of this in vitro study suggested that exposure to an environmentally relevant mixture of OPEs resulted in higher lipid retention in macrophages and poor efferocytic response. These effects could translate to enhanced foam cell generation resulting in higher cardiovascular mortality. Furthermore, bacterial phagocytosis was lower in OPE-exposed macrophages in an in vitro setting, which may indicate the potential for reduced bacterial clearance in models of infections. Taken together, our data provide strong evidence that mixtures of OPEs can influence the biology of macrophages and offer new mechanistic insights into the impact of OPE mixtures on the immune system. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP13869

Introduction

Manufacturers use organophosphate esters (OPEs), the most prominent class of flame retardants, in consumer and commercial products to prevent or reduce the spread of fire.1,2 The use of OPEs as flame retardants in furniture, textiles, building materials, and electronics and as plasticizers in floor polishes, coatings, thermoplastics, and epoxy resins is increasing.2,3 Importantly, because OPEs are mixed into products as additives and thus not covalently bound,2 they can rapidly enter the environment through processes such as volatilization, leaching, and dissolution.4–6 OPEs have been detected on every continent across a variety of environmental matrices, including water,9,11 soil,9 air,9,12 sediment,10 and, particularly, dust.7,8

Over the years, the concentrations of OPEs in household dust have increased. The highest ΣOPE enrichments found worldwide was in Japan (266.5μg/g).13 Studies sampling Canadian house dust found that over 50% of samples contained at least 19 distinct OPEs, 8 of which have a detection frequency of 100%.7,8 Dust, including OPEs, can enter human tissues through multiple pathways, such as inhalation, dermal absorption, and ingestion.14 Estimations for daily intake (EDI) in humans via house dust varied by route of exposure and geographic location.15 Concerningly, literature overwhelmingly agrees that exposure also varies by life stage, with children having disproportionately higher EDIs than adults.1,16 OPEs distribute systemically and were detected in breast milk,16 urine,14 serum,14,17 as well as whole and cord blood,14 indicating that OPEs can cross the placental barrier.

Although the health hazards of OPE exposure lack comprehensive characterization, it is clear that multiple organ systems can be affected by OPEs.18–22 A few studies have reported that OPEs target the immune system, particularly macrophages. Macrophages play a vital role in the immune response by recognizing a wide range of pathogens through pattern recognition receptors, allowing them to swiftly respond to infectious agents, apoptotic cells, cellular debris, and tumors.23,24 As phagocytes, macrophages engage in phagocytosis to engulf dying cells and microbes. The engulfed cargo is either recycled or presented on the cell surface as antigens to activate the antigen-specific adaptive immune response.24,25

Two studies have documented that individual OPEs attenuate or intensify inflammatory responses in monocytes/macrophages.26,27 For example, antigen presentation on monocytes (precursors to macrophages) isolated from peripheral blood is decreased when treated with triphenyl phosphate (TPHP).26 Furthermore, some evidence suggests that OPEs have contrasting effects on the same end point under identical conditions. For instance, phagocytic efficiency was higher in THP-1 macrophages following exposure to tri-n-butyl phosphate and triorthocresyl phosphate (TOCP), whereas efficiency was lower following exposure to TPHP and tris(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate (TBOEP).27 Similarly, secretion of tumor necrosis factor -α (TNFα), a proinflammatory cytokine, was higher in THP-1 cells exposed to tris(1,3-dichloro-2-isopropyl) phosphate (TDCIPP) and TBOEP but lower than controls when treated with TOCP.27

These inconsistent findings create an inherent problem in assessing these chemicals, especially because they are found in mixtures. In real-world settings, multiple sources simultaneously leech OPEs, forming mixtures.7,17,28,29 The relative components of a mixture will dictate the direction and magnitude of response of examined end points. We hypothesize that a mixture of OPEs, representative of household dust, can help identify the environmentally relevant effect of OPEs on macrophages. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the effects of a mixture of OPEs commonly found in Canadian homes on THP-1 macrophages.7,8

Cell Line Culture

THP-1 (No. TIB-202) and Jurkat (No. TIB-152) cell lines were purchased from ATCC. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 without phenol red at 37°C in 5% CO2, and Jurkat cells were cultured in standard RPMI-1640. The media was supplemented with 10% charcoal-stripped FBS, 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin, and 5×10−5 M 2-mercaptoethanol. All the cells in this study were used within 1 month of thawing from cryogenic preservation. All cells were cultured at a cell density of 2.5–10×105 cells/mL. The culture medium was renewed every 2–3 d. Unless otherwise specified, all collections of THP-1 cells occurred via scraping. All centrifugations occurred at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. THP-1 macrophages are a well-characterized human macrophage model; the macrophages that are differentiated from the THP-1 monocytes reliably express the key markers and functions found in primary cells.30–32 THP-1 monocytes were treated with 75 ng/mL PMA for 120 h at 37°C in 5% CO2 to initiate macrophage differentiation with media replacement after 72 h. This is a modification on the procedure outlined in Daigneault et al.33

hPBMCs Isolation and Culture

All procedures involving human blood collections were approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee at West Central Montreal Health (CIUSSS, protocol No. 12-026). Human peripheral blood from healthy males and females 22–28 y of age was collected in EDTA-coated BD Vacutainer plasma tubes (BD BioScience, No. 366473). Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) were isolated from whole blood; 21mL of whole blood was centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. Plasma was removed, and the buffy coat was collected and treated with 50mL red blood cell lysis buffer (10× concentrate=41.5g NH4Cl, 5g KHCO3, 1mL, 0.5M, pH 8.0 EDTA, ddH2O to 500mL, pH 7.4) for purification. Cells were centrifuged at 800×g for 10 min at room temperature and then washed twice with 50mL PBS before resuspension in RPMI-1640 without phenol red. After seeding, hPBMCs were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 1 h, after which the media was changed to RPMI-1640 without phenol red containing 25 ng/mL human Macrophage Colony Stimulating Factor (M-CSF; No. 300-25; Peprotech). Media was changed on day 2 with RPMI-1640 without phenol red containing 25 ng/mL human M-CSF. Differentiation was complete on day 6. All collections of hPBMC-derived macrophages after differentiation occurred via scraping. All centrifugations occurred at 1,500 rpm for 5 min.

High-Content Imaging

THP-1 monocytes (8,000/well) were seeded in black opaque 96-well plates (No. 6055300; Perkin Elmer) coated with 50μg/mL Collagen I (No. A1048301; Gibco). The monocytes were differentiated into THP-1 macrophages prior to OPE exposure. The stock solution of the mixture was diluted in complete media to create treatment groups (1:10,000; 1:20,000; 1:50,000; 1:100,000; 1:300,000; 1:1,000,000); 0.5% DMSO was used as a vehicle control. DMSO was added to each of the OPE treatment groups to adjust the final concentration of DMSO in the dilutions to 0.5%. Cells were treated in technical triplicates.

To determine the effects of OPE exposure on cell phenotypes, THP-1 macrophages were stained post treatment (4 h, 24 h, or 48 h; non-consecutive groups) with cell-permeable fluorescent dyes suitable for live-cell imaging (Table S2). Cells were stained for 30 min at 37°C in 5% CO2 before being washed three times with serum-free RPMI-1640. The Operetta High-Content Imaging System (Perkin Elmer) was used for live-cell imaging (12 fields/well, 4 planes/well, 40× magnification). Images were analyzed using the Columbus Image Data Storage and Analysis System (Perkin Elmer).

Confocal Microscopy–Lipid Droplets

The number and volume of lipid droplets was analyzed using confocal microscopy. In brief, THP-1 macrophages (2×105 cells/well) and hPBMCs (5.4×106 cells/well) were differentiated in a 12-well plate containing an ethanol-sterilized coverslip (No. 64-0733; Warner Instruments). After complete differentiation, THP-1 macrophages were treated for 48 h with either 0.5% DMSO, the 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture, or 250μM oleic acid, a standard control included to assess the accuracy of the image analysis. Human PBMC derived macrophages were treated for 48 h with 25 ng/mL human M-CSF concomitant with 0.1% DMSO, the 1:50,000 dilution of the OPE mixture, or the 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture. Following treatment, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) before staining with a combination of BODIPY 493/503 and Hoechst 33342 (Table S2) for 20 min at 37°C. Cells were then washed 5 times with PBS and mounted with prolong gold antifade liquid mountant (P36930; Thermo Fisher). THP-1 macrophages were imaged on the Leica TCS SP8 with 15–35 z-stacks per image to develop three-dimensional renderings of the cells (Leica). Experiments were done with technical duplicates. The Imaris software (version 9.5; Oxford Instruments) was used to quantify lipid droplet numbers and volume. Human PBMCs were imaged on the Zeiss LSM800 (Zeiss).

Measurement of Intracellular Cholesterol

Total and free cholesterol in THP-1 macrophages were measured using a commercial kit (STA-384; Cell Biolabs). THP-1 macrophages were differentiated and then treated for 48 h with either vehicle control (0.5% DMSO) or the OPE mixture (dilutions=1:1,000,000; 1:50,000; 1:20,000). An internal control was done by adding 4μg/mL 22R-hydroxycholesterol for 18 h. After treatment, cells were collected and then 5×105 cells were centrifuged, washed in PBS, and then lysed using a 7:11:0.1 solution of chloroform (CX1005-2; EMD), isopropanol (A416-1; Fisher), and IGEPAL CA630 (No. 18896; Sigma-Aldrich) concomitant with sonication. The organic layer was isolated and concentrated in a Centrivap Console (Labconco) by centrifuging in a 60°C vacuum chamber for 30 min at 1,725 rpm. The organic condensate was resuspended and loaded into a 96-well plate. Total and free cholesterol were measured using a SpectraMax Plus 384 microplate reader at 540 nm (Molecular Devices). Raw values were calculated using a cholesterol standard curve. Both the standard curve and sample measurements were done in duplicate.

Phagocytosis–Efferocytosis Assay

A total of 56×106 Jurkat T cells in PBS were exposed to 254 nm ultraviolet (UV) light inside a Logic+ class A2 biological safety cabinet (Labconco) for 15 min. followed by a 5-h incubation in 5% FBS RPMI-1640 media at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2 to induce apoptosis. After 5 h, the apoptotic Jurkat cells were fluorescently labeled with 5μM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) for 20 min at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2 (Table S2). 2×106 Jurkat cells were then added to cultures of THP-1 macrophages (2×106 THP-1/10 cm2 plate) or hPBMC derived macrophages (10.8×106 hPBMCs/well in 6-well plates) that were differentiated and treated with either a vehicle control (0.5% DMSO) or the OPE mixture (1:50,000; 1:20,000) for 46 h prior to co-culture. After a 2 h co-culture incubation at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2, the cells were washed 2 times with PBS to remove the non-adherent Jurkat cells. THP-1 macrophages were then collected, centrifuged, and resuspended in a near-infrared (NIR) live–dead stain (Table S2) for 30 min on ice to obtain viable cells for analysis. The cells were washed once with PBS and resuspended in 1% PFA for fixation before storage at 4°C. THP-1 macrophages were analyzed using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (Lady Davis Institute Flow Cytometry Core; BD BioScience). Human PBMC-derived macrophages were analyzed using a BD LSR Fortessa (Lady Davis Institute Flow Cytometry Core; BD BioScience). Macrophages that had engulfed apoptotic Jurkat cells were distinguished by negative staining for a membrane-impermeable live–dead stain (NIR) and positive staining for CFSE. Experiments using THP-1 were completed as technical duplicates.

Phagocytosis–Efferocytic Receptor Expression

THP-1 macrophages (6×106 cells/plate) were treated for 48 h with either a vehicle control (0.5% DMSO) or the OPE mixture (1:50,000; 1:20,000). After treatment, THP-1 macrophages were collected and stained with the NIR live–dead stain as previously described. The cells were then washed with PBS before staining with antibodies against CD36, SCARB1, CD14, MerTK, and SIRPα (Table S3) for 30 min on ice, washed with FACS buffer (PBS, 5% FBS, 0.1% sodium azide), fixed in 1% PFA, resuspended in FACS buffer, and protected from light until analysis. The BD FACS Canto II or BD LSR Fortessa (BD BioScience) was used for analysis.

Phagocytosis–Bacterial Engulfment

To determine whether OPEs disrupt the engulfment of bacterial pathogens, we employed two models of bacterial phagocytosis. We purchased Escherichia coli (No. E13231) and Staphylococcus aureus (No. S2337I), both heat-killed, and fluorescently labeled with Alexa Flour 488 (AF488; Thermo Fisher). Following differentiation, THP-1 macrophages (2×106 cells/plate) were treated with either a vehicle control or the OPE mixture (1:50,000; 1:20,000) for 46 h. At that time, 3μg/mL fluorescent E. coli or S. aureus were added. After a 2-h co-culture incubation at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2, the cells were collected and stained as previously described in the efferocytosis assay. A BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) was used to detect cells that were positive for AF488. All experiments were completed as technical duplicates.

Macrophage Migration

The migratory potential of bone marrow–derived macrophages was assessed using a wound-healing assay. These experiments, using primary mouse cell cultures, were done in accordance with Animal Use Protocol (AUP 5664), approved by the McGill University Animal Care Committee, and they comply with ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were housed in the animal facility of the Lady Davis Institute for Medical Research. Bone marrow was isolated from the tibias and femurs of male C57BL/6 mice 12–13 wk of age as described previously.34 All mice were anesthetized using isoflurane before CO2 asphyxiation. Cells (1.25×106) were plated in RPMI-1640 without phenol red in a 12-well plate and incubated at 37ºC in 5% CO2 to allow for monocyte adhesion. After 45 min, the non-adhering cells and media were removed and replaced with fresh media containing 50 ng/mL M-CSF to induce differentiation over 5 d, with a media change on day 2. In addition to 50 ng/mL M-CSF, vehicle control (0.1% DMSO) or the OPE mixture (dilutions=1:50,000; 1:20,000) were added on day 5. After 48 h, a scratch was made in the confluent cell layer of each well using a p200 pipette tip. The treated media was removed and replaced with new media with 50 ng/mL M-CSF before imaging. Following the scratch, the macrophages were imaged for 24 h in 4-h increments using the Incucyte live cell analysis system (Sartorius). Relative wound density, which is the cell density (number of cells over a given area) in the wound area relative to the cell density outside of the wound area at each time point, was calculated and analyzed using the method described by Grada et al. without deviation.35 The images obtained were assessed using National Institutes of Health (NIH) Image J (version 1.53t) to determine wound and total area and cell numbers through segmentation. All experiments were completed and analyzed as technical triplicates.

Statistical Analyses

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1; GraphPad Software, Inc.). The number of independent experiments (experiments conducted on different days) is documented as “n=” for each assay in the corresponding figure legend for results using THP-1 macrophages. In human primary cells, “n=” refers to samples from different donors that are biologically distinct. Where technical duplicates or triplicates are used, the average of the technical replicates is equal to one independent experiment. Holm-Bonferroni corrected one-sample t-tests were used to assess normalized data. Two- or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used on non-normalized data followed by multiple comparison testing. The minimum level of significance was set at p<0.05. All datasets were tested for outliers using a Grubbs statistical outlier test. All outliers were removed prior to statistical analyses.

Results

Effects of an OPE Mixture on Cytotoxicity and Morphology of THP-1 Macrophages

We hypothesized that exposure to an OPE mixture would modify macrophage biology. To assess multiple biological parameters, we conducted high-content imaging of THP-1 macrophages over 48 h. To determine appropriate dilutions of the OPE mixture for analysis, we assessed the cytotoxicity of the mixture in THP-1 macrophages using the fluorescent live cell dye Calcein AM. Cells exposed to the OPE mixture at dilutions ranging from 1:1,000,000 to 1:20,000 did not exhibit a significant loss of viability after 48 h (Figure 1A). However, we observed only significantly lower viability following a 48 h exposure to the 1:10,000 OPE dilution, which was the only dilution with resultant cell death >30% of controls (Figure 1A,B). Cells exposed to the subcytotoxic range of dilutions did not show significant differences in cellular or nuclear area in comparison with the vehicle control (Figure 1C,D).

Effects of the OPE Mixture on ROS

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) act as markers of aberrant cellular signaling and oxidative stress, and we can visualize them using the fluorescent probe CellROX Deep Red.36 Both the control and treated groups of THP-1 macrophages displayed a predominantly diffuse staining pattern, with some accumulation of CellROX in high-intensity spots (Figure 2A). The fluorescent intensity of CellROX was significantly higher following an acute (4 h) exposure to the OPE mixture, ranging from 139% to 157% of the control (Figure 2). The fluorescence gradually returned to control levels by 48 h (Figure 2A). An interesting finding was that the intensity of CellROX remained high after 48 h for the lowest dilution of 1:20,000, maintaining a level equivalent to the measurement taken from the 24 h group. A two-way ANOVA analysis of these data indicated the significance of the main effect of time (p<0.01) and the interaction between time and OPE dilution (p<0.01).

Effects of the OPE Mixture on Mitochondria

To identify a potential source of ROS, we first examined the number and activity of mitochondria, as changes in these factors can be associated with ROS production.37 In THP-1 macrophages, cells exposed to the OPE mixture did not differ significantly in staining intensity or pattern of MitoTracker Green (total mitochondria) or Red (mitochondrial membrane potential, Figure S1A–D) from the controls. We also evaluated the ratio of MitoTracker Red to Green and found no significant difference (Figure S1E).

Effects of the OPE Mixture on Lysosomes

Aspects of lysosomal dysfunction are also linked to excessive ROS.38–40 We assessed changes in the overall presence of lysosomes using LysoTracker red, which appeared as distinct spots within the cytosol (Figure 3A). Analysis of the high-content images revealed two distinct patterns of OPE-induced lysosomal changes. Through a two-way ANOVA, we determined that these patterns depended on the main effect of time (p<0.01) and the interaction between time and OPE dilution (p<0.01). For dilutions ranging from 1:1,000,000 to 1:50,000, LysoTracker intensity was higher than control after 4 h of exposure, but then lower after 24 h (1:1,000,000) or 48 h (1:300,000 to 1:50,000) h (Figure 3B). For all dilutions from 1:1,000,000 to 1:50,000, the initial effect of the OPE mixtures reversed, with LysoTracker intensity measuring 66%–82% of the control intensity after 48 h (Figure 3B). In contrast, treatment with a 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture showed an opposite pattern of differences in LysoTracker intensity in comparison with higher dilutions. LysoTracker intensity was initially lower than the vehicle-matched control after 4 h, but steadily increased with longer time, reaching 135% of the control level at 48 h (Figure 3B).

Effects of the OPE Mixture on Lipid Content

In addition to enhanced ROS, previous studies have reported another OPE-induced phenotype: lipid retention.20,22,41 We used Nile red to label lipid droplets and hydrophobic membranes. Nile red–positive staining manifested as clusters of spots in the cytoplasm (representing lipid droplets) and diffuse staining on cellular membranes (Figure 4A). Analyzing these data with a two-way ANOVA revealed that the duration of exposure to the OPE mixture significantly contributed to the variability in the results (p<0.01 for the main effect of time). No differences from control were observed in lipid content after 4 h or 24 h of exposure. However, a significantly greater Nile red intensity was recorded after 48 h. For dilutions lower than 1:1,000,000, the difference in lipid content demonstrated a dose–response relationship (p<0.01 for the main effect of OPE dilution and interaction) rising to a maximum of 146% of the control level (Figure 4B).

The images obtained from the Nile red high-content staining suggest that the higher fluorescent intensity observed in OPE-treated macrophages results from changes in lipid droplets within the macrophage cytosol. To test this hypothesis, we used confocal microscopy and used BODIPY 493/503, a marker for the neutral lipids present in lipid droplets.42 Examination of the three-dimensional renderings showed that the droplet volume in THP-1 macrophages exposed to the OPE mixture did not significantly differ from the controls, despite observing an increase after exposure to oleic acid, a positive control (Figure 4C,D). However, we found that treatment with the OPE mixture significantly augmented the number of lipid droplets within THP-1 macrophages (Figure 4E). Lipid droplets were enlarged and more numerous after exposure to oleic acid, a positive control (Figure 4C,E). It was remarkable that, following exposure to the 1:20,000 OPE mixture, the number of cytoplasmic lipid droplets was higher than the number of droplets observed in the oleic acid condition. This change suggests that OPEs exert a significant impact on lipid homeostasis in macrophages.

Evaluation of Cholesterol in OPE-Exposed THP-1 Macrophages

In THP-1 macrophages, lipid droplets are largely composed of neutral cholesterol.43 We evaluated both total cholesterol (free and neutral) and free cholesterol in THP-1 macrophages following a 48 h exposure to either a vehicle control or dilutions (1:1,000,000; 1:50,000; 1:20,000) of the OPE mixture. The 1:1,000,000 OPE dilution did not produce any measurable impact on total or free cholesterol levels (Figure 4F,G). However, treatment with the 1:50,000 and 1:20,000 dilutions of the OPE mixture resulted in higher total cholesterol relative to control, with fold changes of 1.29 and 1.39, respectively (Figure 4F). Furthermore, both the 1:50,000 and 1:20,000 dilutions of the OPE mixture resulted in significant elevations in free cholesterol, with fold changes of 1.32 and 1.52, respectively (Figure 4G).

The Role of the OPE Mixture on Efferocytosis

A functional deficit associated with intracellular cholesterol accumulation is a reduction in the recognition and engulfment of apoptotic cells by macrophages.44 This process, known as efferocytosis, is vital for maintaining healthy tissue and represents a critical macrophage function.45 Following a 48 h exposure to either the 1:50,000 or 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture, the percentage of macrophages that contained engulfed apoptotic cells was significantly lower in comparison with the control (Figure 5A,B). In addition, within the NIR−CFSE+ macrophage subset, exposure to both the 1:50,000 and 1:20,000 OPE dilutions displayed a significantly lower CFSE intensity (Figure S2). Because CFSE intensity is proportional to the number of ingested apoptotic cells, these data support the hypothesis that OPEs decrease efferocytic capacity.

Mechanistic Insights into the Suppression of Efferocytosis

The accumulation of intracellular cholesterol in plasma membranes can slow or halt macrophage migration and subsequently decrease efferocytosis.46 To determine whether OPE exposure slows cellular migration, we conducted a wound-healing assay to monitor the migration of murine bone marrow–derived macrophages at 4 h intervals for 24 h. However, we found that a 48 h pretreatment with either the 1:50,000 or 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture did not result in any observable differences in macrophage migration in comparison with the controls (Figure S4).

A modification in the surface receptors involved in efferocytic signaling offers an alternative explanation for the decline in efferocytosis. To investigate this, we used flow cytometry to quantify the expression of phosphatidylserine receptors (SCARB1, CD36, CD14, and MerTK) on the cell surface of THP-1 macrophages. We also assessed SIRPα, an inhibitory regulator of efferocytosis. Exposure to the 1:50,000 dilution of the OPE mixture did not significantly modify the expression of SCARB1, CD36, CD14, MerTK, or SIRPα, although the fold change for MerTK expression was 0.78 of the vehicle-matched control (Figure 5C; Figure S3). Similarly, the 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture did not alter the expression of SCARB1 or SIRPα (Figure S3). However, exposure to the 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture resulted in higher CD36 expression and lower expression of CD14 and MerTK (Figure 5C).

Effect of the OPE Mixture on Bacterial Phagocytosis

In addition to its role in efferocytosis, CD14 plays a role in the recognition and engulfment of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.47,48 Vehicle-treated THP-1 macrophages exhibited a strong fluorescent signal from engulfed AF488-S. aureus (Figure S4). In contrast, THP-1 macrophages treated with the OPE mixture showed a diminished fluorescent signal, indicating a lower percentage of THP-1 macrophages containing AF488-S. aureus. Macrophages exposed to either the 1:50,000 or the 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture exhibited significantly lower AF488-S. aureus fluorescent signal in comparison with control (Figure 5D). Similarly, vehicle-treated THP-1 macrophages co-cultured with AF488-E.coli exhibited a distinct difference in fluorescent signal in comparison with their OPE-treated counterparts (Figure S4). However, only exposure to the 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture resulted in a significant loss in AF488-E.coli engulfment (Figure 5E).

Effect of the OPE Mixture on Human Primary Macrophages

To confirm our findings in primary human cells, we generated macrophages from six different donors (three male, three female). OPE-exposed hPBMC-derived macrophages were in parallel either stained with BODIPY 493/503 or used in functional studies to validate efferocytic outcomes. An increase in lipid content can be visually observed in four out of six independent donors (Figure 6A; donors 1, 2, 4, 5; two males and two females). This increase was most visually apparent in the 1:20,000 group but can also be observed in the 1:50,000 group. BODIPY fluorescence was noticeably lower in macrophages from two of six independent donors (Figure 6A; donors 3 and 6), which appeared to have fewer lipid droplets when exposed to the 1:20,000 dilution of the OPE mixture in comparison with those from other donors. When assessed in functional assays, a lower geometric mean fluorescent intensity (gMFI) for CFSE (average decrease=42%) was detected in four of six donors when exposed to the 1:20,000 OPE dilution, whereas two of six donors did not display any differences from control in CFSE gMFI (Figure 6B). It is interesting to note that the same two donors who demonstrated a muted lipid response also exhibited few to no differences from control during the assessment of efferocytic outcomes after OPE exposure. Overall, macrophages from most donors recapitulated the THP-1 phenotype and demonstrated a conserved outcome in primary cells.

Discussion

OPEs, a class of persistent indoor pollutants, modulate the function of immune cells in vitro and in vivo.26,27,41,50–55 An interesting finding is that individual OPEs with similar structures can either suppress or exacerbate the same immune responses in macrophages.27 This differing effect poses a challenge for regulators because indoor OPE exposure primarily occurs through house dust,7,8,14 which contains a mixture of OPEs. Therefore, we hypothesized that a Canadian household dust-based OPE mixture would dysregulate the phenotype and functions of THP-1 macrophages. In these studies, we observed three phenotypes related to exposure to the OPE mixture: ROS generation, lysosomal dysfunction, and lipid retention. Furthermore, we discovered a correlation between lipid retention, higher levels of cholesterol, and the number of lipid droplets. Functionally, these findings suggest that the OPE mixture suppresses two subtypes of phagocytosis (efferocytosis and bacterial). Together, these data suggest that mixtures of OPEs are immunomodulatory and impair the normal functioning of macrophages.

ROS levels following exposure to the OPE mixture were significantly higher at 4 h and 24 h, as indicated by CellROX intensity in THP-1 macrophages. Our findings were similar to results reported in RAW264.7 murine macrophages, where both high and low doses of TDCIPP resulted in comparable levels of ROS.56 Notably, the elevated ROS levels were lower, similar to controls, at the 48 h mark, suggesting a potential up-regulation of antioxidants and/or inhibitory checkpoints that mitigate ROS levels and prevent oxidative damage. We investigated potential sources of OPE-induced ROS. An interesting finding was that although exposure to individual OPEs lowers mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), resulting in higher mitochondrial ROS levels,18,19,22,57,58 the mixture of OPEs did not affect MMP (as observed using MitoTracker Red) or the overall number of mitochondria (as observed using MitoTracker Green). These findings highlight the significance of studying OPEs as a mixture. In an environmentally relevant model, the concentration of mitochondria-toxic OPEs may not be effective or may be counteracted by OPEs with preferential selectivity but limited potency. Furthermore, these data suggest that the OPE-induced ROS we observed is generated through an alternate organelle. The NADPH oxidase (NOX) family of enzymes produces intracellular ROS that fulfills important roles in inflammatory signaling through pathways such as hypoxia inducible factor 1α.59 The early onset of non-mitochondrial ROS following OPE exposure with decline over time may indicate the induction of ROS-mediated signaling pathways and subsequent regulation with prolonged exposure. Alternatively, peroxisomes abundantly produce hydrogen peroxide through metabolic regulation of β-oxidation and cholesterol metabolism.60 This could provide a mechanistic link to the lipid phenotype wherein peroxisome activity offsets lipid buildup during acute exposure which accumulates following the exhaustion of lipid metabolism.

Lysosome coalescence under normal circumstances reduces the endogenous number of lysosomes available.38–40 This process has been shown to be inhibited by ROS through mechanisms such as fragmentation and impaired fusogenicity resulting in an overall increase in lysosomes.38–40 Therefore, we predicted a lower incidence of lysosome fusion and subsequent lysosome accumulation. LysoTracker staining was elevated during early time points, then decreased, coinciding with the rise and fall of OPE-induced ROS. This finding suggests that continuous exposure to the OPE mixture initially accumulates lysosomes through an ROS-dependent effect; however, once ROS are cleared, normal coalescence resumes decreasing LysoTracker intensity. Notably, this response was not true at the lowest dilutions of the OPE mixture, where LysoTracker levels were lower during heightened ROS and higher after OPE-induced ROS subsides. The differential response of lysosomes to the OPE mixture is an area for further exploration as it may suggest an additional or alternative concentration dependent effect.

When THP-1 macrophages were exposed to the OPE mixture for 48 h, a dose–response relationship was observed between OPE concentration and the intensity of Nile red, a marker of total lipid content.61 Staining with BODIPY 493/50, which specifically labels lipid droplets, revealed a correlation between total lipid content and the number of lipid droplets but not their volume. In murine macrophages, TPHP exposure was found to accumulate lipid droplets through decreased cholesterol efflux.41 Analysis of cholesterol levels in THP-1 macrophages exposed to the OPE mixture supported a dose–responsive increase in cellular cholesterol. These findings support our previous data and hypothesis that the lipid retained in THP-1 macrophages is cholesterol-based.

In vivo, dysregulation of lipid metabolism has been reported in metabolomic data from the serum of mice exposed to TPHP or tributyl phosphate (TNBP).62–64 These studies found fatty acids to be the dominant dysregulated lipid species in TPHP-exposed mice62,63; however, serum cholesterol was also found to increase with higher concentrations of TPHP. In opposition, exposure to TNBP resulted in lower circulating fatty acid levels in serum.64 The OPE mixture used in our study contains both compounds. Thus, the mechanism(s) underlying mixture-induced dyslipidemia via lipid droplet formation are likely to be multifaceted and active across diverse biological machinery.

Some of the differences in how OPEs differentially influence lipid metabolism may be a result of their chemical structure. The intracellular partition of OPEs in mammalian cells following exposure is unknown; however, agricultural studies have demonstrated that lipophilic OPEs are enriched in cell walls, whereas OPEs with low lipophilicity are retained at higher levels extracellularly.65–67 The OPE mixture used in this study contained compounds with both low and high lipophilicity (Table S1). It is reasonable to assume that OPEs within the mixture will not have equal penetrance for cellular membranes. OPEs preferential to relatively polar environments may act on targets located on the extracellular membrane where they are enriched. This action may occur in contrast or additive to OPEs associated with high lipophilicity that may selectively accumulate in and target lipid-rich organelles, such as the droplets themselves. In this manner the mixture could have multiple mechanisms, with the net result being lipid accumulation.

The accumulation of lipids in macrophages plays a crucial role in suppressing efferocytosis, the phagocytosis of apoptotic cells.44,68 To our knowledge, we are the first to report that exposure of THP-1 macrophages to OPEs leads to a dose–dependent reduction in the engulfment of apoptotic cells. However, the relationship between lipid accumulation and efferocytosis remains correlative. One possibility is that the sequestration of cholesterol in macrophage plasma membranes reorganizes the cytoskeleton, impairing migration.46,69 Our initial attempts using the THP-1 cell line to induce migration were unsuccessful (Figure S5). Therefore, we chose to use the bone marrow–derived macrophage model, which has been previously successful in motility assays70,71 and exhibited a similar lipid phenotype on exposure to OPEs (Figure S6). Studies with this model did not find any statistical relationship between OPE exposure and macrophage migration (Figure S7). This finding suggests that migration is not a driving factor in the lower efferocytic response we observed. In interpreting these results, one should consider that the use of primary murine cells constitutes a change in species with unknown confounds potentially resulting in type II error.

Phosphatidylserine (PS) is expressed on all apoptotic cells and is recognized by a variety of functionally active receptors, predominantly expressed by phagocytes.72 In THP-1 macrophages exposed to the OPE mixture, the expression of CD14 and MerTK, both of which recognize PS, was lower than the vehicle-matched control. It is interesting to note that the expression of CD36, which also recognizes PS, was higher following exposure to the OPE mixture. MerTK is believed to play a greater role in the recognition of apoptotic cells, thus explaining the OPE-induced impairment of efferocytosis, despite higher expression of CD36.72

The lower expression of CD14 noted following exposure to the OPE mixture raises additional concerns regarding bacterial defense. CD14 binds teichoic acids and lipopolysaccharides, along with toll-like receptor 2 and 4, activating signaling pathways that lead to the engulfment of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.47–49 A loss of CD14 would therefore be expected to slow bacterial phagocytosis leading to higher rates of infection. Our results support this prediction, because phagocytosis of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria by THP-1 cells was lower in OPE-treated macrophages than in the control.

The differences from control in CD36, CD14, and MerTK suggest a potential transcriptional connection between phagocytic suppression and lipid accumulation. OPEs are known to interact with several nuclear receptors, including peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ (PPARγ)73,74 and liver X receptor α (LXRα),41 which transcriptionally regulate CD36 and CD14/MerTK, respectively.75–77 The expected agonistic effect of PPARγ and antagonistic effect of LXRα by different components within the mixture41,73 would explain the observed changes in CD36, CD14, and MerTK. CD36 plays a role in cholesterol uptake, whereas LXRα is the master regulator of cholesterol efflux, linking the functional deficits to the dysregulated lipid phenotype.

Results from primary macrophages also suggest a link between dyslipidemia and poor efferocytic response. It was observed that the two donors who did not display gross dyslipidemia were the same donors who had no functional deficits in the efferocytosis assay in cells that were isolated, differentiated, and treated in parallel. In every other case, the cells that displayed lipid accumulation also responded with lower functionality.

An alternative explanation could be that the decrease in efferocytosis is induced though polarization of THP-1 macrophages to a classically activated M1 state. Classically activated M1 macrophages display a reduced capacity to detect and respond to apoptotic cells, which would be in line with our results.78 Furthermore, TPHP has previously been suggested to promote an M1 phenotype in RAW264.7 macrophages at concentrations >25μM.79 However, this theory is not supported by current evidence. M1 classical activation resulted in enhanced CD14 surface expression and promotes bacterial phagocytosis,80 which was not observed following treatment with the OPE mixture.

Several of our findings are indicative of poor health outcomes. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) in adults is positively associated with mixtures of OPEs; a predicted consequence of suppressed efferocytosis and lipid retention in macrophages.20 In addition, our results forecast higher rates of infection through the suppression of phagocytosis. To our knowledge, no animal or human studies have been completed that explore this aspect of OPE toxicology. However, epidemiological and in vitro evidence suggests that TDCIPP is associated with pathogenic changes in microbiota, which could indicate suppressed immunosurveillance by intestinal macrophages.81

It is important to note that the composition of OPE mixtures found in homes varies based on geographic location. Therefore, the results obtained in our study may not be consistent with a different mixture. It is also important to recognize that the levels of OPEs in human tissues are likely to be lower than the environmental measurements on which the mixture concentrations are based. Modeling biological levels of OPE exposure is complicated by covariates with prominent factors including age, location, and unequal distribution. For example, children have higher levels of urinary metabolites for OPEs than adults,1,15 but children from the United States have different urinary levels than children from China.82,83 In addition, these studies only account for urine metabolites, which may not accurately capture OPEs accumulated in lipid rich tissues such as adipose.3 As such, there is currently no consistent way to model OPE exposure at a biological level, and this remains a limitation of the field at large. It is important to note that these studies were conducted on PMA-stimulated THP-1 macrophages that endogenously retain an M1 polarized bias after differentiation, which may drive certain interactions with the OPE mixture.33 These studies were also completed in vitro and may differ in effect from in vivo models. Despite these limitations, our results provide strong evidence that mixtures of OPEs can influence the biology of macrophages and suggest the potential for impact on human health.
