Cerebellar contributions to action and cognition: Prediction, timescale, and continuity
Authors: Jonathan S. Tsay, Richard B. Ivry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The cerebellum is implicated in nearly every domain of human cognition, yet our understanding of how this subcortical structure contributes to cognition remains elusive. Efforts on this front have tended to fall into one of two camps. On one side are those who seek to identify a universal cerebellar transform, a single algorithm that can be applied across domains as diverse as sensorimotor learning, social cognition, and decision-making. On the other side are those who focus on functional specializations tailored for different task domains. In this perspective, we propose an integrated approach, one that recognizes functional specialization across different cerebellar subregions, but also builds on common constraints that help define the conditions that engage the cerebellum. Drawing on recurring principles from the cerebellum’s well-established role in motor control, we identify three core constraints: 1) Prediction—the cerebellum performs anticipatory, simulation-based computations; 2) Timescale—the cerebellum generates predictions limited to short intervals; and 3) Continuity—the cerebellum facilitates the manipulation of mental representations in a continuous, but not discrete manner. Together, these constraints define the boundary conditions underlying how the cerebellum supports cognition, and, just as importantly, specify the types of computations that should not depend on the cerebellum.

The Cognitive Cerebellum

Inspired by the parallel expansion of the prefrontal cortex and lateral cerebellum, Leiner, Leiner, and Dow offered the conjecture that the cerebellum helps coordinate mental processing, similar to how this subcortical structure helps coordinate movement (1). Since their seminal paper, a diverse literature has emerged over the past 40 y implicating the cerebellum in a broad array of cognitive, social, and affective function (2–5). This work has led, for example, to the establishment of a new clinical diagnostic category, Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome (CCAS) (6, 7), in recognition of the symptoms that can be observed in patients with neurological disorders of the cerebellum. Complementing the work involving human participants, there has been a surge in studies with nonhuman species to explore the circuitry and physiological processes that underlie the contribution of the cerebellum to nonmotor behavior such as spatial cognition, social cognition, and reinforcement learning (8–10).

Much of the literature has been anchored to a binary question, “Is the cerebellum involved in X?,” with X spanning domains such as language, attention, working memory, social cognition, and emotional regulation. Similarly, the items on the CCAS battery have been selected because of their diagnostic value in evaluating impairments in these domains (6). Studies using functional neuroimaging or neurophysiological recordings have probed how information is encoded in the cerebellum, with the results providing detailed maps depicting functional specialization in nonmotor domains across much of the cerebellar cortex (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) (5, 11).

While this work has certainly led to an appreciation of the broad functional domain of the cerebellum, the field still struggles to understand how the cerebellum contributes to cognition. We have a large catalog showing that the cerebellum is engaged in a broad range of tasks but have made limited progress in deepening our understanding, be it models that describe computational principles at the psychological level or detail how these computations emerge from the physiological activity of the cerebellum.

In seeking to understand how the cerebellum supports cognition, many researchers have drawn inspiration from the idea that computations supporting motor control might extend to cognition. This approach is motivated by the strikingly uniform architecture of the cerebellar cortex (12). Although there are variations across subregions (13), the basic cell types and connectivity patterns have been maintained over the long evolutionary history of the cerebellum. A second motivation comes from anatomy: the cerebellum forms recurrent loops not only with sensorimotor regions but also with association areas of the cerebral cortex including the prefrontal cortex (14). Together, these observations have led to the proposal that computational principles derived from cerebellar architecture—and well characterized in motor control—may also serve as candidate mechanisms for mental coordination and nonmotor learning.

These computational principles are most clearly articulated in what is commonly referred to as the Marr–Albus–Ito model. Impressed by the massive degree of divergence and convergence in the cerebellar cortex, Marr proposed that the cerebellum operates as a pattern recognition system (15). Focusing on sensorimotor control, pattern recognition would be essential for adaptive behavior, specifying the context that enables organisms to produce a near-infinite set of movements (16, 17). For example, detecting whether one is walking on ice or solid ground is critical for adjusting movement kinematics to prevent a fall (18).

Building on Marr’s theory, Albus and Ito proposed a learning scheme in which the climbing fibers, axons of neurons in the inferior olive, provide error information to shape how the Purkinje cells respond to the descending and ascending input arising from the pons, signals assumed to convey information about the current state and forthcoming movement (19, 20). This contextual information can be used to predict the sensory consequences of an upcoming action—an operation commonly referred to as a forward model (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) (21). These predictions modulate downstream motor circuits, allowing movements to be rapidly coordinated without relying on delayed sensory feedback. Interneurons within the cerebellar cortex refine the timing of these predictions, ensuring they influence behavior precisely when needed (13). When the sensory outcome deviates from the predicted outcome, the activation of the climbing fibers relays both instructional and error signals (22, 23) which can trigger immediate corrective responses and induce plasticity within the cerebellar circuit (24) to update the forward model. These processes incrementally refine behavior, helping ensure that movements are performed accurately in a continuous, coordinated manner (25).

Extending the principles of the Marr-Albus-Ito model to cognition has led to the hypothesis that the cerebellum builds forward models that run internal simulations to generate domain-general predictions (3, 21, 26, 27). For example, in the language domain, semantic representations in the cerebellum could be used to generate predictions regarding the upcoming words in an utterance (28, 29). In the social domain, the cerebellum might generate a forward model of the internal state of another agent and anticipate their behavior during a social interaction (9, 30).

While the generalized prediction hypothesis offers a compelling starting point, the mapping of core principles between motor and cognition is not straightforward. Consider the learning process: In motor control, the cerebellum receives an efference copy of the motor command and, in combination with inputs specifying the current context, generates a prediction of the expected consequences of that movement. Learning within this system is driven by sensory prediction errors, the difference between the expected and actual sensory outcome. These errors have a clear vector, which, through gradient descent, can be used to guide corrections that progressively improve the action [see reviews: (31)].

This idea can be naturally extended to social cognition, where the relevant inputs include the current context and the states of other agents. A forward model could generate predictions about others’ behavior—for example, anticipating whether a friend encountered at the airport will greet you with a hug or a kiss. A mismatch between the expected and actual outcome (e.g., anticipating a hug but receiving a kiss) would constitute a social prediction error.

However, whereas sensory prediction errors in motor control update parameters along continuous dimensions (e.g., direction, force), the representational structure of social prediction errors remains underspecified. What is the “vector” of a social prediction error: a kinematic mismatch in another agent’s actions, a deviation in affective valence, or some combination of both? And how does such an error specify the change required to improve future responses, for example, whether an unexpected kiss should bias future behavior toward reciprocating a kiss, or instead prompt an adjustment in posture to signal a preference for a hug? At present, both the structure of these representations and the learning rules governing the cerebellum’s role in social cognition remain poorly defined.

A broader concern with the generalized prediction hypothesis is that predictive processing is not unique to the cerebellum. Indeed, as exemplified in the Predictive Coding Framework, prediction is viewed as the canonical computation of the brain (32), one that is performed at all stages of the neural hierarchy. As such, if prediction is the hallmark of all neural processing, the key question then becomes: What is unique about cerebellar-dependent predictions?

When applied to nonmotor functions of the cerebellum, the concept of prediction is often invoked in an underspecified manner. For instance, it is reasonable to suppose that the ability to anticipate future states will enhance “mental coordination,” supporting cognitive processes across domains (2). Yet without clearly defined constraints, such claims risk becoming too broad for rigorous testing. What would disprove such a hypothesis? What are the cognitive symptoms of disrupted cerebellar predictions, and under what conditions do they emerge?

Equally important, specifying the constraints on cerebellar-dependent prediction should yield not only predictions about tasks impaired by cerebellar pathology or altered by cerebellar stimulation, but also about tasks that remain unaffected. In the rush to define the cerebellum’s role in cognition, spared functions are often overlooked. Yet these functions can be especially diagnostic of what makes cerebellar computation unique.

Constraints Governing Cerebellar Computation in Action and Cognition

What constraints govern cerebellar computation in action and cognition? To tackle this question, we consider the role of the cerebellum in motor control, identifying recurring principles that have emerged over decades of research. We use these constraints as a springboard to ask whether similar principles extend to cerebellar computation in a range of task domains.

Because sensory feedback from movement is delayed, the nervous system cannot operate as a closed-loop system for real-time motor control. To avoid the instabilities such delays would cause, the sensorimotor system must anticipate future states, ensuring a smooth, continuous transition from the current state to the desired goal (21). As described in Section I, the cerebellum is a core node in this control scheme, generating predictions about the sensory information that will arise from these upcoming state transitions (i.e., a forward model). To be effective, these predictions must integrate both time and space: Reaching for a water glass requires anticipating the sensory contact at a specific limb position and at a precise moment in time.

This model provides an elegant account of key features of cerebellar ataxia (CA). These patients struggle to anticipate the kinematic changes that occur during movement (33). Unlike patients with apraxia or hemiplegia, they can recruit the necessary muscles and show little loss of force. However, impaired feedforward control prevents proper coordination of agonist–antagonist activation, making it difficult to stop the movement at the intended position and at the intended time. As a result, their movements are ataxic—undershooting or overshooting the target—and, with increased reliance on delayed sensory feedback, exhibit intention tremor. Transient disruption of cerebellar activity, either through transcranial magnetic stimulation in humans (Fig. 1A) (34) or nerve stimulation/optogenetics in animal models (35, 36) produces similar ataxic errors, reinforcing the cerebellum’s role in generating predictions of the limb’s state across space and time.

The importance of the cerebellum for predictive, feedforward control—and its relative unimportance for reactive, feedback control—is evident across a wide range of sensorimotor tasks (33). One of the best-studied paradigms for probing feedforward control is sensorimotor adaptation, with one variant involving a manipulation such that the visual feedback is perturbed during movement (Fig. 1B). The resultant sensory prediction error is used to recalibrate the sensorimotor system and, correspondingly, refine the forward model associated with that motor command (38, 39). Patients with CA show marked impairments on adaptation tasks across a wide range of effectors and tasks (38, 40). In contrast, performance on tasks that depend on reactive, feedback-driven control is minimally affected. For example, when visual feedback is randomly displaced mid-reach, patients with CA generate online corrective responses comparable to those of controls (41, 42). This dissociation extends to speech: when the spectral properties of the auditory feedback are perturbed during ongoing speech, patients with CA exhibit intact online corrections (see also: (41)).

Drawing on this rich literature, we highlight three core constraints on cerebellar-dependent computations.

Prediction constraint: Cerebellar computations support feedforward, predictive control—not feedback- or memory-based responses.

The cerebellum will be engaged in tasks that require anticipating future states based on an internal transformation of a current representation. We assume that such predictions involve a simulation process that estimates the transition from the current state to the desired state.

The corollary here is that the cerebellum should not be involved in tasks in which the behavior is reactive, driven by feedback or retrieval processes. For example, predictions can arise from recalling prior associations in episodic memory: upon entering a favorite coffee shop, I might confidently predict that my friend will order an espresso. Retrieval-based predictions are unlikely to rely on cerebellar computation.

Timescale constraint: Cerebellar computations operate over short timescales—not extended temporal intervals.

Predictions extend over different time scales. A large body of literature shows that the cerebellum is critical for tasks that require high temporal precision, but only over a limited temporal range (milliseconds). Such precision is essential for coordinating the pattern of neural activity needed to grasp an object or anticipate the trajectory of a fast-moving ball. Similarly, in sensorimotor adaptation tasks in which the feedback is only shown at the end of the movement, delaying the onset of the feedback by as little as 1 s will completely abolish cerebellar-dependent learning (43, 44).

The corollary is that temporally diffuse predictions, such as learning that the smell of spoiled food predicts delayed adverse consequences or keeping track of when tea has steeped long enough, are not directly reliant on cerebellar computation. Instead, such behaviors increasingly depend on cortical memory and associative mechanisms, with any cerebellar contribution being indirect or embedded within broader recurrent cerebello–cortical networks.

Continuity constraint: Cerebellar computations perform state estimation over continuous dimensions of space and time—not discrete or categorical variables.

This constraint holds that the cerebellum is essential when predictions involve the transformation of a continuous variable. This encompasses movement where the corporeal body must move in a continuous manner, smoothly transitioning from one state to another. It would also apply to other contexts in which the prediction requires a continuous transformation such as anticipating the trajectory of a moving object.

The corollary here is that the cerebellum should not be essential for predictions involving discrete, symbolic, or categorical variables. For example, anticipating the final word of a sentence based on contextual association frequencies: “After the sun came out, the snowman:___.”

Evaluated as a whole, these three constraints—grounded in the well-established motor control literature—suggest that the cerebellum is essential for computations involving continuous representational transformations, or what we refer to by the acronym CoRT (Fig. 2). In the following sections, we consider the relevance of these constraints for cognition, with an eye on sharpening our understanding of how the cerebellum supports mental coordination. Importantly, these constraints lend themselves to rigorous empirical tests, specifying which tasks require cerebellar computation and which do not.

Empirical Support for CoRT

Cerebellar Involvement in Continuous Temporal Prediction over Short Intervals.

Studies of temporal prediction, the ability to anticipate when an event will occur, have provided some of the clearest evidence for cerebellar-dependent computation [for reviews, see (3, 45)]. A prominent example comes from the eyeblink conditioning literature (Fig. 3 A, Left). In a basic form of this classical conditioning task, a tone precedes an air puff delivered to the eye. Initially, the animal reflexively blinks in response to the air puff, but with repeated pairings, the tone alone elicits the response. Richard Thompson and colleagues in the early 1980s reported the striking finding that lesions of the rabbit cerebellar cortex abolished the conditioned response but had minimal impact on the unconditioned response (46). This dissociation, since replicated in multiple species, suggests that the deficit is not purely motoric (47, 48) but instead reflects an impairment in associative learning (49).

In contrast, heart-rate conditioning, measured as conditioned heart rate deceleration when the tone is paired with a face shock, is spared (Right) [adapted from (50)]. (B) In a trace conditioning task—where there is a temporal gap between the CS and US, patients with CA (orange) showed a selective impairment relative to control participants (gray) in acquiring conditioned eyeblink responses at short trace intervals (400 ms, Left). In contrast, acquisition was preserved at longer intervals (1,000 ms, Right) [adapted from (51)].

The conditioned eyeblink is only adaptive if the eyeblink response is precisely timed to anticipate the onset of the air puff, ensuring the eye is protected at the right moment. A blink that occurs too early would allow the eye to reopen before the air puff arrives, whereas a blink that occurs too late fails to protect the eye. Interestingly, a conditioned response acquired in a similar paradigm, heart rate deceleration, remains unaffected by the same lesion (Fig. 3 A, Right) (50). Heart rate deceleration is a form of classical conditioning in which a neutral cue comes to elicit a transient slowing of heart rate in anticipation of an aversive outcome. Unlike the eyeblink response, this predictive response unfolds over longer timescales and lacks fine temporal specificity.

How the cerebellum refines the timing of the conditioned eyeblink has been the focus of extensive research. A central idea emerging from this work is that interactions among interneurons in the cerebellar cortex, together with activity in the granule cell layer, generate a functional “basis set” of timing elements (52). Metaphorically, these can be thought of as a collection of hourglass timers, tuned to activate the Purkinje cells at specific intervals. In this way, the representation of the tone is temporally maintained. In response to the error signal arising from the air puff, the synaptic activity of a subset of these elements will be depressed. This will reduce Purkinje cell inhibition of the deep cerebellar nuclei and, thus, elicit a conditioned response that is timed to ensure the eye is protected at the onset of the air puff.

This simple associative learning paradigm highlights all three of our core constraints. Under the Prediction constraint, the cerebellum is essential for the predictive, conditioned eyeblink, but not essential for the reactive, nonpredictive unconditioned response. Under the Timescale constraint, the cerebellar-dependent conditioned response is precisely timed, tailored to anticipate the onset of the aversive air puff; the cerebellum is not required for the temporally diffuse, conditioned heart rate response when the interval extends beyond the millisecond range (50, 51). Under the Continuity constraint, the basis set defines a continuous representation of time in the cerebellar cortex for precise temporal anticipation (53).

We note that eyeblink and heart-rate conditioning differ, not only in timescale, but also in terms of the response effector and physiological system (skeletal versus autonomic). Importantly, even within the eyeblink conditioning literature, relatively subtle manipulations of the interval between the CS and US reveal a striking dissociation: cerebellar patients show a selective deficit in acquiring a precisely timed eyeblink in response to the pairing of a neutral tone and an air puff when the interval is short, but show little to no deficit when the interval is long (Fig. 3B) (51).

The cerebellum is also essential for temporal predictions in perceptual tasks. Patients with CA exhibit elevated discrimination threshold when comparing the duration of stimuli in the millisecond range (54), an effect that is also observed following noninvasive cerebellar brain stimulation in healthy individuals (55, 56). The specificity of this impairment is evident in findings showing preserved performance when the psychophysical judgment is based on nontemporal properties such as loudness (54). Neuropsychological and neuroimaging studies have associated the cerebellum with a bevy of other psychophysical tasks that require precise millisecond-level temporal resolution (57–60).

An intriguing dissociation comes from the work of Grube and colleagues (61). Across a set of temporal judgment tasks, CA participants were impaired when the target interval was presented in isolation, but not when it was embedded in a rhythm. This pattern points to two distinct modes of temporal representation, interval-based and rhythm-based. As with eyeblink conditioning, judging the duration of an isolated interval appears to rely on a cerebellar-dependent temporal basis set whose population activity provides a continuous representation of time. From a prediction perspective, the onset of the stimulus defining the target interval triggers the replay of an internalized reference interval, with the judgment based on whether the stimulus ends before or after the replay is completed. In contrast, when the standard is presented as part of a rhythmic series, the brain can become entrained and use the internalized beats as discrete temporal landmarks, evaluating whether the test stimulus occurred before or after a beat.

The selective involvement of the cerebellum in interval-based timing has also been observed in attentional orienting tasks. Perceptual detection and discrimination can be enhanced when a cue indicates when a stimulus is likely to appear. Here, too, we see a dissociation between interval and beat-based timing: CA participants show a marked deficit when the cue is provided as an isolated interval, but perform normally when the cue is a series of beats that can induce entrainment, even when task difficulty is matched (62, 63). Interestingly, patients with Parkinson’s disease show the opposite pattern: They are impaired in using the rhythmic cues to orient attention but perform similarly to control participants with interval-based cues. Altogether, these findings strongly support the constraints of CoRT, underscoring the cerebellum’s specialized role in generating continuous predictions over short intervals.

Cerebellar Involvement in Continuous Spatial Prediction over Short Intervals.

Coordinated movement requires anticipation of how the body will interact with the environment in time and space. As physical entities, movement entails continuous transformations with the control system anticipating future states of the body and environment in a time-sensitive manner. In this section, we highlight nonmotor tasks that rely on continuous spatial transformations and provide evidence that the integrity of the cerebellum is essential for optimal performance.

Deciphering a complex scene is challenging, not only because it is cluttered with many objects, but also because factors such as occlusion or shadows can introduce ambiguities concerning surface ownership. Moreover, recognition may be made difficult if the to-be-perceived object is in an atypical orientation with respect to the viewer. In such situations, recognition is facilitated by mentally manipulating the image to align with what we consider its prototypical orientation (64).

Experimentally, this process has been studied with mental rotation tasks. In the classic work of Shepherd and colleagues, participants judged if a letter, when viewed upright, would be in its normal orientation or mirror reversed. The key finding was that reaction time increased in a near-linear manner as the angle of the stimulus relative to upright was increased (65). This observation, coupled with data from multiple measures, indicates that the internal transformations required on such tasks appear to be continuous: For example, when presented with a letter that is tilted by 30°, we cannot internally reformat the letter to its upright position without passing through the intervening orientations. This continuity constraint has been hypothesized to reflect the internalization, or embodiment, of how we might manipulate an object if inspecting it from multiple perspectives (66).

To test whether the cerebellum supports continuous spatial transformations, we assessed patients with CA on a variant of the mental rotation task (Fig. 4A) (67). The CA group was considerably slower than matched controls in judging whether the target letter was in its normal orientation or mirror reversed. Notably, the RT (reaction time) difference between groups became more pronounced with the magnitude of the required rotation. This interaction suggests that the patients were slower in the rate at which they internally manipulated the mental image, consistent with the hypothesis that the cerebellum facilitates the internalized spatial transformation. Indeed, all CoRT constraints would appear to be operative during mental rotation: Over a short time scale, a continuous transformation is applied to assess if the presented stimulus matches a predicted stimulus (e.g., normal orientation).

We used CoRT to guide our selection for a control task to compare with mental rotation. As prerequisites, we required the control task to involve visual matching and, critically, to demand iterative retrieval of discrete visual representations rather than continuous stimulus transformation. Given these considerations, we employed a spatial working memory task (Fig. 4B) (68). Here, a series of dots were presented sequentially at different locations on a ring, with the memory set size varying from 1 to 5. After a short delay, a single dot was shown, and the participant made a speeded response, indicating its ordinal position in the encoding period. Although RT increased with working memory load in both groups, the similar slopes for the CA and control participants suggests the cerebellum is not essential for the retrieval of discrete items from working memory. The same dissociation was observed in a second experiment involving a different visual working memory task in which a set of abstract images (1-5 items) was presented and participants judged whether a probe was present or absent in the memory set. Compared to control participants, the CA group exhibited a larger slope on the mental rotation task and a similar slope on the visual working memory task.

These studies in the domain of visual cognition offer a mechanistic account of the CA groups’ performance based on the Continuity constraint. These patients were less facile in mentally rotating an internal visual image, an operation we assume requires the continuous transformation of an internal representation. In contrast, their rate of retrieval from spatial working memory was on par with the control group, presumably because the cerebellum is not essential for the discrete transformations required when iteratively searching through working memory. The importance of the cerebellum in continuous spatial processing is consistent with the evidence from many other tasks and measures. In terms of perceptual tasks, patients with CA have difficulty judging the velocity of a moving object (69) or predicting when and where an object will reappear after passing behind an occluder (59) [also see: (60)].

Spatial transformations are fundamental to many of our cognitive abilities such as mathematics, linguistic relationships, and metaphorical thinking (70, 71). To test whether the cerebellum supports continuous spatial representational transformations in more abstract domains, we compared CA patients and controls on simple addition and multiplication problems (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Single-digit addition is thought to involve a mental traversal along an internal, spatialized number line (72, 73). Thus, verifying that 4 + 3 = 7 takes less time than verifying that 8 + 6 = 14 because the mental distance traveled is shorter (74, 75). In contrast, solving single-digit multiplication problems is assumed to rely on a discrete look-up table, with faster verification for problems like 4 × 3 = 12 than 8 × 6 = 48 due to stronger memory traces accumulated over a lifetime of practice.

Based on this computational distinction, we predicted that patients with CA would show an elevated RT slope for the addition problems but not for the multiplication problems. The results were consistent with this prediction: As the problem size increased, the patients showed a steeper RT slope than matched controls on the addition problems but not on the multiplication problems (67). Unlike the earlier comparison between mental rotation and memory recall—where visual stimuli and task demands differed substantially—the addition and multiplication tasks used visually matched operands and identical verification (True vs False) responses across conditions. We interpret this dissociation as arising because only the addition task requires an internal, continuous transformation, a process dependent on cerebellar computation.

In a follow-up study, we used problems with either two or three addends (76). Again, the CA group showed an elevated slope when plotting RT as a function of problem size. However, the CA group showed a similar RT increase as controls when moving from two- to three-addend problems. We interpret this as a case where discrete and continuous operations are combined within the same task: The RT increase with problem size reflects a continuous transformation along a mental number line, whereas the additional RT for three-addend problems reflects the cost of retrieving the third discrete item after summing the first two. Interestingly, patients with Parkinson’s disease show the opposite pattern: A greater increase in RT compared to controls (and CA) when going from two to three addends, but a similar slope as controls as a function of the problem size. This double dissociation reinforces the cerebellum’s specificity for predictions involving continuous, not discrete, internal transformations.

This section has emphasized the Continuity constraint. Nonetheless, the Prediction and Timescale constraints are also operative here. Mental rotation is inherently predictive—the current state is internally transformed onto a future state, and within the context of mental rotation, the default prediction is that the stimulus will be in its normal orientation. Similarly, movement along a number line predicts the transformed value of an initial number (e.g., “Does adding 3 to 5 yield 8?”). These operations also unfold over a short, millisecond timescale (e.g., mental rotation rates of around 2 ms/deg for controls and 3 ms/deg for the CA group).

We do want to highlight one problematic—and intriguing—feature of these results. In evaluating the relevance of CoRT-related constraints in the spatial domain, we focused on the slope measure, treating it as an index of the fluidity or rate with which individuals manipulate a mental representation (e.g., mental rotation). The absence of a slope difference was critical to our claim that CA patients were not impaired in the rate at which they performed noncontinuous transformations (e.g., iterative retrieval from working memory). However, across all conditions there was a robust main effect of group: the CA group showed a pronounced slowing in reaction time relative to controls, on the order of 300 to 500 ms, even in the simplest mental rotation and working memory conditions. Critically, this slowing cannot be attributed to ataxia per se. On simple detection and perceptual discrimination tasks, patients with CA are typically only ~80 to 120 ms slower than matched controls (77, 78), a much smaller difference than that observed when the tasks are more cognitively demanding.

The large main effect suggests that CA is associated not only with selective impairments in specific cognitive operations such as mental rotation, but also with a more general increase in processing time as cognitive demands increase. This cost, should it be generic across all task domains, would be hard to reconcile within a model that limits cerebellar processing to operations that conform to the prediction, continuity, and timescale constraints. An important challenge for future work is to develop mechanistic hypotheses that can account for both general and specific forms of impairment. We note that this increase in RT cost with task complexity may reflect a more general executive or processing-speed deficit associated with brain damage, rather than a cerebellum-specific impairment.

Extending CoRT to other cognitive domains.

Moreover, the constraints of Continuity, Prediction, and Timescale were developed as a general framework for understanding cerebellar function across action and cognition. While the preceding sections focused on temporal prediction and spatial cognition, domains with clear continuous spatiotemporal structure, these constraints can be used more broadly to generate testable hypotheses in other cognitive domains. In Supplement 1, we sketch out ideas on how CoRT can guide future research in three domains that figure prominently in the cerebellar literature: language, social cognition, and cognitive control.

Refining the Dimensions of Cerebellar Computation

We have focused on prediction, timescale, and continuity as useful organizing principles that capture different dimensions relevant for considering how the cerebellum supports cognition. Our central claim is that the closer a psychological process lies toward the predictive, short timescale, and continuous ends of these continua, the greater the cerebellar contribution. However, we recognize that these constraints are broad and not always easy to operationalize. In this section, we consider these limitations and how future work might sharpen our understanding of the constraints underlying cerebellar function. We refer the reader to Supplement 2 for a discussion of a few meta-issues not related to the CoRT framework which need to be considered when developing and evaluating mechanistic hypotheses concerning the role of the cerebellum in cognition.

Refining the Timescale constraint.

The Timescale constraint is currently the most empirically tractable. The importance of the cerebellum for behaviors requiring temporal precision on the millisecond scale is well established across many task domains. By contrast, how the cerebellum contributes to learning at longer timescales remains an open question. Two broad possibilities merit consideration. One is that cerebellar involvement is restricted to short-timescale prediction, with longer-timescale learning dependent on extracerebellar mechanisms. An alternative is that the cerebellum continues to contribute at longer timescales, but does so through interactions with extracerebellar regions—such as recurrent systems that maintain or reinstate conditioned stimuli to bridge temporal gaps (79). Discriminating between these possibilities will require future studies that jointly manipulate interval duration and learning mode (e.g., delay vs. trace conditioning with short and long interstimulus intervals).

To date, manipulations of timescale have largely been confined to relatively simple perceptual and motor tasks. The relevant temporal dynamics may differ substantially as one moves to more complex domains such as social cognition and language. Although we adopt a pragmatic distinction here—treating intervals below ~1 s as “short” and longer intervals as “long”—this boundary is necessarily arbitrary. A key goal for future work is to more precisely identify the timescales over which cerebellar computations are engaged across domains.

Complicating matters further, ecological behaviors rarely rely on predictive mechanisms operating at a single timescale. Instead, they recruit multiple predictive processes that operate concurrently across many timescales. Eating, for example, involves rapid predictions supporting orofacial motor control alongside slower predictions about metabolic consequences such as glucose fluctuations (80). Such behaviors provide a natural opportunity to assess whether the cerebellum is selectively engaged in short-timescale prediction, and whether longer-timescale prediction increasingly depends on recurrent interactions with cortical circuits.

Refining the Prediction constraint.

Operationalizing the Prediction constraint presents its own challenges. Although we have placed simulation and retrieval as the anchors for on the prediction continuum, the distinction between these two is not always clear. Consider the forward model, an idea central to models of sensorimotor adaptation, in which the cerebellum takes the efference copy of the motor command to simulate the anticipated sensory consequences of that movement. Yet even this canonical example of simulation could be construed as a retrieval process, with the cerebellum having stored the sensory consequences associated with a given motor command (81).

The picture is further complicated by the likelihood that many behaviors rely on both feedforward simulation and retrieval-based processes. Without clear criteria for distinguishing simulation from retrieval, it becomes difficult to precisely operationalize prediction as a constraint to specify the functional domain of the cerebellum. At present, the primary value of this constraint lies in capturing a robust empirical observation: retrieval-based tasks—such as mathematical fact retrieval during multiplication and semantic retrieval during sentence completion—are generally spared in cerebellar ataxia. This pattern suggests that the representations and processes supporting retrieval are largely extracerebellar.

A second core distinction we have made with respect to prediction in the motor domain is that the cerebellum primarily supports simulation-based, feedforward prediction rather than feedback-driven control. We note that this dissociation does not preclude indirect interactions between feedforward and feedback control circuits. Cerebellar predictions may be transmitted to downstream cortical, brainstem, and spinal circuits that implement rapid, reflexive corrections, effectively tuning the operating point or gain of reactive controllers without the cerebellum itself serving as the locus of feedback control. This framework may explain why perturbing cerebellar activity in rodents alters reactive corrections during the production of well-learned behaviors. The perturbation may degrade predictive signals that can facilitate fast, accurate feedback responses (82). More broadly, these effects underscore the need to study cerebellar function in a systems context, as part of recurrently coupled control circuits rather than as an isolated module.

Refining the Continuity constraint.

Superficially, the distinction between discrete and continuous mental operations may appear quite clear. We have emphasized that the latter reflects those operations in which a given representation is manipulated in a continuous manner, smoothly transitioning from one state to another, be it planning a movement of the body or modifying the orientation of a mental image to enable object recognition. In contrast, discrete operations do not entail continuous transformations. When deciding whether to buy an apple or a pear, we do not transform one into the other and compare the resulting states; rather, we retrieve the values associated with each fruit and compare these distinct representations.

However, the processes underlying many behaviors are likely composed of both discrete and continuous operations. For instance, while a continuous transformation is applied to reorient a tilted letter in a mental rotation task, the decision might depend on a discrete comparison to determine whether the upright image is normal or mirror-reversed. The manner in which representations are transformed can also vary with task demands: judging the similarity of two colors might entail continuously morphing one into the other, whereas judging if the same two colors are both blue might entail a more discrete operation. Similarly, a social judgment may arise from a continuous simulation of another agent’s internal state yet be shaped by inputs such as social norms or etiquette that impose biases in a more discrete manner.

Neurons in many brain regions, including the cerebellum (83, 84) exhibit ramping activity during decision formation. While it is tempting to interpret such activity as reflecting a continuous computation, we do not see this as diagnostic of continuity at the algorithmic level. In principle, similar ramping profiles could arise from discrete retrieval processes as evidence accumulates, or from genuinely continuous transformations as intermediate states are traversed. Thus, when we refer to continuity, we mean a property of the underlying representation at the algorithmic level—specifically, whether computation proceeds through intermediate representational states) rather than the temporal smoothness of neural activity at the level of implementation.

Taken together, these definitional and levels-of-analysis challenges underscore that our emphasis on prediction, timescale, and continuity is primarily heuristic. These dimensions are intended to capture distinct features of mental processes and to serve as a springboard for understanding how the cerebellum supports cognition. In this spirit, we regard CoRT not as a fixed taxonomy but as a generative starting point. Empirical and theoretical work will refine which dimensions best capture the constraints on cerebellar computation.

Closing Comments

In the search for a unifying theory, many researchers—including ourselves—have been guided by the idea of a universal cerebellar transform (UCT): the notion that the cerebellum implements a specialized computational algorithm that is applied broadly across motor and cognitive domains (2). This idea was motivated by the strikingly uniform anatomy and physiology of the cerebellum, coupled with its extensive connectivity with the cerebral cortex— features that suggest a domain-general, algorithmic function. Although varying in their level of specificity, candidate UCTs have focused on computations related to error correction (25), timing (85), homeostatic maintenance (2), and forward modeling (21).

Empirically, recent neurophysiological findings suggest that the picture is more nuanced. While the cerebellum certainly lacks the cellular diversity of the cerebral cortex, there is considerable anatomical heterogeneity in terms of microcircuitry with distinct microzones (13). Compounding this, even within the motor domain, signals represented in the cerebellum are more diverse than previously assumed. For example, in addition to providing an error signal, complex spikes encode higher-order variables such as reward and goal states—features that would be difficult to capture within a narrow computational framework (86).

It is also possible that the very theoretical construct of UCT is misguided. Instead of searching for a singular computation shared across task domains, it may be more fruitful to examine how diverse cerebellar circuits carry out distinct computations. Alternatively, it may be that the cerebellum supports cortical processing through its sheer number of neurons that can facilitate pattern expansion and recognition. By this view, the cerebellum need not be considered to represent something per se. Rather, in the way that oil is essential to the smooth operation of an engine, the cerebellum might ensure that the mind operates fluidly and efficiently, what might be described as mental coordination.

Here, we advance an integrated perspective, one that seeks to account for why cerebellar dysfunction disrupts performance in disparate task domains, as well as why performance is unaffected in other domains. Rather than positing a single unifying algorithm or treating cerebellar computation as bespoke to each domain, we highlight a set of constraints that consistently characterize cerebellar processing. Like mathematical postulates, these constraints—together with those yet to be identified—can be precisely defined, empirically tested, and used to evaluate consistencies and discrepancies across functional domains, moving us closer to a comprehensive understanding of the cerebellum’s role in cognition and action.
