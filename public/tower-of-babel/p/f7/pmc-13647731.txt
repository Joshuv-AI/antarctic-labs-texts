Ultrasound-guided platelet-rich plasma myofascial injection for chronic low back pain: a retrospective study
Authors: YongJia Chen, Ruju Lv, Yannan Sun, Qian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRetrospective cohort study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ultrasound-guided myofascial platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection for myofascial pain syndrome (MPS)–induced chronic low back pain.MethodsFifty-eight patients treated between May and December 2024 received a single ultrasound-guided PRP injection (n = 32) or two weekly normal saline injections (n = 26), and both groups underwent myofascial hydrodissection targeting trigger points in the longissimus lumborum. Pain intensity was assessed via Visual Analog Scale (VAS), functional disability by the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Surface electromyography (sEMG) measured neuromuscular activity through root mean square (RMS) and median frequency (MF). Statistical analyses used SPSS v.27.ResultsBoth groups showed significant VAS reduction at 12 weeks (p < 0.001), with greater improvement in the PRP group (r = 0.501). PRP also yielded significant ODI improvement (r = 0.611) exceeding the minimum clinically important difference. RMS values decreased markedly in the PRP group (p = 0.001) and modestly in the NS group (p = 0.006). MF demonstrated a transient decline at week 4 (p = 0.014). No adverse events occurred.ConclusionsUltrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection effectively reduces pain, enhances function, and modulates MTrPs activity in MPS at short-term follow-up, with good safety. These results support further randomized trials to validate the rehabilitative benefits of PRP for chronic low back pain.Keywords: Myofascial pain syndrome, Platelet-rich plasma, Ultrasound-guided injection, Low back pain, Myofascial trigger points

Introduction

Myofascial pain syndrome (MPS) is a chronic musculoskeletal pain disorder characterized by hyperirritable trigger points (MTrPs) within taut muscle bands that cause local and referred pain, stiffness, and restricted motion, leading to significant impairment of functional capacity and quality of life [1–7]. The development of MTrPs is closely associated with motor endplate dysfunction, where excessive acetylcholine release induces sustained sarcomere contraction, leading to localized ischemia, hypoxia, and an energy crisis. Prolonged persistence of this condition may further promote abnormal adhesion and fibrosis [7]. Epidemiological studies report an MPS prevalence of 30%–93% among patients with musculoskeletal pain [5, 8]. MPS is a common etiological factor of chronic low back pain. However, due to inconsistent diagnostic criteria and overlapping symptoms with other pain disorders, it is frequently underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed; coupled with its recurrent nature and poor response to conventional treatments, MPS remains a challenging condition to manage clinically [9–14].

The treatment of MPS remains challenging due to the lack of objective criteria for evaluating MTrPs, hindering the establishment of standardized therapies [15, 16]. Common invasive options include dry needling (DN) and trigger point injection (TPI), which delivers various agents such as local anesthetics, corticosteroids, or botulinum toxin [16–20]. Among these, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has gained interest as an autologous blood product rich in growth factors that may promote tissue repair and modulate inflammation [21, 22]. It is hypothesized that PRP exerts its effects on MTrPs through anti-inflammatory action, stimulation of healing, and improved perfusion [23]. Nevertheless, this approach has not been able to fully halt the progression of MPS, mainly due to imprecise localization and insufficient deactivation of MTrPs [24].

Based on the mechanisms of activation of MTrPs, ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection has been proposed as a precise method to inactivate MTrPs, and a newer needling technique called myofascial hydrodissection has been developed, in which fluid is injected between fascial layers under ultrasound guidance to separate fascial planes, improve gliding, and potentially block nerve branches [25, 26]. Therefore, combining PRP injection with myofascial hydrodissection may represent a potentially synergistic strategy for both inactivating MTrPs and restoring normal myofascial gliding. In the operational regimens of PRP, ultrasound is used to enable real-time visualization and to guide needle placement precisely into MTrPs. Under the ultrasound guidance, an appropriate volume of PRP is injected into MTrPs, followed by sequential injection into adjacent myofascial layers for hydrodissection of the perimuscular membrane, perimysium, and epimysium surrounding the MTrPs. This combined approach not only facilitates the precise delivery of PRP but may also modulate the local biochemical microenvironment and promote tissue repair. The precise injection and layer hydrodissection could inactivate the MTrPs and restore its surrounding myofascial system.

To objectively assess therapeutic effects, surface electromyography (sEMG) has been used to evaluate neuromuscular activity at MTrP sites, where abnormally elevated signals are often observed [25, 27, 28]. Sánchez-Infante et al. reported that DN reduced the root mean square (RMS) and increased the mean frequency (MF) of sEMG signals, indicating normalization of muscle activity and pain alleviation [29]. These findings highlight the potential of sEMG as an objective method for evaluating the neuromuscular regulatory effects of PRP therapy.

Based on the premise that growth factors released from autologous platelets can enhance tissue healing, the objective of this retrospective study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection for treating patients with MPS-induced chronic low back pain and to further investigate the possible mechanism on alterations in neuromuscular activation.

Methods

Setting and patients

This retrospective comparative cohort study analyzed data from two independently conducted, prospectively registered randomized controlled trials (RCTs) at the Rehabilitation Medicine Center of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, in which all enrolled patients received treatment between May and December 2024.

The PRP group (n = 32) was extracted from Trial 1 (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2300074199; registered on August 1, 2023). The original design of Trial 1 was an RCT comparing ultrasound-guided PRP myofascial injection plus routine rehabilitation versus routine rehabilitation alone.

The normal saline (NS) control group (n = 26) was extracted from Trial 2 (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2300067984; registered on February 2, 2023). The original design of Trial 2 was a prospective RCT comparing ultrasound-guided normal saline hydrodissection versus lidocaine hydrodissection for myofascial pain syndrome, with a planned sample size of 20 patients per arm. To compensate for potential loss to follow-up, additional eligible patients were enrolled during the study period. The 26 patients included in the present study represent all consecutive NS-arm participants from Trial 2 who met our strict inclusion criteria and had complete baseline and 12-week follow-up assessment data.

Both trials adopted strictly identical inclusion/exclusion criteria, target muscle, ultrasound-guided interfascial hydrodissection procedure, and standardized outcome assessment protocols. For the present analysis, we selected participants who received ultrasound-guided PRP injection with hydrodissection (from Trial 1) and those who received ultrasound-guided normal saline injection with hydrodissection (from Trial 2) to form two comparable cohorts. All data were de-identified prior to analysis.

Patients were identified using ICD-10 codes (M79.1). Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) confirmed MPS-induced low back pain; (2) complete pre- and post-treatment assessment records of pain intensity, functional status, and electromyographic activity; and (3) receipt of designated injection therapy. Exclusion criteria included: (1) lumbar radiculopathy; (2) history of lumbar trauma/surgery; (3) fibromyalgia; (4) accompanying nausea/vomiting/dizziness; (5) nerve block or botulinum toxin injection within 12 months; and (6) injection targeting non-longissimus muscles. A total of 58 patients were included, with 32 in the PRP group (cases) and 26 in the NS group (control).

Intervention

Approximately 40 mL of venous blood was drawn using a 20G needle and gently mixed with sodium citrate anticoagulant (4 mL, 0.04 g/mL) at a ratio of 9:1 to prevent microbubble formation before distribution into anticoagulant vacuum tubes. PRP was prepared using a WEGO Platelet-Rich Plasma Preparation Kit (WEGO Ltd., Shandong, China) following a standardized two-step centrifugation protocol: an initial centrifugation at 1400 rpm for 10 min to separate the buffy coat, followed by a second centrifugation at 2350 rpm for 5 min [25]. After removing the supernatant, approximately 6 mL of leukocyte-rich PRP was obtained. Calcium chloride and thrombin were added to activate the PRP and maintain its bioactivity. The entire procedure was performed at room temperature (21 °C), with no pre-centrifugation cooling, and the freshly prepared PRP was used immediately after preparation [30].

Prior to treatment, patients were instructed to discontinue nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and other analgesics (except acetaminophen as rescue) for two weeks before baseline and throughout follow-up. Each patient received either a single ultrasound-guided PRP injection (n = 32) or two NS injections once weekly (n = 26), and both groups underwent myofascial hydrodissection targeting the MTrPs within the longissimus lumborum muscle. The MTrPs were identified based on clinical manifestations and manual palpation. Under real-time ultrasound guidance using a GE LOGIQ E9 ultrasound system, the needle was advanced using an in-plane approach to maintain continuous visualization of the needle tip. When the needle tip reached the predetermined MTrP, a brief local muscle twitch was observed on the ultrasound image as transient movement of the surrounding tissue, which confirmed accurate positioning. An experienced musculoskeletal physician then injected PRP or 5 mL of 0.9% NS into the MTrPs and adjacent myofascial tissues with sequential hydrodissection of perimuscular, perimysial, and epimysial membranes. Aspiration preceded each injection to avoid intravascular entry, followed by slow infusion. Afterward, passive lumbar extension and flexion were performed five times, and patients remained supine for 10 min before discharge.

Outcome assessments

Baseline information included demographic characteristics as well as clinical data such as side of involvement, symptom duration, prior treatments (e.g., physical therapy), and current use of analgesics. All data were extracted from medical records and patient interviews. In this retrospective study, outcomes were assessed at baseline and during follow-up visits, as documented in the medical records, which occurred at approximately 2, 4, and 12 weeks after the final treatment session. All records were reviewed and cross-checked by two independent researchers to ensure completeness and accuracy.

Visual analog scale

The primary outcome was pain intensity, retrospectively extracted from medical records. The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) is a 10-cm validated scale that quantifies self-reported pain from 0 (no pain at all) to 10 (worst possible pain) [31]. Patients were instructed during routine clinical practice to indicate their pain level by marking a point on the line, and scores were determined as the distance from the left end to the patient’s mark. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was interpreted based on the mean difference for the 11-point (0–10) pain scale; as established in previous literature, the MCID for VAS is 1–2 cm [32].

Oswestry disability index

The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) is a widely used questionnaire designed to assess the degree of disability in patients with low back pain. The standard ODI consists of 10 items, each scored on a scale from 0 to 5, with a total possible score ranging from 0 to 50. For consistency and ease of interpretation, the total score is conventionally normalized to a percentage scale (0-100%), calculated as the ratio of the obtained score to the maximum possible score multiplied by 100. A higher score indicates greater disability. The MCID for functional disability on ODI was found to be at least 10 points [33].

Surface electromyography

A wireless surface electromyography (sEMG) system (FREEEMG, BTS, Milan, Italy) was used to acquire signals at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. The system was calibrated before each recording session according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In this retrospective study, activity of the lumbar longissimus muscle was assessed. The protocol was as follows [25]: (1) The skin overlying the lumbar longissimus muscle was lightly shaved and cleaned with alcohol to minimize impedance. (2) Patients were positioned prone on the examination table in a standardized posture during electrode placement. (3) Bipolar Ag/AgCl electrodes (Kendall™ 930, Cardinal Health Inc., Dublin, OH, USA) were placed bilaterally on the muscle belly, aligned with the fiber direction, with a 20-mm interelectrode distance. (4) A standardized trunk extension task was performed in this position. (5) Each subject completed six trials, with 5s of maximal voluntary contraction followed by 5s of rest. Only the middle 3s of the contraction phase were selected for analysis. The raw EMG data were filtered using a 50 Hz notch filter and a third-order Butterworth band-pass filter (20–450 Hz). RMS (µV) was extracted as the time-domain feature, and MF (Hz) was obtained as the frequency-domain feature.

Adverse events

In this retrospective study, adverse events related to ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP or NS injection were identified from clinical records. Documentation included both immediate and delayed responses to the intervention, such as local discomfort, swelling, muscular soreness, bleeding, hematoma, needle pain, or any other treatment-related adverse events. All events had been routinely recorded in the medical charts during follow-up visits or through patient self-reports that were subsequently entered into the records. This allowed for a comprehensive assessment of treatment safety within the study cohort.

Statistical analysis

The extracted data were coded as Group A or B and analyzed by an independent statistician, who remained blinded to group allocation until all analyses were completed. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0). Patients were assigned to the PRP group (n = 32) or the NS group (n = 26). Data normality was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), for normal and non-normal distributions, respectively. For within-group comparisons (each post-treatment time point [2, 4, and 12 weeks] vs. baseline), normally distributed variables were analyzed with paired t-tests and non-normally distributed variables with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For between-group comparisons at each time point (PRP vs. NS), normally distributed variables were compared using independent-samples t-tests and non-normally distributed variables using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Effect sizes for normally distributed measures were expressed as Cohen’s d. For non-normal variables, effect sizes were calculated as rank-biserial correlation (r = Z/√N) and interpreted as: r ≥ 0.5 (large), 0.3–0.5 (moderate), 0.1–0.3 (small), and < 0.1 (trivial). Multiple comparisons across follow-ups were not performed because inter-interval changes did not exceed the MCID threshold. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses used all available data at each time point [34].

Results

Characteristics of enrolled subjects

This study consisted of 58 patients (PRP, n = 32; NS, n = 26) met the eligibility criteria in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The median age (IQR) was 47.5 (34.0-64.7) years in the PRP group and 41.0 (29.8–54.0) years in the NS group. The gender distribution was similar between groups (PRP: 31.3% male / 68.7% female; NS: 30.8% male / 69.2% female). The median disease duration (IQR) was 24.0 (12.3–36.0) months in the PRP group and 20.0 (12.0–25.0) months in the NS group. Effects of PRP on functional measures in MPS

As illustrated in Fig. 2d-f, ODI scores decreased steadily in both groups after treatment, with the PRP group maintaining lower scores across all post-treatment visits (Tables 2 and 3). After 12 weeks of injection, significant improvements were observed in both PRP (r = 0.745, p < 0.001) and NS (r = 0.581, p = 0.004) groups. Between-group comparison demonstrated greater functional recovery in the PRP group (r = 0.611, p< 0.001).

Effects of PRP on neuromuscular electrophysiological activity in MPS

Figure 2g-l demonstrates that RMS values declined significantly only in the PRP group after 12 weeks of injection (p = 0.001), whereas no significant changes were detected in the NS group (p > 0.05) (Tables 2 and 3). Between-group comparison also showed a significant difference at post-injection of week 12 (p = 0.006). MF exhibited a transient decrease in the PRP group only after 4 weeks of injection (p = 0.014), but no further within-group or between-group differences were observed thereafter (p > 0.05).

Adverse events

No adverse events were identified or recorded upon retrospective review of the study period.

Discussion

This retrospective study investigated the effects and safety of ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection in treating MPS-induced low back pain. The findings indicated meaningful improvements in pain alleviation and physical function over the follow-up period. Moreover, neuromuscular electrophysiological activity showed a gradual downward trend, suggesting a potential normalization of neuromuscular activity. No adverse events were identified or recorded upon retrospective review.

This study found a significant, MCID-level reduction in pain at 12 weeks after ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection, exceeding that of the control group. This finding suggests an analgesic benefit and is consistent with previous reports. A randomized controlled trial of PRP injection on masseter MTrPs showed comparable VAS improvement [20],while recent meta-analyses have associated PRP with significant and sustained pain alleviation in various chronic musculoskeletal conditions [35, 36]. However, high-quality studies specifically focusing on the efficacy of ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection for MPS remain limited.

The 12-week reduction in ODI scores exceeded the MCID, indicating substantial functional recovery with PRP treatment, greater than that in the control group. Echoing this finding, a prospective study on discogenic pain reported significant ODI improvements following intradiscal PRP injection [37], while a recent meta-analysis confirmed that PRP injections provide superior functional benefits over corticosteroid injections in various tendinopathies [38]. These functional benefits are likely mediated by PRP’s growth factor–driven repair and anti-inflammatory effects, supporting its potential as a therapeutic approach addressing both pain and function in musculoskeletal disorders.

Previous studies have reported that in patients with MPS, DN or ischemic compression treatment leads to improvements in pain and physical functionl, accompanied by decreases in RMS values and reductions in muscle excitability and tension, consistent with our findings [29, 39, 40]. As an indicator of muscle fatigue, MF typically increases when fatigue decreases [25]. In our study, the PRP group showed a slight but significant reduction in MF, accompanied by a decrease in RMS values. Taken together, these changes suggest enhanced metabolic efficiency and stabilization of neuromuscular activity rather than the onset of fatigue. In contrast, although myofascial NS injection effectively alleviated pain, its influence on neuromuscular activity and functional improvement appeared limited. PRP not only further reduced MTrPs excitability and muscle tension but also alleviated pain and improved function, playing a positive role in the normalization of neuromuscular activity with more sustained and durable therapeutic effects over time.

The two groups exhibited different temporal response patterns after treatment. Although both groups showed some degree of pain alleviation at the early time points (2 and 4 weeks) with no significant intergroup difference, by 12 weeks the VAS reduction in the NS group only approached the lower limit of the MCID, whereas the reduction in the PRP group was more pronounced and clinically meaningful. As for functional recovery, the ODI reductions in the NS group did not reach the MCID at any time point, suggesting that its functional benefits may lack clinical significance. In contrast, the functional improvement in the PRP group was not only statistically significant but also reached the threshold for a clinically important difference. These observations suggest that the myofascial hydrodissection procedure in the NS group may mainly provide limited short-term symptom alleviation, which is speculated to be related to its mechanical effects such as temporary fascial separation and improved tissue gliding, particularly failing to produce sustained clinical benefit in functional recovery. PRP combined with myofascial hydrodissection, however, showed a progressively increasing effect over time, with sustained benefits in the PRP group, whereas the benefits in the NS group were limited and transient. This time-dependent therapeutic effect is suggested to be closely related to the multiple biological mechanisms of PRP.

These modulations are consistent with the biological actions of PRP in tissue repair and inflammation. This phenomenon likely reflects the multifaceted mechanisms of PRP. Bioactive components such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) not only modulate pain-related inflammatory mediators including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) to attenuate neurogenic inflammation and reduce nociceptor sensitization, but also promote angiogenesis and tissue repair [41]. These combined effects help alleviate microcirculatory disturbances and energy crises in the MTrP region, thereby restoring normal muscle metabolic homeostasis [42–45]. These mechanisms may provide not only short-term pain alleviation but also longer-term physiological improvement. Future research should also explore the underlying mechanisms linking electrophysiological modulations with clinical outcomes, which may provide new insights into optimizing PRP therapy.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and acupuncture are commonly used for chronic musculoskeletal pain but may cause adverse effects, including gastrointestinal or dermatological complications and procedure-related bleeding or discomfort [46, 47]. By contrast, no adverse events related to ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection were observed in this study, supporting its preliminary safety. Nevertheless, existing evidence indicates potential risks. Arita and Tobita reported postoperative infection, vision loss, inflammatory reactions, allergic responses, and nodule formation, with infection being the most common [48]. Case reports further describe local infection and ulceration following gastrocnemius PRP injection [49]. Thus, while PRP demonstrates a favorable safety profile, strict adherence to aseptic techniques and standardized procedures is essential to minimize complications.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the difference in treatment frequency between the PRP and NS groups may have introduced bias: the two NS sessions could have produced a greater cumulative needling and myofascial hydrodissection effect, or conversely, the single PRP session might have had a longer-lasting biological effect. This confounding factor prevents us from attributing the observed differences solely to the biological action of PRP. Second, we did not quantitatively measure platelet concentration or the enrichment ratio relative to whole blood. PRP composition can vary considerably depending on the preparation method and individual differences, and the lack of such quantitative data may affect the reproducibility of our findings and comparability with other studies. Third, the retrospective, non-randomized design may have introduced selection bias. PRP group patients typically could afford the higher cost and understood the potential advantages of PRP, whereas NS group patients were treated according to routine clinical pathways. The allocation process was influenced by physician judgment, patient preference, and cost, leading to selection bias. Fourth, no a priori power analysis was performed due to the retrospective design. The relatively small sample size may limit statistical power, particularly for secondary outcomes. Fifth, the follow-up period was limited to 12 weeks, and long-term outcomes remain unknown. Finally, while sEMG provides objective data, its interpretation in MPS is complex, and the lack of standardized protocols may affect reproducibility. Future multicenter prospective randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In this retrospective study, ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection resulted in greater pain alleviation, functional improvement, and normalization of neuromuscular activity compared with the control group at the 12-week short-term follow-up in patients with MPS-induced chronic low back pain. No adverse events were observed, supporting its safety. These findings indicate that ultrasound-guided myofascial PRP injection may be a safe and effective option for MPS, but randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm this rehabilitative effect.
