Repeatability of gray value-based bone density measurements in cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images under different acquisition protocols
Authors: Luciano Augusto Cano Martins, Leszek Szalewski, Krzysztof Pałka, Paweł Kalinowski, Marcelo Gusmão Paraiso Cavalcanti, Ingrid Różyło-Kalinowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate and repeatable assessment of bone density is a critical factor in dental implant planning, as it influences decisions related to implant size, site preparation, and primary stability, ultimately affecting treatment outcomes and long-term osseointegration. The variability in Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) gray values across different acquisition protocols can compromise measurement repeatability, challenging the reliability of preoperative bone assessments. This study aimed to assess the repeatability of a gray value-based bone density measurement tool along three-thirds of a virtual implant in CBCT under different acquisition protocols.MethodsThree pork ribs (5 × 3 × 2 cm) were scanned three times each using a VistaVoxS 3D CBCT scanner under four acquisition protocols, varying field-of-view (5 × 5 and 13 × 5), kVp (98, 94, 79), mA (11, 9, 6), and voxel size (0.08 and 0.2 mm), resulting in 144 scans. A virtual implant (10 × 5.2 mm) was centered on each rib image, and gray values along the thirds of the implant were measured using AIS 3DAPP 5.0 software. Repeatability was assessed with Friedman’s test, Kendall’s agreement coefficient, and the chi-square test.ResultsThe mean CBCT gray values in the cervical region were significantly lower regardless of the protocol (p ≤ 0.5). Individual exposures differed but showed very good agreement (0.89–0.83).ConclusionsThe bone density tool showed reliable gray value measurements across CBCT protocols, with field of view affecting repeatability more than voxel size. Significant gray value differences in the cervical region highlight the need for cautious interpretation in this area. Standardizing imaging settings is recommended to improve implant planning accuracy.

Background

Dental implants have emerged as a viable alternative for assisting patients who are partially or completely edentulous in their dental rehabilitation. A detailed planning process before dental implant placement is highly important for ensuring successful outcomes. Careful planning allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s oral health, including the condition of the surrounding teeth, gingiva, and bones. This technique enables the dentist to assess the quantity and quality of available bone at the implant site, which is crucial for determining the appropriate size, position, and angle of the implant [1]. By conducting a detailed bone evaluation, the dental professional can anticipate potential challenges, plan for necessary bone augmentation procedures if needed, and ultimately increase the overall success rate and longevity of the dental implant. Bone evaluation focuses on assessing the quantity and quality of the bone at the intended implant site. The quantity of available bone determines whether additional bone grafting procedures are necessary to provide adequate support for the implant. The quality of bone, on the other hand, affects the stability and long-term success of the implant.

Three-dimensional imaging modalities play crucial roles in preoperative assessment, treatment planning, and prediction of the success of dental implant procedures because of their ability to evaluate bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume [2, 3]. Micro-CT, Multislice CT (MSCT), and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) are used for evaluating bone health and suitability for dental implant placement. Micro-CT, known for its high-resolution imaging capabilities, enables the quantification of BMD and the visualization of intricate bone microarchitecture, providing valuable insights into bone quality and strength [4]. However, because of its limited field of view and relatively high exposure parameters, this technique is used only in vitro for research purposes. On the other hand, MSCT and CBCT, which are widely used in medical and dental fields, can provide information about bone density and volume through gray value measurements and 3D reconstructions, aiding in implant planning and assessment [5].

In MSCT, tissue densities are expressed in Hounsfield Units (HU), a standardized scale based on the attenuation coefficients of water and air. This calibration allows for accurate and reproducible quantification of tissue density, making HU a reliable metric for diagnostic and treatment planning purposes. In contrast, the gray values generated in CBCT are influenced by several variables, including imaging parameters, reconstruction algorithms, and scanner type. As a result, CBCT gray values are not standardized and cannot be directly interpreted as HU. Additionally, CBCT has lower spatial resolution and may introduce some image artifacts, which can lead to potential inaccuracies in gray value quantification. Additionally, CBCT devices often use a lower tube voltage than MSCT devices do, resulting in fewer available HU values and less accurate density measurements for some tissues [6]. Both MSCT and CBCT have limitations in terms of gray value quantification. MSCT requires relatively high radiation exposure, which can be a concern for patients, especially when multiple scans are necessary. CBCT, while providing lower radiation exposure, has limited soft tissue contrast compared with MSCT, which can be a drawback when evaluating detailed anatomical structures [7].

Nevertheless, despite those limitations, gray values in CBCT have shown meaningful correlations with bone density and are increasingly used for relative assessments in implant planning and bone quality evaluation. The quantification of gray values in CBCT is used to represent tissue density, with higher values indicating denser tissues such as the mandibular cortical bone and lower values corresponding to less dense tissues such as soft-tissue organs or air-filled spaces. Absolute CBCT gray values have shown a significant linear association with the linear X-ray attenuation coefficient of tissues, even though they cannot be used for quantitative examination of bone quality, as HU units can [7, 8]. Moreover, both techniques may face challenges in accurately quantifying tissue densities in areas with metal restorations or implants, as these objects can cause streak artifacts and obscure adjacent structures, leading to inaccurate gray value measurements [6].

In addition to artifacts, gray values in CBCT images may be influenced by many factors, such as exposure parameters, image formation, reconstruction algorithms, image artifact formation, technological factors, and anatomical location [9–11]. In general, the milliamperage (mA) determines the number of electrons available in the X-ray tube that will partially lose kinetic energy in the form of X-ray photons; a higher mA leads to an increased signal-to-noise ratio, which is expected to improve image quality but also increases the radiation dose delivered to the patient [12]. Although it is not appropriate to assume specific physical features of the object of interest based on any given CBCT-derived gray value, a strong linear relationship between gray values and object density has been observed, and it has been demonstrated to be a promising tool for differentiating individuals with osteoporosis from individuals with normal BMD [13]. New-age implant planning software allows for a detailed examination of the implant site, and some of them provide a bone density tool that seems to be helpful for an objective analysis of the area. Although previous studies have extensively investigated the variability and instability of gray values in CBCT images, it is highly advisable to conduct studies that assess the application and effectiveness of these new extra tools as well as their relationship with potential accuracy-influencing variables. For that reason, this study aimed to investigate the reproducibility of bone density measurements via cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) with a novel bone density tool along three-thirds of a virtual implant under different acquisition protocols with varying fields of view and voxel sizes.

Methods

Three fresh pork ribs measuring 5 cm in length, 3 cm in height, and 2 cm in thickness were obtained from a local market. Micro-CT imaging was employed to characterize the internal bone structure and density of the rib samples, serving as a reference for sample quality and consistency due to its superior spatial resolution and ability to provide detailed internal structural information. Additionally, Micro-CT demonstrated an excellent correlation with multislice computed tomography (MSCT) and a strong correlation between CBCT gray values and both micro-CT and MSCT values, as demonstrated in a previous study [5]. The ribs were scanned using a Micro-CT scanner - Xradia 510 Versa system (Carl Zeiss X-ray Microscopy, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA) with a voxel resolution of 25 μm and selected for the study. To determine the bone parameters, a set of 1601 images (.bmp format) was acquired with X-rays generated under a tube voltage of 80 kV, and the images were calibrated using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol for approximating Hounsfield units. The set was then reconstructed into cross-sections using Reconstructor software (Carl Zeiss X-ray Microscopy, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA) and analysed using CTAnalyser software (Bruker micro-CT, Kontich, Belgium). Three bone mineral density measurements derived from the gray level histogram were made at different places of the ribs in both cortical and trabecular bones The mean cortical bone density ranged from 0.62 to 0.79 g/cm3, and the mean trabecular bone density ranged from 0.42 to 0.54 g/cm3 (Fig. 1A, B, D and E).

After that, the three fresh ribs were fixed in a plastic container with utility wax, and then the container (12 × 8 cm) was filled with saline solution to simulate soft tissue attenuation and mimic clinical imaging conditions. Each phantom was positioned in a standardized position and scanned using a VistaVoxS 3D CBCT device (Dürr Dental Se, Bietigheim-Bissingen, Germany) at 4 acquisition protocols by varying the acquisition mode (HQ- high Quality and SD- Standard Quality), field-of-view size (5 × 5 cm and 13 × 5 cm), kVp (98,94 and 79), mA (11, 9 and 6) and voxel size (0.08 and 0.2 mm), as shown in Table 1, three times each. This range allowed us to evaluate the influence of acquisition parameters on image quality and measurement reliability across practical and high-demand scenarios. Examples of CBCT images acquired at Protocol 1 are shown in Fig. 1C and F. A total of 36 scans for each protocol under the same conditions were acquired from the three phantoms (3 ribs × 3 acquisitions × 4 protocols) to test the reproducibility of the software measurements.

After acquisition, DICOM files of the 144 scans were exported from the acquisition software and imported to the software AIS3DAPP 5.0 (De Götzen S.r.l. – Oligate Olona VA-Italy). Following a methodology from a previous study [14], a virtual implant measuring 10 mm × 5.0 mm was centered in the image of each rib. The implant was aligned perpendicular to the cortical surface of the superior border of the rib and positioned such that, the implant axis followed the vertical midline of the image slice. The apical third did not contact the inferior cortical plate, and the cervical third was fully embedded within the cortical bone. This standardized orientation was applied consistently across all scans and protocols to ensure comparability of gray value measurements. The gray value along three-thirds of the implant was measured by using the bone density tool available in the software (Fig. 2B). To better visualize the different densities in real clinical conditions around the virtual implant (Fig. 2A), the tool displays a color map threshold in a circled area surrounding the area of interest. High gray values are represented by green, followed in descending order by orange, brown, and red.

A cross was drawn above the circle created by the tool as a reference position to measure the gray values. Because the bone density quantification of an implant site depends on both cortical and trabecular bone, the mean gray values of the most anterior, posterior, left, and right values of the circled region of interest for each implant third and rib were used for statistical analysis. Additionally, we chose to test differences between FOVs and voxel sizes using comparable acquisition parameters, avoiding extreme combinations—such as comparing an SQ scan with a large FOV to an HQ scan with a small FOV—as these may introduce excessive variability and compromise reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post hoc tests to evaluate differences in gray value measurements among the acquisition protocols for each implant third (cervical, middle, and apical). A significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was used. The repeatability of measurements was tested across 36 scans per protocol (3 ribs × 3 repetitions × 4 protocols), and inter-protocol agreement was evaluated using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W).

Discussion

Many studies have investigated the limitations of cone-beam CT (CBCT) imaging for assessing bone density. CBCT systems have been widely used for dental diagnosis and treatment planning due to several advantages over multislice CT (MSCT), including lower radiation doses, shorter acquisition times, and greater accessibility in clinical settings, although they generally offer lower soft tissue contrast and resolution than MSCT. Despite the well-known advantages of CBCT for multiple diagnostic tasks in dentistry, implant planning is more accurate when it is performed using surgical software, which is also useful for bone quality and quantity measurements [15]. CBCT-based gray values correspond to the linear attenuation of anatomical structures or dental materials. The higher the density is, the more X-ray attenuation may occur, and the gray values increase, resulting in brighter values [7, 14]. In the present study, we evaluated bone density by measuring the gray values from a CBCT-based gray value measurement tool provided by an implant planning software. The CBCT-based bone density tool studied represented those values in green, indicating that they were greater in bone. In this study, gray values measured in the cervical third of the implant site were significantly lower compared to those in the middle and apical thirds across all acquisition protocols. However, it is important to consider that, according to our methodology, the cervical measurements may have been affected by the presence of buccal and lingual soft tissues surrounding the superior portion of the specimens (left and right regions of interest). These tissues could have lowered the local gray values and introduced variability in this region. Therefore, while the differences observed in the cervical area may have clinical relevance, the potential influence of soft tissue must be acknowledged as a limitation when interpreting the data and extrapolating it to clinical scenarios. It is important to provide evidence that although absolute CBCT gray values, unlike CT-derived gray value units, cannot be used for quantitative analysis of bone quality, they have demonstrated a strong linear correlation with the linear X-ray attenuation coefficient of bone types in all implant thirds of the virtual implant, corroborating previous studies [1, 2, 8, 16]. Our in vitro study effectively evaluated the CBCT gray value-based bone density tool integrated into implant planning software by assessing gray values in a controlled environment using fresh pork ribs. The results demonstrated high reproducibility, highlighting the tool’s potential clinical relevance. In clinical settings where CBCT is routinely used for bone density evaluation, focusing exclusively on CBCT acquisition protocols ensured a consistent and relevant assessment of the tool’s performance. However, it did not directly measure bone density, and further research is needed to validate its clinical applicability and to establish correlations with implant stability. Moreover, inaccurate bone density readings may lead to either unnecessary bone augmentation procedures or insufficient preparation, increasing the risk of implant failure. Understanding how acquisition parameters affect, these measurements enable clinicians to interpret CBCT data more accurately and tailor surgical protocols accordingly, such as adjusting drilling techniques, selecting appropriate implant designs, or planning for grafting when lower bone density is identified. Thus, standardizing imaging protocols or applying correction factors is essential to improve diagnostic reliability and optimize patient outcomes in implant dentistry.

Although Micro-CT was used to verify the density characteristics of the bone samples, a direct comparison with CBCT gray values was not performed, as our primary aim was to assess the behavior and clinical applicability of CBCT gray values independently, rather than to replicate previous studies that demonstrated the applicability of CBCT gray values for bone density measurements [5, 15]. Additionally, the Micro-CT files after reconstruction (bmp) were not compatible with the planning software we used and converting them into DICOM format could potentially alter the measurements. For the above-mentioned reasons, the present study focused on evaluating the repeatability of CBCT-based gray value measurements across different acquisition protocols, offering critical insights for implant dentistry, particularly when the tool becomes integrated into planning software for streamlined clinical implementation.

Some factors can influence gray values, such as the exposure parameters, field of view (FOV), and voxel size. Previous studies have shown that the smaller the field of view is, the better the image quality by reducing scatter radiation but increasing the contrast-to-noise ratio [10, 12, 17]. However, in the device we used in this study, we could not isolate those factors; we were able to test two FOVs (5 × 5 cm and 13 × 8 cm) and two different voxel sizes (0.08 and 0.2 mm). We observed that when the FOV was kept the same and the resolution was reduced, the device decreased the standard adult exposure parameters (kVp and mA), not the voxel sizes. Aside from potential variations in scatter and reconstruction, differences in gray values due to FOV size can be attributed to the varied endo- and exo-masses for small- and large-diameter FOVs and the higher noise production [10, 17, 18]. The penetrating power of an X-ray beam is regulated by the kVp. Every time an exposure is conducted, the X-rays need to be powerful enough to sufficiently penetrate through the target area. The likelihood that the X-ray beam can pass through denser or thicker material increases with increasing kVp. On the other hand, an increased tube current (mA) causes more electrons to be produced inside the X-ray tube, which increases the amount of X-radiation. More radiation means that more photons will be captured by the detector, which will cause a decrease in apparent structural density, but an increase in signal intensity will improve image quality by reducing noise and increasing radiation exposure [19]. This explains why our values were lower when the exposure parameters and FOVs were decreased, and we found this reproducibility for the middle and apical thirds of the implant, as also reported in a previous study [20]. This finding is clinically relevant because the exposure parameters can influence the radiation dose received by the patient, so we may reduce the acquisition parameters without affecting the gray value measurements, and then the CBCT-based bone density tool can properly identify the different attenuations. Nishino et al. reported that even with identical radiation fields, CBCT voxel values vary depending on the location of the z-axis of the object in the radiation field [21].

One of our greatest limitations was the inability to test the tool in human subjects, as exposing the same patient to multiple CBCT scans with varying acquisition parameters would result in excessive radiation exposure, making such an approach ethically unacceptable. Future research could address this limitation through ethically approved retrospective analyses of archived CBCT scans and clinical data, allowing in vivo validation without additional radiation exposure to patients. Additionally, the use of ex vivo mandibles present challenges in preserving the freshness of bony structures, which could impact measurement accuracy. Another limitation of our study was the absence of metallic restorations in the vicinity of the virtual implants. This controlled study design was essential to eliminate confounding factors and isolate the effects of imaging parameters on gray value reproducibility. However, in clinical practice, metallic objects are common and can introduce significant artifacts, such as beam hardening and streaking, which may affect the accuracy of gray value measurements. To validate these findings, future research should focus on clinical cases involving metallic artifacts and utilize retrospective data to assess the tool’s performance in vivo without additional radiation exposure. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these results to real-world scenarios.

Conclusions

The bone density measurement tool evaluated in this study proved reliable and accurate across various CBCT acquisition protocols. Our findings highlighted that field of view settings have a greater impact on gray value consistency than voxel size, underscoring the importance of selecting appropriate FOV parameters to ensure measurement reliability. Additionally, the significant differences in gray values observed in the cervical region suggest that clinicians should interpret bone density data in this area cautiously, considering possible influences from adjacent soft tissues.
