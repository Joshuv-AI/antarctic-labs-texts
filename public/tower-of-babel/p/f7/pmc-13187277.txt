Codon usage patterns and phylogenetic analysis of chloroplast genomes reveal evolutionary insights into Asphodelaceae species
Authors: Kun Zhang, Ken Li, Jiaying Feng, Wanying Liu, Jiayin Feng, Weiqiang Fan, Xiaofei Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The phenomenon of codon usage bias (CUB) is prevalent across various genomes and offers critical insights into the molecular genetic evolution of organisms, as well as their adaptive strategies. Comparative analyses of CUB in chloroplast (cp.) genomes further deepen our understanding of the intricate phylogenetic relationships across taxonomic groups. The Asphodelaceae family, a globally distributed plant group with significant ecological and economic value, has been extensively applied in traditional medicine, landscape beautification, and nutritional supplementation. In this study, we systematically analyzed the codon usage patterns and phylogenetic relationships of 13 diverse Asphodelaceae species based on their cp. genomes. Our findings indicated that both base composition and codon usage preferences were highly similar among the 13 cp. genomes examined. The Asphodelaceae species displayed a pronounced bias toward A/T-rich nucleotides and A/T-ending codons; notably, the corresponding values of the effective number of codons (ENC) were in the range of 35–61, indicative of a relatively weak CUB in this family. Each species contained 30 identical high-frequency codons and 13–18 individual optimal codons, presenting a striking prevalence of terminating in A/T nucleotides. The findings from the ENC plot, neutrality plot, and parity rule 2 plot analyses augmented the inference that both natural selection and mutation pressure exert influence on the codon usage patterns of Asphodelaceae species. Correlation analysis indicated a significant association between codon bias indicators and genomic nucleotide composition (particularly GC3), suggesting that compositional constraints correlate with and may underlie the observed CUB patterns. In addition, the genetic relationships inferred from the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were basically consistent with the phylogenetic topology derived from the cp. protein-coding genes. All taxa within the studied genera of the Asphodelaceae family exhibited strong phylogenetic clustering, thereby confirming that species with closer evolutionary relationships tend to share more similar codon usage preferences. This finding illustrates that RSCU-based clustering analysis can effectively supplement sequence-based phylogenetics in elucidating evolutionary relationships among species at the family level. The CUB of cp. genomes in Asphodelaceae plants is relatively weak and is subject to the combined effects of multiple factors. All taxa within the studied genera of the Asphodelaceae family have been confirmed to be closely related to each other in terms of their evolutionary relationships as well as the codon usage patterns of their cp. genomes. This study elucidates the codon usage patterns of Asphodelaceae species and enhances the understanding of codon biology and genetic evolution across a diverse range of organisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46203-8.

Introduction

The Asphodelaceae family includes 41 genera comprising approximately 1,200 species widely distributed in Africa, Asia, the Mediterranean region, and Australia (Plants of the World Online, https://powo.science.kew.org/). As the largest genus within the Asphodelaceae family, Aloe L. primarily inhabits arid climates and is widely distributed across Africa, India, and other dry regions. This genus encompasses a diverse group of perennial succulent plants that comprise 590 species widely known and used as food and medicine1. Aloidendron pillansii (L.Guthrie) Klopper & Gideon F.Sm. is a striking and rare succulent tree native to the arid regions of Namibia and South Africa. It has previously been classified in the Aloe genus and is characterized by its unique, sculptural appearance and remarkable adaptability to extreme desert conditions2. The genus Hemerocallis L. is a collection of perennial herbs with about 16 species that diverged in East Asia3. There are approximately 11 species native to China, among which Hemerocallis fulva (L.) L. and Hemerocallis citrina Baroni are considered to possess significant economic value4. Eremurus M. Bieb represents a genus of perennial herbaceous plants characterized by unique traits, including leafless flowering stems and rhizomatous rootstocks. It encompasses over 50 species naturally distributed in Central and West Asia5,6. Additionally, the Asphodelaceae taxa also include characteristic plant species such as Xanthorrhoea preissii Endl., Dianella ensifolia (L.) DC., and Kniphofia uvaria (L.) Oken. Xanthorrhoea preissii stands as an emblematic grass tree native to southwestern Australia. This slow-growing perennial species distinguishes itself through exceptional drought tolerance and fire resilience traits and serves as a critical component in maintaining local ecosystems7. Dianella ensifolia is a popular perennial herb prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions of Asia, Australia, and the Pacific islands8. This species is well known for its ecological adaptability, ornamental value, and medicinal uses9,10. Kniphofia uvaria, a perennial herbaceous plant native to South Africa, is highly prized in ornamental gardening for its distinctive architectural form and extended blooming period11. Altogether, it can be concluded that the Asphodelaceae family encompasses numerous species of significant economic and ecological value, which have long been appreciated for their medicinal, ornamental, and nutritional importance.

Chloroplasts (cp.), the semi-autonomous organelles governing photosynthesis and plant metabolism, possess compact genomes characterized by structural conservation and slower nucleotide substitution rates relative to nuclear and mitochondrial genomes12,13. These attributes make cp. genomes invaluable tools for elucidating plant diversification and evolutionary processes14,15. Codons, the fundamental link between nucleic acids and proteins, play crucial roles in carrying and transmitting instructions for genetic information. Redundant codons that are decrypted into identical amino acids are termed synonymous codons; they exhibit markedly disparate frequencies of use during protein synthesis across various organisms16. The preference for specific codons, referred to as codon usage bias (CUB), is a phenomenon of distinct codon usage across different genes or genomes17. This phenomenon arises from evolutionary selection favoring optimal codons—those that are preferentially used in organisms. Codon usage bias significantly impacts protein translation efficiency; genes encoded by optimal codons typically exhibit high expression levels, thus maintaining the stability of genetic information while optimizing functionality throughout evolution18. It is evident that the utilization of CUB is a consequence of deciphering the optimization strategy and plays a vital role in gene expression regulation17,19. The emergence of CUB in specific genes or genomes is imposed by a complex interplay of evolutionary forces. Previous studies suggest that this bias is primarily influenced by natural selection, mutation pressure, and random genetic drift20. Furthermore, factors of complexity, including tRNA abundance, GC content, gene length, and expression levels, along with other genomic attributes, have been demonstrated to impart their influence on codon usage patterns18,21–23. While codon usage patterns vary substantially across taxa, closely related species or those occupying similar ecological habits seem to retain conserved biases24,25. Consequently, investigating codon bias is essential not only for deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying plant gene expression but also for offering perspectives on the long-term evolution of the species26,27.

With the continuous advancement of high-throughput sequencing technology, an expanding array of cp. genomes has been decoded across various organisms, providing foundational resources for studies on the evolution and classification of plant species4,15. Integrated CUB analyses across cp. genomes offer critical insights into molecular evolutionary patterns and hold significant promise for guiding genetic improvement strategies. Recent efforts have substantially expanded the cp. genome database for Asphodelaceae species28–31. However, comprehensive characterization of the codon usage patterns across this family remains elusive, with phylogenetic relationships among corresponding taxa still awaiting systematic resolution. In addition to the continuous research on phytochemical constituents and pharmacological actions, accelerating the genetic improvement and evolutionary understanding of the Asphodelaceae plants can unlock their full potential while ensuring long-term conservation. In this study, we implemented a comparative analysis of codon usage patterns among 13 cp. genomes within the Asphodelaceae family while focusing on their corresponding influencing factors and optimal codons. We reconstructed the phylogenetic relationships among Asphodelaceae species employing cp. protein-coding sequences and compared the resulting topology with that inferred from relative synonymous codon usage values. This work enriches our understanding of the evolutionary progress and adaptability of Asphodelaceae species and lays a solid foundation for further research on genetic improvement and biological resource conservation within this family.

Materials and methods

Genome acquisition and processing

The entire cp. genomes of 13 Asphodelaceae species (i.e., Hemerocallis citrina, Hemerocallis fulva, Hemerocallis minor Mill., Xanthorrhoea preissii, Aloe arborescens Mill., Aloe maculata All., Aloe vera (L.) Burm.f., Aloidendron pillansii, Eremurus robustus (Regel) Regel, Eremurus chinensis O. Fedtsch., Eremurus zoae Vved., Dianella ensifolia, and Kniphofia uvaria) were retrieved through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed on 11 November 2024). The basic information on the cp. genomes of all 13 Asphodelaceae species is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Regarding the sequence selection criteria for codon preference analysis32, appropriate coding sequences (CDSs) were extracted from their complete cp. genomes based on the following rules: (1) minimum length of 300 bp with triplet nucleotide alignment; (2) exclusion of duplicate sequences and sequences containing ambiguous bases (non-ATGC characters); (3) canonical initiation (ATG) and stop codons (TAG/TGA/TAA); (4) elimination of sequences containing intermediate termination codons.

Codon composition and index analysis

The CDSs of all 13 Asphodelaceae cp. genomes were processed using the CodonW v1.4.2 program33 and a series of codon usage indicators, including (1) the codon adaptation index (CAI), (2) the codon bias index (CBI), (3) the frequency of optimal codons (Fop), (4) the effective number of codons (ENC), and (5) the average GC composition at the third position of the synonymous codons (GC3s), were obtained to determine CUB. CAI was calculated using highly expressed genes as the reference set, in accordance with the program’s default settings. The reference gene set was composed of 11 genes (rps8-14-18, psbA, petD, atpF, rpl16-22, ndhF-G, and accD). The nucleotide composition was analyzed using MEGA v11.0.1334, specifically evaluating the third-position base frequencies of the synonymous codons (A3s, C3s, G3s, and T3s). The EMBOSS CUSP suite (http://www.bioinformatics.nl/emboss-explorer/) was further employed for analyses of genomic GC composition (GCall) and GC frequencies across codon sites (GC1, GC2, and GC3). Pearson correlations among the main codon usage parameters were calculated using the Correlation Plot module of Origin v2022 (OriginLab Corporation, USA).

Evaluation of relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU)

RSCU quantifies the deviation between the usage frequency of synonymous codons and their expected theoretical frequency, reflecting the usage bias of particular codon compositions encoding amino acids35. When RSCU equals 1, the codon usage aligns with random expectations. An RSCU value greater than 1 classifies a codon as a high-frequency codon with a strong positive usage bias for a specific amino acid. Conversely, a low specific frequency indicates a negative usage bias36. We conducted RSCU quantification across the 13 Asphodelaceae species using CodonW v1.4.233, with subsequent analysis implemented in TBtools v2.14937 to generate a codon usage heatmap.

Identification of optimal codon

Optimal codons are the evolutionarily favored codons that are identified by computing the ENC score of all genes. High-frequency codons with ENC differences above a certain threshold are generally identified as optimal codons38. Overall, highly expressed genes represent stronger codon preferences, resulting in low ENC values39. For each Asphodelaceae species, the cp. genes were stratified by ENC quantiles, with extreme deciles (top/bottom 10%) constituting high- and low-bias gene groups. ΔRSCU values were derived from the RSCU differentials between these two groups. High-frequency codons that satisfied the condition ΔRSCU > 0.08 were recognized as optimal codons25.

Analysis of evolutionary drivers

Three analytical approaches were used to evaluate the predominant determinants shaping the CUB of cp. genomes: ENC plot, parity rule 2 plot (PR2 plot), and neutrality plot. ENC is an important indicator reflecting the degree of unbalanced use preference of synonymous codons40; its value generally spans 20–61, with lower values denoting stronger bias, and vice versa41. An ENC threshold of 35 is conventionally established as the critical demarcation for evaluating codon bias magnitude41. ENC-GC3s correlation analysis was implemented, plotting GC3s (x-axis) against ENC (y-axis) for each gene to identify the dominant factors shaping CUB. The standard curve (Formula 1) was superimposed onto the plot, delineating genomic loci governed solely by neutral mutational forces40. Neutrality plot analysis is applied to intuitively display the correlation between codon base composition, thereby revealing the role of evolutionary forces in codon preference32,43. In the presented graph, the individual cp. genes of the 13 Asphodelaceae species are denoted by discrete points. We plotted GC12 (the average value of GC1 and GC2) on the vertical axis against GC3 on the horizontal axis to establish a rectangular coordinate system for regression analysis. When the regression slope approximates 1, a significant correlation between GC12 and GC3 indicates that mutation pressure dominates the CUB. Conversely, a slope approaching 0 suggests a lack of significant correlation, implying that natural selection overrides other factors in driving codon preference44.

Genomic analyses establish that the formation of codon usage patterns is closely related to the third-position base composition of the codon45,46. The PR2 plot analysis is typically employed to identify base parity at fourfold degenerate codon sites and to estimate the impact of natural selection, mutation pressure, or other factors on CUB47. A scatterplot was generated for G3/(G3 + C3) and A3/(A3 + T3) as abscissa and ordinate, respectively. The central point of the plot, where G = C and A = T, illustrates that codon bias is entirely affected by mutation. When the distribution of genes deviates from the center, it is assumed that the codon preference may be influenced by factors other than mutation, such as natural selection. Thus, the degree to which the distribution deviates from the center can evaluate the direction of deviation between mutation pressure and natural selection47,48.

Analysis of evolutionary relationships

A cluster analysis was conducted in SPSS v25.0 (IBM Corp., USA) using the RSCU indices from Asphodelaceae cp. genomes. Each species was treated as an independent entity, with RSCU values for 59 codons (excluding two non-synonymous and three termination codons) serving as input variables. The cluster pedigree was subsequently performed via squared Euclidean distance calculations49. The phylogenetic trees were designed using protein-coding sequences of Asphodelaceae species. Single-copy orthologous genes were extracted using OrthoFinder v3.0.1b150, and the corresponding sequences were aligned and concatenated using MAFFT v7.01751 and PhyloSuite v1.2.352, respectively. ModelFinder53 utilized the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to identify the optimal evolutionary model (CpREV + G+ I + F). Subsequently, a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.1254, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Bayesian inference (BI) phylogenetic reconstruction employed the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method, incorporating the CpREV + F + I + G4 substitution model identified using ModelFinder under BIC optimization53. BI phylogenies were then generated using MrBayes v3.2.655, executing 200,000 generations with tree sampling at 1,000-generation intervals. Following a 25% burn-in removal, the retained iterations were utilized to construct a consensus tree. Final phylogenetic tree visualization and annotation were performed using iTOL v756.

Results

Features of codon base composition

The number of filtered CDSs extracted from the 13 Asphodelaceae species is presented in Supplementary Table S1. The analyzed cp. genomes exhibit significant compositional conservation, characterized by higher AT and lower GC contents. As shown in Table 1, the expression frequency of third-position nucleotides of the synonymous codons was asymmetrical, following this trend: T3s (46.46%–47.19%)> A3s (43.24%–43.93%)> C3s (16.04%–16.84%)> G3s (15.67%–16.57%). At the same time, the GC contents of the overall genome (GCall) and all three loci of codon GC1, GC2, and GC3 remained below 50%, emphasizing a preference for codons containing and ending with A/T bases in Asphodelaceae species. Additionally, the GC contents at the three loci of the 13 genomes were not balanced, conforming to the order ranking of GC1 > GC2> GC3. Furthermore, the GC contents of the third position of the synonymous codons (GC3s) were notably less than 30%, reinforcing Asphodelaceae’s pronounced codon-terminal A/T bias. The differences in the base content of the codons were all within 1%, indicating that the cp. protein-coding genes (PCGs) from the 13 Asphodelaceae species were highly conserved in the nucleotide composition of coding regions.

Determination of the codon usage indicator

We analyzed the codon usage indicators to evaluate codon bias within Asphodelaceae cp. genomes. The results demonstrated tightly clustered ENC values spanning 47.22–47.94, with a mean of 47.44 across the 13 cp. genomes (Table 2). Only four genes (0.60% of the total 664 genes) with ENC values less than 35 exhibited strong codon usage preferences. Almost all the genes displayed ENC values exceeding the 35 threshold, with a substantial subset (17.32%) surpassing 50, collectively reflecting fairly weak CUB in Asphodelaceae family. Furthermore, the CAI value was 0.17, far less than 1, and the CBI and Fop values were clustered around − 0.10–−0.09 and 0.35–0.36, respectively. Altogether, these results suggest that codon usage preferences are relatively weak in the 13 Asphodelaceae plants analyzed.

Codon usage patterns across Asphodelaceae species

The results of RSCU analysis of the Asphodelaceae cp. genome sequences are presented in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S2. A total of 59 synonymous codons were extracted in the analysis, excluding two non-synonymous codons (UGG and AUG) and three termination codons (UAG, UGA, and UAA). The distribution of RSCU values of the cp. genome codons coincided with each other, demonstrating that the preferences for codon usage are highly similar among 13 Asphodelaceae species (Fig. 1). Each species had 30 identical high-frequency codons (RSCU > 1), 28 of which terminated with A/U (T) (93.33%) and two with G/C (6.67%). Furthermore, among the 30 high-frequency codons identified in cp. genomes, the codon exhibiting the highest RSCU value was UUA encoding leucine (Leu), followed by AGA encoding arginine (Arg) (Supplementary Table S2). These results demonstrate that the codon usage patterns exhibit significant similarities across all the analyzed Asphodelaceae plants, with a notable bias in the cp. genomes toward codons ending in A/U (T).

Optimal codon analysis

The optimal codons for Asphodelaceae species were determined by comparing the RSCU values derived from the two extreme bias gene groups established by ENC differentials (Supplementary Table S3). We found a total of 202 optimal codons comprising 20 specific types in these cp. genomes. The vast majority of optimal codons comprised A or U (T) at the terminal position, accounting for over 90% of the total. There were subtle variations in optimal codons among these species, even within the same genus. As statistically compiled in Supplementary Table S4, the number of optimal codons in Asphodelaceae cp. genomes ranged from 13 in Aloidendron pillansii to 18 in Aloe arborescens. Additionally, the analysis revealed that the Asphodelaceae species contained nine identical optimal codons (AAA, ACU, AGU, AUU, CCU, CGU, GCU, GGU, and UGU), and all of them exhibited A/U (T) termination without exception, revealing the AT bias in their codon usage patterns.

Analysis of the source of variation in codon usage

Correlation analysis

To evaluate the impact of the composition characteristics on CUB, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed between the essential indicators of codon usage (Fig. 2). Significant positive correlations were observed between GCall and the GC content indexes at different codon positions, indicating a strong overall correlation in composition among the three codon loci in the 13 cp. genomes of the Asphodelaceae plants. The ENC value showed a significant positive correlation with GC3 and GC3s (P < 0.01), indicating that the base composition of the synonymous site has a crucial influence on CUB. Except for Xanthorrhoea preissii and three Hemerocallis species, the results suggested that CAI, CBI, and Fop were significantly correlated with GC3, implying that GC3 contributes significantly to codon bias. CAI, CBI, and Fop were also significantly correlated with GCall, indicating that GCall may be a necessary factor affecting the CUB of Asphodelaceae species.

ENC plot analysis

ENC plot analysis offers an approach for detecting codon usage variations and is instrumental in understanding the influence of GC3s on codon preference. Based on the outcomes of the ENC plot analysis using the cpDNA, a similar distribution of CDSs was observed in the 13 Asphodelaceae species (Fig. 3). In these plots, only a subset of genes was positioned above or near the standard curve, implying that their codon preferences were weak and significantly impacted by base mutation40. In contrast, a large number of genes exhibited deviations from the ideal curve, suggesting that natural selection has exerted considerable influence on codon usage patterns. Meanwhile, only four genes (rps14 in Dianella ensifolia and three Hemerocallis species) were distributed far below the curve, indicating strong codon bias. This observation implies that the codon usage patterns within the Asphodelaceae family may be shaped by the joint effects of multiple factors, including mutation pressure and natural selection.

Neutrality plot analysis

To further evaluate the extent of influence exerted by various factors on the CUB of the Asphodelaceae family, a neutrality plot analysis was performed by calculating the GC content of codons in different positions. As shown in Fig. 5, narrow distribution ranges were observed for GC12 and GC3, and only a few genes were diagonally distributed in the scatterplots. The slopes of the regression lines for the 13 Asphodelaceae genomes ranged from − 0.07 to 0.17, indicating that the effect of mutation pressure accounted for less than 20% of the codon usage patterns. Notably, the slope for Xanthorrhoea preissii (k=−0.0062) was extremely close to 0, revealing that natural selection had a strong influence on the CUB of its cpDNA. The absolute slope values were consistently less than 0.02, with most not exceeding 0.01, supporting the hypothesis that natural selection is the primary determinant of codon usage patterns across the Asphodelaceae plants. Furthermore, no significant correlation was observed between GC12 and GC3 (P > 0.05), indicating greater diversity in base composition at each site within the cpDNA codons across all examined species in this family. In summary, the CUB of the analyzed Asphodelaceae species was probably intensely driven by natural selection.

PR2 plot analysis

We used PR2 plot analysis to visualize the bias relationship concerning the third base of codons and comprehensively assess multiple factors affecting CUB. As depicted in Fig. 6, the genes in cp. genomes of the 13 Asphodelaceae species were unevenly distributed within the PR2 plane; a substantial proportion resided in the fourth quadrant where G3/(G3 + C3) > 0.5 and A3/(A3 + T3) < 0.5—revealing a higher frequency of G and T use as third bases in their respective codons. Only a few genes approached the central axis, suggesting that their observed codon bias mainly resulted from mutations. However, most of the genes deviated from the center, demonstrating that other factors, such as natural selection, play critical roles alongside genetic mutation in influencing overall codon usage patterns within the Asphodelaceae family. In conclusion, the unbalanced usage of bases again reveals that natural selection may be the dominant factor affecting the CUB in the Asphodelaceae family, with mutation pressure and other factors playing a lesser role.

Clustering of codon usage preferences and phylogenetic analyses

RSCU-based clustering analysis was conducted to more precisely understand the divergence in codon usage in the Asphodelaceae cp. genomes. The results indicated that the 13 species in this family formed two distinct clusters (Fig. 7A). Three Hemerocallis representatives, along with Xanthorrhoea preissii and Dianella ensifolia, were classified into one cluster, indicating a high degree of similarity in their codon usage patterns. The other cluster included Aloidendron pillansii and Kniphofia uvaria, along with three representatives each of Eremurus and Aloe.

Phylogenetic trees were constructed using ML and BI approaches based on an aligned data matrix of 44 single-copy orthologous genes from these species. As shown in Fig. 7B, both the ML and BI trees exhibited consistent phylogenetic typology, and most branches received strong support, evidenced by high bootstrap values and posterior probabilities, underscoring the strong reliability of the phylogenetic relationships revealed by PCGs. The branching patterns observed in the phylogenetic trees were generally consistent with the clustering results derived from RSCU values (Fig. 7B). In both phylogenetic tree models, the 13 Asphodelaceae species in the Asphodelaceae family were categorized into two primary clades. Within the first clade, three Eremurus representatives formed a sister branch with Kniphofia uvaria, while three Aloe species grouped closely with Aloidendron pillansii, all nested within a branch with Xanthorrhoea preissii. The second clade consisted of two branches: a larger branch comprising three Hemerocallis representatives and a separate branch with Dianella ensifolia. All taxa studied in the Asphodelaceae family demonstrated effective clustering characteristics, with species belonging to the same genus grouping closely together within a single clade.

Discussion

Codon usage preferences refer to the unequal use of synonymous codons in gene coding, representing a significant aspect of genome evolution. Studies have revealed that different organisms or genes exhibit unique codon usage patterns57. Continued analysis of cp. genomes has prompted the exploration of codon bias across various taxa, including Euphorbiaceae58, Theaceae59, Rutaceae60, Halimedineae61, and Aroideae62. However, the codon usage patterns within the Asphodelaceae cp. genomes remain poorly characterized, and the phylogenetic relationships among its constituent taxa require further clarification. Here, we systematically analyzed the codon usage patterns, factors influencing CUB, and phylogenetic relationships among cp. genomes of the 13 Asphodelaceae species. Our findings regarding codon usage patterns and phylogenetic relationships could help optimize heterologous genes and genetic evolution.

Our composition analysis revealed an average GC content of 37.83% across these species, with a progressive decline observed in GC1, GC2, and GC3 values; this indicates a predominantly AT-rich environment as opposed to one rich in GC content. Previous studies have reported significant differences in nucleotide preferences between monocotyledons and dicotyledons in their cp. genomes63,64. Specifically, dicotyledons favor AT-enrichment, while monocotyledons exhibit GC-richness and biases toward C- or G-terminated codons63,64. Our analyses of Asphodelaceae species revealed a preference for A/T-rich nucleotides and A/T-terminated codons, consistent with patterns observed in dicotyledons, but different from the C or G endings favored by monocotyledons63,64. This observation aligns with previous research on monocotyledonous groups such as Aroideae62, indicating that organisms have evolved unique codon usage patterns to enhance adaptation to their respective environments. Moreover, the high-frequency and optimal codons in the 13 Asphodelaceae species are predominantly A/T-ending, mirroring tendencies also ascertained in other plant cp. genomes, including those from Euphorbiaceae58, Rutaceae60, and Vitaceae65. This shared bias may reflect convergent evolutionary adaptations of Asphodelaceae species to arid or drought-prone environments. Collectively, our findings further substantiate that GC composition is the factor that most directly reflects codon usage patterns.

Since the advent of genomics, researchers have persistently explored the determinants of CUB across various genomes. Several theoretical frameworks have been developed to elucidate the mechanisms behind CUB variations. Among these, two predominant models have gained widespread acceptance: the selection–mutation–drift model20 and the neutral theory66. While multiple factors contribute to CUB, its evolutionary trajectory primarily reflects a balance between selective pressures and mutational forces. Previous studies on Helianthus annuus L.67 indicate that mutation pressure is the main force driving the observed CUB. Nevertheless, empirical evidence demonstrates that natural selection is more prominent in influencing codon usage patterns in most cp. genomes58,68,69. Our investigation revealed distinct codon usage distribution patterns, with a limited number of genes aligning with the expected curve, while the majority exhibited dispersed positioning in the ENC plot. This distribution pattern suggests a relatively minor role of mutation pressure in shaping codon usage patterns within the cp. genomes of Asphodelaceae species. Evaluating the neutrality and PR2 plots showed that the observed codon usage patterns in the Asphodelaceae family are primarily influenced by natural selection and mutation pressure, with natural selection being the decisive factor. Furthermore, analyzing the correlation of codon usage indicators revealed that the CUB patterns identified in Asphodelaceae species are largely an effect of the genome’s nucleotide composition, especially the GC content at the third codon position. Therefore, we infer that compositional constraints are also involved in framing these codon usage patterns. Together, these findings demonstrate that the codon usage patterns in the Asphodelaceae cp. genomes arise from a confluence of multiple evolutionary forces, including but not restricted to natural selection and mutation pressure, with particular emphasis on the former.

Codon usage analysis has significant implications in the field of biotechnology. Codon optimization—an approach capable of modulating gene expression efficiency—has been proposed as a crucial strategy in transgenic research27. Studies have demonstrated that optimal codons enhance both accuracy and efficiency during gene translation, resulting in elevated expression levels70,71. Consequently, investigating characteristics associated with codon usage preferences can provide dependable data to improve the expression efficiency of exogenous target genes and increase the yield of desired products in host organisms. In our study, we found considerable conservation of cp. genomes across the 13 evaluated Asphodelaceae species, which shared a total of 30 commonly used high-frequency codons. In addition, each species contained 13–18 optimal codons. Of these, a total of nine identical optimal codons—AAA, ACU, AGU, AUU, CCU, CGU, GCU, GGU, and UGU—were identified across the Asphodelaceae species, probably reflecting their ecological adaptability in extreme environments. A striking prevalence of A/T-terminated codons was observed among both high-frequency and optimal codons, aligning with previous findings in diverse plant lineages, including Rutaceae60, Halimedineae61, and Aconitum L72. The results derived from our analysis contribute essential information for refining codon optimization strategies and promoting significant applications in cp. genetic engineering.

The study of plant phylogeny from the perspective of the cp. genome has emerged as a prominent approach in contemporary taxonomic research. The diversity of the CUB across different species offers significant insights for taxonomic classification and molecular evolution. Current evidence suggests a potential correlation between phylogenetic relationships and codon usage preferences within species61,65,73,74. In this study, we inferred the phylogenetic relationships among Asphodelaceae species using both RSCU values and cp. PCG sequences, seeking to compare the topological concordance and divergence of these two molecular approaches. The topological structure of the phylogenetic tree was highly congruent with the clustering tree derived from RSCU values. Both molecular reconstructions consistently categorized the 13 Asphodelaceae species into two major clades. All Asphodelaceae taxa exhibited strong phylogenetic clustering, with species belonging to the same genus forming well-supported monophyletic clades. For instance, three Aloe representatives—Aloe arborescens, Aloe maculata, and Aloe vera—formed a distinct branch in both the phylogenetic tree and the clustering lineage, suggesting that species with closer evolutionary relationships may share more similar codon usage preferences. This is also reflected in the Hemerocallis and Eremurus branches. Moreover, in both trees, the three Aloe species formed a sister clade to Aloidendron pillansii, supporting the hypothesis of independent evolution within the Aloe lineage following its divergence from a common ancestor with Aloidendron. However, it is worth noting that the results derived from clustering based on codon bias do not entirely align with those obtained from constructing a phylogenetic tree based on PCGs. Specifically, Xanthorrhoea preissii was closely related to three Hemerocallis representatives in the clustering tree but formed a larger group with branches containing Kniphofia uvaria, Aloidendron pillansii, and three representatives each of Eremurus and Aloe in the phylogenetic tree. Additionally, Kniphofia uvaria formed a sister branch to the Eremurus branch in the phylogenetic tree but formed a separate branch in the second clade of the clustering tree. These discrepancies indicate that genetic relationships based solely on codon bias may omit crucial evolutionary information because weak or non-preferred codons can also significantly influence the evolution of Asphodelaceae. Our findings are fairly consistent with previous reports on cp. genomes in Halimedineae61 and Vitaceae65, providing evidence that RSCU-based clustering prediction constitutes a valuable complement to sequence-based phylogenetic analysis. Overall, the results derived from this study emphasize the critical role of codon usage patterns in elucidating phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary divergences among plants through comparative cp. genomics.

Conclusions

In this study, we present a systematic investigation of codon usage patterns in the cp. genomes of Asphodelaceae species, complemented by a comprehensive assessment of the evolutionary forces shaping CUB. Our analyses revealed a pronounced preference for A/T-rich nucleotides and A/T-ending codons across the cp. genomes of all species. Extensive analytical approaches were employed to evaluate the extent and determinants of CUB, as illustrated by estimates of codon usage characteristic indicators, correlation analysis, ENC plot assessment, neutrality plot investigation, and PR2 plot evaluation. The results demonstrated that the Asphodelaceae cp. genomes have relatively weak CUB, reflecting the interplay of various evolutionary factors. While natural selection may have emerged as the dominant driver of CUB, additional influences from mutation pressure and compositional constraints were also evident. The use of high-frequency and optimal codons showed remarkable consistency across the 13 Asphodelaceae species, with a total of 30 commonly shared high-frequency codons and 13–18 optimal codons individually screened for each species. Cluster and phylogenetic analyses revealed that even less-preferred codons may contribute to the adaptive evolution of Asphodelaceae species. RSCU-based clustering analysis provides valuable insight into the phylogenetic relationships of Asphodelaceae species and may help resolve long-standing taxonomic ambiguities. In conclusion, this study sheds light on the evolutionary mechanisms underlying CUB in Asphodelaceae species, providing a new perspective that not only promotes codon optimization for precision genetic engineering but also facilitates phylogenetic construction across a wide range of species through codon preference profiling.
