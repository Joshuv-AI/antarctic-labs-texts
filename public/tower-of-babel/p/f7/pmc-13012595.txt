Introduction

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) is a variety of clinical syndromes categorized by the presence of ANCAs and inflammatory infiltration of the walls of small and medium-sized blood vessels, resulting in necrosis.1 More than 85% of patients with these clinical syndromes present with ANCAs against proteinase-3 (PR3) with or without myeloperoxidase (MPO). Interestingly, infections can also trigger the formation of ANCAs by provoking an immune response. Consequently, PR3- or MPO-ANCAs are part of the pathophysiology in patients with infective endocarditis (IE).1

There are three different types of subacute bacterial endocarditis (SBE)-related rheumatological diseases. The first presents classically like systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with a positive ANA and glomerulonephritis secondary to immune complex deposition. If a renal biopsy is obtained, it will most likely show immune reactants.2 The second presentation is ANCA-positive without systemic involvement notable of AAV.3 Lastly, the third is an ANCA-positive disease with kidney, lung, and skin changes that suggest that it is ANCA-related.3

These immunological syndromes can be the cause of valvular destruction or occur because of an infected valve. Thus, differentiating IE from ANCA-associated vasculitis is consequential to guiding appropriate management. This case highlights the importance of cardiac workup with diagnosis of AAV because the etiology of the valvular pathology dramatically alters the management and delivery of care.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old man presented to a pulmonology clinic with a persistent cough for three months. His past medical history included atrial fibrillation that precipitated tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy that improved after treatment. His echocardiogram six months prior was significant for his tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, and the one two months prior showed return to normal ejection fraction after guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). Over the previous three months, he presented to the emergency department (ED) twice for the cough and was treated both times with systemic corticosteroids. On the second ED visit, a contrasted Chest CT scan demonstrated multiple scattered 4-mm pulmonary nodules without significant mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

In the pulmonology clinic, he presented with 16-kilograms of unintentional weight loss, a productive cough provoking emesis, and a petechial rash on his legs extending to his thighs without involvement of his trunk, upper extremities, palms, or soles (Figure 1). He denied fever, hemoptysis, or hematuria. His cardiothoracic physical exam demonstrated regular rate and rhythm with a short burst of irregular rhythm without murmur. The constellation of pulmonary nodules, petechial rash, and persistent cough prompted the diagnostic workup for ANCA-associated vasculitis. Initial labs included a negative ANA, a creatinine of 1.3 mg/dL (reference: 0.6–1.3), an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 33 mm/h (reference: ≤ 11), and a positive ANCA. He was referred to Rheumatology. His labs at initial rheumatology visit were notable for a significant increase in his creatinine to 2.4 mg/dl and his ESR to 64 mm/h. His blood counts showed white blood cells of 14.6 k/mm3 (reference: 3.6–10.6), platelets 187 k/µl (reference: 150–450), and hemoglobin of 13.1 gm/dl (reference: 13–17). His PR3 was elevated to 2.6 U/ml (reference: ≤ 0.9), and his MPO and perinuclear ANCA were negative. His urinalysis was significant for 1+ proteinuria and 3+ hematuria.

Bilateral lower extremities with petechiae.

He was admitted to the hospital and initially started on high-dose corticosteroids to treat the vasculitis with plans for a renal biopsy. The practice at our facility, based on experience, is to obtain an initial set of blood cultures to rule out infectious causes, given the known association of PR-3 SBE-related ANCA vasculitis. These blood cultures resulted in positive for Streptococcus sanguinis, raising concern for IE as an etiology of this syndrome and prompting cardiac imaging for a more definitive evaluation. The transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal systolic function with severe aortic insufficiency and a large mobile vegetation on the aortic valve. His transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrated a tri-leaflet aortic valve with large mobile vegetation measuring 16 mm with an eccentric posteriorly directed jet of regurgitation with severe aortic insufficiency and an associated aortic root abscess that extended into the aortomitral curtain (Figure 2). In the context of the above, corticosteroids were discontinued after receiving two doses of methylprednisolone 500 mg IV. He was started on antibiotics, ceftriaxone 2000 mg IV daily. Cardiovascular surgery performed a bio-Bentall aortic root replacement within 36 h. Blood cultures were persistently positive prior to surgery. Intraoperatively, the aortic valve was bicuspid and perforated, with the primary source of infection beneath the valve that had eroded the mitral curtain and the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve. The first set of negative cultures was postoperative day one from this initial surgery and remained negative throughout his hospitalization. His postoperative course was complicated by progressive mitral regurgitation secondary to suspected dehiscence of the aorto-mitral curtain, resulting in poor respiratory status. Ten days after the initial surgery, he required a redo bio-Bentall with a bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement. Intraoperatively, the sutures from the bovine patch were partially dehisced. The patch was removed and there was only one centimeter of residual septum. Thus, the decision was made to implant a bioprosthetic mitral valve. Postoperatively, he required cardiopulmonary support via veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) and intra-aortic balloon pump secondary to the development of new-onset left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF 25–30%). Pathology showed benign cardiac valve tissue with myxoid degeneration and chronic inflammation. He experienced sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT). Coronary angiogram showed non-obstructive coronary artery disease. He was eventually decannulated from VA-ECMO and made clinical improvements. In light of his significantly reduced left ventricular systolic function in the setting of recurrent sustained VT, an ICD for secondary prevention was placed five days after completion of antibiotics. He received IV ceftriaxone 2000 mg for one month after the second surgery. At a six-month follow-up, he was overall doing well, tolerating GDMT, and undergoing rehabilitation. A repeat of rheumatologic serology showed complete resolution of the ANCA panel with MPO and PR3.

Discussion

This case highlights the importance of distinguishing between primary AAV and AAV secondary to other conditions, such as IE, as in this case. SBE can trigger complex immune responses and mimic SLE or AAV. Recent cohort studies have reported rates of ANCA detection in IE patients from 24% to 33%.4 Patients with IE-induced AAV are typically younger and more likely to present with weight loss and renal involvement.3 Staphylococcal and streptococcus infections are common pathogens in SBE-related ANCA. Interestingly, anti-PR3 antibodies are more commonly associated with SBE as the genetic sequences of S. Aureus are complementary to a critical sequence of PR3.5 This case also prompts mention of two other differential diagnoses, including Libman-Sacks endocarditis and concomitant right-sided IE.

The European Society of Cardiology recommends systematic evaluation by ECG and echocardiogram during early stages or presentations of vasculitis, given that cardiac involvement is often silent.6 While the Vasculitis Foundation advises screening for infection in refractory vasculitis, there is no official guideline for routine blood cultures in the workup for AAV.7 Our patient was promptly diagnosed with SBE due to our rheumatologist including cultures as part of the evaluation of vasculitis. Futhermore, given the intersection between these AAVs and cardiac disease, cardiologists should be involved in the evaluation of these conditions.

A type of primary AAV, granulomatosis polyangiitis (GPA) can cause valvular heart disease. While this is thought of as uncommon, it has been reported in 6% to 44% of cases.8 It most commonly presents as an aortic valve lesion, which accounts for about half of affected cases. One quarter of cases present as a mitral valve lesion and one quarter present as combined mitral and aortic valve destruction.9 Valvular destruction by GPA also typically requires valve replacement once discovered.10

Our patient’s lower extremity lesions, his kidney injury and pulmonary lesion, in addition to a positive ANCA, low level PR3, negative ANA, and normal complements, suggested an ANCA vasculitis. Unfortunately, without biopsies, these lesions cannot be differentiated from bacteremia. Typical renal biopsy findings in IE-related glomerulonephritis show immune-complex features with C3 subendothelial deposits.4 However, given his critical illness and lack of inpatient dermatology at our center, respectively, kidney and skin biopsies were unable to be obtained. Thus far, no diagnosis or long-lasting features of ANCA disease have been reported, as in our patient. Furthermore, the majority of patients also lose positivity of their ANCA titers.1

When there is serious organ involvement, it is controversial whether to treat with immune therapeutics plus antibiotics or with only antibiotics.4,11 Because of his positive blood cultures and cardiac vegetation, we reclassified his presentation from AAV to IE with secondary immunologic phenomena. It is unique that he was treated with antibiotics alone, and within days, there were no skin lesions. Given that the presentations are very similar, the misclassification risk is extremely high. Furthermore, misclassification can be extremely harmful as antibiotics are essential for the treatment of occult infection. Presently, treatment strategies can range from antibiotics alone to steroids and antibiotics to surgery followed by antibiotics alone. It has been proposed that patients with high PR3 titers or those unresponsive to antibiotic treatment should also be treated with steroids.4

Ultimately, treatment with antibiotics alone appears to have led to seronegativity of ANCA and resolution of pathology to the affected extracardiac organs. This case highlights the importance of cardiac workup when an AAV is diagnosed due to potential IE presenting as an immune reaction or due to silent cardiac disease because of the primary vasculitis.
