Prevalence and associated risk factors with overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity among women of childbearing age residing in Nouakchott, Mauritania
Authors: Mariem Youssouf Issa, Yacouba Diagana, El Kari Khalid, Sidi Mohamed Coulibaly, Alioune Gueye, Rabab. M. H. Dehah, Ould El Kebir Mohamed Vall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Weight gain among Mauritanian women, particularly Arab-Mauritanian women, remains a major public health concern. This phenomenon is largely explained by its cultural valorization, as it is perceived as a symbol of beauty and social prestige. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of weight gain and to identify the associated risk factors, including overall and abdominal, among women of reproductive age residing in urban areas of Mauritania. We conducted a cross-sectional survey of 1166 women of childbearing age (15–49 years) residing in Nouakchott, Mauritania. The study combined the use of a questionnaire with precise anthropometrics measurements, and body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC) and waist to hip ratio (WHR) were used as indicators to assess overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity based on WHO classification and IDF Classification criteria. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to assess risk factors for overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity. The mean age (± SD) of these women was 31.8 (± 7.3) years. The prevalence rates for overweight and obesity were 31.6% and 39% respectively, while for abdominal obesity, determined by waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip circumference ratio (WHR), the prevalence rates were 85.4% and 67.2% respectively. The results reveal a significant association between age and both BMI and abdominal obesity (p < 0.001). In the regression analysis, marital status (married and divorced) (reference: single), low level of education (reference: higher education), as well as the age ranges 19–49 (reference: 15–18), were identified as significant risk factors for overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity based on WC and WHR. The high prevalence of overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity among women of childbearing age living in Nouakchott, Mauritania is a cause for serious concern. This worrying trend is strongly correlated with age (19–49 years), marital status and low level of education. These findings underscore the urgent need for health policies specifically targeted at these women, aimed at reversing this alarming trend and anticipating the resulting health Complications.

Introduction

The number of women of childbearing age suffering from obesity is increasing in several countries, making this phenomenon a major public health problem on an international scale1.

The combined burden of underweight and obesity has increased in most countries, driven by the rise in obesity2. In 2022, 2.5 billion adults were overweight, including over 890 million who were obese, of whom approximately 504 million were women. In sub-Saharan Africa, data indicate that 8.9% of women are undernourished, while 39.7% are overweight or obese3. According to available projections, these trends are expected to persist, and may even intensify over the coming decades in the absence of coordinated, evidence-based interventions4,5.

Because of its serious consequences in terms of morbidity and mortality, as well as its high prevalence, obesity has become a major global public health challenge6. In 2019, a higher-than-optimal body mass index (BMI) was responsible for an estimated 5 million deaths from non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancers, neurological disorders, chronic respiratory diseases, and digestive disorders7. Obesity is also frequently associated with reduced female fertility, recurrent miscarriages, and congenital malformations8,9. In addition, the economic burden of the obesity epidemic is substantial: if no interventions are implemented, global costs of overweight and obesity are projected to reach US$3 trillion per year by 2030 and exceed US$18 trillion by 206010. Available data indicate that overweight and obesity are increasing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in urban areas compared to rural settings11. High rates of obesity have been reported in LMICs such as Mali12, Morocco13, and Tanzania14, with Mauritania being no exception. In Mauritania, obesity is highly prevalent among women, especially those of childbearing age, and is increasingly recognized as both a cause and a consequence of multiple health problems. In 2022, 54% of Mauritanian women of childbearing age were overweight, of whom 27% were obese15. This high prevalence is likely attributable to dietary behaviors, including excessive consumption of starchy foods such as rice, wheat, and corn16, combined with low levels of physical activity, which compromise the energy expenditure required to maintain a healthy weight17–19. Cultural practices also contribute to obesity. The aesthetic preference for overweight motivates the force-feeding of young girls, a practice that involves providing excessive quantities of food from ages 9 to 11. The medical consequences of this practice, particularly when combined with physical inactivity, are numerous and include cardiovascular diseases, kidney failure, arthritis, sleep apnea, diabetes, and hypertension. In recent years, even more extreme methods have emerged, including the use of cortisone-containing products, appetite stimulants, and psychotropic drugs20. Despite these significant health risks, obesity is only timidly addressed in Mauritania, and no national strategy currently exists to prevent or control it. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of overweight, obesity, and abdominal obesity, and to examine their associations with economic, sociodemographic, and behavioral factors in an urban population of Mauritanian women of childbearing age.

Materials and methods

Study setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from May 25 to June 10, 2023, in Nouakchott, the most populous city in Mauritania, with a total population of 1,370,707, of whom 26% were women of reproductive age (15–49 years)15,21. The city is administratively divided into three distinct wilayas (regions): Nouakchott West, Nouakchott South, and Nouakchott North. Participants were recruited from all three wilayas to ensure representative coverage across the city.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All female volunteers residing in the three wilayas of Nouakchott, aged 15 to 49 years old, who completed the questionnaire were included in the study. Women who did not consent, were ill, pregnant or did not complete the questionnaire were excluded.

Survey type

A two-stage random survey was conducted. In the first stage, households were randomly selected using the random walk method22. In the second stage, one woman of reproductive age was randomly selected from each chosen household. The random walk method involves selecting a starting point at random, after which investigators follow different routes originating from that point. This approach provides a practical and effective way to obtain a representative sample in field surveys.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the SCHWARTZ formula [n = Z²× p (1 - p)/d2]. With p the prevalence of obesity (p = 27%)23, Z² the accepted risk of error (1.96²) and d the desired precision (2.5%). Thus, using the proportions mentioned above, the sample size was 1211, but 45 women were excluded because they did not complete the questionnaire or they refused to be measured. So the final sample size was 1166 women of childbearing age.

Data collection methods

Dietary and sociodemographic data: Dietary diversity was assessed using the standardized questionnaire from the Guidelines for Measuring Household and Individual Dietary Diversity24, supplemented with additional questions on participants’ economic, sociodemographic, lifestyle, and dietary habits. This instrument was selected due to its validated, standardized framework, which enables reliable measurement of dietary diversity at both the individual and household levels and ensures comparability with international studies. Face-to-face interviews were conducted in participants’ homes, with the questionnaire initially drafted in French and subsequently translated into the four national languages (Arabic, Soninke, Pular, and Wolof). Translations were performed independently by three to four translators per language, reconciled through discussion, and finalized based on majority consensus to ensure cultural and semantic appropriateness. All translations were incorporated into a single questionnaire, allowing interviewers to administer the survey in participants’ preferred language.

Anthropometric measurements: In addition to dietary and sociodemographic data, anthropometric measurements were collected to provide complementary information on participants’ nutritional status. Weight was measured using a SECA® digital scale accurate to 0.1 kg, and height was measured using a SECA® portable measuring tape (capacity 2.20 m, accuracy 0.1 cm). Waist circumference (WC) was measured using a non-stretch tape measure at the midpoint between the last palpable rib and the iliac crest at the end of a normal exhalation. In cases where the natural waist level was difficult to identify, the smallest circumference at the waist level was recorded. Hip circumference was measured at the level of the pubic symphysis and at the maximum circumference of the buttocks25. All measurements were conducted following standardized protocols, and equipment was routinely calibrated to ensure accuracy.

Assessment of obesity: The patient’s corpulence and degree of obesity were assessed by calculating BMI (weight (kg)/height (m²)). For women aged 15 to 18, BMI was interpreted using the World Health Organization’s (WHO) AnthoPlus software, which incorporates BMI curves for this age group. BMI for age was used to determine thinness (Z score < -2), normal weight (Z score = -2 to + 1), overweight (Z score > + 1) and obesity (Z score > + 2) according to the growth standards published by the WHO in 2007 for children and adolescents26. Among women aged over 18, underweight corresponds to a BMI less than < 18.5 kg/m², normal nutritional status to a BMI between 18.5 kg/m² and 24.99 kg/m², overweight to a BMI between 25 kg/m² and 29.99 kg/m², and obesity to a BMI 30 kg/m² or more27,28.

Assessment of abdominal obesity: Abdominal obesity was evaluated using WC and the waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) criteria30, a WC ≥ 80 cm in women indicates abdominal obesity. WHR provides an indicator of body fat distribution, particularly abdominal versus hip fat, with a value above 0.85 generally associated with increased risk of obesity-related complications, including cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes31,32.

Training and pre-testing: Interviewers underwent five days of training, including a four-day theoretical phase covering survey objectives, methodology, data collection techniques, and instruments, followed by a one-day practical phase in which instruments were pre-tested in the field. The pre-test involved approximately 100 randomly selected women of childbearing age from Nouakchott, assessing clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance of the questions. Ambiguous items were reformulated to optimize understanding. Pre-testing also allowed adjustments to ensure accurate administration in participants’ native language and under conditions replicating the main survey.

Quality assurance and final refinement: Data collection teams comprised three to four interviewers to cover all languages, with adjustments made when individual interviewers were proficient in fewer languages. Experts in nutrition, public health, and survey methodology reviewed the questionnaire, contributing to improved content validity, internal consistency, and linguistic adequacy. After each survey day, collected data were reviewed by interviewers to ensure completeness and minimize potential errors. All procedures adhered to relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Data were entered and collected using CSPro version 7.7 software. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 for Windows33, and Microsoft® Office Excel version 2013. Descriptive statistics were used to calculate BMI, WC, and WHR. Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to assess the significance (at the maximum 5% level) of the association between explanatory (independent) variables such as age, education, marital status, etc., and dependent variables such as overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity. A multivariate logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity in the women taking part in the study. This analysis measures the magnitude of the association between the independent variable and the dependent variable(s), using the Odd Ratio (OR) test. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Ethical aspects

The study was approved on May 10, 2023, by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Nouakchott, Mauritania (reference number: 11/20). Prior to participation, all participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study’s objectives, methodology, and potential benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and for minors, consent was additionally obtained from a parent or legal guardian. This procedure ensured that all participants fully understood the implications of their involvement and voluntarily agreed to participate.

Economic, socio-demographic and lifestyle factors

The study results in the Table 1 reveal that the average age of the surveyed women was 31.8 years (± 7.3). The majority 62.7% of the sample are aged 30 or over. 88% are married, 77.8% have less than secondary education. In addition, 70.9% are not professionally active and have no income, over 67% of the households surveyed have 7 or more members and receive at least a source of income. About the lifestyle habits, 95.3% of the surveyed women had 3 meals or more per day and 69% of them spent an hour or more sitting in front of the screen (TV or computer).

Risk factors associated with overweight and obesity determine by BMI

Table 3 shows that compared to the 15 to 18 years, women aged 19 to 29, 30 to 39 and 40 to 49 years show a high correlation with overweight and obesity (OR = 3.3, 4.2 and 3.1 respectively for overweight and OR = 3.7, 6.2 and 6.2 respectively for obesity). Married women have a higher risk of overweight or obesity compared to single women (OR = 1.9 and 2.3 respectively), and divorced women have an increased risk of developing obesity compared with single women (OR = 3.0, p = 0.029). Women who spend less than one hour a day sitting in front of a screen also run an increased risk of being overweight compared to those who do not watch a screen (OR = 1.9, p = 0.034). Whatever is the level of women education (none, Koranic, primary and secondary), they have an increased risk of being overweight and obese (OR = 2.1, 3.7, 2.1, 1.9 respectively for overweight OR = 1.2, 1.1, 1.1 respectively for obesity) compared to those with a higher level of education. The women whose household has 2 sources of income (OR = 1.1) also had a higher risk of obesity than those whose household has one or has no source of income. On the other hand, women who were not in paid employment had a reduced risk of being overweight (OR = 0.8) than those who are. Women with no level of education (OR = 0.9), who are not in paid employment (OR = 0.9), whose household size is less or equal to 6 (OR = 0.8), and who ate less than 3 meals, all had a reduced risk of being obese.

Risk factors associated with abdominal obesity

Table 4 shows a significant association between abdominal obesity, measured by both WC and WHR, and the age groups 19–29, 30–39 and 40–49 compared with 15–18 years of age (OR = 3.0, 5.2 and 6.7 respectively for WC; OR = 2.4, 4.2 and 4.7 respectively for WHR). Married women have a higher association with abdominal obesity, measured by WC and WHR, than single women (OR = 3.7 and 1.9 respectively). Whatever is the women’s level of education (none, Koranic, primary and secondary), they had an increased risk of abdominal obesity by WC or WHR compared with those with a higher level (OR = 1.5, 2.5, 2.1 and 1.1, respectively for WC; OR = 1.6, 1.5, 1.8 and 1.4, respectively for WHR). Women who are not in paid employment (OR = 1.1) have higher risk of developing abdominal obesity by WHR than those who are in paid employment. The table also shows that women living in households with two sources of income, with 6 or less family members, who eat less than 3 meals, and who spend more or less than 2 h in front of the screen are less likely to have abdominal obesity based on WHR (OR = 0.8, 0.9, 0.8, 0.6 respectively). In addition, women living in households with two sources of income and those who spend more or less than 2 h in front of the screen are less likely to develop abdominal obesity measured by WC (OR = 0.6, 0.6 and 0.7 respectively).

Discussion

This study evaluated the prevalence and risk factors of overweight, obesity, and abdominal obesity among women of reproductive age residing in the urban environment of Nouakchott, Mauritania, with the aim of informing effective intervention strategies. Clinical and epidemiological measures were used to assess body fat distribution, including waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and body mass index (BMI).

The results show high rates of overweight and obesity according to BMI among women, with a prevalence of 31.6% and 39.0%, respectively (70.6% overall). These results are high compared with those reported by the Demographic and Health Survey 2019–2021 (54%)15, and alarming when compared with studies carried out in other countries such as Morocco (45.3%)13, Benin (31.7%)34, Bangladesh (30.9%)35, India (33.3%)36, Tanzania (50.4%)14 and Algeria (38.3% for only obesity)37. Abdominal obesity, measured by WC and WHR, is also high, with a prevalence of 85.4% and 67.2%, respectively. These rates in turn are very alarming, and higher than those reported by Islam et al. in Bangladesh (58.6% for WC)35 and by Barich et al. in Morocco (60.1% for WHR)13. This is worrying because obesity and abdominal obesity are linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes and certain cancers38,39.

The differences observed with country results may be due to variations in lifestyle, socio- demographic factors, and genetic or behavioral factors35,40.

The study also showed an increase in the prevalence of overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity with age, consistent with the findings of Hu et al.6. This increase can be attributed to hormonal changes, sedentary lifestyle, diet quality and socio-economic factors41–43. Young women are also affected by overweight and obesity, which may be linked to increased consumption of processed foods, sugary drinks and a sedentary lifestyle14,35. In Mauritania, obesity is sometimes perceived as a sign of prestige and beauty, which can influence young women’s behavior.

This study also showed that marital status (married and divorced) is significantly associated with overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity based on WC and WHR. This result is consistent with several other studies13,14,36. Marital status, for its part, may have an impact on body weight because, after marriage, women may pay less attention to their physique44. Also, according to some authors45,46, marriage is an important predictor of women’s contribution to obesity. Indeed, this may be explained by the fact that fat is accumulated in women due to increased parity46–49. Education level is positively associated with overweight, obesity and abdominal obesity based on WC and WHR. This result is also reported by Seidu et al.12 but contrast with otherstudies13,35. This result, may be explained by the fact that illiteracy or a low level of education seems to be another factor of obesity. Illiterate women may be less aware of the health consequences of obesity and the risk of unhealthy eating habits45,50. These consequences are manifested in women of reproductive age in general by the risk of chronic disease51.

Sedentary behavior, specifically time spent sitting in front of screens, was associated with lower odds of both general and abdominal obesity. This finding contradicts previous studies reporting that physical inactivity generally contributes to obesity14,40. However, similar results have been reported in some cross sectional studies. Mukhtar et al. reported a weak or non-significant association between screen time and obesity in a Pakistan population52. Likewise, Singh et al. found no significant association between screen time and either general or abdominal obesity among females adolescents53.

This result can be explained by the multifactorial complexity of obesity, which depends not only on sedentary behaviors but also on diet, overall physical activity, metabolism, and socioeconomic determinants54,55. In this study, sedentary behavior was primarily assessed through screen time, without accounting for total time spent at rest. In Mauritanian context, women may experience prolonged periods of rest or inactivity unrelated to screen use, influenced by sociocultural habits, and daily routine. Therefore, rest time is a broader component of sedentary behavior and can vary considerably among women, influencing the risk of obesity. This factor could thus act as a confounding factor, attenuating the apparent effect of screen time in our population54,56.

Furthermore, the nature of screen-related activities and associated eating behaviors (such as snacking) can modulate this relationship, and other lifestyle factors, such as compensatory physical activity, can also influence the risk of obesity55,56. Furthermore, the nature of self-reported data, subject to memory bias and social desirability bias, can lead to an underestimation of actual screen time, reducing the variability of the explanatory variable and decreasing the statistical power to detect a significant association57,58. Therefore, screen time alone may not be a reliable indicator of obesity risk in Mauritanian women in this study.

Diversity of income sources is associated with obesity but not with abdominal obesity. Households with multiple sources of income have a higher prevalence of obesity among women, influenced by socio-cultural perceptions valuing greater corpulence59,60 and unhealthy eating due to higher purchasing power61.

In addition, variables such as, Household size was not significantly associated with overweight, obesity or abdominal obesity. Seidu et al.12 found similar results. This may be due to the perception of excess weight as a sign of wealth and good health in many African countries62,63. However, excess weight in women of childbearing age is associated with an increased risk of chronic disease and negative reproductive health outcomes, reducing the efficacy of infertility treatments36,64.

It should be noted that some authors have questioned the reliability of BMI as a predictor of mortality risk associated with obesity-related comorbidities65,66. Indeed, these comorbidities are more strongly associated with abdominal adiposity than with total body fat67.

Study strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of a representative sample at the Nouakchott level, which enhances the generalizability of the findings to women residing in the three districts of the city. The use of a validated and standardized dietary diversity questionnaire, calibrated anthropometric instruments, and trained data collectors further strengthened the reliability of the data and minimized measurement error. Standardized data collection procedures also contributed to internal validity.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and therefore only associations between variables can be established. Second, the study was conducted exclusively in the urban setting of Nouakchott, which may limit the external validity of the results, particularly for rural populations in Mauritania, where socioeconomic conditions, access to healthcare, and dietary patterns may differ substantially. Third, key behavioral variables such as screen time, meal frequency, and physical activity were self-reported, potentially introducing recall bias and social desirability bias. In addition, the absence of a control group and comprehensive reference data—due to logistical, financial, and sociocultural constraints—may have increased the risk of selection bias and residual confounding.

Finally, although anthropometric measurements were objectively collected, other potentially relevant factors, including micronutrient intake, clinical biomarkers, and detailed quantitative dietary assessment, were not evaluated and may have influenced the observed associations.

Future longitudinal and multicenter studies incorporating rural populations, objective behavioral measurements, and biochemical markers are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study describes the epidemiology of overweight, obesity, and abdominal obesity among urban women of reproductive age in Nouakchott, Mauritania. The results indicate that overweight and obesity are highly prevalent, affecting 31.6% and 39.0% of women, respectively, while abdominal obesity is even more common, affecting up to 85.4% of women according to waist circumference (WC). These conditions were significantly associated with factors such as age, marital status, sedentary behaviors, and low educational attainment.

The findings highlight the growing public health burden of obesity and underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions. Recommended strategies include raising public awareness of the health risks associated with obesity and promoting healthy lifestyles through nutrition education programs tailored to local customs in schools and communities. Policies should also aim to reduce sedentary behaviors and foster environments conducive to physical activity, including the promotion of active transportation such as walking and cycling. Additionally, initiatives to improve household economic conditions could enhance access to healthier food options.
