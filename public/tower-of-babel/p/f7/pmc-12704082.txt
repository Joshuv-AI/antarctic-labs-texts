Mesenteric inclusion in Crohn's disease surgery: Promising breakthrough or tempest in a teapot? An updated meta-analysis
Authors: Mohamed Maatouk, Mohamed Ben Khalifa, Nada Essid, Aymen Mabrouk, Mariem Nouira, Moez Boudokhane, Mounir Ben Moussa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecently, renewed concern has been centered on the role of the mesentery in the development of Crohn's disease (CD). However, there are minimal data supporting the extended mesenteric excision (EME), which may lead to reticence in the adoption of this surgical option. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the post-operative outcomes in patients undergoing EME vs limited mesenteric excision (LME) for CD.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search from January 2018 to January 2025 for studies reporting outcomes in patients undergoing EME compared with LME for CD. A pooled meta-analysis was performed. The risk of bias was examined using the ROBINS-I v2 and RoB2 tool.ResultsNine studies were included in final analysis, comprising two randomized controlled trials, enrolling a total of 4823 patients. Definitions of EME differ, with some studies preserving the ileocolic trunk and others resecting the mesentery with proximal ligation. No significant difference in surgical or endoscopic recurrence was observed between EME and LME. Concerning secondary outcomes, including overall morbidity, intra-abdominal abscess, anastomotic leak and hospital stay, the two groups showed no significant differences.ConclusionsEME appears as safe as the LME in terms of morbidity, but does not significantly reduce surgical or endoscopic recurrence. Due to the absence of a precise definition of the EME procedure in CD and the need of further high-quality research, the approach of EME still lacks sufficient validation and cannot be broadly recommended for routine clinical practice.

Background

Despite advances in the treatment of Crohn's disease (CD), anastomotic recurrence after ileocolic resection (ICR) remains high. More than 40 % of patients require reoperation [1]. Delaying postoperative surgical recurrence represents a challenge for surgeons. Several therapeutic options and surgical features aimed to reduce this complication have been investigated [2]. Recently, renewed concern has centered on the role of the mesentery in the development of CD. The role of the mesentery in the pathogenesis of CD is still unclear. The mesentery has been recognized as a ‘separate immune organ’, containing adipocytes, nerves, blood vessels, lymphatics, stromal cells, and fibroblasts. Li et al. indicated that high value of visceral fat content and increased mesenteric lymphatic vessel density in the proximal margin were predictive factors of early recurrence in patients undergoing primary ileocolonic resection [3,4]. This may be explained by the findings that morphological abnormalities in the mesenteric fat called “creeping fat” associated with an increased production of inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins and cytokines, can extend from the mesentery into the adjacent intestine causing transmural inflammation and contribute to disease progression in CD [5]. However, mesenterectomy has not been a widely adopted practice. This is mainly attributed to the technical considerations related to the risk of bleeding and potential damage to the mesenteric root when manipulating a thickened mesentery. In 2011, Kono et al. [6] were the first to introduce this concept into surgical practice. They performed an exclusion of the mesentery at the anastomosis after resection keeping the mesentery away from the site of anastomosis. The potential benefits associated with exclusion of the mesentery were confirmed in a randomized clinical trial, indicating that Kono-S significantly reduces clinical recurrence [7]. Subsequently, Coffey et al. introduced the concept of extended mesenteric excision (EME) and concluded that mesenteric excision reduce significantly the surgical recurrence compared to conservative limited resection (2.9 % vs. 40 %) [8]. However, there are minimal data supporting the EME, which may lead to reticence in the adoption of this surgical option. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the outcomes of patients having EME for intestinal resection in CD, compared to conventional treatment with limited mesenteric excision (LME).

Methods

This systematic review was completed based on the 2020 guidelines of the PRISMA Statement (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-analyses) and follows the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Interventions [9,10]. A priori protocol was published in PROSPERO (CRD420251033781).

Eligibility criteria

Studies included in this meta-analysis were chosen according to PICOs criteria.

P (patients):

Patients undergoing primary or redo colorectal, ileocolic, or rectal resection for CD were included. Dysplasia and colorectal cancer complicating CD were not eligible.

I (Intervention) and C (Control):

We included all studies that compared EME to conservative limited resection. No restrictions were placed on surgical approach (open or minimally invasive) or the anastomotic type (side-to-side, end-to-side, end-to-end; stapled, hand-sewn, or Kono's anastomosis).

O (outcome):

The primary outcomes of the study were surgical and endoscopic recurrence. The surgical recurrence (SR) was defined as the requirement of reoperation for recurrent CD and endoscopic recurrence was defined as Rutgeerts score ≥ i2 or modified Rutgeerts score ≥ i2b [11].

The secondary outcomes focused on perioperative characteristics (operative time), intra operative bleeding and postoperative parameters (intra abdominal septic complications, overall morbidity, anastomotic leak; and length of hospital stay).

S (study type):

All available studies (RCTs and non RCTs) that directly compared EME versus conservative limited resection were eligible for inclusion. Case series with fewer than 10 patients and studies not published in English were excluded.

Search strategy

The online databases of PubMed, Web of science, and ScienceDirect were searched from January 2018, corresponding to the inaugural study by Coffey et al. which introduced the concept of EME in CD, until January 2025 combining keywords and using Boolean operators: mesenteric, mesentery, excision, resection, removal, removing, Crohn's disease, Crohn's Colitis, Crohn Disease.

The electronic search was supplemented with a manual examination of reference lists of the retrieved studies to ensure the search was as complete as possible.

Study selection and data collection

Two authors independently carried out the searches and articles were screened for relevance based on title and abstract. Full texts of potentially eligible publications were obtained. If two separate publications included the same cohort of patients, the most recent publication was included in the analysis. Data were extracted by one author (MM) with complete and independent verification by a second author (NE). Disagreements were resolved through discussion as needed or by appeal to a senior author.

Assessment of study quality and risk of bias

Review authors (MM and NE) independently assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomized controlled trials (Rob v2) and the risk of bias assessment tool for non-randomized studies of interventions (ROBINS-I) for interventional studies and risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Exposure tool (ROBINS-E) for Observational epidemiologic studies. Any disagreement was resolved via discussion. Then, risk of bias was analyzed using the Robvis tool [[12], [13], [14]].

Statistical analysis

Measure of effect size:

The odds ratio (OR) was used as the statistical measure for dichotomous outcomes with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) estimated using the Mantel–Haenszel method.

For continuous variables, the mean difference with 95 % CIs was calculated. If mean values were not available for continuous outcomes, medians were used. If standard deviations were not available in the original article, they were estimated from the available data they were calculated according to Cochrane Collaboration guidelines.

Assessment of heterogeneity:

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Chi square test (Q test) and the I2 test. If the I2 statistic was greater than 50 %, heterogeneity was considered as high and a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the study to address the issue of heterogeneity. All statistical analysis were carried out using the Review Manager 5.1 (Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2008).

Results

Search overview

Using the predefined search strategy, the literature search identified 591 papers. After removing duplicate records 369 were retrieved, of which 12 full-text manuscripts were screened.

Three studies were excluded from the final analysis: one was a congress abstract and the remaining two were study protocols. Finally, nine eligible studies [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]] involving 4712 patients remained and were included in the final analysis. Two of these studies [15,21] were conducted in the same department. SR was noted in the study of Coffey et al. [15] while short-term post-operative complications were not reported. However, in 2023, the same team [21] published a retrospective series of 76 patients with CD who underwent intestinal resection including mesenteric excision and compared 30-day postoperative morbidity between LME and EME. Thus, both studies were included as one focused on SR and the other on postoperative morbidity. The selection process flow chart is presented in (Fig. 1).

Prisma flow diagram.

Baseline characteristics of included studies

A total of 4823 patients (EME 1264, LME 3559) were included in the meta-analysis. Studies were published between 2018 and 2025. Of the nine studies included, two studies [22,23] were randomized controlled trials, one retrospective database review (ACS NSQIP database) [18] and the rest were retrospective studies.

Risk of bias assessment

According to the RoB2 tool, the SPICY study and Mesenteric colic study demonstrate a low risk. For non RCT, one study were classified to have critical ROBINS-I V2 [15]. Fleming et al. [21] was judged to have a serious risk of bias. One study [17] was classified as having a low risk of bias while the remaining four studies were classified as having moderate risk of bias. The overall risk of bias per study is detailed in Table 1S and demonstrated in Fig. 1S.

Outcomes measures

Primary endpoints

Surgical recurrence

Three of the six included studies consisting of 510 patients provided data for SR. The rate of SR in EME group was significantly lower (14/298, 4.7 %) than that in LME group (35/212, 16.5 %) (OR 0.28; 95 % CI [0.08, 0.96]; p = 0.04). There was high heterogeneity between the included studies (Chi2 = 0.71, (p = 0.08); I2 = 61 %) (Fig. 3a1).

Primary outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis of the data was undertaken to account for this heterogeneity. By excluding the study by Coffey et al. [15], with critical risk of bias, the heterogeneity was reduced, and the difference in SR between the EME and LME groups was no longer statistically significant (13/270,4.8 %) and (23/182, 12.6 %) (OR 0.41; 95 % CI [0.17, 1.02]; p = 0.06, Chi2 = 0.13, p = 0.23; I2 = 30 %) (Fig. 3a2).

Endoscopic recurrence

Three studies reported data on endoscopic recurrence (2 RCTs and one cohort study), which was 41.3 % (129/312) in the EME group and 45.6 % (105/230) in the LME group, and there was no significant differences between two groups (OR = 0.77; 95 % CI [0.46, 1.27], p = 0.30). There was moderate heterogeneity between the included studies (Chi2 = 0.09, (p = 0.17); I2 = 43 %) (Fig. 3b).

Secondary endpoints

Overall morbidity

When evaluating the data available of 4794 patients on overall morbidity in 8 studies (2 RCTs and 6 cohort studies), no significant difference was observed between the EME and LME (OR = 0.95; 95 % CI [0.79, 1.11], p = 0.56). There was moderate heterogeneity between the included studies (Chi2 = 0.12, (p = 0.04); I2 = 41 %) (Fig. 4a).

Secondary outcomes.

Intra-abdominal abscess

Six studies (one RCT and 5 cohort studies) examined rates of intra-abdominal abscess. There was no significant difference between the two groups (OR = 0.93; 95 % CI [0.68, 1.26], p = 0.62). There was low heterogeneity between studies (Chi2 = 0.06, (P = 0.81); I2 = 0 %). (Fig. 4b).

Anastomotic leak

Six studies (2 RCTs and 4 cohort studies) provided data on anastomotic leak between EME and LME. There was no statistically significant difference (OR = 1; 95 % CI [0.52, 1.92], p = 1) with low heterogeneity between studies (Chi2 = 0.30, (P = 0.59); I2 = 0 %) (Fig. 4c).

Intra-abdominal bleeding and blood loss

The incidence of intra-abdominal bleeding was documented in four studies and three studies provided data for the blood loss. There was no significant difference in intra-abdominal and blood loss between the two groups (OR = 0.89; 95 % [0.39, 2.06], p = 0.79; I2 = 21 % and MD = −12.02; 95 % CI [−32.03, 7.99], p = 0.24 respectively) (Fig. 4d, e).

Operative time

Five studies (2 RCTs and 3 cohort studies) provided data for the operative time. There was a high level of heterogeneity (Chi2 = 19.85, (P = 0.04); I2 = 60 %). No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups in the meta-analysis (MD = 0.57; 95 % CI [−4.74, 5.88], p = 0.61).

Hospital stay

There was no significant difference in hospital stay between the two groups (MD = −0.08; 95 % CI [−0.40, 0.24], p = 0.63) with low heterogeneity between studies (Chi2 = 0, (P = 0.37); I2 = 0 %).

Discussion

According to our meta-analysis, EME in CD appears to be safe as the LME in terms of early complications, without significant gain in post-operative recurrence reduction.

The contribution of the mesentery in the pathogenesis of CD remains unclear and controversial [24]. The current dominant theory in CD is based on the unidirectional axis of dysbiosis–innate immunity–adaptive immunity–mesentery-body system. However, emerging clinical evidence strongly suggests that this axis is bidirectional. Some studies suggest that mesenteric processes occur earlier than previously thought [17,25]. MRI-based studies demonstrate the presence of mesenteric abnormalities in the absence of endoscopic evidence of mucosal disease. Mesentery is now recognized as an immunologically active organ that produces various inflammatory mediators, including pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, and pro-fibrotic factors [26]. These molecules modulate both local immune responses and intracellular signaling processes, influencing immune cell activity and contributing to intestinal inflammation. The hypothesis that mesenchymal abnormalities may then extend from the mesentery into the adjacent intestine was highlighted in Coffey et al. [15], study where authors were interested in histological findings in diseased mesentery and adjacent mucosa in CD. They found that no distinct serosa was evident in the intersection between the diseased mesentery and the intestine in CD and mesenteric disease activity index was correlated with mucosal disease activity index. Thus, the mesentery might not just be a spectator of the MC, but an active participant and they introduced in 2018 the concept of EME. The rational of this technique was that mesenchymal abnormalities migrate to other adjacent areas of the intestine and that the resection of the mesentery will reduce the “mesenchymal canals”, and remove greater percentage of lymph nodes resulting in reducing the immune response and local recruitment of fibroblasts and other mediators of inflammation involved in Crohn's disease. Authors compared the incidence of SR between a historical cohort having conservative surgery and a second cohort having an EME. They concluded that the mesenteric excision reduces significantly the SR when compared to conservative limited resection (2.9 % vs. 40 % of cumulative SR rate at five years) and that retention of the mesentery was an independent predictor factor for SR on a multivariate analysis. The manuscript presented interesting observations and gained interest among researchers. However, these results should be interpreted with caution due to several considerable limitations and a high risk of bias. These include the small number of patients, which precluded the use of multivariate analysis; the comparison of two cohorts enrolled during different time periods; differences in preoperative medication, particularly the use of biologic agents; unequal follow-up durations between cohorts; and differences in the rates of positive resection margins. Later in 2021, Zhu et al. [16] also evaluated the effect of EME on postoperative recurrence, and their conclusions were consistent with those of Coffey et al. On the contrary, Mineccia et al. [18] reported no difference in disease SR after extensive versus LME (2.8 vs 4 % p = 0.6), they even reported that SR didn't seem to be effected by the presence or absence of a thickened mesentery itself. They also assessed endoscopic recurrence in this retrospective comparison and observed no significant difference between the two groups. Recently, de V Bellato et al. [22] published similar results in the « spicy» study, the first randomized controlled trial, comparing endoscopic recurrence in two groups undergoing EME and LME for CD and they concluded that EME was not superior to LME with regard to endoscopic recurrence at 6 months post operatively. We believe this study is of particular interest due to its blinded design for endoscopic evaluation between the two groups, as well as the high degree of homogeneity in the surgical approach. All included patients underwent a one-stage procedure consisting of a primary ileocecal resection, with the same type of anastomosis performed by a single surgeon at each participating center. The two groups were also similar regarding the length of the resected specimen, managing a standardized surgical technique in the EME group. In this approach, mesenteric excision was performed along the lower border of the ileocolic trunk without ligating it, allowing for a colonic transection level similar to that of the mesentery-sparing group. Despite its high quality and valuable contribution to the current evidence on this emerging topic, certain aspects of the SPICY study have been critically discussed, notably concerning the definition of the so-called “extended” technique [27]. In fact, in this study, the ileocolic trunk was preserved, and the mesentery was not resected in its origin, while the original rationale for EME described by Coffey et al. included removal of all mesenteric tissue through a high ligation of the ICT. In the SPICY study, the procedure may be more accurately described as a “less limited” or “wedge” mesenteric excision rather than a true extended excision, as initially defined. We believe it would be interesting to clearly establish the criteria for extended mesenteric excision (EME) to ensure more precise results and reliable conclusions. Most recently, a novel randomized controlled trial “the MESOCOLIC trial”, featuring Coffey as part of the investigative team, reported its preliminary results using an EME technique with high ligation of the ileocolic trunk at its origin. This trial revealed reduced endoscopic recurrence and severity of ER, in patients who underwent an EME, versus LME. The divergences in outcomes reported by the two randomized trials, as noted by Duan et al. [23], may be explained by the preservation of the ileocolic trunk and the retention of mesentery distal to the trunk. Our meta-analysis can be viewed as a response to Mr. Coffey, who called for a new meta-analysis including the MESOCOLIC trial [28], which was not included in the recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses published on the subject [[29], [30], [31]]. In our opinion, the most appropriate plan is not simply to include multiple RCTs but to standardize the definition of EME, or alternatively, to perform a direct comparison between “true” EME and the so-called “wedge” EME.

Certainly, our meta-analysis has several strengths, notably being the first to include both randomized controlled trials evaluating EME. However, it also has some limitations. First, the number of included trials and sample size were relatively modest and the quality was not high with a moderate risk of bias for non-RCTS, which may weaken the results of this analysis and result in analytical bias. Second, the evaluation of SR and endoscopic recurrence was limited, as only three studies evaluated these outcomes and reported no significant difference between EME and LME. Third, there were notable variations across the included studies, particularly regarding the location of the disease (ileocecal colonic, rectal, and even upper gastrointestinal involvement in some series), as well as in the definition of endoscopic recurrence, with the use of different scoring systems (Rutgeerts' score and modified Rutgeerts' score). Most importantly, as previously mentioned, there were inconsistencies in the definition of extended mesenteric excision (EME). Given these considerations, the question arises regarding the true value of the procedure: is this a new concept with great promise, or is it simply a tempest in a teapot? Such findings suggest the need for further investigations and future research, particularly with standardized surgical techniques and uniform outcome measures to ensure more reliable and comparable results.

Due to the absence of a precise definition of the EME procedure in CD, particularly whether to resect the mesentery with or without ileocolic trunk preservation, the EME approach still lacks sufficient validation. To determine the true value of this technique, if it represents a promising breakthrough or merely a tempest in a teapot, future research should prioritize standardized surgical protocols and uniform outcome measures. The results of ongoing randomized trials are highly awaited to clarify the role of EME. Meanwhile, current recommendations from expert societies supporting mesenteric preservation should continue to be the standard for clinical practice.
