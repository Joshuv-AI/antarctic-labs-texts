Abnormal Cervical Facet Orientation and Congenital Stenosis: Case Reports and Biomechanical Implications—Illustrative Cases
Authors: Fawaz Almotairi, Mohannad M Mallat, Ahmed Makrami, Mohammed AlSaeaer, Amro Al‐Habib
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn patients with congenital cervical stenosis, the addition of abnormal cervical facet orientation (horizontal or hypoplastic joints) may predispose to segmental instability, subluxation, and canal compromise. Full assessment of facet anatomy is essential pre‐operatively, as selected patients (e.g., those with demonstrable subluxation, widened facet joint spaces, or high risk of adjacent‐segment instability) may require instrumented fusion in addition to decompression to maintain stability.Keywords: biomechanical changes, cervical facet joints, cervical instability, cervical spinal stenosis (CSS), spinal congenital malformations

Introduction

Cervical facet joints (CFJ) are paired structures with a posteromedial orientation that facilitate mobility in the healthy spine. The orientation of the CFJ is usually represented by the intersection angle of the midsagittal line of the vertebra with the facet line, and the normal orientation is typically roof‐tile‐shaped and slopes away from the horizontal plane by approximately 45°, with slight changes at each level [1, 2].

Abnormalities affecting CFJ have been reported in different disease conditions. A study reported that patients with degenerative cervical spinal stenosis (CSS) have smaller axial CFJ angles than the normal population [3]. Congenital cervical spinal stenosis (CSS) is typically defined using the Torg–Pavlov ratio (< 0.82) or absolute anteroposterior (AP) canal diameter, while abnormal facet orientation is defined relative to the normal 45° angulation. Others have considered an abnormal development of the posterior elements, mainly CFJ, as one of the primary causes of degenerative CSS [4, 5]. This supports the idea that facet joint anomalies can alter the biomechanical properties of the cervical vertebrae, leading to stenosis and narrowing of the spinal canal [4].

Moreover, several studies have highlighted facet malorientation and asymmetry as a biomechanical risk factor for cervical disc herniation (CDH) and ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL). Increased facet angle asymmetry has been shown to be more frequent in patients with CDH, particularly at C4–C5, C5–C6, and C6–C7, where herniation tends to occur toward the side with the larger facet angle, suggesting that facet anomalies may predispose patients to asymmetric loading and disc protrusion [6]. Furthermore, facet anomalies are strongly correlated with the development of OPLL. Studies show a significantly higher incidence of asymmetry in segmental and local OPLL types compared to continuous or mixed forms. This suggests that altered facet mechanics may contribute to the localized mechanical stress that underlies OPLL pathogenesis [7]. Taken together, these findings support the notion that facet hypoplasia and malorientation are not merely an anatomic variation but a clinically relevant factor influencing the development of multiple degenerative cervical spine conditions, underscoring the need for further biomechanical and longitudinal studies to clarify its pathophysiological role. Hence, a comprehensive study of the CFJ in patients with cervical myelopathy is warranted [8, 9].

Understanding the various anatomical variations of the facet joints can help explain the underlying process of canal stenosis development. Moreover, it can guide the best surgical approach, if warranted, that is, decompression alone versus instrumented fusion [4].

Case History/Examination

Case 1: A 27‐year‐old man presented with a 9‐month history of severe neck and back pain associated with progressive weakness in all four limbs. one month before presentation, he developed intermittent sleep apnea. He had wasting and weakness of all four limb muscles, right‐sided hyperreflexia, and a positive Romberg sign. He has a normal sensory examination. Computed tomography (CT) imaging showed mild to moderate bilateral hypoplasia of the C3 facet joints with a C3–C4 facet joint orientation that was nearly parallel to the horizontal plane (Figure 1a, Figure 1b). Additionally, there was widening of the C3–C4 joint space (more on the left side), resulting in anterior subluxation of C3 (Figure 1c). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed C3–C4 stenosis and subluxation with spinal cord compression associated with myelomalacia and T2 cord signal changes. The patient underwent C3–C4 laminectomy with a C2 pars screw and lateral mass screw fixation of C4 (Figure 1d).

Case 2: A 16‐year‐old girl known to have corpus callosum agenesis and hydrocephalus with ventriculoperitoneal shunting performed at birth. The patient complained of neck pain and transient quadriplegia following a trivial fall, 2 months prior to presentation. She was using a wheelchair because of spastic quadriparesis with reduced fine bilateral hand movements. She was spastic in all four limbs, with power of 4/5 in the upper limbs and 3/5 in the lower limbs. Her deep tendon reflexes were exaggerated and had a positive Hoffmann sign. The majority of her sensory examination was intact, and she had intact sphincter control. CT revealed congenital osseous fusion of C1 to C4 (involving the anterior and posterior elements), forming a block vertebra. C4–C5 facet joints were hypoplastic with decreased posterior articulation surface area and subsequent widening of the posterior element joint space rendered stability uncertain. No anterior spondylolisthesis was observed between C4 and C5, and canal stenosis was observed at the C2 and C4 levels (Figure 2a, Figure 2b, Figure 2c). MRI revealed degenerative disc disease and cord compression with T2 signal cord changes. The facet joint of C2–C3 showed a horizontal orientation and inclination, similar to those of case 1 (Figure 3a). Dynamic MRI (utilizing active flexion and extension positioning) revealed no changes in extension or flexion (Figure 3a, Figure 3b). Given the massive congenital block vertebra from C1 to C4 acting as a rigid segment, the adjacent C4–C5 level, which demonstrated hypoplastic facets and widened posterior joint spaces, acted as a hypermobile stress riser. Because of the required posterior decompression, the existing hypoplasia of the posterior tension band rendered the C4–C5 segment highly vulnerable to post‐operative instability. Therefore, instrumentation was chosen prophylactically to address the biomechanical strain and prevent delayed subluxation Table 1. The patient underwent C1–C3 posterior decompression with C1 lateral mass screw fixation, C2 right‐sided pedicle screw fixation, left‐sided translaminar screw fixation, and bilateral translaminar screw fixation at C3 (Figure 2d).

Differential Diagnosis

Case 1: The patient presented with progressive quadriparesis and focal C3–C4 canal compromise, the differential diagnosis included degenerative cervical myelopathy, post‐traumatic ligamentous instability, inflammatory arthropathy of the facet joints, congenital cervical canal narrowing, and intramedullary pathology such as neoplasm or syringomyelia. Cross‐sectional imaging demonstrating horizontally oriented, hypoplastic facets with segmental malalignment supported congenital facet malorientation with secondary stenosis as the primary etiology.

Case 2: For this patient, the presence of congenital central nervous system anomalies and myelopathy following minor trauma, the differential diagnosis encompassed craniovertebral junction malformations, Klippel–Feil spectrum disorder, traumatic cervical instability, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, inflammatory arthropathy, and intramedullary lesions. The presence of multilevel congenital vertebral fusion with abnormal facet orientation and canal narrowing was most consistent with congenital cervical stenosis associated with posterior element dysplasia.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Case 1: The patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 with a C‐collar and ambulated without assistance. Upon follow‐up 6 weeks postoperatively, the patient was doing well and had improved hand power. Radiography and CT imaging confirmed well‐maintained alignment, adequate decompression, and intact hardware without complications (Figure 1d).

Case 2: Postoperatively, the patient did well and regained normal muscle power by examination at 4 months postoperatively with improved limb spasticity. Post‐operative imaging confirmed maintained alignment, excellent decompression, and intact hardware (Figure 2d).

Discussion

Facet joint anatomy and orientation: The zygapophyseal or facet joints in the spine are complex biomechanical structures with intricate anatomy, mechanical performance, and effects on general spine behavior and health [1]. In a healthy spine, motion is facilitated by the facet joints, which are typical diarthrodial joints with cartilage surfaces; each level has two facet joints, one on each motion segment's posterolateral orientation. Typically, the orientation of the cervical facet joints is roof‐tile‐shaped and slopes at approximately 45° from the horizontal plane.

Cervical facet joint orientation varies substantially by level. The normal physiological transition of facet orientation shifts from a posteromedial orientation in the upper subaxial spine (e.g., C2–C3 and C3–C4) to a more posterolateral pattern in the lower cervical spine (with C6 often acting as a transition level). It is critical to anchor claims of “abnormal” orientation to these specific vertebral levels; in our presented cases, the profound horizontal orientations were anchored specifically to the C3–C4 and C4–C5 levels. Tulsi et al. [10] Panjabi et al. [11] and Pal et al. [1] observed that the superior articular facets are oriented posteromedially rather than in the coronal plane. Shah et al. [12], noted that the inclination angles of the superior facets with respect to the transverse plane ranged from 22° to 45°, whereas the inferior facet angles ranged from 29° to 53°, indicating the variability and importance of the degree of inclination [4, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Biomechanical impact of facet malorientation and hypoplasia. While facet tropism (angular asymmetry) is a known biomechanical factor, the presence of bilateral horizontal malorientation and facet hypoplasia creates a more profound disruption of spinal biomechanics. These anomalies can increase intradiscal pressure and alter load transmission, rendering the corresponding segment highly vulnerable to external forces and degenerative cascades [18, 19]. Furthermore, one study confirmed a significant association between sagittal orientation and osteoarthritis and degenerative spondylolisthesis of the lumbar facet joints [4, 5, 20].

Relationship between facet orientation and spondylolisthesis: Increased sagittalization of the facet joints has been identified as a risk factor for decreased resistance to forward displacement in multiple studies and is linked to the development of hypermobility and degenerative spondylolisthesis. Although most studies support this finding, an alternative perspective is also presented in the literature, which proposes that the observed sagittalization associated with degenerative spondylolisthesis, when compared with the general population, is more likely attributable to secondary remodelling of joint orientation rather than being a preexisting morphological characteristic. This divergence in views underscores the complexity of the relationship between facet orientation and degenerative spondylolisthesis and invites further investigation into the underlying mechanisms [21, 22, 23]. It is important to note that much of the literature establishing the relationship between sagittalization and spondylolisthesis is derived from the lumbar spine. While this serves as valuable indirect, analogous support, cervical spine biomechanics differ meaningfully from lumbar biomechanics, and these extrapolations must be interpreted with caution in the cervical context.

Management options for concomitant cervical malorientation and cervical stenosis with or without spondylolisthesis: Anomalies in the facet joints, particularly their orientation of facet joints in relation to the horizontal plane, have significant implications for surgical treatment. It is crucial to consider such anomalies and facet hypoplasia to minimize stress concentration following spinal surgery. This may lead to the inference that instrumented fusion should be considered in these patients. However, surgical decision‐making in the cervical spine must be framed as multifactorial. Rather than recommending prophylactic fixation based on facet orientation alone, the decision to instrument should incorporate clinical presentation, radiographic evidence of instability or malalignment, specific anatomical risk factors (such as congenital block vertebrae), and the presence of cord signal changes. This approach can potentially mitigate the effects of these unmodifiable anomalies [3, 6, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28].

In our patients, we demonstrate the importance of an inclusive and focused preoperative radiological assessment of the facet joints in delineating the developmental and biomechanical changes associated with CSS. Mild‐to‐moderate trauma is a known risk factor for myelopathy in patients with CSS. This report suggests that the severe symptomatology observed in both cases was likely predisposed by their anatomical anomalies, although we acknowledge that other factors, such as degenerative components and minor trauma, likely contributed to the acute clinical presentation. In addition to clinical assessment and history, the anomalies were identified using CT images and dynamic MRIs, which demonstrated facet joint hypoplasia and lack of posterior articulations between the affected vertebrae, which led to widening of the space joint and anterior subluxation, spondylolisthesis, loss of angulation, and horizontal orientation of the facet joints. We herein reported a prominent correlation between facet orientation and congenital cervical stenosis, which, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported. Limitations of this report include the relatively short follow‐up duration (6 weeks and 4 months, respectively) and the lack of standardized functional outcome scores (e.g., mJOA or NDI) inherent to retrospective case reviews, which warrants future prospective longitudinal studies. A comparison between the facet angles observed in patients with CSS and those in the general population is needed to clearly highlight their importance.
