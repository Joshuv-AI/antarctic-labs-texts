Preterm birth and the risk of respiratory syncytial virus hospitalisation in Australia’s Northern Territory: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Michael J Binks, Tejal Shah, Sasha Underhill, Jemima Beissbarth, Robyn Marsh, Lisa McHugh, Peter S Morris, Kiarna Brown, Bianca Middleton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Northern Territory (NT) of Australia has high reported rates of both preterm birth and childhood respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between preterm birth and risk of RSV-associated acute lower respiratory infection (RSV-ALRI) hospital admission among NT infants during their first year of life.MethodsA retrospective, population-based cohort study of NT mother-infant pairs from 2008 to 2017. Preterm birth was defined as gestation < 37 weeks. RSV-ALRI hospitalisations were identified using ICD-10-AM codes (J12.1, J20.5, J21.0, or J09-J22 plus B97.4). Risk of RSV-ALRI in preterm infants was assessed using generalised linear regression to calculate risk ratios (RR). A multivariable risk model adjusted for all available maternal, perinatal, and infant characteristics. Temporal trends in hospitalisation incidence were assessed using negative binomial regression.ResultsOverall, 10% (3839/39148) of infants were born preterm, and 2% (846/39148) were hospitalised with RSV-ALRI. Preterm infants (4%) had twice the risk of RSV-ALRI compared to term infants (2%) (risk ratio [RR] 2.26, 95% CI 1.90–2.69; adjusted RR 1.72, 95% CI 1.43–2.08), with the highest risk among those born < 32 weeks’ gestation (7%). Elevated risks were also observed in First Nations infants (4.5%), infants of young mothers aged < 19 years (4.9%), those living in very remote Central desert regions (7.1%), and infants of mothers who had a urinary tract infection during pregnancy (5.1%). The episode-based RSV-ALRI hospitalisation rate was 2.3 per 100 child-years, declining steadily across the decade of observation (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 0.90 per year, 95% CI 0.86–0.94).ConclusionPreterm birth is an independent and significant risk factor for infant RSV hospitalisation in Australia’s NT. Other important risk factors include younger maternal age, residence in remote or Central desert regions, maternal urinary tract infection (UTI) during pregnancy, and male sex. RSV immunisation strategies will be crucial in protecting high-risk infants in the NT and elsewhere.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13960-w.Keywords: Fetus/Newborn, Respiratory tract, RSV

Introduction

Preterm birth is a significant concern in Australia’s Northern Territory (NT), with a reported prevalence as high as 16% among First Nations families [1]. NT infants also have among the world’s highest rates of acute lower respiratory infection (ALRI) requiring hospitalisation (20% in the first year of life). Respiratory syncytial virus is a leading respiratory pathogen [2]. As many as 8% of First Nations children living in remote NT communities are hospitalised with RSV-associated ALRI (RSV-ALRI) in their first year of life [3], causing significant social disruption and strain on health services. The high burden of respiratory infections among First Nations infants reflects multiple intersecting social and environmental determinants of health, including the enduring impacts of colonisation and intergenerational trauma, inadequate housing infrastructure, tobacco and environmental smoke exposure, remoteness and distance to hospital care, and limited access to culturally safe health services [4]. There is evidence indicating that RSV seasonality is unique in the NT and varies by region, correlating with high rainfall and humidity (December to May) in the tropical Top End [3, 5] and with winter/spring seasons (May to September) in the temperate Central desert region [3].

Globally, preterm birth is a prominent risk factor for early childhood RSV infections. A 2024 meta-analysis of 47 studies found preterm infants account for 25% of all RSV-ALRI hospitalisations in children under two years of age [2]. Another review of 20 studies found preterm infants have nearly double the risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation (odds ratio [OR] 1.96, 95% CI 1.44–2.67), with additional important risk factors including low birth weight, maternal smoking, crowded households, male sex, and lack of breastfeeding [6]. Preterm birth is also linked to severe RSV outcomes and increased infant mortality [7]. Few Australian data exist, but a New South Wales study found preterm infants had a 2.5-fold higher incidence of RSV-coded hospitalisations during the first six months of life [8].

There are no published data on the association between preterm birth and RSV-ALRI hospitalisation in the NT, no longitudinal data on preterm birth in the Central desert region and no published NT RSV-ALRI incidence data since 2015. This study primarily evaluates the association between preterm birth and RSV-ALRI hospital admissions among NT infants (2008–2017). Secondary objectives include identifying other maternal, perinatal, and infant risk factors for RSV-ALRI hospitalisation, describing temporal trends in RSV-ALRI incidence, and characterising regional variation in RSV seasonality. Together these analyses aim to guide best practice for prevention strategies including maternal RSV vaccination and infant immunoprophylaxis.

Methods

Study design, population, and setting

This retrospective population-based cohort study included all infants born in the NT from January 2008 to December 2017 (Fig. 1). The NT covers 1.4 million km2, with 233,000 residents (2021), 26% identify as First Nations, and most living outside Greater Darwin [9, 10]. Five public hospitals and one small private hospital serviced the region, with acute paediatric inpatient care managed almost exclusively by public hospitals.

Northern Territory (NT) birth cohort: 2008–2017. The cohort was defined by a live-birth record in the NT perinatal dataset during the study period. Children with an inpatient admission within their first 12 months of life (including birth-related admissions), identified through ICD-10 coding records in the NT public hospital trends dataset, were deterministically linked to their corresponding perinatal birth records using unique identifiers. Children with a perinatal record only and no linked hospital admission in their first 12 months (n=6257) were classified as NT residents, noting that the perinatal dataset captures births in both public and private facilities. Hospital admissions occurring outside the NT are a potential source of under-ascertainment. Children with a hospital record but no corresponding perinatal birth record were presumed to have been born outside the NT and were excluded from the analysis (n=1060). During the study period, all acute paediatric hospital admissions in the NT were serviced by five public hospitals: Royal Darwin Hospital, Katherine District Hospital, Gove District Hospital, Tennant Creek Hospital, and Alice Springs Hospital. The NT’s sole private hospital offered birthing services but provided almost no acute paediatric inpatient care

Data sources and linkage

Data were sourced from the NT Midwives’ Collection (perinatal data) and NT Territory Hospital Trends Inpatient Activity (hospital admissions). The Midwives’ Collection includes all births ≥ 20 weeks or ≥ 400 g or any signs of life at birth, with gestational age based on last menstrual period or earliest ultrasound. First Nations status was validated against a master index and hospital records [11]. The Hospital Trends dataset contains ICD-10-AM coded summaries for all public hospital admissions. Datasets were linked deterministically using a project-specific key.

Exposure and outcomes

Exposure: preterm birth was defined as < 37 completed weeks of gestation, with sub-classifications 34–36, 32–33, 28–31, and 20–27 weeks [12, 13].

Primary outcome: RSV-ALRI hospitalisations between birth and 12 months of age, defined by the ICD-10-AM codes: J12.1 or J20.5 or J21.0 or J09-J22 plus B97.4 (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1). The infant age at each hospital episode was calculated by subtracting the date of birth from the date of admission. Repeat admissions within 7 days were consolidated into a single episode to avoid counting rapid readmissions for the same illness as separate events. To maximise case ascertainment, all diagnostic codes assigned to an admission contributed to the outcome classification, regardless of the position in which they were recorded. A single hospital admission could have multiple outcome classifications. We assumed complete follow-up from birth to 12 months of age for all cohort infants. This assumption is supported by the structure of NT acute paediatric care during the study period, during which all five NT public hospitals provided inpatient paediatric services and the sole private hospital offered almost no acute paediatric care. Hospital admissions occurring outside the NT may result in some under-ascertainment.

Other outcomes: Viral and bacterial pneumonia, bronchitis, bronchiolitis, and non-specific ALRI (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1). Common non-respiratory conditions with largely distinct aetiologies (gastroenteritis, A00-A09.9; skin infection, L00-L08.9 or P39.4; and scabies, B86) were included as specificity comparators.

Analysis

All analyses were conducted using Stata software version 17.

Descriptive analysis

Antenatal, delivery, and infant characteristics were stratified by the preterm birth exposure. Between-group differences were assessed using the univariate risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Geography classifications

Geography was categorised using Local Government Area boundaries to define Top End and Central desert regions, and the ARIA+ Remoteness Index to define remoteness: outer regional (Darwin and Palmerston region), remote (Greater Darwin and Alice Springs regions), and very remote (all other areas) [14]. Notably, there are no urban classifications in the NT; even Darwin, the territory’s capital, is classified as outer regional. Very remote communities may be several hundred kilometres from the nearest hospital, with limited road access, particularly during the wet season. Four geographical categories were used: (i) Top End, regional or remote; (ii) Top End, very remote; (iii) Central desert, remote; and (iv) Central desert, very remote.

Age-specific risk

Age-associated trends were assessed through cumulative proportion (by age in months) and normalised frequency distribution (monthly episodes/total episodes) of first admissions.

Temporal trends

Episode-level rates were used to assess temporal trends, as these capture all admissions, including repeat admissions per child, over observation time. Hospitalisation incidence rates were calculated as episodes per 100 child-years at risk. Annual hospitalisation incidence rates were plotted graphically.

Temporal trends in hospitalisation incidence were assessed using negative binomial regression models fitted to episode counts, with a log link and robust standard errors clustered at the child level to account for multiple admissions per child. Calendar year was modelled as a continuous variable to estimate average annual changes in incidence. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) therefore represent the multiplicative change in hospitalisation rate per additional calendar year. To minimise lead-in truncation bias, analyses were restricted to admissions occurring from 2009 onwards.

Geographical variation in seasonality was visualised by aggregating and plotting monthly RSV-ALRI hospital admissions for Top End and Central desert regions.

Results

Preterm birth and cohort characteristics

The cohort (Fig. 1) included 39,148 infants (27866 mothers), with 10% (n = 3839) born preterm (< 37 weeks), most (6.8%) between 34 and 36 weeks and few (0.7%) extremely preterm at 20–27 weeks (Table 1A). Preterm birth risk was stable across 2008–2017 (Table 1B) and over twice as high in First Nations (14.6%) versus non-First Nations (6.8%) families.

75% of pregnancies commenced antenatal care in the first trimester; 27.2% of pregnancies were among women living in very remote regions, and 23.9% involved maternal smoking. Gestational diabetes (10.7%) and anaemia (5.9%) were the most common complications. Most infants were born vaginally (68.5%), 17.6% were admitted to the special care nursery, and 38.1% were identified as Australian First Nations (Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 1).

Compared to term infants, preterm infants were more likely to be First Nations, have mothers < 19 years old, mothers who smoked or consumed alcohol, and mothers who resided in very remote areas. Preterm infants were less likely to be born at the private hospital or to mothers who received first-trimester antenatal care. Preterm birth was also associated with maternal diabetes, hypertension, renal disease, pre-eclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction, and multiple pregnancy. Preterm infants had a higher infant mortality in the first 12 months (Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 1).

Risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation

There were 881 RSV-ALRI hospitalisations among 846 infants (2.1% of the cohort, Table 2). The majority (812, 96.0%) experienced a single episode, while 33 (3.9%) infants had two episodes, and one infant (0.1%) had three. RSV-ALRI was the primary diagnosis in almost all cases (748/846, 88.4%). The median age at first admission was 130 days (IQR 68–209; Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 2). For all-cause ALRI, there were 5192 episodes among 3,576 infants (9.1% of the cohort). Most infants (2614, 73.1%) had one episode, 608 (17.0%) had two, 201 (5.6%) had three, and 153 (4.3%) had more than three episodes. ALRI was the primary diagnosis in 91% of cases. The median age at first admission was 147.5 days (interquartile range [IQR] 61–213 days).

Preterm-born infants had twice the risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation (4.4% vs. 1.9%; RR 2.26, 95% CI 1.90–2.69) compared to term-born infants (Table 2), with the highest risk observed in those born at 28–31 (7.6%) and 20–27 (5.5%) weeks gestation (Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 3). The cumulative proportion of RSV-ALRI hospitalisations diverged between preterm and term infants from 1 to 12 months of age (Fig. 2A). Frequency of first RSV-ALRI admissions peaked earlier in term infants (3 months) compared to preterm infants (5 months) (Fig. 2B). Preterm birth was also associated with higher risks of hospitalisation for all-cause ALRI, viral and bacterial pneumonia, bronchiolitis, and non-specific ALRI (Table 2). Bronchitis diagnoses were rare (< 1%) and excluded from further analysis.

Given that preterm birth and infant respiratory infections share overlapping risk factors, multivariable analyses were conducted to account for potential confounders that were available in the NT Midwives Collection. After adjusting for general maternal characteristics, pre-existing health risks, obstetric complications, and birth/infant characteristics (Table 2; Fig. 3; Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 4), the positive association between preterm birth and RSV-ALRI hospitalisations remained statistically significant, though attenuated (aRR 1.72, 95% CI 1.43–2.08). Similarly, significant associations persisted for all-cause ALRI, bronchiolitis, and non-specific ALRI after adjustment, but the associations with viral and bacterial pneumonia were either not significant or weak.

The complete multivariable model (Fig. 3; Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 4) identified several additional factors associated with a higher risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation. These included being a First Nations infant (aRR 2.40, 95% CI 1.97–2.93), having a mother younger than 19 years (aRR 1.80, 95% CI 1.40–2.30, compared to mothers aged 30–39), living in the very remote Top End (aRR 1.61, 95% CI 1.31–1.98), or in remote (aRR 2.72, 95% CI 2.18–3.38) or very remote (aRR 3.61, 95% CI 2.93–4.30) Central desert regions (compared to Top End regional or remote areas), and having a mother with a urinary tract infection during pregnancy (aRR 1.45, 95% CI 1.10–1.89). Conversely, female infants (aRR 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.91), infants of primiparous women (aRR 0.65, 95% CI 0.55–0.78), and infants whose mothers received antenatal care in the first trimester (aRR 0.86, 95% CI 0.74–1.01) had a lower risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation. Univariate comparisons of RSV-ALRI hospitalisations by these characteristics are presented in Additional file 2: Supplementary Tables 5 A, B, and C. Risk factors were largely consistent across all respiratory conditions. However, preeclampsia was specifically associated with increased risk of all-cause ALRI, pneumonia, and bronchiolitis, while first-trimester antenatal care was protective across most respiratory conditions. Maternal smoking and alcohol use were weakly associated with all-cause ALRI and bronchiolitis.

The pseudo R² values from the multivariable regression models (Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 4) indicate that, while the models capture important associations, a substantial proportion of the outcome variance remains unexplained.

Finally, to determine whether preterm birth was a specific risk factor for RSV-ALRI or a general risk factor for poor health, we also evaluated its association with several other hospitalised conditions (Additional file 2: Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). In the multivariable analyses, preterm birth showed minimal associations with gastroenteritis (aRR 1.12, 95% CI 0.99–1.27), skin infection (aRR 1.04, 95% CI 0.87–1.25), and scabies (aRR 1.18, 95% CI 0.99–1.40).

Notably, infants living in the very remote Central desert region (compared to those in regional or remote Top End areas) had the highest risk of hospitalisation for gastroenteritis (aRR 4.44, 95% CI 3.92–5.02), skin infection (aRR 2.88, 95% CI 2.39–3.46), and scabies (aRR 5.71, 95% CI 4.45–7.32). Additionally, First Nations infants had a markedly higher risk of hospitalisation with a diagnosis of scabies (aRR 32.17, 95% CI 19.76–52.36) compared to other children.

Temporal trends in RSV-ALRI hospitalisations

After excluding data from 2008 to avoid lead-in truncation bias, the average annual incidence of RSV-ALRI hospitalisations over the decade was 2.3 cases per 100 child-years (Fig. 4; Additional file 2: Supplementary Table 8). The rate decreased by about 9% each year (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 0.91 per year, 95% CI 0.86–0.95). Similar downward trends were observed for both viral and bacterial pneumonia. In contrast, the incidence rates of bronchiolitis, non-specific ALRI, and all-cause ALRI remained relatively stable.

Annual hospitalisation rates for RSV-ALRI and other respiratory infection classifications among Northern Territory infants: 2008–2017. RSV-ALRI: Respiratory syncytial virus-associated acute lower respiratory infection. Bact: bacterial

Adjusting for year of hospitalisation episode, the average annual incidence rate of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation was higher among preterm compared to term infants (IRR 2.33, 95% CI 1.87–2.92; Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 2A), higher among First Nations compared to non-First Nations infants (IRR 4.59, 95% CI 3.48–6.06; Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 2B) and higher among infants residing in the Central desert compared to those in the Top End (IRR 3.43, 95% CI 2.76–4.27; Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 2C). There was no significant interaction between time and preterm birth status, nor between time and residing in the Central desert region, indicating that the time trends in RSV-ALRI were similar for these groups. However, the interaction between being a First Nations infant and time approached significance (interaction IRR 0.92, 95% CI 0.84–1.01), suggesting a closing of the gap in RSV-ALRI rates between First Nations and non-First Nations infants over time. In models including this interaction, the main effect of time was no longer significant, supporting the validity of this finding (data not shown).

Interestingly, the frequency of first RSV-ALRI admissions among non-First Nations children was highest in the first three months of life. In contrast, for First Nations infants, admissions were elevated and sustained across the first six to eight months of life (Fig. 5). The frequency of admissions by infant month of age did not differ between the Top End and Central desert regions (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 3). However, there were distinct seasonal trends by region: in the Top End, aggregate RSV-ALRI cases per month (summed over the decade) peaked from December to April, while in the Central desert, cases were highest from June to October (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate the association between preterm birth and the risk of infant RSV-ALRI hospitalisations in Australia’s NT. Aligning with our hypothesis, preterm birth was associated with an increased risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation in the first year of life. In absolute terms, 4.4% of preterm infants were hospitalised with RSV-ALRI compared to 1.9% of term infants, with rates exceeding 7% among those born at < 32 weeks gestation. The minimal association between preterm birth and non-respiratory hospitalisations supports the interpretation that this is a respiratory-specific rather than general risk factor. Furthermore, this work highlights the disproportionate burden of both preterm birth and RSV-ALRI among First Nations infants and those in remote regions, underscoring the role of social and environmental determinants of health in shaping these outcomes.

The period prevalence of preterm birth (10%) in this NT-wide study was consistent with earlier studies in the Top End [1, 15] and national trends [16]. First Nations families had more than double the risk of preterm birth than non-First Nations families (14.6%), well above the global average (11.1%) [17]. Key risk factors included preexisting diabetes and hypertension, pre-eclampsia, antepartum haemorrhage, and intrauterine growth restriction, most of which are more common among First Nations women [1].

Preterm infants had a 1.7-times higher adjusted risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation than term-born infants, with the most significant risk among infants born < 32 weeks of gestation. A multinational meta-analysis spanning 52 countries (including 13 low- and middle-income nations) found that preterm infants account for 25% of all RSV hospitalisations [2]. The biological susceptibility is driven by underdeveloped airways and immune function, reduced maternal antibody transfer, and chronic lung conditions [18].

Preterm birth is an important consideration for RSV prevention. Although the study period predates current RSV immunisation programs, the risk factors and seasonal patterns identified provide a pre-vaccination baseline against which program impact can be assessed. From February 2025, all pregnant women in Australia became eligible for a free dose of maternal RSV vaccination (Abrysvo) between 28 and 36 weeks gestation, ideally at least two weeks before delivery. Earlier vaccination may benefit those at risk of preterm birth. While some studies suggested a possible link between maternal RSV vaccination and preterm birth, especially in lower-middle income settings, recent data show no increased risk when administered from 32 weeks onwards [19–23]. If birth occurs before or within two weeks of vaccination, long-acting monoclonal antibodies (nirsevimab) are recommended for infant protection [24].

Preterm birth was one of several risk factors for early-onset RSV-ALRI hospitalisation in the NT, alongside younger maternal age, residence in remote or Central desert regions, maternal urinary tract infection (UTI) during pregnancy, and male sex. To our knowledge, this is the first report linking maternal UTI to increased RSV risk in infants, an effect that was independent of preterm birth status, suggesting distinct biological pathways. Urinary and genital tract infections can complicate pregnancy through inflammatory pathways and disruption of the vaginal microbiome [25], which are subsequently critical for infant immune development [26–28]. However, residual confounding related to broader maternal health or healthcare access cannot be excluded, and further research is needed to clarify this relationship. Interestingly, maternal smoking was not a risk factor for infant RSV hospitalisation after accounting for preterm birth.

We observed a decline in RSV-ALRI hospitalisation rates in the NT between 2008 and 2017, with a more marked reduction among First Nations infants, suggesting some progress in narrowing the health gap. However, changes in testing, coding, health-care initiatives, and random variation may have influenced these trends, and ongoing surveillance in the post-COVID era is needed for confirmation. Historically, First Nations communities in Australia and internationally have experienced a disproportionate burden of RSV-ALRI [3, 8, 29–31]. Across the decade, First Nations infants had 2.3-times the (adjusted) per child risk of RSV-ALRI hospitalisation than others. This disparity reflects complex factors, including limited access to culturally safe health care, socioeconomic disadvantage, and systemic racism. Continued efforts are essential to achieve health equity for all children.

Our study confirms a regional contrast in RSV seasonality across the NT. In the Top End, RSV-ALRI hospitalisations peaked during the wet season, consistent with earlier studies showing correlations with rainfall and humidity [5]. In the Central desert, peaks occurred in the cooler winter and spring months, despite low rainfall and humidity, a pattern more typical of temperate southern Australia. This paradox likely reflects different dominant drivers. In the tropics, humidity promotes viral stability [32] while in arid and temperate zones, lower temperatures and lower humidity enhance viral survival and transmission [33]. In both regions, indoor crowding is more common during these periods. These findings indicate that environmental and social factors shape RSV seasonality across diverse climates.

The strengths of this study include the large, population-based cohort spanning a decade of births, deterministic linkage of perinatal and hospital datasets, and the inclusion of First Nations families and remote communities who are often underrepresented in epidemiological research. The multivariable models captured important risk factor associations; however, pseudo R² values (0.13–0.20) indicate that a substantial proportion of outcome variance remains unexplained, as is expected in population-based analyses where unmeasured individual-level and environmental exposures contribute substantially to outcome variation. Several established RSV risk factors were not available or not included in the multivariable model. Postnatal smoke exposure, household crowding, and childcare attendance were not captured in the perinatal dataset. Breastfeeding data were available but excluded due to high missingness (~ 60%). Area-level socioeconomic indices (IRSAD) were assessed but were largely collinear with remoteness classifications and therefore not retained. Limitations include reliance on ICD-10-AM codes, which, while highly specific for RSV, may underestimate the true burden due to low sensitivity (~ 25%) and diagnostic imprecision, especially for single admissions or non-specific codes [34, 35]. Clinical severity indicators such as oxygen requirement, intensive care admission, and length of stay were not available in the administrative dataset. Diagnostic practices likely improved over the study period, including the transition from immunofluorescence to PCR-based testing, which would be expected to increase RSV detection and suggests the observed decline in hospitalisation rates may be conservative. Infants who died during follow-up (n = 250) were counted in the denominator but had a shortened window in which to be hospitalised with RSV-ALRI, and none were. Because deaths were far more common among preterm (4.6%) than term infants (0.2%) and mostly occurred before the peak age of RSV-ALRI, this disproportionately reduced the observed risk in the preterm group, and the true association between preterm birth and RSV-ALRI may therefore be underestimated. Similarly, the assumption of complete follow-up may slightly overestimate person-time at risk and underestimate incidence rates. Out-migration could not be measured but would have a comparable conservative effect.

Conclusion

Preterm birth is an independent and significant risk factor for RSV-ALRI hospitalisation among infants in the NT. First Nations families and those living in remote areas bear a disproportionate burden of both preterm birth and RSV-ALRI. Addressing these disparities is complex and requires a multifaceted approach, including First Nations-designed antenatal care and child health programs alongside tailored RSV prophylaxis and management for preterm born infants. Over the last 15 years, culturally enhanced midwifery group practice [36], smoking cessation [37], cervical length screening [38] and birthing on country programs [39, 40] have been introduced to tackle preterm birth among NT First Nations families and rates have remained stable over this time. The recent national rollout of maternal RSV vaccines and infant immunisation therapies [24], and the inclusion of RSV as a nationally notifiable disease from 2021 [41], are important advances in RSV prevention. The distinct RSV seasons (peaking December to April in the Top End and June to October in the Central desert) mean that RSV circulates across the NT for most of the year. This supports the NT’s adoption of year-round maternal RSV vaccination and infant nirsevimab programs, in contrast to seasonal programs implemented in southern Australian jurisdictions. Monitoring uptake and effectiveness across the NT, with its high preterm birth rate, diverse geography and social inequities, will be crucial to ensure adequate ongoing protection for all preterm-born and at-risk infants.
