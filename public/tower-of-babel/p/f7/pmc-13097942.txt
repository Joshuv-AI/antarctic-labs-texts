Foreign body ingestion in adults: a retrospective cohort study of clinical characteristics and Endoscopic outcomes
Authors: İbrahim Doğan, Halil Alper Bozkurt, Berk Topaloğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundForeign body ingestion (FBI) in adults represents a clinically relevant emergency requiring endoscopic management in selected cases. Predictors of procedural complications remain incompletely defined, particularly regarding the incremental effect of endoscopic timing.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed adults (≥ 18 years) who underwent endoscopic intervention for confirmed esophageal or gastric foreign bodies between January 2020 and January 2025. Continuous predictors were used in multivariable logistic regression to avoid information loss from arbitrary categorization. Ridge-penalized regression and bootstrap internal validation (1000 resamples) were performed to address potential small-sample bias. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsA total of 122 patients were included (median age 49 years, IQR 33–65; 54.9% female). In the multivariable continuous model, endoscopy timing was independently associated with procedural complications (OR 1.12 per hour delay, 95% CI 1.01–1.24; p = 0.037), corresponding to a clinically meaningful incremental increase in complication risk. Hematemesis demonstrated a large point estimate but did not remain a stable predictor after penalized regression. Apparent model discrimination was acceptable (AUC 0.74), with moderate discrimination after optimism correction (bootstrap-corrected AUC 0.66).ConclusionsIn adult foreign body ingestion, delay to endoscopy appears to exert a cumulative and clinically meaningful effect on complication risk. Penalized and internally validated analyses suggest that procedural timing, rather than object morphology alone, is the most consistent determinant of adverse outcomes.Keywords: Foreign Body Ingestion, Endoscopy, Complications, Logistic Regression

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion (FBI) is predominantly observed in pediatric populations. However, it also represents a significant medical emergency in adult patients, particularly among the elderly and those with cognitive impairments [1, 2]. Esophageal food impaction constitutes a significant etiology of foreign body ingestion among adult populations. In Western societies, meat is predominantly implicated, whereas in other cultural contexts, fishbones and animal bones can be more frequently identified as causative agents [3].

While conservative management is effective in addressing up to 90% of cases, there are specific circumstances that necessitate urgent endoscopic intervention. Only about 1% of cases require emergency surgery [4, 5]. The ingestion of foreign objects can lead to a range of serious medical complications, which are often contingent upon the object’s shape, size, sharpness, and location within the gastrointestinal tract. These objects frequently become lodged in anatomical strictures, thereby increasing the risk of obstruction, perforation, bleeding, and, in severe cases, mortality. Understanding these variables is crucial for assessing the potential health risks associated with ingested foreign bodies [6, 7].

In the evaluation of ingested foreign bodies, clinical symptoms should be initially assessed, and radiological imaging is advised to confirm both the type and anatomical location of the object, as endoscopic intervention is not routinely necessary in all cases. According to the guidelines established by the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE), therapeutic esophagogastroduodenoscopy is indicated on an emergent basis (preferably within 2–6 h, and at the latest within 6 h) for cases of complete esophageal obstruction, sharp-pointed objects, or esophageal batteries. For other esophageal foreign bodies without complete obstruction, as well as for gastric sharp objects, magnets, batteries, or large/long items, urgent intervention within 24 h is recommended. Conversely, medium-sized blunt objects located in the stomach may be managed with nonurgent endoscopic removal within 72 h [8–10].

Endoscopic retrieval of foreign bodies from the gastrointestinal tract is generally considered safe. Nonetheless, complications such as mucosal injury, bleeding, or perforation may occur, and in severe cases, these may become life-threatening. The likelihood of such adverse events may be increased in elderly patients, cases involving prolonged retention of the foreign body, or when the object is lodged in the upper esophagus. While these factors are thought to elevate risk, there is currently no definitive consensus on how to effectively predict or stratify these risks. Identifying these factors is crucial for minimizing complications and enhancing patient outcomes [11–13].

In this context, the present study aims to characterize the nature of ingested foreign bodies in adults, to examine the therapeutic interventions performed, and to analyze the clinical factors that may affect treatment outcomes.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was conducted between January 1, 2020, and January 1, 2025. It included adults (≥ 18 years) who presented to the emergency department with confirmed foreign body ingestion in the esophagus or stomach, verified by radiological imaging and/or endoscopic evaluation, and who underwent endoscopic intervention as the primary therapeutic approach. Patients managed conservatively without endoscopic intervention were excluded from the analysis. Therefore, the findings of this study reflect endoscopically managed cases rather than all foreign body ingestions.

Patients with foreign bodies lodged in the airway or located proximal to the cervical esophagus (oropharynx), those with foreign bodies retained secondary to esophageal or gastric malignancy, patients with neurologically associated eating disorders, patients managed conservatively without endoscopic intervention, patients who refused endoscopic treatment, and cases with incomplete clinical or procedural data were excluded from the analysis.

The study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (approval number: [60KAEK/2025.07.03]) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The following variables were retrospectively recorded and analyzed: age, sex, type of foreign body, size of foreign body, anatomical location of the foreign body, presenting symptoms, time to endoscopy, and complications. Time to endoscopy was defined as the interval (in hours) between emergency department presentation and the start of endoscopic intervention. Foreign body size was recorded based on endoscopy reports and radiologic measurements when available. All endoscopic interventions were performed using standard retrieval devices (e.g., forceps, snare, or basket) and techniques according to institutional practice.

All endoscopic and surgical interventions were performed by the same surgical team. Endoscopy served as the main therapeutic modality. Surgical intervention was performed only in cases where endoscopic removal was unsuccessful or when complications occurred.

Radiological assessment

All patients underwent an initial clinical evaluation supported by radiological imaging when indicated. Plain radiography was primarily used in cases with suspected radiopaque foreign bodies to confirm the presence and approximate location of the object. Computed tomography was selectively performed in patients with inconclusive radiographic findings, suspected complications such as perforation, or unclear object characteristics. Endoscopy was subsequently performed as the primary therapeutic modality rather than a routine diagnostic tool.

Definition of complications

Procedural complications were defined as any adverse events directly related to foreign body ingestion or endoscopic intervention, including gastrointestinal perforation, clinically significant bleeding requiring medical intervention or hospitalization, endoscopy-related adverse events, and the need for surgical management.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python-based statistical packages. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was conducted using continuous predictors to avoid arbitrary categorization. To address potential small-sample bias due to limited complication events, ridge-penalized logistic regression was additionally performed.

Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples, and optimism-corrected AUC values were calculated. Calibration was assessed using calibration slope.

A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study analyzed 122 patients with a mean age of 50.5 ± 19.2 years (median 49, IQR 33–65). The gender distribution included 55 males (45.1%) and 67 females (54.9%). The interval from presentation to endoscopy was less than 12 h for 106 patients (86.9%) and 12 h or more for 16 patients (13.1%). Anatomical location at presentation was distributed as follows: cervical esophagus (58.2%), stomach (22.1%), thoracic esophagus (18.0%), and abdominal esophagus (1.6%). Foreign objects were categorized into bone (57.4%), food (19.7%), needle (13.9%), fishbone (2.5%), and others (6.6%). In the multivariable logistic regression model using continuous predictors, endoscopy timing was independently associated with procedural complications (OR 1.12 per hour delay, 95% CI 1.01–1.24; p = 0.037). Age, foreign body length, hematemesis, and object morphology were not statistically significant independent predictors in the adjusted model. Although hematemesis demonstrated a large point estimate in the standard model, this association attenuated substantially after penalized regression, suggesting potential instability due to limited event numbers. The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination in the development dataset (AUC 0.744), with moderate discrimination after optimism correction (bootstrap-corrected AUC 0.655). (Table 3)

Discussion

Foreign body ingestion, although classically described in children, also constitutes a clinically relevant problem in adults. While the majority of ingested objects pass spontaneously without intervention, endoscopic management becomes mandatory in cases of symptomatic foreign body retention or when complications such as obstruction or perforation occur. Imaging prior to intervention is generally recommended to confirm the presence and location of the object and to assess potential adverse events. In our series, we evaluated 122 adult patients, focusing on the characteristics of the ingested objects, their clinical course, and the outcomes of endoscopic management. These findings highlight that clinically significant presentations in adults are not uncommon and frequently necessitate active intervention. Notably, there remains no universal consensus in the literature regarding the optimal timing and approach to endoscopy, underscoring the importance of further studies to clarify best practices [4, 10, 12].

Although foreign body ingestion is often presumed to occur predominantly in frail or elderly individuals, our cohort demonstrated involvement across a broad age spectrum, with a mean age of 50.5 years (± 19.2; median 49, IQR 33–65). This finding indicates that clinically significant presentations are not confined to a specific demographic group. Among symptomatic cases requiring urgent intervention, the cervical esophagus was the most frequent foreign body location (58.2%), consistent with prior studies that have reported cervical localization in 35–70% of upper gastrointestinal cases. This distribution likely reflects the predilection of foreign bodies to become lodged at anatomical sites of narrowing, in conjunction with the structural characteristics of the ingested objects [5, 11–13].

In terms of object distribution, bones were the most frequently encountered foreign body in our cohort (57.4%). A particularly noteworthy finding was the relatively high proportion of needles (13.9%), which in this context primarily referred to safety pins used by women to secure headscarves, a practice common in our region. The impact of sociocultural context is also evident in other populations; for instance, Zhong et al. reported on 1,058 patients in China, where fish bones accounted for 50% of cases, followed by other bones (27%) and jujube shells (12%). These examples emphasize that the spectrum of ingested objects is strongly shaped by cultural and dietary factors, and accordingly, expectations regarding diagnosis, therapeutic strategies, and potential complications should be tailored to the characteristics of each population [14].

The optimal timing of endoscopic intervention in foreign body ingestion remains a subject of debate. Current ASGE guidelines recommend urgent endoscopic intervention in high-risk scenarios and advise that esophageal foreign bodies should not remain beyond 24 h due to increased complication risk [15]. In the revised continuous model, each additional hour of delay was associated with approximately 12% increased odds of complication (OR 1.12 per hour), supporting an incremental rather than threshold-based effect of procedural timing.

This finding is biologically plausible, as prolonged mucosal contact may promote ischemia, edema, and secondary injury, thereby complicating both retrieval and recovery. Although hematemesis initially demonstrated a large point estimate in the standard model, this association attenuated after penalized regression, suggesting statistical instability due to limited events. Hematemesis should therefore be interpreted as a marker of mucosal injury rather than a stable independent prognostic determinant. Notably, in contrast to traditional assumptions, neither sharp morphology nor large object size independently predicted procedural complications in our cohort, suggesting that timing of intervention and the patient’s clinical status may outweigh object characteristics in determining procedural safety.

Hong et al. analyzed 194 patients with esophageal foreign bodies and demonstrated that a retention time exceeding 12 h (HR 2.42, 95% CI 1.12–5.25; p = 0.025) and the presence of sharp objects (HR 2.48, 95% CI 1.07–5.72; p = 0.034) were independent predictors of complications [11]. In contrast to prior studies emphasizing sharp morphology, hematemesis did not demonstrate a stable independent association after penalized regression in our cohort. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in the nature of ingested objects across populations, emphasizing that cultural and dietary factors substantially shape the risk profile. Consequently, risk factors identified in one cohort may not be universally generalizable, and regional variations should be taken into account when interpreting outcomes or developing management algorithms.

In our cohort, blunt objects were significantly more likely to be advanced into the stomach during attempts at retrieval (aOR 5.32, 95% CI 1.06–26.59; p = 0.042). This is clinically plausible, as smooth and rounded objects are less prone to embed in the mucosa and may slip distally during manipulation, whereas sharp items typically remain fixed at the site of lodgement. Although advancing a foreign body into the stomach can be considered an acceptable management strategy in some circumstances, our practice favors removal whenever feasible, since distal migration carries the risk of delayed obstruction or perforation. In the continuous model, each additional hour of delay was associated with a decreased likelihood of distal advancement (OR 0.90 per hour, 95% CI 0.82–0.99; p = 0.034), suggesting that prolonged retention over time may reduce the probability of displacement during endoscopic manipulation. This suggests that delayed intervention may reduce procedural flexibility and alter strategy selection. These findings underscore the importance of timely intervention and careful procedural planning, as the decision between extraction and distal advancement represents a strategic choice that may influence both immediate technical success and overall procedural safety.

This study has several limitations. First, it deliberately focused on adult patients who required endoscopic intervention for foreign body ingestion. Cases in which the ingested object passed spontaneously without the need for endoscopy were not included, as these patients represent a different clinical course and management strategy. Therefore, the findings of this study primarily reflect risk factors and outcomes relevant to endoscopically managed cases rather than the entire spectrum of foreign body ingestion. Its retrospective and single-center design restricts the generalizability of the findings, and the heterogeneous spectrum of ingested objects together with the relatively small number of complications may have limited the power of subgroup analyses. In addition, management decisions may have been influenced by operator preference and institutional logistics, which could not be fully standardized. Despite the use of penalized regression and internal validation, calibration performance was modest, likely reflecting the limited number of complication events. External validation in independent prospective cohorts is required before implementation into clinical risk stratification models.

Nevertheless, this cohort provides one of the more detailed analyses of adult foreign body ingestion in our region. The evaluation of hematemesis as a potential clinical marker of injury severity and the observation that blunt morphology increases the likelihood of distal advancement are novel contributions that complement existing literature. The predominance of culturally specific objects, such as headscarf safety pins, further illustrates the role of sociocultural context in shaping clinical presentation and outcomes.

In conclusion, foreign body ingestion in adults demonstrates wide heterogeneity in etiology and clinical course. While endoscopy is generally effective and safe, delayed endoscopy demonstrated a cumulative association with complication risk, with each hour of delay contributing incrementally to adverse outcomes. Blunt objects were more prone to distal advancement during retrieval attempts, though removal remains preferable to avoid delayed morbidity. These findings emphasize that both clinical status and object characteristics should be considered in management decisions, and that regional practices must inform risk assessment. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these results and to refine evidence-based algorithms for the timing and method of intervention.
