Consistent Reports of Pregnancy/Birth Contexts and Links to Parental Experiences
Authors: Karen Benjamin Guzzo, Gabrielle Juteau, Wendy D. Manning, Monica A. Longmore, Peggy C. Giordano, Alyssa G. Sherman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The link between pregnancy/birth contexts and subsequent maternal, family, and child well-being is the subject of extensive research. Much of this work uses retrospectively reported survey data on birth contexts, particularly for birth intendedness and relationship status at birth, but rarely has research considered whether or how people might report birth contexts differently over time. In this paper, we used Waves III, IV, and V of the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health), the only nationally representative longitudinal dataset with repeated measures of pregnancy/birth contexts for the same live birth. Focusing on first births, we (a) examined if, and to what extent, individuals changed their retrospective reports of pregnancy wantedness and relationship status at birth, (b) identified if there are sociodemographic correlates of inconsistent reports, and (c) explored whether inconsistent reports are associated with parental experiences. Introduction

The context in which individuals have children has long been a focus of both social and academic attention, particularly for unintended fertility (either a birth occurring to someone who does not want any (more) children or birth occurring to someone who wants a birth but at a later point in their lives) and nonmarital fertility (especially births to people who are neither married nor living with their child’s other parent). Earlier work linked unintended and nonmarital fertility to poorer outcomes for parents, children, or both (Barber et al., 1999; Gipson et al., 2008; Shah et al., 2011; Williams et al., 2011). More recent and methodologically rigorous work suggests that much of the strength of the association between children’s birth characteristics and subsequent outcomes among children is largely due to selection, but less favorable outcomes across some domains remain even after accounting for selection, especially for mothers (Crosnoe et al., 2023; Mark & Cowan, 2022; Nomaguchi & Milkie, 2020; Williams et al., 2011).

Because vital statistics data include neither measures of intendedness nor full categories of relationship status, and they do not include post-birth measures of well-being, the field often relies on survey data to investigate the linkages between birth contexts and subsequent outcomes. Although there is literature debating whether unintended fertility is a valid concept (e.g., Kost & Zolna, 2019; Potter et al., 2019), less attention is given to whether it is a reliable (i.e., consistently reported) concept, which has implications for understanding the links between birth characteristics and well-being. Similarly, despite increased recognition of a wide range of, and considerable change over time in, relationship circumstances among new parents (Carlson et al., 2004), the field has not fully considered how a parent’s current circumstances could be linked to how they characterize that relationship at a point in the past (see Teitler et al. (2006) for an exception).

This paper focuses on retrospective reports of relationship status at birth and pregnancy wantedness—two characteristics that have received considerable scholarly attention as both outcomes and predictors (Guzzo & Hayford, 2020)—in the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health).1 Intendedness, of which wantedness is a key component (along with timing), has long been the focus of concern over measurement, with critiques often centering on social desirability bias and ex post facto rationalization (Rosenzweig & Wolpin, 1993). People may be embarrassed to admit they had a birth following an unwanted pregnancy, or young parents may be reluctant to admit they wanted a birth at a very young age given social norms discouraging young parenthood. They may also be reluctant to describe a child they very much love now as unwanted at conception. And some people may not have had strong feelings either way; ambivalence about a pregnancy or birth—how people felt about having a child at that time under those circumstances—may wax and wane over time and bias retrospective characterizations of birth intendedness (Santelli et al., 2003).

There is also potential for social desirability and stigma to affect how relationship status is reported. Although nonmarital fertility is common, marital fertility continues to be the preferred normative context for childbearing and childrearing in the United States (Parker & Minkin, 2023). The nonmarital and noncoresidential relationships in which children are born are often unstable (Carlson et al., 2004), in part because some of the more serious and committed relationships progress to coresidence (if not marriage) during pregnancy and prior to birth (Lichter et al., 2014, 2016). Although we might expect that relationship status at birth might also suffer from social desirability bias (though perhaps to a lesser degree), we would not expect that people would feel ambivalent and have trouble deciding how to characterize, say, cohabiting unions; that is, they might not have mixed feelings about whether they were living together and how to report about their relationship. However, individuals might have recall issues related to timing and sequencing of births and relationships (i.e., did a couple decide to become exclusive and committed right before the baby was born or after? When did a couple start living together exactly—was it before or after the birth?). Thus, by examining two distinct measures (relationship context and wantedness) for which retrospective reporting would likely be influenced by different psychosocial and cognitive processes, we are better able to identify if retrospective birth reporting overall may be subject to these issues or if some birth characteristics are more prone to inconsistency than others. Further, we explore whether there are patterns of socioeconomic and demographic variation in inconsistency. Finally, we consider whether inconsistency could bias how we interpret theoretically relevant ‘outcomes’ measured later in the life course. Here, we consider parental experiences, as it is possible that those with more inconsistency exhibit more negative and less positive experiences with parenthood. To preview the results, we find evidence of inconsistency for both first birth relationship status and wantedness reports across waves (conditional on being able to match births across waves by century month of birth), but that inconsistency was not strongly patterned by sociodemographic characteristics other than age. We did not find evidence that the association between birth contexts and later parental experiences were biased by inconsistent reports.

Background

Survey research studies suggest that retrospectively collected data may be subject to change across time for many reasons (Pearson et al., 1992). Survey methodologists have documented key cognitive steps respondents undertake when answering questions (comprehending the question, retrieving relevant information, deciding whether that information is appropriate, and reporting that information), and error can occur at any or all of these stages (Tourangeau et al., 2000). If people do not comprehend the questions they are being asked or do not understand them as the researcher intends, respondents may simply give an answer that makes sense to them at the time they are being asked. Perhaps respondents confuse the characteristics of two similar events (such as mixing up two children’s birth dates) or simply forget as time passes. Life history calendars may address such problems, as they help respondents recall the timing and sequencing of events (Freedman et al., 1988); however, such tools may be less helpful for subjective recollections about events. Alternatively, people may think differently about a past event in light of their current circumstances. Anchoring bias, a phenomenon in which people use recent information about an event or experience to inform their recollection of the same event further back, is well-documented (Godlonton et al., 2018). Or perhaps respondents deliberately misrepresent something due to concerns over social desirability and stigma, and those concerns may change over time and thus result in reporting changes. There is reason to believe that within-person inconsistency in retrospective reporting may occur more often among less advantaged individuals or those with poorer mental health (Sudman et al., 1996; Tourangeau et al., 2000), perhaps reflecting greater mental stress, limited comprehension, or other challenges that affect the cognitive processes at play in survey responses.

This paper focuses on whether birth characteristics are consistently recalled and reported. Even outcomes that could be less subjective than unintended fertility, such as marital status at birth, may be prone to misreporting due to social desirability and social stigma. Moreover, and particularly relevant for work addressing both selection and temporal ordering, there is the possibility that parents change how they report characteristics of births depending on how their lives are progressing. For example, individuals might originally characterize a pregnancy/birth as planned but retrospectively re-cast that same birth as planned if their parental experiences (such as challenges with partners or economic hardship) cause them to re-evaluate the context in which they had their child (Chamberlin et al., 2022). Similarly, parents could report a birth as occurring within a cohabiting union when asked about a birth, even if they did not cohabit until after the birth occurred, to avoid social stigma. But if that same relationship later breaks up, parents could retrospectively report differently about that relationship status to avoid the stigma of relationship dissolution (Teitler et al., 2006).

In the United States, there is a large literature on birth intendedness and marital status at birth, much of which uses the National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG). The NSFG is cross-sectional and is important for documenting trends over time, but the consistency of the retrospective information it collects cannot be assessed, nor can it be used to study relationship characteristics for births occurring outside of coresidential relationships.2 Most studies using the NSFG are careful to avoid causal claims about the links between unintended fertility or union status at birth and whatever outcome is being studied given the cross-sectional nature of the data; there are many advantages to cross-sectional data over longitudinal data, because the latter are generally restricted to a single cohort and follow-up waves are often infrequent or irregularly spaced. Still, the possibility that birth characteristics are related to some outcome is typically the motivation for studies using the NSFG and other cross-sectional sources of data. But rarely do studies consider whether their focal independent variables are consistently reported, beyond the broader discussion about whether unintended fertility is a useful and meaningful concept noted above (Kost & Zolna, 2019; Potter et al., 2019).

Nonetheless, there is some prior research examining whether women change how they characterize the intendedness of a pregnancy/birth, largely working from the perspective that women become less likely to report a birth as unintended as the child ages and their relationship with their child deepens. This work has usually compared a single prospective versus a single retrospective report, finding substantial variation (Rackin & Morgan, 2018); such differences reflect, to some degree, actual shifts in desired or intended fertility between the prospective measurement and the later round. Few studies, however, explicitly compared two retrospective reports for the same pregnancy/birth. Guzzo and Hayford (2014), analyzing two early waves of data from Add Health (the same data used here), found that about one in five young mothers changed the characterization of wantedness for their first birth when asked about the same birth across waves. Rosenzweig and Wolpin (1993), using data with wantedness measures during pregnancy and post-birth, found that about one in four mothers changed their reports of wanting the pregnancy between these two time points depending on infant well-being (proxied by birthweight). Using longitudinal survey data, Barber and Gatny (2021) found that young women’s pregnancy desire was not stably recalled but, instead, seemed to reflect their circumstances at the time of the survey, suggesting that ex post facto rationalization and temporal consistency bias influence how past fertility events are reported. This was also observed among women with an unplanned pregnancy who had sought an abortion but were unable to obtain one (Rocca et al., 2019). Teitler et al. (2006) reported that, among a cohort of unmarried mothers, a substantial share revised their relationship status at birth between their initial report at the time of birth and the one-year follow-up (when they were again asked about the relationship status at birth).

How might we understand the link between birth characteristics, on the one hand, and theoretically relevant outcomes, on the other, in the presence of inconsistency? Temporal ordering is often assumed if one event would have preceded another event in time, even when information on both events is collected at the same time (as is the case in cross-sectional surveys). For instance, age at sexual initiation and patterns of contraceptive use are often collected simultaneously in retrospective surveys of reproductive behavior, and researchers have used such cross-sectional data to test whether early sexual debut is linked to later contraceptive use patterns (e.g., Finer & Philbin, 2013; Magnusson et al., 2012). Such analyses of cross-sectional data are valuable as they help establish key correlations, though it is possible that people could, at different points in their life, be more or less willing to disclose or give a response that could be stigmatized.

Birth intendedness and relationship status at birth have been theorized as important for a range of later outcomes. For instance, Barber et al. (1999), using longitudinal data, took information on whether a birth was categorized as unwanted at conception from earlier waves of data and linked that to aspects of parent–child relationships at later waves. They found that mothers who reported that they had unwanted births at an earlier wave later reported lower-quality emotional relationships with their children. They also note, though, that retrospective reports of fertility intendedness may “largely reflect mothers’ current relationships with their children, based on retrospective evaluations, and may thus severely constrain our ability to make causal inferences” (p. 24). Similarly, although evidence suggests that changes in parents’ relationship status are associated with elevated parental stress (Osborne et al., 2012), it is possible that parenting experiences could themselves affect how individuals retrospectively report about their relationship with their child’s other biological parent. Thus, we might expect that people who report consistently about the wantedness and the relationship status of a birth at multiple time points might have better parenting experiences—more positive experiences and fewer negative experiences—than those whose reports are less consistent.

As such, better attention to consistency in retrospective reports is warranted for two reasons. First, within-person inconsistency might exhibit social patterning, suggesting that survey questions do not reflect reproductive and childbearing experiences equally well across groups. There is already evidence of this related to socioeconomic status as well as race and ethnicity (Aiken & Trussell, 2017; Chamberlin et al., 2022; Guzzo et al., 2020; Teitler et al., 2006). Second, within-person change in how birth characteristics are reported may have implications for how we interpret the links between such characteristics and theoretically relevant outcomes such as parental experiences.

In the current paper, we address the gaps in the literature by focusing on three goals. First, using a large, nationally representative longitudinal survey, we assess the extent to which individuals report retrospective relationship status at birth and pregnancy wantedness consistently when asked about their first birth across waves of data collection. Second, using multivariable analyses, we investigate whether there are patterns regarding who is, or is not, likely to inconsistently report relationship status at birth and wantedness, focusing on sociodemographic and economic characteristics that reflect aspects of social disadvantage that may affect parents’ ability to effectively recall past events. Third, we considered whether inconsistent first birth reports are associated with theoretically relevant outcomes, focusing here on positive and negative parental experiences. As sensitivity checks, we also (a) examine consistency of an alternative demographic indicator collected at two time points in Add Health, (b) supplement the Add Health analyses by looking at consistency of relationship status at conception and pregnancy intendedness using a small, nonrepresentative longitudinal dataset, and (c) confirm the validity of our approach of focusing on multiple reports about the same birth by using a measure of ‘ever’ reported their first birth as occurring outside of a “steady” union or unwanted at any wave and identifying socioeconomic and demographic correlates as well as the links with our outcome measures.

Data and Methods

This project primarily used the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health; Harris et al., 2019). Add Health is a nationally representative longitudinal survey of adolescent boys and girls in grades 7–12 in the 1994–1995 school year. The original sampling frame included 80 high schools and their feeder middle schools, stratified by region, urbanicity, sector, race, and size. From school rosters, adolescents were selected to complete in-home interviews at Wave I (1995) including oversamples of some key groups (certain minority groups, sibling pairs, disabled youth, and adopted youth). In total, there were 20,745 adolescents interviewed at Wave I. Youth still in school one year later (grades 8–12) were re-interviewed at Wave II (1996). All the original Wave I respondents were re-interviewed three more times: Wave III 2001–2002, ages 18–24 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document} = 15,197); Wave IV 2008–2009, ages 26–31 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document} = 15,701); Wave V 2016–2018, 35–40 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document} = 12,300).3 Add Health is the only recently collected nationally representative data set that includes repeated retrospective reports on the same births across interview waves. Notably, Add Health does not include prospective measures of fertility desires. Full birth retrospective histories, nested in relationship histories, were collected at Waves III–V, with limited self-reported birth information at earlier waves. There are 9349 respondents who participated in Waves III, IV, and V, declining to 9149 when accounting for sample weights. There are 10,685 respondents who participated in Waves IV and V with valid sample weights.

Analytical Sample

To ensure that the respondents’ retrospective reports about their birth refer to the same birth, we first organized the births by birth date and restricted analyses to first births that matched on birth date (month and year) across all waves and that were reported as a singleton birth at each wave (Fig. 1). This resulted in an analytical sample size of 1651 parents who were in Waves III, IV, and V (which we refer to as the “First Birth Wave III” sample), and 4911 parents who participated in Waves IV and V (referred to as the “First Birth Wave IV” sample). As is evident from Fig. 1, there was a substantial case loss related to matching on first birth dates, a necessary step to ensure we are comparing information about the same birth. For the First Birth Wave III sample, there was a total loss of 443 cases (about 27% of those who report a birth), with 186 lost to not reporting a first birth date at one or more analytical sample component waves and 257 lost because the reported dates of first birth did not match across the component waves. For the larger First Birth Wave IV sample, 980 cases were lost (about 20% of those who report a birth), with 557 missing because there was no date of birth information at one or both component waves and 423 lost because the reported date of first birth did not match across Waves IV and V.

The missing dates of first birth were due to issues primarily in Waves III and Wave V. In Wave III, the wording of how birth data was gathered relative to relationship histories in Wave III led to some confusion about which relationships were to be included in the birth history, resulting in a considerable number of births being omitted (see Schoen et al., 2007 for a discussion of the issue). At Wave V, information on births was collected in three different ways across the survey. First, there were three relevant questions in the section on Sexual Experiences and Pregnancy: “Has a partner of yours (if male)/have you (if female) ever been pregnant with your biological child?” If the respondent answered affirmatively, they were then asked, “How many times [has a partner of yours (if male)/have you (if female) been pregnant?” and, if respondents report a number greater than zero, “How many live births resulted from these pregnancies? Please include babies who died shortly after birth and babies who were placed for adoption.” Second, there was information in the section on Pregnancy, Live Births, Children and Parenting. Respondents were asked “Have you or any current or past partner ever been pregnant with your biological child?” Those who responded affirmatively were later asked “The next few questions are about pregnancies that ended in a live birth. We only want to know about your biological children with any partner. How many biological children do you have?” Third, there was an indirect way, again using information from the Pregnancy, Live Births, Children and Parenting section. Respondents who reported having at least one live birth were asked a series of questions for up to six children that pertain to dates of birth (month and year), relationship status at birth, and other health/social measures. Some respondents who lacked a valid response to the direct question about live births resulting in biological children nonetheless had responses for the detailed follow-up questions, whereas others who had an answer to the direct question about number of children had no responses for some or all of these questions.

It is unclear why some respondents either were not asked or did not provide valid information across sections at Wave V. Perhaps some respondents did not realize they were being asked to provide birth data again, in addition to the information they already provided earlier in the survey. Compared to prior waves, Wave V had more variation in administration type (in-person, mail, telephone, or web; the latter included different platforms such as PC, smartphones, tablets, and gaming consoles), different modules (most respondents got two modules, but a small subset got only one module), and different sub-samples (including a very small pilot). Some of the fertility items have a code of “question not asked,” and others are simply missing (i.e., with no code despite the presence of codes for “legitimate skip” and “don’t know”). Other work analyzing the patterns of missing first birth data in Add Health suggest it not entirely missing at random but instead is positively associated with age, and higher among men, racialized minorities, those with 10 or more sexual partners, and those with less than a high school degree (relative to those with a college degree; Guzzo et al., 2024).

In addition to being unable to match dates of birth due to missing birth data, cases were also lost because reported dates of first birth simply did not match across waves. Some of this is likely due to data entry or minor recall issues, such as birth dates differing by exactly month or one year. One-month or one-year differences account for about 15–25% of the overall number of cases lost due to birth date issues (and 35–40% of those who have non-missing dates of first birth), with the remainder having dates that do not exhibit any clear pattern. As with those missing first birth dates in at least one wave, mismatches due to discrepancies in birth dates (being off by exactly one month or year as well as those with no clear pattern) vary by sociodemographic and economic characteristics: they are positively associated with age and are higher among racialized minorities and those with less education (Guzzo et al., 2024). We included these covariates (and others, as discussed below) to partially account for the selectivity of our analytical samples.

Measures

Focal Variables

The two focal fertility measures were retrospective reports of first birth relationship status and pregnancy wantedness. These were asked for each pregnancy/birth that the respondent reported; this analysis only looked at pregnancies resulting in a live birth given under-reporting of miscarriages and abortions (Lindberg et al., 2020). The first of the two measures concerns relationship status. Respondents were asked about relationship status at the time of birth. In Waves III and IV of Add Health, this information is derived from a series of questions. Respondents were first asked if they were married to the child’s other parent at the time of birth and then, if the answer was no, were asked if they and their child’s other parent lived together at that time. Respondents who were neither married nor living with the child’s other parent were then asked, “Which of the following statements best describes your relationship with the [child’s other parent] at the time of this birth?” Response categories included: “we did not see or talk to each other,” “we hardly ever saw or talked to each other,” “we were just friends,” “we were involved in an on-again, off-again relationship,” “we were romantically involved on a steady basis,” “refused,” and “don’t know.” In Wave V, these questions were combined into a single question, “Which of the following statements best describes your relationship with your pregnancy partner at the time of this child's birth?” Response categories included: “we were married,” “we were not married, but living together,” “we were not living together, but were romantically involved,” “we were involved in an on-again, off-again relationship,” “we were just friends,” “we hardly ever saw or talked to each other,” and “we did not see or talk to each other.”

Looking next at wantedness, respondents were asked at Waves III and IV “Before you got pregnant, did you want to get pregnant by your partner at that time?” Response categories included no, yes, “refused,” or “don’t know.” In Wave V, the question was phrased as “Thinking back to the time just before this pregnancy, did you want to have a baby then?” Response categories included no and yes; there was no option for “don’t know” at this wave.

To address whether inconsistency ‘matters’ for theoretically relevant outcomes, we considered parenting experiences. Add Health has four parenting questions at Wave V: (1) “I am happy in my role as a parent,” (2) “I feel close to my child(ren),” (3) “The major source of stress in my life is my child(ren),” and (4) “I feel overwhelmed by the responsibility of being a parent.” Responses ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). These are global parenting experiences and are not specific to any child/parity. Covariates

Additional variables included sex, race-ethnicity, age at first birth (categorized as ≤ 17, 18–19, 20–21, and ≥ 22 for the First Birth Wave III sample and as ≤ 18, 19–20, 21–23, 24–26, and ≥ 27 for the First Birth Wave IV sample); educational level at Wave V; employment status at Wave V; use of public assistance or food stamps at Wave III or IV (not available at Wave V); a depression score above 0.66 or higher on a truncated CES-D depression scale at any wave (only three questions were repeated across all three surveys, and the scale ranged from 0 = never/rarely to 3 = most or all of the time, with 0.66 roughly corresponding to the 75th percentile); reported any intimate partner violence perpetration or victimization in current/most recent relationship at Waves IV or V (not necessarily with other parent and not available for Wave III); coresidential union experience by Wave V (currently cohabiting/married, previously cohabited/married, never cohabited/married); a dichotomous indicator of self-reported good health (defined as reporting a score of three or higher on a scale of 1–5, where 1 = poor, 2 = fair, 3 = good, 4 = very good, and 5 = excellent); subjective social class at Wave V (based on a question asking respondents to rank themselves on a ladder of 1–10, where 1 is lowest and 10 is best), and parity as of Wave V. Missing Information on the Key Variables of Interest

Our analytical samples were further restricted to those with valid data on relationship status at birth and pregnancy wantedness at each wave and with valid data on parental experiences. For relationship status, 27 respondents were missing valid responses in one or more of the three waves of the Firth Birth Wave III sample, and six respondents were missing valid responses for either Wave IV or V when considering only the First Birth Wave IV sample. For wantedness, because “don’t know” was not included as an option at Wave V, only respondents with responses other than “don’t know” or “refused” at Waves III and IV were included. This led to the loss of an additional 44 cases in the First Birth Wave III sample and 37 cases in the First Birth Wave IV sample. Finally, for parental experiences at Wave V, in the First Birth Wave III analytical sample, there was missing information for an additional 13 respondents. For the First Birth Wave IV analytical sample, we lost an additional 37 respondents without data for parental experiences. After accounting for our analytical sample restrictions and missing data on the key measures of interest, the final analytical sample size was 1,083 for the First Birth Wave III sample and 3,766 for the First Birth Wave IV sample, as shown in Fig. 1.

Analytical Plan

First, we descriptively documented the extent to which respondents are inconsistent in how they retrospectively characterized relationship status at birth and pregnancy wantedness for first births. Second, we considered the link between inconsistency and sociodemographic and psychosocial measures using multivariable logistic regression, presenting odds ratios (ORs). Third, we examined whether inconsistency potentially affected the strength of the association between first birth characteristics and a theoretically subsequent outcome, parents’ experiences. We used OLS models regressing positive and negative parenting experiences on the consistency measures, controlling for covariates. For all analyses, we show results separately for the First Birth Wave III sample and for the First Birth Wave IV sample. The analyses were weighted to account for the complex survey design. For analyses using the First Birth Wave III sample, we used the longitudinal weight for respondents who participated in Waves I, III, IV, and V. For analyses using the First Birth Wave IV sample, we used the longitudinal weight for the respondents who participated in Waves I, IV, and V. We imputed data on missing covariates using multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE).

We also conducted three sensitivity tests. First, we used an alternative demographic measure—nativity—asked at Waves III and Wave V to determine whether a basic yes/no question unrelated to childbearing exhibits inconsistency.4 Second, we used another dataset, the Toledo Adolescent Relationships Study (TARS), to examine change in relationship status at birth and intendedness across waves of data. TARS is a much smaller dataset and has different operationalization of the key measures but does provide another opportunity to consider the prevalence of inconsistency. Finally, we examined the face validity of our pregnancy/birth context measures by using a measure of ‘ever’ reports (i.e., ever reporting an unwanted birth or a birth outside of a relationship) and linking them to covariates and parenting experiences.

Turning to the First Birth Wave IV sample, consistency is higher for both measures than the First Birth Wave III sample; this likely reflects not only fewer ‘chances’ to change reports but also the less select and more advantaged group of parents in First Birth Wave IV sample. About three fourths of respondents consistently reported their relationship status. Finally, looking at wantedness, the share who characterized their birth as wanted at conception increased across time from 55.9 to 62.2%, with relatively small standard errors; 80.3% had consistent reports at both waves.

Consistency and Sociodemographic Relationships

Table 5 shows odds ratios (ORs) of consistent reports across the First Birth Wave III sample and the First Birth Wave IV sample separately based on multivariable logistic regression models with sociodemographic and economic covariates. In the First Birth Wave III sample, non-Hispanic Black parents were less likely to report relationship status consistently across waves relative to non-Hispanic White parents (OR = 0.570, p = 0.013). Age was also important for consistent relationship reports. Relative to those aged 21–23, those 17 and younger at first birth were less likely to have consistent retrospective reports of first birth relationship status (OR = 0.439, p = 0.002), whereas those 22 and older were more likely (OR = 2.249, p = 0.006). Finally, those who reported depressive symptomology at any wave were less likely to report relationship status consistently (OR = 0.543, p = 0.042). For wantedness consistency across the waves of the First Birth Wave III sample, only two covariates achieved statistical significance. Compared to those with a high school degree or equivalency, those with a four-year college degree or higher were more likely to report birth wantedness consistently across waves (OR = 2.243, p = 0.007). Public assistance receipt was also important, with those reporting receiving public assistance at either Wave III or Wave IV being less likely to report wantedness consistently across all three waves Sensitivity Tests

We conducted several sensitivity tests. First, we considered whether respondents exhibited inconsistency in a characteristic that is unrelated to births. There are few such items to compare, as most surveys do not re-collect information that would be considered static. However, Add Health did collect information on respondent’s nativity at two of the three waves considered here. Respondents had nearly 100% consistency across Waves III and Wave V in whether the respondents reported that they had been born in the United States, as shown in Table S3 in Online Supplement Section B. It appears that inconsistency across waves of data collection for the same, ostensibly fixed, characteristic is not an inherent feature of repeated measurement, suggesting that birth characteristics might be uniquely subject to recharacterization over time.

Second, we examined inconsistency among another longitudinal dataset that collected complete fertility histories at multiple waves, the Toledo Adolescent Relationships Study (TARS; see Online Supplement Section C). Like Add Health, it has up to three waves with complete fertility histories and so can be analyzed as either consistency across three waves (Waves 4, 5, or 6, referred to in the tables as “First Birth Wave 4”) or the latter two waves (Wave 5 and 6, referred to as “First Birth Wave 5”). However, it is not nationally representative and is considerably smaller (with 90 cases with a first birth across in the earlier birth sample and 250 cases across the latter two waves in the later birth sample); the birth characteristic measures are also operationalized differently than Add Health (and differently than in most surveys with such information). Although there are large standard errors for point estimates when comparing across waves, we find evidence of inconsistency across waves for both relationship status at conception and pregnancy intendedness in TARS as in Add Health. For relationship status at first birth conception in the First Birth Wave 4 sample (younger parents), more than 30% reported different statuses at least two waves, and for first birth intendedness (which has four categories in TARS) across waves, 60% reported something different in at least two waves (see Online Supplement Table S4). When looking at consistency in the First Birth Wave 5 sample, between 17 and 30% reported different relationship statuses (depending on how these were collapsed given changes in wording across waves), and more than 40% reported different intendedness across waves. Intendedness inconsistency is likely higher in TARS than wantedness inconsistency in Add Health due to the greater number of categories (four compared to two)—meaning more chances one could report differently—and the fact that two of the categories (“hadn’t thought about it” and “didn’t care”) likely capture ambivalence, which may be strongly linked to inconsistent reports. Thus, that TARS—a very different dataset with different sampling and measures—also contains inconsistencies for how first birth characteristics are reported by the same person over time suggests that the main findings are not solely an artifact of the Add Health.

Finally, as a confirmation of face validity of our two pregnancy/birth context measures when analyzed across multiple waves, we created versions of these measures in which we coded respondents who had ever reported their first birth—at any wave—as something other than committed (i.e., boyfriend/ girlfriend, cohabiting, or married) or something other than wanted; prevalence estimates for these are listed in the final column of Table 2. The sociodemographic variables linked to these ‘ever’ reports are consistent with prior work using other datasets (see Online Supplement Section D). For instance, measures indicative of marginalization or disadvantage (i.e., race and ethnic minority status, low education, public assistance receipt) were associated with a higher likelihood of reporting, at least once, that a birth occurred outside of a committed relationship or was something other than wanted (see Online Supplement Table S5). Similarly, these ‘ever’ reports were linked to parenting experiences as would be expected. Those who had ever reported their birth was not wanted had significantly lower positive parenting experiences at Wave V, though only for First Birth Wave IV sample (see Online Supplement Table S6).

Discussion and Conclusion

This project addressed an important but overlooked area in the wider body of work on how birth contexts influence subsequent parental, family, and child well-being. Both scholarly literature and public conversation have long debated the implications of nonmarital and unintended fertility, with most research about the contexts of births drawing from survey data. Certainly, measurement of unintended fertility has increasingly been contested in terms of its validity (e.g., Kost & Zolna, 2019; Potter et al., 2019)—is it measuring what scholars think it is capturing? Is it a meaningful concept to parents? Similarly, the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study revealed the nuances of unmarried parents’ relationships (Carlson et al., 2004) that are not captured in standard vital statistics data.

Despite evidence that pregnancy/birth contexts are not always consistently recalled (e.g., Chamberlin et al., 2022; Teitler et al., 2006), this knowledge does not seem to have influenced how longitudinal surveys collect birth information. Most longitudinal datasets that collect self-reported fertility data only ask about each birth once, at the first round of data collection that occurs after the birth (e.g., National Longitudinal Survey of Youth), and subsequent follow-ups ask only about events since the last interview. If people retrospectively re-cast past events to make them consistent with their current lives or have recall issues over long periods of time, the extent to which this occurs is important to understand. Essentially, this paper asked three questions: (1) How consistent are people’s retrospective reports about their pregnancy/birth contexts?, (2) Are there patterns of variation in consistency across sociodemographic and pyschosocial characteristics?, and (3) Does it matter—is inconsistency linked to subsequent theoretically interesting outcomes?

The answer to the first question is ‘yes.’ Inconsistent reporting occurred in about half of reports of relationship status at birth and a third of the reports for wantedness when looking across the young parents whose first birth occurred by Wave III. This declined to about a quarter for relationship status and about a fifth for wantedness when considering those whose first birth occurred by Wave IV, when the sample was larger and less skewed toward younger parents. Overall, the takeaway seems to be that a) the more frequently individuals are asked to report about the same event, the more often they will report something differently; b) more categories generally increase the chances of changes in retrospective reports; c) change is less common for categories that are more clearly and objectively defined (like marriage); but d) much of the overall change, at least for relationship status, occurs among those in serious but unmarried relationships. Although less than one in five young parents reported not being in some sort of committed relationship when the child was born, respondents who initially reported not being in those relationships largely continued to characterize those births in similar ways. However, first births were increasingly characterized as “steady” but not coresidential over time, and this seems to be largely attributable to those who had initially characterized their births as cohabiting and then reporting later the birth as occurring in a “steady” but non-coresidential relationship. This is further evidence that cohabitation remains a ‘fuzzy’ category that is difficult to capture in surveys (Manning et al., 2019).

The answer to the second question is a qualified ‘no’—there is only weak patterning of inconsistency across sociodemographic and psychosocial characteristics. The strongest finding relates to age. Respondents with earlier births exhibit more inconsistency among first birth relationship status reports; this could reflect the impact of the passage of time on recall accuracy. It could also result from those with earlier births having greater exposure to other life course changes that directly affect recall or decrease respondents’ mental capacity in ways that recall is impacted. There is also some evidence that consistent reports occur less often among non-Hispanic Black parents as well as those who had received public assistance, a proxy for poverty. In general, though, there was little clear evidence of systematic factors linked to inconsistent reports. As such, inconsistency could be largely a random occurrence when respondents are asked about fairly subjective facets of past events and given somewhat subjective response categories; perhaps survey fatigue comes into play given the extensive topics and questions asked in Add Health. That there is at best only weak evidence of social patterning for inconsistency despite the clear presence of social patterning for the ‘ever’ reports supports this interpretation.

And lastly, does it matter if retrospective characteristics are not consistently reported, in terms of biasing the link between ‘outcomes’ and birth characteristics? The answer is ‘no,’ at least for the positive and negative parenting measures considered here. Inconsistency does not seem to be linked to parental experiences, and there is little evidence to suggest that the inevitable challenges that accompany parenting lead parents to retrospectively re-cast whether they wanted to get pregnant with their firstborn child or describe their relationship at first birth.

Inconsistency in retrospective reports of birth characteristics is not unique to Add Health nor is it specific to how birth characteristics are measured. We found evidence that it also occurred in a very different sample (TARS) that measured birth contexts related to relationship status at birth and intendedness differently. Further, inconsistency is not specific to the United States. Chamberlin et al. (2022) found, in Malawi, that half of the pregnant women recalled their pregnancy planning differently three years after the pregnancy than they reported during the pregnancy. Relationship status is also likely to be subject to retrospective recall inconsistency; one study, also using data from Malawi, show that 18% of women and 28% of men omitted at least one marriage when full marriage histories were collected at multiple time points (Chae, 2016). Thus, we would expect the findings here to generalize to other contexts outside the United States.

Limitations

A primary concern relates to the substantial loss of cases in the first stage of the process (matching first births across surveys based on birth dates). It is possible that there was considerable socioeconomic and demographic selection at that initial stage, such that there is less selection or social patterning at the stage in which specific characteristics of matched births are compared across points in time (Guzzo et al., 2024). Additionally, because respondents had to report a birth in an early wave to thus compare subsequent reports, the samples used here are disproportionately comprised of young parents and early births, not all parents/births. Add Health also lacks prospective fertility intention data, so we cannot compare prospective and retrospective reports of intentions for the same birth. Additionally, although we drew on the literature on birth intendedness, the measure available in Add Health strictly measured wantedness. We also did not consider lagged indicators of sociodemographic measures due in part to the varying and potentially consequential time lapses between interview waves. Lagged analyses would be better suited to data with regular, short-term spells between interview waves. Another limitation is the ‘outcome’ was measured at the same point as the latest possible opportunity for respondents to change their reports about births; as such, any links between inconsistency and ‘outcomes’ should be interpreted as associations rather than causal pathways. It is also possible that measuring ‘outcomes’ at a point in time closer to birth or examining different ‘outcomes’ might yield different results. We also did not explore within-wave inconsistency (for instance, whether direct reports of relationship status at birth align with dates of relationships) nor did we examine whether inconsistency in related family behaviors, such as dates of marriage, occurred across waves. Those are potentially important questions to pursue but beyond the scope of the current analysis. Finally, we have no way of knowing precisely why people report differently across waves —whether they misunderstood the question, whether they are mixing up different births, whether they feel less stigma or social pressure over time, or whether they have survey fatigue and do not give the question sufficient thought, among other possible reasons.

Conclusion

Surveys are, and will continue to be, a primary method in demography and other social science fields for collecting a wide range of details about past events, and this is true even as administrative data becomes more readily available. Although an important avenue for providing otherwise unavailable information for broad swaths of the population, administrative data often excludes the type of measures that inform and underlie social demography as a field. The findings here—using Add Health but supplementing with a different survey with a very different sampling frame and question wording—suggest that inconsistency among birth characteristic reporting is not specific to a particular survey, sampling approach, or question wording. Further, although the sensitivity test revealed virtually no inconsistency for the respondent’s reporting of nativity over time, it is likely that inconsistency exists for topics besides fertility, particularly for those that entail describing a context surrounding an event (i.e., aspects of a relationship prior to marriage or job search strategies prior to employment). As such, survey point estimates about the characteristics of retrospective events, especially subjective evaluations of those events, should be taken with a grain of salt. In survey data collections, it may be an efficient strategy from a cost perspective to not repeat questions from prior interview waves, but the results here point to a potential shortcoming of this strategy. Although those using survey data certainly incorporate both sampling error and measurement error into their interpretations, it may also be worthwhile to consider the degree of consistency related to how respondents interpret, process, and respond to survey items about events that happened in the past. That said, we did not find evidence of strong social patterning beyond age (conditional on matching on dates of first birth) or that changes in how people characterized their birth contexts biased the association between birth contexts and parental experiences. This suggests that cross-sectional data is likely adequate for many research purposes.

Whether the field should think of inconsistency as error, however, is less clear. People’s responses to questions reflect how they recall their experience of a past event at a given point in time and, thus, are meaningful even if perhaps not entirely accurate. To paraphrase Luker (1999), the way people think about their own lives and experiences often does not align with how researchers would like to operationalize, categorize, and analyze those same lives and events. Pairing survey data with qualitative interviews might provide important context that could aid in understanding the ways that respondents process questions and their replies (Gómez et al., 2019). Should longitudinal surveys move to re-collect data about events that have already been discussed in prior waves—as might be warranted given the findings here—scholars will need to decide from which wave to draw data and how to handle discrepancies. Some of the choice would likely be driven by the research question and approach to causal modeling; for instance, it may be necessary to use an earlier wave of data to predict an outcome at a later wave. Researchers might wish to conduct sensitivity tests, perhaps restricting the analytical sample to those who exhibit no inconsistency (though this could introduce selectivity along age, race, and other characteristics). Another alternative would be to consider whether respondents ever reported about an event in a particular way. Perhaps it matters more for long-term outcomes among mothers, for instance, if they ever considered their birth unintended. This is an empirical question that has parallels beyond the measurement, correlates, and consequences of fertility behaviors. It also has implications for how we theorize and construct models about demographic events and experiences that rely on reports from real people.
