Sensory decline in aging and neurodegeneration: A mitochondrial perspective
Authors: Oluwamayomikun Adeboye, Bianca L Gonda, Nikita Gulati, Keming Hu, Alexa R Karp, Dylan C McNally, Wesley Tsai, Jennyfer G Zarate, Madison Holmes, Lanae Johnson-Kleinpeter, Hay Young Kwok, Victory J Miranda, Julie H Nguyen, Karishma A Patel, Apurva Polagoni, Bryan Ramirez, Renata T Da Costa, Cameron R Love, Andrey Grigoriev, Maria E Solesio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sensory organs (touch, sight, hearing, smell, and taste) are the primary way humans perceive the environment. Accordingly, sensory decline deleteriously affects patients’ quality of life, and it is often associated with a worse prognosis of related pathologies. This decline has been broadly described in aging and in the main aging-related neurodegenerative disorders, even though it is often understudied and considered a minor symptom. The molecular pathways that drive aging-associated sensory decline are broad and diverse; however, mitochondrial dysfunction has been described at the intersection of many of these mechanisms. Mitochondria have robust mechanisms to maintain their homeostasis. These mechanisms include, for example, the unfolded protein response, the antioxidant systems, and mitophagy. During aging, these mechanisms are dysregulated, which contributes to the dysfunction of the organelle. For example, increased reactive oxygen species generation and mitochondrial DNA mutations are present in cells and tissues, the dysregulation of which is crucial for the onset of sensory decline. In this bibliographical review, we systematically and comprehensively discuss the most significant and recent findings in the field. The studies we describe here suggest that targeting mitochondrial dysfunction could be a valid, innovative, and promising pharmacological target against sensory decline in aging and aging-related neurodegenerative disorders. Moreover, targeting mitochondrial dysfunction might have positive effects on other symptoms of aging and associated pathologies, including Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s Diseases. Further research in the field could significantly contribute to decreasing sensory decline, which could positively impact both lifespan and healthspan.

Introduction

Aging is a major risk for developing many human diseases, including all the most common neurodegenerative disorders [1]. In past few decades, as the global population ages, the study of aging-related diseases and their socio-economic consequences has gained interest worldwide [2,3]. The effects of aging on human pathology span multiple systems. For instance, 94% of older adults in the United States experience sensory decline in at least one sense, with 38% experiencing it in two senses, and 28% in three or more [4]. These findings are expected to be comparable in other regions. Sensory decline has often been considered a minor symptom in the context of aging and aging-related pathologies. However, the literature associates this decline with decreased lifespan and healthspan [5,6], and multiple authors have proposed using this decline as a biomarker in the context of several major neurodegenerative disorders (see [7–9]). Accordingly, identifying the molecular mechanisms at the intersection of sensory decline; and aging, and aging-related pathologies has great potential to contribute to increasing both lifespan and healthspan, as well as to provide better biomarkers for the onset and progression of these pathologies. This is key, especially considering that we live in an aging society.

Mitochondria are crucial organelles in the maintenance of mammalian cell metabolism [10]. They are the main net producers of mammalian ATP [11], as well as of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [12]. Mitochondria are also critical hubs for intracellular signaling, including calcium [13–16]; and crucially involved in macromolecules synthesis [17]. Under stress conditions, such as those encountered during aging and aging-related pathologies, mitochondrial quality systems are compromised. Specifically, dysregulated mitophagy, fission/fusion, unfolded protein response, ubiquitin-proteosome system; have been described under stress conditions [18–20]. Dysregulation of these physiological processes ultimately is a major contributor to mitochondrial dysfunction [21]. This dysfunction has been broadly described in a wide range of human pathologies, including COVID–19, cardiac diseases, diabetes, and all the major neurodegenerative disorders [18, 22–29]. Interestingly, mitochondrial dysfunction has also emerged as a key element at the intersection between sensory decline; and aging, and aging-related pathologies, such as Parkinson’s Disease and Alzheimer’s Disease [30–33]. In fact, multiple authors have described the mechanistic role of mitochondrial dysfunction in the dysregulation of specific tissues and cells that are crucially involved in sensory decline, across all five senses. For example, increased ROS generation, mutated mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), defective electron transport chain (ETC), and associated dysregulated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), are present in many of these cells and tissues (Fig. 1). Accordingly, mitochondrial-addressed therapies are currently the focus of many studies [34–38], since they have a broad therapeutic potential in this context. These studies include both developing new drugs and repurposing old ones [18,39].

In this bibliographical review, we have critically discussed the literature available on the intersection between mitochondrial dysfunction, and sensory decline associated with aging, and aging-related diseases (Graphical Abstract). The reviewed articles collectively suggest that targeting mitochondrial dysfunction has significant potential to mitigate aging-related sensory decline; further research in this field could help increase lifespan and healthspan.

TOUCH

Touch sensing is the detection of mechanical stimuli by the skin. It is mostly mediated by cutaneous mechanoreceptors, which can detect many stimuli, including but not limited to stretch, vibration, and pressure [40]. The wide array of touch stimuli has been associated with a broad range of specialized mechanoreceptors types [40]. Touch has been classically described as the most robust sense, even though its dysregulation has been documented in some human pathologies [41–43]. Interestingly, aging-related touch dysregulation has been described, and mitochondrial dysfunction has been proposed as a component of its molecular mechanism. For example, exposure to ultraviolet radiation (UV) is a major external stressor for the skin, and UV-associated damage often appears during aging [44–46]. While the molecular mechanisms that underlie this UV-induced damage are complex, the involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction has already been demonstrated [47–49]. In fact, mitochondrial dysfunction deleteriously affects the function of the sebaceous glands via increased lipid accumulation and modification of these glands (including increased size, and nuclear and cellular abnormalities). It also promotes increased proliferation of sebaceous gland cell progenitors, and increased expression of stem cell-associated markers and NOTCH ligands in mtDNA-depleter mouse models [50]. This is similar to what is found in chronologically induced skin photoaging [51]. In fact, depleted mtDNA is present in skin aging [50,52]. Accordingly, alterations present in the mtDNA-depleter mice include some symptoms also present in elder humans, such as erythema, scaling, and xerosis [50].

UVB, which penetrates the epidermis more deeply to induce direct cellular DNA damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction; is a higher-energy radiation than UVA, which causes indirect mtDNA damage mainly by increasing ROS generation [53]. Wang et al., using cultured human epidermal keratinocytes isolated from healthy donors, show that high doses of UVB (>100 mJ/cm2) trigger increased mitochondrial fission and translocation of pro-apoptotic Bax to the mitochondrial outer membrane, ultimately increasing apoptosis [54]. These findings are further supported by the evidence that UVB irradiation, particularly that in the wavelength range of 280–315 nm causes direct mtDNA damage and increased oxidative stress in human skin [55]. The impact of UVB irradiation on mitochondrial network configuration has also been explored using human epidermal keratinocytes [56]. In another study, Jugé et al., show that Drp1 knock down (KD) attenuates UVB-induced mitochondrial fragmentation in human epidermal keratinocytes [56]. The authors also show that Drp1 KD in epidermal keratinocytes results in a significant dose-dependent increase in mitochondrial circularity (the ratio between mitochondrial area and perimeter) accompanied by concomitant decreased aspect ratio (the ratio between the major and minor axes of mitochondria). These results demonstrate that the response of the mitochondrial network to UVB irradiation is fission-dependent and Drp1-mediated [56].

Factors other than photoaging also contribute to aging skin. For example, Yamamura et al., using mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM)-deficient and wild-type (Wt) mice in young and aging groups; show that epidermal cells isolated from aged mice exhibit reduced mitochondrial mass and volume, due to a deficiency in TFAM within keratinocytes [57]. The authors also show that TFAM-deficient mice exhibit delayed hair regrowth and impaired wound healing. Moreover, ROS levels within the epidermis of both aged and young mice were relatively similar, but the number of mitochondria was significantly decreased in aged vs. young mice [57]. Lastly, the authors show that inducing mitochondrial dysfunction in epidermal cells of young mice through TFAM deficiency increases aging-related deterioration of skin structure and function [57].

Paradoxically, mitochondrial antioxidant deficiency within keratinocytes is not always detrimental to the skin in the context of aging. This was demonstrated using a keratinocyte-specific Sod2-deficient mouse model, which exhibits age-dependent pleiotropic effects in mitochondrial dysfunction [58]. Using this model, Velarde et al., studied how wound healing was affected by epidermal differentiation, collagen formation, and inflammatory response. Their results show that epidermal SOD2 deficiency induces senescence in keratinocytes. However, in young mice, SOD2 deficiency increases cell differentiation and wound closure. Interestingly, cellular senescence leads to decreased cellular proliferation but increases epidermal differentiation [58]. The overall findings support the antagonistic pleiotropy of Sod2 depletion in murine epidermal cells; SOD2 deficiency can be beneficial at early age, but deleterious in aging mice [58]. The authors also used primary human keratinocytes treated with rotenone (a well-known inhibitor of Complex I of the ETC [59]) to further determine the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in aging in this model. Their findings show that, in addition to triggering mitochondrial dysfunction, rotenone decreases mRNA levels of the pro-proliferation genes cyclin A2 and B1, both crucial markers of the aging epidermis. Notably, rotenone treatment upregulates differentiation-associated genes (CD36, KLF9, SL100A3), further supporting the antagonistic pleiotropy of SOD2. In conclusion, mitochondrial dysfunction accelerates wound closure in young SOD2-deficient mice, but it decreases the number of dividing keratinocytes in aging animals [58].

Another group, using C. elegans, studied the effects of BAM15, a protonophore and uncoupler of the inner mitochondrial membrane, in the maintenance of the physiology of mechanosensory neurons [60]. Compared to other uncouplers, BAM15 has lower toxicity, reducing potential side effects on other signaling pathways [61]. The authors studied the effects of BAM15 on touch receptor neurons (TRNs), which are responsible for the gentle touch sensation along the body wall and the tail of C. elegans, in young and aging animals [60]. Nematodes treated with BAM15 suppress ROS generation, which is associated with delayed aging and preservation of TRNs function. Accordingly, nematodes treated with BAM15 show reduced structural defects in TRNs; as well as decreased axonal beading and swellings, abnormal dendritic branching, axonal breaks, and misplaced synaptic elements; compared to untreated nematodes. Consequently, visual, tactile, and mechanosensory signaling are preserved [60]. Using calibrated mechanical stimulation, the authors show that BAM15-treated nematodes exhibit larger withdrawal amplitudes and shorter response latencies, indicating stronger and faster mechanosensory responses than the controls. Lastly, they show that BAM15 treatment sustains neuronal volume, neuronal functional connectivity, and aging-related sensory processing, and it improves neural system functioning and lifespan [60].

Studies of genetic neurodegenerative disorders reveal more pathways involving mitochondrial physiology in decline. Cockayne Syndrome (CS) is caused by mutations in two genes (CSB and CSA), leading to DNA repair defects, and accelerated senescence and associated mitochondrial dysfunction (including dysregulated mitophagy [62]); increased photosensitivity is also present in CS [63,64]. Using C. elegans in which csb–1 was mutated (csb–1 is the ortholog of the human CSB), Lopes et al., show that csb–1 worms have progressive neurodegeneration. This ultimately results in reduced sensitivity to gentle mechanosensory stimuli in the mutant nematodes. Loss of csb–1 is also associated with decreased mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates (OCR), and hyperfused mitochondrial network, compared to the control group [63]. Moreover, the authors show that these effects in the mutants are enhanced when UV-induced DNA damage is present. Specifically, UV radiation accelerates age-dependent loss of touch sensitivity; and increases mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal degeneration [63]. Interestingly, since dysregulated mitophagy has also been described in CS [62], molecules that stimulate this mitochondrial quality-control mechanism have demonstrated positive effects against CS. For instance, NAD+ precursors are a notable example of this [65].

The role of dysregulated mtDNA in skin aging has also been studied. Singh et al., using a conditional knock-in mouse model for the dominant-negative mutant of the polymerase gamma 1 (POLG, the only DNA polymerase acting in the mtDNA [66]), show that this mutation is linked to decreased overall body weight and length, and hair loss. As expected, mutant mice also show severe mtDNA depletion and mitochondrial dysfunction, compared to the control animals. For example, the activity of all OXPHOS components is significantly reduced, and mitochondrial structure is impaired, including cristae [52]. This mutation is also associated with prominent aging-related skin manifestations, including wrinkling and hair loss (alopecia), and excessive epidermis thickening [52]. Moreover, mtDNA depletion triggers an inflammatory response leading to the accumulation of inflammatory cells in the skin, a major factor in skin aging and pathologies. The authors also describe mitochondrial dysfunction -mediated upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases, which ultimately leads to increased collagen degradation and skin wrinkles. Aging-related markers are also altered in the mutated mice, such as reduced levels of the anti-aging protein Klotho, elevated levels of IGF–1R, and over-expression of VEGF (which is associated with aging-related vascular dysfunction in the skin [67]). Lastly, the authors found infiltrating macrophages and histiocytes in the dermis and epidermis of the POLG knock-in mouse, ultimately contributing to the skin lesions. All these phenotypes were reversed when POLG mtDNA is rescued [52].

Further studies on the role of POLG in aging skin and mitochondrial dysfunction were conducted by Ahmed et al. The authors use a mouse model in which mtDNA is ubiquitously depleted by the inducible expression of the dominant-negative POLG [50,52]. mtDNA-depleter mice show early signs of aging, including hair loss and wrinkles; along with sebaceous gland dysfunction manifesting as skin xerosis, yellow umbilicated papules (resembling human sebaceous gland hyperplasia), scaly rash, and flaky itchy skin (similar to seborrheic dermatitis in aging); compared to control animals [50]. Histologically, mutant mice show peri-adnexal (surrounding hair follicles) inflammation and destruction of sebaceous glands. The authors conclude that the main morphologic and histopathologic alterations in the sebaceous glands result from inflammatory changes accompanied by abnormal lipid accumulation in sebocytes, skin surface with insufficient sebum accumulation, and upregulation of androgen receptors and lipogenesis-regulating genes [50]. Accordingly, tissues physiologically enriched with large sebocytes, such as the mouse preputial gland, show a significant upregulation of inflammatory mediator genes, including IL–6, TNF-α, and COX2 in the mtDNA-depleter mice, compared to the control animals [50].

The same study shows significantly increased levels of cytoplasmic lipofuscin (which is a classical biomarker of senescence) in sebocytes from the mtDNA-depleter mice, compared to the control mice. Increased cytoplasmic lipofuscin was accompanied by distension of sebaceous glands that are filled with large lipid droplets; and reduced skin surface lipid, creating roughness, xerosis and scaliness; all of which are aging skin features [50]. In skin physiology, sebaceous gland homeostasis depends on a regulated balance between progenitor cell proliferation and sebocyte differentiation [68]. However, persistent hyperproliferation is triggered by an mitochondrial dysfunction -mediated shift towards cellular senescence, which ultimately depletes stem cell reserves [69]. Accordingly, the authors show that sebaceous glands from mtDNA-depleter mice initially exhibit significantly increased expression of stem cell markers; but these markers shrink over time, reducing the regenerative capacity and leading to emergent aging skin phenotypes [50].

SIGHT

In recent years, the concept of “eyes as windows to the brain” has been explored by multiple researchers [70,71]. The close connection between sight and the central nervous system is supported by multiple factors, including the fact that the retina is a direct extension of the central nervous system, which was first described by the Nobel Laureate Ramón y Cajal in the 19th century. Moreover, many neurodegenerative disorders, including AD and PD [72,73], also manifest with effects in the retina. A decline in vision, including broad symptoms, is commonly associated with aging [74,75]. Moreover, symptoms such as cloudiness, distortions, and blindness are not only present in regular aging, but they also often appear in neurodegenerative diseases [76]. Specifically, two of the most common neurodegenerative diseases in which impaired vision is present are age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and glaucoma [77,78] (Fig. 2). Several authors have shown an important role for mitochondrial dysfunction, including increased mtDNA damage and ROS generation [79,80], in the onset and progression of both diseases.

AMD is characterized by damage to the retinal pigment epithelium cells (RPE), a layer of cells underneath the macula that provides nutrients to the photoreceptor cells within the macula [81]. AMD can be classified into two types: wet and dry [80]. Wet AMD involves the formation of aberrant blood vessels in the RPE and has multiple known effective treatments, mostly involving blocking or sealing these vessels [82–84]. Dry AMD is the more common form of AMD, and it is the leading cause of irreversible blindness in developed countries [85]. It involves the degeneration of the RPE, which leads to the death of photoreceptor cells [86]. Currently, the pharmacological options for dry AMD are very limited, mostly due to a lack of understanding regarding the molecular mechanisms that underlie this pathology. However, as detailed below, multiple studies have found mechanistic connections between dry AMD and mitochondrial dysfunction.

The macula has a high potential for exhibit increased levels of oxidative stress due to its high metabolic rate, high oxygen concentration, and its continuous ROS output into the surrounding area [87]. Moreover, AMD RPE are both more sensitive and less able to detoxify this ROS. For example, Golestaneh et al., using iPSCs from AMD patients and sex- and age-matched controls differentiated into RPE cells, demonstrate that treatment with H2O2 significantly increases ROS generation and associated cell death to a higher magnitude in the AMD RPE cells, than in the control samples [87]. Additionally, AMD RPE cells show decreased SOD2 levels. Other proteins that are classically involved in the regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense, such as Sirt1 and PGC–1α [88], are also decreased in AMD RPE, both under control conditions and after treatment with H2O2, compared to the control conditions [87]. In the same study, increased ATP levels are present in AMD RPE cells under basal conditions, compared to the control samples. However, the authors show that these increased ATP levels are due to increased glycolysis, not aerobic respiration [87].

In both aging and AMD RPE, increased ROS generation has been associated with increased mtDNA mutations. For example, Karunadharma et al., used qPCR to show that primary human AMD RPE have more widespread and severe mtDNA damage than RPE obtained from age-matched controls [89]. Other authors demonstrate that such damage is manifested as mtDNA deletions commonly present in region III of the mtDNA, in control RPE [90–92]. However, AMD RPE achibit damage across all mtDNA regions. Overall, mtDNA is about eight times more damaged than nuclear DNA in the same cells, and AMD RPE show 14% more deletions than age-matched controls [89]. Further studies found that AMD RPE harbor mutations in regions that include loci coding for various tRNAs; and proteins involved in ATP production, including NADH dehydrogenase subunits and cytochrome B [93].

Further studies examined the intersection between aging, AMD, and mitochondrial dysfunction. For example, Feher et al., using RPE isolated from a two-year-old individual, show that mitochondria are numerous and bacillus-shaped, orientated perpendicular to the macula, and contain well-preserved cristae. However, in RPE isolated from aging individuals, mitochondrial number is decreased, and their shape and orientation are mixed, even though they generally still show healthy cristae [80]. RPE obtained from AMD and aging individuals show similar levels of lipofuscin. Moreover, comparison between AMD RPE with age-matched controls reveals that in AMD, RPE have significantly decreased mitochondrial number and area. Critically, a significant number of AMD RPE mitochondria show damaged or completely absent cristae, compared to aged-matched controls. Lastly, the authors show that AMD RPE have significantly increased peroxisomes, also compared to age-matched controls, which suggests the presence of a compensatory mechanism aimed at counteracting higher ROS generation [80]. To further explore the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in AMD, Fisher et al., used proteomics to show decreased levels of PINK1 and mitophagy, in AMD RPE, compared with age-matched controls. After treatment with FCCP, which is a well-known mitochondrial uncoupler, AMD RPE show significantly increased levels of the fission protein MFF, and decreased levels of the fusion proteins Mfn2 and OPA1-L, as compared to age-matched control samples [86]. These results suggest that AMD RPE are more sensitive to stress, and have an impaired ability to efficiently remove stress-induced damaged mitochondria.

Urolithin A (UA) is a dietary antioxidant with neuroprotective effects in models of aging [94–96], and neurodegenerative disorders [97]. Jimenez-Loygorri et al., evaluated these neuroprotective effects in AMD, using in vivo and in vitro approaches. Specifically, they created a mouse model of AMD by injecting the animals with 50 mg/kg of sodium iodate, Control animals were treated with a placebo, while UA was administered to the AMD model three days prior to the sodium iodide injection. The authors conducted visual function studies in these animals, including dark, mixed, light, and electroretinogram oscillatory (light/dark) and flicker (strobe). Interestingly, visual function is preserved in UA-treated AMD mice, despite the retinal degradation produced by the sodium iodide injections [81]. In addition, UA treatment also restores proteostasis, and lysosomal homeostasis; and it reduces inflammation in human-derived ARPE–19 cells treated with sodium iodate; all of which are biomarkers of AMD progression. In the same cells, sequential KD of PINK1, PINK2, UBE2QL1, and ATG5 shows that the protective effects of UA in this AMD model are exerted via modulation of p62-dependent lysophagy. Altogether, these results show that UA could be a valid pharmacological tool in AMD [81].

Glaucoma is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), cupping, and atrophy of the optic nerve head [98]. It is the second most common cause of moderate and severe vision impairment [99]. Increased retinal ganglion cells (RGC) death, which is a hallmark of glaucoma, is linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, including mtDNA damage [100,101]. However, most genetic studies that analyze mtDNA have been conducted using peripheral blood samples. This poses a limitation to addressing somatic mitochondrial mutations in disease-specific tissues relevant to glaucoma, which may have been overlooked in blood. To override this limitation, Vallabh et al., used both peripheral blood leukocytes and Tenon’s ocular fibroblasts from glaucoma patients and age- and sex-matched control individuals to conduct parallel sequencing of mtDNA [79]. The authors show increased homoplasmic mtDNA variants in the leukocytes from glaucoma patients. However, increased amounts of heteroplasmic mtDNA variants were found in fibroblasts from the same patients [79]. Homoplasmic and heteroplasmic mtDNA variants have been associated with different types of mitochondrial-associated neurodegenerative disorders in the field of vision. For example, the homoplasmic variants have been found in Leber’s optic neuropathy, while the heteroplasmic variants are present in MELAS [102]. Increased potentially pathogenic mtDNA variants were also identified in fibroblasts obtained from the glaucoma patients, compared to the control individuals, but no significant changes were found in the leukocytes [79].

One of the molecular mechanisms underlying elevated IOP-mediated damage to the RGC is the age-dependent decline of levels [103]. NAD is critically involved in maintaining energy homeostasis [104]. A recent study conducted using a well-known mouse model of glaucoma (DBA/2 J) investigated the effects of variations in NAD levels, and the effects of nicotinamide (NAM, a precursor of NAD [104]) on the Wallerian degeneration slow allele (WLDs 103). WLDs is a fusion protein that improves viability and mitochondrial function by contributing to the production of NAD [105]. Williams et al., show that WLDs and NAM increase retinal levels of NAD, concomitantly restricting optic nerve soma degeneration, thereby consistent with RGC protection [103]. This combination of a partially active enzyme and an NAD precursor could be a valid pharmacological strategy in glaucoma. Another study conducted by the same authors further demonstrates the potential therapeutic role of NAD in glaucoma. Specifically, using DBA/2 J mice orally supplemented with NAM, the authors show that this supplementation not only prevents the glaucoma-associated phenotypic changes in RGC but also reverses these changes in glaucoma mice. For example, increased NAD reverses dysregulated mitochondrial gene expression, and restores axonal transport and mitochondrial morphology to pre-glaucoma levels in mice [106].

Zeng et al., used the oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion in vitro model, and the elevated IOP (via saline injection) in vivo model, to determine the effects of mitochondrial dysfunction on RGC degeneration in glaucoma. The in vitro model also shows decreased mitochondrial membrane potential; and increased ROS generation, and phosphorylation of Drp1 and Erk1/2 in the glaucoma model, compared to the control samples [107]. In the in vivo model, the authors describe decreased RGC number, and increased activation of the Erk1/2-Drp1 pathway. Inhibition of this pathway resulted in partial recovery of RGC. The authors also demonstrate that Drp1 initiates PANoptosis in this model, a process characterized by the simultaneous activation of apoptotic, necroptotic, and pyroptotic pathways. Activation of PANoptosis-related proteins leads to quicker degeneration of RGC, while pharmacological inhibition of Drp1 has protective effects by mitigating the elevated IOP-mediated PANoptosis [107].

Further studies using glaucoma models were conducted to determine whether antioxidant capacity and mitochondrial efficiency are linked to resistance in optic nerve degeneration. For example, Lascaratos et al., demonstrate elevated levels of mitochondrial complexes I, II, and IV, and elevated levels of ADP phosphorylation in lymphocytes isolated from peripheral blood, and urine samples from patients resistant to glaucomatous neuropathy, compared to control individuals. These effects were observed even after years of exposure to elevated IOP. The authors also show mitochondrial hyperpolarization and increased mtDNA levels in patients who have not developed glaucoma, even after elevated IOP. Mitochondria in these patients can also better counteract increased oxidative stress and cytosolic calcium overload. Lastly, the authors report significantly elevated TOM20 levels in ocular hypertensive individuals, which aligns with the observed increased ADP phosphorylation [108]. These findings were expanded by Baltan et al., using a mouse model of glaucoma induced by elevated IOP, in both old and young animals. The authors describe reduced axonal population (especially small-to-medium-sized axons) and ATP levels in the optic nerve of mice with elevated IOP, even though decreased ATP levels appear to be aging-related in this model [109]. These findings raise some questions regarding whether changes in ATP levels are disease-specific or related to neuronal senescence.

Affected vision has also been described in other neurodegenerative disorders, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [110–112]. ALS is a highly aggressive neurodegenerative disease that affects upper motor neurons in the motor cortex, and lower motor neurons in the spinal cord and brainstem. In most ALS cases, pathogenic mutations in the genes TARDBP, SOD1, FUS, and C9ORF72 link mitochondrial dysfunction to disease progression [113–116]. Interestingly, the ALS-associated TDP43M337V mutation affects the retina in a sex-specific pattern [110]. The authors show that three to four-month-old female mice have decreased retinal ERG signaling, Mfn2, and ATP5A levels. Male mice show no changes in these proteins [110]. The authors hypothesize that the sex-specific pattern could be mediated by hormonal differences. In fact, estrogen stimulates gene expression, contributing to ocular inflammation in dry eye disorders [117,118].

Regular aging has also been linked to vision loss. While the exact molecular mechanisms of this process are complex, the role of XBP1, a key regulator of the unfolded protein response, has already been proposed [119]. This response, which is closely related to the mitochondrial unfolded protein response [19], is a crucial mechanism, mainly orchestrated by the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), to maintain proper protein homeostasis under cellular stress [120]. XBP1 is also involved in the regulation of neuronal function and longevity, linking prolonged ER stress and aging [121–123]. Using a retinal XPB1 KO (knock-out) mouse model, Mclaughlin et al. show that aging-related retinal degeneration occurs significantly earlier in these mice than in Wt controls [119]. Moreover, the authors show that XBP1 loss accelerates aging-related retinal degeneration, through dysfunctional glycolysis and increased microglial activation [119].

As previously mentioned, increased mtDNA mutations have also been associated with aging [124]. Moreover, mtDNA mutations affecting subunits of complex I of the ETC have been strongly associated with optic neuropathies and progressive visual impairment [125]. These mutations can disrupt OXPHOS, leading to reduced ATP generation and increased ROS in the RGC, leading to increased apoptosis, which contributes to optic nerve degeneration and associated vision loss [125]. RGC are among the most metabolically active neurons in the body [126], they are especially sensitive to mitochondrial dysfunction.

Furthermore, Wang et al., examined the impact of aging on mitochondrial quality and cellular communication in the zebrafish retina. The authors show that the aging zebrafish retina is accompanied by decreased mtDNA stability and abundance [127]. Moreover, some genes involved in the regulation of mitochondrial fusion (MFN2 and OPA1), fission (FIS1), and mitophagy/autophagy (PINK1, LC3B) decrease with aging; whereas the Akt/mTOR pathway and inflammatory markers were overexpressed in the same samples, as compared to the control samples. These results suggest increased mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated aging-related signaling during retinal aging [127]. In the same study, the authors show that resveratrol, a widely used drug in cellular models to prevent and/or delay senescence [24], re-establishes mtDNA copy number and quality [127]. Moreover, the expression of mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy genes is simultaneously increased, inhibiting the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Resveratrol treatment also activates the AMPK/Sirt1/PGC–1α pathway, which is linked to improved metabolic health and mitochondrial biogenesis [127]. These findings suggest that resveratrol may be a valid pharmacological target against mitochondrial dysfunction-mediated aging-related retinal degeneration.

HEARING

Aging-related hearing loss (ARHL), also known as presbycusis, affects approximately 25% of the population worldwide over the age of 60. It is also one of the most common chronic conditions in adults over the age of 65 in the U.S. [128]. ARHL impacts the ability to perceive high-frequency sounds, with decreased ability to perceive lower frequencies following thereafter [129,130]. ARHL arises from the dysfunction of the peripheral (the outer, middle, and inner ear), and central auditory systems (the cochlear nerve, brainstem nuclei, and the auditory cortex) [131,132]. ARHL has a negative impact on the quality of life of patients, including social isolation, a higher risk of dementia, anxiety, and depression [129,133]. While the exact molecular mechanisms that underlie ARHL remain incompletely understood, it is known that it can be caused by intrinsic factors, such as genetic predisposition, and mutations; and extrinsic factors, such as smoking, use of ototoxic medication, and exposure to high-intensity sound [134,135]. Mitochondrial dysfunction has also been proposed in the etiopathology of ARHL. For example, Rivas-Chacon et al. demonstrate that H2O2-associated increased oxidative stress induces premature senescence in auditory cell lines (HEI-OC1 -from house ear institute-organ of Corti 1, OC-k3 -from organ of Corti, and SV-k1 -from stria vascularis), leading to ARHL [130]. Moreover, Chen et al., show that acoustic trauma reduces mtDNA content, mitochondrial gene expression, and ATP and TFAM protein levels in rat cochleae [136]. To further demonstrate the important role of mitochondrial physiology in loud noise-induced ARHL, the authors show that treatment with mitoTEMPOL reduces cochlear degeneration and reverses mitochondrial dysfunction in their model [136].

Other groups have also conducted research on the intersection between mitochondrial dysfunction, ARHL, and aging, using HEI-OC1, OC-k3, and SV-k1 cells⋅H2O2-treated auditory cells exhibit an increased number of mitochondria, ROS generation, oxidative stress, and Foxo3 levels (Foxo3 plays a regulatory role in apoptosis and oxidative stress resistance, as well as in longevity [137–139]), compared to control samples. Increased senescence is also present in these cells. The authors attribute the increased number of mitochondria to the onset of senescence and in response to continuous mtDNA damage [140]. In fact, H2O2-induced senescence was detected at shorter times in HEI-OC1 and OC-k3 cells, compared to the SV-k1 cells [130], and HEI-OC1 cells exhibit increased cell duplication rate nine days post-treatment at all H2O2 concentrations, which is not observed in OC-k3 and SV-k1 cells. Interestingly, H2O2-associated increased ROS is of lower magnitude in SV-k1 cells. Moreover, although increased oxidative stress is present in the three cell lines, HEI-OC1 and OC-k3 cells require higher H2O2 concentrations to observe this effect than SV-k1 cells. In the case of Foxo3 levels, H2O2-induced changes are greater at higher concentrations in HEI-OC1 and OC-k3 cells than in SV-k1 cells. Finally, although increased H2O2 concentration over time resulted in decreased cell viability in all three cell lines, this effect is more pronounced in HEI-OC1 cells, especially at lower H2O2 concentrations. The authors suggest that these difference between the cell lines could be due to their origins [140].

Lin et al., established an H2O2-induced senescence model in HEI-OC1 cells to determine the role of Drp1-mediated fission and mitophagy on this model. Their results show that senescent cells exhibit reduced Drp1 expression, leading to decreased mitophagy, increased mitochondrial dysfunction, and decreased mtDNA copy number [141]. These findings were further expanded by examining mitochondrial co-localization of GFP-LC3 and MitoTracker Red. Their results show suppressed mitophagosome formation under oxidative stress conditions, similar to what is observed in aging. HEI-OC1 cells were also treated with rapamycin, a drug that induces mitophagy by inhibiting mTOR [142]. The authors found that pretreatment with rapamycin followed by H2O2 stress preserves mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP content, although mtDNA copy number remains decreased, compared to H2O2 treatment alone. Silencing Drp1 in the same model blocks mitophagy, which leads to increased presence of senescent cells, depolarized mitochondrial membrane, and decreased ATP generation. However, it does not significantly alter mtDNA copy number (likely due to decreased Drp1-mediated mitophagy). In contrast, Drp1 overexpression preserved mitochondrial function. Consistent with the in vitro findings, aged cochlear explants also show decreased Drp1 expression and mitochondrial dysfunction after H2O2 treatment. Lastly, pretreatment with Mdivi–1 (a pharmacological inhibitor of Drp1 [143]) in H2O2-exposed cochlear explants further suppresses mitophagy and exacerbates cellular senescence, and loss [136]. Overall, the authors conclude that aging-related Drp1 decline is associated with cochlear senescence via increased mitochondrial dysfunction [141].

To further investigate the role of mitochondrial dysfunction on ARHL, Zhang et al., studied the role of OPA1 and Sirt3 in this process. Their results show that increased OPA1 acetylation and ARHL are modulated by Sirt3 activity [144]. Specifically, using aged and young murine cochlear hair cells, the authors show increased OMA1-mediated cleaved and acetylated OPA1, which leads to increased ROS generation and reduced ATP synthesis, in the older animals [144]. Consequently, aged hair cells show increased mtDNA damage, and decreased mitophagy efficiency, compared with control samples, ultimately increasing their sensitivity to aging-related stress, and impaired associated-ribbon synapses with spiral ganglion neurons. Parallel studies conducted using D-galactose-treated (D-gal-treated) auditory cultured cells and the cochlea of aged mice with a mutated SUMOylation site in Sirt3 show that OPA1 suppression further aggravates mitochondrial dysfunction, and accelerates cellular aging. Sirt3 is a known regulator of OPA1 [145]. Accordingly, pharmacological or genetic activation of Sirt3 in both aged mouse hair cells and D-gal-treated cultured cells restores mitochondrial function by decreasing OPA1 acetylation. In the mouse model, enhancing Sirt3 activity also preserves hair cells, spiral ganglion neuronal structure, and hearing sensitivity. Lastly, the authors show that OPA1-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction triggers pyroptosis-related inflammation, which supports a connection between inflammation and cochlear aging. Overall, these results place the Sirt3/OPA1 pathway as a promising therapeutic target that could attenuate mitochondrial dysfunction and, therefore, ARHL [144].

The peripheral auditory system includes the cochlear basilar membrane (CBM) and inner hair cells. Together, they convert vibrations from sound waves into electrical signals, which are then transmitted to the central auditory system [146,147]. Structural changes, specifically the loss of hair cells in the CBM, are associated with ARHL [148]. Guo et al., administered different doses of D-gal to CBM isolated from the cochlea of C57BL/6 mice. The authors describe a dose-dependent increased in mitochondrial ROS generation in the CBM of all D-gal-treated tissue [149]. ACBM treated with higher doses of D-gal also show increased accumulation of mtDNA deletions, and apoptosis; and decreased ATP levels and mitochondrial membrane potential, compared to the control group and the other groups treated with lower doses of D-gal [149]. As the inner hair cells rely on mitochondrial ATP to release synaptic vesicles at their ribbon synapses with spiral ganglion neurons, decreased ATP levels in the CBM are associated with dysfunctional transportation of these synaptic vesicles, ultimately affecting the transmission of the signal to the central auditory system [150].

The stria vascularis is the cochlear tissue that produces endolymph, which is crucial for hearing [151]. It is composed, among other components, of marginal cells (MC), which are rich in mitochondria, and, accordingly, ATPases [151]. MC can be damaged by oxidative stress via ROS-mediated upregulation of the PARP1/PAR pathway, which ultimately increases neuronal apoptosis through both the classical caspase-dependent pathway, and a specifically PARP1-dependent pathway (parthanatos) [152]. Under physiological conditions, PARP1 responds to oxidative stress by producing PAR polymers and repairing damaged mtDNA. However, when oxidative stress causes severe DNA damage, PARP1 becomes hyperactivated, resulting in the accumulation of PAR polymers, ultimately leading to parthanatos [153]. Parthanatos is present in different neurodegenerative diseases, such as PD, AD, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [154]; but its relationship with ARHL remains much less explored. Wan et al., using young and old Sprague-Dawley rats show that older animals have a higher auditory brainstem response (ABR), which is consistent with high-frequency hearing loss. After the ABR test, cochlear tissue was dissected; aged rats have significantly increased levels of parthanatos-related proteins and increased oxidative stress in this tissue, compared to control samples.

The authors cultured MC and treated them with glucose oxidase for 24 h to mimic increased oxidative stress, mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, and ultimately, apoptosis. These results were corroborated by using veliparib, a PARP1 inhibitor, in glucose oxidase-treated rats [155]. Veliparib reverses decreased cell viability in MC from older animals, thus supporting the idea that increased MC cell death results from the overactivation of PARP1, which is absent in younger rats [156]. Interestingly, calcium-activated cysteine proteases, such as calpains, have been shown to mediate the release of the apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF), a mediator of parthanathos. Under physiological conditions, AIF gets truncated when released, and it translocates from mitochondria to the nucleus, inducing DNA damage and chromatin condensation [157]. The authors found decreased calpain activity in the glucose oxidase-treated cultured MC. However, in the absence of glucose oxidase treatment, MC show decreased truncation and translocation of AIF. Lastly, in MC obtained from the same animals, the authors show that both calpain activation and mPTP opening are calcium-dependent. In conclusion, the authors demonstrate that the PARP1-dependent pathway plays a key role in the progression of presbycusis, revealing a new pharmacological target [156].

Another study on the intersection between ARHL and mitochondrial physiology was conducted by Cho et al. The authors show that treating C57BL/6 J mice homozygous for the Cdh23Ahl allele, which is involved in ARHL, with UA has positive effects on mitophagy-mediated prevention of ARHL [158]. In fact, strategies aimed at modulating mitophagy, such as treatment with NAD+, have been described in ARHL [36,159, 160]. In this case, ABR shows significantly reduced hearing thresholds in UA-treated mice, compared to the untreated controls, at eight and ten months of age, that is, when ARHL begins in this model. The reduced hearing threshold indicates that UA-treated mice require a softer sound to elicit a brainstem response, compared to the untreated mice, thus demonstrating improved hearing. Additionally, the study also shows that mtDNA integrity and ATP production are significantly higher in the UA-treated group, in both the cochlea and auditory cortex [158]. Moreover, UA-treated mice show significant upregulation of mitophagy-related RNAs and proteins (such as PINK1, Parkin, BNIP3, and LC3B) and ETC complexes in both the cochleae and the auditory cortex [158]. Expression of TOM20, LC3B, and LAMP1 is also significantly increased in the UA-treated group in hair cells, spiral ganglion, inferior colliculus, and auditory cortex [158]. Together, these results demonstrate improved mitophagy in both the peripheral and central auditory systems of mice treated with UA, compared with the control animals.

Moreover, Du et al., show that treatment with apocynin, (a NADPH oxidase inhibitor) on D-gal-treated rats reduces aging symptoms [131]. Their results also show that ventral cochlear nucleus cells (VCN) from apocynin-D-gal-treated rats have decreased H2O2 generation, and increased antioxidant activity (SOD and GSH-Px), compared to the D-gal-treated rats [131]. Accordingly, the apocynin-D-gal-treated rats show decreased mtDNA oxidative damage within the VCN, decreased accumulation of common deletions in the mtDNA, and apoptosis, compared to the D-gal-treated rats. The authors concluded that NADPH oxidase activity influences oxidative stress and associated aging-related mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis in the VCN, ultimately modulating ARHL [131].

Mutations in genes coding for mitochondrial proteins have also been associated with ARHL. For example, loss of isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2), which is an enzyme crucial for providing NADPH to major mitochondrial antioxidants [161], results in accelerated loss of spiral ganglion neurons and hair cells in the cochlea of male mice, and associated ARHL [162]. The same authors show that IDH2 KD HEI-OC1 cells have reduced cell viability, and NADPH and thioredoxin reductase 2 activity [162]. Another group of researchers describe that treatment with MitoQ, a potent mitochondrially targeted antioxidant [18], prevents hair cell loss in H2O2-induced ototoxicity in mouse cochlear explants from IDH2 KO mice [163]. Moreover, as mentioned, mtDNA mutations are linked to ARHL. However, the mechanistic relationship between these two processes remains incompletely understood.

Kim et al., investigated the relationship between mtDNA mutations, and the onset of ARHL in mice [164]. They used two strains of CBA/CaJ mice, a well-known model of late-onset hearing loss: Polg+/+ (control group, referred to as Wt), and Polgmut/mut (mutated POLG). The authors quantified mtDNA point mutations and deletions in five-month-old and 17-month-old Wt and Polgmut/mut male mice. Both five-month-old and 17-month-old Polgmut/mut mice show increased mtDNA point mutations, compared to age-matched Wt mice. No significant differences in mtDNA point mutations are present between five-month-old and 17-month-old Wt mice, and five-month-old and 17-month-old Polgmut/mut.

To further characterize ARHL in this model, the authors used the ABR test, a well-established method for assessing hearing in the peripheral and central auditory pathways [165]. Their results show that both male and female 17-month-old Polgmut/mut mice have increased hearing thresholds, compared to five-month-old Polgmut/mut mice, and 17-month-old Wt mice. ABR wave I latencies, which were measured to assess the auditory nerve, show that 17-month-old male Polgmut/mut mice have increased latencies compared to five-month-old Polgmut/mut mice and Wt mice, which suggests the presence of neural processing deficits [164]. Compound action potential (CAP) amplitudes and thresholds were also assessed to evaluate suprathreshold neural activity. These experiments allowed the authors to evaluate the overall strength of the auditory nerve response, and to determine whether neural output differs [166,167]. Both male and female 17-month-old Polgmut/mut mice show decreased CAP amplitudes at mid-to-high frequencies, compared to the age-matched Wt mice. Decreased CAP amplitudes are associated with diminished peripheral neuronal output, and consequently, reduced sound-evoked activity transmitted from the cochlea to the auditory system. Thus, despite the loud stimuli, 17-month-old Polgmut/mut mice are unable to generate a physiological neural response. ABR and CAP findings were corroborated by counting spinal ganglion neurons in the cochlea and conducting cochleograms in mice from all groups [164]. The findings from this manuscript show the crucial role of POLG in the onset and severity of ARHL, as well as the presence of aging-related structural differences in the inner ear, although inner hair cell function is not significantly affected [164].

Based on this work, Kim et al., suggest that the lack of significant difference between different age groups in their study may be due to mtDNA mutations that were introduced to the maternal germline, since a Polgmut/+ cross was used, and the inherited mutations are present in both Polgmut/mut and Polg+/+ mice [164]. Moreover, Farhadi et al., show that reduced mitochondrial gene expression and variations in the mtDNA control region are associated with the onset of ARHL [134]. Specifically, the mtDNA control regions from ARHL patients harbor 113 distinct variants, with all patients carrying at least one variant, and 84% of mutations located in hypervariable regions. Although many variants were shared between ARHL patients and control individuals, four specific variants were more common in ARHL patients. The authors propose that these variants are likely pathogenic, and they serve as biomarkers of ARHL risk. The authors also note that these four mtDNA control mutations in ARHL occur near well-characterized regulatory elements in the mitochondrial control regions, which coordinate key steps of mtDNA replication and transcription. Sequence changes in the vicinity of these regions could affect mtDNA quality and associated gene expression, providing a possible link between these variants and mitochondrial dysfunction previously reported in ARHL [134].

Studies in aging cochlear hair cells show that activation of FOXG1 gene expression protects cells via a pathway in which increased autophagy and mitophagy are involved. This ultimately reduces oxidative stress, preserves mtDNA integrity, and prevents exacerbated cell death. Specifically, He et al., show that in both cellular and animal models of senescence, FOXG1 expression initially rises its levels when acute stress is present, suggesting a protective response. However, during chronic stress, such as in senescence, it declines, which is associated with increased mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis. FOXG1 KD is also associated with increased mtDNA deletion frequency, decreased mtDNA copy number, and mitochondrial depolarization. In this model, acetylsalicylic acid restores autophagy, reduces ROS accumulation and, ultimately, preserves mtDNA integrity. Accordingly, increasing FOXG1 could help to prevent ARHL progression [168].

Genetic studies in the field expanded further than mtDNA. For example, Chen et al., addressed the role of Cisd2 (a protein localized in both the ER and mitochondria [169], and it is involved in the regulation of calcium homeostasis, including interaction with Bcl–2 [170]) in ARHL [133]. Previous studies have shown that Cisd2 KD in mice is associated with ARHL [171]. In this study, the authors isolated RNA from white blood cells obtained from aged ARHL patients and young controls without ARHL. They found decreased Cisd2 expression in ARHL patients compared with control individuals. Using a Cisd2 KO mouse model, the authors also show that aging progression is associated with increased ABR threshold. Accordingly, impaired hearing is present in the mutant mice at middle and old age, compared to control mice, while no significant differences are present between the mutant and the control, young mice. TUNEL assays in the organ of Corti, spiral ligament, and spiral ganglion tissue show increased apoptosis in the Cisd2 KO mice, compared to the control animals. Moreover, the authors describe impaired axons of auditory nerves in the cochlea, decreased OCR, increased glycolysis and lactate generation, downregulation of antioxidant proteins, and decreased overall ATP production in the mutant mice, compared to control animals. These results clearly link Cisd2 with ARHL and mitochondrial dysfunction. Lastly, using Cisd2 KO HEI-OC1 cells, the authors show decreased cell viability and significant morphological changes, suggesting an important role for Cisd2 in cell survival in the organ of Corti [133].

Boucher et al., show that a substantial number of ARHL cases are caused by rare, single-gene mutations [172]. The authors performed whole-exome genome sequencing and identified ultrarare pathogenic variants in genes responsible for autosomal dominant early-onset deafness in 25.7% of familial ARHL and 22.7% of sporadic ARHL, compared to 7.5% of controls. For example, mutations in the myosin encoding gene (MYO6, MYO7A) are present in 8.9% of ARHL cases but in < 1% of the controls. The authors also detected mtDNA mutations in ARHL cases. For example, m.1555 A>G, which is known to cause non-syndromic deafness and make individuals susceptible to aminoglycoside antibiotic toxicity [173]; and m.7444 G>A, which is associated with myopathy-related deafness [174]. In one familial case of ARHL, both and mtDNA mutation and an ultrarare nuclear mutation in the ATP-dependent myosin encoding gene MYO6 were detected. This suggests a combined pathogenic effect, where mitochondrial dysfunction may exacerbate the effects of nuclear mutations, especially considering that myosin requires substantial ATP levels to exert its effects [175].

Further genetic studies show that single nucleotide polymorphisms and insertion can be associated with ARHL onset. For example, Han et al., using the A/J mice with a mutation in the Cdh23 gene, which is associated with the onset of ARHL, show that decreased citrate synthase leads to increased apoptosis in cochlear cells. This increased apoptosis could be mediated by increased ROS generation and decreased ATP production, ultimately inducing degeneration and loss of hair cells, spiral ganglion neurons, and stria vascularis, leading to ARHL. The postnatal accumulation of apoptosis-related mRNA from caspase 3 and 9, suggests inner ear apoptosis is present since birth in these mice [176].

Using computational approaches, Ma et al., created a protein-protein interaction network including 10 connected genes. Among them, Aco2, Bcs1l, and Ndufs1 are identified as hub genes associated with both mitochondria and ARHL; they are significantly downregulated in ARHL. After the bioinformatics analysis, the authors validated their results using HEI-OC1 cells, under control and D-gal-induced senescence. While the results from these studies are mostly descriptive, they indicate the genetic components of the interaction between ARHL and mitochondrial dysfunction, which may be used by other researchers to develop therapeutic approaches in this field.

Smell

The reduction of the sense of smell, also known as hyposmia, is a common sensory deficit that occurs during physiological aging, and that can be exacerbated by aging-related neurodegenerative diseases [177]. In fact, olfactory dysfunction occurs in the early stages of the most common neurodegenerative pathologies, often preceding the onset of other symptoms [177]. Different from other sensory systems, adult neurogenesis is necessary to maintain optimal olfaction [178]. Accordingly, the olfactory bulb (OB) is one of the few areas of the brain where neurogenesis is maintained in adults [179]. Specifically, sensory neurons in the OB undergo continuous turnover and are replaced by neural progenitor cells (NPCs) from the subventricular zone, which differentiate into new mature OB neurons [180–182]. When NPCs differentiate, cellular metabolism increases due to the higher energetic demands of mature neurons [183]. Accordingly, these cells are extremely sensitive to mitochondrial dysfunction.

Hyposmia has been broadly described in many neurodegenerative disorders [184,185]. For example, Moralez-Martinez et al., using a mouse model of PD, show that increased αSynuclein (αSyn) aggregation and neurodegeneration are accompanied by decreased ipsilateral neuronal population in the OB. This decreased neuronal population is, at the same time, associated with increased oxidative damage and dysfunctional mitochondrial complex I, connecting mitochondrial dysfunction to hyposmia [186]. A similar study was conducted by Kalyn et al. In this case, MPTP (which is converted into MPP+, a metabolite that specifically targets Complex I of the ETC [187]), was injected into adult transgenic zebrafish to model PD. The authors found that dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the MPTP-injected group exhibit increased mitophagy and apoptosis, due to significantly increased mitochondrial fission [188]. Impaired olfactory response is present in the same zebrafish model. In another study, Pab et al., used the MitoPark mouse model of PD, which was generating by deleting mitochondrial TFAM in DA neurons, to investigate the effects of PD on the OB [189]. Their results show that depletion of DA neurons in the midbrain is followed by a reduction in the quantity of small anaxonic DA neurons in the OB [189]. Anaxonic cells are periglomerular interneurons that modulate odor input and undergo adult neurogenesis from NPCs [189,190]. The authors also show that the differentiation of NPCs into anaxonic neurons is not affected in the MitoPark mice; however, they detect more newly differentiated cells in the OB. This could be a partial compensatory neurogenic response, possibly resulting from increased vulnerability to mitochondrial dysfunction [189]. These findings suggest that olfactory dysfunction in PD may not only originate from midbrain degeneration, but also from mitochondrial dysfunction affecting local DA circuits in the OB [189].

Further studies in the field have been conducted. For example, Maftei et al., show that PD patients accumulate αSyn in the olfactory neurons during the early stages of the disease. This accumulation is similar in newly diagnosed patients and in patients under active treatment for PD [191]. The authors of the study also show that HSP60 (a mitochondrial protein involved in the maintenance of protein homeostasis [192]); and COXIV (a subunit of complex IV [193]) are distributed differently in olfactory neurons from control individuals and PD patients; as well as that αSyn accumulation deleteriously affects mitochondrial movement through the microtubules, ultimately contributing to increased death of the OB neurons [191]. These findings align with other studies showing that increased αSyn aggregation inhibits mitochondrial mobility [194,195].

AD patients can also develop impaired olfaction in the early stages of the disease. Accordingly, previous studies have shown that olfactory deficits are associated with AD-classical cognitive decline [196,197]. Interestingly, the status of the olfactory mucosa (OM) has been proposed as a biomarker for early AD diagnosis [183,198]. The OM includes epithelial cells and olfactory receptors that release signals directly to the brain cortex [198,199]. Specifically, hyposmia in AD patients can be caused by increased death of OB neurons due to oxidative damage to the DNA [177]. This damage is mediated by a well-described decline in base excision repair during aging [200], which is further exacerbated in AD [201]. NPCs that have migrated to the OB are more susceptible to DNA double-strand breaks, leading to increased neuronal cell death in newly differentiated cells [183].

Misiak et al., using the 3xTG AD mouse model of AD, show that olfaction in POLβ-deficient 3xTgAD mice is significantly more impaired than in control mice, POLβ-deficient C57BL/6 mice, and 3xTgAD mice [183]. The same authors found that the POLβ-deficient 3xTgAD has increased amounts of DNA double-strand breaks in the OB, which can be associated with increased neuronal apoptosis of neurons within the OB [183]. Using neurospheres grown from POLβ-absent neural potentiator cells, they also show that newly differentiated neurons and astrocytes have dysregulated energy metabolism and significantly increased ROS levels [183]. These findings demonstrate the importance of DNA repair mechanisms in the maintaining olfactory function and in mitigating oxidative stress, as well as the relationship between these two processes.

The study of transcriptomes from transgenic mice expressing human APOE3 (a control for AD [202]) and APOE4 (whose expression is associated with increased risk of AD [202]) further demonstrates a relationship between hyposmia and AD [203]. Specifically, 17 genes which are dysregulated in APOE4 mice, compared to APOE3, are odorant receptors. Interestingly, oxidoreductases, which are crucial for mitochondrial metabolism, are less abundant in APOE4 than in APOE3. This effect became more significant in advanced age, and it may also be associated with aging-related differences in glucose, lipid, and fatty acid metabolism. No changes in apoptosis levels in olfactory sensory neurons at six months old are present between APOE3 and APOE4; but apoptosis increases in the latter at 10 months old. This age coincides with the time at which most neuronal degeneration is observed in this AD model [203].

Further evidence linking changes in the OB and the progression of AD is found in a proteomics study of samples obtained from AD patients from the initial, intermediate, and advanced stages of the disease [204]. The authors detected increased amyloid β (Aβ) and phosphorylated Tau levels in the OB from all AD patients. Specifically, phosphorylated Tau deposits are increased in the anterior olfactory nucleus, in addition to the OB, in patients with advanced AD. Moreover, Aβ levels in the OB increase as the disease progresses. Since the initial stages of the disease, dysregulated protein clusters involved in DNA repair and oxidation are also detected. In fact, the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein system, the gene cluster responsible for DNA repair, is downregulated in the OB. In the intermediate AD stages, proteins involved in signaling and metabolism are affected in the OB. For example, the V-type proton ATPases, a network of proteins involved in ATP breakdown and function in neurotransmission [205], are downregulated in AD patients. In the advanced AD stages, many proteome changes are present, including downregulation of mitochondrial complexes (such as NDUFs, SDHB, UQCRC2, and ATP5), which suggests impaired mitochondrial function. Together, these findings suggest that AD-related hyposmia could be mediated by amyloid-related changes in protein expression, which deleteriously affect mitochondrial physiology.

Another study that links mitochondrial dysfunction, hyposmia, and AD was conducted by Lampinen et al. Using single-cell RNA-seq on OM cells from healthy individuals and AD patients, they show dysregulated ETC and ATP synthase genes in the AD patients, leading to dysfunctional ATP synthesis [206]. To validate these findings, the authors used Seahorse to show that OM from the AD samples has an approximately 30% reduced OCR and, accordingly, decreased ATP production, compared to the control samples. This shows decreased ability to respond to metabolic stress and compromised mitochondrial efficiency in OM of AD patients [206].

Using a Drosophila model of AD created by Aβ42 expression, Wang et al., investigated the status of the cellular environment of mushroom body neurons, a major processing center for regulating the formation and storage of olfactory memory [207]. When expressing Aβ42, these neurons contain more mitochondria in the soma, compared to control flies [208]. The authors also found an increased number of fragmented mitochondria in the soma of the AD-mimicking animals, which could indicate that only healthy mitochondria are trafficked throughout the cell. Mitochondria expressing Aβ42 also exhibit smaller surface area and volume on average, and they are more spherical, suggesting increased mitochondrial fission in these animals, compared to the control flies [208]. Lastly, the authors show similar morphological changes in dendritic and axonal mitochondria at later ages. However, mitochondrial calcium import is reduced in isolated Drosophila brains from the Aβ42 group, compared to controls. Altogether, they show the toxic effects of Aβ42 on mitochondrial physiology and olfactory memory formation.

Lastly, Mussalo et al., reported the presence of traffic ultrafine particles (UFP) on the OM cells, in both healthy individuals and AD patients [199]. UFP can reach the lungs and translocate to different regions of the body, including the brain [209,210]. The authors show altered expression of the ETC complexes leading to increased oxidative stress in the OM in AD patients, versus controls. Since OM are located on the surface of the nasal cavity, they are in direct contact with the air where UFP are located; therefore, UFP can potentially reach the olfactory cortex. The authors also show that UFP could aggravate AD by altering the expression of the ETC and of the uncoupling protein 2. Altered expression of this protein has been linked to AD development [211,212].

As mentioned at the beginning of this section, the relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction, aging, and hyposmia has also been demonstrated. For example, Hussain et al., using a Drosophila aging model, found that aging-related loss of smell may originate from mitochondrial dysfunction and associated increased oxidative stress in cholinergic projection neurons (PNs), rather than in olfactory sensory neurons within the antennal lobe [213] (the structural equivalent of the OB in Drosophila [214]). Between one and ten weeks old, the number of olfactory sensory neurons remains relatively unchanged, while PNs undergo early degeneration characterized by reduced synaptic bouton density, cell body shrinkage, decreased choline acetyltransferase expression, and a loss of mitochondrial puncta in axon terminals. All this suggests that the death of PNs is primarily responsible for aging-related hyposmia [213]. Interestingly, Sod2 levels are significantly reduced in aged brains in this model. After using RNAi to downregulate the expression of ~20 genes associated with aging, the authors found that PNs-specific Sod2 KD reduces odor responsiveness and synaptic integrity in mutant flies. Conversely, overexpressing Sod2 in PNs rescued aging-related olfactory decline, highlighting that oxidative stress-driven mitochondrial impairment in central olfactory neurons is sufficient to cause hyposmia [213].

TASTE

Taste is the chemosensory detection of molecules by taste buds, allowing the identification of five main tastes: umami, salty, sweet, sour, and bitter [215]. Although taste and smell are tightly interconnected, the human olfactory system can discriminate over a trillion odorants, whereas the gustatory system detects only five basic tastes [216]. The interaction between taste stimuli and receptors triggers the release of neurotransmitters, including serotonin [217]. This release activates afferent gustatory neurons, ultimately triggering the perception in the gustatory cortex [215].

Taste perception decline has been associated with aging and aging-related neurodegenerative disorders [218,219]. Ageusia (complete loss of taste), hypogeusia (partial loss or reduced taste sensitivity), and dysgeusia (distorted or altered taste perception) have been described in aging [220]. Some authors have studied the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in taste decline. Using electroencephalography, Iannilli et al., demonstrate that decreased taste sensitivity is not only associated with aging-related deterioration of the gustatory system, but also with aging-dependent neural changes within the central gustatory network [219]. Specifically, in older adults, a broader inferior frontal activation and loss of cerebellar/posterior insula involvement are present, suggesting slower and altered processing of taste signals. This could lead to the diminished taste sensitivity that is sometimes present in aging [219].

While gustatory decline is commonly associated with aging, neurodegenerative disease-related sensory decline may also arise from disease-specific mechanisms. For example, using the rotenone-based PD mouse model, Yin et al., show that 18 h of water deprivation in PD mice leads to reduced avoidance of bitter-flavored water compared to control animals [221]. Interestingly, in this model, mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are associated with degeneration of catecholaminergic neurons within gustatory-related nuclei, including the nucleus of the solitary tract. Alterations of the reward system are also present in this PD model. These neuronal changes could be associated with the early disruption of bitter-specific neural signaling, which is observed before the onset of motor symptoms in this PD model [221].

Using Drosophila, Brown et al., have demonstrated that both AD models and aged flies have reduced taste sensitivity to sucrose but not to fatty acids, compared to young flies [222]. However, this decline in sugar taste sensitivity could arise through distinct mechanisms in the two groups. For example, reduced taste sensitivity in aging flies is associated with downregulation of the sweet-sensing receptor protein Gr64f in aged flies, compared to young flies, along with other 66 downregulated genes, 63 of which are sensory-related. In contrast, AD flies show gene expression dysregulation, but they exhibit reduced axonal innervation and differential expression of the mitochondrial large ribosomal RNA, and a long non-coding RNA, in sugar-sensing neurons [222]. Interestingly, upregulated mtRNA levels have been also described in plasma exomes in AD patients [223]. Further studies aimed at elucidating the molecular mechanisms that link aging, mitochondrial dysfunction, and gustatory deficits were conducted by Ren et al., using single-cell transcriptomic analysis of aging mouse taste papillae. Among all upregulated genes, mt-ND4l and mt-ATP8 are two of the top three upregulated in the aging papilla. Both genes are involved in regulating OXPHOS. Moreover, mt-Co3, which is downregulated in multiple types of circumvallate papillae, is critical for the ETC. Dysregulation of these genes could be associated with increased ROS generation, ultimately contributing to aging-related damage in taste papillae and leading to impaired taste function [224].

Interestingly, differences in taste changes between regular aging and neurodegeneration have been described. For example, the human taste 2 receptor family, which encodes bitter taste receptors that are GPCRs expressed in both gustatory tissue and the brain [225], is nearly entirely silenced in rapidly progressing dementia with Lewy bodies. However, this silencing is not observed in typical dementia with Lewy bodies or middle-aged controls [218]. These GPCRs are crucial to maintain mitochondrial physiology, including bioenergetics [226]. Similarly, in a study comparing adults without neurological or psychiatric diseases and AD patients, Sakai et al., found that gustatory decline correlates to dementia severity, independent of other demographic variables and age [227]. The authors also noted that sensitivity detection of sweet, salty, and bitter tastes and the sweet and sour taste recognition sensitivity declines in AD patients. The decreased recognition and detection thresholds for sucrose in AD aligns with findings from the AD Drosophila study conducted by Brown et al., discussed above [222,228].

While mitochondrial dysfunction in aging and aging-related disorders, such as AD and PD, contributes to sensory decline, pathogens can also disrupt mitochondrial function and induce similar effects. Notably, some of the hallmark symptoms of SARS-CoV–2 infection, including ageusia, hypogeusia, and dysgeusia (as well as anosmia - the complete loss of smell - and hyposmia), likely due to prolonged oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction resulting from the to hijacking of cellular metabolism [229]. These changes may contribute to persistent sensory impairment. SARS-CoV–2 is known to disrupt mitochondrial dynamics and reduce ATP production [22]. Viral infection can compromise olfactory and gustatory neurons, via inducing mitochondrial dysfunction. For example, Szogi et al., show that post-COVID patients have increased Mfn2 and Drp1, while no changes are observed in Mfn1 and Fis1 genes. The same patients show elevated SOD1 levels, but also increased ROS generation. Increased levels of proteins involved in autophagy (ATG4B) and decreased circulating cell-free mtDNA are present in the same samples. All these changes could be part of a cellular-compensating mechanism to counteract COVID-associated stress. However, over time, impaired gustatory and olfactory neuronal survival arises, contributing to the long-lasting taste and smell deficiencies in post-COVID patients. Impaired neuronal survival further compromises mitochondrial physiology, creating a deleterious feedback loop [229].

CONCLUSIONS

The bibliography shows that mitochondrial dysfunction is present in the etiopathology of aging and aging-related sensory decline. While different cell types and consequences of this dysfunction have been proposed in each of the senses, common pathological mechanisms at the mitochondrial level have been described (Table 1). Accordingly, pharmacological targeting of mitochondrial dysfunction could be an innovative and valid strategy against this decline, which is associated with decreased healthspan and lifespan (Fig. 3). However, further investigation is needed in this field before pharmacological targeting can be successfully developed. For example, the molecular mechanisms that drive mitochondrial dysfunction in aging and aging-related pathologies still remain poorly understood. Without a deeper understanding of these processes, therapeutic targeting of the organelle will remain largely ineffective. Moreover, the precise temporal relationship between the onset of sensory decline and that of mitochondrial dysfunction in aging and aging-related pathologies remains unclear. This is a crucial point, since successfully targeting mitochondria should probably occur in the very early stages of sensory decline to be efficient. Lastly, a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying sensory decline is also needed to develop effective pharmacological approaches for these conditions.
