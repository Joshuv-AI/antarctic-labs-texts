Structural insight into IscB’s RNA-lid-based inactivation mechanism
Authors: Feizuo Wang, Ruochen Guo, Senfeng Zhang, Yinuo Cui, Junlan Wang, Tao Hu, Kunming Liu, Qi Wang, Yao Liu, Ki Hyun Nam, Ziqing Winston Zhao, Quanquan Ji, Xin Xu, Ercheng Wang, Youyuan Zhu, Yao Yang, Min Luo, Peixiang Ma, Shengsheng Ma, Chunlong Xu, Chunyi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIscB, a compact Cas9 ancestor from the obligate mobile element guided activity system, has attracted growing interest as a programmable genome editor because of its small size and therapeutic delivery potential. Despite its promise, structural insights into IscB’s regulation remain limited, with only a target-bound R-loop structure previously reported. Here, we present the structural trajectory of an engineered IscB, capturing its transition from a resting state to activation. Using cryo-electron microscopy, we resolve four high-resolution structures: the apo resting state, two intermediate complexes with 6-nt and 10-nt guide–target pairing and a fully paired 16-nt primed cleavage state. These structures uncover a dual inactivation mechanism mediated by RNA lids; the ωRNA lid blocks HNH domain access, while the guide RNA lid occludes the RuvC active site. As guide–target pairing progresses, the guide RNA undergoes a stepwise displacement, mimicking a ‘car pedal’ motion that triggers activation at 11-nt pairing. The HNH domain also contributes to R-loop stabilization through a positively charged R-wedge motif and undergoes a ~90° activation-driven rotation mediated by two hinge regions. In variants IscBHig1 and IscBHig2, engineering these hinge motifs to enhance conformational flexibility notably improved genome-editing efficiency in cells. In summary, our study reveals the molecular basis underlying IscB autoinhibition and activation, identifies previously uncharacterized regulatory features and establishes hinge elements as a target region for engineering compact, efficient genome editors.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Enzyme mechanisms, Nucleases, DNA metabolism

Main

The CRISPR–Cas system originated as an adaptive immune mechanism in prokaryotes, enabling defense against invading mobile genetic elements through RNA-guided nucleases1–4. This unique ability to program nucleic acid recognition has since been repurposed as a revolutionary genome-editing technology5. However, one of the major limitations in therapeutic genome editing remains the large size of many CRISPR–Cas effectors, particularly class 2 systems such as Cas9 and Cas12, which hampers efficient delivery through clinically relevant vectors such as adeno-associated viruses5,6. In this context, compact and programmable nucleases have drawn increasing interest as next-generation genome-editing tools with improved delivery potential7.

Among these, the OMEGA (obligate mobile element guided activity) systems—an ancient class of RNA-guided nucleases derived from transposon-associated elements—have garnered considerable attention7–15. One such effector, IscB, is believed to be the evolutionary ancestor of Cas9, sharing core architectural features including an HNH nuclease domain and a RuvC nuclease domain, which respectively cleave the target and nontarget DNA strands8,10. Despite its natural role in transposition, IscB uses a structured ωRNA containing a programmable guide region, enabling repurposing for targeted genome editing. Remarkably, IscB comprises fewer than 500 amino acids—only about 40% the size of Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9—making it one of the most compact RNA-guided nucleases with considerable potential for therapeutic delivery and engineering9.

Driven by this promise, efforts to characterize and optimize IscB for genome editing have accelerated in recent years16–23. However, despite growing functional insights, structural understanding of IscB has remained limited. Prior studies have resolved only the full R-loop structure in the DNA-bound state, with no structural information available for the apo form or any intermediates during target DNA engagement8,10. This lack of knowledge has hindered mechanistic dissection of how IscB transitions from autoinhibition to activation and how its guide–target pairing dynamics influence nuclease activation.

In this study, we systematically resolve the functional trajectory of an engineered IscB variant (enIscB) by capturing four distinct cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures at high resolution: a resting (apo) state without DNA, two intermediate DNA-bound states with 6-nt and 10-nt guide–target complementarity and a fully engaged 16-nt matched R-loop complex representing the primed cleavage state. Together, these structures provide unprecedented insight into the dynamic conformational changes governing IscB activation.

Our data reveal a dual RNA-lid-mediated autoinhibition strategy. In the resting state, the HNH nuclease domain is catalytically inactive, with its key histidines loosely arranged. Importantly, the ωRNA folds into a lid-like structure that blocks the HNH active site from encountering nonspecific DNA substrates. Meanwhile, the guide RNA itself adopts a bent configuration, capping the RuvC catalytic pocket and preventing premature or off-target cleavage. These RNA-based lid structures serve as intrinsic safeguards against unwanted DNA cleavage and represent an RNA-meditated mode of CRISPR effector regulation. Furthermore, comparison of the DNA-bound intermediates unveils a stepwise activation mechanism. As the length of guide–target base pairing increases, the guide RNA undergoes a progressive displacement, mimicking the motion of a ‘car pedal’ being pressed. At approximately 11 nt of hybridization, the guide RNA shifts sufficiently to trigger IscB activation; this defines a minimal threshold for productive R-loop formation. These findings establish a physical and structural basis for how guide–target pairing depth governs catalytic readiness.

Beyond its catalytic activation, the HNH domain appears to have a critical role in DNA unwinding and R-loop stabilization, facilitated by an R-wedge motif and a positively charged patch. Notably, activation of the HNH domain is accompanied by a dramatic ~90° rotational movement, which is driven by two flexible hinge regions flanking the domain. These hinge motifs act as conformational levers, coupling guide RNA movement to enzymatic activation. To further probe the functional importance of these hinges, we engineered variants with enhanced flexibility. Strikingly, these modifications significantly boosted genome-editing efficiency in cellular assays, demonstrating that hinge dynamics are not only central to IscB function but also represent promising targets for protein engineering.

Altogether, our study reveals the complete structural and mechanistic path from IscB inhibition to activation. By uncovering a dual RNA-lid-mediated autoinhibition strategy and a stepwise car pedal activation mechanism, we provide foundational insights into OMEGA nucleases. Moreover, the identification of hinge regions as engineering hotspots paves the way for future design of compact and efficient genome-editing tools. In parallel, the resting-state structure offers a valuable template for the de novo design24,25 of synthetic inhibitors—such as minibinders that lock IscB in its inactive conformation—providing a potential route toward controllable or externally tunable gene editors.

Results

Structural elucidation of IscB activation pathway using an engineered variant

Previously, we and Nishimasu/Zhang groups resolved the DNA-bound structure of OgeuIscB in its full R-loop state, laying the foundation for understanding the structural architecture of this minimal RNA-guided nuclease8,10. In the subsequent years, numerous studies focused on improving the genome-editing activity of IscB through sequence optimization and guide engineering16,18–23. However, the molecular basis of its regulation—particularly how IscB transitions from an inactive to an active state—remained elusive because of the absence of structural snapshots representing intermediate conformations during target recognition (Extended Data Fig. 1a).

To address this gap, we used an engineered variant of IscB (enIscB)23 optimized for biochemical stability and activity. Compared to the wild-type (WT) OgeuIscB, enIscB harbors four amino acid substitutions (E85R, H369R, S387R and S457R) within its conserved domains and uses a shortened ωRNA guide scaffold that is 25 nt shorter than the original (Fig. 1a,b and Extended Data Fig. 1b–c). In vitro cleavage assays confirmed that enIscB exhibits enhanced target DNA cleavage activity relative to the WT enzyme (Extended Data Fig. 2), validating its suitability for structural investigation.

We subsequently used cryo-EM to determine a series of high-resolution structures capturing distinct functional states of enIscB (Table 1). Each of the four states was obtained from an independent sample preparation, grid and dataset. Starting with the apo binary complex (enIscB bound to ωRNA but without target DNA), we solved its resting-state structure at 3.0-Å resolution. We then incubated enIscB with target DNAs of increasing guide–target complementarity—6 nt, 10 nt and 16 nt—and successfully captured structures corresponding to two intermediate states and the full R-loop state, with overall resolutions ranging from 3.1 to 3.3 Å (Fig. 1c–e and Extended Data Figs. 3 and 4). State-specific assembly conditions were as follows: apo, 6-nt and 10-nt complexes were prepared in 150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 and 5 mM MgCl2 and incubated on ice before vitrification (Mg2+ density is visible in these maps) and the full R-loop (16-nt) complex was assembled without Mg2+ and with 0.5 mM EDTA on ice, thereby suppressing nuclease activity and trapping a precleavage full R-loop. These structural snapshots collectively depict a continuous activation trajectory, from the inactive apo conformation to the catalytically competent full R-loop state. Notably, the intermediate structures with 6-nt and 10-nt target pairing revealed guide RNA displacement and conformational remodeling not previously observed, suggesting a stepwise recognition and activation process.

Tightly packed HNH–RuvC architecture stabilized by dual hinge elements

Our cryo-EM reconstruction of the apo enIscB complex revealed a remarkably well-resolved HNH domain, with continuous and high-quality density allowing confident model building (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 4). Unlike the loosely arranged architecture observed in Cas9 (refs. 26–30), IscB’s HNH and RuvC domains form a compact and interlocked configuration. This tight spatial organization is reinforced by two defined hinge elements—hinge 1 and hinge 2—situated at the interfaces of the HNH and RuvC domains (Fig. 2a,b). In cartoon representations using piped α-helices, hinge 2 connecting HNH to RuvC appears as a largely continuous helix, with only minor distortions (Fig. 2a, right). In contrast, corresponding regions in Cas9 (for example, in Nme1Cas9) are considerably longer and more flexible, with extended unstructured linkers separating the catalytic domains26 (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 5). This observation suggests that the compactness of IscB is not merely a consequence of shorter sequence length, but is structurally enabled by a tightly coupled domain arrangement.

Detailed structural analysis revealed that both hinge regions have key roles in stabilizing the overall fold. In the resting state, hinge 1 tethers the HNH domain to a hydrophobic pocket on RuvC through the aromatic residue Y211, assisted by Q212 (Fig. 2c). Hinge 2 also anchors the structure through V298 and L299, which insert into a second hydrophobic cavity on RuvC (Fig. 2d). The two hinge elements are further connected by an interhinge salt bridge between N194 (hinge 1) and N300 (hinge 2), reinforcing the entire domain architecture (Fig. 2e).

To functionally validate the importance of these hinge domain interactions, we performed a plasmid interference assay in Escherichia coli. In this system, active IscB cleaves a target plasmid containing chloramphenicol, leading to loss of chloramphenicol resistance and failure to grow on selective plates (Fig. 2f). As expected, amino acid substitutions disrupting hinge interactions—Y211A/E and Q212A from hinge 1 and V298R and L299R from hinge 2—severely impaired plasmid interference activity (Fig. 2g). In particular, substitutions Y211E and V298R nearly abolished function, underscoring their critical role in maintaining structural integrity and catalytic competence. These findings demonstrate that IscB exploits a highly compact HNH–RuvC arrangement stabilized by rigid hinge-mediated contacts. This tight domain packing not only reflects its evolutionary divergence from larger Cas9-like nucleases but also suggests a structurally efficient strategy for maintaining function within a compact molecular framework.

ωRNA-lid-mediated autoinhibition and active site remodeling of the HNH domain

Structural analysis of the HNH domain revealed a highly defined catalytic center featuring a cluster of four conserved histidine residues—H243, H245, H269 and H273—coordinating a clearly resolved Mg2+ ion (Fig. 3a). Notably, H243 was not previously assigned as part of the active center in earlier research8,23. In the resting state, only H245, H269 and H273 contribute to Mg2+ coordination, while H243 remains distal and disengaged. A nearby acidic residue, E279, is positioned to potentially assist metal ion stabilization but its longer distance (~6.3 Å) suggests a suboptimal configuration, consistent with an inactive conformation incapable of DNA phosphodiester cleavage (Fig. 3a). In the full R-loop structure, however, we observed a coordinated repositioning of all four histidines toward the metal ion. H243 undergoes the largest conformational shift—approximately 5.7 Å—to complete the catalytic triad and enable robust Mg2+ coordination (Fig. 3b,c). This conformational reorganization likely underlies the activation of the HNH domain and suggests that H243 acts as a previously unrecognized ‘gatekeeper’ residue critical for catalysis.

To test this hypothesis, we substituted H243 to alanine (H243A) and performed an in vitro cleavage assay using dual-labeled DNA substrates. The H243A mutant completely abolished cleavage of the target strand (TS), whereas cleavage of the nontarget strand (NTS) by the RuvC domain remained intact (Fig. 3d). These results confirm that H243 is essential for HNH-mediated catalysis and its activation is tightly regulated by structural rearrangement.

Beyond metal coordination, we identified a unique lid-mediated autoinhibition mechanism centered on the HNH domain. In the apo structure, the ωRNA adopts a lid-like configuration directly above the HNH catalytic cleft. Three highly basic residues—R251, K252 and K253—form electrostatic interactions that help draw the ωRNA lid into position, thereby physically occluding the active site and blocking access by nonspecific single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) substrates (Fig. 3e). This mechanism likely protects against off-target cleavage under nonproductive binding conditions. In the full R-loop structure, these basic residues are repositioned and appear to stabilize the TS, rather than the ωRNA lid (Fig. 3f). This suggests a dual functional role: contributing to autoinhibition in the resting state and facilitating TS stabilization upon activation. Together, these findings uncover a previously uncharacterized autoinhibitory mechanism in IscB mediated by both conformational plasticity and RNA-based shielding. They further establish H243 as a key molecular switch, defining the transition from inactive to catalytically competent states.

Guide RNA-lid-mediated autoinhibition of the RuvC domain

We next investigated the mechanism by which the RuvC domain is autoinhibited in the resting state. Unexpectedly, our cryo-EM structure revealed a striking difference in guide RNA conformation compared to previously reported target-bound states. Specifically, in the resting state, we were only able to resolve guide RNA density beginning from nucleotide position +5, with no observable density for the 5′-terminal four nucleotides (Fig. 4a,b). The visible portion of the guide RNA, starting from position +5, is positioned directly above the RuvC catalytic center (Fig. 4b). The RuvC active site appears preorganized and catalytically primed; key catalytic residues—H339, D342, D60 and E192—coordinate a Mg2+ ion within the catalytic pocket (Fig. 4c). This suggests that, in the absence of a specific substrate, RuvC exists on the brink of activation. As such, an effective self-inhibition mechanism is crucial to prevent unintended cleavage of host nucleic acids.

Remarkably, the phosphate backbone of the guide RNA’s +5 nucleotide interacts directly with the Mg2+ ion and its surrounding residues, effectively anchoring the RNA over the RuvC active site (Fig. 4c). This configuration ensures that the guide RNA itself sterically occludes access to the nuclease center. Given that RuvC cleaves DNA rather than RNA, the guide RNA is resistant to cleavage despite this close contact. Moreover, the spatial arrangement appears suboptimal for nucleophilic attack on the guide RNA backbone, reinforcing its role as a stable protective lid. This RNA lid blocks access to the RuvC active site and would prevent any nonspecific ssDNA from being mistakenly loaded or cleaved (Fig. 4d). To test the importance of this autoinhibitory configuration, we substituted each of the four key RuvC catalytic residues and assessed IscB function using a plasmid interference assay in E. coli. All four substitutions—D60A, E192A, H339A and D342A—completely abolished interference activity, confirming the essential role of these residues in IscB function and validating our structural observations (Fig. 4e).

Lastly, a structural comparison between the resting and full R-loop states revealed a dramatic shift in guide RNA positioning. In the resting state, only nucleotides +5 to +16 are visible, whereas, in the active, DNA-bound structure, all 16 nucleotides of the guide RNA are resolved, starting from position +1 (Fig. 4f). This pronounced structural rearrangement underscores the importance of guide RNA reorganization in triggering activation and clearing the RuvC active site, likely representing a key conformational checkpoint in IscB activation.

A car pedal model for guide RNA displacement and threshold-dependent activation

Building on the observed conformational rearrangement of the guide RNA between inactive and active states, we systematically compared four cryo-EM structures of IscB to understand how progressive guide–target base pairing influences RNA positioning and protein activation. In the absence of any target DNA or in the presence of a 6-nt matched target, the guide RNA conformation remains largely unchanged, with the first visible nucleotide beginning at position +5 (Fig. 5a,b). In this partially engaged state, the guide retains unused base positions and floats stably above the RuvC catalytic center. However, once a 10-nt matched target DNA is introduced, a structural change begins to occur. The +7th guide nucleotide (corresponding to the 11th matched base pair with the target) bends downward, suggesting an imminent shift in guide orientation (Fig. 5c). This rearrangement is further exaggerated in the full R-loop state with 16 matched base pairs, where the guide RNA undergoes a pronounced displacement, fully engaging with the TS and transitioning to an active conformation (Fig. 5c,d). These transitions suggest that reaching 11-nt complementarity may be the minimal threshold required to trigger a mechanical ‘push’ on the guide RNA.

Inspired by this structural behavior, we propose a car pedal activation mechanism for IscB. In this analogy, the guide RNA acts as a spring-loaded pedal, with the target DNA exerting increasing pressure through progressive base pairing. Once the pairing length reaches 11 nt, the ‘pedal’ is pressed sufficiently to overcome the activation barrier, enabling full R-loop formation and domain rearrangement (Fig. 5e,f). To experimentally test this hypothesis, we designed a panel of 17 plasmid substrates containing target sites with increasing guide–target complementarity from 0 to 16 nt (Fig. 5g). Using an in vivo plasmid interference assay, we observed a sharp transition in IscB activity between 10-nt and 11-nt matching; targets with ≤10-nt complementarity exhibited little to no interference activity, while 11-nt pairing resulted in a clear gain in activity (Fig. 5h). Beyond 11 nt, activity increased progressively and plateaued at 14–16 nt, consistent with efficient R-loop stabilization and cleavage. Together, these results establish 11-nt base pairing as a critical molecular threshold for IscB activation and support a car-pedal-like mechanism in which guide RNA conformational displacement is triggered by progressive target engagement.

An HNH-derived wedge and positively charged band stabilize R-loop formation

Once the guide RNA is sufficiently displaced by ≥11-nt target pairing, IscB will initiate local DNA strand separation to allow R-loop formation. This process is reminiscent of the wedge-based mechanisms observed in other CRISPR effectors such as Cas9 (type II)31,32 and Cascade (type I)33–35, where a conserved loop element—often termed the Q-wedge—splits the target duplex. Interestingly, in IscB, we identified a structurally analogous wedge element originating from the HNH domain (Fig. 6a), which was not observed in previous IscB structures because of missing HNH density8,10.

This wedge comprises a short loop containing three polar and positively charged residues—R251, K252 and N253—that project into the target DNA duplex, prying apart the base pairs between the TS and NTS (Fig. 6b). The wedge is positioned directly within the R-loop bubble and appears to physically obstruct reannealing of the DNA strands, thereby maintaining strand separation (Fig. 6a–c). In addition to strand opening, R-loop stabilization is further supported by electrostatic interactions between the hinge regions and the DNA backbone. Hinge 1 and hinge 2 are enriched in positively charged residues (for example, K205, R213, K291 and K292), which form a continuous surface that contacts the phosphate backbone of the NTS (Fig. 6c). These interactions likely anchor the displaced strand and reinforce the structural integrity of the R-loop35–38.

To validate the functional importance of the R-wedge and hinge-based stabilization, we introduced alanine substitutions at key residues and assessed IscB activity in vivo using plasmid interference assays. Substitutions in the wedge region (R251A and K252A) or the hinge residues (K291A;K292A and R195A;K205A;R213A) all led to substantial loss of interference activity (Fig. 6d), confirming the essential roles of these elements in DNA unwinding and R-loop formation. Together, these findings reveal an HNH-originating R-wedge unique to IscB, along with a band of positively charged hinge residues that collectively orchestrate R-loop initiation and stabilization—critical steps in the transition from DNA binding to nuclease activation.

Hinge-guided domain rotation enables IscB activation and identifies hotspots for effector optimization

Our structural trajectory of IscB revealed that guide RNA displacement and R-loop formation are tightly coupled with dramatic rearrangement of the HNH domain. In particular, the HNH domain undergoes a ∼90° rigid-body rotation between the resting and R-loop-bound states (Fig. 7a and Extended Data Fig. 6). This conformational change is not intrinsic to HNH itself but is instead driven by structural remodeling of two flexible hinge elements that bridge HNH and RuvC. Comparison of the resting and active states shows that the bridge helix and RuvC domain remain largely static, whereas the hinge regions exhibit extensive conformational shifts that orchestrate HNH reorientation (Fig. 7a,b and Extended Data Fig. 6). Specifically, hinge 1 appears to maintain its secondary structure but notably shifts position to push the HNH domain into the active conformation (Fig. 7c). In contrast, hinge 2 undergoes both positional and structural transitions; the N-terminal segment of the hinge initially forms a helix continuous with HNH, while the C-terminal region forms a loop connecting to RuvC. Upon activation, the helix unwinds and is replaced by a newly formed helix on the opposite side (Fig. 7d), indicating a dynamic helix–loop exchange that contributes to domain mobility.

To test this, we selected a panel of hinge residues (for example, R195, K204, W208, Y217, K290, H294 and L296) that are both nonconserved and involved in high-contact interfaces. We substituted these positions to small or polar residues (for example, alanine, serine, threonine and asparagine) to reduce local packing and potentially increase conformational freedom (Extended Data Fig. 9). These engineered variants were tested in HEK293T cells using a GFP-based homologous recombination reporter (GFxP), in which target DNA cleavage by IscB restores GFP fluorescence (Fig. 7e). Consistent with our hypothesis, nine of 14 variants showed increased editing activity in cells, with R195S, R195A, W208R, Y217N, Y217R, L296A and L296T producing marked improvements (Fig. 7f). As a negative control, substitution of H243—previously identified as critical for HNH catalysis (Fig. 3)—abolished activity, confirming the assay’s sensitivity. For hinge variants with reduced activity, we cannot exclude that protein destabilization and lower intracellular abundance contribute to the phenotype; our selection ultimately favored designs that maintain stability while enhancing flexibility and activity.

Encouraged by these results, we combined the most promising substitutions into dual-variant constructs. The combinations R195A;Y217N (from hinge 1) and H294G;L296G (from hinge 2) yielded robust and reproducible enhancements and we designated these as IscBHig1 and IscBHig2, respectively (Fig. 7g,h).

In summary, our findings identify the hinge regions as tunable allosteric levers that control IscB activation. By leveraging their inherent flexibility, we demonstrate a structure-guided strategy to enhance the function of compact genome editors. These results open previously overlooked avenues for protein engineering by targeting dynamic interdomain connectors that were previously overlooked.

Evaluation of enIscB hinge variants: flexibility, activity and off-target activity

To connect hinge engineering to function, we evaluated the two lead variants from our cell-based screen (Hig1: R195A;Y217N and Hig2: H294G;L296T) across structural dynamics, biochemistry and endogenous editing. All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (three replicas per state, ≥500 ns each) revealed that localized root-mean-square fluctuation increases at the engineered hinges (hinge 1, residues 193–221; hinge 2, residues 288–299) (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b). Hig1 reproducibly elevated hinge 1 mobility in both apo and target-bound states, with a modest ‘bystander’ rise in adjacent elements, whereas Hig2 primarily increased hinge 2 mobility in the apo state but converged toward WT upon target binding—consistent with a model in which added flexibility lowers the barrier to engagement but restabilizes once a productive R-loop forms. These MD trends align with the RNA lid autoinhibition and car pedal framework: hinge loosening facilitates the RuvC and HNH rearrangements that follow sufficient guide–target pairing.

We next asked whether flexibility gains translate to catalysis. Purified-protein cleavage time courses showed faster accumulation of both NTS and TS products for Hig1 compared to WT under identical condition (0–30 min; n = 3) (Extended Data Fig. 10c,d), supporting an intrinsic catalytic enhancement rather than an expression artifact. To benchmark cellular performance beyond the GFP reporter screen, we targeted the endogenous DNMT1 locus in HEK293T (Extended Data Fig. 10e,f). Deep sequencing of fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS)-enriched mCherry+ cells showed the on-target efficiency for enIscB Hig1 (~45.1% indels) relative to SpG Cas9 at the same site (~51.0% indels) (Fig. 7i,j; mean ± s.d., n = 3). Crucially, genome-wide specificity profiling indicated a lower off-target burden for enIscB Hig1: ~1.06% versus ~2.0% for SpG Cas9, an approximately twofold reduction at comparable efficacy (Fig. 7i,j). Together, these results establish a flexibility to function link for hinge-tuned enIscB; targeted increases in hinge dynamics correlate with faster cleavage in vitro and deliver Cas9-level editing with reduced off-target activity at an endogenous human locus.

Discussion

Despite increasing interest in IscB as a compact RNA-guided nuclease for genome editing, its molecular mechanism of regulation and activation has remained poorly understood. In this study, we provide a comprehensive structural trajectory of IscB across multiple functional states, uncovering a range of unique and previously unappreciated regulatory features.

One of the striking observations is the compactness of the IscB architecture. The HNH and RuvC nuclease domains are tightly packed together, connected by two short hinge regions. This domain organization contrasts sharply with larger nucleases such as Cas9 and offers an evolutionary explanation for IscB’s small size; rather than relying on extended flexible linkers, IscB uses a spatially economical folding strategy. Beyond structural compaction, both nuclease domains use a form of autoinhibition. Specifically, our structures reveal that the ωRNA scaffold forms two distinct RNA lids: the guide RNA overlays the RuvC catalytic pocket, while the ωRNA backbone blocks the HNH active site. This dual RNA lid strategy restricts nonspecific DNA access and provides structural evidence of RNA-mediated autoinhibition in RNA-guided nucleases.

IscB activation also follows a highly distinctive path. We demonstrate that progressive base pairing with target DNA gradually displaces the guide RNA—resembling the pressing of a car pedal. At 11-nt complementarity, this displacement reaches a threshold that triggers a cascade of structural transitions, most notably a ~90° rigid-body rotation of the HNH domain. This movement is driven by conformational remodeling of two hinge elements that act in a push–pull manner. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the stepwise, energy-coupled activation of IscB and define 11-nt base pairing as a likely minimal threshold for catalytic switching.

In addition, we uncover an HNH-derived DNA-splitting wedge—distinct from the Q-wedges found in Cas9 and Cascade complexes. This ‘R-wedge’ is composed of positively charged residues that invade the duplex DNA and promote strand separation. Notably, the wedge is situated on the HNH domain and was not observed in previous IscB structures because of the absence of resolved HNH density. Together with the hinge elements, the R-wedge completes a coordinated mechanism for R-loop formation and stabilization.

Perhaps most importantly, our study highlights the hinge regions as a previously overlooked target for protein engineering. By identifying nonconserved residues within the hinge helices that contribute to rigid contacts, we rationally introduced substitutions aimed at enhancing local flexibility. In mammalian cells, several of these hinge-optimized variants demonstrated improved editing efficiency, with R195S, Y217N and L296T among the most effective. Combinatorial variants—termed IscBHig1 and IscBHig2—further increased activity. These findings demonstrate that catalytic activity can be engineered by modulating hinge flexibility and provide a strategy for optimizing protein function by targeting interdomain dynamics. An important caveat is that loss-of-function hinge mutations may primarily reduce protein stability or abundance rather than impair catalysis per se; thus, optimal hinge engineering should balance flexibility gains with structural robustness.

While these findings greatly expand our understanding of IscB biology, several limitations remain. Our structural series spans key transitions—from the apo state to 6-nt, 10-nt and 16-nt DNA-bound states—but lacks finer intermediates around the critical 11-nt activation threshold. Capturing structures with 11-nt or 12-nt matching could provide more direct support for the car pedal model. Furthermore, although our hinge mutations enhance cellular activity, we currently lack detailed physical evidence (for example, nuclear magnetic resonance dynamics and hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry) to confirm whether these changes indeed increase hinge flexibility.

Lastly, the discovery of an autoinhibited resting state has broader implications. Unlike CRISPR–Cas systems, which are frequently targeted by anti-CRISPR proteins42–46, IscB originates from transposons and lacks known natural inhibitors9. Our resting-state structure, thus, provides a blueprint for future inhibitor design. For example, one could envision designing de novo minibinders that lock IscB in its apo conformation, preventing activation and enabling external control of gene editing activity.

In conclusion, this study reveals fundamental insights into the architecture, inhibition, activation and engineering of IscB. It clarifies the underlying mechanism and defines principles for compact nuclease design and engineering, supporting studies of RNA-guided nucleases and rational genome-editing tool development.

Methods

Plasmid constructions

The IscB DNA sequence was codon-optimized and synthesized with Twin-Strep-tag, SV40 NLS. The IscB construct was cloned into PACYC184 vector. The ωRNA sequence was synthesized and cloned into pCDFDuet-1 vector.

The IscB DNA sequence was codon-optimized and synthesized with SV40NLS and DYKDDDK tags. The ωRNA sequence was synthesized with U6 promoter. These constructs with were cloned into pCMV5 vector. The variant plasmids were generated by site-directed mutagenesis PCR.

Cell culture, transfection and flow cytometry analysis

HEK293T cells (American Type Culture Collection, CRL-3216) cultures were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and grown at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

Then, 1 μg of IscB plasmids and reporter plasmids were cotransfected with Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, cells were cultured for 72 h and mCherry, BFP and EGFP fluorescence was analyzed using a BD FACSymphony 3 cell analyzer. The flow cytometry data were analyzed with BD FACSDiva software.

Protein purification

IscB and ωRNA plasmids were cotransformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells. The cell culture was grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.8. Subsequently, 0.5 mM of IPTG was added to the mediums and bacteria were cultured overnight at 16 °C.

Cell pellets were harvested by centrifugation and lysed by sonication in buffer A (175 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH7.4, 2.5 mM TCEP, 5% glycerol and 2.5 mM MgCl2) with 1 mM PMSF. The lysate was subsequently centrifuged at 11,420g for 30 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was incubated with STarm Streptactin Beads4FF for 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were then washed sequentially with 25 ml of buffer A with 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 2 μg of DNaseI, 20 ml of buffer B (1 M NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 2.5 mM TCEP and 2.5 mM MgCl2) and finally 40 ml of buffer A. Elution was performed using buffer A supplemented with 5 mM D-biotin. The elutes were concentrated and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL, Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer C (175 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 2.5 mM TCEP and 2.5 mM MgCl2). The peak was collected, concentrated and flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen.

General complex assembly

For cryo-EM assembly, enIscB–DNA–ωRNA complexes were prepared at ~1.0 mg ml−1 enIscB with duplex DNA at a 1.5:1 (DNA to protein) molar ratio, followed by 30 min incubation on ice before vitrification. Each structural state (apo, 6-nt and 10-nt partial R-loops and full R-loop) derived from an independent preparation, grid and dataset (that is, no heterogeneous refinement across states). Apo and the 6-nt and 10-nt partial R-loop samples were assembled in 150 mM NaCl and 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 supplemented with 5 mM MgCl2. To capture a precleavage full R-loop, the 16-nt sample was assembled in 150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 with 0.5 mM EDTA and no added Mg2+, with all assemblies kept on ice to further suppress catalysis. In total, a panel of 16 complexes (1–16-nt pairing) was prepared and imaged; the apo, 6-nt and 10-nt partial R-loop and 16-nt full R-loop conditions yielded the most uniform particles and high-resolution maps suitable for model building.

Cryo-EM data acquisition

A volume of 3.5 μl of size-exclusion chromatography-purified complex at 2 mg ml−1 was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil holey carbon grids (R1.2/1.3, 300-mesh). Grids were glow-discharged for 50 s at 30 mA before sample application. Blotting was performed at 8 °C for 6 s using a blot force of 6 and 100% humidity, followed by rapid vitrification in liquid ethane with a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI, Thermo Fisher).

Cryo-EM data were acquired on a 300-kV Thermo Fisher Scientific Krios transmission EM instrument equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector. Each video was recorded over 4 s, corresponding to a total electron dose of 49 e− per Å2, fractionated into 48 frames. Super-resolution video stacks were dose-fractionated and subsequently 2× binned, yielding a final pixel size of 0.834 Å. The defocus range was set between −1.0 μm and −2.5 μm.

Cryo-EM data processing

Motion correction, contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation, blob-based particle picking, two-dimensional (2D) classification, three-dimensional (3D) classification and nonuniform 3D refinement were carried out using cryoSPARC version 2. Refinement followed a standard workflow, involving iterative rounds of 2D and 3D classification using C1 symmetry, as illustrated in Extended Data Fig. 3, to obtain the final reconstructions.

A solvent mask was generated and applied during all subsequent local refinement steps. CTF refinement was also performed to correct for beam-induced particle motion, further improving the quality of the final maps. Detailed data processing workflows and refinement statistics for all cryo-EM structures are provided in Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 3.

DNA substrate preparation

DNA oligonucleotides were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies. The oligonucleotides were annealed in duplex buffer (30 mM HEPES pH 7.4 and 100 mM potassium acetate) by heating to 95 °C for 10 min followed by gradual cooling. Annealed DNA substrates were then bound with recombinant proteins for cryo-EM studies.

For the in vitro cleavage assay, the annealed DNA substrates were cloned into pET vectors digested with BamHI and XhoI. PCR was performed using recombinant plasmids and primers conjugated to Cy3 and Cy5. The PCR products were purified by 6% native PAGE.

In vitro cleavage assay

First, 1 μM IscB and 20 nM DNA substrates were incubated in cleavage buffer (50 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 2.5 mM TCEP and 5 mM MgCl2) at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction products were mixed with denatured loading dye and heat at 95 °C for 10 min and run on 12% urea–PAGE. Fluorescence signals were detected using the iBright imaging system (Invitrogen).

Interference assay in E. coli

IscB, ωRNA and target DNA plasmids were cotransformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells. The cell culture was grown in LB medium supplemented with 5% glucose and appropriate antibiotics at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.8. Subsequently, 0.5 mM of IPTG was added to the medium and bacteria were cultured for 3 h. The bacterial cultures were subsequently diluted and inoculated to a selective plate and cultured overnight. Colonies were captured using an iBright imaging system (Invitrogen).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The number of independent biological replicates is indicated in the figure legend. Data are presented as the means ± s.e.m. Statistical analysis was conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with differences in means considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001).

RCS from protein structure through distance-based interaction analysis

The provided amino acid contact score matrix was generated by analyzing the 3D atomic coordinates obtained from a protein structure (PDB format). Initially, the atomic coordinates for each amino acid residue were extracted directly from the provided PDB file. Subsequently, amino acids were individually grouped, considering all constituent atoms within each residue. For every residue pair, the Euclidean distances between each pairwise combination of atoms from the two residues were calculated. The minimum atomic distance was selected to represent the proximity of the two residues.

Residue interactions were quantified and scored on the basis of these minimum distances, applying a predefined scoring system. Residues with atom pairs closer than 3.0 Å received the highest interaction score of 10, indicative of strong interactions such as hydrogen bonding or hydrophobic packing. Distances from 3.0 Å to 4.0 Å were scored 7, indicating strong interaction, and distances between 4.0 Å and 5.0 Å received a moderate interaction score of 4. Residues whose atoms were separated by distances exceeding 5.0 Å were considered noninteracting and scored as 0.

Lastly, the resulting interaction scores were compiled into a symmetrical matrix, clearly showing the degree of contact between each residue pair, which facilitated rapid identification of strongly interacting regions within the analyzed protein segment.

MD simulations

All mutant structures were generated from the WT structure by introducing amino acid substitutions using PyMOL (version 3.1.0). All complex structures were subjected to unrestrained MD simulations on Amber22 package. The complex was modeled using the tleap module in Amber22. The Amber ff19SB force field was used for the protein. The OL15 and OL3 force fields were used for DNA and RNA, respectively. All systems were neutralized by adding Na+ or Cl− counterions and solvated in rectangular boxes filled with explicit water molecules. The system was solvated by OPC water in a 10-Å cuboid box. Energy minimization was first performed for 2,000 steps using the steepest descent method and subsequently for 3,000 steps with the conjugate gradient algorithm, while keeping the positions fixed. Long-range electrostatic interactions were efficiently handled using the particle mesh Ewald method. The system was subsequently heated to 300 K under constant volume for 50 ps, after which a 50-ps constant-pressure equilibration was performed using a Langevin thermostat at 300 K. The SHAKE algorithm was applied to constrain all covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms. The cutoff was set to 8.0 Å for the electrostatic and van der Waals interactions. Finally, a 500-ns production simulation was performed at 300 K with no positional restraints. The root-mean-square deviation and fluctuation values were calculated using the cpptraj module of Amber22.

ωRNA integrity evaluation

First, 100 μl of IscB–ωRNA complex was mixed with an equal volume (1:1, v/v) of phenol, chloroform and isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) (Sigma-Aldrich). The mixture was vortexed for 60 s and incubated on ice for 5 min, before centrifuging at 13,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The upper aqueous phase was separated and run on 12% urea–PAGE. The gel was stained with 0.01% Ultra GelRed (Vazyme) solution and visualized using an iBright imaging system (Invitrogen).

PEM-seq off-target analysis

Genome-wide off-target analysis was performed following the PEM-seq protocol as previously described17. Genomic DNA (20 μg) was extracted from the samples of IscB-edited HEK293T cells and then fragmented into 300–700-bp fragments using a Covaris sonicator. The DNA fragments were tagged with biotin at their 5′ ends through a single round of biotinylated primer extension. After removing residual primers with AMPure XP beads and purifying with streptavidin beads, the ssDNA bound to the streptavidin beads was ligated to a bridge adaptor containing a 14-bp random molecular barcode. Subsequently, nested PCR was performed to amplify and enrich DNA fragments harboring bait double-strand break events and tagged with Illumina adaptor sequences. The prepared sequencing library was sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using 150-bp paired-end reads. PEM-seq data analysis was conducted using the PEM-Q pipeline with default parameters.
