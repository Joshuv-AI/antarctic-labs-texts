Mapping the TNFR2-targeting antibody patent landscape: Insights from macro trends to structural signatures
Authors: Shiyun Chen, Yibo Chen, Yexuan Zhang, Gengyuan Yao, Hong Cai, Xin Chen, Yuanjia Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPharmacological modulation of TNFR2 function has emerged as an effective strategy for modulating immune responses in cancer and inflammatory diseases. Both academic and industrial efforts have pursued therapeutic approaches targeting TNFR2, leading to a substantial accumulation of patents. Beyond documenting translational activity, these patents disclose molecular and structural design features that are often overlooked in academic literature, yet remain insufficiently integrated into systematic analyses. In this study, 3,291 TNFR2-related patents indexed in the Derwent database were first categorized by therapeutic strategy. Structural analyses were performed to characterize binding interfaces and epitope features of patented TNFR2-targeting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) using AlphaFold3, alongside comparative analyses of complementarity-determining region (CDR) sequences to identify conserved motifs and physicochemical properties distinguishing agonists from antagonists. Results show a strategic shift from soluble TNFR2-based approaches toward receptor-targeting biologics, with mAbs now dominating. Thus, structural features of antibodies emerge as a central element for understanding TNFR2-related patent design strategies. Structural mapping classified TNFR2-targeting antibodies into five archetypal binding modes, delineating distinct agonistic and antagonistic epitope hotspots. Sequence analysis demonstrated conserved amino acid positions within CDRs, and antagonist antibodies exhibited a significantly higher frequency of acidic residues, particularly glutamate, in the CDR-H3 loop, suggesting a role for electrostatic complementarity in receptor inhibition. While this analysis relies on predicted rather than experimentally resolved structures, it delineates clear structure-function trends. It provides a forward-looking, structure-informed framework to guide the rational design and optimization of TNFR2-directed antibodies. These insights will shape the next generation of more precise and effective immunotherapies.

Introduction

Now there is compelling evidence that tumor necrosis factor receptor II (TNFR2) is a key modulator of immune responses. TNFR2 exhibits restricted expression, primarily found on specific immune cell subsets.1 Notably, TNFR2 is highly expressed on CD4+ Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), marking a subset with potent suppressive activity.2,3 Furthermore, TNF-TNFR2 signaling pathway plays a decisive role in the activation, proliferative expansion, immunosuppressive function, and phenotypic stability of Tregs.4,5 The TNF-TNFR2 axis was also reportedly attributable to the function of immunosuppressive cell populations, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs),6,7 which supports the notion that TNFR2 plays a pivotal role in the activation of a broad spectrum of suppressive immune cells. Therefore, TNFR2 agonism or TNFR2 antagonism has emerged as a promising strategy to modulate the immune responses. TNFR2 agonists have been shown to alleviate the autoimmune inflammatory responses by enhancing Treg activation and expansion in vivo,8–10 while TNFR2 antagonists significantly inhibited tumor growth through reduction of tumor-infiltrating Treg and enhancement of CD8 T cell function.11–17

Building on these preclinical results, seven TNFR2-targeting mAbs, including both agonists and antagonists, have entered clinical trials (Supplementary Table 1). BI-1808 was the first TNFR2-targeting antibody to advance into clinical development and has received both Orphan Drug Designation and Fast Track Designation by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of T-cell lymphoma, highlighting the therapeutic potential of TNFR2 antagonists in hematologic malignancies.18 In contrast, the TNFR2 agonist antibodies are being evaluated for solid tumor or inflammatory diseases. HFB200301 is the first TNFR2 agonistic antibody to enter clinical trials (NCT05287001), aiming to activate CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells to enhance anti-tumor immunity.19 In addition, BioInvent is developing TNFR2 agonistic antibody (BI-1910) for cancer immunotherapy.20 Importantly, although agonistic antibodies have shown promise in oncology, their development for autoimmune diseases remains limited, with only one agonistic antibody (TRB-061) having recently advanced to a Phase 1 clinical trial. Preclinical studies suggest that TNFR2 agonists alleviated inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis,9 multiple sclerosis,21,22 and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)8 by promoting the activation and expansion of Tregs. Representative candidates in development include NKTR-0165 and TRB-061 (Supplementary Table 1). Despite the preliminary results being encouraging, the seemingly contradictory effects and underlying mechanisms of TNFR2 agonists in the treatment of autoimmune diseases and cancer remain to be elucidated. Previous studies have demonstrated that the epitopes of TNFR2 agonistic antibodies are mainly located in the CRD1 and CRD2 regions of the TNFR2 protein.23,24 Analyses of the interactions between TNFR2 agonistic antibodies and TNFR2 indicate that these antibodies can stabilize TNFR2 in an active conformation without interfering with TNF binding.23 In contrast, TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies predominantly bind to the CRD3 and CRD4 domains, thereby locking TNFR2 in an inactive conformation.25 Accordingly, TNFR2 agonistic and antagonistic Fabs may exhibit distinct epitope preferences. A systematic analysis of the epitope distribution of TNFR2 antibodies may facilitate the discovery of novel mechanisms for modulating TNFR2 function.

Patents serve as a critical complement to academic literature, often disclosing detailed molecular and structural information not fully captured in publications. Such information may contain underexplored design principles that could offer valuable insights into antibody molecular features. Based on these considerations, we reason that a systematic analysis of patents may help reveal the molecular determinants of the function of the antigen-binding fragment (Fab) of TNFR2 antibody. Integrating these data with computational tools enables the construction of epitope maps and complementarity-determining region (CDR) profiles, supporting more rational antibody design.

From a broader perspective, a comprehensive understanding of TNFR2 patents holds significant scientific and commercial value. This study aims to provide the first multidimensional mapping of the global TNFR2-related patent landscape from temporal, geographic, and technological viewpoints. The findings are expected to support technology evolution and industrial progress while alleviating information asymmetry among stakeholders. Meanwhile, this work also illustrates how patent data can support drug discovery and provide a framework applicable to other therapeutic targets.

Methods

Data

This study utilized the Derwent Innovation platform, a global provider of enhanced patent documents, to obtain a patent dataset dealing with TNFR2-related inventions. A comprehensive patent search was conducted on November 27, 2024, retrieving patents with publication dates prior to this date. The full name, abbreviation, or synonym were entered into the database’s patent CTB (title, abstract, claims) field. The search formula applied was CTB = (TNFBR OR TNFR80 OR CD120b OR TNFR2 OR Tumor ADJ Necrosis ADJ Factor ADJ Receptor ADJ Superfamily ADJ Member ADJ 1B OR Tumor ADJ Necrosis ADJ Factor ADJ Receptor ADJ Type ADJ II OR Tumor ADJ Necrosis ADJ Factor ADJ Receptor ADJ 2 OR P80 ADJ TNF-Alpha ADJ Receptor OR TNFRII OR P75 ADJ TNF ADJ Receptor OR Etanercept OR TNFR1B OR TBPII). All retrieved records were exported in the native Derwent Innovation database format for subsequent analysis. A total of 24,124 patents were identified and further processed following the Reporting Items for Patent Landscapes (RIPL) statement,26 with reference to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The unrelated and duplicated patents, as well as the patents that could not be obtained, were removed. Ultimately, we collected 3291 patents involving 644 patent families for the macro study (Figure 1), of which 533 patent documents (124 patent families) were further used in the agent-based micro study. Network analysis

Collaboration and citation dynamics within the TNFR2-related patent landscape were analyzed using Gephi 0.10.1. Separate networks were constructed for assignee collaborations and patent citations, with nodes representing assignees or patent documents and edges denoting co-assignment relationships or citation links. Network-level and node-level metrics, including out-degree (number of outgoing edges) and in-degree (number of incoming edges), were calculated to characterize cooperative intensity and intellectual connectivity. Community structures were identified using the modularity algorithm to reveal clusters of closely connected assignees or patents. Network layouts were generated using the ForceAtlas2 algorithm to facilitate visualization and interpretation, after removing isolated nodes (degree = 0) to improve clarity.

Patent selection for structural analysis

Patents describing TNFR2-targeting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with publicly disclosed sequences of either or both heavy and light chains were selected for antibody-antigen interaction prediction. Only patents containing full or partial sequence information (e.g., either the full chain or key regions such as CDRs) were included in the analysis. Patents lacking any sequence information were excluded. The resulting subset of patents was then used as input for downstream structural modeling.

Antibody-antigen interaction prediction

The Alphafold3 online tool (https://alphafoldserver.com/) was employed to predict the binding between biologically active antibody sequences (heavy and light chains or heavy chain only) derived from patents targeting TNFR2 and the TNFR2 protein. The prediction exhibiting the highest predicted interface template modeling score (i-ptm) was selected for subsequent analysis and statistics. The interface between the antibody sequences and TNFR2 was visualized by Pymol. Amino acid residues within the TNFR2 protein sequence that were located within 4 Ångstroms (Å) of this binding interface were defined as the antigenic epitope. Chiplot was used to statistically analyze the distribution of these epitope residues (https://www.chiplot.online/). Based on the distribution patterns of antigenic epitopes observed for different types of antibodies on TNFR2, we further utilized PyMOL to map and visualize these epitopes onto the TNFR2 protein structure (PDB ID: 3ALQ).

Isoelectric point (pI) prediction

The isoelectric point (pI) of antibody antigen-binding fragments (either heavy and light chains combined, or heavy chain alone) was determined using the online ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam). The difference in pI of TNFR2 agonistic antibody and TNFR2 antagonistic antibody was compared by Student’s T-test. It should be noted that this computational estimate does not account for structural context or potential post-translational modifications.

CDR sequence analysis

The CDR sequences of TNFR2 antibodies were annotated according to the Kabat numbering scheme. A multiple sequence alignment of all CDR sequences was then conducted using MEGA11 (Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis, version 11). Position-specific amino acid frequencies in CDRs were statistically compared between agonistic and antagonistic antibody groups through computational frequency analysis. Beyond the frequency analysis of individual amino acids, all residues were classified into five functional categories: polar, nonpolar, acidic, basic, and aromatic, which were based on the physicochemical characteristics of their side chains. The comprehensive classification framework is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analysis

The predicted antigen epitopes were mapped to 4 cysteine-rich domains (CRDs) located on the extracellular domain of TNFR2. The epitope frequency for each antibody was quantified separately for CRD1, CRD2, CRD3, CRD4, and the loop region. To characterize representative patterns of epitope distribution, agonistic and antagonistic antibodies were independently clustered using the K-means algorithm. Before clustering, the regional epitope counts for each antibody were normalized to reflect their relative frequency within the total epitopes of that antibody. The silhouette coefficient was employed to evaluate the clustering results’ consistency and validity and determine the optimal number of clusters. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5).

Results

Temporal trend and geographic location

To map the temporal trends and geographical distribution of TNFR2-related patents, we identified 3,291 patents belonging to 644 patent families after removing irrelevant records (Figure 1). As shown in Figure 2(A), patent activity in the early 2000s remained relatively low. Since 2010, however, filings have risen markedly, increasing from 72 in 2010 to 209 in 2015. This period coincided temporally with the expiration of core patent (WO2000062790) covering the TNFR2 fusion protein etanercept, which may have facilitated biosimilar development and increased downstream patenting activity. After 2016, the number of TNFR2-focused patents continued to grow steadily, reflecting evolving therapeutic strategies and a new phase of innovation in this field. Technology evolution and milestones

TNFR2-related patents can be classified into six technological categories (Figure 4(A), Supplementary Figure 1). Patents involving soluble TNFR2 and etanercept dominate the landscape (62.8%), followed by TNFR2-targeted therapeutic agents (16.2%) and disease diagnostics (7.5%). As one of the most successful anti-inflammatory biologics, etanercept has generated extensive patents since commercialization, covering manufacturing, indication expansion, and efficacy prediction. Its core patent expiry in Europe in 2015 triggered a peak in biosimilar-related filings, followed by a gradual decline. In contrast, since 2016, filings for TNFR2-targeted therapeutics have risen sharply and, for the first time in 2022, surpassed those related to etanercept (Figure 4(B)). This shift highlights a transition from classical soluble TNF antagonists toward next-generation TNFR2-directed drugs as the main innovation focus. Figure 4.Technology categories and temporal trends. A timeline of highly cited patents further illustrates the field’s evolution (Figure 5(B)). Before 2015, influential patents were mainly centered on etanercept, such as WO200062790 from Amgen, involving psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis treatments. After 2015, however, TNFR2-targeted drug patents gained prominence. The most influential core patent family is WO201687068 from Mass General Brigham, which discloses a TNFR2 antagonistic antibody for cancer therapy and is widely regarded as the first publicly reported TNFR2 antagonistic antibody patent. It has been cited by 14 other patent families, reflecting strong technological impact. Additional influential patents include WO2017220711 and WO2021055253, both related to antagonistic antibodies. In contrast, WO2020180712 is the only patent that reports a TNFR2 agonistic antibody exhibiting antitumor activity. Overall, technological innovation in the TNFR2 field has followed a clear iterative trajectory which shifted from soluble receptors such as etanercept to emerging antibody-based therapeutics. Patent citation evolution provides valuable insight into innovation hotspots and future directions for TNFR2-targeted drug development.

Biological activity of patented TNFR2-targeting antibody

To comprehensively summarize and quantify the biological activities of patented antibodies, we analyzed the modes of interaction between the antibodies and TNFR2 based on the descriptions provided in the patent specifications. Antibodies were classified as agonistic or antagonistic according to whether they competitively bind TNFR2 with TNF and their resulting in vitro activities, with the classification primarily based on Fab-mediated activity. Based on these criteria, 25 sequences were categorized as agonistic antibodies and 20 sequences as antagonistic antibodies. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, most antibodies have been validated for in-vivo activity. Moreover, the development of human TNFR2 knock-in mouse models has enabled the evaluation of in vivo efficacy of TNFR2 antibodies. Among these, 13 TNFR2-targeting antibodies exhibited antitumor activity in human TNFR2 knock-in mice, predominantly in subcutaneous murine colon carcinoma models. Activation of CD8 T cells was observed with both agonistic and antagonistic antibodies. Interestingly, consistent with previous reports, TNFR2 agonistic antibodies did not promote Treg expansion. In addition, a TNFR2 antibody described in WO2020041361 was shown to inhibit the growth of SW480 subcutaneous tumors in nude mice, indicating that targeting TNFR2 expressed on tumor cells represents an additional mechanism for tumor growth suppression, in line with recent findings.27 In contrast to tumor models, only two agonistic antibodies were reported to exhibit anti-inflammatory activity (WO2024151515, WO2023228082). Although both antibodies adopt a tetravalent format, the underlying mechanisms appear to differ. Consistent with recent studies, WO2024151515 demonstrated that a tetravalent TNFR2 agonistic single-domain antibody markedly enhanced Treg activation and proliferation in a human TNFR2 knock-in mouse model of arthritis. In contrast, WO2023228082 reported that, in a GvHD model established in NOG-SCID mice, the therapeutic effect of a tetravalent TNFR2 agonistic antibody was mainly associated with the induction of CD8 T cell exhaustion without significantly altering Treg frequencies. Although previous studies have indicated that Tregs are indispensable for TNFR2 agonist mediated amelioration of GvHD,28,29 this patent suggests that TNFR2 agonists may also suppress autoimmune inflammation through additional mechanisms.

Taken together, these findings indicate that patented TNFR2-targeting antibodies display context-dependent biological activities, with both agonistic and antagonistic profiles associated with antitumor efficacy, while exhibiting divergent immunomodulatory effects in inflammatory disease models.

IgG conjugation

Although the Fc region does not directly participate in antigen binding, it plays a critical role in determining antibody function. Therapeutic antibodies are primarily engineered with IgG1, IgG2, or IgG4 Fc domains, or with certain mutants that preferentially bind specific Fcγ receptors (FcγRs).30 IgG1 exhibits strong binding to FcγRs and complement, mediating effector functions such as antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cell phagocytosis (ADCP), and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC). Therefore, TNFR2 antibodies with an IgG1 Fc can further induce depletion of Treg cells. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, most antibodies entering clinical trials are IgG1-conjugated. Interestingly, TNFR2 agonistic antibodies are also commonly IgG1-conjugated, likely because bivalent antibodies require Fc-FcγR interactions to cluster TNFR2 on target cells for full activation.31 However, since tumor-infiltrating Treg cells still highly express TNFR2, the primary effect of IgG1-conjugated TNFR2 agonists may remain depletion of TNFR2-expressing cells especially for Tregs. While few of the patents specified the biological activity of anti-TNFR2 mAbs with different IgG conjugation. WO2020089474 compared the antitumor effects of a TNFR2 agonistic antibody (5A05) and an antagonistic antibody (3F10) across different IgG backbones. The results showed that both 5A05 and 3F10 achieved the strongest antitumor activity when conjugated to either mIgG1 or mIgG2. When 5A05 was conjugated to an IgG lacking Fc-binding activity, it still showed a moderate antitumor effect. In contrast, 3F10 largely lost its activity under the same conditions.

Epitope distribution

Given that over 60% of TNFR2-targeted therapeutics were mAbs, we further systematically analyzed structural signatures of mAbs and their corresponding antigen epitopes within the dataset, aiming to elucidate structure-function relationships and underlying mechanisms of action. From a total of 3,291 analyzed patents, we identified 45 biologically active TNFR2-targeting antibodies (Supplementary Table 2).

TNFR2 could be divided into CRD1, CRD2, CRD3, and CRD4 in its extracellular domain, with CRD1 containing the pre-ligand assembly domain (PLAD) that was essential for TNFR2 trimerization on the cell surface.32 CRD2 and CRD3 directly mediate TNF binding.33 WO2017040312 previously proposed that antibodies binding to the KCSPG motif (aa 42–46) but not the KCRPG motif (aa 142–146) within TNFR2 were agonistic antibodies, whereas those binding to KCRPG but not KCSPG were antagonistic antibodies. However, this rule was not universally applicable, as exemplified by TNFR2 agonistic antibody developed by Bioinvent (WO2020089473-5A05) or Pfizer (WO2023228082-TF2053), which did not bind to KCSPG located in CRD1.

Since the majority of patents did not specify the epitopes of TNFR2 antibodies, we utilized AlphaFold3, a tool that revolutionizes the prediction of protein structure and protein-protein interaction, to analyze the interface (intermolecular distance <4 Å) between patented mAbs and TNFR2.34 The prediction results were presented in Supplementary Figure 2A-2AS. Based on AlphaFold3 predictions, we mapped the epitope distribution of each antibody in Figure 6(A). By analyzing the amino acid distribution of antibody-TNFR2 binding interfaces across CRD domains, we performed K-means clustering to group all TNFR2 mAbs into five distinct epitope-based types: two agonist classes and three antagonist classes. The average silhouette coefficients for agonists (0.53) and antagonists (0.42) indicated moderate clustering consistency, suggesting that these groupings capture recurrent but not strictly discrete epitope patterns (Supplementary Figure 3A–B). To identify the characteristic epitope of these five TNFR2-targeting antibodies, we summarized the frequency distribution in Figure 6(B), and their spatial localization within the TNFR2 structure is illustrated in Figure 7(A). As shown in Figure 6(B), TNFR2 agonistic antibodies exhibited distinct epitope distribution patterns, with one class predominantly bound to the CRD1-CRD2 interface and another targeted the CRD3-CRD4 regions. TNFR2 Agonist-1 demonstrated high-frequency epitopes similar to those described in WO2017040312, where most antibodies engaged the KCSPG motif (aa 42–46). Notably, Agonist-1 showed higher binding frequency to residue W87 than to KCSPG itself, suggesting that W87 served as a potential anchoring role. These antibodies primarily bound CRD1 and CRD2 without blocking TNF-TNFR2 interaction. Instead, they stabilized TNFR2 in an active conformation through PLAD engagement, thereby potentiating TNF-TNFR2 complex stability and signaling.23 In sharp contrast, Agonist-2 predominantly targeted CRD3, with some epitopes extending into CRD4 (Figures 6 and 7). However, TNFR2 Agonist-2 did not interfere with the binding of TNF-TNFR2 either. In addition, despite overlapping CRD3 and CRD4 localization between Agonist-2 and TNFR2 Antagonist-3, there was also a key difference in their epitope distribution. Agonist-2 exhibited significantly lower KCRPG motif (aa 142–146) binding frequency than Antagonist-3. This demonstrated that agonistic and antagonistic antibodies may adopt distinct interfacial features.

Compared to agonists, the majority of TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies blocked TNF-TNFR2 interaction (Supplementary Table 2). The predictive results from Alphafold3 also supported the notion that the epitopes of TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies were predominantly located on CRD2 and CRD3. As shown in Figure 6(A,B), TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies were stratified into three different classes. TNFR2 Antagonist-3 exhibited high-frequency epitope clustering at the KCRPG motif (aa 142–146), consistent with the antagonistic antibody characteristics described in WO2017040312 (Figures 6 and 7). In contrast, the other two classes of TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies did not prefer to bind the KCRPG motif. The epitope distribution of TNFR2 Antagonist-1 was mainly located on CRD1 and CRD2, which shared similarity with TNFR2 Agonist-1. TNFR2 Antagonist-1 also had Q131 as a characteristic epitope compared with TNFR2 Agonist-1 and other TNFR2-targeting mAbs (Figure 6(B)). Moreover, TNFR2 Antagonist-2 demonstrated preferential binding to the aa125–139 region. These findings revealed that the interaction of TNF-TNFR2 could be blocked through distinct structural mechanisms. However, 161-hVH5-48 appeared to be a unique TNFR2 antagonistic antibody, as it was demonstrated that this TNFR2-targeting VHH did not interfere with TNF-TNFR2 interaction (WO2023103962). This property of 161-hVH5-48 was reflected by its unique epitope predicted by Alphafold3 (Supplementary Figure 2AH).

Overall, the AlphaFold3 prediction of epitopes for TNFR2-targeting antibodies provides a computationally derived framework. As antibody design continues to advance, the relationship between epitope features and antibody function is becoming clearer. Analyzing patent data can provide valuable insights that further refine our understanding of antibody design optimization. However, these computational results still require experimental validation, especially in comparison with existing immunological data.

CDR characteristics

The antigen-binding domain of conventional mAbs consists of light and heavy chains, with antigen recognition primarily determined by CDR-L1-3 and CDR-H1-3. CDR-H3 exhibits the highest sequence variability and contributes most significantly to antigen-antibody interactions.35 Analysis of CDR sequences can reveal conserved patterns, aiding in the design and optimization of TNFR2-targeting mAbs.

We further characterized the amino acid features of the CDR-H and CDR-L regions in the 45 biologically active TNFR2 antibodies, including 20 TNFR2 agonistic antibodies and 25 TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies (Figure 8(A,B)). To facilitate this analysis, the 20 essential human amino acids (AAs) were classified into five categories based on the chemical properties of their side chains (R groups): acidic, basic, aromatic, polar, and nonpolar (Supplementary Table 3). Notably, high-frequency residues in both TNFR2 agonistic and antagonistic antibodies may not directly mediate TNFR2-specific binding. For instance, the first two residues in CDRH1 and the last two residues in CDRH3 most frequently adopted the DY motif across both antibody classes. Similar patterns were previously observed in HER2 antibodies.36 Predictions from AlphaFold3 further corroborate this notion (Supplementary Figure 2A-2AS). The enrichment of acidic amino acids in CDR-H3 appeared to be a distinguishing structural signature of TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies compared to agonistic antibodies (Figure 8). AlphaFold3 predictions further revealed that while the terminal aspartic acid in CDRH3 lay outside the TNFR2-binding interface, other aspartic or glutamic acid residues within CDR-H3 predominantly localized to interaction hotspots. This suggested that electrostatic forces played a critical role in the specific recognition of TNFR2 by antagonistic antibodies. Notably, a sequence spanning the CRD3 and adjacent loop regions (aa130-aa144) harbored five basic amino acids (K130, R135, R141, K142, R144), which coincided with the high-frequency epitope regions targeted by TNFR2 antagonist 2 and antagonist 3. On the other hand, the increased prevalence of acidic residues in CDRs lowered the isoelectric point (pI) of TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies (TNFR2 agonistic antibody and TNFR2 antagonistic antibody: 7.47 ± 1.29 vs. 6.19 ± 1.24, p = 0.0017). Analysis of TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies revealed that 17 of 25 sequences exhibited pI values below 7 (Supplementary Table 2). Such low pI may compromise target-binding specificity and promote aggregation due to imbalanced charge distribution. Consequently, antibody engineering to optimize charge profiles or formulation adjustments may be necessary to ensure therapeutic efficacy.

These results reveal differences in the CDR sequences and amino acid composition between TNFR2 agonistic and antagonistic antibodies, with the enrichment of acidic residues in CDR-H3 and lower pI values being characteristic of antagonistic antibodies. The optimization of antibody design has driven the evolution of patents, and data-driven analysis has facilitated a deeper understanding of how CDR characteristics correlate with antibody functionality. The results also offer potential pathways for refining the direction of antibody design.

Discussion

This study systematically analyzed 3,291 TNFR2-related patents, uncovering a strategic shift in therapeutic approaches in the TNFR2-related translational research and therapeutic development. Patent data indicate a transition from early strategies by globally inhibiting TNF, exemplified by agents such as etanercept, toward the rational design of mAbs with target specificity against TNFR2. Building on the molecular characteristics disclosed in these patents, we constructed functional epitope maps and CDR amino acid feature profiles for TNFR2-targeting antibodies, thereby delineating the phenotypic molecular characteristics of distinct antibody classes. These findings provide important insights into the mechanistic underpinnings and structure-function relationships of TNFR2-targeting antibodies. The following discussion will elaborate on the implications of these findings.

Evolution of TNFR2 patents: from etanercept to TNFR2-targeted therapeutics

The TNFR2 patent landscape has shifted markedly from a historical dominance of etanercept-related technologies toward innovation in direct TNFR2-targeted therapeutics. This evolution likely reflects deeper insights into TNFR2 biology and its expanding therapeutic potential. While traditional pharmaceutical companies largely maintain established TNF-neutralizing strategies. Amgen continues benefiting from its core etanercept intellectual property, others such as Roche are exploring modulation of TNFR2 splice variants via oligonucleotides. Yet these approaches still indirectly affect TNFR2 signaling and have not fully transitioned into next-generation mechanisms. In contrast, a smaller group of organizations, including Mass General Brigham and Roche, hold more substantial portfolios focused specifically on TNFR2-targeting agents. Bioinvent is emerging as a notable player with its antagonistic antibody BI-1808, which received FDA Orphan Drug and Fast Track Designations in 2025; however, its patent foundation remains limited due to its later entry and specialized focus in cancer immunotherapy. Mass General Brigham, by comparison, has developed a broader pipeline targeting Treg expansion, autoimmune disorders, and diagnostics, positioning it as the frontrunner in technological influence. Patent timing also plays a key role in industrial engagement: filings began to accelerate in 2016 and peaked in 2021, aligning with the first clinical trial initiation and reflecting a typical translation gap. Thus, the caution of major pharmaceutical companies appears to be strategic rather than driven by scientific uncertainty. It should be noted, however, that conclusions drawn solely from patent trends are inherently limited. Patents may not capture all ongoing research or early-stage innovation, and their number may only partly reflect innovation quality.

Globally, Western institutions still dominate TNFR2 patent output, yet China is rapidly advancing in clinical translation. Of seven ongoing global clinical trials involving TNFR2-targeting antibodies, three are led by Chinese companies, demonstrating strong development capabilities despite relatively limited early-stage IP portfolios. Meanwhile, growing investment from industry leaders – such as Pfizer’s TNFR2 agonistic antibody TF2053 and its collaborative partnerships with Eli Lilly and Johnson & Johnson on TRexBio programs, indicates that the field is entering a more active stage of therapeutic development. Overall, TNFR2-targeted innovation is progressing from exploratory research toward tangible clinical realization, with increasing stakeholder involvement across both academic and industrial sectors.

Progress in the development of TNFR2-targeted therapeutics

Utilization of TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies to eliminate tumor-infiltrating immunosuppressive Tregs forms a cornerstone of their scientific rationale for cancer immunotherapy.37,38 As shown in Supplementary Table 2, majority of patents for TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies demonstrate biological activity through their capacity to suppress TNF-induced Treg proliferation. Clinical trial data further validate these findings, revealing significant Treg depletion in cancer patients treated with TNFR2 antagonists. These results underscore the pivotal role of recent advances in understanding TNFR2-mediated immune regulation in driving therapeutic development. Currently, TNFR2 antagonistic antibodies remain in early-phase clinical trials, with further studies needed to identify their optimal indications. Notably, preclinical synergy between TNFR2 antagonists and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in animal models has spurred multiple clinical trials evaluating combination regimens (e.g., NCT05781386, NCT04752826).12,16,39–41 Positive outcomes from such combination studies may catalyze the development of TNFR2-targeting bispecific antibodies, potentially yielding agents with superior antitumor efficacy. To date, several bispecific antibody designs based on TNFR2 antibodies have been reported. For example, TNFR2 agonistic antibodies can be combined with other agonistic antibodies or ligands from the TNFRSF family to generate bispecific molecules, thereby further enhancing T cell costimulation in target cells (WO2019129644, WO2024037626). In addition, given the synergistic antitumor effects observed in preclinical models, bispecific antibody patents which target TNFR2/PD-1, TNFR2/PD-L1 or TNFR2/CCR8 have emerged (WO2024037628, CN117586411A, CN118638246A).

The clinical application of TNFR2 agonists diverges from their fundamental research profile. In preclinical murine models, TNFR2 agonists are predominantly associated with their efficacy in the treatment of autoimmune diseases.1 Paradoxically, the most advanced TNFR2 agonists in clinical development are being evaluated for their capacity in cancer immunotherapy. In preclinical studies, TNFR2 agonists are predominantly based on multivalent constructs derived from TNF mutants that are specifically engineered to bind and activate TNFR2, and these constructs typically lack FcγR-binding functionality. In contrast, TNFR2 agonistic antibody currently in clinical trials are conventional bivalent mAbs with IgG1 conjugation, whose structural and biological basis for TNFR2 activation is fundamentally different from that of the TNFR2 agonists used in basic research. Moreover, the in vivo biological effects of mAbs, particularly IgG1-based antibodies, result from the combined actions of both the Fab and Fc regions. Given the markedly high expression of TNFR2 on tumor-infiltrating Treg cells and the relatively lower expression on effector cells, IgG1-coupled TNFR2 agonistic antibodies may simultaneously function as Treg depletors and effector T cell activators.42 In addition, given the broad expression of TNFR2 across diverse immune cell types and its capacity to activate both effector cells (e.g., NK cells and CD8 T cells) and immunosuppressive cells (e.g., Tregs and MDSCs), this pleiotropic activity represents another important factor contributing to the disconnect between findings from basic research and outcomes observed in clinical studies. This dual activity may help explain the disconnect between preclinical findings and clinical outcomes observed for TNFR2 agonists.

Novel agonist design strategies

The development of agonists targeting the TNFRSF (Tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily) members remains a significant challenge in current drug discovery.43 The in vivo activity of conventional bivalent agonistic antibodies is influenced by multiple factors, including IgG subclass, concentration, and binding affinity complexities likely rooted in the unique activation mechanisms of TNFRSF members.44,45 Under physiological conditions, TNFRSF receptors require engagement with their endogenous trimeric ligands (e.g., TNF-α) to initiate downstream signaling.33 In contrast, conventional bivalent antibodies can only bind two receptor molecules, creating suboptimal spatial geometries that complicate receptor activation. Consequently, designing TNFR2 agonists that align with its structural activation logic is critical. Emerging strategies focus on multivalent architectures. For instance, tethering multiple TNFR2-binding peptides or antibody fragments to synthetic oligomerization domains has shown promising results, although this study does not provide a detailed discussion of the important field.46,47 Patents describing multivalent TNF mutants engineered for exclusive TNFR2 binding demonstrate potent TNFR2 activation and Treg proliferation, constituting a major innovation in TNFR2-targeted peptide therapeutics (WO2018185247, WO2020260368, WO2018226750, etc). Notably, recent studies reveal that multivalent TNFR2 agonistic antibodies activate TNFR2 signaling independent of Fc-mediated crosslinking.48,49 This is exemplified in patents WO2022207921, WO2024151515 and WO2023228082. These findings position multivalent TNFR2 antibodies as superior agonistic candidates. Furthermore, in comparison with conventional antibodies, introduction of multivalent single domain antibody will further simplify the manufacture and enhance the stability of multivalent TNFR2 agonist.49 In fact, similar designs have already been reported in the development of multivalent single-domain antibodies targeting O×40 and DR5.50,51

Molecular characteristics of TNFR2 mAbs

Our study investigated the molecular basis of TNFR2-targeting mAbs, particularly focusing on CDR sequences. By systematically analyzing the CDR sequences of 45 patented TNFR2 antibodies, we found that several amino acid positions within the CDRs displayed a high degree of conservation across antibodies, regardless of their target antigen. These conserved residues are unlikely to be central to antigen recognition per se but instead serve to maintain the structural integrity of the CDR loops by stabilizing their framework architecture. This structural conservation imposes a functional constraint on CDR sequence variability, which may in turn streamline the antibody engineering process by narrowing the design space, potentially reducing optimization complexity and development costs. Moreover, this structural mapping uncovered recurrent “hotspots” that were consistently targeted by multiple antibodies with similar functional outcomes. These epitope fingerprints provide rational design templates that could be leveraged for the development of new therapeutic candidates. The identification of such hotspots holds promise for computational antibody engineering. For example, generative models like RFdiffusion52,53 or other AI-driven protein design tools can be guided by these structural blueprints to generate de novo TNFR2-targeting antibodies or peptides with high affinity and desired agonistic or antagonistic activity. This approach shifts the paradigm from random screening or empirical affinity maturation toward rational, epitope-centric antibody design. In summary, systematic analysis of the rich antibody sequence information contained in patents, together with AI-driven structural predictions and evidence from animal models and clinical studies, may substantially reduce the cost and time required for drug development. However, it should be noticed that current computational predictions remain constrained by limited TNFR2-specific structural data. In addition, sequences derived from patents should be interpreted with caution, as they often represent early-stage designs. Accordingly, experimental validation remains necessary to confirm functional relevance and translational potential.

Conclusion

Our comprehensive patent analysis of TNFR2 therapeutics has delineated precise technological trajectories within this field. The development of TNFR2-targeted drugs has undergone rapid advancement in recent years, with a notable shift in driving forces from large multinational corporations to academic institutions and biotech companies. During this transition, foundational scientific discoveries have catalyzed industrial drug development. However, it should be acknowledged that patent-based analyses remain inherently constrained by the selective disclosure of innovations. Notably, a disconnect remains between the preclinical findings and clinical efficacy of TNFR2 agonists, which may be attributable to the complex and intertwined roles mediated by the Fab and Fc domains of anti-TNFR2 mAbs. Furthermore, TNFR2 may also have a bidirectional role in regulating immune responses. Therefore, systematic investigation of the immunomodulatory effects of TNFR2 agonists in patients with cancer or autoimmune diseases remains essential to inform their future clinical development and application.

Looking ahead, by integrating cross-disciplinary validation methods, such as computer simulations, in vitro and in vivo models, and standardized assays to assess TNFR2 antibody functionality, AI-driven approaches can enhance the precision of therapeutic development. This integrated strategy is expected to streamline the transition from preclinical research to clinical application, ultimately accelerating the discovery of next-generation therapeutics.
