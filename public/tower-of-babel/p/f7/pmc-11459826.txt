Comparative analysis of codon usage patterns in 16 chloroplast genomes of suborder Halimedineae
Authors: Jiao Fang, Yuquan Hu, Zhangfeng Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Halimedineae are marine green macroalgae that play crucial roles as primary producers in various habitats, including coral reefs, rocky shores, embayments, lagoons, and seagrass beds. Several tropical species have calcified thalli, which contribute significantly to the formation of coral reefs. In this study, we investigated the codon usage patterns and the main factors influencing codon usage bias in 16 chloroplast genomes of the suborder Halimedineae. Nucleotide composition analysis revealed that the codons of these species were enriched in A/U bases and preferred to end in A/U bases, and the distribution of GC content followed a trend of GC1 > GC2 > GC3. 30 optimal codons encoding 17 amino acids were identified, and most of the optimal codons and all of the over-expressed codons preferentially ended with A/U. The neutrality plot, effective number of codons (ENc) plot, and parity rule 2 (PR2) plot analysis indicated that natural selection played a major role in shaping codon usage bias of the most Halimedineae species. The genetic relationships based on their RSCU values and chloroplast protein-coding genes showed the closely related species have similar codon usage patterns. This study describes, for the first time, the codon usage patterns and characterization of Halimedineae chloroplast genomes, and provides new insights into the evolution of this suborder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10825-x.Keywords: Halimedineae, Codon usage bias, Chloroplast genome, Natural selection

Introduction

Halimedineae, a suborder of Bryopsidales (Chlorophyta), consists of several families including Caulerpaceae, Dichotomosiphonaceae, Halimedaceae, Pseudocodiaceae, Rhipiliaceae, and Udoteaceae [1–3]. Halimedineae are siphonous green algae characterized by heteroplastidic thalli (with both chloroplasts and amyloplasts) [4]. These algae play a crucial ecological role in marine ecosystems, primarily through their contribution to primary production and the regulation of carbonate fluxes [5–7]. Some calcified species contribute significantly to the formation of coral reefs, which are unique marine landscapes [7–11]. In addition, the taxa also include notorious invasive species such as Caulerpa taxifolia and Caulerpa cylindracea [12–14].

Despite the important ecological functions of Halmedineae in marine ecosystems, studies of codon usage bias based on chloroplast genomes are still poor. Codons are triplets of adjacent nucleotides on the messenger RNA (mRNA) that encode specific amino acids during protein synthesis [15]. There are 64 types of codons in all organisms, including three stop codons, and the remaining 61 types encode 20 amino acids. Except for methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp), which are each encoded by a single codon, numerous amino acids are encoded by two to six codons, a phenomenon known as codon degeneracy [16, 17]. Codons that encode the same amino acid are known as synonymous codons. During the process of protein translation, different organisms tend to favor specific synonymous codons, a phenomenon known as codon usage bias (CUB) [18, 19]. Codon usage biases are the result of genome adaptations to both transcription and translation mechanisms, and they are significantly correlated with protein and mRNA levels in Neurospora, highlighting their crucial role as determinants of gene expression [20]. Therefore, the study of codon usage bias (CUB) is of great importance for understanding gene expression regulation and genetic evolution. With the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technology, a considerable number of Halimedineae chloroplast genome sequences are available in database [4, 21–23], providing a solid foundation for studies on codon usage bias.

Research on CUB has been carried out in many organisms, including Theaceae species [16], ciliates [24], cherries [18, 25], citrus [26], Aconitum species [27], and Epimedium species [15]. However, little is known about codon usage bias in Halimedineae chloroplast genomes. In this study, we conducted comparative analysis of codon usage bias of 16 chloroplast genomes in suborder Halimedineae and explored the factors influencing codon usage bias. We inferred the phylogenetic relationship of different Halimedineae strains based on relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) data and compared it with the phylogenetic relationship based on chloroplast genome sequences. This study enhanced the comprehension of codon usage characteristics and genetic evolution of this suborder.

Materials and methods

Sequence collection and filtering

The 16 Halimedineae chloroplast genomes were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) under the accession numbers MH591114, KY819068, KX809677, MH591104, MH591102, MH591103, KY819065, MH591105, MH591110, ON584177, MH591109, NC_026796, MH591112, MH591111, MH591113, KY819064. The detailed information on 16 chloroplast genomes is listed in Supplementary Table S1. Protein-coding sequences (CDSs) were extracted from the chloroplast genomes using PhyloSuite v1.2.3 for subsequent analysis [28]. To ensure the accuracy of the analysis, all CDSs were filtered according to the following conditions [16, 29, 30] (1) the sequence started with a start codon (ATG) and ended with a stop codon (TAA, TGA, TAG); (2) the number of bases was a multiple of three; (3) the length of the CDS was ≥ 300 base pairs; and (4) sequences containing intermediate stop codons and uncertain bases were filtered out.

Nucleotide composition analysis

In the present study, several parameters based on CDS in 16 Halimedineae strains were analysed, including (1) overall nucleotide compositions (A, C, U, and G); (2) nucleotide composition at the third codon position (A3, C3, T3, and G3); (3) GC content at the first (GC1), second (GC2), and third codon positions (GC3); and (4) total GC content (GC). MEGA v 6.06 was used for the analysis of nucleotide composition (A, C, U, G, A3, C3, U3, G3). The other parameters (GC, GC1, GC2, GC3) were calculated using the CUSP program in EMBOSS (https://bioinformatics.nl/cgi-bin/emboss/cusp).

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis

The RSCU value is the ratio of the observed frequency of a codon to the expected frequency of codon. RSCU > 1 indicates codon usage bias, RSCU < 1 indicates that these codons are used less frequently than any other synonymous codons, and RSCU = 1 indicates no bias in codon usage, indicating that the synonymous codons are used equally and randomly [31]. Furthermore, synonymous codons with RSCU values greater than 1.6 and less than 0.6 were considered as over-represented and under-represented codons, respectively [32]. The RSCU values were calculated using Codon W v1.4.2, and presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Optimal codons

The effective number of codons (ENc) is a simple measure of overall codon bias and is analogous to the effective number of alleles used in population genetics [33]. The ENc value always falls between 20 (when only one codon is effectively used for each amino acid) and 61 (when codons are used randomly) [33]. The lower the ENc value of a codon, the stronger the codon usage bias. The ENc values were calculated using Codon W v1.4.2. According to the ENc values, 10% of the chloroplast genes at both ends were selected to generate high and low-expression libraries, respectively. The RSCU value and ∆RSCU in each library were calculated using CodonW v1.4.2 software, as shown in Supplementary Table S3. Codons with ∆RSCU > 0.08 and RSCU > 1 were identified as the optimal codons [34, 35].

ENc plot analysis

GC3s represents the content of G and C at the third position of synonymous codons, excluding Met and Trp. A scatter plot of the actual ENc (ENcact) values against GC3s was generated to estimate the codon bias. The ENc values were calculated using Codon W v1.4.2. The expected ENc values (ENcexp) were calculated using formula (1) to plot the standard curve [33]. In addition, the ENc ratio (ENcratio), which indicates the difference between the actual ENc and expected ENc values, was calculated using the following formula (2).

If the actual ENc is close to the standard curve, mutation pressure may be the primary driver of CUB. If there is a deviation from the standard curve, natural selection may be the primary driver of CUB [27, 36].

PR2 plot analysis

In the PR2 plot analysis, the G3/(G3 + C3) and A3/(A3 + T3) values were used as horizontal and vertical coordinates, respectively, to estimate the influence of selection and mutation pressure on CUB [37]. Based on this, the distribution of the four bases at the third position codon was determined. The central point, where A = T and G = C, indicates that there is no deviation between natural selection and mutation pressure [38].

Neutrality plot analysis

The neutrality plot analysis was plotted with GC12 as the ordinate and GC3 as the abscissa. The GC12 was the average value of GC1 and GC2. When the regression coefficient is closer to 1, the codon usage bias is dominated by mutation pressure; when the regression coefficient is closer to 0, the codon usage bias is dominated by natural selection [30, 39].

Correspondence analysis (COA)

COA is a common multivariate analysis method used to investigate the major trends in codon usage variation in genomes [16]. COA creates a series of orthogonal axes to identify trends that explain the variation in the data, with each subsequent axis explaining a decreasing amount of the variation. The first axis (axis 1) represents the majority of the variation in codon usage. In this study, COA was performed based on RSCU values of codons using CodonW v1.4.2 software. Correlation analysis between axis 1 and codon usage parameters, including GC, CAI (Codon Adaptation Index) and L_aa (the length of amino acids), was performed using SPSS version 25.0.

Cluster and phylogenetic analysis

The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of 16 Halimedineae strains was clustered using the Mev v4.8.1 software [40]. The phylogenetic trees based on 72 protein-coding genes of Halimedineae chloroplast genomes were reconstructed using the IQ-TREE web server (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) and MrBayes (https://nbisweden.github.io/MrBayes/index.html). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT v7.525 [41]. Ambiguously aligned regions were removed using trimAl v2.rev0 with gt = 0.8 [42]. Sequences were concatenated using the Concatenate Sequence program in PhyloSuite v1.2.3 [28]. ModelFinder was used to infer the best-fitting model according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [43]. The phylogenetic trees were edited using FigTree v1.4.2 software.

Results

Nucleotide composition analysis

The nucleotide composition of the filtered CDS is shown in Supplementary Table1. Higher A/U content and lower G/C content were observed in 16 Halimedineae strains (Fig. 1A). The percentages of total GC, GC1, GC2, and GC3 were less than 50%, indicating a preference for A/U bases and A/U-ending codons in Halimedineae (Fig. 1B). Among these species, Rhipilia penicilloides had the highest GC content at 38.11%, while Boodleopsis sp. H.0758 had the lowest at 28.12%. Furthermore, the GC content was unevenly distributed among the three codon positions, following this trend: GC1 > GC2 > GC3. In conclusion, Halimedineae species showed a preference for the use of A/U nucleic acids, and most codons in Halimedineae ended with A/U.

The RSCU is a crucial parameter for measuring codon bias in codon correlation analyses. The RSCU values of 16 Halimedineae chloroplast genome sequences were calculated and were presented in Supplementary Table S2. There were subtle differences in RSCU values between these species. For example, only in Boodleopsis sp. H.0758., the RSCU value of AUA was greater than 1. In addition, 24 codons with RSCU > 1 in Boodleopsis pusilla and Rhipilia penicilloides. 26 codons with RSCU > 1 in Tydemania expeditionis. 27 codons with RSCU > 1 in Avrainvillea sp., Caulerpa manorensis, Caulerpa okamurae, Boodleopsis sp. H.0758, Boodleopsis sp. FL1161, Rhipiliopsis peltata, Halimeda opuntia, Pseudocodium devriesii, Udotea flabellum, Udotea argentea and Udotea sp. 28 codons with RSCU > 1 in Callipsygma wilsonii and Chlorodesmis fastigiata. All codons with RSCU > 1 ended with A/U, suggesting relative conservation between species during the evolution of suborder Halimedineae. The Halimedineae species strongly preferred the codons UUA, AUU, and GUU (all RSCU > 2, except AUU in Boodleopsis sp. H.0758.), which encoded leucine, isoleucine, and valine, respectively.

Optimal codon analysis

The optimal codons for 16 Halimedineae strains were identified and were presented in Supplementary Table S3. A comprehensive analysis revealed a total of 30 optimal codons encoding 17 amino acids, 24 optimal codons ended in A/U, accounting for 80% of the total. Notably, the variation in the optimal codon was observed across 16 Halimedineae chloroplast genomes, as shown in Fig. 3. Udotea sp. had the highest number of optimal codons, 12, while Boodleopsis sp. H.0758 and Udotea argentea had the lowest number, 4. It was clear that there were differences even within the same genus (Fig. 3). Specifically, UUU, CAU, CUU, CCA, AGA, CGA and AGU were the most common optimal codons in the majority of Halimedineae strains.

ENc plot analysis

The ENc-GC3s plot analysis was used to explore the factors shaping codon usage patterns in Halimedineae. As depicted in Fig. 4, a similar distribution of ENc and GC3s values across 16 Halimedineae species was observed. A few genes were located on or near the standard curve, indicating that the codon usage bias observed in Halimedineae chloroplast genomes was minimally affected by mutation pressure. The majority of genes, however, were positioned below the standard curve, suggesting that other factors, such as natural selection, exerted a considerable impact on shaping the codon usage bias.

The (ENcexp − ENcobs)/ENcexp ratio was calculated to further investigate the difference between the actual ENc values and the expected ENc values. The ENc ratio mainly ranges from 0.1 to 0.3 (Fig. 5), indicating that the actual ENc values are slightly lower than the expected ENc values. These results also prove that most of the points are located below the standard curve, which is consistent with Fig. 4.

PR2 plot analysis

The PR2 plot analysis was performed to determine the degree of deviation of the third base of codon. The results showed that the genes were unevenly distributed in four regions (Fig. 6). Most of the genes in Halimedineae chloroplast genomes were found in the fourth quadrant, where A3/(A3 + T3) < 0.5, and G3/(G3 + C3) > 0.5, indicating that U is used more frequently than A, and G more frequently than C on the third base of codon. In other words, the third base of codons has a preference for the nucleotides U and G when compared to A and C, respectively. The result was consistent with the analysis of nucleotide composition, which revealed that U is more abundant than A, and G is more abundant than C at the third position of the codon (Fig. 1).

PR2 plot of the chloroplast genomes in 16 Halimedineae strains. The center point at 0.5 represents A = T and G = C, which means that there is no codon usage deviation between the two DNA strands. The points are randomly distributed in the four quadrants, and are mainly concentrated in the regions of G3/(G3 + C3) > 0.5 and A3/(A3 + T3) < 0.5

Neutrality plot analysis

Neutrality plot analysis was conducted for the Halimedineae species to explore the influence of mutation pressure and natural selection on codon usage bias. As shown in Fig. 7, the distribution ranges for GC12 and GC3 were relatively narrow. The slope of the regression line reflects the proportion of mutation pressure shaping the codon usage pattern. The slope of Avrainvillea sp., Boodleopsis sp. H.0758, and Callipsygma wilsonii were greater than 50% (Avrainvillea sp.: 0.58, Boodleopsis sp. H.0758: 1.23, Callipsygma wilsonii: 0.56), indicating that mutation pressure is a significant driver in shaping codon usage patterns (Fig. 7). In contrast, for the majority of Halimedineae species, the absolute slope values were less than 50%, often not exceeding 20%, indicating that natural selection and other factors are predominant in determining codon usage patterns. Furthermore, only in Boodleopsis sp. H.0758 GC12 and GC3 showed a general correlation (r = 0.49, P < 0.01), and the rest of the Halimedineae species showed no or weak correlation (Table S4). In summary, mutation pressure may strongly drive codon usage bias of Boodleopsis sp. H.0758, while natural selection plays a leading role in the formation of codon usage patterns in most other Halimedineae species.

Correspondence analysis

Correspondence analysis was performed to investigate codon usage variation in 16 Halimedineae chloroplast genomes. As shown in Supplementary Table S5, axis 1 and axis 2 accounted for 15.07-21.90% and 8.62-14.86% of the variation in synonymous codon usage variation, respectively. Axis1 accounted for approximately 15% of the total variation in 16 Halimedineae species, with each subsequent axis explaining a decreasing amount of the variation. Therefore, axis 1 was the major source of variation. We plotted the graph with the first axis as the horizontal coordinate and the second axis as the vertical coordinate (Fig. 8). The points were close to the central axis, suggesting that compositional constraints under mutational pressure might have influenced the CUB of Halimedineae species. Additionally, the scattering of the points in the graph indicated that CUB was not affected by a single factor but rather was determined by many different factors.

Correspondence analysis of the chloroplast genomes of 16 Halimedineae strains. The dots indicate that different genes are separated from each other in the figure

To identify the factors leading to the dispersion of chloroplast genes along axis 1 and axis 2, a correlation analysis was performed, specifically on the relationships between axis 1 and the variables CAI, GC3s, and L_aa (Table S4). Based on the correlation analysis, axis 1 had a significant correlation with GC3s (p ≤ 0.01) for 4 Halimedineae species (Avrainvillea sp., Caulerpa manorensis, Caulerpa okamurae, Halimeda opuntia), and axis 1 had a correlation with GC3s (p ≤ 0.05) in Boodleopsis sp. H.0758 and Rhipilia penicilloides, indicating the importance of GC3s in shaping codon usage patterns. In contrast, the majority of Halimedineae species (10 species, accounting for 63%) showed no correlation with L_aa. Halimedineae strains except for Boodleopsis sp. FL1161, Rhipilia penicilloides and Rhipiliopsis peltata, displayed a correlation with CAI, suggesting the influence of expression levels. Additionally, variations in base composition across the three codon positions were observed, with GC content exhibiting a significant correlation with GC1 and GC2 (see Table S4). However, the content of GC3 showed no significant correlation with GC1 or GC2 in most Halimedineae species, suggesting differences between GC1, GC2 and GC3. Axis 1 was significantly correlated with GC, GC1, GC2, and GC12, indicating that the base composition influenced codon usage bias.

Discussion

The study of codon usage bias is of vital significance for exploring genetic evolution and understanding gene expression characteristics [44]. Codon usage bias is widespread in bacteria, viruses, plants, fungi, and animals [27, 44–47]. However, the codon usage pattern in Halimedineae chloroplast genomes has not been thoroughly studied. In this study, we systematically analyzed the codon usage patterns, the factors that influenced codon usage bias, and the phylogenetic relationships of 16 chloroplast genomes of Halimedineae.

The chloroplast genomes of Halimedineae species preferentially used A/U bases and A/U-ending codons, which is consistent with previous studies on cherries [18], Gynostemma species [29], Oryza species [48], and Euphorbiaceae species [31]. Specifically, nucleotide composition analysis revealed that the content of GC, GC1, GC2 and GC3 was less than 50%, indicating the codons in Halimedineae suborder preferred to use A/U base (Fig. 1A). GC3 is less than 50%, indicating that the codons of Halimedineae preferred to end with A/U. This was consistent with previous studies in Theaceae [16], Aconitum [27], and Gynostemma [29]. Moreover, all the over-expressed codons and the majority of the optimal codons (80%) were A/U-ending (Table S2, Table S3). It was previously reported that highly expressed genes in cyanobacteria tended to use A/T-ending codons at the synonymous position compared with low expressed genes [49], which is consistent with our result. Previous studies showed organisms have evolved distinct codon usage patterns to better adapt to their environment. For example, Galdieria sulphuraria may have obtained a large number of AT-rich genes from various bacteria and archaea through horizontal gene transfer to adapt to the environment [50]. The bacteria Aquifex aeolicus and Thermotoga maritima have acquired archaeal genes through horizontal gene transfer, which provides a selective advantage for thriving in high-temperature habitats. This acquisition significantly impacts the codon bias of their respective genomes [51]. Similarly, in this study, the codons of Halimedineae strains showed a preference for A/U and tended to end with A/U base, which may be related to their adaptation to marine habitats.

Marine microorganisms living at different depths exhibit unique codon usage bias, particularly those at 4,000 m, which show a strong preference for amino acids such as Ala, Arg, Gly, Ile, Leu, Pro, and Thr [52]. Additionally, variations in environmental factors such as light intensity, temperature, and pressure with increasing depth may influence microbial selection of optimal codon usage [52]. Previous studies revealed that evolution toward positively charged residues such as Ile, Tyr, and Ala appears to be a highly adaptive strategy for deep-sea metazoans to enhance protein stability in the face of moderate to extreme temperatures [53]. In this study, the majority of Halimedineae strains preferred to use Phe, His, Leu, Pro, Arg, and Ser (Fig. 3), probably reflecting their ability to adapt to marine environments.

Codon usage bias (CUB) is shaped by numerous factors, with mutation pressure and natural selection being the major factors [27]. Our ENC-plot analysis showed that most points were far from the standard curve, implying natural selection might play a primary role in shaping the codon usage bias. Only a few points lie on the standard curve, suggesting that mutational pressure may also slightly influence codon bias formation. PR2 plot analysis can reflect the difference in the use frequency of A, T, C and G at the third position of the codon. When G, C or A, T at the third codon position are used equally, there is no deviation between natural selection and mutation pressure [37]. Our PR2 plot analysis revealed the unequal usage of GC and AT at the third codon position (the ratio of G3/(G3 + C3) > 0.5, A3/(A3 + T3) < 0.5), suggesting that the formation of codon usage patterns are not only affected by natural selection, but also by mutation pressure. In neutrality plot analysis, a regression slope close to 1 indicates that codon use bias is primarily driven by mutation pressure. Conversely, a slope towards 0 indicates that natural selection is the main force shaping codon usage patterns [44]. In the present study, the slope of the regression line (except Avrainvillea sp., Boodleopsis sp. H.0758, and Callipsygma wilsonii) was close to 0, suggesting that natural selection played a major role in shaping codon usage bias of Halimedineae. The slope of Avrainvillea sp., Boodleopsis sp. H.0758, and Callipsygma wilsonii were 0.58, 1.23 and 0.56, respectively, indicating that mutation pressure contributes to the formation of codon usage bias in these strains (Fig. 7).The factors influencing CUB are very complex, other factors, such as gene expression levels, gene length, recombination rates, and nucleotide composition, may also contribute to CUB [54–57]. COA analysis showed axis 1 and axis 2 accounted for 15.07-21.90% and 8.62-14.86% of the synonymous codon usage variation, respectively. CAI is a codon adaptation index that can be used to predict the expression level of a gene [44]. Correlation analysis revealed that axis 1 displayed a correlation with CAI, GC, GC1, GC2, and GC12, suggesting that gene expression and nucleotide composition may have a relatively minor impact on CUB in the chloroplast coding sequences (CDSs) of these strains. In summary, the codon usage bias in Halimedineae was influenced by various factors, including natural selection, mutation pressure, nucleotide composition, and gene expression, with natural selection playing a major role.

There were some similarities and differences between the phylogenetic tree and the clustering tree. For example, four representatives (Udotea argentea, Udotea flabellum, Udotea sp. Chlorodesmis fastigiata) of Udoteae clustered into a branch in both the phylogenetic tree and clustering tree, indicating that closely related species have similar codon usage patterns (Figs. 2 and 9). This is also reflected in the branch of Caulerpa manorensis and Caulerpa okamurae, and the branch of Boodleopsis pusilla and Rhipilia penicilloides. In addition, there are some differences between the phylogenetic tree and the clustering tree. For example, Tydemania expeditionis was closely related to three representatives of Udoteae and Chlorodesmis fastigiata in phylogenetic tree, but was closely related to with branch including Rhipiliopsis peltata, Halimeda opuntia and Pseudocodium devriesii in clustering tree. Callipsygma wilsonii and Rhipiliopsis peltata are clustered into a small group in the phylogenetic tree and then clusters into a branch with Halimeda opuntia, but Callipsygma wilsonii forms a sister relationship with branch containing four representatives of Udoteae and Boodleopsis sp. FL1161 in the clustering tree. Avrainvillea sp. is located at the base of the phylogenetic tree but is nested in the first branch of the clustering tree. The results of the phylogenetic tree constructed based on chloroplast protein coding sequences and the clustering tree constructed based on RSCU values are not entirely consistent. This suggests that genetic relationship solely based on the RSCU may overlook some information, as weak or non-preferred codons may also play an irreplaceable role in the evolution of Halimedineae.

The suborder Halimedineae consists of the families Caulerpaceae, Dichotomosiphonaceae, Halimedaceae, Pseudocodiaceae, Rhipiliaceae, and Udoteaceae. However, the relationship between the families within the core Halimedineae (Caulerpaceae, Rhipiliaceae, Halimedaceae, Pseudocodiaceae, and Udoteaceae) remained poorly resolved. A branch contained five representatives of the Udoteaceae was clustered with Pseudocodium devriesii in Fig. 9B, which was in line with previous study [4]. The branch of Callipsygma wilsonis + Rhipiliopsis peltata was sister relationship with Halimeda opuntia, which was consistent with the results from previous phylogenetic studies based on larger datasets [4]. The two representatives of Boodleopsis genus are clustered with Rhipilia penicilloides, formed a small branch in Fig. 9B. In previous studies, Dichotomosiphonaceae was recovered as sister to the core Halimedineae and the Caulerpaceae was always recovered as sister to the remaining lineages in the core Halimedineae [4, 21, 58]. However, the two representative species of the genus Caulerpa were not completely separated from the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 9B), which may be related to species selection and inadequate taxon sampling.
