Prevalence of obsessive-compulsive symptoms and associations with psychological distress and quality of life among medical students in Egypt: a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Mostafa Shaheen, Farah A Amasha, Nada Riad, Omar Mohamed Ail, Mohamed Ibrahim Morshed, Maram Ahmed, Ahmed M Yasin, Yahya Al-sarhi, Ali S Salem, Heba Sheta, OCS team of collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundObsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterised by obsessive thoughts and compulsive actions. These obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS) are subclinical manifestations that do not meet the full diagnostic criteria for OCD and are associated with anxiety, depression and lower quality of life (QoL). Medical students are vulnerable to developing OCS due to stress in medical school. This study assessed OCS prevalence and its association with the mental well-being and QoL of medical students in Egypt.Method(s)A nationwide cross-sectional study was conducted across 15 Egyptian medical schools. Using convenience sampling, 1850 students participated by completing a self-administered questionnaire that used validated scales. We assessed OCS with the Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R) using a screening cut-off of ≥21, QoL with the Quality-of-Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire (Q-LES-QSF), and anxiety and depression with the 4-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4). Descriptive statistics and logistic regression were employed.ResultsClinically significant OCS prevalence among medical students was 51.1%. Significant predictors for OCS included being female (adjusted OR (AOR)=1.25), attending a private university (AOR=1.64), and having personal (AOR=2.05) or combined personal and family history of mental illness (AOR=2.69). OCS presence was associated with a lower QoL score (Q-LES-QSF: 41.00 vs 43.97) and higher psychological distress score (PHQ-4: 5.93 vs 3.57) compared with students without OCS (p<0.001).ConclusionsOCS are prevalent among Egyptian medical students, especially females, private university attendees and those with a personal or family history of mental illness. These symptoms are associated with higher psychological distress and a lower QoL. As OCS were identified using a screening cut-off, and given the cross-sectional design, findings should be interpreted cautiously, warranting further longitudinal investigation. Universities should consider implementing mental health support, screening and awareness programmes to address these issues.Keywords: MENTAL HEALTH, PSYCHIATRY, Psychological Stress, Prevalence

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a psychiatric disorder characterised by intrusive obsessive thoughts that lead to repetitive compulsive behaviours.1 In contrast, obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS) represent subclinical manifestations that may occur in individuals who do not meet the full diagnostic criteria for OCD.2 Common manifestations of OCS include an unhealthy concern with contamination, leading to excessive cleaning, repeated checking behaviours and a powerful need for symmetry and order.3

Medical students are particularly vulnerable to developing mental health issues due to high levels of stress,4 which is often driven by intense academic competition.5 Additionally, their susceptibility to OCS may be further compounded by constant exposure to medical stimuli during their training, which can trigger exaggerated concerns about contamination and disease.6 Compulsions and intrusive obsessions may impair concentration, reduce study efficiency and negatively affect clinical performance during training.7 These difficulties may contribute to suboptimal knowledge retention and skill acquisition.8 As future physicians, these impairments may have implications for patient safety in clinical practice.

OCS can also have significant consequences for the well-being and overall life satisfaction of medical students. Obsessions tend to induce significant anxiety and emotional distress, while compulsions are done in response to an obsession to neutralise or reduce anxiety.9 These symptoms can be time-consuming and may interfere with daily functioning by requiring considerable time to complete routine activities.10 Furthermore, psychiatric comorbidities, such as mood disorders and major depressive disorders, are common among individuals with OCS.11 These comorbidities, along with the time-consuming nature of these symptoms, can also diminish overall quality of life.12

Numerous studies have emphasised the widespread occurrence of OCS in medical students worldwide. For example, research conducted in Brazil and Jordan indicated that medical students show a markedly higher prevalence of probable OCS than the general population.6 13 Similarly, studies conducted in Saudi Arabia reported prevalences of 46.4% and 26% of clinically significant OCS among medical students, while a study from Iraq reported a prevalence of 43%.14,16 Additionally, a study conducted among third-year medical students at Ain Shams University in Egypt regarding psychiatric morbidities reported a 44.4% prevalence of probable OCD symptoms using a self-administered questionnaire rather than clinician-confirmed diagnoses.17

Although OCS are common among medical students, there are no national studies on their prevalence among Egyptian medical students or their effects on their quality of life. Recognising the substantial correlation of OCS on the well-being of medical students, we hypothesised that the prevalence of OCS symptoms among Egyptian medical students would be higher than that reported in the general population, reflecting the combined impact of intense academic pressure and a high-stakes assessment culture. Thus, this study aimed to determine its prevalence among them and examine its impact on life enjoyment and satisfaction to increase awareness of OCS and offer support to those who have it.

Methods

Study design and participants

A descriptive, cross-sectional study with an analytic component adhering to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies.18 The study was conducted between August and September 2024 at the faculties of medicine in 15 universities across five distinct regions of Egypt. According to the General Organization for Physical Planning, Egypt is divided into seven regional units.19 To ensure adequate geographical and cultural representation and to enhance the generalisability of the study findings to the Egyptian medical students, data were collected from multiple regions based on the feasible access to data collectors during the data collection period. We proactively addressed potential selection bias by forming an extensive collaborative network. This network engaged data collectors from 15 different medical schools, enabling us to obtain a diverse sample across five distinct geographical regions. Medical students from the first to fifth academic years were eligible to participate in the study.

Patient and public involvement

This research did not involve patients or members of the public in setting research questions, designing the study, selecting outcomes, conducting research or planning dissemination. As this study focused on medical students, patient and public involvement was not considered appropriate.

Data collection

A convenience sampling method was used. Data were collected through a structured questionnaire created using Google Forms and shared online via popular social media platforms, including WhatsApp and Telegram. The questionnaire was distributed through university student groups and reached with the help of data collectors from the participating universities as well as through medical student organisations.

Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were offered. Respondents could complete the questionnaire at their convenience. Participants were required to provide explicit consent by selecting ‘I agree to participate’ before accessing the questionnaire. To ensure privacy, no personally identifiable information (eg, names or dates of birth) was collected. All data were securely stored on a password-protected drive accessible only to members of the research team. To avoid duplicate entries, email restrictions were enabled, requiring users to sign in with a Google account to limit each participant to a single response. In addition to minimising missing data from the final dataset, the questionnaire required responses to all items before submitting the form.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the OpenEpi online calculator (Version 3.01).20 The expected prevalence of clinically significant OCS, the primary objective of this study, was based on a previous study conducted in Saudi Arabia, reporting a prevalence of 26% among medical students.16 Assuming a 95% CI and a 5% margin of error, the minimum sample size required is 296 participants. A design effect of five was applied to account for the variance inflation expected from the non-random convenience sampling method and clustering effect across multiple universities; therefore, the required sample size was estimated at 1478 participants. The final sample size achieved was 1850 participants.

Study tool

The English questionnaire consisted of four sections: sociodemographic information, the Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R) scale, the Quality-of-Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire–Short Form (Q-LES-Q-SF) scale and the Four-Item Patient Health Questionnaire for Anxiety and Depression (PHQ-4) scale. All scales are widely used, with well-established psychometric properties and prior use in Middle Eastern populations.15 21 22 The questionnaire was administered in English, consistent with the primary language of medical education in Egyptian medical schools.23

The first section collected sociodemographic data, including age, sex, academic year, place of residence (urban or rural), and whether the participants themselves, their families, or both suffer from mental health disorders.

The second section assessed students’ OCS using the OCI-R.24 The OCI-R is a validated, self-reported 18-item scale with high internal consistency, as demonstrated by a previous study (Cronbach’s α=0.81–0.93).24 In the current study, the OCI-R exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91, indicating good internal consistency within the sample of Egyptian medical students. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely), yielding a total score of 0 to 72. A cut-off score of 21 or higher indicates clinically significant OCS.24

The third section assessed students’ quality of life using the Q-LES-Q-SF, a validated, self-reported 16-item tool that assesses life enjoyment and satisfaction over the past week using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very poor) to 5 (very good). Higher scores indicated a better quality of life. The Q-LES-Q-SF demonstrated high reliability (Cronbach’s α=0.90; test-retest r=0.93), with 80% sensitivity and 100% specificity in a previous study.25 In the current study, the Q-LES-Q-SF exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83, indicating good internal consistency.

The fourth section evaluated psychological distress using the PHQ-4, a validated, self-reported screening tool consisting of four items.26 Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), producing a total score from 0 to 12. Higher scores reflect greater symptom severity. The PHQ-4 has demonstrated strong reliability in a previous study (Cronbach’s α>0.80).26 The current study yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89, indicating good internal consistency among medical students in Egypt.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Jamovi software (version 2.3.28 for Windows).27 Descriptive statistics were used to summarise data: frequencies and percentages were used for sociodemographic characteristics and OCS and means and SD were used for Q-LES-Q-SF and PHQ-4 scores. Normality of continuous variables was tested using Q-Q plots.

OCS were defined as having an OCI-R score ≥21.24 To examine the association between OCS and sociodemographic factors, a univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression model was used with age, gender, academic year, university type (public vs private), residence (urban vs rural) and personal/family history of mental illness included as covariates in the multivariable model. Crude OR (COR) and adjusted OR (AOR) were calculated for each variable to assess its association with OCS.

An independent samples t-test was used to assess the relationship between mean Q-LES-Q-SF and PHQ-4 scores and participants with and without OCS. Cohen’s d was calculated to estimate the effect size of these differences. A p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

A total of 1850 medical students participated in the study. Among them, 53.0% were female, and 57.2% were aged 21 years or younger. The majority resided in urban areas (74.4%), and 91.9% attended public universities. Fourth-year students constituted the largest academic group (32.8%), while first-year students were the smallest (14.1%). A history of mental health disorders was reported by 7.4% of them, 15.0% of their families and 6.8% of both (table 1).

OCS among study participants

As shown in table 1, clinically significant OCS (OCI-R ≥21) were present in 51.1% of participants.

Initial univariate analysis revealed that female students had 35% higher odds of OCS than males (COR=1.35, p value=0.001). However, this association was attenuated yet remained significant in the multivariate analysis (AOR=1.25, p value=0.023), suggesting the presence of confounding by other variables. Younger students (≤21 years) had higher OCS prevalence (61.7% vs 38.3%, p value=0.001), though this effect attenuated after adjustment (AOR=0.93, p value=0.586). Academic progression appeared protective, as fourth-year students had 30% lower odds of OCS compared with first-year students (AOR=0.70, p value=0.043). Notably, private university attendance was linked to 64% higher adjusted odds of OCS (AOR=1.64, p value=0.007).

The strongest predictors were mental health histories: students with personal history had double the odds (AOR=2.05, p value=0.001), while those with both personal and family history had nearly triple the odds (AOR=2.69, p value=0.001) (table 1).

Quality of life and psychological distress by OCS status

Participants with OCS reported significantly lower quality of life (Q-LES-QSF: 41.00 vs 43.97, p value <0.001; Cohen’s d=0.28, a small effect size) and higher psychological distress (PHQ-4: 5.93 vs 3.57, p value <0.001; Cohen’s d = −0.8, a large effect size). The PHQ-4’s large effect size suggests a robust association between OCS and distress (table 2).

Discussion

In the present study, the prevalence of clinically significant OCS was 51.1%, close to a recent multicentric study conducted across four medical schools in Saudi Arabia,15 which reported 46.4%. However, our result is higher compared with other previous studies conducted among medical students in Saudi Arabia, which reported prevalences ranging from 20% to 35.3%.16 28 29 Our findings are also consistent with a study from Ain Shams University, which found that, when using a self-administered questionnaire, 44.4% of students reported having probable OCD symptoms.17 In contrast, our results also vary significantly from those of a Brazilian study among medical students that reported a significantly lower prevalence of 3.8%; however, this may be attributed to the higher cut-off point they used.6 Among health science students, Madoro et al reported an OCS prevalence of 28%.30 On the other hand, OCS also varies across academic disciplines, with the highest rates observed in engineering (58.3%), applied medical sciences (57.1%), art and design (57.1%) and Sharia and law (47.8%), while the lowest were reported among medical students (25.8%).31 In contrast, prevalence in the general population is lower, with 11.3% exhibiting clinically significant OCS, decreasing to 10.3% after excluding individuals with pre-existing mental illness.32

Most studies assessed OCS using the OCI-R with a cut-off score of ≥21, as originally proposed to distinguish individuals with OCS from non-clinical populations.24 Alternatively, Torres et al used a higher threshold of 27, which more effectively discriminates between moderate and severe OCS cases.6 33 However, the Ain Shams study used a different instrument, the Structured Clinical Interview for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), yet reported a similarly high prevalence, close to our findings.17 This observation suggests that elevated prevalence may not be solely attributable to higher cut-off scores. Contextual factors may also contribute, including mental health stigma and limited help-seeking among Egyptian medical students.34

Also, differences between the general population and students in other faculties can be explained by variations in academic environments and levels of stress exposure within medical schools.6 Medical education in Egypt is characterised by extensive study and extended clinical hours, factors that likely contribute to elevated stress levels.35 National studies have consistently reported high stress prevalence among Egyptian medical students, ranging from 60% to 86.5%.36 37 Chronic stress may exacerbate obsessive-compulsive symptomatology by elevating anxiety levels and reinforcing maladaptive coping mechanisms.38 This may partly explain the higher prevalence of OCS observed among students enrolled in private universities, as they may face additional stressors related to financial burdens and heightened familial expectations. Furthermore, we found an inverse relationship between academic year and OCS. Fourth-year students showed 30% lower odds of clinically significant OCS compared with first-year students. These findings aligned with a Brazilian study highlighting freshmen as vulnerable to OCS due to adaptation challenges.6

Our findings revealed that female students were 1.25 times more likely to report OCS than males, aligning with findings from studies in Saudi Arabia and Brazil.6 15 However, our findings contrast with those of Taher et al and Torres et al, who reported no significant association between gender and OCS.6 14 The observed gender difference may reflect higher anxiety and depression symptom reporting among female medical students,39 as well as sociocultural expectations related to cleanliness, orderliness and perfectionism.40 However, the underlying reasons for the observed gender difference remain unclear. These findings highlight the need for gender-sensitive mental health support in universities. Awareness campaigns and mental health support programmes can help students, particularly in managing anxiety and perfectionistic tendencies and dealing with symptoms.

The present study also demonstrated a significant association between OCS and a positive family history of mental illness. This is consistent with findings from studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and India.15 16 41 In contrast, a study conducted in Iraq reported no significant association,14 possibly due to cultural differences, including variations in mental health stigma, reporting behaviours and family disclosure practices. In Iraq, stigma surrounding mental illness and negative societal attitudes may reduce willingness to report personal symptoms and family psychiatric history.42 The observed association may be attributed to genetic predisposition, as supported by twin and family studies demonstrating a heritable component in OCD.43 44 Furthermore, highly controlling or unstable familial environments can contribute to the development and exacerbation of such symptoms.45 These findings suggest the value of including screening for family history of mental illness during student orientation. Early identification can help provide targeted support and referrals.

Our findings showed that students with clinically significant OCS reported significantly higher PHQ-4 scores, reflecting that students with OCS are more likely to experience psychological distress, which is consistent with research showing that up to 76% of individuals diagnosed with OCD also meet criteria for comorbid anxiety disorders.46 Depression is also similarly recognised as the most prevalent comorbidity in OCS.47

In addition to elevated psychological distress, students with OCS also reported a significant association with reduced quality of life. This finding is consistent with prior studies indicating that individuals with OCS exhibit substantial impairments in QoL compared with both healthy controls and individuals with other psychiatric conditions.48,50 One contributing factor may be the time-consuming nature of compulsions, which can significantly interfere with academic performance and daily functioning, particularly in high-pressure settings such as medical school.10 These findings highlight the importance of supporting students by reducing stigma and promoting awareness that such symptoms are manageable. Implementing time management workshops and stress-reduction strategies can help students cope with compulsions.

This study has several strengths. The large sample size may enhance the generalisability of the findings. Additionally, the use of validated instruments to assess OCS ensures the reliability of the results. Furthermore, using appropriate statistical analysis methods to adjust for potential confounders. The study also provides valuable insights into the prevalence of OCS and their associations with psychological distress and quality of life among medical students in Egypt, which is an area with scarce data.

Limitations and recommendations

However, some limitations must be considered. The cross-sectional design of this study precludes the determination of the directionality of the relationship between OCS and other variables, particularly quality of life. It is plausible that factors such as baseline stress or pre-existing psychological vulnerability may independently contribute to both reduced quality of life and increased psychological distress. Conversely, the high prevalence of OCS relies on the OCI-R questionnaire, which is based on self-reported symptoms and lacks an objective assessment, introducing the possibility of recalling and reporting bias that may require further clinical investigation to confirm an OCD diagnosis. Additionally, convenience sampling may lack generalisability. Moreover, using online recruitment via social media and university networks may introduce selection bias, as participants might be more digitally engaged or interested in mental health. The open recruitment strategy also prevented the calculation of response rates. The lack of a control group (eg, non-medical students or the general population) limits contextual interpretation of prevalence estimates. Although the overall sample size was large, statistical power may still be limited in subgroup analyses (eg, by university type or smaller mental health history categories), and therefore these results should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the online format did not allow independent verification of participants’ identities as medical students.

We recommend future research with a longitudinal design to examine causation between OCS, distress and quality of life in medical students. Future qualitative research will be helpful to provide insight into the barriers that face medical students seeking mental health support. Comparative studies across different countries may be needed to identify the disparities in the prevalence of OCS, maybe through cultural or educational system variance. Finally, intervention-based studies are necessary to evaluate appropriate preventive approaches to manage stress and alleviate symptoms.

Clinical implications

The high prevalence of OCS necessitates the implementation of mental health screening programmes in medical schools for early identification. Additionally, high-risk students should be referred to psychiatrists for counselling. On the other hand, universities should establish support systems to help medical students cope with stress, including peer support and awareness initiatives. These interventions may reduce psychological distress and improve academic performance and quality of life.

Conclusions

Our study found that approximately half of the Egyptian medical students had OCS, with higher rates among females, private university students and those with a personal or family mental illness history. These symptoms are associated with increased psychological distress and a reduced quality of life. As OCS were identified using a screening cut-off and the study was cross-sectional, the findings should be interpreted cautiously, warranting further longitudinal research. These results highlight the need for systematic mental health screening and awareness programmes in Egyptian medical education.
