How the application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) affects Chinese art design professionals’ career exploration behavior: the roles of job insecurity, psychological distress, and proactive personality
Authors: Yibei Ran, Yunxi Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) technology is reshaping the industry ecosystem at an unprecedented pace in the field of art and design. AI technology has enormous potential to enhance creative efficiency and expand artistic expression. However, the widespread application of AI also poses serious challenges to practitioners’ professional development. Therefore, this study, based on data from 485 questionnaires, used Mplus 8.8 to explore the mechanism of the relationship between AI application and career exploration behavior among art and design professionals. The findings are: (1) The application of AI is positively related to career exploration behavior among art and design professionals. (2) Job insecurity mediates the relationship between AI application and career exploration behavior. (3) Psychological distress mediates the relationship between AI application and career exploration behavior. (4) Job insecurity and psychological distress play a chain mediating role between AI application and career exploration behavior. (5) Proactive personality moderates the relationship between AI application and career exploration behavior. These conclusions encourage collaboration between individuals and organizations and promote sustainable career ecosystems amid technological change.Keywords: Application of AI, Job insecurity, Psychological distress, Proactive personality, Career exploration behavior

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) technology is rapidly transforming the art and design industry ecosystem. From a historical perspective, the integration of AI and art design has undergone significant evolution: Leonardo da Vinci’s “Automaton’ in the 16th century foreshadowed the early combination of machinery and art [72]; in 1973, Harold Cohen developed the AARON painting robot, which achieved autonomous algorithmic creation for the first time [10]; by the 2010s, breakthroughs in technologies such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) elevated AI art to new heights. Specific examples include the 2018 creation of the ‘Portrait of Edmond Belamy’ by the French team Obvious, which sold for $430,000 at auction, and the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics opening ceremony, which used 3DAT technology to capture the motion of 600 performers in real time [72]. These successful applications demonstrate the enormous potential of AI technology to enhance creative efficiency and expand artistic expression. However, the widespread adoption of AI also poses serious challenges for practitioners. Research indicates that by 2030, AI could replace 800 million jobs worldwide, with 59.5% of Chinese occupations facing a high risk of automation [19]. Acemoglu and Restrepo [3], further quantified that adding one robot per thousand workers could lead to a decline in employment rates of 0.18–0.34% points. Importantly, for art and design professionals, the impact of AI goes beyond simple estimates of job displacement. Recent studies specific to the field highlight a core dilemma: AI’s ability to perform tasks traditionally seen as central to creative identity—such as generating visual concepts, styles, and even “original” artworks—can create unique conflicts over the value and uniqueness of human creativity [42, 72]. This threatens not only jobs but also designers’ professional self-perception, as AI moves from being merely a tool to a potential rival or partner in the creative process [21]. From the perspective of Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) [39, 40], this substitution effect not only directly triggers career anxiety among practitioners but also stimulates individuals’ career exploration behaviors through self-management mechanisms. Career exploration behavior is defined as ‘purposeful actions and cognitions aimed at acquiring information about career options’ [59], which is especially critical in contexts where technological changes create career uncertainty [30].

Current research primarily focuses on AI’s immediate effects on employees, linking them to outcomes such as anxiety [67], turnover intention [7], psychological distress [48], and avoidance behaviors [7]; however, there is a lack of in-depth exploration of the professional adaptation behaviors of art and design practitioners. Therefore, this study focuses on the theme of ‘How the application of AI technology influences the career exploration behaviors of art and design practitioners’ and aims to investigate the following core issues: What are the transmission pathways and boundary conditions of AI technology application in career exploration behaviors? Clarifying this question not only helps improve career management theories but also provides practical support for the professional growth of art and design practitioners.

Theoretical review and research hypotheses

Theoretical review

According to SCCT, environmental uncertainty and threats are key antecedents of career exploration behavior [39]. Within this theoretical framework, applying AI as a major environmental input—by altering the work environment in the art and design field—elicits cognitive and emotional responses from practitioners. Specifically, the rapid adoption of AI technology is fundamentally reshaping the art and design industry ecosystem [72], and these changes may directly influence practitioners’ perceptions of job stability. Job insecurity refers to an individual’s subjective perception of occupational threats. Debus et al., [12], Qian [52], . When art and design practitioners encounter advanced AI capabilities, such as machine learning and image recognition, they often experience a crisis of confidence that their skills are being overtaken by technology, heightening concerns about their career prospects [72]. This cognitive process is closely related to the self-efficacy mechanism in SCCT, such that an individual’s belief in their abilities significantly influences their career goal setting and behavioral choices [40]. Psychological distress refers to an emotional response, including symptoms like anxiety and depression [46], and in SCCT, it influences behavioral adjustments through outcome expectations. If practitioners believe that changing careers could alleviate psychological pressure associated with AI, they are more likely to actively explore new career options [39]. For example, facing the risk of job displacement from AI, art and design professionals may become more proactive in gathering career information and planning due to emotional fluctuations. Proactive personality refers to a consistent tendency to spark environmental change [4] and was included as a foundational personal dispositional factor. SCCT acknowledges that stable dispositions affect how individuals interpret and engage with their context [39]. For art and design professionals facing AI, this mindset directly energizes the core SCCT processes: it encourages active learning with new AI tools, which boosts task-specific self-efficacy; it reframes the uncertain threat of technological disruption as a challengeable opportunity, positively influencing outcome expectations; and it naturally leads to goal-oriented, information-seeking behaviors that characterize career exploration [59]. This aligns with stress theories, in which traits influence stress appraisal and coping [8, 36]. A proactive personality thus permeates the SCCT process, shaping the initial orientation toward the AI context. Career exploration behavior is a goal-directed process in which individuals gather information and plan their careers to adapt to environmental changes [59]. In the SCCT framework, this behavior results from the combined influence of environmental input, cognitive evaluation, and emotional response, embodying the dynamic process of career self-management.

This model, using SCCT theory, examines multiple levels of environmental, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral factors, offering a systematic view of how art and design practitioners adapt their careers in the AI era. This framework not only supports the development of targeted career policies but also encourages collaboration between individuals and organizations, promoting sustainable career ecosystems amid technological change.

Application of AI and career exploration behavior

SCCT indicates that personal, behavioral, and environmental factors are in dynamic interaction, with environmental factors capable of promoting or restricting individuals’ career-related behaviors [39]. When significant changes occur in the external environment, such as technological disruptions, individuals may actively evaluate their career goals and adjust their paths through personal agency to regain a sense of control and reduce career anxiety, thereby exhibiting more career exploration behaviors [39, 59]. The widespread application of AI technologies, especially generative AI, automated design tools, and intelligent image processing systems, is profoundly reshaping the workflows, skill requirements, and professional boundaries within the art and design industry [35, 41]. For professionals in the field of art and design, this technological infiltration not only changes the way they create but may also be perceived as a potential threat to their unique creative roles, work value, and even career development [65]. This perceived AI substitution threat constitutes a key source of situational pressure, which can trigger anxiety about future career prospects [14], thereby ‘driving’ individuals into an adaptive self-management state aimed at coping with uncertainty and seeking new pathways.

According to SCCT, environmental uncertainty or threats are important antecedents of career choice behavior [39]. In the context of organizational change and technological substitution, perceived threats have been shown to effectively stimulate adaptive career behaviors, including career exploration behavior [17, 30]. Concerns among art and design professionals that AI applications may erode their core competitiveness or replace their traditional responsibilities will prompt them to proactively seek information on industry trends, emerging skills, cross-disciplinary opportunities, and potential alternative career paths [27]. This exploratory behavior, whether cognitive (such as reflecting on one’s career direction) or behavioral (such as learning new skills, researching freelance markets, or exploring cross-field possibilities), is a strategic coping mechanism individuals adopt to alleviate anxiety and rebuild a sense of control over their careers [15, 51]. Research shows that, in the context of rapid AI technological development, the stronger employees’ perceived threat of job displacement, the more motivated and active they are in exploring alternative options [32, 53]. Therefore, in the highly creative and professional field of art and design, the environmental impact and threat perception arising from AI applications will serve as key catalysts, driving this group to engage in extensive, in-depth career exploration. Based on the above, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1: The application of AI is positively related to art design professionals’ career exploration behavior.

H1: The application of AI is positively related to art design professionals’ career exploration behavior.

The mediating role of job insecurity

The widespread application of AI in art and design is reshaping the creative process, challenging traditional skill values, and blurring professional boundaries. This creates a significant perception of environmental threat, which directly exacerbates job insecurity among art and design professionals. Job insecurity, as a subjective concern and experience of uncertainty about job continuity [11], stems from individuals’ assessments of threats to job stability and their perceived ability to cope [20]. The rapid development of AI technologies, especially generative AI, automated design tools, and style imitation algorithms, has brought about a fundamental impact on the highly creative, aesthetically unique, and skill-dependent profession of art and design. When art and design professionals observe AI’s ability to efficiently generate visual concepts, complete basic layout and retouching, or even imitate specific artistic styles [63], they may develop the perception that ‘AI is more efficient or capable in certain tasks.’ This perception directly challenges their professional self-efficacy, which is the belief in their irreplaceability and future value of their specialized skills [39], triggering deep concerns about skill obsolescence and role marginalization [51]. According to SCCT, environmental factors can significantly influence individuals’ cognitive and emotional states [39]. As an environmental stressor, AI application presents a high level of ‘threat severity’—it touches on the core values and identity of creative workers; at the same time, many designers may feel their personal power is limited when facing this systemic technological change, leading to a significant sense of ‘helplessness’ [20]. The combination of high threat and high helplessness jointly fosters and amplifies job insecurity. Specifically, the involvement of AI causes designers to doubt the stability of future work content, clarity of career development, and the continuity of employment relationships [60], fearing that their core creative roles may be weakened or replaced by technological tools [65]. Empirical studies also show that perceptions of being replaced by emerging technologies are important antecedents of job insecurity [7, 33]. Therefore, in the field of art and design, the extent of AI applications is closely linked to professionals’ perceived threats and the resulting job insecurity levels. Based on these considerations, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2a: The application of AI is positively associated with job insecurity among art and design professionals.

H2a: The application of AI is positively associated with job insecurity among art and design professionals.

Job insecurity, once triggered by AI, becomes a strong psychological motivator, encouraging art and design professionals to actively pursue career exploration as a way to adapt to uncertainty, regain a sense of control, and seek stability. According to SCCT, changes in personal factors (such as emotional state and self-efficacy) directly influence an individual’s motivation for career-related actions [40]. Job insecurity, as a persistent stressor [62], erodes individuals’ sense of identity and belonging in their current professional roles, reducing their expectations of positive outcomes from remaining in those positions (such as stability and growth) [61]. When art and design professionals feel their jobs are at risk and their professional value is threatened by AI, their trust in organizational commitment and career pathways declines [54], and the anticipation of job loss triggers anxiety. To alleviate this anxiety and regain control over career development, individuals are highly motivated to take proactive, problem-oriented coping actions. Career exploration behavior—that is, actively seeking out and evaluating information about one’s interests, abilities, external job opportunities, and organizational information through cognitive and practical actions [59]—is precisely such a key adaptive mechanism. Through exploration, individuals aim to gather information to reduce uncertainty about the future, identify potential alternative career paths or the new skill sets needed, and mentally and practically prepare for possible career transitions [15]. For designers feeling insecure, exploration behaviors can include actively learning about emerging niche areas in the design industry during the AI era, such as user experience design and AI interaction design. They may also consider switching to freelancing or entrepreneurship, invest in learning new tools and skills to collaborate with AI, or re-examine and redefine their unique creative value in a technology-driven environment. This process not only provides practical options but, more importantly, helps restore individuals’ sense of agency and control, which feelings of insecurity can undermine [34, 71]. Extensive research confirms that job insecurity is a significant antecedent of turnover intentions, job search behaviors, and various forms of career adaptation (including exploration) [37, 62]. Therefore, in the context of industry transformation driven by artificial intelligence, the job insecurity experienced by art and design professionals will significantly drive them to actively and strategically engage in career exploration to secure jobs and support sustainable development. Based on this, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2b: Job insecurity is positively related to the career exploration behavior of art and design professionals.

The mediating role of psychological distress

According to SCCT, individuals’ cognitive appraisal of environmental factors is the core mechanism shaping their emotional states [39]. The widespread use of artificial intelligence in art and design acts as a significant environmental stressor. It threatens professional identity, core skill value, and future stability, systematically causing and deepening psychological distress among art and design professionals. Psychological distress usually involves negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression, which result from cognitive and emotional reactions to stressful events [24]. For art and design professionals, AI technologies (such as generative AI and automated design tools) are seen not only as potential replacements for certain tasks or roles but also as a significant challenge to their uniqueness as creative agents, their professional authority, and the limits of their occupational identity [70]. This sense of ‘AI substitution perception’ triggers an ongoing threat assessment: people worry that their long-term accumulated aesthetic judgments, manual skills, and creative thinking abilities might quickly decline or become outdated. They also feel uncertain about their future roles in AI collaboration or competition scenarios [22]. According to SCCT, this strong expectation of negative career outcomes (such as unemployment or loss of value) directly leads to a decline in positive outcome expectations, thereby causing significant emotional distress [16]. The rapid and unpredictable development of AI technology further amplifies this uncertainty, making it difficult for individuals to adapt effectively and leading to increased stress responses [29]. Specifically, perceptions of AI capabilities (such as efficient generation and style imitation) may diminish designers’ sense of control and self-efficacy. This feeling of helplessness, combined with pessimistic views about career prospects, drains psychological resources and triggers anxiety, worry, and even symptoms of depression [45]. Research confirms that, in the context of technological change, perceptions of technological replacement are significantly associated with levels of psychological distress, especially among groups with relatively weaker digital adaptability [47]. In the creative industries, because work outcomes are closely linked to personal identity, the challenges brought by AI are more personalized, and the psychological distress they induce may be particularly pronounced [9]. Therefore, the degree of AI application in the field of art and design positively predicts the level of psychological distress experienced by professionals due to perceived occupational threats and future uncertainty. In summary, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H3a: Application of AI is positively correlated with psychological distress among art and design professionals.

H3a: Application of AI is positively correlated with psychological distress among art and design professionals.

Psychological distress is not only a negative emotional response triggered by the use of AI but also a key driver that encourages art and design professionals to engage in adaptive occupational behaviors, especially career exploration. According to SCCT, emotional states are vital precursors to career-related actions, and individuals are motivated to act to eliminate negative feelings and regain psychological balance and a sense of control [39]. When art and design professionals experience persistent psychological distress due to AI threats, this painful experience can lead them to form negative evaluations of their current professional environment and strongly desire to change their situation to alleviate emotional suffering [1, 18]. This motivation to seek change for relief significantly increases their valuation of alternative career paths and their willingness to explore them [69]. Career exploration behavior, as an active process of information seeking and opportunity assessment [59], serves a dual function in this context: First, it acts as a positive coping strategy by acquiring information and clarifying options to reduce uncertainty about the future, thereby cognitively restoring a sense of control and indirectly alleviating emotional stress [43]; second, it serves as a prelude to substantial career transitions, directly pointing to the possibility of leaving current sources of pressure (such as environments threatened by AI) [56]. Empirical research shows a dose-response relationship between levels of psychological distress and the frequency of exploration behaviors—that is, the higher the distress, the more frequently and intensely individuals engage in activities like information gathering, skill learning, and opportunity evaluation [2, 47]. In the context of AI technology disruption, to prevent ongoing emotional pain caused by technological replacement, individuals are more motivated to actively pursue opportunities for career transformation, skill enhancement, or cross-disciplinary growth [5]. Such exploration may involve gaining an in-depth understanding of new demands in the design industry in the AI era, exploring new modes of human-machine collaboration, seeking psychological counseling or career coaching [6], or actively evaluating fields less affected by AI or where AI can be used to enhance their creativity. Therefore, the psychological distress caused by AI applications is not just a passive emotional response; it also acts as a motivating force that encourages art and design professionals to actively pursue strategic career development to enhance their emotional well-being and professional situation. Based on this, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H3b: Psychological distress is positively related to career exploration behavior among art design professionals.

Job insecurity and psychological distress

Job insecurity is defined as the subjective concern about the continuity of employment and the expectation of being threatened by unemployment [11]. Its core centers on long-term uncertainty and a feeling of loss of control. When art and design professionals believe that their creative roles, skills, and career prospects are threatened by the widespread use of AI, they experience intense job insecurity. This feeling of insecurity itself becomes a persistent and harmful source of psychological stress [49]. Individuals must constantly mobilize cognitive and emotional resources to handle the uncertainty of this future. This extended state of vigilance and worry can exhaust psychological resources, directly causing emotional distress such as anxiety, depression, and perceived pressure [46, 68]. Specifically, concerns about ‘skills becoming outdated’ and ‘being replaced by AI’ continuously erode individuals’ emotional stability, leading to impaired functioning and a decline in subjective well-being [28, 55]. In creative work, professional identity is closely linked to personal value, so job insecurity has a particularly significant impact on mental health. Based on this, we propose the following hypotheses: H4b: Job insecurity is positively correlated with psychological distress among art and design professionals. The moderating role of proactive personality

Proactive personality is defined as a stable individual tendency characterized by actively taking action to change the environment rather than passively adapting to it [4]. It is regarded as a key component of an individual’s psychological capital, providing intrinsic motivational resources and behavioral guidance for positive adaptation in changing environments [44]. In the field of career development, this trait has been shown to significantly enhance an individual’s adaptability, making them more adept at proactively identifying and seizing career opportunities [38]. In the context of rapid technological iteration, its role is particularly prominent, as highly proactive individuals are more sensitive to environmental changes [64], and tend to take forward-looking actions such as proactively learning new technologies, adjusting work modes, and even exploring entirely new career fields [44]. This intrinsic tendency to actively seek change is believed to strengthen the connection between technology application, cognition, and career behavior [38].

On the one hand, a proactive personality shapes an individual’s primary assessment of environmental stimuli, such as AI. According to stress appraisal theory [36], an individual’s initial interpretation of an event (evaluated as a ‘challenge’ or ‘threat’) is crucial. Art designers with a proactive personality are more likely to see industry disruptions and skill requirements caused by AI as manageable challenges and opportunities for skill development and creative breakthroughs. Conversely, those with a less proactive personality tend to view these as threats to their job security and existing expertise [64]. This difference in assessment directly influences the development of key personal cognitive variables in the SCCT model. Viewing AI as a challenge enhances individuals’ self-efficacy beliefs in learning and applying AI skills and fosters more positive outcome expectations regarding career exploration behavior. At the cognitive-motivational level, this leads to stronger intentions to engage in career exploration within an AI environment.

On the other hand, a proactive personality directly enhances the personal agency component in the SCCT model, thereby encouraging the transition from exploration intentions to exploration behaviors. It naturally predisposes individuals to take proactive actions that constitute career exploration behaviors [59]. In the face of AI infiltration, highly proactive professionals do not passively wait for change to happen. Instead, they actively seek out resources to learn new tools, experiment with integrating AI into their creative workflows, and continuously monitor the industry to find emerging career opportunities driven by technological shifts [38]. Therefore, this trait strengthens the behavioral pathway from environmental input to specific exploration actions, making individuals more likely to convert potential exploration motivation into actual exploration behaviors, such as information gathering, skill learning, and goal setting, when facing the uncertainties posed by AI.

In summary, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H5: Proactive personality moderates the relationship between AI application and art design professionals’ career exploration behavior.

Methods

Data collection

Before collecting data, ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the School of Art and Design at Zhengzhou University of Aeronautics (Approval No.: EA-2025037; Date: April 19, 2025). This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments, ensuring that all procedures complied with relevant Chinese regulations.

Data were collected through an online survey. The questionnaire had three sections. The first section introduced the study, explaining its goal to explore the transmission mechanism between the application of AI and the career exploration behaviors of art and design professionals. Participants were assured of anonymity and informed that their data would be used solely for academic purposes, to protect privacy and encourage honest responses. The second section included the consent form; only after giving consent could participants proceed to the third section, which contained items measuring the main constructs. To reduce order effects, the presentation order of all items was randomized using a computer-generated sequence.

A pretest (N = 70) was administered prior to the formal survey. Results showed strong internal consistency for all subscales (Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.75), with all item factor loadings above 0.6. After clarifying a few items to improve logical flow, understanding improved significantly. The average completion time was 6.3 min (SD = 1.25 min), which served as an objective measure to identify invalid responses (e.g., those with excessively short durations) in the main study.

Before collecting data, this study estimated the sample size. According to the empirical rule of a 10:1 ratio for the sample size in Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), the calculated sample size should be at least ten times the total number of items [25, 31]. Based on this principle, the sample size for this study should not be less than 290. Additionally, the model used in this study is relatively complex, incorporating latent variables and mediating and moderating effects. In real-world scenarios, data may contain missing values or other issues that require excluding observations. Therefore, the sample size should be increased by an additional 20%. Consequently, the final sample size for this study is 350.

Data collection occurred from June 12 to September 21, 2025. Participants were recruited through major Chinese design communities and platforms, including Zcool, Tezign, JS Design, and Huaban Pro. Of the 600 questionnaires distributed, 517 were returned, resulting in an initial response rate of 86.17%. All participants were adults (according to Chinese labor laws) and provided electronic informed consent. According to existing literature [13, 14], this study carefully screened the returned questionnaires. First, responses completed in less than 2.5 min or more than 10 min were excluded, based on the pilot test results (approximately Mean ± 3 SDs). Second, responses showing clear logical inconsistencies were removed. Finally, questionnaires with missing data for key variables were excluded. This process yielded 485 valid responses, corresponding to an effective recovery rate of 93.8%. Such a sample size exceeds the pre-calculated 350, indicating that the sample size for this study is sufficient. The demographic profile of the valid sample is presented in Table 1. Female participants slightly outnumbered males (58.6%). The largest age group was 26–35 years old (46.8%). Education levels were predominantly bachelor’s degrees or lower (54.9%) and master’s degrees (39.4%). Years of professional experience were relatively evenly distributed across the defined categories.

Measure

The measure of AI application, adapted from Sheng et al., [58], consists of 4 items. For example, “I frequently use AI tools (e.g., image generators, layout assistants) to obtain design materials or inspiration.”

Job insecurity was measured using the 3-item scale developed by Hellgren and Sverke [23]. An example item is: “I feel uneasy about the possibility of losing my job due to the growing adoption of AI in the design industry.”

Psychological distress was measured using the 5-item scale developed by Ware and Sherbourne [66] and utilized by Jiang et al., [26]. An example item is: “I often feel so down that nothing can cheer me up.”

Proactive personality was assessed using the Chinese version of the Proactive Personality Scale, adapted by Shang and Gan [57] from the original scale by Bateman and Crant [4]. This scale contains 11 items, such as: “I am always ready to help others when they are in trouble.”

Career exploration behavior was measured using the 6-item scale developed by Stumpf et al., [59]. The scale asked respondents to indicate the extent to which they had exhibited certain behaviors in the past three months. An example item is: “I have investigated other career possibilities within or related to the design field.”

A 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (disagree) to 5 (agree), was used to score the items. Demographic information such as gender, age, and years of work experience was included, consistent with earlier studies on AI [7, 13, 50].

Tools

This study used Mplus 8.8 for data analysis.

Common method bias test

Since the study’s key variables were measured via self-reports, potential common-method bias was addressed through both procedural and statistical methods. Procedurally, respondent anonymity was ensured to reduce evaluation apprehension. Questionnaire items were carefully designed and partially reverse-coded to break response patterns, and the order of measurement items was randomized to prevent hypothesis guessing. Statistically, Harman’s single-factor test was performed. An exploratory factor analysis, forcing all items onto one factor, showed it accounted for only 32.7% of the total variance, well below the 40% or 50% thresholds, indicating that common method bias was effectively controlled statistically.

Model fit

As shown in Table 4, the single-factor model tests the most parsimonious but theoretically unlikely idea that all variables originate from a single source. The two-factor model groups variables based on a broad stressor-outcome logic (AAI + JI as external/conditional threats; PD + PP+CEB as internal affective, personal, and behavioral responses). The three-factor model directly represents the proposed mediation pathway, combining the sequential mediators (JI + PD) while keeping the antecedent (AAI) and the moderator/outcome (PP + CEB) separate. The four-factor model further refines this by distinguishing the two mediators (JI; PD), but grouping the personal resource (PP) with the behavioral outcome (CEB), testing whether proactive personality and exploration behavior are distinct. The five-factor model, where each theoretical construct is uniquely specified, shows the best fit to the data across all indices and fits significantly better than any other tested model. Therefore, the five-factor model is confirmed as the optimal choice.

Mediating effect

The results of the path analysis, presented in Table 5, reveal a significant total effect of AI application on career exploration behavior (effect = 0.39, p < .001). This effect was partially mediated, with a direct effect is 0.19 (p < .001), explaining 48.7% of the total variance. The remaining 51.3% was explained by significant indirect pathways. Specifically, the indirect effects via job insecurity (AAI→JI→CEB, effect = 0.09, p< .01), via psychological distress (AAI→PD→CEB, effect = 0.07, p< .01), and via the serial path through both mediators (AAI→JI→PD→CEB, effect = 0.04, p< .05) were all statistically significant. Bootstrap analysis (5,000 iterations) confirmed these findings, as none of the 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects included zero, thereby supporting hypotheses H1 to H4. The model demonstrates partial mediation.

Discussion

Based on the SCCT, this study examines the transmission mechanism linking the application of AI to career exploration behavior among art and design professionals, mediated by job insecurity and psychological distress, and moderated by proactive personality. This mechanism can be comprehensively interpreted. SCCT provides a robust lens for understanding the dynamic interplay between environmental inputs, personal-cognitive-affective factors, and career-related behaviors [39, 40].

First, the positive relationship between AI application and career exploration behavior (H1) aligns with the SCCT principle that important contextual factors, such as technological disruptions, are key antecedents of adaptive career behaviors [39]. The widespread integration of AI, including generative AI and automated design tools, represents a significant environmental change that fundamentally transforms the art and design industry ecosystem, disrupting established work routines and skill values [41, 72]. According to SCCT, when individuals perceive such environmental shifts as threats to their existing career paths, this perception activates self-regulatory processes aimed at regaining control and managing uncertainty [40]. Career exploration—defined as purposeful behavior and cognition aimed at gathering career information [59]—is a primary expression of this self-regulation, as individuals seek information and develop plans to navigate the changed landscape.

Second, the mediating roles of job insecurity (H2) and psychological distress (H3) explain the specific cognitive and emotional processes underlying this effect. The use of AI functions as an environmental stressor. How a person perceives this stressor—particularly, the view that AI threatens job security and stability (i.e., job insecurity)—serves as a key cognitive assessment within the SCCT framework [20, 60]. Research confirms that perceiving technological replacement as a threat is a major cause of job insecurity [7]. This mental evaluation then leads to an emotional response—the feeling of anxiety and discomfort (i.e., psychological distress) [46]. According to SCCT, these thinking and feeling states greatly influence career actions [39]. The desire to reduce negative feelings associated with job insecurity and psychological distress can prompt individuals to proactively explore new options to restore career stability [27, 62]. Therefore, the process from the application of AI (the environment) to career exploration (behavior) flows through the perception of a threat (job insecurity) and the resulting emotional distress (psychological distress).

Third, the chain mediation effect (H4) further supports this process. It indicates that the cognitive appraisal of threat (job insecurity) exacerbates emotional responses (psychological distress), resulting in a combined negative internal state that strongly encourages exploratory behavior. This aligns with the idea that job insecurity is a long-term stressor that depletes psychological resources and causes distress [28], thereby encouraging adaptive career behaviors, such as exploration, to address the situation [2].

Finally, the moderating role of proactive personality (H5) adds an important personal dispositional factor to the SCCT model. The proactive personality, characterized by a consistent tendency to influence the environment [4], shapes how people interpret and respond to their career setting [39]. People with high proactive personality are more likely to see the AI-driven environment as a challenge full of opportunities rather than just a threat, potentially changing how they view the disruption [64]. Additionally, this trait directly enhances the connection between the environmental stimulus (AI) and adaptive behavior (exploration) by representing the agency part of SCCT. Proactive individuals naturally tend to take initiative, gather information, and act to shape their situation [38], making it more likely that they will turn the pressures of AI adoption into positive career exploration activities.

Theoretical contribution

First, it extends the application of SCCT to a new and important context: the influence of AI on creative professions. While SCCT has been widely used to understand career behaviors, its use in studying the AI-driven transformation of the art and design industry, a field facing significant risks of substitution [3, 19], is still in the early stages. This study extends beyond general employment contexts to examine a sector in which creativity, identity, and technical skills converge, thereby testing the theory’s explanatory power under conditions of severe occupational identity threats and skill redefinition [65]. By empirically confirming SCCT’s core ideas in the context of AI substitution threats, the research not only demonstrates the theory’s strength but also shows its usefulness in explaining how professionals in creative and knowledge-based fields handle existential technological threats that challenge their core value proposition [30].

Second, it explains and empirically confirms a dual-pathway mechanism connecting AI-induced environmental stressors to adaptive career behavior. The study identifies and confirms two parallel yet different mediating paths—one cognitive (job insecurity) and one emotional (psychological distress). This clarification advances SCCT by revealing the processes that link environmental input (AI application) to behavioral output (career exploration). It clarifies that the threat of AI replacement is not processed uniformly but can simultaneously trigger a cognitive assessment of stability (i.e., job insecurity) [7] and an emotional reaction of distress (i.e., psychological distress) [48], both of which independently and sequentially drive exploration [39]. The chain mediation result (H4) further shows how these pathways are connected, demonstrating that the cognitive appraisal of threat (job insecurity) can heighten emotional responses (psychological distress), creating a combined motivational force for behavioral change. This integrated model offers a more detailed and dynamic understanding of the stress-adaptation process than examining individual mediators, emphasizing how cognitive and emotional reactions to technology can influence one another.

Third, the research thoughtfully integrates the key dispositional trait of proactive personality into the SCCT framework as a crucial boundary condition. Although SCCT recognizes person inputs, it has been critiqued for under-specifying how stable dispositions interact with its core processes [40]. This study addresses this by showing how proactive personality moderates the direct relationship between AI application and exploration. It reveals that highly proactive individuals are more likely to channel environmental technological pressure directly into exploratory actions. This may be achieved by reframing AI-driven disruption as a manageable challenge rather than a purely threatening obstacle [36, 64], thus displaying stronger agency [4] and aligning with the proactive coping and change orientation central to this trait [38]. This finding greatly improves SCCT by highlighting that the strength and nature of the environment-behavior connection depend on individual differences in proactive disposition. It offers a more detailed, person-focused view of career adaptation, explaining why some professionals respond to AI with strategic exploration, while others experience paralyzing anxiety.

Practical implications

First, art and design professionals should actively seek information on emerging opportunities (e.g., AI-augmented design roles), upskill in digital tools (e.g., generative AI software), and participate in industry networks to mitigate perceived threats. The mediating role of psychological distress underscores the need for emotional self-regulation; professionals could benefit from mindfulness training or counseling to channel anxiety into constructive career planning. Moreover, individuals with lower proactive personality traits should intentionally cultivate agency through mentorship or structured self-assessment tools, as a proactive disposition amplifies the capacity to turn AI-related challenges into growth opportunities.

Second, organizations and employers should implement transparent communication about AI integration plans to reduce ambiguity-induced insecurity, for example by clarifying how AI tools will augment rather than replace human creativity. To address psychological distress, organizations could provide mental health resources (e.g., employee assistance programs) and design AI training workshops to build competence and confidence. Structuring team compositions to include proactive individuals can foster a culture of innovation, as they are more likely to reframe AI disruptions positively. Collaborations with researchers, as emphasized in science-practice partnerships, can help tailor AI tools to practical needs, enhancing relevance and reducing resistance.

Third, policymakers and industry associations should support lifelong learning and mental well-being in the creative economy. Funding for reskilling programs (e.g., subsidies for AI certifications) can reduce fears of skill obsolescence, while regulations that promote ethical AI use may alleviate job insecurity. Public campaigns highlighting AI as a tool for creativity—rather than a threat—can shift societal perceptions. Additionally, grants for research-practice partnerships, as seen in successful innovation ecosystems, can bridge the gap between theoretical insights and ground-level needs, ensuring that policies are informed by empirical evidence.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of cross-sectional data restricts our ability to draw definitive causal inferences. Although the hypothesized model is grounded in theory, the relationships among variables (e.g., between AI application, mediators, and career exploration) were tested at a single point in time. This design cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causality or the influence of unmeasured third variables. For instance, individuals actively exploring new careers might be more inclined to adopt AI tools, creating a potential bidirectional relationship. Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to establish the temporal precedence and causal directions among these constructs more robustly.

Second, the reliance on a sample of art and design professionals from China limits the generalizability of the findings. The impact of AI and individuals’ responses to it are likely shaped by socio-cultural contexts, industry-specific norms, and national policies. For example, the level of job insecurity or the role of a proactive personality may manifest differently across cultural settings with varying social safety nets, career mobility patterns, and attitudes toward technological disruption. Consequently, the proposed mechanisms may not be fully transferable to other cultural or professional contexts. Future studies are encouraged to replicate and extend this model with diverse samples from different countries and creative industries to examine its cross-cultural validity and boundary conditions.

Finally, relying solely on self-reported data, while a practical way to capture internal states such as perceptions and emotions, has inherent limitations. These include the risk of common method variance and biases, such as social desirability, which, despite statistical checks, may still affect the observed relationships among the constructs of AI application, job insecurity, psychological distress, and career exploration. Future research could improve the validity of findings by combining self-reports with more objective or multiple-source indicators. For example, career exploration behavior could be assessed through verified participation in professional development activities or supervisor evaluations, and the extent of AI application could be measured using organizational implementation metrics rather than relying solely on personal perceptions.

Conclusions

This study concludes that AI applications significantly influence career exploration behavior among art and design professionals through a dual-path mechanism involving perceptions of job insecurity and experiences of psychological distress. Furthermore, a serial mediation pathway confirms that AI-induced job insecurity can exacerbate psychological distress, which in turn motivates career exploration. The moderating role of proactive personality suggests that individuals with this trait are better equipped to turn AI challenges into proactive career development actions. However, caution is warranted when interpreting these relationships due to the cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference, and the culturally specific Chinese sample, which restricts generalizability. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish causality and replicate the model in diverse cultural contexts. These insights are crucial for developing targeted organizational support systems and fostering resilient, sustainable career pathways in the evolving AI-driven creative economy.
