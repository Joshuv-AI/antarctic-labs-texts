Mechanical thromboprophylaxis for preventing intradialytic hypotension in people undergoing maintenance haemodialysis
Authors: Natsuki Taira, Hiroki Nishiwaki, Noyuri Yamaji, Reiji Takami, Hisashi Noma, Erika Ota, William MM Levack, Takeshi Hasegawa, supported by Cochrane Kidney and Transplant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:This review aims to evaluate the benefits and harms of mechanical thromboprophylaxis for preventing intradialytic hypotension in adults with kidney failure undergoing maintenance haemodialysis.

Background

Description of the condition

Intradialytic hypotension (IDH) is one of the most common clinical complications in people with kidney failure undergoing haemodialysis (HD). It is characterised by a decrease in systolic blood pressure (SBP) of at least 20 mmHg or a reduction in mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 10 mmHg, accompanied by hypotension‐related symptoms such as abdominal discomfort, yawning, sighing, nausea, vomiting, muscle cramps, restlessness, dizziness, fainting, or anxiety [1]. The reported prevalence ranges from 15% to 50% of HD sessions [2]. IDH occurs when a reduction in circulating blood volume due to ultrafiltration is compounded by an inadequate compensatory response in cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance [3]. It is more likely to occur in the presence of factors such as advanced age, reduced cardiac reserve, autonomic dysfunction, predominant endogenous vasodilation, and impaired vasopressin responsiveness [4, 5]. Rapid fluid removal and other dialysis parameters can also act as triggering factors [6]. IDH increases the incidence of intradialytic discomfort, including fatigue, nausea, and headache [7]. Moreover, IDH is associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including hospitalisation for heart failure, increased death, and cognitive decline. These adverse outcomes are mediated by impaired organ perfusion, such as dialysis‐induced myocardial stunning, cerebral ischaemia, and intestinal ischaemia. In addition, IDH contributes to reduced quality of life (QoL), inadequate dialysis delivery, and vascular access complications [6, 7, 8, 9]. Conventional management of IDH includes the use of cooled dialysate, adjustment of dialysate sodium concentration and dry weight, modification of dialysis duration and ultrafiltration rate, transition to haemodiafiltration, and pharmacological interventions [3]. However, lowering the dialysate temperature may induce cold discomfort in participants [10], while increasing dialysate sodium concentration can lead to excessive thirst, interdialytic weight gain, and hypertension. Although the administration of midodrine has been reported to increase blood pressure (BP) during dialysis, the number of cases studied remains limited, and its safety profile has not been sufficiently evaluated [3]. Overall, the evidence supporting conventional management strategies for IDH remains limited [2]. IDH disproportionately affects not only people with medical risk factors (older age, reduced cardiac reserve, autonomic dysfunction, diabetes, or multiple comorbidities) but also socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, including those with limited education or income, those living in remote or rural areas, women, and people from certain racial or ethnic backgrounds, who tend to have poorer access to renal care [11, 12, 13].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Mechanical thromboprophylaxis is a non‐pharmacological intervention originally developed to prevent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism, thereby avoiding the bleeding risk associated with pharmacological prophylaxis. By promoting venous return through external lower‐limb compression, mechanical thromboprophylaxis may also help maintain central blood volume and stroke volume during HD, acting as a compensatory mechanism against IDH [14, 15]. This intervention therefore represents a useful, low‐cost, non‐invasive option for adults receiving maintenance HD, particularly those at high risk of IDH or for whom pharmacological treatments are contraindicated. The comparator in this review is conventional HD without mechanical thromboprophylaxis. We will evaluate two forms of mechanical thromboprophylaxis: compression stockings and intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC). Common adverse effects of both compression stockings and IPC include skin pressure injury, pruritus (itching) and discomfort; and both are contraindicated in people with severe peripheral arterial disease (PAD) [16, 17, 18]. IPC is also contraindicated in people with lower‐limb ischaemia, active skin infection, and active DVT or pulmonary embolism [14, 19]. Because their mechanism, technical requirements, application durations, and access barriers differ, we will evaluate the effects of compression stockings and IPC as separate interventions.

Why it is important to do this review

Mechanical thromboprophylaxis is low‐cost, non‐invasive, and easy to apply, making it feasible for implementation across a wide range of clinical settings, including small dialysis units. It is also well suited for elderly people and for those with multiple comorbidities, in whom pharmacological treatment may be contraindicated. Although several randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have suggested that compression stockings and IPC may reduce the incidence of IDH, the evidence remains inconsistent [16, 20, 21, 22, 23]. If the effectiveness of these non‐pharmacological interventions is established, they may contribute to reductions in medication‐related costs and the need for emergency interventions, thereby alleviating the overall burden on the healthcare system. While generally well tolerated, compression‐based interventions may be limited by discomfort, skin complications, contraindications such as severe PAD, and adherence during dialysis sessions.

This review will focus on the effectiveness of mechanical, non‐pharmacological interventions for the prevention and management of IDH in people undergoing HD. Although an existing systematic review by Torres and colleagues has assessed the effects of compression stockings and IPC [19], that review includes a mixture of RCTs and non‐randomised case series and evaluates outcomes that differ from those examined in the present review. Moreover, it did not employ GRADE methodology to assess the quality of evidence. Since the previous review, additional RCTs have emerged [23], warranting an updated, RCT‐focused synthesis incorporating GRADE assessment.

An updated and comprehensive review of the effects of mechanical thromboprophylaxis in people with kidney failure undergoing HD is therefore warranted.

Methods

We will follow the Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews when conducting the review [24] and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 for the reporting [25]. For this protocol, we followed both guides until the end of the Methods sections.

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include RCTs, including those with cross‐over designs and cluster randomisation. Quasi‐RCTs will be excluded because their allocation methods are predictable and lack sufficient concealment, thereby posing a high risk of selection bias. We will not impose any language or date restrictions on the search strategy. For all potentially relevant studies published in languages other than English, we will attempt to obtain translations. If a translation is not available prior to submission of the completed review, such studies will be listed under ‘awaiting classification’.

Types of participants

We will focus on adults with kidney failure undergoing maintenance HD. This will include people receiving HD in hospitals, other healthcare facilities, or at home. Eligible participants will be adults aged 18 years or older who are receiving HD for kidney failure. If a study includes both eligible and ineligible participants, it will be included only if outcomes for participants undergoing HD are reported separately or can be obtained from the study authors. Studies will be included if at least 80% of participants are adults with kidney failure receiving maintenance HD.

Types of interventions

Studies that investigate any type of mechanical thromboprophylaxis as an intervention during HD sessions will be included. There will be no restrictions on the type, manufacturer, model, number of chambers, pressure settings, inflation‐deflation cycles, site of application (e.g. below the knee, thigh, or including the abdomen), duration, frequency, or operating mode of the compression devices used. If other types of mechanical thromboprophylaxis beyond compression stockings or IPC are identified, they will be reported descriptively and considered separately.

Each of the two interventions will be defined as follows.

Compression stockings: non‐pneumatic elastic garments applied to the lower limbs, which provide continuous external compression without the need for an external power source.IPC: mechanical devices that deliver intermittent cycles of inflation and deflation via cuffs wrapped around the lower limbs, applying air pressure in a controlled manner to promote venous return. IPC devices can be single‐chamber (i.e. one cuff around part of the leg that inflates and deflates as a whole) or multi‐chamber (i.e. with multiple compartments that can be inflated and deflated sequentially from the foot to the thigh). IPC devices can also cover only one part of the leg (e.g. the foot or calf) or the whole leg.

Critical outcomes

Symptomatic intradialytic hypotension

IDH will be defined as a decrease in BP during HD, as defined by the original study authors, accompanied by at least one symptom attributable to hypotension (e.g. dizziness, muscle cramps, headache, nausea, cold sweats, or syncope) [1]. For the incidence outcome, we will extract the occurrence of symptomatic IDH during the observation period as reported in each study. Episodes without documented BP measurements will not be considered symptomatic IDH.

We will collect and report data on when episodes of symptomatic IDH required therapeutic intervention (e.g. fluid administration, discontinuation of ultrafiltration, postural adjustment, or use of vasopressors), but we will not analyse these data separately.

Fatigue

We will assess fatigue using validated patient‐reported outcome measures, such as the Fatigue Assessment Scale [28], the Post‐Dialysis Fatigue Self‐Assessment Scale [29], dialysis recovery time, the Visual Analogue Scale, and the Fatigue Scale. If these standardised measures are not used, fatigue will be assessed according to the criteria defined by the study authors.

Data collection and analysis

We will perform the review following the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [38], and we will perform the data analyses using Review Manager (RevMan) [39].

Selection of studies

We will combine records retrieved from different databases, and we will perform the entire screening process using Covidence software, which can also identify duplicates in the same way as a standard reference manager system [40]. Two review authors (from NT, HN, RT) will independently screen the titles and abstracts of all identified studies and discard studies that are not applicable. If discrepancies in decisions occur, they will be resolved by group discussion. We will then obtain the full text records of potentially eligible studies and reassess their eligibility for inclusion. Again, any discrepancies will be resolved through group discussion. If necessary, we will contact the authors of the primary studies to request further information to decide whether to include individual studies. We will identify duplicate studies (those reporting on the same set of participants) and combine these under a single study identification.

If a study includes more than two intervention arms, only those interventions that meet the eligibility criteria will be included in this review.

Data extraction and management

Data extraction will be carried out independently by two review authors (from NT, HN, RT) using standard data extraction forms in Covidence [40]. Prior to full data extraction, we will develop and pilot‐test the data collection form on two studies to ensure that we can extract data in the appropriate format and structure for subsequent analysis. We will translate studies reported in non‐English‐language journals using software before assessment. When more than one publication of a study exists, reports will be grouped together, and the publication with the most complete data will be used in the analyses. Where relevant outcomes are only published in earlier versions, these data will be used. Any discrepancies between published versions will be highlighted.

We will only assess the risk of bias for critical outcomes. Any disagreements will be resolved by consensus.

We will assess the effect of assignment to the intervention. We will analyse participants in the intervention groups to which they were randomised, regardless of the intervention they actually received; and we will include all randomised participants in the outcome analyses, i.e. we will perform our analyses based on the intention‐to‐treat principle. We will use RevMan for all statistical analyses [39]. For dichotomous outcomes, we plan to calculate risk ratios (RR) with 95% CIs. For continuous outcomes, we will measure the mean difference (MD) with its 95% CI if all retrieved records provide data on the same scale, or the standardised mean difference (SMD) with its 95% CI if different scales were used during reporting. For SMD, we will use the rule of thumb where Cohen's d = 0.2 will be considered a 'small' effect size, 0.5 represents a 'medium' effect size, and 0.8 a 'large' effect size [46].

Unit of analysis issues

For studies using parallel‐group designs, we will assess participants as randomised per intervention group. When more than one intervention group is identified, we will collect data for those intervention groups that meet the inclusion criteria for this review. To overcome unit of analysis errors for a study that could contribute multiple, correlated comparisons, we will combine all relevant experimental intervention groups of the study into a single group, and combine all relevant comparator intervention groups into a single comparator group [47]. For the analysis of results from cross‐over trials, we will include only the first period results (i.e. use only data from the first treatment period, before the cross‐over) in the overall analysis [44].

For cluster‐RCTs, the unit of analysis will be the cluster. To avoid unit of analysis errors, we will re‐analyse the data by taking account of the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC). If the ICC is not reported, we will attempt to obtain it from the study authors or estimate it from similar studies. If adjustment is not possible, the study will not be included in the meta‐analysis and will be reported narratively [44].

Dealing with missing data

When needed, we will request further information from the original authors by written correspondence (e.g. emailing the corresponding authors), and any relevant information obtained in this manner will be included in the review. Evaluation of important numerical data, such as screened, randomised participants, as well as intention‐to‐treat, as‐treated and per‐protocol populations, will be carefully performed. Attrition rates (e.g. drop‐outs, losses to follow‐up, and withdrawals) will be investigated. Issues of missing data and imputation methods (e.g. last observation carried forward) will be critically appraised [47].

For studies that do not report continuous outcomes as means or SD, and where authors do not respond to enquiries for more detail, we will convert medians, quartiles, and ranges to means and SDs using the quantile estimation method as proposed by McGrath and colleagues [48].

Reporting bias assessment

We will examine the possibility of within‐study selective outcome reporting for each study included in the review. We will search electronic sources such as PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the WHO ICTRP for study protocols of included studies to assess whether outcome reporting appears sufficiently complete and transparent.

We will assess small‐study effects using funnel plots [49]. Recognising that visual interpretation of funnel plots is inherently subjective and requires cautious interpretation, where appropriate, we will supplement visual inspection with a formal test for funnel plot asymmetry [50]. We will perform such tests only when there are sufficient studies, generally at least 10 studies in a meta‐analysis, and when the included studies vary sufficiently in size or precision. For dichotomous outcomes, we will use Harbord's modified test [51] or another appropriate test, such as Peters' test [52], depending on the effect measure and data structure. The results of these tests will be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with visual inspection of the funnel plot and consideration of other possible explanations for asymmetry, and will not be regarded as definitive evidence of non‐reporting bias.

Synthesis methods

We will perform random‐effects meta‐analysis using the inverse‐variance method [53], with between‐study variance (τ²) estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) [54]. For random‐effects meta‐analyses, we will calculate CIs using the Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman (HKSJ) method [55] when more than two studies are included, and the estimated between‐study variance is greater than zero [53]. When only two studies are included, or when the estimated between‐study variance is zero, we will use the Wald‐type CI method [53]. When two or three studies are included in a random‐effects meta‐analysis, we will conduct a sensitivity analysis comparing HKSJ and Wald‐type CIs, where feasible. Any differences will be interpreted cautiously, acknowledging that Wald‐type intervals may be too narrow whereas HKSJ intervals may be overly wide when the number of studies is very small [53]. We will analyse data using RevMan [39].

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

Within each main comparison, a subgroup analysis by economic status (high‐income versus low‐ and middle‐income countries, World Bank Atlas method) will be conducted when 10 or more studies are available within that comparison [60].

We will consider the P value from the test for subgroup differences to determine whether a statistically significant subgroup difference is detected. We will use this test to assess the difference between the meta‐analysed effect estimates for each subgroup. A P value < 0.1 will be considered indicative of a significant subgroup effect. Subgroup analyses may not be feasible if the number of included studies is insufficient or the available information is inadequate.

Certainty of the evidence assessment

We will use the GRADE approach to prepare summary of findings (SoF) tables, using the GRADEpro GDT software [43, 62, 63]. In the SoF tables, we will present outcomes for symptomatic IDH, fatigue, and any adverse events. We will use T1 and T2 for GRADE. GRADE uses five factors for assessing the certainty of evidence: 1) RoB, 2) consistency of effect, 3) imprecision (we will calculate the optimal information size), 4) indirectness, and 5) publication bias. We will use the overall judgement of risk of bias for each outcome. ‘Low’ risk of bias will indicate ‘no limitation (the certainty will not be rated down)’; ‘Some concerns’ will indicate either ‘no limitation’ or ‘serious limitation (the certainty will be rated down one level)’; and ‘High’ risk of bias will indicate either ‘serious limitation’ or ‘very serious limitation (the certainty will be rated down two levels)’. We will use the 'overall' risk of bias when assessing and reporting the certainty of evidence for GRADE and in the SoF tables. We will justify all decisions to downgrade study quality in footnotes, and we will provide comments to aid the reader’s understanding of the review where necessary.

Based on defined criteria for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness of evidence, imprecision, and the presence of publication bias, we will downgrade the evidence by one level for serious or two levels for very serious limitations. We will describe the levels of summary evidence as 'high', 'moderate', 'low', or 'very low'.

Equity considerations

People on dialysis for kidney failure who have the lowest education and income levels, live in remote areas, come from certain racial or ethnic backgrounds, and are women tend to have poorer access to renal care and dialysis, worse cardiovascular outcomes, and a higher risk of death [11, 12, 13]. Moreover, socioeconomic disadvantage may affect access to technologically dependent mechanical thromboprophylaxis methods, such as IPC. This review will systematically address health inequities by extracting and reporting equity‐related information based on the PROGRESS‐Plus framework [64]. From all included studies, data will be collected on participants’ sex and gender, race and ethnicity (as defined and reported in each study), educational attainment, socioeconomic status, and place of residence (e.g. urban or rural settings). In the 'Results' section, these equity‐related characteristics will be narratively summarised to the extent that data are available. However, given that few studies are expected to report effect estimates stratified by these equity‐related characteristics, this assessment will remain exploratory. For subgroup analyses based on the economic context of the study countries, we will apply the World Bank country income classification (Atlas method) [60]. Countries will be categorised as high‐income countries or low‐ and middle‐income countries according to their 2026 classification. It is anticipated that the available data will not be sufficient to produce separate SoF tables for each equity‐related subgroup. Therefore, SoF tables will not be stratified by equity categories. Any subgroup findings will be treated as exploratory. Where such analyses are added during the review process, they will be clearly identified as post hoc, where applicable [64].

Consumer involvement

This review focuses on people with kidney failure receiving HD. Approaches to consumer involvement, including workshops, advisory groups, and focus groups, were considered. Due to limited resources, the substantial physical and time burdens imposed by HD, and concerns that additional research participation could exacerbate inequities for those who frequently experience IDH, people receiving HD or their family members will not be directly engaged in the planning and conduct of this review. As an alternative, the SONG‐HD core outcome set will be adopted. The outcomes adopted in this review were selected by people receiving HD and family members themselves through SONG‐HD consumer focus groups. Thus, although consumers will not be directly engaged, the outcomes to be reported have been selected by consumers, and these people's voices have already been incorporated at the stage of outcome selection. Through this approach, person‐centred priorities will be reflected in the review design without imposing additional research‐related demands on people undergoing HD. Direct forms of consumer involvement will be considered for future iterations of this review, should resources and infrastructure permit.
