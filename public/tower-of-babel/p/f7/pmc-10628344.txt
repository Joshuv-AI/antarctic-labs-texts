The alteration of cortical microstructure similarity in drug-resistant epilepsy correlated with mTOR pathway genes
Authors: Hang Cao, Penghu Wei, Yuda Huang, Ningrui Wang, Lin-Ai Guo, Xiaotong Fan, Zhenming Wang, Liankun Ren, Yueshan Piao, Jie Lu, Yongzhi Shan, Xiaosong He, Guoguang Zhao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDrug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) is associated with distributed laminar disruptions due to cytoarchitectonic pathologies, which may be characterized by multimodal MRI approaches such as morphometric similarity networks (MSNs). However, the genetic and histological underpinning of MSN alterations in DRE remains poorly understood, hampering its clinical application.MethodsWe enrolled 60 patients with DRE and 23 controls, acquiring T1 and diffusion spectrum imaging data with a 3.0T GE SIGNA Premier scanner. Morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) were constructed and analyzed to identify microstructure similarity differences between patients and controls. Subsequently, patient-specific MSN alteration patterns were associated with gene expression using the GAMBA tool, and layer-specific neuronal signature mapping were also applied. During these analyses, sex and age were adjusted as covariates and multiple comparisons corrections were applied when appropriate.FindingsWe observed widespread MSN changes in patients with DRE and identified five distinct MSN alteration patterns. Major patterns presented pattern-specific associations with expressions of epilepsy-related genes, particularly involving the mTOR pathway. Histological analysis confirmed the presence of cortical microstructure changes in areas with MSN alterations and revealed cellular abnormalities matching the aforementioned genetic risks.InterpretationOur findings highlight the potential of quantifying laminar-related microstructure integrity using MSN to uncover the cytoarchitectonic changes in the pathophysiology of DRE. This approach may facilitate the identification of genetic and histological underpinnings of MSN alterations in DRE, ultimately aiding in the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.FundingsThe National Natural Science Foundation of China, the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China, and the Beijing Municipal Health Commission.Keywords: Drug-resistant epilepsy, Laminar-related microstructure organization, Morphometric similarity networks, mTOR

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We performed a PubMed search for studies published, up to July 22, 2023, evaluating the knowledge of cortical laminar similarity alteration in the patient with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). The search terms used were (drug-resistant epilepsy) AND ((microstructure similarity) OR (cortical laminar similarity)). Ten study reports were found and primarily demonstrated the existing evidence of laminar alteration in animal DRE models and cases, as well as specific MRI appearances associated with focal cortical dysplasia and distinct cortical morphometric alteration patterns in temporal lobe epilepsy. However, no results of laminar or microstructure similarity specific gene expression analysis have been reported.

Added value of this study

This study integrated noninvasive MR imaging phenotyping, transcriptomics, and histologic evidence, providing a comprehensive understanding of the genetic and histopathological correlates for laminar-related microstructure alterations described by morphometric similarity network (MSN) in patients with DRE. Our findings demonstrate the potential of MSN to characterize the integrity of cytoarchitecture in neurological diseases like epilepsy, adding value to the existing literature on the subject.

Implications of all the available evidence

Combining our findings with existing evidence, we highlight the relevance of MSN integrity to human health in the context of epilepsy, revealing their association with specific gene expression features involving in known epileptic and focal cortical dysplasia pathways. Our study advances the understanding of disease mechanisms underlying DRE and suggests that MSN analysis could be used to inform future therapeutic approaches and potentially improve surgical strategies for patients with DRE.

Introduction

The laminar structure is one of most apparent and conservative features of the brain cortex.1 Through histological tract tracing across species, it has been consistently reported that cortico-cortical connections are mostly found between similar cortical laminars.2, 3, 4 Such micro-to-macro scale connectomic principle has provided promising interpretations for the brain's complex functionalities, and it has been preliminarily supported by recent laminar functional MRI studies as well.5 These evidence suggest that the similarity between laminar structures of different regions may withhold unique characteristics of the connections between them, and can serve important role in shaping brain connectomes. Disruption to the healthy organization of laminar structures, on the other hand, can lead to various neurological conditions, rendering both local and remote adverse impacts to the brain.6,7

A prototypical example of lamination-irrupted lesion is focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), which is one of the predominant causes of drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). The FCD type I is characterized by abnormal cortical lamination, and altered neuronal densities among laminar hierarchy were found in FCD type II lesions.8,9 The GABAergic association neurons, as the largest transcriptomic changes in DRE, are found distributed along the laminar structures.10 Moreover, Hong et al. found that different FCD types are associated with different functional connectivity patterns, providing support of phenotyping connectomic disruptions in DRE through the lens of laminar organization.11

However, the direct evaluation of laminar organizations in DRE is still rare. Nowadays, the laminar-related microstructure can be quantified by multimodal MR imaging techniques. For instance, by analyzing a high-resolution 3D histological model of a complete human brain, the Bigbrain, Wagstyl et al. found that cortical microstructure metrics including curvature and thickness are associated with laminar hierarchy.12 In addition, several metrics from diffusion imaging can also inform the laminar structure, such as quantitative anisotropy (QA) and isotropic diffusion component (ISO), which are orientation distribution function (ODF) derivatives from diffusion spectrum imaging known to be sensitive to laminar hierarchy.13,14 QA and restricted diffusion imaging (RDI) are also associated with neuronal and dendrite densities.15 To unify all these laminar-sensitive measures, Seidlitz et al. introduced the morphometric similarity network (MSN) to calculate the Pearson correlation coefficients of those above morphometric and diffusion metrics between different brain regions.16 They found that human MSN modules recapitulated known cortical cytoarchitectonic divisions that were microscopically differentiated by cortical lamination Patterns. This evidence seems to suggest the association between MSN and inter-regional laminar similarity.16 Accordingly, this technique may provide a non-invasive quantification of the integrity of the laminar-related microstructure organization in DRE.

Laminar alterations are also related to known genetic risks in DRE. It has been found that the hyperactivity of the mTOR genetic pathway, a well-known epileptogenic pathway in DRE, can lead to the proliferation of laminar-specific balloon cells in patients with FCD type II lesions.17 The prevalence of FCD type I lesion is also associated with the risk gene SLC35A2.18 Accordingly, the expression of these risk gene in DRE can lead to localized disruptions in laminar organization and hence may be quantified through MRI imaging. For instance, mutation in SLC35A2 is the predominant cause of mild malformation of cortical development with oligodendroglial hyperplasia in epilepsy (MOGHE), a subtype of DRE, which can be characterized by laminar subcortical hyperintensities in FLAIR and T2 sequences.19 In generalized epilepsy, including both drug-sensitive and drug-resistant, Li et al. reported that epilepsy-related risk gene-set was correlated with T1 extracted regional MSN differences.20 Thus, we hypothesize that theoretically, the abnormalities in laminar-related microstructure may colocalize with expressions of another epilepsy type, drug-resistant focal epilepsy-related risk genes across the brain. This hypothesis can be tested through a recently developed technique, imaging transcriptomics.21 By evaluating the special correlation between the imaging phenotype (i.e., MSN anomalies) and AHBA open-source transcriptomics database, we can identify the gene list best interpreting the genetic substrates of such imaging phenotype. In DRE studies, Larivi'ere et al. applied imaging transcriptomics analysis pipeline to the ENIGMA epilepsy database and found that, in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), the topological features of the patients' structural covariate network (SCN) was associated with the expression patterns of epilepsy risk genes.

Accordingly, here we use MSN to quantify the laminar-related microstructure in patients with DRE and apply imaging transcriptomics to explore the association between abnormalities in the microstructure organization and the genetic risks underneath these structural anomalies. To do so, we derived MSN measures from 60 patients with DRE and identified atypical MSN patterns among these patients. Subsequently, we conducted imaging transcriptomics for each atypical MSN pattern to explore their relevance to known genetic pathways associated with the prevalence of DRE. Last, we verified our imaging transcriptomics findings of atypical MSN patterns with histological analysis of surgical resections to show that the cytoarchitecture of regions with MSN anomalies is also presented with cellular abnormalities matching the expression of epilepsy risk genes. By doing so, our study unifies noninvasive MR imaging phenotyping, transcriptomics, and histologic evidences, thereby providing supports for using MSN to characterize epileptogenic changes to the cytoarchitecture of the brain in DRE.

Methods

Ethics statement and patients

The study cohort included 60 participants and was collected based on patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) diagnosed and treated in Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University. The inclusion criteria for this study were preoperative T1-MPRAGE and DSI availability. All patients included in the study had focal onset seizures defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). Cases of Focal Bilateral Tonic-Clonic Seizures (FBTCS) and suspected cases were excluded based on initial evaluations and additional testing, such as ictal behaviors and EEG findings. Patients under the age of fourteen or with visible artifacts on T1-MPRAGE or DSI scans used for MSN calculation were also excluded. The drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) diagnosis was made based on ILAE definition as those who have not achieved seizure freedom despite appropriate treatment with two antiseizure medications. In the local epilepsy center, the diagnosis and treatment followed a multidisciplinary approach, involving clinical evaluation, neuropsychological testing, routine and video-EEG, and thin-layer MRI for diagnosis and, where possible, localization and lateralization of the epileptic focus. For complex cases, additional examinations such as PET, magnetoencephalography, stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG), and genetic sequencing were available. In cases of patients undergoing surgery, fMRI was the primary preoperative assistive evaluation method due to dissatisfaction with compliance for WADA testing in the local center. The main surgical approaches included resective surgery, such as temporal and non-temporal surgery, RF-TC, and LITT. Temporal surgery involved individual-tailored standard anterior temporal lobectomy, surgery including but not limited to resection of the temporal lobe and surgery resected specific lesions within the temporal lobe. Non-temporal surgery referred to seizure foci resection not involving temporal lobe structures. The patients routinely underwent postoperative CT scan. Surgical outcomes were evaluated using the Engel surgical outcome scale through regular telephone follow-up and clinic visits if necessary. Outcomes were classified as good (Engel I) or poor (Engel II-IV) separately. For the control cohort, 23 adult participants were enrolled after passing the neurological and psychological examination. A detailed description table of included patients and controls was included in Table 1 and Table S1, Supplementary File 1. The ethics committee of Xuanwu Hospital has proved the study protocol (reference number: LYS[2021]-117), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians (for underage patients with epilepsy only).

Image acquisition and processing

The T1-MPRAGE and DSI data were collected using a 3.0T GE SIGNA Premier scanner at Xuanwu Hospital. The T1-MPRAGE imaging scan parameters were as follows: TR/TE = 2477/2.69 ms, flip angle = 8°, slice thickness = 1 mm, spacing between slices = 1 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. The DSI scan parameters were as follow: TR/TE = 5589/84.2 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 224 × 224 mm2, slice thickness = 2 mm, spacing between slices = 2 mm, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3, total diffusion encoding directions = 257, bmax = 7000 s/mm2. For T1-MPRAGE images, we first used Freesurfer with an external annotation file based on the Desikan Killiany atlas to partition the whole-brain surface into 308 equal-sized ROIs. The size of each ROI was about 500 mm2. The visual checking was executed to evaluate topological defects and segmentation boundary inaccuracy. The QSIPREP was then used to preprocess the T1 and DSI scan data.22 The anatomical data pre-processing steps included N4 bias field correction, skull stripping, spatial normalization, and brain tissue segmentation. The diffusion data pre-processing steps included MP-PCA denoising, B1 bias correction, head motion correction, Eddy current correction, and 1 mm3 resampling. Eight brain morphometric metrics included mean curvature (MC), surface area (SA), gaussian curvature (GC), grey matter volume (GM), cortical thickness (CT) derived from T1-MPRAGE imaging, and isotropic diffusion component (ISO), quantitative anisotropy (QA), restricted diffusion imaging (RDI) derived from the diffusion spectrum imaging was extracted with Freesurfer and DSIstudio.

Morphometric similarity calculation and pattern recognition

Based on the methods introduced by Morgan et al. the 308 ROI-MSN of the study cohort and control cohort was constructed by averaging the coefficient matrix of Pearson's correlation between ROI pairs across eight brain morphometric metrics.23 The regional MSN value was the mean correlation coefficients between the target region (ROI) and the rest regions. The spatial distribution of regional MSN values in 308 ROIs at the whole-brain level formed an MSN map. After validating the linear assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variance for residuals, and linearity, and observing that the linear mixed-effects model accounting for matching as a random effect demonstrated comparable performance between matched patients with DRE and HCs (results could be found in Tables S1 and S2, Supplementary File 2), we proceeded to adjust for covariates in the MSN map using a linear regression model, incorporating sex and age, as implemented by Li et al.24,25 We used the sex data self-reported by study participants.

In the pattern recognition step, we tried two strategies to minimize heterogeneity inside each pattern. First, we applied the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) clustering algorithm on the MSN map of all participants. We also measured spatial distribution characteristics, including patient-control differentiated ROI and individual MSN alteration represented by the regional Z-score. Notably, here we used the mean and standard deviation of the control cohort instead of the patient with DRE cohort as classical Z-transformation would do to reveal its relative position in the control cohort.26,27 The more positive and negative Z of specific patients would represent a greater level of MSN alterations.

Morphometric similarity pattern-gene expression correlation and bioinformatics analysis

To find the genes correlated with each morphometric similarity pattern, we used the Gene Annotation by Macroscale Brain-imaging Association (GAMBA) tool to correlate the reference gene expression from the Allen human brain atlas (AHBA, http://human.brain-map.org) with the average MSN z-score map of each pattern.28 The AHBA expression data was fetched via the software Abagen and was modified to fit the equal size parcellation atlas used. Since the tissue source of AHBA data was predominantly located in the left hemisphere, the correlation test was performed only on the left hemisphere. The permuted spatial null model was used to examine the spatial specificity of correlated genes. A Circos plot was used to visualize the overlap between pattern-specific gene lists. The pathway enrichment status of gene lists was analyzed via the software Metascore. We performed the Layer-specific neuronal signature mapping by following Marquez-Galera et al.‘s protocol. The referenced single-cell RNA-seq data and cell types taxonomy is available for public access through the Allen Brain Map portal (https://portal.brain-map.org/atlases-and-data/rnaseq/human-multiple-cortical-areas-smart-seq).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of this study was performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and packages (see Table S2, Supplementary File 1 for detailed information). All MSN matrices are available for all participants. However, some of the clinical variables are not available in some patients (Noted in Fig. 3). These samples were treated as null and omitted during the statistical analyses. The significance of categorical variables was ascertained using either the uncorrected Chi-squared test or the Fisher's exact test, contingent on the expected cell counts. The distribution normality of continuous variables such as age, epilepsy duration, and MSN alteration burden was checked by Shapiro–Wilk test and quantile–quantile plot (see Fig. S1, Supplementary File 1). Based on the distribution type, the significance was determined by the independent t-test and nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis H test. We utilized the two-sided hypergeometric test to quantify the non-random association probability between ontology entities and a gene list specific to the given pattern with a significance level set at P < 0.001.29 The Bonferroni correction was applied in multiple hypothesis testing. The spherical permutation with 1000 times randomly rotated parcellation was applied in the gene score-MSN value correlation test. The significance criteria encompassed a P-value < 0.05, in conjunction with the stipulation that the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the association measures should not overlap with the null value. Additionally, Haldane-Anscombe correction was applied to calculate odds ratios in scenarios where zero values were encountered.

Results

Study design

In this study, we enrolled a cohort of 60 patients diagnosed with DRE. To create a demographically matched comparison group, we identified 23 healthy controls (HCs) specifically matched based on sex and age to our study cohort (Table 1). All participants, both patients and matched controls, underwent an MRI scanning session that included a T1-weighted anatomical scan and a diffusion spectrum imaging scan. From each participant's MRI data, we derived their MSN maps following Seidlitz et al. and identified MSN alteration patterns for our patients in reference to HCs' data.16 Subsequently, for each pattern, we explored the bulk- and single-cell level transcriptomics data annotation to explore their specific genetic underpinning. At last, from histological analysis of the surgically resected tissues, we provided further cellular evidence of atypical microstructure organization associated with MSN alterations (Fig. 1). To minimize the influence of age and sex on our findings, they were controlled as covariates during subsequent analyses (see Methods).

Patterns of MSN in epilepsy

To characterize the MSN patterns of our participants, we calculated the inter-ROI Pearson's correlation matrix of eight brain morphometric metrics, including surface area, grey matter volume, cortical thickness, mean curvature, gaussian curvature, quantitative anisotropy, isotropic diffusion component and restricted diffusion imaging. In specific, for each participant, each morphometric feature vector was z-normalized across all ROIs, on which Pearson's correlation was conducted between each pair of ROIs.23 Then for each ROI, their correlation coefficients with the rest of the brain were averaged, yielding our final MSN map as a vector containing 308 regional mean correlation coefficients for each participant. We have also compiled the group-average MSN map for patients with various DRE subtypes and controls. The MSN distribution closely aligns with that reported by Morgan et al. (See Figs. S2 and S3 in Supplementary File 1).23

We started by performing an exploratory data analysis to understand the distribution patterns of MSN across our patients with DRE. By applying a t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) clustering on the MSN maps across all participants, we observed expected discrimination between patients and HCs, as well as discrimination between patients with and without structural lesion (Fig. 2a; Fig. 2b), suggesting that MSN is sensitive to the prevalence of DRE and its underlying pathologies. However, we also observed mixed clustering outputs in sex, age, antiepileptic drug (AED) treatment duration, surgery type, epileptogenic hemisphere, and Engel outcome (Fig. 2c–h). Alternative clustering methods were also explored and yielding divergent results (Table S3, Supplementary File 1).

Acknowledging that the MSN pattern is mostly sensitive to the existence of pathology (i.e., patient-control difference) and structural lesions, we sought to further identify representative MSN alteration patterns from our patient cohort. To do so, we calculated the deviation Z score for each region for each patient based on the mean and standard deviation of the MSN maps of the HC cohort (Figs. S2 and S4, Supplementary File 1). These MSN z-scores provided descriptive observations of MSN alterations across our patients. From these Z maps, we noticed two identifiable features. From a local perspective, through a one-sample t-test with FDR correction for multiple comparisons, we found that for 25 out of the 308 regions, patients presented elevated MSN z-scores compared to the baseline representing the HC's data (Fig. 2i). Among the 25 regions, the left banks of the superior temporal sulcus part one (left-bankssts part 1) was the most significant differed ROI between the patient and control cohort, with the highest mean Z-score of 4.652, suggesting the most extreme MSN alterations in DRE are found in this area. From a global perspective, another observation on the Z maps of patients with DRE was that the alteration burden of each patient was remarkably varied by burden loads and spatial distributions (Fig. 2j and k). In specific, we counted the number of ROIs with |Zmsn| > 3 in each hemisphere for each patient as their burden load. We found such alteration burden loads could vary from 0 to more than 70 across patients. In addition, patients also presented differences in the lateralization of MSN alteration burden. For patients with burden, 65% (32/49) presented bilateral distribution, 29% (14/49) presented left hemisphere distribution, and 6% (3/49) presented right hemisphere distribution.

Taking together, we identified five different MSN alteration patterns among out patients: 27 patients with both bilateral burden and left-bankssts part 1 abnormality were categorized into Pattern A; five patients with bilateral burden but not left-bankssts part 1 abnormality were categorized into Pattern B; 14 patients with unilateral burden and left-bankssts part 1 abnormality were categorized into Pattern C; three patients with unilateral burden but not left-bankssts part 1 abnormality were categorized into Pattern D; the rest 11 patients presented with no MSN abnormality above the given threshold were categorized into Pattern E. Interestingly, limbic MSN abnormalities were only found in patients with bilateral burdens (Patterns A and B).

We subsequently delved into the inter-pattern heterogeneity of clinical characteristics, as elucidated in Fig. 3, Table S1 in Supplementary File 1, and Tables S4–S6 in Supplementary File 2. The most pronounced disparities were observed in Pattern C compared to other patterns. Initially, a salient gender-related distinction emerged. In comparisons with Pattern A and Pattern E, the likelihood of being female patients in Pattern C was notably diminished, with OR values of 0.155 (95% CI: 0.029–0.828; P = 0.0194, Chi-squared test) and 0.107 (95% CI: 0.008–0.860; P = 0.0168, Fisher's exact test). Additionally, MRI evaluations offered significant insights into Pattern C. There was a conspicuous elevation in the odds of presenting abnormal MRI findings in Pattern C when set against Patterns D, E, and B. The ORs were 35.000 (95% CI: 1.344–911.338; P = 0.0147, Fisher's exact test with Haldane-Anscombe correction), 9.355 (95% CI: 1.163–129.656; P = 0.0168, Fisher's exact test), and 18.535 (95% CI: 1.167–1261.024; P = 0.0173, Fisher's exact test), respectively. Notably, the wide confidence intervals observed for some of these associations underscore the need for further validation in larger cohorts. In the realm of PET scans, Pattern C, when juxtaposed with Patterns B and A, displayed a significant elevation in the odds associated with the left sided hypometabolism. The corresponding ORs were 37.800 (95% CI: 1.457–980.812; P = 0.0123, Fisher's exact test with Haldane-Anscombe correction) and 27.000 (95% CI: 1.454–501.514; P = 0.0014, Fisher's exact test with Haldane-Anscombe correction). Concluding on a surgical note, the odds for surgeries on the left side witnessed a substantial increase in Pattern C as opposed to Pattern A, registering an OR of 17.333 (95% CI: 1.901–158.005; P = 0.0027, Chi-squared test). Our rigorous analyses have spotlighted marked variances within Pattern C across several crucial features, bolstering our categorization of MSN alteration patterns among the studied patients. The presence of wide confidence intervals in some of our findings, however, emphasizes the imperative of corroborating these results with studies involving more expansive samples.

Brain-specific gene expression related to epilepsy MSN patterns

To elucidate the microscale pathogenesis mechanism of MSN alterations in DRE, we examined the specific gene expression correlated with the spatial distribution of MSN abnormalities for each pattern. Given the limited sample size of Patterns B and D, which could lead to unreliable results, we focused on Patterns A, C, and E for subsequent analyses. Initially, we obtained standardized baseline brain expression data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas using the Abagen Toolbox, at a resolution of approximately 500 sampling points per hemisphere.30,31 We then identified the gene list associated with MSN changes by employing Wei et al.'s GAMBA software, which utilizes a linear regression algorithm in conjunction with spatial null model statistical validation. As a result, we found three gene lists containing 487 unique genes that correlated with the mean MSN z-score map of the three major MSN alteration patterns.

The overlap chord plot (Fig. 4a) showed that Pattern A and E appeared more unique than Pattern C. In enrichment analysis (Fig. 4b, Figs. S5–S30 in Supplementary File 1), the MetaCore database emphasized Pattern C in the AKT signaling and regulation of tissue factor signaling with the minimum P-value of all. In the detailed AKT signaling pathway map (Fig. S6, Supplementary File 1), the gene Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Kinase 1 (PDK1) and Ras Homolog Enriched in Brain (RHEB) were the Pattern C-specific differentially expressed genes. We multiplied the Abagen extracted brain baseline gene expression and coefficients from GAMBA to get a double-gene (PDK1 and RHEB) score and calculated its spatial correlation with the mean MSN z-score map of Pattern C (Fig. 4c). The result further showed this double-gene signature was still significantly correlated with MSN alteration in Pattern C (R = 0.141, Pperm = 0.042). Since PDK1 and RHEB were both included in the AKT signaling pathway, we further explored the spatial co-expression status of two genes, and permuted Pearson's correlation was insignificant (R = 0.155, Pperm = 0.474). The above evidence suggested PDK1 and RHEB may play separate roles in determining MSN alteration patterns. Pattern A and E showed enrichment in ROS-induced cellular signaling and activin-A signaling regulation (Fig. 4b).

Interestingly, Metascape, which utilized multiple ontology sources such as the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) signatures, reported several DRE-related terms (available in Supplementary File 2).34, 35, 36 For the Pattern A gene list, the immunologic signature “GSE45365 WT VS IFNAR KO CD8A DC MCMV INFECTION DN” was identified as the top enriched term (P = 8.91 × 10⁻⁵, both the current and subsequent P-values calculated by Metascape were obtained using the hypergeometric test), which was found in the experiment of C57BL/6 mice infected by murine cytomegalovirus.37 Subsequently, similar human cytomegalovirus and other virus infection immune responses followed (P = 1.072 × 10⁻⁴; P = 1.023 × 10⁻⁴).38,39 This evidence is consistent with the known pro-epileptogenesis role of neurological inflammation frequently caused by perinatal and childhood virus infections.40

In the DisGeNET database enrichment, frontotemporal dementia (FTD) was the top enriched term (Concept ID: C0338451, P < 0.0001) followed by multiple DRE-related congenital phenotypes such as macrocephaly and medial flaring of the eyebrow (Concept ID: C0221355, P = 7.94 × 10⁻⁴; Concept ID: C1844562, P = 5.01 × 10⁻⁴). Since patients with Pattern A exhibited a higher ratio of frontal surgery than Patterns C or E, FTD disease enrichment was expected. The enriched terms of the Pattern C gene list predominantly involved protein synthesis, intracellular trafficking, and other AKT signaling functions, which were also observed in the Pattern E gene list. Uniquely, Pattern E showed considerable overlap with the target genes of transcription factor DYRK1A (P < 0.0001), a key regulator in CNS development, and its alteration was found to cause epilepsy, intellectual disability, and other neurological deficits. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the gene list of Pattern C exhibits some overlap with both Patterns A and E, which might suggest a transitional relationship between them. Moreover, the functional similarities between Patterns C and E seem to lend support to the notion of a transient role for Pattern C between Patterns A and E. This evidence tentatively provides a better understanding of the possible transitional role of Pattern C between Patterns A and E in terms of gene expression and functional overlap.

To further verify the relation between MSN and microstructure organization, we explored if the gene lists corresponding to MSN abnormalities contain gene expression signatures of neurons with layer-specific distribution. We employed the neuron gene expression signatures reported by Marquez-Galera et al., which are based on the Human Multiple Cortical Areas SMART-seq data derived from the Allen Human Brain Atlas.41 As the majority of intratelencephalic neurons exhibit homogeneous expression, we selected signatures from six layer-specific neuron types, including layer IV intratelencephalic neurons (L4 IT), layer V intratelencephalic neurons (L5 IT), layer V/VI intratelencephalic Car3 neurons (L5/6 IT Car3), layer V/VI near-projecting neurons (L5/6 NP), layer VI corticothalamic neurons (L6 CT), and layer VIb neurons (L6b). We then deconvolved the gene lists corresponding to MSN abnormalities into cell-type-specific gene expression patterns. Our results revealed that the gene expression signatures of neurons with layer-specific distribution in the gene lists corresponding to MSN Patterns A and E are generally similar, both containing expression signatures of L4 IT and L5/6 NP. Notably, Pattern A is distinguished from Pattern E by a more pronounced L5/6 IT Car3 signature. In the deconvolution results of Pattern E, we also discovered some co-expressed genes that could not be annotated by the selected six layer-specific neuron signatures. These genes can be annotated as neuron expression signatures in the middle temporal gyrus and anterior cingulate gyrus according to the Human Multiple Cortical Areas SMART-seq data.

Discussion

Leveraging morphometric information obtained via T1-weighted and diffusion spectrum imaging, we evaluated the integrity of morphometric similarity network (MSN) in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) and demographically matched healthy controls (HCs). We found significant MSN alterations in patients with DRE, suggesting the existence of changes in their laminar-related microstructure organization. By categorizing the MSN alteration patterns among these patients, we identified the microstructure alteration-correlated gene expression features which showed significant overlap with known epileptic and focal cortical dysplasia pathways such as mTOR, ROS, and Activin A signaling. Importantly, our histological analysis provided further evidence of microstructure alteration in patients with MSN alterations. These findings unified noninvasive MR imaging phenotyping, transcriptomics, and histologic evidences, thereby providing supports for using MSN to characterize epileptogenic changes to the cytoarchitecture of the brain in DRE.

The cortical laminar organization, a crucial feature of the brain, has been observed to be disrupted in various neurological and psychological diseases. In DRE, this disruption presents an especially complex situation, as it involves two seemingly opposing phenomena. On one hand, localized damage corresponds to focal pathological changes in the cortical laminar organization. On the other hand, remote injuries align with the role of cortical laminar organization in mediating brain connectivity. Both phenomena have been observed in various diseases, which makes the interpretation of the underlying mechanisms more intricate and challenging. For instance, in focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) type II, the complete resection of the brain region containing dysmorphic neurons and enlarged ballooned cells has been proven efficient in achieving satisfactory outcomes, supporting the idea of localized damage.17 In contrast, FCD type I, characterized by broad neocortex architectural disorganization, cannot be accurately delineated using current evaluation methods, resulting in significant surgical failures in seizure control and highlighting the remote injuries associated with brain connectivity.42 To reconcile these seemingly contradictory findings, a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between localized damage and remote injuries in different diseases is needed. Further research is required to disentangle the complex roles of cortical laminar organization disruption and to unveil the underlying mechanisms involved.

In our study, we calculated the MSN value of the DRE study cohort containing mixed FCD types and localization of epileptic foci. In unsupervised clustering, the study cohort showed continuous cortical microstructure alteration similar to Lee et al.43 We observed that five major patterns (namely Patterns A to E according to the descending order of alteration) had apparent variations in left banks of the superior temporal sulcus (L-bankssts) involvement and lateralization. Previous studies have reported a correlation between Bankssts alteration and cognitive decline and neuropsychiatric symptoms of mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease.44,45 However, there are still limited reports on the association of Bankssts alteration in DRE. We hypothesize that the multi-modal and multi-metrics morphological methods like MSN may be especially sensitive to changes in sulcus like the Bankssts. Currently, there is also a lack of understanding regarding the pathophysiological significance of Bankssts alteration. In terms of physiological conditions, a GWAS study by Eising et al. based on neuroanatomical features of 34,000 individuals revealed an association between the surface area of the banks of the L-superior temporal sulcus, and the processing of spoken and written language.46 It is hypothesized that this region may function as a hub where different language modalities are integrated, as well as the lateralization of such functions. Considering that language impairments have been reported in patients with DRE independent of side of seizure onset zone and language lateralization,47 we suspect that L-Bankssts alteration may contribute to this pathological characteristic in DRE. We also observed that these cortical microstructure alteration Patterns were correlated with known clinical characteristics of DRE. Patterns A and B were with bilateral cortical microstructure alteration and differed by the left-side bankssts involvement. After the temporal surgery treatment, including temporal lobectomy and selective amygdalohippocampectomy, the seizure of patients with Pattern B without left side bankssts involvement was not well controlled. Nearly all bilateral Positron emission tomography (PET) hypometabolism (7/8) and all extratemporal epilepsy cases were included in the above two patterns. Pattern C was similar to Pattern A at left side bankssts involvement, but the cortical microstructure alteration was limited to the left hemisphere; the patients with this pattern all presented with left-side PET hypometabolism and predominantly underwent left-side treatment (12/13). Interestingly, the patients with Pattern D were all with a single ROI alteration and, similar to small-scale Pattern B, had non-lesional epilepsy, while Pattern E without above threshold alteration was composed of both lesional and non-lesional epilepsy as well as other major patterns such as A and C. Previously, Spitzer et al. reported the subgroup non-lesional epilepsy.48 The difference between major microstructure alteration patterns was not observed in the pathological category, and the FCD type aspect, which was concordant with the recent ENIGMA study on FCD detection, showed the overlapped surface-based morphological features of FCD types.48 Taken together, the cortical microstructure alteration pattern characterized by the MSN-Z score has a clinical correlation.

An important observation of this study is that different MSN patterns are associated with different genetic risks. For example, FCD is one of the most important pathogeneses in DRE.42 Previously, its well-defined category, the FCD type II, was confirmed to be genetically caused by the mTOR signaling pathway dysregulation.49 We also observed that the Pattern C was matched with AKT pathway signaling, in which the mTOR regulator RHEB and PDK1 were pattern specific. The RHEB p.P37L and p.S68P mutation has been reported to cause intellectual disability, epilepsy, and megalencephaly.50 The PDK1 conditional knockout mice exhibited striking neuron loss and microcephaly, while the homozygous PDK1 deletion has embryonic lethality.51 Mucha et al. reported 16p13.3 microdeletion syndrome featured by simultaneous haploinsufficiency of TBC1D24, ATP6V0C, and PDPK1 could induce epileptic disorders, microcephaly, and developmental delay.52 A previous study also reported that the balloon cell in FCD type II has stronger p-PDK1 (Ser241) staining than dysmorphic neurons.53 The Pattern A was enriched in ROS signaling, which could be explained by oxidative stress related to FCD and seizure onset. For the Pattern E, the activin A signaling was enriched. Activin A is a known biomarker of neuronal destruction in neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, the common DRE cause.54 Previously, Li et al. reported that T1 extracted regional MSN differences were associated with genes enriched in several biological processes, including ‘synapse organisation’, ‘neurotransmitter transport’ pathways and excitatory/inhibitory neuronal cell types.20 The differences between Li et al. and the current study arise from several aspects. First, focal epilepsy studied in the current study is a distinct epilepsy type with distinct genetic mechanisms compared to the generalized epilepsy studied by Li et al. Second, our pattern specific analyses provided additional fine-grained resolution of MSN alterations. Last, our MSN was calculated based on both T1 and diffusion metrics, which provide higher accuracy in describing microstructure similarity between brain regions compared to T1-only matrics.16

Like previous imaging-transcriptomics studies, the right-side cortical microstructure alteration was not matched with the gene expression matrix due to the left-predominant AHBA data availability.23,24 We noticed two major alteration patterns, A and C, shared the same left-bankssts involvement, while the mTOR signaling genes that cause FCD were only observed in Pattern C. And the bilaterally involved Pattern A cases, primarily matched with ROS signaling. A reasonable concern would be if the above was caused by the mixed laterality of DRE cases. Here we argue that the preoperative evaluation of patients with epilepsy underwent surgery treatment could be limited. Especially considering their bilateral cortical microstructure alteration and unsatisfactory outcome, the introduction of laterality could be risky in confounding. Another debate was about ROS signaling as the major hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy-related pathway that enrichment in Pattern E at a low-level while dramatically enriched in Pattern C. It is more likely that, as the ENIGMA study found that the cortical morphological characteristics of FCD subtypes are repetitive, Pattern A is the identification of ROS signaling pathway activities shared by FCD with different molecular pathogenic mechanisms. Another controversial point is the low-level enrichment of ROS-guided cell signaling pathways in Pattern E as an important pathogenic mechanism of neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. This may be related to the differences in the tolerance mechanism of immature brains to ROS and mature brains. The resulting phenotypes may not be restored by the AHBA database, which offered pure-adult gene information. Activin A matching Pattern E has protective effects on nerve damage from development to adulthood. In conclusion, the altered pattern of cortical stratification in DRE can be linked to the currently identified molecular mechanisms of epileptogenicity.

At last, we verified whether MSN alteration indeed relates to in vivo cortical microstructure alteration through clinical, metabolic and histological perspectives. In six patients with SEEG-proven temporal lobe epilepsy and confirmative pathological diagnosis (Fig. 5), we found three patients with Pattern C appeared to have significant angiogenesis activity and more physiological cortical lamination compared with Pattern A. Previous studies showed the angiogenesis of epilepsy could interfere with the blood–brain barrier and neurovascular coupling and was seizure-related and seizure-promoted. And the dysregulation of PDK1 expression and mTOR signaling could also cause angiogenesis. Thus, Pattern C might represent a subgroup of patients with DRE with concentrated lesions and should be verified in future study. We did not find the preference of cortical microstructure alteration pattern on the type of FCD or other pathological findings. The resection field limitation might cause this. In one of three Pattern C cases featured left-side cortical microstructure alteration, the one-year outcome after right-side surgery was unsatisfactory.

This study presents a few key limitations. Firstly, our retrospectively collected cohort, while being of a limited size, could have benefited from a control group developed via the property score matching method. Yet, this design would entail challenges given the individual-level matching intricacies, particularly during the Z-score calculation. Secondly, we adjusted for age and sex using a linear regression model, a method in line with current practices.55, 56, 57, 58, 59 However, it might not fully address the nonlinear effects of these variables, nor does it account for potential confounders like educational attainment and social interactions, which might influence our findings.60,61 Thirdly, to maintain methodological consistency with prior neuroimaging studies, we employed independent t-tests.62,63 This choice, while facilitating cross-study comparability, could challenge the independence of our matched observations. Future endeavors should consider models more apt for matched datasets. Fourth, as in previous studies, the Allen Human Brain Atlas microarray expression data used in imaging transcriptomics analysis was limited from the left hemisphere of six adult brains.23,24 The right hemisphere was not selected due to limited case numbers and lack of representativeness.30 Therefore, the matched gene lists calculated by the GAMBA did not consider the right hemisphere MSN alteration. Since the MSN alteration was primarily found in the left hemisphere and an unbalanced number of left and right-side hemisphere expression data might introduce excessive bias, it seems acceptable. The known heterogeneity of pathological appearance in patients with DRE was another issue.64 Efforts were made to amend it by presenting the whole layers of the cortex in a similar location of resected tissue. Lastly, the immunohistochemistry staining of PDK1 and RHEB could bring matching status information between cortical lamination pattern-related gene expression and real-world molecular pathology characteristics. In the future, insightful views from such immunohistochemistry analysis and further whole-genome sequencing would be expected.

Taken together, our study demonstrated the potential of morphometric similarity network as a promising tool to provide noninvasive characterization of cortical laminar-related microstructure integrity in patients with DRE. Patterns of morphometric similarity network alteration, i.e., microstructure alteration, are found to be associated with different genetic risks in epilepsy supported by known disease molecular pathogenesis. Accordingly, this study paves the way for using morphometric similarity network to characterize epileptogenic changes to the cytoarchitecture of the brain in DRE.
