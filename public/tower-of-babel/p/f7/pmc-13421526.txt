Impact of atmospheric electrical charges on ryegrass pollen rupture and sub-pollen particle release
Authors: Sudharsun Venkatesan, Ali Zare, Zoran D. Ristovski, Behnaz Alinaghipour, David A. V. Morton, Sara Vahaji, James Lamont, Svetlana Stevanovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thunderstorm asthma (TA) is a significant health concern, recorded in 26 instances globally, primarily linked to ryegrass pollen. Existing research has shown that physical forces such as wind and rain can cause pollen to rupture during thunderstorms, creating micronic, inhalable allergen-rich fragments called sub-pollen particles (SPPs). However, little is known about the role of electrical charges in storm clouds, such as those associated with lightning, in triggering pollen rupture. This study presents the first simulation-based evidence that the static electric fields typical of storm clouds (105–870 kV/m) can fragment pollen into SPPs. The results reveal a clear increase in pollen fragmentation and dispersal when exposed to these charges - especially at higher electric field strengths and higher humidity. Under discharge conditions, such as simulated lightning (arcing), fragmentation intensifies, generating abundant, irregularly shaped ruptured pollen particles, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy. The results identify both static and discharge electrical processes in storm clouds as drivers of pollen rupture, advancing the mechanistic understanding of TA. In the context of climate change-driven increases in severe storm frequency and intensity, this dual-mechanism insight has important implications for forecasting TA risk and mitigating respiratory health impacts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54231-7.

Introduction

Global trends around pollen allergies are changing, with studies indicating that climate extremes could contribute to longer and more intense pollen seasons1–6. This trend will not only affect the environment, but also people, as the increasing prevalence of pollen allergies and associated health risks can drive up healthcare costs, strain medical resources and impact individuals’ quality of life7–10. In general, pollen grains are too large to enter the lower respiratory tract. This means that intact pollen does not pose a severe threat to public health11–13. However, under certain atmospheric conditions, pollen grains can rupture and fragment, breaking into micronic inhalable particles called sub-pollen particles (SPPs)14,15. This fragmentation marks a critical shift in allergen exposure dynamics, triggering the release of biological airborne allergens in the fine (respirable) fraction of atmospheric aerosol.

During thunderstorms, SPPs can be released in very high amounts and trigger more severe allergic reactions, intensifying conditions like Thunderstorm Asthma (TA)14,16,17. This observation has prompted further investigation into the combination of factors that cause TA, which remain unclear. The leading current hypothesis, proposed by Taylor and Jonsson17, suggests that grass pollen is lifted by warm updrafts into storm fronts, where high humidity and possibly electrical charges (e.g. lightning) cause pollen to rupture. These micronic inhalable particles are then dispersed by downdrafts, resulting in high concentrations at ground level and triggering asthma in large populations. Physical factors are typically recognised as primary contributors to pollen rupture - for example, osmotic pressure (i.e., rainwater causing pollen grains to swell and break apart) and mechanical forces (i.e., wind). However, emerging evidence suggests that electrical charges in storm clouds may also play a role14,18–22.

Thunderstorms are complex, multilayered electrical systems that demonstrate remarkable spatial and temporal variability23. One aspect of this complexity is reflected in the varying intensities of electric fields observed within these systems, with values ranging significantly depending on the location within the cloud24. Near the charge centres within a cloud, electric fields can reach as high as 150 kV/m 25,26. In comparison, the edges of clouds generally experience electric fields around 50 kV/m. This intensity diminishes to 3–5 kV/m at a distance of 5 km from the cloud perimeter27. During fair weather conditions, the electric field values taper off significantly to about 100 V/m23.

For lightning discharge to occur, electric fields typically need to reach strengths between 100 and 300 kV/m at critical breakdown points - the threshold at which the air can no longer insulate against the charge and becomes conductive. The presence of substances such as ice droplets, water, and graupel particles (soft hail formed when supercooled water freezes onto snowflakes) can raise this threshold to around 500 kV/m – making lightning strikes more intense23. Peak current values, the maximum strength of electric current in lightning strikes, vary significantly. Negative flashes, which carry a negative charge from the cloud to the ground, and generally come from the bottom of the cloud, are less powerful – around 200 kA. Positive flashes are less common but more intense, carrying a charge from the top of the cloud to the ground and reaching a peak current of 340 kA, with some extreme remote observations reporting peak currents up to -957 kA 28. Positive flashes are associated with more severe damage, such as bushfire or power outage. These parameters are essential for understanding environmental conditions during thunderstorms.

A key concept is the breakeven field – the electric field strength at which fast electrons generate additional electrons by ionising air molecules, sustaining an avalanche. This leads to two distinct electrical regimes within thunderstorms: (1) a high static field phase before breakdown, when charge accumulates but no lightning occurs, and (2) the discharge phase once breakdown is triggered, producing intense, transient fields and currents29. Recognising these regimes is essential for linking storm cloud conditions to biological effects, including pollen rupture relevant to thunderstorm asthma events.

This study investigates how electrical charges might cause pollen to rupture and fragment, by simulating the electrical fields present in storm clouds and observing the impact on pollen grains. We used wind-pollinated pollen grains from the Lolium (Perennial Ryegrass) family. Perennial Ryegrass (Lolium Perenne) has been implicated in the greatest number of TA events and has a strong airborne footprint in Australia and worldwide, making it a perfect candidate for our investigation14,16,30–33. To replicate the environmental conditions, these simulations will be conducted within the house-made, novel ‘Environmental Chamber for High Voltage and Oxidative Air Pollutants’ (ECHO). The impact of electrical charges on pollen was examined under different conditions: static electric fields (105, 210, 315 and 420 kV/m) and discharge conditions simulating lightning or arcing (775–870 kV/m), thereby replicating the charge regimes present in storm clouds.

There is a notable lack of studies examining the impact of electrical charges on ryegrass pollen during thunderstorms – particularly in relation to pollen rupture and the release of sub-pollen particles, and how these airborne allergens may contribute to TA events16,22,34. This research investigates how electrical charges during thunderstorms trigger ryegrass pollen rupture and dispersal, and how these processes contribute to airborne allergen levels. Through improved understanding of these processes, we aim to advance current knowledge of TA and support the development of targeted strategies to reduce health risks and more effectively manage airborne allergens.

Methods

Sample collection

Ryegrass pollen used in this study was obtained from the processing of ryegrass flowers harvested from the Surf Coast region of Victoria, located in southeastern Australia, during the early spring and summer months of 2024-25. The collected ryegrass flowers were initially dried at a controlled temperature of 20 °C for a duration of 24–48 h. Subsequently, the dried flowers underwent processing and sieving for 12–15 h, following the methodologies outlined by Lucas, et al. 5, Ouyang, et al. 36 and Galveias, et al. 37. The material retained in the pan after drying of flowers and sieving through 45 μm and 75 μm meshes was then subjected to microscopic evaluation for quality assurance. Following quality checks, the contents of the pan were weighed and stored at -20 °C until experimentation38–40. Pollen grains were weighed in a cuvette and thawed at room temperature before exposure to experimental conditions, allowing them to attain homeostasis and preventing rapid rehydration. Ryegrass pollen used in the study was found to be in the size range of 19.8 to 34.8 μm and contained less than 10% impurities. The samples were collected after obtaining relevant permissions, and additional details on the sample collection and identification are included in the supplementary information. Information on sample purity assessment is also provided in the supplementary information (Refer Figure S1).

Experimental setup

The ECHO chamber is made of Perspex and has a volume of 17 L. ECHO’s top plate is removable, thereby facilitating easier system operation, maintenance, and cleaning (Fig. 1). Ports for conditioning flow and updraft flow, designed to push pollen, are located on the inlet side of ECHO, while the outlet side houses the ports for the humidity sensor and the sampling port. The electrodes used in the setup are welding rods made of stainless-steel alloy SS308, which are mounted through ports on both sides of the chamber and coiled at one end (see Fig. 1). The centre of the chamber houses the coiled side of the electrodes, exposing pollen to the high electric field conditions. The coiled side of the electrode is also important for adjusting the system’s configurations to change exposure conditions. The electrode mounted on the inlet side is the cathode; the other electrode is the anode, which synchronises the flow of charges and air inside the chamber. The ECHO walls were plastered with a PVF film (Spandex) to prevent charge buildup on the chamber walls (see Fig. 1).

ECHO chamber along with its components. The left side of the chamber is the inlet side, while the outlet is on the right side.

The pollen disperser uses a combination of acoustics and air flows to dry dispense pollen with uniformity and high reproducibility. The acoustic component (speaker) serves a dual purpose: it prevents the aggregation of pollen grains and facilitates their suspension in the air within the cuvette. The supplied airflow then disperses these suspended pollen grains into the ECHO chamber. A 12 V DC supply powers the speaker and amplifier, while the signal generator is powered by a 9 V battery and employs a 60 Hz sine wave. A cuvette tube containing pollen grains is placed on top of the speaker after removing the top plate of the ECHO, thereby exposing the pollen to the speaker’s acoustics. Furthermore, a HEPA-filtered flow of 1.2 L/min is supplied through a 1 mm nozzle to nebulise the pollen (See Figure S3, Supplementary information).

High voltage generation setup and testing conditions

The TERCO 9000 high voltage (HV) generation setup was employed in this study. The primary components of the HV setup include the Data Acquisition and Control (DAC) unit (measurement range 0-120 kV DC potential and linearity < 1%), which features a dual-screen interface for controlling and monitoring the real-time status of the electrical components (Figure S2, Supplementary section). This is facilitated by a remote desktop housed inside the switching cabinet module, which contains relays and equipment protection components essential for the safety of the HV apparatus. Additionally, the setup includes a single-phase transformer with three windings to step up the input AC voltage, and a rectifier that allows current to pass only during the positive half of the cycle, aiding in the generation of DC voltage. Resistors, connecting cups, and connecting rods are used in the circuit to limit current while filtering and smoothing the generated DC voltages which are relayed to the system as electric fields through the electrodes, which are welding rods made from SS308 alloy (Manufacturer: BOC). These welding rods generally contain 19.71% Chromium, 10.37% Nickel, and an ultra-low carbon level of 0.016% with their microstructure primarily composed of austenite with 5–8% ferrite.

The configuration used here is aimed at converting AC voltages into DC voltages with two different objectives based on test requirements: (a) the generation of an electric field between the electrodes (static electricity, also referred to as static electric field) and (b) the generation of impulses between the electrodes (simulating lightning conditions characterised by electrical breakdown, leading to the formation of impulse arcs, or arcing). The typical conversion in the HV setup begins with stepping up AC voltage using a transformer, followed by rectification with a silicon rectifier to convert AC into DC voltage while limiting current flow during the positive half of the cycle. Subsequently, a current-limiting resistor is used to filter and stabilise the DC output. This setup enables simulation of DC voltages ranging from 10 to 40 kV in a controlled environment, using a metal Faraday cage, to protect sampling devices and personnel from HV hazards. The supplementary section contains the circuit diagram of the Simulink file used to simulate the apparatus.

HV setup is used to establish electric fields inside ECHO under two configurations: static electric field and discharge field (arcing). In the static electric field configuration, the coiled side of the electrodes in ECHO is positioned with a gap of 9.5 centimetres between them, whereas in the arcing configuration, the coil is unwound so that both electrodes function as needle electrodes, with the gap between them reduced to 4 centimetres. This reduction in gap for the arcing configuration offers the ability to simulate the conditions of lightning with the voltage between the electrodes being around 31–35 kV, thereby keeping the electric field conditions in line with the values used for the static electric field (10, 20, 30 and 40 kV). For tests under arcing conditions, approximately 2–4 arcs are generated following which the conditions were tapered down. The values of electric fields chosen for the experiments were based on the values of the field strengths described inside the clouds. The conditions of electric fields simulated inside ECHO using the HV generation setup, along with their field strengths with peak current values are reported in Table 1. It is important to note that, according to Paschen’s law, electrical breakdown in clouds occurs at lower field strengths due to the reduced atmospheric pressure. Consequently, in our experiments, higher fields were required to achieve breakdown conditions compared to those naturally present in clouds.

Testing procedures and aerosol instrumentation

The testing procedure for this study involved exposing redispersed pollen to varying charge regimes under two conditions of humidity (Relative humidity, RH): dry (35–40% RH) and wet (90–95% RH) to clearly demarcate the influence of electric charges on airborne pollen. The ECHO chamber is subjected to pressure tests following every test to ensure the system is leak-free. This is achieved by assessing ECHO’s ability to maintain a pressure difference of 0.5 kPa between the inlet and outlet sides using HEPA-filtered air for a duration of 10 min. The ECHO, along with its flow components and pollen disperser apparatus, is separated from the sampling apparatus and HV DAC unit by an HV safety cage, which acts as a barrier to protect aerosol instrumentation and personnel from HV exposure.

Before and after each test, the ECHO chamber is purged with HEPA-filtered air at 5 L/min for 5 min and cleaned with de-ionised (DI) water to remove all remnants. A 2 mg sample of pollen grains are weighed and loaded into the cuvette tube that is affixed on top of the speaker beneath the nozzle of the pollen disperser. The chamber is then coated with DI water, and with the help of a conditioning flow at 2 L/min, the required humidity levels are achieved and continuously monitored by the humidity sensor throughout the testing period.

For the static electricity testing protocol, background aerosol levels are first measured over a 5-minute baseline period with the updraft flow system operational at 2.5 L/min and the pollen disperser inactive. Subsequently, the pollen nebuliser is activated to introduce particles into the chamber, followed by energising the HV system to generate the desired electric field conditions. The electric field is maintained for 10 min during which aerosol sampling is conducted. After this period, the chamber is purged, and the top plate of ECHO is unsealed to remove the pollen cuvette and clean the chamber.

The sampling protocol diverges between configurations to accommodate the transient energy release during arcing tests, which necessitates protective measures for sensitive components (e.g., RH sensor, pollen dispersion speaker). To mitigate surge risks, the pollen cuvette (preloaded with pollen) is secured atop the speaker prior to testing, in line with safeguards employed in static electricity experiments. The procedure initiates with a 5-minute baseline aerosol measurement under an operational updraft flow of 2.5 L/min, with the pollen nebulizer inactive, to quantify background aerosols within the ECHO. Subsequently, arcing conditions are induced by incrementally increasing the potential difference between electrodes via the HV system until electrical breakdown occurs, with continuous aerosol sampling capturing particles generated during discharge events. Approximately 30 s following arcing cessation, the pollen disperser is activated, and residual aerosols in ECHO are sampled for 10 min to evaluate arcing-induced effects on pollen fragmentation and dispersion. Finally, the chamber is purged with HEPA-filtered air, after which the ECHO top plate is unsealed to remove the pollen cuvette and decontaminate the chamber for subsequent trials.

Aerosol sampling is conducted using a suite of instruments capable of detecting and characterising a wide range of aerosol sizes, from 60 nm to 35 μm. The setup includes a GRIMM Optical Particle Counter (OPC, 11-D), and Droplet Measurement Technology’s Ultra-High Sensitivity Aerosol Spectrometer (DMT-UHSAS) for real-time particle size measurements, providing high-temporal-resolution sampling at 1 s intervals. Additionally, a TSI Nanometre Aerosol Sampler (TSI 3089) collects aerosols onto TEM grids for size distribution analysis and further characterisation. For each exposure condition, a minimum of three replicate experiments were conducted.

Results and discussion

Three distinct static electric field intensities were applied: 20 kV, 30 kV, and 40 kV, corresponding to 210, 315, and 420 kV/m, which fall within the range of values observed in thunderstorm clouds29. Figure 2 illustrates the temporal variation in airborne sub-pollen particle (SPP) concentration during the experiment. Initially, particle concentration remains low, establishing a baseline before any rupture occurs. Particles in the background primarily derive from the water utilised for humidity generation (see Supplementary information, Figure S4 and S5 for reference). Background concentrations for varying electric field strengths are 350 particles/cm³ at 20 kV and 35% relative humidity, and 400 particles/cm³ at 20 kV and 95% relative humidity. The background concentration for other testing conditions was within ± 10% and was independent of the static electric field strength.

After pollen was introduced between the electrodes, a sudden surge in the concentration of submicronic particles was observed, which indicates the onset of pollen fragmentation triggered by the presence of the electrical field. This sharp increase in submicronic particles was observed in the case of dry and wet conditions with a potential difference of 40 kV between electrodes. These observations indicated that the majority of the fragments measured were in the submicron size range, specifically between 60 and 200 nm, as measured by the UHSAS. The maximum SPP concentration corresponded to the most intense rupture phase, occurring around 3 min after exposure to the electrical field. Following this peak, the concentration gradually declined, likely due to losses of highly charged fragments onto the chamber walls or their removal by the outflow, coupled with reduced pollen rupture activity. Another observation is a marked delay between the onset of pollen fragments and the application of the electrical field, which is common in both dry and wet scenarios during static electric field exposures with a potential difference of 20 to 40 kV. This delay could be attributed to changes in the hydration state and electrostatic charge of pollen, both of which are related to pollen grain size. In particular, charge acquisition by pollen grains following ionisation depends on the pollen grain radius, as described in a study by Bowker and Crenshaw41. However, the radius of pollen also varies with the hydration state (i.e., Harmomegathy42, creating a complex interplay between hydration state and the electrostatic charges associated with pollen.

Pollen rupturing was tested under three electric fields and under two relative humidities. Measured concentrations over the first 3 min under high (95% RH) and low (35% RH) humidity conditions and for the three different applied voltages are presented in Table 2. These results clearly demonstrate that the extent of pollen rupturing increases with both the strength of the electric field and humidity conditions.

Figure 3 illustrates the impact of electric field strength and humidity on the size distribution of SPP. At elevated electric field strengths (30 kV and 40 kV, corresponding to 315 kV/m and 420 kV/m, respectively), SPP count considerably increases, particularly in high humidity conditions (95% RH). An electrical field of 388 kV/m (40 kV) generates the maximum particle concentration, with notable peaks at approximately 80 nm and 200 nm. In contrast, at reduced humidity (35% RH), the particle concentration remained lower across all field strengths, suggesting that moisture is essential for stabilising or facilitating SPP production. The 20 kV condition (210 kV/m), while generating fewer particles than higher field strengths, demonstrated a distinct rise above the background, confirming that particle generation is attributable only to the electric field impact. Our prior research (Venkatesan, et al. 43) indicated that pollen can rupture exclusively under high humidity conditions as observed with ryegrass pollen. However, current results suggest that electric fields have a substantially increased influence than humidity alone. Furthermore, when both factors coexist, they synergistically produce a significantly greater effect - conditions that are highly relevant to what may occur within storm clouds.

It should be noted that UHSAS can underestimate particle concentrations in its lowest-size bin due to several factors. Pollen fragments characterised from rupturing in these experiments have irregular shapes and heterogeneous compositions, which can lead to inaccuracies in optical measurements reliant on Mie scattering theory44. The refractive index of pollen fragments or SPPs may also cause mis-sizing, affecting how light is scattered. It is therefore expected that pollen-rupturing derivatives can also contain smaller particles than those detected, implying that the distribution measured by the instrument may differ somewhat from reality. Nonetheless, the detected particles are undoubtedly very small and fall within the nanosized range.

The plots provided in the Supplementary information (Figures S4, S5, and S6) do not show evidence of ruptured particles or particles of the sizes described earlier. Data from the background measurements shown in these plots help distinguish the pollen fragments observed from background aerosols ECHO before and during conditioning. Initially, air from the empty chamber (Background-1) was sampled and analysed. Then, humidity was introduced (at 35% and 95% RH), and a second background measurement (Background-2) was recorded. For the third background (referred to as Background-3), a static field was applied to Background-2 (see S4 and S5, Supplementary information). Finally, pollen was dispersed without applying an electric field, and this was recorded as an additional background measurement (see Figure S6, Supplementary information).

These results suggest that the detected particles originate from ruptured pollen rather than other sources. To further confirm this, particles were also collected using a Nanometer Aerosol Sampler (NAS 3089, TSI) and analysed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). This analysis was conducted during experiments in which pollen was nebulised into humidified ECHO, with the electrical field applied across all previously described settings.

SEM images in Fig. 4 suggest that the sampled particles are primarily ruptured pollen grains, as opposed to salt crystals, which might be expected from the water used to humidify the chamber. The particles in the images range from submicron to a few micrometres in size. The irregular morphology, rough surfaces, and non-crystalline structure strongly indicate ruptured pollen fragments rather than background aerosols. In addition, size distribution plots for the background, measured over 0.25–5 μm, did not show particles in this size range (Refer to Figure S4-S6, Supplementary information), which may indicate that indicates that these particles originate from pollen rupture rather than from water droplets used to increase RH levels.

To further confirm the origin of the particles, we collected them using the NAS and analysed them with the SEM (Fig. 6 & Figures S8-S9, Supplementary information). SEM images show that the pollen grain rupture is evident from the segmentation, showing the irregular shape and fragmented outer structure. At least two distinct structures are observed: one likely representing the main pollen body and another a fragment or debris. The surrounding debris may consist of remnants of the pollen wall or organic matter released upon rupture. The aspect ratio suggests elongation, possibly due to stretching or breakage of the outer shell. The particles observed in SEM ranged from 0.2 to 5 μm, indicating the presence of submicron and larger particles, alongside sub-100 nm particles observed using DMT-UHSAS (Refer to Figure S7, Supplementary information).

The elemental composition of the fragments (excluding Carbon, which also exhibited presence) revealed a high oxygen (O) content (81.4%), suggesting the presence of organic material. Potassium (K) (12.1%) - commonly found in pollen grains due to its role in cellular processes - was also detected, along with Nitrogen (N) (6.5%), likely from proteins or other organic compounds in the pollen structure. Traces of components such as Si, Al, Mg, Br and Cl were also observed; all these components have been reported in the literature as constituents of airborne Poaceae pollen45,46. The prominent Oxygen (O) peaks are characteristic of sporopollenin (C90 H142 O36 )47 and cellulose, which are complex biopolymers that make up the tough outer and inner walls of pollen grains, typically consisting of several oxygen atoms48,49. These findings confirm that the fragments analysed are consistent with the known chemical composition of airborne Poaceae pollen and of typical pollen-associated material and do not come from the electrodes or other materials within the chamber.

Conclusion

For the first time, this study provides strong evidence that electric fields, like those found in storm clouds, can cause pollen to rupture and fragment. Stronger electric fields produce even smaller submicron particles that remain suspended in the air, a process amplified by high humidity. This powerful synergy between moisture and electric fields mirrors thunderstorm conditions, underscoring their critical role in pollen fragmentation and airborne allergen dispersion.

The findings indicate both static and discharge electrical processes in storm clouds are drivers of pollen rupture, advancing mechanistic understanding of TA. The rapid onset of pollen rupture upon exposure to an electric field and the resulting, predominantly submicron particles provides direct evidence to support the idea that electrical charges in storm clouds cause pollen grains to burst and disperse. Complementary SEM imaging supports this conclusion, as detected particles exhibit irregular morphologies characteristic of ruptured pollen rather than other potential sources such as dried water droplet residues. Additionally, the sizes of the SPPs detected vary from the typical descriptions of pollen-rupturing derivatives, such as p-particles (0.3–0.4 μm) and starch granules (0.6–2.5 μm), which may be due to the mechanism and intensity of pollen rupture, an aspect that can be explored in future research.

This work uses pollen that was obtained by processing plant material and stored at low temperatures to retain pollen’s natural traits over the storage period. Studies assessing pollen responses to exposure treatments that lead to pollen rupture have reported differences in behaviour between pollen of different ages (fresh/stored) and origins (commercial/natural)15,50–53. Thus, the nature of the pollen and its subsequent ageing during storage at low temperatures used in this work are limitations of this study, arising from experimental needs and the length of the grass pollen season in Australia. Future work could focus on how pollen age and type influence grass pollen responses to electric fields, as it is important to clearly understand the differences between laboratory studies and real-world scenarios that lead to TA events.

The ECHO chamber used in this work is a dynamic flow-through system in which the electrical conditions and humidity are established prior to pollen exposure. The conditions of the electric field and humidity were considered in the design of ECHO, as they are simplified, controllable proxies for conditions that persist during thunderstorms. However, other aspects of thunderstorms, such as pressure gradients and polarity reversal, are crucial to the phenomenon, and future work should account for them, as they may influence the behaviour and dynamics of interactions involving airborne pollen and its derivatives.

The peak number of particles (less than 1 μm) produced per pollen in this work translates to 0.06 particles/ryegrass pollen introduced and 0.275 particles/ ryegrass pollen introduced in the case of static electricity and arcing conditions, respectively. ECHO’s design as a dynamic flow-through system can lead to particle loss due to turbulence and gravitational settling, suggesting that the actual number of SPPs produced may exceed the number of particles/pollen introduced described earlier. Future research on pollen-charge interactions could involve capture of pollen (using sampling systems like impactors) after exposure to electrical charges, as this can offer vital insights into physical (such as the ratio of ruptured pollen to exposed pollen and number of SPPs per pollen) and chemical changes (like the hydrophilicity of SPPs, protein content, and protein transformation) in pollen, aiding in understanding how atmospheric electrical conditions influence pollen behaviour. This information can be useful for studies in atmospheric sciences (cloud physics through interactions with water vapour and hydrometeors) and can also inform possible health outcomes related to pollinosis.

While this study advances our understanding of pollen rupture in simulated thunderstorm conditions, further research is necessary to explore the chemical nature and dynamics of submicron pollen fragments, while also considering health impacts. Future investigations should also consider examining a wider range of pollen species, different electric field configurations, and real-world atmospheric conditions to refine predictive models of allergen exposure and air quality. Nonetheless, this study demonstrates a clear link between atmospheric charge under thunderstorm conditions and the formation of airborne, allergen-rich biological particles. It advances our understanding of thunderstorm asthma and supports better assessment of allergen-related health risks and their environmental impact.
