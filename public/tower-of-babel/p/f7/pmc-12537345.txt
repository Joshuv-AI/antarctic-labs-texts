Glymphatic dysfunction as an imaging biomarker for cognitive impairment in patients with β-thalassemia major: A multimodal MRI study
Authors: Xingye Yang, Meiru Bu, Xi Deng, Haifeng Zheng, Chengli Wu, Wei Cui, Muliang Jiang, Bihong T Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeCerebral lymphatic abnormalities have been associated with neurodegenerative processes. However, brain glymphatic system and its role in cognitive impairment in β-thalassemia major (β-TM) remain unknown. This study assessed brain glymphatic alterations in β-TM using advanced neuroimaging and their correlation with cognitive deficits.MethodThirty-five β-TM patients and forty matched healthy controls (HC) underwent standardized cognitive assessment and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Automated segmentation derived glymphatic parameters: Analysis Along the Perivascular Space index (DTI-ALPS index), choroid plexus volume fraction (CPVF), perivascular space volume fraction (PVSVF), and fractional volume of free water in white matter (FW-WM) and were compared between groups. Correlations with cognitive scores were analyzed.ResultCompared with the HC, β-TM patients showed significantly lower DTI-ALPS index values in the left hemisphere (1.4527vs1.5275,P = 0.003), the right hemisphere (1.3648vs1.4492,P = 0.004) and bilateral hemispheres (1.4087vs1.4884,P = 0.001), alongside higher CPVF values in the left hemisphere (0.5426vs0.4655,P = 0.034), the right hemisphere (0.5296vs0.4439,P = 0.018) and bilateral hemispheres (1.0731vs0.9095,P = 0.01). The patient group also had higher PVSVF values in the white matter (0.2848vs0.2488,P = 0.841), the subcortical regions (0.1718vs0.1470,P = 0.349) and whole brain (0.4566vs0.3958,P = 0.678), and had higher FW-WM value (0.1580vs0.1557,P = 0.384). Lower DTI-ALPS index and higher FW-WM value was associated with poorer cognitive performance in the β-TM patients (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe DTI-ALPS index is a potential neuroimaging biomarker for glymphatic dysfunction and cognitive impairment in β-TM. Increased CP volume implicated underlying glymphatic alterations, supporting integration of glymphatic MRI metrics into clinical cognitive assessment for this population.Keywords: β-Thalassemia, Glymphatic system, Diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space index, Choroid plexus volume, Perivascular space, Cognitive impairment

Introduction

β-thalassemia (β-TM) is a hereditary blood disorder caused by a deficiency in the globin gene, leading to reduced or absent synthesis of the globin peptide chain [1]. It is more common in the Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, Central Asian, Indian, and southern Chinese regions [2]. Studies have shown that patients with β-TM have cognitive impairment, and the underlying mechanism has not yet been clarified [3], [4]. Glymphatic system is a crucial mechanism for waste clearance in the brain, particularly during sleep. The unique structure and function of the lymphatic system in the brain enable it to play a significant role in the distribution and metabolic regulation of substances within the brain. This system facilitates cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation through a perivascular channel network formed by astrocytes [5]. Previous studies have found that glymphatic dysfunction is associated with accumulation of extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) in Alzheimer’s disease and may be related to cognitive impairment [6], [7]. Identifying biomarkers and glymphatic changes associated with cognition is crucial for diagnosis and treatment of patients with cognitive impairment.

Prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility of measuring brain lymphatic function using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium-based contrast agents [8]. However, either intrathecal or intravenous administration of gadolinium-based contrast agents is relatively invasive. Therefore, other non-invasive and non-contrast MRI-based methods have been assessed and have shown promising results. These MRI methods include the measurements of choroid plexus (CP) volume [9], perivascular space (PVS) volume [10], the free water (FW) volume fraction using a bi-tensor diffusion tensor imaging model [11], and the advection of the lymphatic perivascular spaces (ALPS) index to indirectly assess the activity of the perivascular network [12]. As a core structure of the lymphatic system, the abnormal expansion of PVS is closely related to the decreased efficiency of brain metabolic waste clearance [13]. PVS enlargement is commonly observed in white matter (WM) and subcortical regions such as the basal ganglia and thalamus, and has been implicated to be associated with interstitial fluid drainage obstruction [14]. MRI studies have demonstrated a higher frequency of PVS enlargement in patients with thalassemia as compared to healthy individuals, and the enlargement is associated with age [15]. Although traditional visual grading system of PVS enlargement is easy to use and is reproducible, this grading system does not allow the entire brain to be mapped and is limited by insufficient sensitivity and floor/ceiling effects [16], [17], [18]. Fully automated whole-brain PVS volumetric measurement methods based on isotropic 3D MRI have shown good correlation with visual rating scales [19], [20]. However, PVS volume changes in the WM and subcortical regions of patients with β-TM have not been fully studied.

Additional MRI glymphatic metrics such as the FW-WM volume fraction in voxel could be quantitatively analyzed using a DTI-regularized tensor model [11], and an elevated value suggests fluid retention associated with impaired glymphatic function [21], [22], [23]. The DTI-ALPS index evaluates perivascular clearance function by measuring diffusion rates along deep medullary veins [12]. This index has been assessed in various neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, cerebral small vessel disease and hypertension [24], [25], [26], and has shown associations with cognitive impairment [27], [28]. Recent studies have validated the DTI-ALPS index as a marker of glymphatic function, showing significant correlations with MRI parameters following intrathecal injection of gadolinium-based contrast agents [25].

Here, we performed a prospective study enrolling patients with β-TM and age- & gender-matched healthy control (HC) participants who underwent cognitive testing, hematological evaluation, and brain MRI scan for assessment of glymphatic metrics such as the DTI-ALPS index, CPVF, PVSVF, and FW-WM. We also performed correlative analysis of these MRI metrics with cognitive function and with hematological condition such as biochemical and metabolic status. We hypothesized that the patients with β-TM may have significant alterations in the MRI glymphatic metrics as compared to the HC participants, which would be correlated with cognitive functioning and hematological variables. The results of this study should help to identify neuroimaging biomarkers for glymphatic dysfunction in patients with β-TM, which should assist in early diagnosis and prompt treatment of vulnerable patients with cognitive issues.

Methods and materials

Participants

The prospective study enrolled 35 patients with β-TM from our hospital from January 1, 2021 to May 30, 2024. During the same study interval, 40 HC participants were recruited from local schools. The diagnosis of β-TM was made based on clinical, hematologic, and molecular testing. All participants underwent cognitive assessments including Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Modified Mini-Mental State Examination (MMMSE) and brain MRI scan; and the patients with β-TM also had blood testing results. Inclusion criteria for the patient group were as follows: all consecutive patients with a diagnosis of β-TM who could undergo the cognitive testing and brain MRI scan. Exclusion criteria for the patient group were as follows: (1) having contraindications for MRI examinations such as being claustrophobic, or having ferromagnetic implants in the orbits or brain, etc.; (2) having neurological disorders which may be associated with lymphatic dysfunction such as encephalitis, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and neurodegenerative diseases; (3) having brain lesions on MRI such as severe white matter hyperintensities at the levels of the lacunae and/or lateral ventricle body, which would affect the DTI-ALPS index; (4) having other central nervous system disorders such as brain tumors. The HC participants were recruited from local school and were excluded if they had cognitive impairment, central nervous system disorders, or systemic illness that may lead to cognitive impairment. Brain MRI acquisition and imaging analysis

For each subject, MRI data were acquired using a 3.0 T scanner equipped with a 48-channel head coil (SIGNA Premier MR, GE Healthcare, WI, USA). The imaging protocol included T1-weighted (T1w) images with an isotropic resolution of 1.0 mm, obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE) sequence, and axial two-dimensional DTI. The main parameters for DTI were: repetition time (TR) = 5000 ms, echo time (TE) = 56.6 ms, in-plane resolution = 2 mm × 2 mm, slice thickness = 2 mm, no inter-slice gap, and one b-value of 1000 s/mm² with diffusion sensitization applied along 64 non-collinear directions. During MRI scanning, participants were instructed to remain awake throughout the examination to minimize lymphatic functional differences associated with sleep status [29]. Post-processing of T1w and DTI data was performed for each subject to derive the DTI-ALPS index, choroid plexus volume, perivascular space volume, and WM free water index (Supplementary file: Fig. 1S).

DTI-ALPS index calculation

For each subject, the DTI-ALPS index was calculated using the automatic pipeline [30]. The diffusion-weighted (DW) images were preprocessed using the MRtrix3 toolbox (https://www.mrtrix.org/), including denoising and Gibbs ringing artifact removal. Head motions and eddy current distortions were corrected using the FSL toolbox (https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/). Subsequently, a DTI model was fitted to the DW data to derive diffusivity maps along the x-, y-, and z-axes (Dxx, Dyy, and Dzz). T1w images were nonlinearly registered to the MNI template using the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). The Dxx, Dyy, and Dzz maps were first rigidly aligned to the T1w images, and then transformed into MNI space using the transformation matrix and nonlinear deformation fields obtained from the T1w-to-MNI registration. Diffusivity values in projection and association fiber regions (Dxxproj, Dxxassoc, Dyyproj, and Dzzassoc) were extracted using predefined spherical regions of interest (ROIs) with a radius of 5 mm in MNI space (Fig. 1). Finally, the DTI-ALPS index was calculated separately for the right, left, and bilateral hemispheres (ALPS-R, ALPS-L, ALPS-Bil) using the following formula: DTI-ALPS Index = (Dxxassoc + Dxxproj) / (Dyyproj + Dzzassoc). An DTI-ALPS index close to 1.0 reflected minimal diffusion rate, while higher values indicated larger diffusion rates.

Quantitative measurement of CP volume

The CP regions were automatically segmented using a clustering-based approach [31]. First, whole-brain segmentation was performed using the FreeSurfer toolbox [32] to extract ventricular and choroid plexus masks. Voxels within these two masks were then clustered using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), based on the signal intensity of the T1w images. Voxels belonging to the cluster with the highest signal intensity were identified and selected as CP regions (Fig. 2 and Supplementary file: Fig. 2S). Subsequently, two experienced neuroradiologists (both have more than 10 years of experience.) independently conducted a quality check of the CP segmentation results for all participants and the final automatic segmentations were deemed satisfactory with no need for manual correction. Finally, the volume fractions of the left, right, and bilateral choroid plexus (CPVF-L, CPVF-R, and CPVF-Bil, respectively) were calculated by dividing the CP volume by the intracranial volume.

Quantitative measurement of PVS volume

A trained deep learning network, based on the nnU-Net framework [33], was used to automatically segment PVS from T1w images [34] (Fig. 3). The PVS segmentation results were reviewed by two neuroradiologists (both have more than 10 years of experience.) using the Insight Toolkit Snap (ITK-SNAP) software to exclude false positives caused by white matter (WM) lesions and head motion artifacts. To eliminate inter-individual variability in brain size, the PVS volume fraction (PVSVF) in the white matter, subcortical regions, and whole brain was calculated by dividing the PVS volume within each region by the intracranial volume.

Quantitative measurement of the perivascular space (PVS) volume in the white matter (red) and the subcortical region (green) from T1- weighted images and PVS masks.

FW calculation

The preprocessed DW images from the DTI-ALPS processing pipeline were fitted to a regularized bi-tensor model to compute free water fraction maps, using the open-source software package Diffusion Imaging in Python (Dipy, https://dipy.org). A white WM mask excluding the PVS regions was generated in T1w image space and transformed into DW image space using the rigid transformation matrix derived from the registration between DW and T1w images. The mean free water fraction within these regions was then calculated (FW-WM). Comparison of the MRI glymphatic metrics between the patient group with β-TM and the HC group is presented in Table 2.

Cognitive assessment

All study participants underwent cognitive testing including MMMSE and MoCA, prior to brain MRI scan. The MMMSE was a screening test for higher mental function and has been modified slightly for use in pediatric outpatient clinics [35]. This test took 5–10 min to perform, covered a range of cognitive functions including orientation, attention concentration, memory, language, and constructional skill. The MoCA included additional and more complex tasks that evaluated executive function [36]. Items in the MoCA assessed orientation, drawing figures, processing speed, naming objects, memory, recall, attention, vigilance, repetition, verbal fluency, and abstraction. The MMMSE and MoCA cognitive function scales and their subdomain scores were obtained, which were used in correlative analysis between the MRI glymphatic metrics and cognitive function. Items and domains for the MMMSE and MoCA testing are presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Social Science Statistical Package (SPSS) software, version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as counts (frequencies and percentages), and were analyzed using the chi-square test. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. To analyze differences in demographic, clinical, and imaging characteristics between the two groups, continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using an independent t-test. For non-normally distributed variables, continuous data were expressed as median (IQR) and were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

To correct for the effects of age and gender on the DTI-ALPS index, CPVF [37], [38], PVSVF [17], [39] and FW-WM, we performed the group comparisons and correlation analysis after adjusting for these factors. One-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to examine the group differences in the DTI-ALPS index, CPVF, PVSVF, and FW-WM while controlling for the effects of age and gender. Finally, after adjusting for age and gender, the Spearman rank correlation test was conducted to evaluate the correlations of the MRI metrics such as the DTI-ALPS index, CPVF, PVSVF and FW-WM with cognitive scores from MoCA and MMMSE testing as well as hematological variables from blood laboratory testing. Correlations among the MRI metrics were also assessed. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 35 patients with β-TM (15 males and 20 females; mean age 11.3 ± 2.6 years; age range 6–17 years) and 40 HC participants (19 males and 21 females; mean age 10.1 ± 2.4 years; age range 6–16 years) were included in the final analysis. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age and gender (both P > 0.05). Table 1 summarizes the demographic and cognitive scale data for all study participants, and the hematological data for the patient group. Compared to the HC group, the patient group performed significantly worse on the MoCA, MMMSE, and their respective subscales (all P < 0.05) as shown in Table 1.

Between-group differences in MRI metrics

After adjusting for gender and age, there were significant between-group differences in the DTI-ALPS index values between the β-TM group and the HC group. Compared with the HC group, the patient group with β-TM showed significantly lower DTI-ALPS index values in the left hemisphere (1.4527 vs 1.5275, P = 0.003), the right hemisphere (1.3648 vs 1.4492, P = 0.004) and bilateral hemispheres (1.4087 vs 1.4884, P = 0.001).

The patient group showed significantly higher CPVF values in the left hemisphere (0.5426 vs 0.4655, P = 0.034), the right hemisphere (0.5296 vs 0.4439, P = 0.018) and bilateral hemispheres (1.0731 vs 0.9095, P = 0.01).

The patient group had higher PVSVF values in the whole brain (0.4566 vs 0.3958, P = 0.678), the WM (0.2848 vs 0.2488, P = 0.841) and subcortical regions (0.1718 vs 0.1470, P = 0.349), and had slightly higher FW-WM value (0.1580 vs 0.1557, P = 0.384), as compared to the HC group, although not statistically significant (Table 2 and Fig. 4).

Correlation of MRI metrics with cognitive function and clinical blood test values

A lower DTI-ALPS index in both the right and bilateral hemispheres was significantly associated with poorer visuospatial/executive function (r = 0.419, P = 0.012 and r = 0.387, P = 0.022), and language (r = 0.375, P = 0.026 and r = 0.400, P = 0.017) in patients with β-TM.

Brain FW-WM values were significantly negatively correlated with naming ability (r = -0.437, P = 0.009), attention and calculation (r = -0.395, P = 0.019) in patients with β-TM (Supplementary file: Table S1).

There were no significantly correlation of the MRI metrics with the clinical blood test data (all P > 0.001) in patients with β-TM. (Supplementary file: Table S2).

Correlation among the MRI metrics

There was a significant correlation between the DTI-ALPS-R index and the CPVF-R (r = 0.354, P = 0.037) (Supplementary file: Table S3).

Discussion

In this study, we found impaired brain lymphatic function in patients with β-TM using multimodal MRI metrics incorporating structural and diffusion imaging. We found decreased DTI-ALPS index and increased CP volume in patients with β-TM. Additional MRI metrics such as PVSVF and FW-WM showed higher values in patients with β-TM, possibly reflecting early damage with expansion of the PVS and an increase in extracellular free water in the WM stroma as noted in literature [40]. Moreover, we found that glymphatic dysfunction indicated with the MRI metrics was associated with poorer cognitive performance.

This study showed a decreased value of MRI-derived parameter for measuring along the PVS, i.e., DTI-ALPS index, which could potentially serve as a biomarker for brain lymphatic damage in patients with β-TM, which was generally in line with the study in Alzheimer’s disease by Taoka et al. [12]. A prior study assessed the DTI-ALPS index in bilateral cerebral hemisphere but found significantly lower DTI-ALPS values only in the right hemisphere [41]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in study population, as our study focused on children with β-TM while their study focused on older adults at risk of dementia. There have been concerns about using DTI-ALPS to assess lymphatic function. A study by Ringstad et al. stated the limitation of DTI-ALPS index, which might not distinguish between water diffusion around blood vessels and other forms of directional water movement such as diffusion along fiber pathways [42]. Another study by Wright, et al. showed that the DTI-ALPS index was influenced by the diffusion rate of water around blood vessels, and may also be affected by radial asymmetry of projection and association fibers due to aging and neurodegenerative changes [43]. A study by Taoka et al. suggested that the DTI-ALPS index was acquired only in specific locations of the brain and therefore may not truly represent the entire glymphatic system, since waste clearance pathway in the central nervous system may operate in different areas of the brain [44]. Therefore, the DTI-ALPS index and its association with glymphatic function in various disorders should be interpreted with caution, and more studies are needed to characterize the association between the DTI-ALPS index and glymphatic function.

This study observed an increased CP volume in patients with β-TM as compared to the HC participants, a new finding being reported for the first time, to the best of our knowledge. CP is a main organ generating CSF and an important component of the blood-brain barrier, its increased volume may play an important role in the brain pathological process related to β-TM [45]. Previous studies have found enlarged CP being associated with more severe cognitive impairment in patients with Alzheimer's disease [46], [47], and being correlated with clinical symptoms and brain tissue inflammation in patients with multiple sclerosis [38]. We speculate that chronic iron overload might be the key factor driving the increased CP volume in our cohort, since patients with β-TM may have iron accumulation in CP due to long-term blood transfusion [48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]. Iron-induced oxidative stress can affect CP through two pathways, i.e., directly by causing thickening of the basement membrane of epithelial cells [55], [56], or indirectly by trigger inflammatory responses via activation of astrocytes [57]. The association between iron deposition and neurodegenerative diseases has been reported previously [58]. Our study result showing increased CP volume provided a novel prospective addressing the neural correlate of cognitive dysfunction in patients with β-TM. Furthermore, we observed significant association between the CP volume as measured by CPVF and the DTI-ALPS index in the right hemisphere, which should support the notion that CP may affect glymphatic function through regulation of CSF dynamics along the PVS as mentioned in literature [37], [59].

Our study showed an increasing trend of PVS volume as measured by PVSVF in patients with β-TM fraction as compared to the HC participants, although not statistically significant. PVS is a major component of the glymphatic system, which transfers interstitial solutes from the brain to the meninges and cervical lymphatic drainage vessels [60], [61], and its abnormal expansion has been implicated in dementia and in cognitive functioning in patients with carotid plaque [62], [63]. However, the non-significant increase of PVS volume identified in our study was different from the literature reporting moderate to severe dilation of PVS in patients with β-TM [15]. We speculate the following reasons may explain the discrepancy of results regarding PVS volume. First, the patients in our study was younger than the previous study, and the volume of PVS was positively correlated with age [15]. Second, our innovative measurement of PVS may have played a role in the varying PVS volumes. Our use of zonal volume analysis for PVS measurements in the WM and subcortical regions was novel, done for the first time, to the best of our knowledge. This new approach may not be comparable with the semi-quantitative scoring method used in previous reports. Third, the heterogeneity of thalassemia classification, the frequency of blood transfusions and the use of iron chelating agents may have affected the size of PVS. Future research with a larger sample size should help to clarify the relationship between PVS and glymphatic mechanism.

This study found an increased extracellular water as indicated by the FW-WM metric in patients with β-TM. This finding was consistent with prior studies of FW differentiating mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease from the controls [21], and linking increasing FW to cognitive impairment in patients with Alzheimer's disease with or without cerebrovascular disease [22]. This result may reflect an impairment in interstitial fluid drainage due to obstruction of perivascular clearance. Our study also observed significant correlations between the FW-WM and cognitive performance such as naming, attention and calculation ability, highlighting the impact of brain interstitial accumulation on cognitive functioning in patients with β-TM.

This study found lower DTI-ALPS index scores being associated with poorer cognitive performance in visual-spatial skills, executive function and language, indicating these cognitive domains being more susceptible to glymphatic dysfunction. It has been shown that lymphatic damage may lead to deterioration of the neuronal extracellular environment, which may have widespread impacts on neuronal dysfunction and cell death as noted in patients with Alzheimer's disease [64]. Another study also found an association of lymphatic dysfunction as measured by MRI index with cognition in patients with mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease [40]. Our study implicated potential similar underlying neuropathological mechanism affecting patients with β-TM, leading to glymphatic dysfunction and cognitive dysfunction. However, our study did not show significant correlation between the MRI metrics for glymphatic function and the blood laboratory testing data, which was unexpected. Published reports have linked hematological variables such as serum ferritin with cognitive impairment in patients with β-TM [51]. We speculate the varying severity of patients with thalassemia, the extent of blood transfusions and the dosage of iron chelating agents may have complicated the blood laboratory testing data, thus rendered it a non-significant correlation with the MRI metrics. Proper control of these confounding variables in a larger cohort may help to tease out relevant correlations, and may allow an assessment of the interactions among the glymphatic system, cognitive function and hematological variables in patients with β-TM.

There were several limitations to this study. First, this study was limited by a small sample size, which did not allow us to take into consideration of confounding variables such as severity of anemia, treatment strategies, etc. during our analysis. In addition, glymphatic function may be influenced by multiple cardiovascular or physiological factors such as brain perfusion, arterial pulsatility, sleep and respiration, which could not be properly controlled in this cohort with a modest sample size. Second, the MRI metrics such as the DTI-ALPS index could not be used to evaluate the brain lymphatic function for the whole brain because this index could only be assessed at the level of the lateral ventricular body [12], [65]. Therefore, this index may not fully reflect the extent of glymphatic dysfunction observed in our patients with β-TM. Third, we did not use gadolinium-based contrast agents to measure lymphatic function because of its invasiveness as an intravenously given agent and the time needed for additional scanning. However, contrast-enhanced brain MRI was considered the gold standard for measuring lymphatic function [8]. Nevertheless, the DTI-ALPS index obtained without the contrast has been shown to correlate highly with the MRI metrics for glymphatic function obtained with contrast administration. [25] Fourth, we only used T1-weighted images for automatic segmentation of CP and PVS. Although Sepehrband et al. [66] observed a higher number of PVSs when using standalone T1w or T2-weighted (T2w) images compared to when enhanced PVS contrast images (generated via filtered image division, e.g., T1w/T2w), no evidence supports a more superior image in PVS segmentation was found. To mitigate this limitation, we conducted manual checks of all CP measurements and PVS masks in this study to ensure accuracy of the automatic segmentation. Furthermore, we noted that segmentation accuracy is inherently dependent on image preprocessing quality and vulnerable to tubular noise misidentification as PVS. Fifth, this study lacked histopathological validation of the MRI metrics such as FW-WM reflecting interstitial fluid stagnation [67], [68], the underlying histopathological process of extracellular water changes remains not fully understood. Sixth, glymphatic function may be affected by circadian rhythm, which may function differently between day and night [69]. However, the timing of MRI scanning for this study was not controlled, which may result in variation of their glymphatic function.

In summary, we found alterations of MRI metrics for glymphatic function in patients with β-TM, which was correlated with their cognitive functioning. Notably, we identified decreased DTI-ALPS index and enlarged CP may potentially serve as the neuroimaging biomarkers for glymphatic dysfunction in patients with β-TM. The study results support integration of glymphatic MRI into overall clinical assessment of the patients with β-TM who are vulnerable for cognitive impairment.
