A systematic review of birth trauma in the Middle East
Authors: Haya S. Zedan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological trauma associated with childbirth is underappreciated in the Middle East as an issue that can significantly alter a woman's perception and satisfaction with her birth experience, increasing risk of postpartum mental health issues such as anxiety, depression or post-traumatic stress disorders (PTSD), impacting her ability to fully engage in motherhood and bond with and care for her newborn. This study reviews published literature on birth trauma and PTSD in the region to identify links to current maternity care practices, maternal mental health, and other health and social outcomes and offer insight into areas for further research and action.MethodsA systematic review of peer-reviewed articles published in English 2000-2025, using CRD reporting guidelines. Pre-determined inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied, selected articles were evaluated using CASP checklists. ResultsEighteen articles report women in the Middle East find giving birth stressful, with notable rates of childbirth anxiety, trauma and PTSD symptoms. This is influenced by maternity care practice issues, routine medical interventions, limited pain management, lack of support, and communication challenges with care providers, linked to women’s perceptions of having negative birthing experiences. Social norms, women’s cultural beliefs, perceptions and satisfaction with labour and birth are intertwined with fear of childbirth, pain tolerance, ratings of traumatic experiences,symptoms of PTSD, and other maternal and infant health and outcomes. Strategies for prevention, management of birth trauma and enhancement of the birth experience are outlined. DiscussionThis study highlights the importance of consideration of women’s birth experiences, needs for enhanced empowerment, support and resources in maternity care and options for pain management and relief during childbirth in Middle Eastern birth settings. Links to postpartum maternal mental health, ability to breastfeed and bond with the newborn, potential for recovery, subsequent pregnancies and wider impact on infant development, relationships and families should not be underestimated. Recommendations and conclusionsIt is crucial to comprehend the fundamental impact birth experiences have on women’s mental health and wellbeing, and their perceptions and capacity for pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding and motherhood. Further research on maternal mental health, birth trauma, PTSD and development of corresponding policy, strategies and interventions in maternity care in the Middle East is necessary.

Statement of significance

Issue

Trauma or Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) as related to childbirth has not yet been widely appreciated or studied in the Middle East.

What is already known

Although childbirth is often considered a positive and rewarding life experience, many women find the experience disturbing or traumatic. Globally, birth trauma has been identified in around 30% of women's experiences, whereas PTSD prevalence is around 4%.

What this paper adds

The identification of prior research on psychological birth trauma and PTSD in the Middle East draws healthcare professionals' and policymakers' attention to the issue. Highlighting links to increasing maternal mental health challenges and other health and social outcomes will provide direction to areas for further research and action.

Introduction

Psychological trauma may be brought on by exposure to shock, harm, or violence, which elicits strong emotional reactions and a stress response in the individual and impairs their defence mechanisms [1]. Physical manifestations such as headaches or nausea, gastrointestinal problems, erratic behavior or feelings, flashbacks, nightmares, disassociation and disruptions to daily functioning can be part of the longer-term effects of exposure to traumatic events. Those who have suffered a traumatic event may experience a sense of helplessness and fear and many people struggle to move on afterward [2]. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), may manifest as hyperarousal or anxiety in a individual, or an aversion to stimuli (places, people, items, images or even scents) that may be associated with or in some way trigger discomforting memories of the traumatic experience. It may also manifest as anger, frustration, depression, despair, or reliving the experience through dreams, nightmares, or flashbacks, as well as difficulty concentrating or insomnia [2–4].

Birth trauma and maternal mental health

Childbirth is often considered to be a positive and rewarding life experience, envisioned as empowering women and offering them feelings of satisfaction and contentment as they enter into the motherhood role. However, in reality, women report a wide range of childbirth experiences, with many finding it to be intense, disturbing, disappointing, upsetting or traumatic [3–8].

Birth trauma as a psychological construct (to be differentiated from physical trauma or birth injury) has been defined as a sense of actual or threatened injury or death to the mother or baby that is subjective and determined only by the woman experiencing it [3–8]. Recent research efforts have developed a woman-centered definition of a traumatic childbirth experience as one where “interactions and/or events directly related to childbirth that caused overwhelming distressing emotions and reactions; leading to short and/or long-term negative impacts on a woman’s health and wellbeing” [9].

Globally, birth trauma has been identified in around 30% of women’s experiences, whereas PTSD prevalence is around 4% [4, 7, 8, 10]. Fear of childbirth, lack of support, previous negative experiences, prior exposure to traumatic events or struggling with health complications or healthcare access during pregnancy, labor and delivery increase the likelihood that women may find the experience difficult or challenging, and increase the risks of maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality [4, 7, 11–14].

Traumatic birth experiences may result in the development of postpartum stress, and psychological disorders such as anxiety, depression or PTSD [4, 8, 13–18]. The perception of not having control over or input into decision-making during labour or birth might result in feelings of trauma [4, 7, 8, 16, 19, 20]. Women’s feelings of isolation, helplessness, lack of support and disconnection during childbirth have been linked to birth trauma, as well as unnecessary medical intervention during labor and mode of birth [4, 7, 16, 19, 21]. Fractured or fraught interpersonal relationships or negative interactions with healthcare providers and issues with mistreatment and obstetric violence are a substantial risk factor for birth trauma and birth-related PTSD [3, 4, 8, 10, 16, 17, 19, 22, 23].

When women’s mental wellbeing suffers during the aftermath of the birth or the postpartum period as a result of a traumatic experience, this may impact her sense of self, her ability to breastfeed and bond with her newborn and may also affect her relationships with her partner or family [4, 7, 8, 13, 16, 19, 24]. Early mother-baby attachment and maternal mental health challenges have also been associated with poor developmental and psychological outcomes for the child [8, 13, 25, 26].

A woman’s traumatic childbirth experience may affect her desire to venture subsequent pregnancies, or reconsider subsequent birth circumstances [4, 7, 8, 13, 15, 27, 28]. Women may prefer to give birth at home, for instance, to prevent another traumatic hospital experience [19, 27–30]. It is apparent that traumatic birth experiences can have significant wide-ranging effects on women and their families [7, 8, 12, 13, 19, 24].

With the global efforts to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals, the World Health Organization has issued updated recommendations and guidance to support the improvement of the quality and positive experience of maternity care during labour and childbirth (intrapartum) and postnatally (after birth), specifically outlining the values and actions that can be considered to prevent or resolve traumatic birth experiences and integrate effective perinatal mental health support [13, 31–33].

Birth trauma in the Middle East

Psychological birth trauma has not yet been widely appreciated or studied in the Middle East, despite the evidence on global prevalence and increasing attention to the importance and impact of the birth experience and healthcare service issues, especially post-COVID and in light of the more recent challenges for refugee women as a result of civil conflict and war in some parts of the region [18, 28, 30, 34–37].

There have been research efforts in the Middle East on broader issues such as maternal mobidity and mortality, postpartum mood disorders, fear of childbirth, mistreatment and obstetric violence and the importance of respectful maternity care [12, 21, 36–47], however published research directly addressing or evaluating psychological birth trauma and PTSD is rare.

Research from the region has shown that women are likely to report feelings of distress and increased anxiety for their safety and the welfare of their newborns, displaying symptoms of trauma and recalling more details of their birth experiences in the days and weeks afterward, as at the time of the birth itself they may be physically fatigued, preoccupied with the newborn and experiencing a rush of hormones that may not offer the mental clarity or space to process the experience fully [6, 8, 38, 39, 48]. Patient satisfaction surveys may be influenced by a reluctance to be seen as complaining or a tendency to avoid offending or disappointing healthcare providers or researchers by sharing unfavorable experiences, particularly if they were thought to be connected to the same maternity care facility [19, 21, 36, 38, 40, 47]. Social norms and cultural expectations have a significant role to play in the views and experiences of childbirth and will therefore also influence perceptions and experiences of trauma; and even though patient conversations and disclosures are meant to be confidential, these issues may cause women to worry that their statements will impact their access to quality health services in the future [21, 36, 40, 41, 47, 48].

With the efforts at increasing the levels of women’s education and empowerment in the region, the awareness of the birth experience as a significant life event and the factors that influence women’s satisfaction and their mental health and social outcomes is now necessary.

Aim of the study

This review seeks to identify prior research on psychological birth trauma and PTSD in women in the Middle East, drawing healthcare professionals’ and policymakers’ attention to the issue and its links to current birth practices, maternal mental health challenges and other health and social outcomes. This review also aims to offer recommendations and insight into areas that may be investigated and actioned through further research.

Methods

Design and search strategy

To achieve the stated aim, a systematic search was used to conduct this review, adhering to The Centre of Reviews and Dissemination Guidelines [49]. Several prominent scientific research databases were selected, including Cochrane, PsycINFO, PubMed (Medline) and others such as Science Direct, BMC and Google Scholar, which encompass widely recognized professional and rigorous peer-reviewed journals.

Search terms

A detailed search strategy was developed for each database according to its’ specific features (Table 1), with the advice of an expert database search librarian. The Boolean operators “OR” and “AND” were utilized to complete the search and combine results. Search results title and keywords were reviewed for initial selection, and duplicates were removed. This was followed by an evaluation of selected abstracts. Inclusion criteria

The articles were selected based on an established eligibility evaluation process for this systematic review. Articles reviewed were published in the past 25 years (2000–2025), that discussed aspects of the birth experience as related to women’s perceptions and assessment of psychological trauma or PTSD. Original studies and systematic reviews of literature written in the English language were included in this review.

Exclusion criteria

Criteria was determined to exclude articles if they:

Did not directly discuss psychological birth trauma or PTSD in women giving birth in the Middle East.Were not published in English; due to translation challenges, resource and time constraints.Were not available to access as full text, grey literature or as academic theses.

Critical Appraisal and Quality Assessment of Included Articles

Data abstraction and synthesis

Data were abstracted from the selected articles and synthesized using a pre-designed Excel sheet to summarize the information for author, publication year, aims, country, study design, setting and sample population, key findings and recommendations. Other articles identified in the bibliographies of the selected articles were subjected to the same inclusion/exclusion criteria and were added as necessary.

Thematic analysis of the findings of the included articles was conducted using The Centre of Reviews and Dissemination Guidelines in an iterative manner, to develop insight into the key areas reported, grouping and re-grouping of issues identified until arriving at the most comprehensive and coherent arrangement of themes to outline and present the issues and factors on the birth experience, psychological trauma and potential development of PTSD and other maternal and infant health and social outcomes [49].

Impact of birth trauma on emotional/mental health: anxiety, depression and PTSD

Seven of the eighteen included articles reported statistical data on childbirth anxiety, traumatic childbirth experiences, trauma symptoms or post-traumatic stress disorder prevalence. One study from Tunisia found 74.5% with severe childbirth anxiety [60]. PTSD symptoms were found to be between 5.8% and 26% [54, 55, 64]. A study from Morocco reported PTSD prevalence at 28.57% [63]. As for traumatic childbirth experiences, several studies reported findings ranging from 23% to 48% [55, 59, 60] (Table 3).

Associations of postpartum PTSD were found with women’s level of education, gestational age at delivery, number of prenatal care visits, pregnancy complications, pregnancy intervals, fear of childbirth, labor duration, and mode of delivery [51, 55, 59, 60, 63] as well as preterm birth, neonatal stay in ICU and feelings of emotional distress, guilt, and risk of abuse or neglect of the baby [52, 55]. Elements of spiritual, religious and faith-based beliefs around childbearing and maternal cultural values around fate and destiny, intertwined with elements of guilt and shame around women’s manner and behaviours as justifying their mistreatment as a form of atonement during their birth experience were also mentioned [52, 55, 62–64]. Significant associations with maternal occupation or work, previous psychiatric vulnerabilities, anxiety, depression levels and risk of PTSD were also found [51, 52, 54, 63, 66, 67].“…Distress(ed) at the fact that all of this was happening when it shouldn’t be, and I didn’t understand why and what was happening. One study from Iran in 2019 [59] reported that lack of exercise during pregnancy, absence of pain relief during labour and birth and fear of childbirth were predictors of birth trauma, and this was reaffirmed in findings from Tunisia and Morocco, as well as significant associations with use of media and internet as a source of information, and repeated vaginal examinations [60, 63]. More recently, studies on Syrian and Palestinian women found that negative perceptions of self-efficacy, control and perceived support were related to reported trauma symptoms and PTSD [64, 66].

Women’s perceptions of traumatic birth experiences: previous trauma, pain, and maternity care

Several articles assessed for factors that shape the birth experience and women’s perceptions of it, such as communication with the mother, awareness and support of her needs, clinical competence, professional responsibility, as well as the provision of routine care, hospital rules, environment and equipment [48, 51, 67]. The availability of continuous support for birthing women is a challenge in hospitals with a shortage of staff, high patient volume and overwork [63, 67]. Other key factors shaping the birth experience were resource limitations in childbirth facilities which may lead to mistreatment, as well as provider factors, women’s expectations themselves, and community barriers [59, 62, 67]. This is important to recognize, especially in the context of more challenging birth settings such as those in conflict zones.“We just dealt with the body. Women found giving birth stressful, and that there was an overuse of routine medical procedures such as episiotomies, analgesics, and vacuum extraction during labour and delivery [60, 67]. Medical interventions and labour pain intensity were generally linked to a poor birthing experience [48, 51, 55, 58–60, 64, 67]. Protracted or stalled labour was considered traumatic, as well as the agony of a forceps or instrumental birth, the loneliness felt in the hospital during a protracted labour and the difficulty of requesting and obtaining help from strangers [59, 60]. Past pain recall significantly influenced women’s fear [59, 60]. The more negatively the birthing experience was perceived and the more painful it was felt to be, the poorer the satisfaction with the birthing process [48, 52, 59, 60, 64]. Intrapartum care quality (during labour, childbirth and after-care) provided also influenced women’s experiences of labour and birth [57, 59, 61, 64, 66, 67].“For the baby, they told me I had to go to the operating room and I felt so bad. Many women who had never personally experienced a traumatic birth were aware of and affected by other women who had. Many shared stories of sisters, neighbors, and acquaintances who had previously lost a loved one during childbirth. Some women had lost their own mothers at birth, a sibling or sister, or both, and therefore dreaded getting pregnant, going through labor, or giving birth themselves [60, 67]. In the literature reported from many countries in the region, women have significant concerns and dread institutionalized childbirth and delivery practices, and this is linked to the influence of the media and anecdotes from peers in their families and social circles [60, 62, 63, 67]. Pregnant women are generally wary of painful physiological processes associated with labor and delivery and other invasive bodily procedures [48, 52, 60, 62, 64, 67]. A systematic review from 2019 studied and categorized women’s needs as relating to health system management and care needs, the educational needs of the health system, primary care follow-up and continity of care, the need to develop physical, psychological and social care packages, and psychosocial support and counselling [58]. A more recent study from Saudi Arabia reported significant impacts of witnessing traumatic childbirth on female midwifery students and their perceptions influencing their desire to have their own children in the future and their preferences for type of birth [67].

Strategies and interventions to prevent psychological birth trauma

Among the prominent and recurrent recommendations observed in the literature was the need to provide appropriate counselling and support options for women and design and integration of psychological and psychosocial interventions [48, 52–54, 58, 66, 67]. Early identification and screening were advised [54, 63].“We needed a psychologist, a person above the nurses but below the doctors, a person that had the time to sit and discuss and explain, a person that could understand the emotions that we were going through, that person who speaks our language.’’ [52].

“We needed a psychologist, a person above the nurses but below the doctors, a person that had the time to sit and discuss and explain, a person that could understand the emotions that we were going through, that person who speaks our language.’’ [52].

One study from 2018 conducted an extensive review of childbirth interventions [57] and another [56] explored expert opinions to identify several key strategies to prevent psychological birth trauma: their findings were similar: the provision of continuous support during childbirth, practical childbirth preparation classes, group prenatal care, and the development of individualized birth plans during antenatal visits in primary care. A study from 2019 recommended woman-centred care and opportunities for active decision-making [59]. Others recommended educational programs and targeted protocols at both national and international levels to improve knowledge and skills for healthcare providers on these issues [58, 67]. In 2020, one study identified psychological birth trauma prevention strategies such as building knowledge and skills in birth preparedness, mothers’ empowerment and understanding the importance of mental health care in maternity services [61].“Maternal mental health can be promoted through education. Prenatal training should include skills for effective communication, coping with stress, managing interpersonal relationships, and decision making in critical situations…” [61].

“Maternal mental health can be promoted through education. Prenatal training should include skills for effective communication, coping with stress, managing interpersonal relationships, and decision making in critical situations…” [61].

Study findings advised responsible caregiving, reviewing and strengthening the support loop, encouraging caregiver good behavior [53, 55, 58, 61, 62, 65, 67] and efforts at deepening trust, reconsidering the over-medicalization of childbirth, and availability of options for labor pain relief [61]. Better integration of prenatal and antenatal care, prioritizing continuity of care and coordination of caregivers, and reconstruction of birth spaces for better efficiency are also advised [61, 67].“Continuity of care is the solution. This is the best that in our country can reduce the fear of childbirth and pregnancy concerns, as well as mental health problems at birth. Midwifery led care and the continuing midwifery support are the development that must be created to reduce maternal mental problems…” [61].

“Continuity of care is the solution. This is the best that in our country can reduce the fear of childbirth and pregnancy concerns, as well as mental health problems at birth. Midwifery led care and the continuing midwifery support are the development that must be created to reduce maternal mental problems…” [61].

Two studies discussed a need for developing and sustaining health policies that guarantee culturally competent and safe care to prevent traumatic birth experiences [63, 64]. Most recently, the study conducted by researchers in Afghanistan and Pakistan recommended a comprehensive approach to address the challenges of birth trauma and postpartum PTSD, including raising public awareness, providing mental health support and resources, and integrating postpartum mental health and research into medical education programs [65].

Discussion

This review sought to identify efforts to study psychological birth trauma in the Middle East and found eighteen articles on women’s views and experiences of birth and links to postpartum mental health issues such as depression, anxiety or PTSD, as well as other maternal and infant health and social outcomes.

This review revealed that Middle Eastern women were generally dissatisfied with how they were treated during childbirth, and many describe these experiences as upsetting and traumatic [51, 59, 60, 62, 64–67]. These findings echo what has been documented in birth trauma research globally [5, 7–9, 15, 16, 20, 24, 26], and require increased attention from healthcare providers and policy-makers in the region, with the appreciation of the challenges in the provision of maternity care [18, 21, 23, 36, 37, 39, 41, 43], WHO guidelines for intrapartum and postpartum care, and international impetus to improve on maternal morbidity and mortality indicators [12, 31, 33].

Women’s health outcomes and quality of life are significantly impacted by difficult or traumatic childbirth experiences [52, 53, 55, 58, 67]. This review identified factors that are key in influencing women’s perceptions of their birth experiences and the potential for development of mental health issues, trauma symptoms and posttraumatic stress disorder, among them dread or fear of childbirth [54, 55, 59, 62–64, 67], previous negative experiences [51, 52, 59, 62, 63], the influence of popular media and cultural beliefs [59, 62, 63] overuse of medical procedures or mistreatment [55, 62, 67], the availability of support and companionship [55, 59, 62], and pain management [54, 55, 59, 62, 67].

The wide range of prevalence rates reported in the included studies from the region is noteworthy, and may be due to variations in the definitions and criteria for screening, and diagnostic measures applied, social or culturally-specific factors in the birthing contexts in these countries, or linked to other methodological issues such as recollection or politeness bias [36, 40, 47, 54, 55, 63, 64]. It is important to consider these issues when reviewing the timing, techniques and means of debriefing, development and use of screening and surveys to assess women’s psychological health and wellbeing postpartum, in order to ensure appropriateness and cultural sensitivity of the pathways for intervention and support offered. Interestingly, the study in a sample of 302 women from Morocco did not find significant correlations between postpartum PTSD, pre-term or instrumental births, episiotomy, emergency cesearean, use of oxytocin or long labor duration, negative experiences with staff, poor sense of control or pain during labor [63]. These factors therefore warrant further study and consideration.

This review also found that birth experience impacted women’s ability to breastfeed and bond with their newborns, as well as on their roles as mothers, on their marital relationships and their desire to venture further pregnancies, however these issues were not explored in-depth [51, 54, 55, 67]. With the knowledge of the challenges and targets for regional breastfeeding rates and their short and long-term maternal and neonatal health impact, the links between traumatic birth experiences and breastfeeding must also be scrutinized and actioned [21, 31–33, 42].

Several articles included assessed strategies and interventions to prevent psychological birth trauma, incorporating a better understanding of women’s needs and enhancing their birth experiences, positively influencing health outcomes for both mother and child [52, 56–58, 61, 65]. Among the proposed strategies were the design and integration of psychological/psychosocial interventions and the provision of appropriate counselling and continuous care and support options for women to engage in active decision making, such as practical prenatal/childbirth preparation classes, group prenatal care, and opportunity to develop individualized birth plans during antenatal visits in primary care and companionship during childbirth [48, 52–54, 56–59, 66, 67].

Several authors advocate for more responsible and respectful woman-centred caregiving, deepening trust, and the necessity of building the research base, reconsidering the over-medicalization of childbirth, the psychological effects on women and the wider health and social impact, factoring in the context of resource availability and the potential to reconstruct birth spaces for better efficiency [53, 55, 58, 59, 61–65].

The findings of this review also emphasize the importance of development of health policies and culturally appropriate medical education, public awareness programs and targeted protocols at both national and international levels to improve knowledge and skills for healthcare providers in mental health in maternity services and understanding the importance of mothers’ empowerment and birth preparedness to prevent traumatic birth experiences [53, 55, 58, 59, 61–65, 67]. The findings of the studies included in this review echo those of previous work in the region highlighting maternity care practices within health systems and their impact on women’s experiences and health outcomes, including maternal mental health [12, 21, 36, 42, 43, 47] and many other prominent international studies on these issues [3–8, 15, 20, 22, 24].

There is a limited literature on the influence of community on the emergence of fear of childbirth, however it is widely accepted that negative media and stories surrounding childbirth are a primary source of maternal dread and worry related to pregnancy and childbirth [8, 10, 20, 39, 45, 60, 62, 63]. Enhancing childbirth awareness and education, as well as encouraging maternal empowerment and training for maternity staff on woman-centred care, shared-decision-making and integrating trauma-informed care practices and perinatal mental health support into services will be a significant means to prevent traumatic experiences and mitigate the risks for perinatal mental health issues, in alignment with World Health Organization guidelines and recommendations for the perinatal period [22, 31–33]. This review highlights the need to re-emphasise the importance and reconsider women’s requirements for education, support, and pain relief during childbirth in Middle Eastern birth settings and maternity systems.

Strengths and limitations

This review utilized a systematic search and analysis methodology and returned comprehensive and informative findings, spanning a 20-year period, and is unique in reviewing the issues of birth trauma and postpartum PTSD in the Middle Eastern region. The review was limited to the English language and published scientific research due to resource and time constraints, however there may be other literature that was not included that could shed further light on the issues discussed here, especially those in more anecdotal or narrative forms in the Arabic language or other regional languages.

Research on psychological birth trauma and PTSD in the Middle East is considerably rare, despite increased awareness and attention towards perinatal mental health, birth experiences, fear of childbirth, mistreatment and obstetric violence. It is noted that Iran currently leads the research efforts in the area of birth trauma and PTSD in the region, it was therefore intentional to include all relevant types of studies -even if their CASP scores were lower- due to the importance of pointing health policy makers to the seeds of research efforts and put forward the importance of further support for work to boost the knowledge base in the Middle Eastern region. Despite some flagged weaknesses in these efforts, as outlined and scored in the methodological assessment of quality, the work presented here is valuable.

Conclusions

This review identifies efforts to study psychological birth trauma in the Middle East, and key factors influencing women’s perceptions and feelings towards their birth experiences and links to postpartum mental health issues such as depression, anxiety or PTSD, and other maternal and infant health and social outcomes. The findings indicate a general dissatisfaction with how women are treated during childbirth, with many women describing these experiences as upsetting and traumatic, impacting their quality of life. The importance of empowered, coordinated, supportive and respectful maternity care must be emphasized. Identifying and comprehending the fundamental effects birth experiences have on a woman’s perception of childbirth, her mental and emotional health and her capacity to nurture her newborn and engage in motherhood, especially considering the cultural and social context, is crucial. The needs of pregnant and birthing women and how birth is approached must be explored in more depth, and the resourcing, design and implementation of appropriate health and psychosocial interventions to prevent and resolve psychological birth trauma in the Middle East are necessary.
