Atlas of intracranial electroencephalography complexity across cortical regions and global states of consciousness in the human brain
Authors: Lina Jeantin, Pierre Bourdillon, Marc Guenot, Hélène Bastuji, Jacobo D Sitt, Lionel Naccache
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectroencephalography (EEG) signal complexity reflects the richness of brain activity and is considered a marker of consciousness. However, its normative values across physiological conscious states using intracranial EEG (iEEG) across cortical areas remain poorly defined. We aimed at referencing iEEG complexity in the human brain and its links with global states of consciousness. We analyzed 5,703 iEEG recordings from healthy cortices in 106 participants during wakefulness, N2, N3, and REM sleep, using complementary markers: Kolmogorov complexity, permutation entropy, and spectral entropy. An independent dataset comprising 474 recordings was used for validation and added data on epileptic cortices and propofol anesthesia. iEEG complexity decreased with reduced consciousness, with the highest values in wake/REM and the lowest under propofol. Complexity was more variable during N2/N3 sleep. Frontoparietal regions exhibited the highest complexity in conscious states. Epileptic cortex showed lower complexity than healthy cortex during wake and REM. A random forest classifier reliably classified sleep stages using complexity markers. Markers of iEEG signal complexity reliably index global states of consciousness. These normative data support the use of complexity as a marker of physiological consciousness, cortical integrity, and sleep-stage detection.

Consciousness varies across wake, sleep, and anesthesia, yet the field lacks reference standards for how neural complexity changes across these states. We assemble 6,177 intracranial electroencephalography recordings from 1,899 channels to create an atlas of regional, state-dependent brain-signal complexity in humans. Using three complementary metrics (Kolmogorov, permutation, and spectral entropy), we show that complexity decreases from wakefulness to N2/N3 sleep, rebounds in REM toward wake, and collapses under propofol; the highest values localize to the frontoparietal cortex, and the epileptic cortex shows lower complexity. A classifier trained on these markers accurately classifies brain states, enabling benchmarking and translation across sleep, epilepsy, and consciousness research.

Introduction

Electroencephalographic (EEG) complexity refers to the degree of variability and unpredictability in neural signals over time. It is considered to reflect the richness and integration of neural activity, as an indirect index of information processing capacity in the brain (1). Signal-derived measures indexing the predictability and regularity of EEG time series have been developed to quantify complexity across temporal and spectral scales. Such measures include spectral entropy (2), permutation entropy (3), and Kolmogorov complexity (4). In recent years, markers of EEG complexity have been validated as tools for assessing residual consciousness in patients with disorders of consciousness (4, 5). However, these studies have relied on surface EEG and in patients with extensive cerebral lesions, preventing the generalizability of the values of EEG complexity to normal cortices and physiological consciousness. In fact, “global” states of consciousness—such as wakefulness, nonrapid eye movement (NREM) sleep stages N2 and N3, and REM sleep (6)—are associated with distinct patterns of brain activity (7). These states involve dynamic changes in brain function (8), providing a natural model for studying fluctuations in physiological consciousness.

In the context of epilepsy, alterations in consciousness are common during seizures. Intracranial EEG (iEEG) enables to explore these phenomena with higher spatial precision (9, 10). Yet, the application of EEG complexity measures to intracranial recordings in physiological states remains largely unexplored. Importantly, normative values of complexity measures during different stages of physiological consciousness in unstimulated resting states have not been well established for identifying normal levels of cerebral signal complexity. Current reference data are based almost exclusively on scalp EEG (4, 5, 11), or studies evaluating evoked responses to sensory stimuli (12) or intracranial stimulation (13). Little is known about how EEG complexity spontaneously behaves without sensory stimuli or intracranial stimulations, and whether it could serve as a reliable index of fluctuations in consciousness. Understanding the regional distribution and state-dependent modulation of EEG complexity measures in the healthy human brain is essential for interpreting deviations in pathological contexts.

In this study, we aimed to systematically quantify EEG complexity markers across a large covering of cortical regions using resting-state intracranial recordings in healthy human cortices. By capturing signal complexity during wakefulness and sleep, we sought to create a normative map of these markers and characterize their evolution during natural transitions in consciousness to assess whether they do index the level of consciousness. We added a propofol-induced anesthesia condition using an external validation dataset and compared iEEG complexity in healthy and epileptic cortices. Finally, to validate our findings, we tested whether a random forest classifier could predict sleep states based on complexity measures in a reverse reasoning exercise.

Methods

Datasets and authorizations

The current study is based on two datasets. The MNI-Open dataset is a collection of iEEG recordings in 106 subjects from the Montreal Neurology Institute and the Grenoble-Alpes University Hospital undergoing iEEG recordings for presurgical evaluation of drug-resistant epilepsy (14). The BRD dataset was obtained from a previous study investigating functional connectivity across states of consciousness (15) that received external approval (Comité Consultatif de Protection des Personnes se Prêtant à des Recherches Biomédicales; authorization no. 22236S). Written consent was obtained from patients for the re-use of their stereo-EEG recordings, which were conducted in the context of drug-resistant epilepsy, for the initial study (15) and subsequent further studies involving the same recordings. No modifications were made to the standard care of the patients.

EEG acquisition

In the MNI-Open dataset, iEEG acquisition was performed using Dixi intracerebral electrodes, homemade MNI intracerebral electrodes, AdTech intracerebral electrodes, and AdTech subdural strips and grids (14). In the BRD dataset, EEG acquisition was performed using Dixi electrodes, including five to 15 contacts (2 × 0.8 mm, 1.5 mm interspace). Signals were recorded with a Micromed system (Micromed France SAS) at a sampling frequency of 256 Hz. EEG acquisition during general anesthesia was performed at the end of the surgical procedure during the per-operative imaging control, using targeted-controlled infusion of propofol monitored by the bispectral index (16) with a target of 50. Recordings under general anesthesia did not extend the length of the procedure.

Electrode localization and region segmentation

In the MNI-Open dataset, electrode localization registered in the MNI space is provided alongside the recordings. Contacts were identified in the subject's space and coregistered in the ICBM 2009 a symmetric template (1 × 1 × 1 mm). In the BRD database, the location of the electrodes was obtained in the MNI space using MRIcro (17) and SPM12 (18) after manual localization on the postoperative MRI performed 24 h after surgery. MNI-Open signals are provided by the dataset authors already in bipolar montage, with bipolar derivations formed by neighboring contacts and channel coordinates corresponding to the midpoint between the two contacts. In both datasets, the channel position was indexed as the midpoint between the electrode contacts used to create the bipolar channel. Each bipolar channel was attributed to a brain region in the Landman and Warfield segmentation template (MICCAI 2012 Workshop on Multi-Atlas Labeling; see Table S1), yielding a total of 38 subregions per hemisphere. Tridimensional representations of electrode positions were computed using MNE-Python (19) and FreeSurfer (20) templates.

Recordings and sleep staging

The MNI-Open dataset included 1-min, single-channel recordings in wake, N2, N3, and REM sleep. The BRD dataset included 9.5-min recordings in wake, N2, N3, REM, and propofol anesthesia (subsequently cropped to 1-min recordings). In both datasets, sleep was manually scored by certified sleep experts in accordance with recommendations and adapted to iEEG data (14, 15, 21, 22). Awake recordings were performed during calm wakefulness with closed eyes. All channels from one patient in one sleep stage were recorded simultaneously.

EEG preprocessing

Detailed preprocessing steps for the MNI-Open dataset can be found in the original publication (14). Briefly, signals were resampled to 200 Hz using an anti-aliasing filter at 80 Hz, and power-line noise was reduced with adaptive filters (50 or 60 Hz). Artifacts were visually identified by an experienced neurophysiologist and excluded, resulting in some subjects having multiple nonconsecutive clean segments that were concatenated with 2-s zero-amplitude buffers and subsequently zero-padded to a uniform length (MNI dataset documentation). Signals were downloaded in EDF format, and no additional filtering or resampling was performed on these traces. To avoid any bias introduced by the inserted zero-amplitude periods, we automatically detected and masked these flat sections prior to analysis. The EDF files were loaded as MNE Raw objects, and, for each channel, the signal was scanned using 200-ms sliding windows with a 100-ms step; windows with an SD below 1 × 10−12 µV were flagged as flat. Consecutive flagged samples were grouped into contiguous segments, and only segments lasting ≥0.5 s were retained. Flat segments were stored as MNE annotations (“zero-amplitude”) applied to the Raw object. Data were then epoched, and any epoch overlapping a zero-amplitude annotation was excluded from downstream analyses (quantitative markers for these epochs were set to NaN). All channels in the MNI-Open dataset were located in healthy cortices. To ensure data comparability between the two datasets, EEG signals from the BRD dataset were resampled at 200 Hz after applying an anti-aliasing filter at 80 Hz. Signals were band-pass filtered using a finite-impulse response filter with a Hamming window between 0.5 and 80 Hz and a notch filter at 50 Hz. Because operating-room propofol recordings exhibited prominent nonphysiological high-frequency noise, they were additionally low-pass filtered at 40 Hz. To assess the potential impact of bandwidth differences on complexity estimates, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which all BRD conditions were low-pass filtered at 40 Hz (Fig. S8). Artifacts were visually detected, an annotation for artifacts was manually added to the EEG raw trace, and the corresponding periods were masked using the same annotation-based approach as described above (epochs overlapping artifact annotations were excluded and their quantitative markers set to NaN). Bipolar channels were created, with bipolar defined within each iEEG electrode by pairing adjacent contacts, with each contact used in a single bipolar pair. Channels in the white matter were excluded. Traces were manually screened by a certified neurophysiologist (L.J.) for the detection of epileptiform abnormalities. Selected channels were subdivided as “healthy cortices” for channels distant from the epileptogenic zone, with normal background activity deprived of any epileptiform activity, and “epileptic cortices” for channels located in the lesional zone with slowed background activity and interictal epileptiform abnormalities. Any doubtful channels that corresponded neither to “healthy cortex” nor to “epileptic cortex” were excluded. This reduced the number of available channels but ensured the robustness of our data. No recording included seizures. Raw EEG traces were segmented into single-channel files, and initial signals were cropped to a duration of 60 s to match data files from the MNI-Open dataset. The mean value of each segment and channel was subtracted from the corresponding segment and channel. Traces were segmented into 2-s epochs with no overlap (additional analyses were performed with 10- and 5-s epochs).

EEG quantitative analysis and markers computation

Only valid EEG epochs devoid of artifacts were used for quantitative analyses. Power spectrum densities (PSDs) were computed for the following EEG frequency bands (delta: 0.5–4 Hz, theta: 4–8 Hz, alpha: 8–12 Hz, beta: 12–30 Hz, gamma: 30–80 Hz) using Welch's method based on a fast Fourier transform. Each Welch segment was windowed using a 200-sample (1,000 ms) Hamming window. It should be noted that, while this is not the main focus of the presented work, analyses in the low delta band (i.e. 0.5–1 Hz) should be interpreted cautiously in relation to the Rayleigh criterion for frequency analysis. Normalized PSDs were then obtained by summing the raw power of a given frequency band and dividing it by the total power across all frequency bands.

We chose three markers to investigate different EEG complexity parameters: amplitude (Kolmogorov complexity), spectrum (spectral entropy), and time (permutation entropy) domains, adapted from the methods of the NICE-tools package (5). Kolmogorov complexity is often explained as the shortest algorithm capable of reproducing a given time series (e.g. an EEG signal), and it can be estimated in the following way: the signal's amplitude is segmented into a number of k bins. Here, k was set to 32 in accordance with existing studies (4, 11), but binning ranging from 8 to 128 usually yields similar results (4). Each value in the time series is attributed to its corresponding bin and assigned to an ASCII character. Once this symbolic transformation has been performed, the resulting string of ASCII characters is compressed. The Kolmogorov complexity value is obtained by dividing the length of the compressed string by that of the original, uncompressed string. This returns an array containing one value per epoch and per channel. This method considers that the bigger the size of the compressed string, the more complex the structure of the time series, and measures the degree of redundancy of the signal captured by a single EEG sensor.

Statistical analysis

Linear-mixed models were used to investigate the variation in marker values attributable to the participants themselves, since the number of available recordings varied between participants. We performed a linear-mixed model including the value of the complexity marker as the dependent variable, sleep stages as fixed effects, and participant number as a random effect. Models were computed using all marker values per epoch, and using the mean value of the marker across one recording, yielding similar results (Table S2). The “participant number” was negligible in explaining the variation in markers: estimated variance of 0.00 (SE: 0.001) for Kolmogorov complexity, 0.00 (SE: 0.001) for permutation entropy, and 0.036 (SE: 0.019) for spectral entropy; and a strong correlation was observed between marker values and sleep stages. Subsequent analyses used Wilcoxon rank sum tests for marker values across recordings due to the negligible participant effect. For tridimensional visualization of quantitative markers across sleep stages, the arithmetic mean of the marker was computed for each electrode across all epochs of the recording. For plots representing quantitative values of markers, the mean of each marker's means across all epochs of the recording was computed for each channel, the mean of all channels was then computed for each participant, and then, the global mean across participants was computed to reduce the bias stemming from the varying number of recordings per patient. For the figures presented in the current work, we selected permutation entropy in the theta-high delta band (τ = 8, see Ref. (4)) because it showed robustness in indexing consciousness in healthy patients and patients with disorders of consciousness (4, 11). Similar patterns were obtained for permutation entropy delta, alpha, beta, and gamma (τ = 16, 4, 2, and 1, respectively; see Source Data, https://github.com/linajeantin/iEEGcomplexity). All of the EEG preprocessing steps, quantitative markers computation, data visualization, statistical analysis, and supervised machine learning analyses were performed using Python 3.12.9, MNE-python 1.9.9 (19) and dedicated packages (including Numpy, SciPy, Pandas, Math, StatsModel, Matplotlib, Seaborn, and SickitLearn).

Results

Recordings

In both datasets, each recording corresponds to one channel in one patient in a specific sleep state. A total of 5,703 recordings from 1,759 channels in wake, N2, N3, and REM sleep were retrieved from the MNI-Open dataset. The recordings were performed on 106 participants with a median age of 33.5 (interquartile range [IQR] 25–40) years, including 48 (45.3%) females. In the BRD dataset, a total of 474 recordings were retrieved in wake, N2, N3, REM, and propofol anesthesia (see flowchart in Fig. S1). The recordings were performed in nine participants with a median age of 33.5 (IQR 25–41.5), including four (44.4%) females. Propofol data were available in five (55.5%) participants with a median age of 24 (IQR 24–41) years, including three (60%) women.

Power spectrum analysis

Normalized PSDs across frequency bands and sleep stages are shown in Fig. S2. Delta power increased consistently with N2 and N3 sleep compared with wakefulness. Beta power was prevalent in precentral regions during wakefulness.

The same pattern across sleep stages was found with different epoch lengths (Fig. S3). Figures S4–S6 represent the variation of marker values in the same electrode between two sleep stages. Similar results were found for permutation and spectral entropies (Figs. S5 and S6).

We explored spatial variability of complexity values by segmenting mean marker values by lobe (frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal, and insula) and cortical subregions (see list in Table S1). The highest values of markers of iEEG complexity were located in frontoparietal regions (Fig. 2), mostly marked during wake and REM sleep. During NREM sleep, these values tended to be more diffuse, despite a persisting frontal predominance.

EEG complexity shows higher variability in states of lower consciousness

Figure S7 shows the variance of iEEG complexity markers across 2-s epochs of the same recording. The highest variance was observed during states of lower consciousness (N2 and N3). In contrast, states exhibiting the highest complexity (i.e. wakefulness and REM) were the most stable in their complexity values, suggesting that iEEG complexity variability could index consciousness to some extent.

Validation dataset

Concordant observations were made on the BRD validation dataset (Fig. S8), including a decrease in iEEG complexity with NREM sleep compared with wakefulness and REM. Interestingly, the “propofol” condition exhibited the lowest level of iEEG complexity, even lower than N3 sleep, with high variability, further supporting that iEEG complexity markers can serve as indicators of consciousness levels.

A random forest classifier can discriminate sleep stages using markers of EEG complexity

We performed a supervised machine learning analysis to identify sleep stages using quantitative EEG data. We trained a random forest classifier on the MNI-Open dataset with a nested cross-validation (see Fig. S9 for procedure description). The model was trained to detect the target (sleep stage) using permutation entropy, spectral entropy, Kolmogorov complexity, and their SDs as features; each recording was a sample. The multiclass classification task during the training phase on the MNI-Open dataset showed good one-versus-rest areas under the curve (AUC) for each class and a micro-averaged one-versus-rest AUC of 0.90 (Fig. 4A and B). Finally, the best model obtained through hyperparameter optimization was trained using the entire MNI dataset and tested using the BRD dataset (with channels in healthy cortices during wakefulness, N2, N3, and REM only) to assess its robustness with “routine” data. The obtained AUCs were similar for wake, NREM sleep, and slightly lower for REM sleep (Fig. 4C and D).

Discussion

In this study, we present a comprehensive cartography of EEG signal complexity in the normal adult human brain across physiological states of consciousness. By analyzing a large dataset of iEEG recordings, we quantified signal complexity using complementary methods: Kolmogorov complexity, permutation entropy, and spectral entropy. Our three markers were chosen to capture complementary notions of “complexity”: (i) compression-based algorithmic complexity (our Kolmogorov complexity proxy using a symbolic transform based on the amplitude of the signal), (ii) ordinal/temporal complexity (permutation entropy), and (iii) spectral distribution complexity (spectral entropy). Compression-based measures are closely related to Lempel–Ziv complexity, which estimates signal diversity after binarization, while our approach uses a multisymbol discretization followed by compression. Both primarily reflect signal compressibility, but absolute values can differ depending on symbolization, sampling rate, epoch length, and preprocessing. Lempel–Ziv complexity has shown robust decreases in spontaneous EEG diversity during propofol anesthesia (26), consistent with the state-dependent modulations we observed. Permutation entropy quantifies the diversity of local vectors at a chosen time scale, providing a complementary view of temporal organization that can be adapted to specific frequency ranges (3). These measures offer convergent (yet nonredundant) perspectives on complexity.

Our main observation is that iEEG signal complexity decreases with deepening sleep, reinforcing the relevance of iEEG complexity as a sensitive index of conscious state, aligning with prior work in surface EEG (11) and studies performed in patients with disorders of consciousness (4, 5). The similarity in complexity profiles between wake and REM sleep further suggests the interpretation of REM sleep as a state of internally generated consciousness, despite behavioral unresponsiveness (7). An independent dataset confirmed these results and extended them to an additional condition—general anesthesia with propofol. Propofol-induced unconsciousness showed the lowest complexity values, supporting previous studies suggesting that deeply unconscious states are associated with a breakdown in the richness and differentiation of neural activity (26). It was also the state with the greatest variability in complexity that could emerge from the unpredictable bursts of iEEG fast activity interrupting phases of low-complexity neuronal signal suppression (27, 28).

This work provides reference values of iEEG signal complexity for healthy cortices in different states of consciousness. These normative values may serve as a framework for future intracranial studies exploring altered consciousness, particularly in epilepsy. We observed that complexity was more variable in NREM sleep than in wake and REM, in line with studies outlying that cortical activity is most stable during conscious perception (29, 30). iEEG complexity also differs across brain regions within the same state. During wake and REM, complexity values tended to be higher in association cortices, including frontoparietal areas, although high-complexity contacts remained widely distributed across cortical regions. These results are descriptive and show consistency with theories highlighting association cortices in conscious processing, such as the global neuronal workspace theoretical framework of consciousness (25, 31), where frontoparietal areas hold a central role in conscious access (32). However, our data do not provide a formal test of preferential enrichment within canonical regions of the Global Neuronal Workspace.

Zelmann et al. (13) demonstrated that iEEG perturbational complexity and effective connectivity decrease during unconscious states, with a marked disruption of prefrontal activity under propofol anesthesia compared with NREM sleep that might underlie nonarousable states of consciousness. While their approach focused on brain responsiveness to stimulation, our results extend these observations by showing that intrinsic resting-state iEEG complexity also decreases with loss of consciousness and show that higher resting-state complexity values are frequently observed during conscious states, including in several association areas. Interestingly, we found increased variability of spontaneous iEEG complexity in propofol and N3 sleep, echoing the heightened intertrial variability observed after stimulation in the unresponsive brain (13). Although a bandwidth-harmonized sensitivity analysis supported the main conclusions, absolute complexity/entropy values involving propofol should be interpreted with caution since recordings under propofol were additionally low-pass filtered at 40 Hz due to operating-room high-frequency noise. Together, these complementary approaches suggest that both spontaneous resting-state and evoked iEEG complexity track state-dependent alterations in neural information processing, reinforcing the notion of distinct mechanisms underlying arousable versus unarousable unconsciousness.

The BRD dataset provided insight into the epileptic cortex, revealing a pattern of reduced complexity compared with healthy cortex in wakefulness and REM, suggesting that complexity measures may help differentiate pathological cortex during presurgical evaluation. Interestingly, this difference was attenuated in lower consciousness states (N3 sleep and propofol), likely due to their uniformly low-complexity activity.

Further supporting this, a supervised machine learning analysis demonstrated that combinations of spectral and complexity-based iEEG markers accurately classified sleep stages. Even when limited to complexity features and their variability, models achieved high classification performance. Importantly, models generalized well when applied to the external BRD dataset, underscoring the translational robustness of these findings. Of note, the usual sleep scoring is made with scalp channels giving information on larger brain areas. Our model was able to robustly predict the sleep stage by considering iEEG complexity as sampled by a unique intracranial bipolar channel, which yields information from a very small cortical area. Moreover, our models did not include usual features for sleep-stage classification, such as detection of sleep-specific transients (spindles, K complexes) or electro-oculogram data, suggesting that their performances could even be improved. Further studies validating the utility of markers of complexity to determine sleep stages may prove high performance for automated sleep staging.

The strengths of this study include the large sample size (with a total of 6,177 recordings from 1,899 channels), the comprehensive coverage of cortical regions, the inclusion of physiological (wake/sleep) and pharmacological (propofol) conditions, and the comparison of epileptic and healthy cortices. The use of an independent, “real-world” validation dataset reinforces the ecological validity of our results. Our study is limited by the inability to determine, given the sample size for epileptic cortices, whether a random forest classifier could reliably discriminate between epileptic and healthy cortices based solely on complexity markers. Heterogeneity in electrode coverage across patients is an intrinsic limitation of human iEEG studies. A further limitation is the imbalance in sample sizes between the datasets, with the MNI-Open dataset being around 10 times larger than the BRD dataset. This may have increased the statistical power and stability of the normative estimates in the MNI dataset compared with the BRD dataset. Bipolar referencing emphasizes local gradients and can affect absolute complexity/entropy estimates by acting as a spatial filter. While montage was consistent within BRD, small differences in bipolar construction and electrode geometry between datasets may contribute to residual variability and warrant caution when interpreting absolute cross-dataset differences. Potential sex-related effects on iEEG complexity were not formally investigated to test sex-by-state or sex-by-region interactions: future work specifically designed for this question, ideally incorporating age and hormonal status, could assess whether iEEG complexity atlases require sex-stratified normative references. Due to the limited number of available recordings for the propofol condition, it was not possible to achieve thorough cortical coverage or draw conclusions about regional disparities in complexity during propofol anesthesia. Finally, both datasets were acquired in patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy, as is typical for human iEEG studies. Although channels were strictly selected to exclude epileptiform activity for the main analysis, and we observed lower complexity in epileptic versus healthy cortices in a dedicated analysis, subtle network-level epilepsy effects may persist and could contribute to residual variability—these results should therefore be interpreted as normative for “physiological/healthy-appearing” cortices rather than from neurologically healthy volunteers. These reference values are intended as normative benchmarks within intracranial recordings; they should not be interpreted as directly transferable numerical standards for scalp EEG, given the differences in spatial sampling, volume conduction, referencing, and signal-to-noise properties between modalities. Translation to scalp EEG is more appropriately possible in terms of expected state-dependent patterns (relative ordering across wake/NREM/REM and anesthesia).

In sum, this work outlines EEG complexity in the human brain and its relation to physiological states of consciousness. It provides a normative atlas of iEEG complexity in healthy adults that may serve as a foundation for future work in epilepsy, sleep, and disorders of consciousness.
