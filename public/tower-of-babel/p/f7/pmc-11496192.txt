Cytomegalovirus tracheobronchitis mimicking lung cancer progression in a patient with lung adenocarcinoma: A case report
Authors: Green Hong, Sung Joon Han, Kyung‐Hee Kim, Dongil Park, Chaeuk Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytomegalovirus (CMV) commonly infects immunocompromised individuals, such as cancer patients. We present a case involving a 60‐year‐old male with Stage 3A lung adenocarcinoma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) diagnosed with CMV tracheobronchitis, initially suspected as cancer progression. Treatment with ganciclovir led to partial improvement in symptoms of shortness of breath and cough, as well as bronchoscopic findings. However, due to ganciclovir‐induced neutropenia, the therapy was switched to foscarnet. Distinguishing between cancer progression and infectious tracheobronchitis through physical examination and chest CT scans remains challenging. In lung cancer patients presenting with airway and bronchial narrowing along with ulcerative mucosal lesions, CMV infection should be considered. A bronchoscopic biopsy is crucial for accurate diagnosis and determining the appropriate treatment in these patients.

INTRODUCTION

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a prevalent viral opportunistic infection in immunocompromised hosts, including cancer patients. 1 In healthy, immunocompetent individuals, primary CMV infection may be asymptomatic or present as a nonspecific febrile illness or an infectious mononucleosis‐like syndrome characterized by fever, lymphadenopathy, and lymphocytosis. Following a self‐limited course, CMV establishes latency in various cell types such as endothelial cells, epithelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts. From these reservoirs, the virus can be carried by peripheral monocytes and circulating endothelial cells to other body sites. 2 This initial infection triggers the production of CMV‐specific IgM, followed by IgG antibodies that persist for life. 3 In immunocompromised individuals, such as those with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, solid‐organ transplant (SOT) recipients, or hematopoietic stem‐cell transplant (HSCT) recipients, diminished CMV‐specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses can allow uncontrolled viral replication, leading to clinical disease. 4 The spectrum of CMV‐related diseases includes enterocolitis, pneumonitis, retinitis, hepatitis, encephalitis, nephritis, and disseminated disease. 5

CMV can also cause central airway disease, a relatively rare condition that can manifest in various forms including inflammatory polyps, 6 granulation tissue resembling tumor masses, deeply ulcerated lesions, and mucosal edema. 7 , 8 CMV tracheobronchitis, in particular, is a significant cause of central airway obstruction (CAO) in immunocompromised patients. 9

CAO is characterized by airflow limitation due to narrowing of the trachea and main bronchi, which can arise from various causes, including malignancies, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and infections. 10 Distinguishing between cancer progression and infectious causes of CAO is a major clinical challenge. Both conditions may present with similar symptoms of airway obstruction and abnormalities on chest CT scans. Accurate diagnosis is essential to guide appropriate treatment and improve patient outcomes. 11

In this case report, we discuss CMV tracheobronchitis that mimicked cancer progression and the subsequent clinical course.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 60‐year‐old male presented with hemoptysis, and a chest CT scan revealed a cavitary mass in the right lower lobe (Figure 1a,b). Histological examination and staging confirmed adenocarcinoma, classified as T2bN2M0, Stage 3A. Genetic profiling showed wild‐type EGFR, negative ALK rearrangement, and positive ROS1 status. Next‐generation sequencing identified a CD74‐ROS1 fusion, along with mutations in ZFHX3 and TP53. PD‐L1 expression was noted to be high.

Chest CT images of the patient at different stages of diagnosis and treatment. (a,b) Initial chest CT images at the time of lung cancer diagnosis displaying an intact carina and both main bronchi. (c,d) Chest CT images taken prior to cytomegalovirus (CMV) treatment showing a mass‐like lesion at the carina and significantly thickened main bronchi. (e,f) Follow‐up chest CT images post‐CMV treatment demonstrating partial improvement in the condition of the carina and main bronchi.

A pulmonary function test indicated airway obstruction, with results showing FEV1 (Forced Expiratory Volume in one second)/FVC (Forced Vital Capacity) at 67%, FEV1 at 2.06 L (73%), and FVC at 3.05 L (77%), classifying the patient into COPD GOLD group B. This led to the initiation of treatment with inhaled bronchodilators for COPD management. He underwent cisplatin and paclitaxel‐based concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT). After CCRT, he developed dyspnea and a chest CT scan showed radiation pneumonitis, which was treated with systemic steroids, specifically prednisolone, starting at 20 mg for 2 weeks, followed by 10 mg for an additional 3 weeks.

A follow‐up CT scan 2 months later revealed significant metastasis in both adrenal glands and the liver. The patient was treated with crizotinib for 6 weeks, but subsequent CT scans showed new pulmonary nodules and worsened adrenal gland metastasis, now measuring 10 cm, accompanied by abdominal pain.

The treatment was revised to include four cycles of cisplatin and pemetrexed, with radiation therapy targeting the adrenal and liver metastatic lesions. Follow‐up CT scans demonstrated a marked reduction in the size of the adrenal gland metastases, while lung lesions remained stable. Recently, steroid inhaler (Beclomethasone) was added to the treatment regimen 2 months ago for COPD management.

After the first cycle of pemetrexed maintenance treatment, the patient developed dyspnea with inspiratory and expiratory wheezing. At that time, the patient's pulmonary function test (PFT) showed FEV1/FVC at 66%, FEV1 at 1.71 L (62%), and FVC at 2.59 L (67%), indicating a significant decline compared to previous results and converted to GOLD group E. Despite treatment with systemic steroids (methylprednisolone 40 mg for 1 week), bronchodilator nebulizers, and antibiotics for COPD exacerbation and bronchitis, his dyspnea and a persistent refractory cough continued. A chest CT scan showed improvement in the previous radiation pneumonitis lesions, but the primary cancer remained largely unchanged. However, infiltrative lesions potentially indicative of invasion were noted around the carina and both main bronchi (Figure 1c,d). A bronchoscopy was performed to assess the endobronchial lesions, revealing foul‐smelling necrotic tissue obstructing the carina and main bronchi, suggestive of tumor invasion or severe viral tracheobronchitis (Figure 2a,b).

Bronchoscopy images of the patient before and after cytomegalovirus (CMV) treatment. (a,b) Images at the diagnosis of CMV tracheobronchitis, showing necrotic tissue obstructing the carina and main bronchi, accompanied by thick mucus. An arrow points to the carina. (c,d) Images after treatment, illustrating partial clearance of the necrotic tissue and some reduction in the obstruction of the main bronchi. The arrow points to the carina.

Bronchoscopic biopsy from multiple sites, including within the ulcerative lesions, revealed atypical cells, necrosis with chronic inflammation, and neutrophilic exudate, but no cancer cells. Notably, several infected vascular endothelial cells were significantly enlarged and displayed prominent basophilic nuclear inclusions (Figure 3a). An immunohistochemical stain for cytomegalovirus identified scattered CMV‐positive endothelial cells, confirming a diagnosis of CMV tracheobronchitis (Figure 3b). Grocott's methenamine silver (GMS) and periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) staining were negative for fungal organisms. The patient's HIV test was negative, and the CMV PCR titer was <137 IU/mL. Complete blood cell count was nearly normal.

Histopathological images of the biopsy samples taken from the trachea and bronchi before and after cytomegalovirus (CMV) treatment. (a) H&E stained image before CMV treatment shows infected vascular endothelial cells that are enlarged with a prominent basophilic nuclear inclusion (x400). An arrow indicates the infected cells. (b) Immunohistochemical staining for CMV prior to treatment shows several CMV‐positive endothelial cells (x400). (c) H&E stained image post‐CMV treatment with no infected cells visible. (d) Immunohistochemical staining for CMV after treatment shows no CMV‐positive cells.

After being diagnosed with CMV tracheobronchitis, the patient discontinued inhaled corticosteroid treatment and began receiving ganciclovir at a dose of 5 mg/kg every 12 h. This led to a gradual improvement in symptoms, including dyspnea and cough. A follow‐up bronchoscopy 2 weeks later showed partial improvement in luminal narrowing around the carina and main bronchus (Figure 2c,d), and subsequent bronchoscopic biopsy revealed no tumor cells, with CMV staining now negative (Figure 3c,d).

By the third week of ganciclovir treatment, the patient's neutrophil count had dropped to 780/μL, indicating an adverse effect of the medication. Ganciclovir was discontinued, and a transition to foscarnet, an alternative antiviral agent with a reduced risk of bone marrow suppression, was considered. Neutrophil count recovery was observed 3 days after discontinuing ganciclovir. However, the patient tragically succumbed to sudden massive hemoptysis a few days after starting treatment with foscarnet.

DISCUSSION

This case describes a patient who presented with dyspnea and was diagnosed with CMV tracheobronchitis, which mimicked the invasion of lung cancer around the carina and both main bronchi. Differentiating between cancer progression and infectious tracheobronchitis based solely on clinical symptoms is challenging, especially in patients with underlying respiratory diseases. This case underscores the difficulty in distinguishing between cancer progression and infectious complications in patients with preexisting malignancies. Both conditions can present with similar symptoms such as dyspnea, hemoptysis, and airway obstruction, as well as overlapping radiologic findings. Initially, the presence of infiltrative lesions around the carina and main bronchi on the CT scan raised concerns for tumor invasion. However, the subsequent bronchoscopic biopsy revealing necrotic tissue and viral inclusion bodies indicative of CMV tracheobronchitis was pivotal in guiding the correct diagnosis. Accurate diagnosis is essential for appropriate treatment, as the management strategies for malignancies and infections differ significantly. This case highlights the importance of considering opportunistic infections, like CMV, in the differential diagnosis for immunocompromised patients, particularly when clinical and radiologic findings are atypical or refractory to standard cancer treatments.

CMV is a significant pathogen in immunocompromised patients, capable of reactivating and causing severe disease. This patient had several risk factors for CMV reactivation, including a history of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and systemic steroid use, which contribute to immunosuppression. Additionally, the patient's underlying COPD further complicated the clinical picture, as the symptoms of CMV tracheobronchitis and COPD exacerbation can overlap, complicating the diagnosis and management.

The immunosuppressed state of the patient likely facilitated CMV reactivation, leading to a severe manifestation of tracheobronchitis with airway obstruction. This emphasizes the need for vigilance in monitoring for CMV reactivation and other opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients. CMV typically causes asymptomatic latent infections, with reactivation occurring during periods of immunosuppression, such as during corticosteroid treatment, leading to a spectrum of diseases. CMV infections are primarily observed in transplant recipients 12 and immunocompromised individuals, such as those with HIV. 13 However, CMV infections have also been reported in critically ill patients, such as those with acute respiratory distress syndrome, 14 and in patients using immunosuppressive agents like steroids. 15 , 16 In this case, although the patient was not neutropenic, the ongoing chemotherapy and steroid treatment likely contributed to an immunocompromised state, which may have led to severe CMV tracheobronchitis.

In our patient, the CMV DNA PCR showed a low titer value of less than 137 IU/mL, yet it tested positive. This test was conducted on a blood sample, indicating that the viral titer in the blood was minimal. However, it is speculated that the virus specifically affected an organ, in this case, the tracheobronchus. There have been numerous reports of CMV disease being identified even with low CMV titers. 17 This indicates that the severity of CMV disease cannot be determined solely based on the viral PCR titer value. It underscores the importance of conducting thorough examinations of specific organs, such as tissue biopsies, where the disease is suspected.

Moreover, the patient suddenly passed away within a few hours due to massive hemoptysis. It is unclear whether this was due to the healing process of an ulcer or the progression of cancer. Nonetheless, it can be speculated that the ulcerative lesion, which was healing, might have deteriorated due to the temporary discontinuation of neutropenia induced by Ganciclovir. Additionally, the possibility of cancer progression seems relatively low, as no signs of lesion progression were observed on the chest CT taken 2 weeks prior to death.

It is crucial to remain vigilant when ulcerative lesions are present in the main bronchi, as they can lead to significant hemoptysis. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of CMV tracheobronchitis in a lung cancer patient where both bronchoscopic examination and biopsy were conducted before and after treatment.

CONCLUSION

CMV infection typically occurs in immunocompromised patients. In this case, factors such as underlying lung cancer, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and corticosteroid treatment contributed to the weakened immune state, leading to severe CMV tracheobronchitis. Bronchoscopic findings of severe airway narrowing, ulceration, and mucosal invasion might suggest cancer progression, but they could also result from infections such as CMV or herpes virus. Therefore, performing a bronchoscopic biopsy is crucial for an accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment.
