Patient-reported dyspnoea predicts six-fold increased hospital mortality
Authors: Jennifer P. Stevens, Richard M. Schwartzstein, Andrew R. Sheridan, Carl R. O'Donnell, Kathy M. Baker, Robert B. Banzett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients can report respiratory discomfort (dyspnoea) on a 0–10 scale. Patients reporting dyspnoea at admission have a four-fold risk of in-hospital death. We asked whether assessing dyspnoea throughout hospitalisation identifies additional patients at risk of death or other poor outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of non-intensive care unit patients at a tertiary care hospital. On each shift, bedside nurses documented patients’ pain and dyspnoea ratings. We tested associations with inpatient mortality, 2-year mortality and other outcomes.ResultsWe evaluated 9785 admissions; 18% of patients reported dyspnoea at admission and 10% developed post-admission dyspnoea. Patients with post-admission-onset dyspnoea had six-fold greater odds of death during hospitalisation (OR 6.0, 95% CI 4.2–8.5, p<0.0001) versus patients without dyspnoea. Compared with those without dyspnoea, patients with dyspnoea had 50% greater mortality during the following 2 years, and those with dyspnoea on the day of discharge had even greater mortality (HR 2.6, 95% CI 2.1–3.2, p<0.0001). Higher patient dyspnoea ratings predicted higher in-hospital and 2-year mortality. Pain (reported by 72% of patients) was not significantly associated with mortality. Dyspnoea was also related to greater length of stay, rapid response team activation, transfer to the intensive care unit and discharge to extended care.ConclusionsIn contrast to pain, both admission dyspnoea and post-admission-onset dyspnoea were associated with substantially increased odds of poor patient outcomes during hospitalisation and following discharge. Documenting dyspnoea throughout hospitalisation provides a powerful alarm of clinical compromise that may warrant additional attention and outpatient follow-up.

Introduction

Dyspnoea, respiratory discomfort perceived by the person experiencing it, is similar to pain in that assessment relies on patient report [1]. Dyspnoea is highly distressing and predicts adverse outcomes in several patient populations [2–5]. Dyspnoea at the time of admission is prevalent among inpatients at our hospital [6] and predicts a substantially increased risk of adverse outcomes [7]. As noted by Decavèle and Similowski [8], documentation of patient-reported dyspnoea “opens the possibility of individualised diagnostic and therapeutic actions”.

Periodic assessment of dyspnoea and pain requires <2 min of nursing time per patient [9] and is necessary for symptom management and to identify potential onset of medical problems. Here we examine whether documentation of dyspnoea throughout the hospital stay identifies additional patients at risk for adverse outcomes. We describe the incidence of new dyspnoea and pain developing after hospital admission (“post-admission-onset dyspnoea/pain”) and examine their association to death and other adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study population and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 9785 consecutive patients aged ≥18 years admitted to our 651-bed tertiary care facility between March 2014 and September 2016. Records were accessed between March 2014 and March 2017. Patient characteristics are described in the Results and in several supplementary tables. Nurses on non-intensive care unit (non-ICU) wards are required to obtain patient ratings of dyspnoea and pain throughout hospitalisation [9]. We sampled consecutive patients admitted to 14 units that were selected to encompass the wide range of patients seen at our hospital (supplementary table S1). The sampled units included general medicine, oncology/transplant, cardiology, bariatric surgery, gynaecology and orthopaedics. At the time of the study, nurses collected admission data using an electronic system, but nursing notes and vital signs during the hospital stay were hand-written and later scanned as images for storage. We manually transcribed the data from scanned records to digital spreadsheets. The present sample of dyspnoea reported by patients throughout the hospital stay comprises a subset of patients from our earlier study of dyspnoea at the time of admission, which used the electronic records of 67 362 patients [7]. Our study was approved by the institutional review board at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, with a waiver of informed consent.

Study variables

Assessment of dyspnoea throughout the hospitalisation

At least once per shift (typically 12 h), nurses asked all patients to report their current breathing discomfort while at rest using a 0–10 scale, where 10 is “unbearable” and the intermediate cues “mild”, “moderate” and “severe” were placed according to previously published word–number scaling data (see figure 1 in reference [9]). Patients were ordinarily seated or recumbent. Patients reported pain on a similar 0–10 scale, where 10 was anchored by “worst pain” and the intermediate cues “mild”, “moderate” and “severe” were spaced evenly across the scale.

Prevalence of dyspnoea among patients at various stages of hospitalisation (n=9785). Patients with “no dyspnoea ever” reported a dyspnoea rating of 0 throughout hospitalisation. Patients with “dyspnoea on admission” reported a dyspnoea rating of ≥1 during the first shift of admission to the unit; about 77% had been seen in the emergency department before unit admission. Patients with “post-admission-onset dyspnoea” were defined as having rated dyspnoea as 0 on the day of admission but subsequently having a reported dyspnoea rating of ≥1 at least once during their stay. Patients with “discharge dyspnoea” were defined as having rated dyspnoea as ≥1 on the day of discharge. The first three bars represent mutually exclusive dyspnoea groups and add up to 100%. The fourth bar represents patients with dyspnoea at discharge, which includes some patients with dyspnoea on admission and some with post-admission-onset dyspnoea (i.e. overlapping groups).

We transcribed every dyspnoea rating along with the concurrent pain rating. Heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate and oxygen saturation values closest in time to any dyspnoea rating were also recorded. We evaluated transcription error rate and found 93% concordance of dyspnoea assessments (supplementary figure S1).

We categorised patients into four “timing groups”: 1) patients with “no dyspnoea” (rating of 0 throughout their stay), 2) patients with “admission dyspnoea” (dyspnoea rating >0 on the calendar day of admission), 3) patients with “post-admission-onset dyspnoea” (dyspnoea rating of 0 on day of admission, with a subsequent dyspnoea rating >0 at least once during the stay), and 4) patients with “discharge dyspnoea” (dyspnoea rating >0 on the day of discharge). Group 4 overlaps with groups 2 and 3.

The analysis of pain paralleled that of dyspnoea.

Outcome assessment

Mortality and readmissions

The primary outcome was mortality during the same hospital stay. Secondary outcomes of interest included mortality at 2 years and all-cause readmission to our institution at 7 and 30 days (restricted to patients surviving to discharge).

Inpatient resource use

Additional secondary outcomes included markers of hospital resource use, including length of stay of ≥7 days, activation of the rapid response team following new-onset dyspnoea or pain, and transfer to the ICU following new-onset dyspnoea or pain (see supplementary table S2 for events that triggered rapid response). We also ascertained whether a patient was discharged to extended care.

Statistical analysis

Dyspnoea prevalence and time course

We first explored prevalence of dyspnoea on admission and then over the course of hospitalisation. We performed descriptive analyses using bivariate correlations between the four dyspnoea/pain groups (no dyspnoea/pain, admission dyspnoea/pain, post-admission-onset dyspnoea/pain and discharge dyspnoea/pain) and patients' demographic and clinical characteristics. We tested for overall group differences using Chi-square and Bonferroni pairwise comparisons between patients with admission dyspnoea/pain and those with post-admission-onset dyspnoea/pain.

Association of dyspnoea with outcomes

The principal null hypothesis tested was as follows: patients who report post-admission-onset dyspnoea have no greater risk of adverse outcomes compared with patients who never report dyspnoea during their hospital stay. We tested this hypothesis for hospital mortality, mortality following hospitalisation and several other adverse outcomes. We also compared risk associated with post-admission-onset dyspnoea with that of admission dyspnoea. We tested parallel hypotheses for patient-reported pain. We also tested the null hypothesis that the level of patient-reported dyspnoea was not important in predicting risk.

To explore whether patients with post-admission-onset dyspnoea are at increased risk, we fit generalised linear models with distributions and link functions appropriate to each outcome. For binary outcomes (in-hospital mortality, length of stay ≥7 days, activation of any medical emergency team, transfer to the ICU, readmissions at 7 and 30 days and discharge to extended care), we used binomial distributions with logit links. In these models, we clustered residuals at the patient level and adjusted for patient characteristics. For discharge dyspnoea, which required the patient to be alive on discharge, we evaluated only its associations with readmissions among our binary outcomes. We also looked at how pain was related to the same outcomes. For 2-year mortality, we used Kaplan–Meier analyses to estimate if there were any significant differences in hazard ratio between the patients in our four categories of dyspnoea. Missing values were handled using a complete case analysis. All statistical tests were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A two-sided type I error of ≤0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance for all comparisons.

Adjunct analyses

We also performed the following adjunct analyses, which are included in the supplementary materials. First, we tested the hypothesis that patient preference for “do not resuscitate” (DNR) status on admission confounded our findings on in-hospital mortality, performing a stratified analysis by code status with mortality as the outcome. Second, as clinicians often associate dyspnoea with hypoxia, we explored the relationship between dyspnoea and oxygen saturation both using Spearman correlation and clustered analysis. Finally, we examined the subgroups of patients with respiratory diagnoses and with cardiac diagnoses.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

We studied a total of 9785 admissions; 77% were admitted after arriving at the emergency department. Supplementary table S3 shows the principal patient characteristics of the entire cohort and of subgroups categorised by dyspnoea onset timing. Supplementary table S4 shows the characteristics of patients grouped into pain onset categories. We also examined post-hospital mortality associated with presence of dyspnoea or pain on the day of discharge. Supplementary tables S5 and S6 show the characteristics of patients with dyspnoea or pain at discharge.

Describing the prevalence, incidence and risk association of dyspnoea was the primary objective, and is thus presented in the main Results. Pain forms a useful comparison, as is explored in the Discussion. The results regarding pain are summarised below and presented in full in the supplementary material.

Dyspnoea prevalence and incidence

Admission dyspnoea

In our cohort, 1739 patients (18%) rated dyspnoea as ≥1 out of 10 at the time of admission to the unit (figure 1). Prevalence of dyspnoea on admission was somewhat higher than in the larger admission cohort, probably because some units with low dyspnoea prevalence, such as obstetrics, were omitted from the present cohort.

Post-admission-onset dyspnoea

Of the 8046 patients who reported no dyspnoea on admission, 926 patients reported post-admission-onset dyspnoea; therefore, 27% of the total sample reported dyspnoea at some point during hospitalisation. On average, patients had improvement in dyspnoea ratings over the course of hospitalisation (figure 2). Most patients experienced only mild dyspnoea, but about 5% rated their dyspnoea as ≥4 at some point during the stay (figure 3).

Day 1 is the day the patient was admitted to the unit. As expected, average dyspnoea declined as treatment proceeded, although this was not true for all patients. We verified that this subgroup was representative: the mean dyspnoea ratings during the first 3 days were within 2% of the scale compared with the larger subgroup of 7138 patients hospitalised for ≥3 days.

About a quarter of patients experienced dyspnoea during hospitalisation, which in most cases was relieved before discharge.

Discharge dyspnoea

Of our patients, 3.8% reported dyspnoea on the day of discharge (figure 1), and these patients were more likely to have severe dyspnoea (figure 3).

Dyspnoea was associated with greater in-hospital and post-discharge mortality

The baseline risk of in-hospital death for patients reporting no dyspnoea at any time was 1.0%. Compared with baseline, patients reporting admission dyspnoea had a three-fold risk of dying during the current hospitalisation (figure 4).

Patients reporting post-admission-onset dyspnoea were at even greater risk than those reporting admission dyspnoea: 5.9% mortality for post-admission-onset dyspnoea versus 3.3% for admission dyspnoea (p<0.0001) (figure 4).

Mortality risk during the current hospitalisation was also related to the maximum patient-reported dyspnoea rating recorded during the hospital stay. Patients with mild dyspnoea had three times the in-hospital death rate, while those with moderate to severe dyspnoea were six times as likely to die (figure 5).

Mortality in the 2 years following discharge

Compared with the no-dyspnoea group, patients who reported dyspnoea during hospitalisation had a greater risk of dying in the 2 years following discharge compared with patients reporting no dyspnoea (figure 6). For admission dyspnoea, the mortality hazard ratio was 1.5 (95% CI 1.3–1.8), and for post-admission-onset dyspnoea, the hazard ratio was 1.7 (95% CI 1.5–2.0). Patients with dyspnoea on the day of discharge had an even greater risk of death within 2 years (hazard ratio 2.6, 95% CI 2.1–3.2).

Mortality in the 2.5 years following hospitalisation, determined using the Social Security Death Index. Both the group of patients who rated dyspnoea ≥1 (out of 10) at the time of admission and the group of patients who reported dyspnoea onset post-admission had about 50% greater risk of death after 2.5 years. Patients who still reported a dyspnoea rating >0 on the day of discharge had more than twice the risk of 2-year mortality compared with patients with no dyspnoea (hazard ratio 2.8, 95% CI 2.1–3.9). For statistical testing, discharge dyspnoea ratings were given priority to avoid double counting. Therefore, patients with both discharge and admission dyspnoea, or patients with both discharge and post-admission-onset dyspnoea, were counted only in the discharge dyspnoea category.

Compared with patients with no dyspnoea, patients with dyspnoea on discharge had a higher comorbidity score (2.5 versus 1.2, p<0.001) and were much more likely to have respiratory diagnoses at discharge (31.7% versus 3.7%, p<0.001) (see supplementary table S5 for further information).

Other adverse outcomes associated with dyspnoea

Admission dyspnoea and other adverse outcomes

Compared with patients reporting no dyspnoea, those with admission dyspnoea were twice as likely to require a rapid response team and transfer to the ICU, spent somewhat longer in hospital, and were more likely to require extended care after discharge (figure 8). Admission dyspnoea predicted readmission in cardiac patients (n=2140), but not in respiratory patients (n=804) or in the whole cohort (n=9785) (supplementary figures S2 and S3).

Post-admission-onset dyspnoea and other adverse outcomes

Patients who reported post-admission-onset dyspnoea were at an even greater risk of other poor outcomes: they were twice as likely to get transferred to the ICU (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.6–2.5; adjusted OR (aOR) 1.9, 95% CI 1.6–2.4), twice as likely to require activation of the rapid response team (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.6–2.3; aOR 1.8, 95% CI 1.4–2.2), were three times more likely to stay in the hospital for ≥7 days (OR 2.8, 95% CI 2.3–3.4; aOR 2.7, 95% CI 2.3–3.3) and twice as likely to need extended care on discharge (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.5–2.1; aOR 1.8, 95% CI 1.5–2.2) (figure 8).

Discharge dyspnoea and other adverse outcomes

There was no significant difference in 7-day or 30-day readmission for patients with dyspnoea on discharge.

Secondary analyses

Additional subgroup analyses are described in the supplementary material and are briefly summarised here.

Dyspnoea and oxygen saturation

We found no significant relationship between oxygen saturation and dyspnoea rating (see supplement figure S4). This is consistent with known physiology, as explained in the legend of figure S4.

Effect of DNR orders

Because about a tenth of our cohort had DNR orders on admission, we were concerned that DNR orders could affect our primary finding, i.e. the relationship between dyspnoea and mortality. Although the presence of DNR orders increased overall mortality in all dyspnoea groups, it did not affect our main finding: in the non-DNR group, admission dyspnoea predicted three-fold mortality, and post-admission-onset dyspnoea predicted six-fold mortality (supplementary figure S5).

Comparative analysis: patient-reported pain

Pain is routinely measured and is presented here as a comparison. Because it was not our primary focus, we briefly summarise the pain results here and present full pain data in the supplementary material. Patient characteristics related to pain are shown in supplementary tables S4 and S6.

Pain prevalence and incidence

Pain was nearly three times as common as dyspnoea in this cohort; nearly half the patients reported non-zero pain on the day of admission, and about a quarter developed pain during their hospital stay. Only a quarter of patients remained pain-free throughout their hospital stay (figure S6).

Pain was not significantly associated with greater in-hospital or post-discharge mortality

There was a weak relationship between pain and in-hospital mortality that did not reach statistical significance (figure S7). There was no discernible relationship between pain during the hospital stay and 2-year mortality (figure S8).

Other adverse outcomes associated with pain

Pain was significantly associated with several other adverse outcomes, including transfer to ICU, the need for rapid response, length of stay, need for extended care and 30-day readmission (figure S9).

Discussion

As in our previous studies [7, 10], we found that patients admitted with patient-reported dyspnoea are at greater risk of adverse outcomes and death. A new finding in the present study is that documenting dyspnoea at each shift identifies additional patients at even greater risk. Pain was not strongly associated with increased risk of death or resource use, consistent with our previous work [7]. To date, there is limited literature on the inpatient resource use of patients with pain [11].

Several epidemiological studies have shown that dyspnoea on exertion is associated with long-term mortality and hospitalisation (5–26 years) [12]. These studies have almost always used an indirect measure of dyspnoea, i.e. the level of physical activity limited by dyspnoea, usually assessed using some form of the Medical Research Council Breathlessness Questionnaire [13]. Other studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of dyspnoea in outpatients with specific diagnoses, such as COPD, cardiovascular disease and oesophageal cancer [4, 5, 14, 15]. Few studies have used a direct assessment of current dyspnoea. In the most relevant study, dyspnoea ratings obtained in the emergency department strongly predicted admission to hospital rather than discharge home [16, 17].

It may be surprising that dyspnoea, a “subjective” measure with implied variation (“noise”) in perception and reporting, should predict mortality as robustly as indexes of “objective” data [7]. The perception of dyspnoea is an integration of information from many interoceptors monitoring the cardiopulmonary gas exchange mechanisms of the body; we posit that this richness of information compensates for the “noise” in patient reports. Why was pain not equally associated with mortality? Pain is often a symptom of localised injury to skin, muscle or bone, while dyspnoea typically warns of existential threat involving the cardiorespiratory systems. The clinical pain information available to us did not distinguish between somatic and visceral pain; the relationship might be different had we been able to restrict our analysis to visceral pain.

This study supports the universal documentation of patient-reported dyspnoea at each nursing shift. We identified a substantial number of additional patients who were at risk of death: those with dyspnoea that developed during their stay and those whose dyspnoea persisted at discharge. Nurses in our hospital reported that dyspnoea documentation does not increase their workload and may even improve workflow. Assessing and documenting dyspnoea takes <1 min [9]. Patient-reported dyspnoea ratings present an opportunity to identify patients who are experiencing a distressing symptom and who are at increased risk of adverse outcomes.

Patients who reported dyspnoea on the day of discharge had substantially increased risk of death during the 2 years following hospitalisation. Most deaths among patients with discharge dyspnoea occurred within the first 150 days. Our results are similar to those of DiNino and colleagues [18] who found increased risk of adverse outcomes among a restricted cohort of patients with congestive heart failure who reported dyspnoea on discharge. Our findings raise a caution for discharging providers: patients who report dyspnoea may be at risk of adverse outcomes.

Limitations

Several issues may have affected our results. First, this study was conducted at a single tertiary academic health centre, and its generalisability to smaller institutions has not yet been tested. Second, real-world measurement of dyspnoea and pain depends on how clinicians ask about a patient's symptoms; the associations between dyspnoea and adverse outcomes might be even stronger with more consistent dyspnoea assessments (e.g. carefully defined queries and electronically collected rating scales). Third, the association between dyspnoea and clinician-decided outcomes (summoning rapid response team and transfer to ICU) may be confounded because dyspnoea may influence the clinician's decision. The primary outcome, mortality, is not subject to this last caveat.

Dyspnoea ratings are not documented on arrival in our emergency department. Documentation of dyspnoea ratings on arrival at the emergency department might provide additional information regarding outcomes.

Application to practice

These data suggest that routine dyspnoea assessment throughout hospitalisation not only provides guidance for symptom management but also alerts staff to patients at greater risk of adverse events. Nurses at our hospital held highly favourable opinions regarding the addition of routine dyspnoea documentation [9]. A number of actions might be taken, for example, explicitly including dyspnoea as a trigger for the rapid response team, as a criterion for additional nursing checks or telemetry, and as an indicator for discharge planning and goals-of-care discussions.

Conclusion

Dyspnoea is easy to measure [9], prevalent, distressing [2, 3] and predictive of subsequent adverse outcomes. Mortality was associated with both the level of dyspnoea rated by the patient and the timing of its onset.

Ongoing periodic dyspnoea assessment is needed both for adequate symptom management and to predict risk. The method and timing of symptom measurement affect the value of the data. Although some patients will volunteer symptom reports, many will not. Even when asked a yes or no question about dyspnoea or pain, an appreciable number of patients will fail to reveal their discomfort [19]. To properly detect and document dyspnoea, it is best to use a scale with sufficient resolution, such as our 0–10 scale or a visual analogue scale [20], which requires <1 min in awake patients [9]. Assessment at admission provides the earliest possible indication of elevated risk, but ongoing assessment during the patient's stay is necessary to identify additional patients at even greater risk during and after hospitalisation. We propose routine ongoing surveillance of dyspnoea throughout a patient's inpatient stay to both improve symptom management and to identify patients at higher risk of clinical decline.
