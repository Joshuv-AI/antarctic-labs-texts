Driving Mechanisms and Future Trends of Migraine and Tension-Type Headache Burden in G20 Countries: A Comparative Analysis Utilizing Decomposition Analysis and Explainable Machine Learning
Authors: Zhuochen Chen, Guifen Fan, Kai Dai, Xueting Liu, Xu Cheng, Jianhong Ye, Jun Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMigraine and tension-type headache (TTH) are leading causes of neurological disability globally. While their macro-trends are documented, the nonlinear drivers and the regulatory role of socio-economic development across G20 countries remain under-explored.MethodsBased on Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 data, the headache burden across G20 members was quantified. Developmental trajectories were identified by integrating the Socio-demographic Index (SDI). Crucially, a comparative analysis was conducted between a decomposition analysis model and an explainable machine learning framework, Shapley Additive Explanations-Extreme Gradient Boosting (SHAP-XGBoost), to quantify nonlinear marginal contributions and complex feature interactions. Finally, future trends from 2024 to 2035 were projected utilizing Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models.ResultsIn 2023, the number of prevalent cases for migraine and TTH across G20 countries was estimated at 705 million and 1.27 billion, respectively. A significant nonlinear correlation between headache burden and the SDI was identified, with the burden for both disorders peaking at an SDI of approximately 0.8 before plateauing or declining. While population growth was identified as the primary macro-driver in both decomposition and SHAP analyses, a “saturation effect” within the headache models was revealed by SHAP analysis. Specifically, the marginal contribution of population size was found to diminish once specific thresholds were exceeded. Furthermore, according to ARIMA model projections, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of migraine is expected to stabilize in the coming years, whereas the ASIR of TTH is forecast to maintain a continuous upward trajectory through 2035.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive re-evaluation of migraine and TTH burden across G20 countries, shifting the focus from descriptive status quo to mechanistic explanation. Our findings confirm that while population growth remains a key contributor, variations in burden appear to be associated with differences in development level, age structure, and population scale.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40122-026-00859-2.

Introduction

Primary headache disorders—encompassing migraine and tension-type headache (TTH)—are among the most prevalent and burdensome neurological conditions globally, collectively driving substantial disability, healthcare expenditure, and societal productivity loss [1, 2]. Migraine, characterized by recurrent moderate-to-severe unilateral headache with photophobia, phonophobia, or nausea, ranks as the second leading cause of disability worldwide and the top cause of disability in women aged 15–49 years [3, 4]. In contrast, TTH presents as mild-to-moderate bilateral “band-like” pain, with a far higher global prevalence, but lower individual disability impact, yet its sheer caseload translates to considerable population-level burden [5, 6]. Together, these two disorders account for over 50% of primary headache cases, making them a critical focus for public health action [7].

The global burden of migraine and TTH is well-documented, but their impact is disproportionately concentrated in the G20—a bloc of 19 countries and the European Union representing over 80% of global gross domestic product (GDP) and two-thirds of the world’s population [8]. Over the past three decades, these nations have undergone a profound transformation in their disease landscapes, driven by the complex interplay of multifaceted factors.

According to our knowledge, research on comparative headache burden across G20 countries remains limited [9]. In addition, several important knowledge gaps persist in the existing literature. First, many existing studies rely on traditional linear decomposition analysis to identify drivers of disease burden. While effective at a macro level, these methods have important limitations in capturing the complex interaction effects between variables [10, 11]. For instance, the impact of population aging on headache burden may not be uniformly proportional across different population scales, and the coupling between gender, age, and socioeconomic status has received limited quantification [12]. Furthermore, the nonlinear relationships between these drivers and headache burden often remain underexplored in traditional epidemiological approaches. The escalation of disease burden frequently exhibits “threshold effects” or “saturation phenomena” that traditional parametric models may not adequately capture [13], which could lead to policy projections that potentially oversimplify future health needs.

To address these gaps, this study leverages the latest Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 (GBD 2023) data to provide a comprehensive, comparative analysis of migraine and TTH burden across G20 countries. Our objectives are to: (1) quantify the 2023 burden using absolute counts and age-standardized metrics to establish a baseline for both disorders; (2) evaluate the correlation between headache burden and the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) to identify nonlinear developmental trajectories; (3) delineate the drivers of burden change via decomposition analysis, partitioning total variation into population growth, aging, and epidemiological shifts at a macro level; (4) implement an explainable machine learning framework, Shapley Additive Explanations-Extreme Gradient Boosting (SHAP-XGBoost), to contrast with traditional decomposition, specifically quantifying the nonlinear marginal contributions of age, sex, and population size to the disease burden; (5) project 2024–2035 trends using Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models to provide evidence-based forecasting for future public health planning.

Methods

Overview

This study used data from the GBD 2023, published by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), University of Washington. The GBD database is widely regarded as the global benchmark for disease burden estimation. It integrates 292 causes of death, 375 diseases and injuries, and 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories, drawing on multiple sources such as vital registration systems, population censuses, epidemiological surveys, and hospital records [14]. All data are harmonized through standardized modeling frameworks to provide consistent long-term estimates across diseases and regions.

Data Sources

Our analysis focused on the G20 members, including Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, France, Germany, India, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, Republic of Korea, Mexico, Russian Federation, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Turkey, the UK, the USA, and the European Union (EU). In this study, the G20 countries were defined according to the GBD 2023 geographic hierarchy (Location Set ID: 1437). Although the African Union was officially admitted as a permanent member of the G20 in September 2023, this analysis adheres to the established G20 composition used within the GBD 2023 data architecture. This study used publicly available data from the GBD 2023 study provided by the IHME. All data were deidentified and aggregated; therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. The GBD database is publicly accessible through the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) query tool (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). Our analysis is governed by the GBD 2023 Publication Policy and Collaborative Agreement [15].

Burden Description

This study extracted data on incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)—both crude and age-standardized—for migraine and TTH from all G20 countries. All metrics, comprising the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR), are expressed per 100,000 population and were standardized to the World Health Organization (WHO) world standard population to facilitate comparative analysis. Descriptive analyses and visualizations were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1) to depict the distribution of ASDR, ASIR, and ASPR for migraine and TTH across G20 countries in 2023. The Average Annual Percentage Change (AAPC) was calculated using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.1.0.0). A positive AAPC value indicates an increasing trend in headache burden, whereas a negative value reflects a decreasing trend. Cases of migraine and TTH were defined per the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) and coded as 8A80 and 8A81, respectively, according to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11).

Socio-Demographic Index

The Socio-demographic Index, developed by IHME in 2015, is a summary measure of a geography’s socio-economic development. Ranging from 0 to 1, the SDI captures the correlation between developmental progress and health outcomes; higher values denote superior levels of regional development [16].

Decomposition Analysis

To disentangle the driving forces behind changes in disease burden, we conducted a decomposition analysis partitioning total variation into three components: population aging, population growth, and epidemiological change. This allowed us to assess how demographic transitions versus disease-specific risk factors contributed to the observed burden over time.

SHAP Analysis

To further scrutinize the drivers of migraine and TTH across G20 countries, we employed the XGBoost algorithm in conjunction with SHAP for systematic model decomposition. Compared with traditional decomposition methods—which often rely on rigid linear assumptions and may fail to capture synergistic or high-order interactions—SHAP provides a robust framework by assigning each feature an additive contribution to the model’s output. This approach provides both local accuracy and consistency, grounded in cooperative game theory [13, 17]. By decomposing the model’s predictions into the marginal contributions of specific variables, we elucidated the granular impacts of biological sex, age-group distributions, and demographic shifts on the evolving burden of headache disorders.

The feature set incorporated sex, age group (excluding individuals < 5 years due to data unavailability), normalized calendar year (Year/1000), and the log-transformed total population (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Log\_Population$$\end{document}Log_Population). Categorical variables, including sex and age, were transformed via one-hot encoding, while continuous variables underwent normalization prior to modeling.

To capture the intrinsic nonlinear relationships between predictors and headache burden, an optimized XGBoost algorithm was implemented, with its predictive performance evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2). The XGBoost model was optimized using a systematic grid search integrated with a fivefold cross-validation strategy. Optimal configurations for key hyperparameters were identified, including a maximum tree depth (max depth = 3) and a learning rate (eta = 0.05). The optimal number of boosting iterations was dynamically determined through an early stopping mechanism (10-round threshold) to prevent redundant training. Furthermore, regularization techniques and stochastic feature/sample selection were implemented, with both the subsample and colsample_bytree ratios set to 0.8. The final model reached convergence at 4338 iterations, achieving an average validation root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.0094, which indicates robust generalization performance and the effective prevention of overfitting.

Model interpretability was facilitated through the SHAP framework; feature importance was quantified and ranked by mean absolute SHAP values, and SHAP dependence plots were constructed to elucidate the nonlinear trajectories of specific drivers in relation to the disease burden.

Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average Model

To forecast the future burden of migraine and TTH among G20 countries, we applied the ARIMA model, a classical time-series forecasting method [18]. The ARIMA framework captures both trend and seasonality by integrating past values (autoregressive terms), differencing operations to ensure stationarity, and moving average components that account for short-term fluctuations. We fitted separate ARIMA models for ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR for each country. Data from 1990 to 2023 served as the training sample for fitting the ARIMA model, with the optimal model determined using the auto.arima() function based on the Akaike Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion values. Using the fitted models, we generated projections for the period 2024–2035, with 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported to represent forecast uncertainty.

All estimates were accompanied by 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) to account for sampling and model-based error. All the analyses and visualizations in this study were performed in the R language (version 4.5.1), and the differences were considered statistically significant at two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Overview of G20 Countries

The burden of migraine and TTH across G20 countries is substantial. For migraine, the number of prevalent cases was 704,681,170 (95% UI: 610,455,397–801,370,230), with 51,918,764 (95% UI: 46,201,137–58,828,018) incident cases. The number of DALYs attributable to migraine was 24,873,985 (95% UI: 16,489,401–34,719,363). Regarding TTH, there were 1,269,387,250 prevalent cases (95% UI: 1,126,527,856–1,414,688,569) and 27,448,460 incident cases (95% UI: 24,099,890–30,923,293). The associated DALYs for TTH were 2,854,591 (95% UI: 1,916,150–4,050,260).

Discussion

Our study shows that the increase in migraine and TTH burden across G20 countries is primarily driven by population growth and aging. While demographic shifts remain the dominant macro-level drivers, our SHAP-XGBoost model successfully identifies critical population “saturation effects” and nonlinear marginal contributions of sex, age, and population size. Furthermore, we identify a burden plateau at mid-to-high SDI levels, with projections suggesting the migraine burden will stabilize, whereas TTH prevalence rates and incidence rates will likely to continue to rise by 2035. These results provide a reference for G20 countries in the formulation and optimization of public health strategies to mitigate the burden of migraine and TTH.

A Comparative Analysis of Headache Burden Drivers in G20 Countries

By comparing two analytical methods, we explored the drivers of the headache burden in G20 countries. Both methods converge on the macro trend that population size is the main driver of the increasing burden. However, the analysis also revealed several divergent conclusions. First, decomposition analysis is essentially a linear description, assuming a constant impetus for each unit of population growth. Conversely, the SHAP dependence plot unmasks a profound nonlinear “saturation effect.” Particularly in the TTH model, the contribution rate drops significantly once the population size crosses a specific threshold. This divergence theoretically hints at a potential “resilience” within the healthcare infrastructures of larger G20 countries. Specifically, the coverage efficiency of public health interventions or the adaptability of social groups may, to some extent, offset the load brought by pure physical population growth [19]. However, it may partially reflect modeling artifacts or the mathematical properties of the log-transformation applied to population data, which tends to compress larger values. This finding should be viewed as a predictive association rather than a confirmed causal mechanism, and further empirical research is required to validate these potential drivers.

Second, traditional decomposition methods are based on a linear assumption that treats aging, epidemiological changes, and population growth as mutually independent dimensions. A potential limitation of this linearity is that it may not fully explain differences in burden trends among countries with similar demographic profiles. Because decomposition analysis assigns a fixed contribution value to each incremental change in a single variable, it may mask the complex, nonlinear interactions between variables [10, 11]. In contrast, the SHAP analysis, through a game-theoretic framework, isolates the interactive contributions of variables [13]. The results demonstrate that sex and age do not act in isolation but exhibit a powerful synergistic amplification effect; the marginal contribution of female status during the peak productivity years (ages 20–54) far exceeds the simple sum of the two. This coupling reflects the complexity of headache pathogenesis. While decomposition analysis masks these overlapping risks due to its inability to handle “multicollinearity” between variables, SHAP restores this authentic socio-biological driving logic.

It is essential to clarify that these two methods operate at fundamentally different analytical scales. Decomposition analysis operates on the absolute population scale, partitioning the total change in absolute numbers of cases between 1990 and 2023 into contributions from population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes [20]. It directly answers: “How many additional patients will healthcare systems need to accommodate?” In contrast, SHAP-XGBoost operates on the log-transformed rate scale. This transformation has important consequences: (1) the log transformation compresses larger values, altering relationships compared with the original rate scale [21]; (2) SHAP values represent multiplicative rather than additive effects, where a one-unit log-scale change represents approximately 10% change on the original scale; (3) nonlinear patterns on the log scale may not reflect true nonlinearity on the original rate scale [22]. It directly answers: “Which demographic features are most predictive of distinguishing high-burden from low-burden nations?” These scale differences are complementary, not contradictory. Both methods are necessary: decomposition reveals absolute burden growth requiring capacity expansion, while SHAP reveals which subpopulations drive rate heterogeneity [17]. This transition from “describing the status quo” to “explaining the mechanism” constitutes the core value of the dual-model framework in this study. It serves as a reminder to policymakers: in populous nations, blindly expanding general medical resources may yield diminishing returns; instead, precision management should be implemented for the high-marginal-contribution groups identified by SHAP. Therefore, we provide individualized SHAP analysis results for each G20 country to serve as an evidence-based reference for national policymakers in tailoring headache management strategies (Supplementary Fig. S1–S20).

Sex and Age Disparities: A Complex Interplay of Biology and Psychosocial Factors

Our analysis revealed a pronounced female preponderance in the prevalence and burden of migraine and TTH across G20 countries, with notable variations across the lifespan. This highlights the critical role of sex and age as key biological variables influencing the distribution of headache disorders. The neurobiological underpinning for this sex disparity is partly attributed to the role of estrogen. Fluctuations in estrogen levels are known to modulate the excitability of the trigeminovascular system, leading to its dysregulation. This can result in increased release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P, promoting neurogenic inflammation and contributing to the peripheral and central sensitization processes that underlie migraine attacks [23, 24]. While TTH does not exhibit as pronounced a sex disparity as migraine, female patients commonly report a higher prevalence of psychosocial stress and comorbid mood disorders. These factors are believed to exacerbate the burden of TTH, potentially through mechanisms involving the central dysregulation of pain processing, leading to heightened pain sensitivity and reduced inhibitory control [25]. The refined age-specific patterns we identified further support the dynamic interplay between biology and life stage. The peak incidence in adolescence coincides with pubertal hormonal changes, while the peak DALYs in middle age may reflect the cumulative burden of a chronic condition during peak productive years, as well as perimenopausal transitions in women [26, 27].

Demographic Composition as the Primary Moderator of Inter-Country Burden Heterogeneity

The differential pace and composition of these demographic transitions across G20 members may partly account for the inter-country divergence observed in cross-sectional comparisons. As our decomposition analysis demonstrates, population growth and aging together account for the dominant share of burden variation over time [20, 28].

Nations exhibiting rapidly increasing burden trajectories—most notably China—share key demographic characteristics: large populations undergoing accelerating aging, with young cohorts shifting into the peak-risk window for headache (ages 20–54) [29, 30]. The finding that population growth accounted for 541.87% of migraine incident case increases in China reflects a demographic amplification effect: as large cohorts move through high-risk age windows simultaneously, even stable age-specific rates generate substantial absolute burden increases [31]. This pattern has potential policy relevance: it suggests that burden growth in such nations may continue even in the absence of any change in underlying disease risk, and that interventions targeting age-specific incidence rates could have outsized population-level impact precisely because of the large cohort sizes passing through high-risk ages.

Conversely, nations demonstrating stable or declining age-standardized rates despite overall population growth illustrate a potentially moderating effect of healthcare system maturation. The SHAP analysis revealed a saturation pattern in which the marginal contribution of population size to TTH burden diminishes beyond a threshold of approximately log-population of 19, potentially consistent with the hypothesis that larger, more developed healthcare systems may partially absorb burden through improved case management and preventive care, though this association warrants caution as it reflects a modeling pattern rather than a confirmed causal mechanism.

The age-specific burden patterns identified in our heatmap analyses further reinforce the potential centrality of demographic structure. The sharp escalation of burden from age 15–19 onward and the concentration of peak DALYs within the 20–54 working-age cohort suggest that nations with large and growing working-age populations may face compounding productivity losses in addition to direct healthcare costs [32, 33]. This age-structured burden profile may inform how G20 countries prioritize interventions: countries with younger demographic profiles and rising burden trajectories may be better positioned for early, preventive investment, whereas those with older profiles might benefit from focusing on managing chronic burden in established patient populations.

The Nonlinear Nexus Between SDI and Headache Burden

The association analysis between age-standardized indicators and the SDI reveals that the headache burden exhibits a distinct nonlinear evolutionary pattern alongside social development. In lower SDI stages, prevalence and incidence show an upward trend driven by “diagnostic expansion” resulting from improved medical resources [34]. However, when the SDI exceeds a threshold of approximately 0.8, several nations enter a descending phase. This suggests that advanced medical interventions and preventative strategies in highly developed societies may partially alleviate the disease burden [19].

These findings have potential policy implications for G20 healthcare planning. Countries with SDI below 0.8 should prioritize diagnostic capacity building and primary care training to identify cases within existing infrastructure. Nations at or approaching the SDI threshold of 0.8 face a critical intervention window, requiring simultaneous investment in capacity expansion and innovative therapeutics to effectively manage the diagnostic expansion phase [35, 36]. High-SDI countries should shift from case-finding to treatment optimization and precision prevention, with particular emphasis on reproductive-age women [37].

Divergent Future Trends in Migraine and TTH

The model forecasts for 2035 provide an exploratory projection based on historical trends [21]. The projected decline in migraine ASPR and ASIR is optimistic and likely reflects the continued positive impact of improved treatments and growing disease awareness [38]. In contrast, the rising trajectory for TTH highlights a persistent and potentially neglected challenge. The lack of specific and effective preventive medications for TTH, coupled with its strong association with ubiquitous modern stressors (e.g., psychological stress, poor posture), positions it as an increasingly significant public health issue [28, 39]. The under-recognition and trivialization of TTH as a “normal” headache further impede effective management and contribute to its chronicity.

Limitations

This study presents several limitations that warrant consideration. First, this analysis relies exclusively on GBD 2023, a systematized, modeled dataset that may not reflect differences among countries in diagnostic thresholds, healthcare-seeking behavior, or case ascertainment methods. GBD 2023 was selected as the primary data source because it is the most recently updated global disease burden database covering all G20 member states; however, this was not the only option. Other data sources—including national disease registries, WHO regional health estimates, and population-based surveys—provide higher granularity in specific regions but lack uniform coverage across all G20 countries and employ heterogeneous methodologies, which limit direct cross-national comparison. To comprehensively address the research questions, an ideal database would combine GBD’s methodological standardization with population-based surveys conducted simultaneously across all G20 countries using unified diagnostic criteria—a resource that currently does not exist. Second, GBD does not comprehensively capture environmental and lifestyle factors critical to headache burden epidemiology, which may underestimate modifiable risk factors [40]. Third, ARIMA projections are extrapolations of historical trends and do not explicitly incorporate emerging therapies approved during the study period, and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive predictions [21]. Fourth, the findings are specific to G20 countries and cannot be directly extrapolated to non-G20 countries; policymakers elsewhere should treat mechanistic insights as testable hypotheses applicable to their own contexts rather than direct policy prescriptions. Finally, GBD modelling uncertainty is particularly relevant for lower-SDI G20 members with sparse headache survey data, affecting the precision of decomposition percentages and SHAP feature importance values, though the relative ranking of countries and broad temporal patterns remain robust [41].

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive reevaluation of migraine and TTH burden across G20 countries, moving beyond descriptive epidemiology to mechanistic explanation. Our findings confirm that while population growth remains the foundational driver of the absolute burden increase, the “internal logic” of this escalation is governed by complex nonlinearities and synergistic interactions. As we look toward 2035, the divergent trajectories of migraine and TTH underscore the need for a shift in public health strategy. We advocate for a move away from uniform, population-wide resource scaling toward risk-stratified, precision intervention strategies. By identifying nonlinear inflection points and specific high-risk clusters, G20 countries can optimize healthcare delivery, ensuring that interventions are both evidence-based and economically efficient in the face of evolving demographic landscapes.
