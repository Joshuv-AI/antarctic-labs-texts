“Pilot intervention study: food and nutrition education and psychoeducation among elementary school students in grades 4 to 6”
Authors: Candelaria Ríos-Reyna, SanJuana Elizabeth Alemán-Castillo, Ana Luisa González-Pérez, Vidalma Del Rosario Bezares-Sarmiento, Andrea Jasso-Hernández, Guadalupe Concepción Rodríguez-Castillejos, Octelina Castillo-Ruiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe implementation of nutritional interventions could improve eating habits, contributing to the prevention of overweight and obesity in children.ObjectiveTo modify eating habits through interventions involving food and nutritional education and emotional psychoeducation among school-age children.MethodsThis longitudinal study included a sample of 635 students from four public primary schools; these students were assigned to an intervention group (IG) or a control group (CG). Anthropometric measurements were performed to determine nutritional status, and body image perception, body dissatisfaction, stress, anxiety and depression were evaluated. A 14-week intervention and a final assessment were carried out. The effect size was calculated using Cohen’s “d” statistic.ResultsThe general prevalence of overweight and obesity was 46.3%. When the size of the intergroup effect of nutritional intervention was analyzed, a positive effect on fruit (d = 0.201) and dairy consumption (d = 242) was observed.ConclusionThe nutritional intervention effectively modulated school children’s food consumption.

Introduction

Childhood obesity is a global public health concern [1]. Mexico ranks among the most affected countries, with one of the highest prevalence rates, at 37.3% combined (19.2% overweight and 18.1% obesity) [2, 3]. Physical inactivity and eating habits are among the primary causes of being overweight among school-aged children [4]. However, previous nutritional interventions aimed at reducing overweight and obesity among children have not achieved their goals [5–7]. This may be because the school environment has a considerable influence on the eating habits of school children [8]. Therefore, it is necessary to design and implement intervention programs aimed at modifying children’s food choices as a more effective strategy [9]. For example, the Healthy School Canteen program prohibits the sale of low-nutrient and high-energy foods and includes a subsidy for fruits and vegetables to increase the number of schoolchildren who consume these foods [10]. Other research has indicated that a multicomponent, multidisciplinary and individualized intervention incorporating dietary modifications, physical activity, behavioral strategies and parental involvement could effectively address childhood obesity in Mexico [11]. This approach should include attention to psychological factors such as anxiety and stress, which predict long-term weight gain and can lead to depression [12, 13]. Therefore, the detection of symptoms of depression and anxiety should be recommended as a preliminary measure for effective intervention in overweight/obese children and adolescents [14]. Other factors to be considered include misperception of body image and body dissatisfaction, both of which are associated with nutritional status [15–17]. However, school intervention studies examining the emotional, psychological and social well-being of children with obesity remain scarce. Individual interventions to improve mental health in the pediatric population are therefore justified [18].

Materials and methods

Study design

School-based food-nutrition education and psychoeducation interventions were implemented among elementary school students in Reynosa, Tamaulipas, Mexico. Following parental informed consent, participants were recruited from four public elementary schools (with morning shifts) in urban Reynosa, Tamaulipas, Mexico, through convenience sampling. All the schools served middle socioeconomic status (SES) populations [19]. Schools were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (IG) or the control group (CG).

Participants

The sample size was calculated using Epidat 4.2 software, with a 95.0% confidence level and a 5.0% margin of error. For a total population of 78,534 basic education students and an expected obesity prevalence of 53.8% [20], the required sample was 381 children. However, to enhance sample representativeness and minimize bias, the research team deemed it methodologically sound to include all eligible students who met the selection criteria, thereby strengthening the robustness of the findings. The study sample included 738 fourth- to sixth-grade students. Students were invited to participate in nutritional assessments and standardized questionnaires measuring the following: dietary habits, emotional health and body image perception. Data were collected at two time points: T1 (October–November 2023 (baseline)) and T2 (May–June 2024 (postintervention)). Both assessments were conducted onsite at the participating schools. Students self-administered all the questionnaires under the supervision of trained research staff. The study included students of all genders. Children who did not provide parental informed consent, who declined anthropometric measurements, who were currently receiving psychological care, and who failed to complete all required questionnaires or anthropometric measurements were excluded. Implementation stages of the pilot program: “promotion of healthy eating habits”

The pilot program comprises three stages: 1). Initial assessment, which includes anthropometric measurements and the administration of questionnaires to evaluate eating habits, psychological factors (stress, anxiety, depression), and body perception and dissatisfaction; 2). Intervention: nutritional education and psychoeducation only in intervention schools; and 3). Final assessment.

Procedure

Schools were selected, and school administrators were contacted with requests for authorization. Meetings were held with parents to inform them about the project, request their authorization to work with their children, and guarantee the confidentiality of the information, as well as to obtain informed assent from the children.

Stage 1

Anthropometric measurements (weight, height) were taken, and questionnaires on food frequency consumption, stress, anxiety, depression, and body perception were administered before recess.

Stage 2

The intervention was carried out over 14 weeks and was divided into two periods: Period I (February/March 2024) and Period II (April/May 2024). On the basis of Bandura’s social cognitive theory, which proposes that individuals acquire learning through observation and direct instruction [21], the IG received a series of educational talks on dietary guidance according to NOM-043-SSA2-2012 [22], emotion management [23, 24], and body image perception [25, 26]. The session were held once a week for 30 min and were led by two nutrition graduates and trained psychology students. Each session began with a presentation of the corresponding topic in an audiovisual format to capture the students’ attention. To reinforce the information and learning, a didactic game or activity related to the topic was conducted. Posters with complementary information were also displayed at the entrance and courtyards of the school, and brochures related to the topics were sent to the students’ parents or guardians (Table 1).

Stage 3

After the 14-week intervention, anthropometric measurements were taken, and food frequency, stress, anxiety, depression, and body image perception questionnaires were administered to both groups (IG and CG).

Measurements

Anthropometric parameters

Weight and height measurements were taken at the beginning and end of the intervention following the techniques established by the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry (ISAK) [27] using a bioimpedance scale (Tanita BF-689®) and a stadiometer (SECA 213®). Nutritional status was determined using the anthropometric indicator of the BMI-for-age z score by sex through the WHO (World Health Organization) Anthro Plus software version 1.0.4 [28], considering the cutoff points recommended by the WHO (underweight ≤ − 2 SD, normal weight between − 1.99 SD and 1 SD, overweight > 1 SD to ≤ 2 SD, and obesity > 2 SD) [29].

Dietary habits

To assess eating habits, a food frequency questionnaire developed on the basis of previous studies was used [30–32]. The foods were selected on the basis of the 2018–2019 National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHNS) and were divided into 13 groups, categorized as recommended foods—fruits, vegetables, legumes, cereals, unprocessed meats, water, eggs, and dairy products—and nonrecommended foods—processed meats, fast food and traditional Mexican snacks, chips, sweets and desserts, sugary cereals, and sugar-sweetened beverages [3]. A score of 1 was assigned for a high frequency and 0 for a low frequency of recommended food consumption. In contrast, nonrecommended foods were scored in reverse: 0 = high frequency and 1 = low frequency [33].

Stress

To measure stress in school children, the Children’s Inventory of Daily Stressors (CIDS) was used. The CIDS consists of 25 dichotomous items (yes/no). These items are used to gather information related to health, school, peers, and family and refer to events occurring at the time of the questionnaire’s administration. The cutoff point for stress symptoms ranged from 0 to 25. The total score, obtained by summing the affirmative responses to the items, indicates a high level of self-perceived daily stress [34].

Anxiety

The Spence Children’s Anxiety Scale (SCAS) was used [35]. The SCAS is a 44-item self-report instrument with four response options: 0 = never, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often, and 3 = always. This instrument is used to assess the six most common anxiety disorders in childhood: separation anxiety, panic disorder, agoraphobia, obsessive‒compulsive disorder, social phobia, generalized anxiety, and fear of physical harm. The scale yields a total score of up to 114, with higher scores indicating greater levels of anxiety. The cutoff point for anxiety symptoms is a total score ≥ 60 points [36, 37].

Depression

The Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) was administered [38]. This inventory contains 27 items that measure depressive symptoms and is divided into two scales: dysphoria (depressed mood, sadness, misery, worry, and loneliness) and negative self-esteem (feelings of ineffectiveness, ugliness, wickedness, guilt, and desire for death). The CDI uses three response options ranging from 0 to 2, yielding a total score of 57 points [39]. The most commonly used cutoff point for research is a score of ≥ 19 [40]. In this study, the total scores for stress, anxiety, and depression were categorized into three levels, namely, low, medium, and high [12], on the basis of intervals of equal width. The instruments were administered collectively in the classrooms by trained psychology students. The treatment duration ranged between 45 and 50 min per group.

Body perception

To assess children’s body image perception, the Children’s Body Image Scale (CBIS) [41] was administered. The scale consists of seven figures based on photographs of boys and girls representing different BMI percentiles (3, 10, 25, 50, 75, 90, and 97), each assigned a value from 1 to 7 according to increasing size, corresponding to the BMI range for children. Underestimation or overestimation was assessed on the basis of the negative or positive difference between self-perception and actual BMI; a difference of zero was interpreted as accurate perception [25].

Body dissatisfaction

To determine body dissatisfaction, the difference between the ideal figure desired by the participants and the figure they perceived as their own body shape (self-perceived body image) was calculated. The greater the difference between the perceived body shape and the ideal body shape, regardless of direction, the higher the body dissatisfaction score [42]. For grouping participants and data analysis, three categories of body image satisfaction were created: satisfied (own image = ideal), dissatisfied (own-ideal = ± 1), and very dissatisfied (own-ideal ≥ ± 2) [43].

The instruments for assessing eating habits, emotional health and body perception were previously validated through test-retest reliability. The food frequency survey obtained Cronbach’s alpha of α = 0.98; CBIS, α = 0.70; CIDS, α = 0.70; SCAS, α = 0.87; and CDI, α = 0.88. These values indicate acceptable reliability and internal consistency according to Toro et al. [44].

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Unidad Academica Multidisciplinaria Reynosa-Aztlán of the Universidad Autonoma de Tamaulipas (registration number: 005/2023/CEI). Additionally, the General Law on the Protection of Personal Data of Mexico, published in the Official Federal Gazette on January 26 [45], was considered, along with the ethical guidelines of the American Psychological Association [46], Sect. 8, for nonintrusive research procedures.

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviations (for quantitative variables) were compared using Student’s t test, and percentages (for qualitative variables) were compared using the chi-square test for independent samples (pre- and postintervention). Pre- and postintervention results (related samples) within each group were analyzed using Student’s t test (for weight, height, BMI, and eating habits) and McNemar’s test (for nutritional status, emotional health parameters, body image perception, and body image dissatisfaction) to identify changes from the beginning to the end of the intervention within the IG and CG. The effect size for both intragroup and intergroup interventions was calculated using the difference of means according to Cohen’s criteria [47], considering the following thresholds: 0 to 0.19, no effect; 0.2 to 0.49, small effect; 0.5 to 0.79, moderate effect; 0.8 to 1.29, large effect; and ≥ 1.3, very large effect [48]. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software (Version 25.0), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The findings revealed that interventions involving food-nutritional education and psychoeducation improved fruit and dairy consumption; however, these interventions did not result in changes in stress, anxiety, depression, overweight or obesity in the IG. Possible reasons why no change in the overweight category was observed include the study design, short intervention period and insufficient exposure in the intervention group. The results of this study are consistent with those of studies carried out in Panama [49], Mexico [7], the USA [50], China [18] and Norway [51]. In contrast, Danielsen et al. [52] in Norway and Liu et al. [53] in China reported decreased obesity in the IG; Stavrou et al. [54] in Greece and Lopez-Alarcon et al. [55] in Mexico reported decreased BMI, anxiety and depression. In Mexico, Radilla et al. [56] reported increased consumption of water, vegetables, and fruits and decreased anxiety and depression in the IG. Fochesatto et al. [57] in Brazil and other studies with psychological focus interventions conducted in different countries reported decreased levels of stress, anxiety and depression [58–61]. Notably, of the studies reviewed, only that of Stavrou et al. [54] combined stress management with diet; thus, more research is needed to integrate psychoeducation with food and nutritional education.

In this study, a significant reduction in overestimated body image perception was observed in both groups, but it was greater in the IG group. However, no significant difference between the IG and CG was found. Notably, the prevalence of inadequate body perception exceeds 80.0% in both groups, and incorrect body perception is related to low self-esteem. Previous studies by Paraskeva et al. [62] and Garbett et al. [63] in India and Diedrichs et al. [64] in England reported a significant increase in body esteem in the IG. In terms of body dissatisfaction, in this investigation, compared with the CG, the IG exhibited a significant decrease (p = 0.033) in the number of schoolchildren who were dissatisfied. However, when the IG and CG were compared, no significant changes in body dissatisfaction were observed. These results are consistent with those of previous studies, such as Yager et al. [65] and Dunstan et al. [66] in Australia and Warschburger & Zitzmann [67] in Germany. Notably, the interventions used for discussion in this section are international and differ in study design; however, no studies similar to the present research were found, which highlights the lack of interventions that assess the body image perception and body satisfaction of school children. This is confirmed by Fiskum et al. [68], who reported that weight-focused interventions rarely take into account adverse effects on children’s body image and fail to notice the increasing number of school children struggling with negative thoughts and feelings about their weight and body image, which could increase the risk of unhealthy eating and weight problems. In this context, Forbes et al. [69] in Australia recommended the development of interventions that can be easily adapted to diverse populations in terms of age, sex or culture, with a focus on positive body image.

Effect size

The results revealed that the intervention in the present study did not affect nutritional status, as the prevalence of overweight and obesity increased in the IG and the CG. Nwachan et al. [70], who evaluated the effect of nutritional education on the nutritional status of school children in Cameroon, Africa, concluded that nutritional education alone is not effective at improving the nutritional status of children. Yu et al. [18] recommended that future studies include psychological indicators as primary outcomes to measure the effects of interventions against childhood obesity.

In terms of eating habits, in the present study, the IG increased the consumption of fruits, legumes, cereals, water, eggs, and dairy products but did not significantly decrease the consumption of processed meats (p > 0.05). In contrast, Alemán-Castillo et al. [21] in Reynosa, Tamaulipas, Mexico, reported a significant increase in fruit and vegetable consumption (p = 0.001) in the control group through nutritional guidance talks and complementary activities in the classroom once a week for 7 consecutive weeks. They also displayed posters with information about healthy snacks in the classroom, on the playgrounds, and at the school entrance. In addition, brochures and examples of healthy snacks were sent to the parents. In this context, Davis et al. [50] in the USA implemented workshops on school gardens and cooking and offered children fruits and vegetables. The authors achieved an increase in vegetable consumption (p = 0.02), whereas fruit consumption (p = 0.55) and the consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages (p = 0.26) did not significantly differ at the end of the intervention. Antwi et al. [71] conducted a nutritional education intervention in primary school children in Ghana and improved nutritional knowledge in the intervention group (p = 0.007). However, these previous studies (Alemán-Castillo et al., Davis et al., and Antwi et al.) did not report the effect size of the intervention.

The present intervention affected fruit (d = 0.20) and dairy (d = 0.24) consumption, which, although small, is important for health, as it is beneficial for school children to consume these food groups. These results are similar to the work of Almanza-Cruz et al. [72], who reported a small effect size (ES = 0.25) for carbohydrate-rich foods and polyunsaturated fatty acids (ES = 0.20) through a nutritional intervention that provided dietary guidance to the school population for 12 consecutive weeks, along with a 6-week follow-up reinforcement. In contrast, Outzen et al. [73] did not find an effect of the FOODcamp educational intervention on the intake of vegetables, fruits, or a combination of vegetables, fruits and juices, meat, fish, or sugary drinks. On the other hand, Vilchis-Gil et al. [9] suggested that to generate significant and lasting changes in eating habits, interventions should include parents. Zaki et al. [74], in a cross-sectional study conducted with adolescents, concluded that improving eating habits requires collaboration among policy-makers, educational institutions, parents/caregivers, and the food industry.

With respect to stress, anxiety, depression, body perception, and body dissatisfaction, the expected results were not achieved, since the effect of the intervention was imperceptible, in contrast to what was reported by Stavrou et al. [54], who reported a significant reduction in BMI in the IG compared with that in the CG (Δ = 1.18 versus 0.10 kg/m²; p < 0.001), indicating that the stress management program applied over 8 weeks facilitated weight loss. On the other hand, Iruegas & González [60] analyzed the effectiveness of integrating cognitive-behavioral techniques and the solution-focused approach in reducing childhood stress during the COVID-19 pandemic and reported a large effect size (d = 1.2) at the end of the intervention. Paraskeva et al. [62] reported significant effects on body self-esteem (d = 0.28) and internalization (d = 0.49) postintervention. With the body image intervention “Confident Me,” which consisted of five interactive 45-minute sessions and was aimed at risk factors for body dissatisfaction in India, Garbett et al. [63] reported a medium effect on body esteem (d = 0.45) and a positive affect on body image (d = 0.58) at the end of the intervention, indicating that this intervention is an effective program for improving body image among British and Portuguese school children. In contrast, Diedrichs et al. [64], with the five-session body image intervention “Dove Confident Me,” reported a small but significant effect on body esteem [d = 0.14] among school children in England. In Norway, Resaland et al. [51] carried out a physical activity intervention in 10-year-old children and reported no effect (ES = 0.0) of the intervention on psychological well-being.

Strengths

With respect to the strengths of this research, no studies have been conducted under these conditions that involving analyzing eating habits, emotional health, body image, and body dissatisfaction in interventions with school children.

The evaluation staff was previously trained by researchers who are experts in the field. The instruments used were previously validated and adapted to the students studied. The data were collected under the supervision of highly experienced researchers.

These results are significant because childhood is a developmental stage and a window of vulnerability for experiencing stress, anxiety, and depression in adulthood because of the increased effects of an unhealthy lifestyle with poor nutritional status and a low-quality diet, which is also related to body dissatisfaction that can lead to eating disorders, forming a vicious cycle between excess weight and eating habits.

Furthermore, the results of the present study provide novel information for adapting interventions to the needs of school children and emphasize the importance of addressing such interventions from a comprehensive perspective that includes not only dietary intake and physical activity but also psychosocial factors.

Limitations

The results of this research cannot be generalized and should be interpreted considering certain limitations. Information on emotional health and food consumption was self-reported; thus, recall bias may have been present. The timing of data collection may have affected the results, as it coincided with the period of school performance evaluation exams.

The breadth of information collected—such as data on food consumption, body perception and dissatisfaction, stress, anxiety, and depression—could have led to the loss of cases. Additionally, the children might have given socially desirable responses to please the researcher.

The significant reduction in overestimated body perception in both groups could be because the parents in both groups received reports of their children’s initial evaluation, which may have led them to pay more attention to their children and influenced the children’s responses in the second evaluation, as parents have been reported to underestimate their children’s body weight.

The duration of the intervention and the training of the researchers could have limited its effectiveness. Additionally, the students were recruited from four schools in a single sector of the city.

Conclusions

The intervention had a positive effect on the consumption of fruits and dairy products. Additionally, it provided information on the presence of psychological variables such as stress, anxiety, depression, body image perception, and body dissatisfaction among elementary school children, which could be useful for further research and a deeper understanding of the interactions among these factors. Notably, there is little evidence from studies with characteristics similar to those of the study sample, which presents opportunities for future research. According to the data obtained, the need to address the problem of childhood obesity with interdisciplinary teams is emphasized, and from the biopsychosocial model to achieve a common goal, it is necessary to carry out nutritional interventions supplemented with emotional psychoeducation to strengthen the development of healthy eating habits.
