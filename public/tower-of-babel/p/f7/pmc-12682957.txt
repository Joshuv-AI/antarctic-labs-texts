Central Message

Partial tricuspid valve detachment enables safe and effective repair of post-infarction ventricular septal defects in adults without requiring ventriculotomy.

Ventricular septal defects (VSDs) are pathologic communications between the left and right ventricles. Postinfarction VSDs in adults carry high mortality rates and present surgical challenges owing to friable tissue and irregular defect geometry.1

Pediatric approaches for congenital VSDs often incorporate partial tricuspid valve detachment to enhance visualization, a technique rarely applied in adults because of anatomic and functional differences.2,3 The friable myocardial tissue and complex defect geometry of postinfarction VSDs often necessitate ventriculotomy in infarcted myocardium, which is associated with such complications as bleeding and suture dehiscence.1,4

Here we report the case of a 58-year-old male with a postinfarction inferior muscular VSD that was repaired successfully using partial tricuspid valve detachment. Case Report

A 58-year-old male with chronic tobacco use and extensive coronary artery disease presented to an outside hospital with acute shortness of breath, nausea, and vomiting. Evaluation revealed an acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction. Emergent coronary angiography demonstrated total occlusion of the right coronary artery due to an acute on chronic occlusion of the artery, which could not be crossed, and mild disease in the left anterior descending artery (Figures 1 and 2). An intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) was placed, and the patient stabilized with supportive care despite worsening renal and hepatic dysfunction. He was then transferred to our facility for surgical intervention. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed a 2.5- cm inferior muscular VSD with persistent left-to-right shunting and right atrial and ventricular dilation. Surgery was deferred for 10 days to allow tissue scarring and improve suture hold.

Surgical Procedure

Following induction of general anesthesia, a median sternotomy was performed, revealing a dilated, congested right ventricle with extensive posterior wall scarring. Coronary bypass was infeasible owing to a thin, scarred inferior wall. After systemic heparinization, aorto-bicaval cannulation was performed, and cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. The aorta was cross-clamped, and Del Nido cardioplegia was administered antegrade.

A right atriotomy exposed the tricuspid valve and a large VSD in the basal ventricular septum. Partial detachment of the posterior and septal tricuspid valve leaflets enhanced visualization. The defect was closed using a heterologous pericardial patch secured with interrupted 4-0 Prolene sutures reinforced with pericardial strips. The leaflets were reattached to the annulus with 6-0 Prolene sutures. A water test confirmed satisfactory valve function with mild regurgitation consistent with preoperative findings. The right atrium was closed in 2 layers.

After thorough deairing, the aortic cross-clamp was removed, and the heart resumed sinus rhythm. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed successful defect closure without residual shunting. Temporary pacing leads were placed, and the IABP was reactivated. The patient was weaned from bypass without difficulty, and the chest was closed after achieving hemostasis (Video 1).

Postoperative Course

The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1, and the IABP was removed on day 3. He was discharged to home on day 7. Predischarge cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed no residual shunting.

Discussion

Conventional repair of postinfarction VSDs often involves ventriculotomy in infarcted myocardium, which carries risks of bleeding and suture line dehiscence. The tricuspid valve detachment technique—commonly used in pediatric VSD repairs—provides enhanced visualization and facilitates precise defect closure.2,3

In adult patients, tricuspid valve detachment is rarely used owing to concerns about postoperative valve dysfunction. Our case demonstrates that partial tricuspid valve detachment can be safely adapted for selected adult patients with challenging VSD anatomy. The technique allows optimal visualization of the defect and precise placement of sutures, minimizing residual shunting and avoiding ventriculotomy-related complications. Delaying surgery for this technique allows for tissue maturation and improved suture hold, which inherently selects patients stable enough to tolerate this waiting period. Our patient's hemodynamically stable condition allowed delayed surgery. Intraoperative testing confirmed the safety and efficacy of this approach, and the patient's uneventful recovery underscores its feasibility.

This case highlights the importance of tailoring surgical techniques to individual patient anatomy. Further studies are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes and broader applicability.

In conclusion, partial tricuspid valve detachment, a technique widely used in pediatric VSD repair, can be safely adapted for adult patients with postinfarction VSDs. This approach facilitates enhanced visualization and precise defect closure, avoiding ventriculotomy and its associated complications. Further investigation is warranted to assess the long-term safety and efficacy of this technique in adults with complex VSDs.
