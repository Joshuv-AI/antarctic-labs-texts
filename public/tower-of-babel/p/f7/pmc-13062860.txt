Feasibility and aesthetic outcomes of immediate fat-augmented latissimus dorsi (FALD) flap reconstruction post-mastectomy: a prospective cohort study from a tertiary breast centre
Authors: Li Ying Teoh, Mee Hoong See, Yin Cheng Lim, Shaqirah Azraq Alya, Lee Lee Lai, Suniza Jamaris, Visnu Lohsiriwat, Wei Qi Soh, Chen Hoi Lee, Yee Chi Wong, Qing Yi Tan, Jing Hui Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional mastectomy often results in aesthetic and psychological concerns for patients. The latissimus dorsi (LD) flap remains a widely used autologous option, especially in Asian women with smaller breast volumes. However, volume deficiency remains a limitation. This study assesses the feasibility and outcomes of fat-augmented LD (FALD) flap reconstruction.MethodsA prospective cohort of 23 patients undergoing immediate or delayed LD flap breast reconstruction with intraoperative autologous fat grafting was recruited between 2019 and 2022. Inclusion criteria included body mass index (BMI) 18.5–30 kg/m2 and insufficient flap volume for contralateral breast symmetry. Fat was harvested from the thighs using Body-Jet® system and injected into the LD flap and surrounding tissues. Cosmetic outcomes were assessed using Aesthetic Item Scale (AIS) and Body Image Scale (BIS) at 1, 6, and 12 months postoperatively.ResultsMedian age was 41 years. Average fat volume injected was 232 mL. Most patients were satisfied with their cosmetic outcomes; however, satisfaction declined slightly over time. The nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) group consistently scored higher in AIS compared to skin-sparing mastectomy (SSM). Interrater agreement between patients and assessors was low [intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) <0.5]. Complications were minimal and manageable.ConclusionsFALD flap reconstruction is a feasible, single-stage procedure with promising cosmetic and patient-reported outcomes, especially in patients unsuitable for larger volume flaps.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Immediate fat-augmented latissimus dorsi (FALD) flap reconstruction post-mastectomy is feasible and safe in a tertiary breast centre setting.

• Patients reported satisfactory short-term aesthetic outcomes, with acceptable complication rates and a defined learning curve.

What is known and what is new?

• The latissimus dorsi (LD) flap is a widely used reconstructive option, but is often limited by insufficient volume. Fat grafting has traditionally been performed as a delayed, staged procedure.

• This study demonstrates that combining immediate fat augmentation with LD flap in a single stage provides comparable safety, reduced need for secondary procedures, and improved aesthetic outcomes.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• FALD offers a practical, autologous alternative for patients unsuitable for free flaps or implants, especially in Asian populations with smaller body habitus.

• Wider adoption of immediate FALD could streamline reconstructive care, enhance cosmetic satisfaction, and reduce the burden of multiple staged operations.

Introduction

Traditionally, breast cancer surgery is frequently associated with mastectomy via a long and occasionally disfiguring surgical incision across the chest wall. The paradigm of management of mastectomy has shifted in the recent decades to more cosmetically acceptable approaches. The current improvement includes performing skin-sparing mastectomy (SSM) or nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM) with immediate or delayed reconstruction. The choices of reconstruction using either prosthesis or autologous tissue are usually affected by factors such as patient’s age and general health status for longer surgical time, volume desired, need for adjuvant radiotherapy, previous surgeries over the planned flap area, risk factors such as smoking, diabetes and obesity, location of the tumour in the breast and patient’s desire (1,2).

Up to date, the commonest autologous flap used is the latissimus dorsi (LD) flap, which is a preferable option in those who desire for more natural and softer reconstructed breast. It also provides the natural ptosis (3,4) and shape, thus benefits the small to medium-sized breasted patients especially. Thus, this is an option for most of Asian females. However, despite being a reliable pedicled flap, the volume might not be adequate with the LD flap alone. Several hybrid methods are used, including prosthesis or fat grafting with the LD flap, which is offered as a delayed procedure to increase the volume of the reconstructed breast.

Recent advances explore more of an immediate technique applying the concept of lipomodelling in the same surgical setting (5-9). Thus, in our study, we are exploring more on the option of immediate autologous fat grafting by incorporating via injection into the harvested LD flap. The benefits include avoidance of dual procedures under general anaesthesia with adequate volume that can be replaced in the same setting and offers immediate liposuction at the fat donor site. Our aim is to observe the feasibility of this technique and to assess of the cosmetic outcome and patients’ satisfaction following the procedure in our centre. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-299/rc).

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Medical MREC of Universiti Malaya Medical Centre (No. 20231019-12992) and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Patient selection and data collection

This is a prospective cohort study looking into the feasibility of fat-augmented LD (FALD) flap method and to assess the level of cosmetic satisfaction. The inclusion criteria are patients above the age of 18 years, those who undergo immediate or delayed breast reconstruction with LD flap, and has volume of unilateral breast measured intraoperatively less than 300 mL (approximately less than cup C), or the expected volume from LD flap is at least one cup size smaller than the contralateral breast; body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 to 30 kg/m2, with adequate fat over the thighs for lipofilling.

Patients who are 60 years old and above with inadequate donor site with inadequate site for fat transfer, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, connective tissue disease, varicose veins or history of deep vein thrombosis, smoker or has history of chronic smoking, patients with vascular disorder or any other cardiorespiratory comorbid that render patients unsuitable for prolonged surgery under general anaesthesia and patients who do not withhold their antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy prior to the surgery, are deemed unsuitable for the recruitment.

Those who fulfil the inclusion criteria are recruited prospectively into our study beginning from June 2019 onwards. The patient’s details, tumour, and treatment profile are compiled using a standard proforma. Selection of suitable patients from the outpatient clinic or inpatients are discussed among the team of breast oncoplastic surgeons and trainees.

Preoperative preparation

Preoperative arrangements for the lipofilling, referral to the anaesthetic clinic for fitness assessment and clearance. Admission takes place a day prior to the date of surgery. Due to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, beginning from mid-2020, 2-day admission prior to the surgery is indicated for preoperative severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) nasopharyngeal swab. On the day of admission, the patient is ensured to have her blood and relevant investigations reviewed, and advice on the preoperative showering methods are given by the ward nurses. One day before surgery, consent is taken for surgery and audio-visual permission, after which preoperative skin markings and measurements for the LD flap are done by the surgeons. Photographs of the bilateral breasts in five views (frontal, lateral, and 45 degrees between the front and lateral views) are taken by the primary surgeon, excluding the patient’s face to protect the patients’ confidentiality. Prophylactic antibiotics and analgesia are prepared as per hospital standard protocol. In case of need for achieving symmetry, patients are given an option for contralateral reduction mammoplasty.

Fat grafting techniques

On the day of surgery, an erector spinae block (ESB) may be offered by the anaesthesia team depending on availability. Patients were positioned in a semi-lateral position, allowing simultaneous draping of both the breast and the LD donor site. The LD flap was harvested using the conventional technique, without extended subcutaneous dissection beyond Scarpa’s fascia. The thoracodorsal nerve was preserved in all cases to maintain muscle viability. The LD tendon was detached and inset into the inframammary fold to provide lower pole projection and breast contour. Once harvested, the flap was rotated anteriorly and positioned over the chest wall. In all patients, flap shaping and inset were performed with the patient brought into a sitting position to allow direct comparison with the contralateral breast and optimize symmetry.

Fat graft was harvested from the thighs by the surgeon using a water-jet assisted liposuction system (Body-Jet®, Human Med AG, Germany), while the mastectomy was performed concurrently by another surgeon.

Donor sites over the thighs were infiltrated with a tumescent solution consisting of 3 L of 0.9% normal saline, 60 mL of sodium bicarbonate (8.4%), 20 mL of lignocaine 1% (not exceeding 4.5 mg/kg), and 3 mL of adrenaline (1 mg/mL). The solution was delivered using a 2.5 mm Biofill infiltration cannula. Fat was aspirated with a 3.5–3.8 mm cannula using the Body-Jet® system (Human Med AG, Schwerin, Germany) and collected as lipoaspirate. The harvested fat was processed by gentle decantation only, without centrifugation, and then transferred into 10-mL syringes for reinjection. The harvested fat was subsequently injected in small aliquots using a 2-mm blunt infiltration cannula. In all patients, fat was injected into the LD muscle. In selected cases where residual volume deficit was noted, additional fat was injected into the pectoralis major muscle along its fibre orientation at three levels: deep, middle, and superficial to enhance projection and achieve contralateral symmetry. The LD flap was harvested using the conventional technique, without extended subcutaneous harvest beyond Scarpa’s fascia. The flap was then rotated anteriorly to the chest wall and fixed in position with 3-0 Vicryl sutures. Symmetry with the contralateral breast was assessed in the sitting position before final fixation.

Vacuum drains (Radivac, Medinvents International, Selangor, Malaysia) were inserted at both the breast and the LD donor site, with an additional axillary drain placed when axillary dissection was performed. An occlusive dressing was applied to the LD donor site, while the fat harvest sites were wrapped with Gamgee pads and crepe bandages for compression. A non-occlusive dressing was placed over the reconstructed breast. Throughout all procedures, care was taken to avoid injury to the vascular pedicle.

Postoperative care and follow-ups

Postoperative management includes strict monitoring of the flap, ensuring good perfusion and viability of the flap. Fat donor site is observed for any haematoma. Patients are advised to purchase a tight fitting, thigh-length compression stockings which is to be worn after removal of the compression dressing. Drain care is of standard practice and transfusion is needed if haemoglobin is less than 10 g/dL. Patient is generally warded for 3 to 5 days postoperatively but subjected to longer hospital stay if any extra medical care is warranted. Post reconstruction bra will be ordered for patient to be worn after day 3 of surgery. Before discharge, patient will receive visits from breast care nurses, physiotherapist and occupational therapist.

Follow up plans are arranged with the primary surgeon which are generally conducted at the daycare centre to monitor drain output and observe for any signs of infection at the flap and donor sites. Subsequent management is as per standard practice.

Aesthetic Item Scale (AIS) first used by Visser et al. (10) which consists of adapting 5-points Likert scale assessing the volume, shape, symmetry, scars and nipple-areola complex and response ranging from ‘very dissatisfied’, ‘dissatisfied’, ‘neutral’, ’satisfied’ to ‘very satisfied’ was used to assess the cosmetic outcome of the subjects. This scale has been proven to be a reliable assessment tool based on the study by Dikmans et al. in 2017 (11). Assessment of the satisfaction is done using the patient AIS score, and the similar score is assessed by the breast care nurse who served as independent but not-blinded assessor. A score between 1 to 5 is given to all aspects, excluding the assessment of the nipple-areola complex in patients who had undergone an SSM. Assessment is also done at the intervals of 1, 6, and 12 months after surgery. As blinding was not implemented, the possibility of observer bias influencing scoring is acknowledged. To address this, inter-rater reliability was therefore evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Hopwood (12) in his study in the year 2000 had introduced a validated assessment method, which was named after him—Hopwood’s Body Image Scale (BIS). This scale is used to assess body image changes in cancer patients which had undergone a psychometric testing on 682 breast cancer patients. This scale provides a good subjective assessment in this study. An assessment using Hopwood’s BIS is done by the patient at the 6th month and 12th month after the operation.

Further assessment is done via a photographic manner at the similar intervals (1, 6, and 12 months) based on the similar five views taken preoperatively.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data were compared using Chi-squared analysis, while continuous variables were analysed with Student’s t-tests. Inter-observer reliability between patients and assessors was tested using Cohen’s kappa coefficient and the ICC, with values >0.8 considered indicative of high agreement (13). Changes in aesthetic scores over time were analysed with repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). For all statistical tests, two-sided P values <0.05 were considered significant.

Results

We are reporting the results of 23 patients, who have completed 1 year of follow-up until July 2022. The mean age of the patients is 41.0±8.0 years.

Eleven patients underwent SSM, 11 underwent NSM, and one patient had delayed reconstruction after a modified radical mastectomy. All the patients have no premorbid conditions, and they are categorised as Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) 1 before their surgeries. Prophylactic antibiotics and the ESB were administered to all the patients. Except for one patient, the rest of the patients had their fat harvested from bilateral medial and lateral sides of the thighs. Eight patients stayed more than 3 days postoperatively due to complications. Fifteen other patients were discharged within 72 hours postoperatively.

Most of the patients are slightly overweight with a mean BMI of 25.2±3.6 kg/m2, and two patients are obese with BMI above 29.9 kg/m2 (Table 1). However, in both the SSM and NSM groups, their BMIs were recorded within the normal range. The average breast volume is 378.2±175.7 g. We observed larger breast volume among the NSM group (388.6±190.3 g). We harvested an average of 311.4±114.8 mL of fat and injected an average of 231.7±98.8 mL of fat to the flap. In comparison, we harvested more fat from the NSM group (323.4±116.3 mL) and injected an average of 259.9±105.5 mL. We spent a mean of 255±45 min operating time for the cases. The mean duration for the fat harvesting procedure was 68±14 min. Our mean injected fat volume of 232 mL is higher than that reported in many prior series. This was achieved by distributing the graft across multiple planes, including the LD muscle, subcutaneous tissue, and, when necessary, the pectoralis major. This technique likely contributed to safe accommodation of larger graft volumes with low complication rates. We observed only one case of oil cyst and no symptomatic fat necrosis, supporting the safety of the approach, although longer follow-up with imaging may reveal subclinical changes.

One NSM patient developed a right-sided, asymptomatic pneumothorax attributed to the introduction of the ESB. Another SSM patient experienced immediate bleeding at the LD donor site, which required wound exploration and haemostasis, while a separate SSM patient developed skin edge necrosis and haematoma at the donor site. Two NSM patients developed nipple-areolar complications in the form of partial necrosis. All of these patients recovered uneventfully and were discharged after a mean hospital stay of 4.5 days. One patient with SSM and bilateral breast cancer died from metastatic disease 1 year postoperatively, and one NSM patient developed a local recurrence over the skin flap 6 months after surgery despite clear margins. No surgical site infections were observed in this cohort.

Donor-site morbidity was generally mild and self-limiting. The most frequently reported issues were thigh bruising, seroma, and donor-site pain, all of which resolved with conservative management. One patient developed wound margin necrosis at the LD donor site that required minor debridement but healed without further complication. Importantly, no cases of chronic donor-site morbidity such as contour deformity, functional deficit, or lumbar hernia were identified during follow-up.

Discussion

From our study, we demonstrated an acceptable learning curve for the FALD flap technique among surgeons in a high-volume centre. Once the learning curve was achieved, each procedure generally required 4 to 5 hours, which is comparable to other published series. This approach appears most feasible in healthy women with small to moderately sized breasts. Our cohort had an average breast volume of 380 g and a BMI not exceeding 25 kg/m2, and our patients were generally younger than those reported in previous series, with a mean age of 41 years (5,9).

Fat distribution across multiple planes likely facilitated the safe accommodation of larger graft volumes. In our series, fat was mainly injected into the pectoralis and LD muscle, with acceptable results (6). Our mean injected fat volume of 232 mL was higher than that reported in prior studies. For example, Santanelli di Pompeo et al. described a mean of 101 mL (range, 60–150 mL) using the Coleman technique (8), while Scafati et al. reported 146.7 mL (range, 100–300 mL) (5). Economides et al. found an average graft take of 66.8% (range, 50–80%) (9). We harvested larger volumes partly to optimize thigh contour, but nonetheless observed volume reduction in several patients at 1 year. To mitigate early resorption, we recommend injecting 10–20% excess volume at the initial procedure. Only one oil cyst was observed, with no symptomatic fat necrosis, supporting the safety of the approach.

We reported a mean fat harvest duration of 68 min, performed concurrently with breast and axillary surgery, thereby avoiding prolonged operative times. This aligns with Scafati et al., who also observed favourable outcomes with the FALD flap method (5). Extended LD harvests, however, are associated with higher donor-site morbidity such as seroma, contour deformities, and lumbar hernia (6). In our cohort, donor-site complications were generally mild (thigh bruising, seroma, donor-site pain) and self-limiting. No chronic functional deficits or lumbar hernia were observed.

FALD must be contextualised among alternative reconstructive strategies. The deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap remains the gold standard, offering durable volume and excellent aesthetic outcomes (14). However, DIEP requires microsurgical expertise, longer operative times, and suitable donor tissue, limiting its applicability. Implant-based reconstructions are another option, but they carry risks of infection, capsular contracture, and implant loss, particularly after radiotherapy. Hybrid LD-implant reconstructions have been described but are associated with implant-related complications. Sequential standalone fat grafting can achieve good contour restoration but usually requires multiple staged procedures (14). Recent systematic reviews suggest that immediate lipofilling alone or fat-augmented approaches may be promising (15). In this context, the FALD flap offers a pragmatic compromise: a single-stage, pedicled reconstruction that combines structural support with fat augmentation while avoiding implants.

In our cohort, 43% of patients received adjuvant radiotherapy. Although subgroup analysis showed no statistically significant differences in AIS or BIS scores, a trend toward lower scores was noted. This aligns with prior studies reporting reduced fat graft retention and fibrosis following radiotherapy (9). Larger studies are warranted to validate these early results and further define the oncological and aesthetic safety of hybrid FALD reconstruction.

We observed consistently poor inter-rater agreement (ICC <0.5) between patients and assessors, with assessors generally awarding higher scores. The lack of blinding likely contributed to this discrepancy. While AIS and BIS are validated tools (10-12), they remain subjective and vulnerable to bias. Future studies should incorporate blinded assessors and multidimensional instruments such as the BREAST-Q to provide more comprehensive, patient-centred evaluation.

One patient in our cohort developed a local recurrence despite clear surgical margins. This finding is consistent with existing literature showing that fat grafting and FALD reconstruction do not increase oncological risk (16). Goncalves et al., in a systematic review, similarly found no evidence of higher locoregional recurrence rates (16). Nevertheless, larger multicentre studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm oncological safety.

The potential cytotoxicity of local anaesthetics on adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) has been reported in preclinical studies, with lignocaine demonstrating dose-dependent effects (17). In our study, lignocaine dosage was limited, and no adverse effects were clinically observed, but future studies with viability assays may help quantify this impact. Additionally, the Body-Jet® water-jet assisted liposuction system was used in this study. This method yields adipocytes with higher viability and angiogenic potential compared to conventional liposuction, potentially enhancing long-term fat retention (18).

This study has several limitations. The small sample size limits statistical power and generalisability. Recruitment was further constrained by COVID-19 and strict selection criteria. The absence of a control group (e.g., traditional LD or DIEP flaps) prevents direct comparisons. The 1-year follow-up period restricts assessment of long-term durability and oncological safety. Non-blinded assessors may have introduced bias, and radiological monitoring of fat retention was not standardised. Moreover, while fat injection into the LD muscle was consistent, additional pectoralis injections were performed selectively, introducing technique variability. Despite these limitations, the prospective design, detailed operative documentation, and validated assessment tools strengthen the reliability of our findings. Larger multicentre studies with comparator arms and extended follow-up are required to define the role of FALD in contemporary breast reconstruction.

Conclusions

In conclusion, immediate lipofilling to latissimus flap is an acceptable hybrid method for immediate and delayed reconstruction following mastectomy. With the good aesthetic outcome, it can be one of the options to be adopted in many breast oncoplastic centres. Furthermore, the procedure can provide an extra option of symmetrisation without the need for reduction surgery for the contralateral breast in the same setting.
