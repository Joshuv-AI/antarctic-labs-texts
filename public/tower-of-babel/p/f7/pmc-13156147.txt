The Burden, Outcomes, and Management of Patients with Aldosterone Dysregulation: A Targeted Literature Review
Authors: Gianfranco Parati, Gbenga Ogedegbe, Abiy Agiro, Shan Luan, Harrison Davis, Ian Daniel, Jan McKendrick, Joanna Huang, Raymond R Townsend
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAldosterone dysregulation (excess aldosterone production at the source within the adrenal glands) in the absence of primary aldosteronism is a complex physiologic driver of uncontrolled hypertension and cardiovascular and kidney outcomes. Understanding the manifestations and burden of aldosterone dysregulation is critical to optimizing treatment and improving outcomes in patients with hypertension. This review examines the prevalence, patient attributes and burden of aldosterone dysregulation, and its impact on patient outcomes.MethodsA targeted literature review of articles published between 2013 and 2024 was conducted. A patient/population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes framework was used, with a predefined search and selection protocol. Articles focused on primary aldosteronism were excluded unless they also referenced essential hypertension. A screening tool that used methods including artificial intelligence, trained using manual (human) screening, was employed to select relevant articles, which underwent human review to confirm inclusion/exclusion.ResultsInitial searches yielded 16,501 unique articles. Following abstract screening, 327 full-text articles were reviewed, yielding 123 relevant articles. Included studies utilized a range of aldosterone-related thresholds and measures to characterize patients with aldosterone dysregulation. Several patient attributes impact aldosterone levels, including age, race, ethnicity, sex, and body mass index. Lifestyle factors such as sodium intake also impact aldosterone, but the effect varies by race and body weight. Long-term excess aldosterone was associated with elevated blood pressure (BP), cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic diseases, and end-organ damage, leading to a greater risk of adverse clinical outcomes and mortality. Further evidence is needed to determine whether these occur independently of BP levels.ConclusionExcess aldosterone is associated with poor cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic outcomes, including increased morbidity and mortality. Aldosterone dysregulation (excess aldosterone production at the source) is an underlying driver of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic diseases, which may not be adequately addressed by current antihypertensive therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03530-6.Keywords: Aldosterone dysregulation, Cardiovascular disease, Hypertension, Kidney disease, Metabolic disorders

Key Summary Points

This targeted literature review examines the prevalence, patient attributes, and burden of aldosterone dysregulation (excess aldosterone production at the source within the adrenal glands) in the absence of primary aldosteronism, a driver of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic diseases, and its impact on patient outcomes.Initial screening of MEDLINE and Embase platforms from 2013–2024 yielded 16,501 unique articles, of which 327 full-text articles were reviewed, generating 123 relevant articles.Several patient attributes and lifestyle factors impact aldosterone levels, including age, race, ethnicity, sex, body mass index, and sodium intake (the effect of sodium varied by race and body weight).Long-term excess aldosterone was associated with elevated blood pressure, cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic diseases, and end-organ damage, leading to greater risk of adverse clinical outcomes and mortality.Aldosterone dysregulation is associated with organ damage (including hypertension, cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic diseases), which may not be adequately addressed by current antihypertensive therapies.

Introduction

Aldosterone is a steroid hormone that contributes to the regulation of sodium reabsorption, water retention, and blood pressure (BP) control [1]. Aldosterone was isolated over 70 years ago, in the early 1950s, and its mechanisms of regulation and action were clarified in the 1960s and 1970s [2]. While subsequent research elucidated the biologic actions of this molecule, more recent studies have established that aldosterone drives a range of common diseases, including nephropathy, cardiomyopathy, and vasculopathy [2].

Aldosterone is a final effector molecule within the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), where it is produced in response to angiotensin II or elevated extracellular potassium levels in the distal nephron of the kidney [3]. It increases the expression of epithelial sodium channels, which facilitate sodium conservation and restore homeostasis [3]. The RAAS is also a key regulator of BP [3]. Aldosterone exerts its effects through both mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) activation and non-MR signaling pathways, which together regulate several aspects of vascular smooth muscle cell pathophysiology, ultimately affecting the overall process of vascular remodeling [3, 4]. Aldosterone dysregulation (excess or autonomous or inappropriate aldosterone production at the source within the adrenal glands) is associated with increased activity of the RAAS, with the suppression of renin, which can lead to increased sodium retention, potassium excretion, and increased BP [3, 5–8].

Aldosterone dysregulation is common in patients with systolic BP (SBP) > 130 mmHg, with an occurrence rate of approximately 30% of patients (in the form of primary aldosteronism) [5]. Higher levels of aldosterone are positively correlated with both an increased risk of hypertension and higher severity of hypertension [5, 9]. Despite treatment with current antihypertensive medications, the prevalence of uncontrolled hypertension remains high and is associated with an increased risk of mortality [10–12]. Moreover, the relationship between increased aldosterone levels and target organ damage [4, 13, 14], including cardiovascular damage and CVD, is well established in earlier and more recent publications and is associated with increased risk of adverse CVD outcomes [15–17]. Furthermore, the increased risk associated with excess aldosterone production is not adequately addressed by current antihypertensive agents [3, 18, 19].

In routine clinical practice, aldosterone dysregulation is an underlying, under-recognized, and therefore often unaddressed driver of uncontrolled hypertension [5, 20–23]. Understanding the clinical burden of aldosterone dysregulation in routine practice is critical to optimizing treatment and improving long-term outcomes in patients with hypertension. This targeted literature review (TLR) was undertaken to examine the burden of aldosterone dysregulation (in the absence of primary aldosteronism) as a driver of disease and its impact on clinical outcomes.

Methods

Review Questions and Search Strategy

The aim of this TLR was to gather pertinent information on various aspects of aldosterone dysregulation, including, but not limited to, epidemiology, economic burden, treatment patterns, and outcomes associated with excess aldosterone. Details of the key research questions and topics are given in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1). The TLR was conducted using the MEDLINE and Embase databases (predefined) and encompassed literature published over a 10-year timeframe between 2013 and 2024. It was anticipated that this timeframe would ensure that the results included recent findings on this evolving topic and that any milestone discoveries pre-2013 would also be included in the more recent studies. The search and selection strategy was predefined and used a patient/population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes framework, as described in Table 1. Additional articles identified from the reference lists of included articles were also screened for inclusion. Key search topics included aldosterone dysregulation, aldosterone excess, resistant hypertension, epidemiology, cardio-renal disease, treatment efficacy, and standard of care. Details of the search strategy (including all search terms used) are given in the Supplemental Digital Content (Tables S2–S5).

While the principal focus of this study was aldosterone dysregulation, data on the RAAS and renin were also included if articles referenced aldosterone production. Articles focused solely on primary aldosteronism were excluded unless they also referenced essential hypertension. The literature search was limited to English language literature. Animal, in vitro, and pediatric articles were excluded.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Review, Data Extraction, and Data Synthesis

Search results were uploaded to a search, screening, and extraction platform, Nested Knowledge®, which employs various methods, including artificial intelligence, to facilitate screening [24]. The predefined criteria for the TLR (Table 1) were added to the screening tool in Nested Knowledge® [24]. Two researchers manually screened a sample of articles (~ 100 titles/abstracts) against predefined criteria to train the tool and establish a baseline for distinguishing relevant from irrelevant articles. An iterative training process was then used to enhance the tool’s accuracy in classifying articles as relevant or irrelevant according to predefined criteria, with a relevance threshold set at 0.5 (i.e., 50% likelihood of relevance). The trained tool was then used to automatically screen and classify the remaining titles/abstracts. All articles identified as relevant and some identified as borderline relevant by the tool, based on their title/abstract, were subsequently reviewed by a human reviewer. A full-text review of all articles considered relevant was undertaken by an experienced human reviewer to confirm their inclusion. Data were extracted from relevant articles and recorded in Excel using a predefined form. Relevant data on aldosterone dysregulation (including reported definitions based on thresholds or cut-offs) were synthesized and reported descriptively.

Results

Search Results

The TLR was conducted in two parts (Tables S2–S5); the initial search yielded 16,501 unique articles (16,483 from MEDLINE and Embase, and 18 additional articles; Fig. 1). Following title/abstract screening, 327 full-text articles were reviewed and yielded 123 relevant articles, which were included in the final reporting of the TLR.

Definitions of Aldosterone Dysregulation and Aldosterone Thresholds Reported in the Literature

Since aldosterone dysregulation exists as a continuum or a spectrum instead of a binary state [5], thresholds or cutoffs are somewhat arbitrary and context-dependent. Nevertheless, a range of aldosterone-related thresholds and measures were used across the studies to characterize patients with aldosterone dysregulation (Table S6). Aldosterone levels were often analyzed using the aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) or plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC). ARR has historically been calculated by dividing the PAC (ng/dl or ng/ml) by plasma renin activity (PRA; ng/dl/h or ng/ml/h) [25]. PRA has also been used to characterize aldosterone levels in the context of renin activity, with participants defined as having either suppressed or unsuppressed renin phenotypes [26, 27]. However, assays measuring plasma renin concentration (PRC), typically measured in ng/dl or mU/l, are progressively replacing PRA as they are faster, methodologically simpler, and more reproducible [25].

Aldosterone levels above a given threshold were sometimes used to indicate a positive test for certain diseases or an increased likelihood of testing positive for those diseases (Table S6) [28–33]. In patients with hypertension, higher serum aldosterone levels (≥ 10 ng/dl) were associated with an 11% increase in CVD risk compared with serum aldosterone levels of < 10 ng/dl in the US-based Framingham Offspring Study (FOS, N = 1433) [31]. In the Chinese Chongqing Primary Aldosteronism Study (CONPASS, N = 1433), patients with primary aldosteronism with an ARR ≥ 1 had a higher CVD risk than patients with essential hypertension and an ARR < 1 (odds ratio [OR] 2.24, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.41–3.55) [34]. In an analysis of FOS and CONPASS cohorts, renin-independent aldosteronism was more closely associated with CVD risk in patients with hypertension than renin-dependent aldosteronism [31]. In the FOS cohort, renin-independent aldosteronism was defined as a serum aldosterone concentration ≥ 10 ng/dl and a PRC ≤ 15 mIU/l, and renin-dependent aldosteronism as a serum aldosterone concentration ≥ 10 ng/dl and a PRC > 15 mIU/l. By applying the conversion factor (Table S7), PRC of 15 mIU/l is equivalent to plasma renin activity of 1.83 ng/ml per hour. In CONPASS, participants with PAC ≥ 10 ng/dl before and after the captopril challenge test (CCT) were classified as having renin-independent aldosteronism, while those with a PAC ≥ 10 ng/dl before the CCT and a PAC < 10 ng/dl after the CCT were classified as having renin-dependent aldosteronism [31].

Patient Attributes

The included articles indicate that patients with aldosterone dysregulation are heterogeneous, and the characteristics used to define them vary. Several patient attributes can impact aldosterone and PRA/PRC in addition to the ARR, including age, race, ethnicity, and sex. However, the strengths of these associations were mixed.

Age appeared to be the only independent determinant of ARR, according to the literature identified in this TLR. In one study (N = 423), a significant increase in ARR with advancing age was largely mediated by an age-related decrease in PRC in a population with essential hypertension [35]. However, the data suggest that age-related changes in PAC were less well defined and more variable [35–37]. Race, ethnicity, and sex appear to interact to affect plasma aldosterone levels and PRA [6, 38–40]. In a longitudinal cohort study (N = 305), black patients had numerically lower PAC than white patients [38]. The results of two studies (N = 305 and N = 525) suggested that black populations had higher SBP and ARR than their white counterparts [6, 38]. Differences in PRA may also be associated with ethnicity—PRA was lower in Nigerian study participants than in their white counterparts in one study (N = 200) [41]. Despite the existence of various independent international studies, a significant geographic influence was not identified. Sex impacts the associations between serum aldosterone levels and PRC and elated comorbidities; in one study (N = 422), there was a significant fall in PAC with age in female patients, with no changes observed in male patients [37]. A modestly higher magnitude of association between serum aldosterone levels and coronary artery calcium scores was also observed in male versus female patients with the suppressed renin phenotype (N = 700) [26]. Higher serum aldosterone concentrations were identified as a stronger risk factor for incident heart failure in male than in female patients (N = 3866) [42]. Interestingly, in a longitudinal study, median serum aldosterone levels were similar between 17-year-old female patients and male patients (n = 871) but were higher in the same female patients at 27 years of age (n = 758). Female patients 17 years of age had significantly lower median PRC versus male patients, and thus a higher ARR [43].

In addition, correlations between higher PAC and higher BMI, weight, adiposity, and obesity were established in seven studies identified in this TLR [44–50]. Lifestyle factors, such as sodium intake and diet, can also impact PAC, but the effects appear to be mixed (i.e., of different magnitudes) and may depend on race and body weight [6, 51–57].

Clinical Burden of Aldosterone Dysregulation

The relevant literature identified in this TLR indicates that long-term excess aldosterone production is associated with elevated BP, cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic illnesses, and end-organ damage, leading to greater risk of adverse cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic outcomes and mortality [23, 26–33, 36, 42, 46, 48, 58–97]. The association between aldosterone levels and cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic illnesses was evaluated across multiple studies (each component had ≥ 8 citations) (Fig. 2).

Number of articles cited by patient conditions

Aldosterone dysregulation was also frequently associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA; 12 citations). Associations between aldosterone dysregulation and liver disease, hypokalemia, and chronic stress were identified less frequently (≤ 5 citations each). No clear trend in reporting the elements of burden by publication year was observed (Fig. 2).

A significant association between aldosterone levels and BP was consistently demonstrated [26, 27, 59, 62]. Aldosterone dysregulation was associated with nocturnal hypertension [62]. In a longitudinal cohort study (N = 6814), there was a positive linear relationship between serum aldosterone concentration and SBP (regression coefficient 2.46, 95% CI 0.81–4.12; p = 0.004) and diastolic BP (DBP) (regression coefficient 1.91, 95% CI 1.06–62.78; p = 0.001) [26]. In the Jackson Heart Study (N = 912), higher log serum aldosterone levels were associated with higher DBP at clinic, awake SBP and DBP, and asleep SBP and DBP (all p < 0.05), even when adjusted for log PRA (all p < 0.05), and 1-unit higher log serum aldosterone levels were associated with higher odds of nocturnal hypertension (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.05–1.81). No association of serum aldosterone or PRA with percent dipping, non-dipping BP pattern, or white-coat hypertension was reported [62].

According to the literature identified in this TLR, aldosterone also induces toxic effects on the heart and kidneys, which may occur independently of the effect on BP (Fig. 3) [4, 13–16].

Clinical outcomes and organ impact associated with aldosterone dysregulation

As previously stated, participants with hypertension in the FOS had a significantly higher risk of CVD with increasing serum aldosterone concentrations (hazard ratio [HR] 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.21) but not with increasing PRC (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.83–1.13) [31]. Participants identified as having renin-independent aldosteronism had a higher risk of CVD than those with serum aldosterone levels < 10 ng/dl (HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.08–1.82), while those identified as having renin-dependent aldosteronism did not show any increased risk of CVD (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.89–1.60) [31]. In contrast to participants in the CONPASS who had PAC < 10 ng/dl, the CVD risk grew with increasing PAC in patients with hypertension who had PRC ≤ 15 mU/l and PAC ≥ 10 ng/dl (OR 1.59, 95% CI 1.10–2.31) or who had CCT-confirmed renin-independent aldosteronism (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.13–5.86). This was not the case among participants who had PRC > 15 mU/l but a PAC ≥ 10 ng/dl (OR 1.43, 95% CI 0.88–2.32) or CCT-confirmed renin-dependent aldosteronism (OR 1.78, 95% CI 0.72–4.41) [31].

In a 2022 observational study (N = 3173), a higher PAC was associated with an increased risk of incident CVD (non-fatal stroke, coronary events, hospitalized heart failure, aortic dissection) and worse clinical outcomes in patients with hypertension [29]. In this study, patients with hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, and a PAC ≥ 12.5 ng/dl had a significantly increased risk of the composite of CVD and all-cause mortality (p < 0.001) compared with participants with a PAC < 12.5 ng/dl [29]. In the Jackson Heart Study, which included 4160 African Americans followed up for a median of ≥ 7 years, higher serum aldosterone levels were associated with an increased CVD risk (including heart failure, coronary heart disease, and stroke) [99]. In the same study, higher serum aldosterone levels were linked to an increased risk of all-cause mortality in an analysis of 4985 participants over a median of 8 years [99]. In patients with coronary artery disease or CKD, increasing plasma/serum aldosterone levels were associated with an increased risk of mortality [14, 15] (Table 3). In the Ludwigshafen Risk and Cardiovascular Health (LURIC) study (N = 3153), a 58% increased risk of CV mortality was observed in patients with the highest quartile PAC compared with those with the lowest quartile PAC [15]. Analysis of specific causes of CVD death revealed that patients with the highest quartile PAC were at increased risk of fatal stroke (HR 7.02, 95% CI 1.79–27.461 p = 0.005) [15].

Aldosterone affects several pathophysiologic processes in the CV system, and high aldosterone levels can lead to increased CV morbidity and mortality [4, 14–16]. At the cardiac level, high PAC can significantly impact left ventricle cardiac remodeling and ultimately affect cardiac function, and it is associated with atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, and ventricular arrhythmias [58, 64]. In a meta‐analysis of 31 studies, the risk of atrial fibrillation was > 3.5‐fold higher in patients with primary aldosteronism (N = 3838) than in those with essential hypertension (N = 9284) after a median of 8.8 years from the diagnosis of hypertension (OR 3.52, 95% CI 2.06–5.99) [16].

Two studies showed that aldosterone dysregulation was associated with increased risk of hypertension (61%) [9] and adverse CVD and kidney disease outcomes, such as an increased risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression (45%) [14]. Furthermore, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at baseline modified the association between serum aldosterone level and CKD progression (p-value for interaction = 0.01); patients with eGFR < 45 ml/min/1.73 m2 had a HR of 1.20 (95% CI 1.12–1.28), whereas those with eGFR ≥ 45 ml/min/1.73 m2 had an HR of 0.99 (95% CI 0.98–1.12) [14]. In an observational study of 3680 participants in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC), a 22% higher risk of all-cause mortality was observed in patients with the highest quartile serum aldosterone levels compared with those with the lowest quartile [14].

The toxic renal effects of high aldosterone include proteinuria, glomerular damage, podocyte loss, oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and nephrosclerosis [98]. According to the literature identified in this TLR, higher plasma/serum aldosterone levels are also associated with OSA [93–96]; patients with resistant hypertension and severe OSA (apnea-hypopnea index > 30 per hour) had twofold higher PAC than those with resistant hypertension and mild OSA [95].

Impact of Pharmacologic and Non-pharmacologic Interventions on Aldosterone Levels

Both pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic interventions have been shown to affect aldosterone levels [6, 36, 51, 100–103]. Restricting sodium intake has been shown to impact PAC, although the effects are mixed and variable, potentially depending on race and body weight [6, 51–57]. Notably, these studies were published before the latest definition of clinical obesity, a systemic condition characterized by excess adiposity that can lead to end-organ damage [104]. Preclinical obesity is associated with excess adiposity with preserved organ/tissue function but an increased risk of clinical obesity and other diseases such as diabetes and CVD [104]. The potential effectiveness of a controlled sodium diet on BP regulation was demonstrated in a study of 525 female patients from the International Hypertensive Pathotype Consortium, but the effect on PAC differed between white and black patients [6]. In a 2017 Cochrane systematic review, a significant increase (p < 0.00001) in aldosterone was observed with low versus high sodium intake, effects that were stable across 125 study populations [105]. A more recent meta-regression analysis of 79 randomized controlled trials revealed a significant increase (p < 0.00001) in PAC with low sodium intake in both patients with hypertension and those with normal BP; however, no association with PAC was identified in those with high sodium intake [53].

In patients with resistant hypertension (N = 1572) on a high-salt diet, BMI was positively associated with urinary sodium and urinary aldosterone levels (p < 0.0001) [45]. This positive correlation was stronger in individuals with obesity than in those of normal weight, but the results were not impacted by either sex or race [45]. In a study of 15 patients with obesity and normal BP, administration of glucose with sodium chloride (NaCl) led to a significant decrease (p = 0.04) in serum aldosterone [52]. After intake of glucose or glucose with NaCl, serum aldosterone was nonetheless significantly higher in patients with versus without obesity (p < 0.001) [52].

Other lifestyle factors, such as aerobic exercise, can also affect aldosterone levels. In a small study of ten patients with untreated hypertension and obesity, 4 weeks of moderate-intensity aerobic training significantly decreased PAC, independently of body weight (p ≤ 0.05) [103]. While no data on the impact of sleep and sleep-related breathing disorders on aldosterone levels and BP were reported in the TLR, several early studies showed that aldosterone levels affected OSA severity, as described in two recent reviews identified in this TLR [106, 107] and confirmed in TLR articles [93–96]. Patients with obesity often have OSA, which has been implicated in the development of hypertension [106, 107].

Current pharmacologic treatments for hypertension, including the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), and β-blockers, can reduce aldosterone levels, although levels remained high in some patients (Table 4) [36, 100, 108]. In an analysis of patients in the BIOlogy study to Tailored Treatment in Chronic Heart Failure (BIOSTAT-CHF study, N = 2039), optimal ACEi or ARB dosing was associated with lower aldosterone levels compared with suboptimal dosing, but levels remained elevated in some patients [36]. Aldosterone breakthrough, where serum aldosterone levels remain elevated despite standard of care treatment, was reported in 10–53% of patients with CHF, CKD, or hypertension who received long-term treatment with an ACEi or ARB [20]. The BP-lowering effects of the β-blocker bisoprolol were not associated with baseline plasma aldosterone in patients with primary hypertension, but after 6 weeks of treatment, PAC levels were significantly reduced (p = 0.017) [108]. While mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists have proven BP-lowering efficacy in patients with resistant hypertension, they have been linked with increased levels of aldosterone, and evidence suggests that their utilization in clinical practice is low, ranging from 15 to 27% in patients with resistant hypertension [100, 109, 110]. A retrospective post hoc analysis found preliminary evidence that patients with heart failure (n = 47) have high circulating levels of aldosterone and large transrenal RAAS gradients [63]. When treated with dobutamine or sodium nitroprusside, to preserve cardiac output and end-organ function, transrenal aldosterone gradients were reduced (p = 0.005, p = 0.030, respectively) [63].

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first TLR undertaken to examine the clinical burden of aldosterone dysregulation and its impact on clinical outcomes. The use of the TLR approach provided flexibility in search strategies, allowing a more detailed analysis and the synthesis of select evidence that aligns with the study's objectives [111]. The studies included in this TLR focused mainly on the negative impact of aldosterone dysregulation on various target organs, such as the heart and kidneys. Additionally, a single study examined the association between aldosterone levels and the risk of liver disease [30]. Some research indicates that these negative effects may be independent of BP levels, but further evidence is required [4, 13–16]. The relationship between aldosterone levels and patient outcomes was not always clear, and the aldosterone thresholds used to determine outcome risk were found to vary among studies. Various patient attributes, including age, race, ethnicity, sex, weight/BMI, and diet, also appeared to impact aldosterone levels; however, some findings were mixed (varying strength of aldosterone's association with age, race, ethnicity, and sex and varying magnitude/direction of influence with age and diet)[6, 35, 37–40, 44–57], suggesting that additional studies are needed.

Literature collated in this TLR demonstrated that elevated aldosterone levels are associated with hypertension, as well as CV, kidney, and metabolic diseases [23, 26–29, 31–33, 36, 42, 48, 58–89, 93, 97]. There was also no evidence that aldosterone dysregulation can be managed fully with currently available antihypertensive therapies [20, 100]. Epidemiologic data on aldosterone dysregulation are limited, as typically patients do not undergo screening until they display symptoms of primary aldosteronism, and many often do not undergo screening for primary aldosteronism, despite recommendations in the treatment guidelines [100, 112–118]. None of the studies identified in this TLR characterized the economic burden of aldosterone dysregulation.

Aldosterone dysregulation was associated with hypertension (including resistant hypertension), heart failure, atherosclerosis, arterial stiffness, cardiac remodeling, and stroke, with a higher risk of cardiac-related mortality, as reported in 37 articles [23, 26–29, 31–33, 36, 42, 58–84]. An association between aldosterone levels and kidney function was reported in ten articles, with higher aldosterone levels linked to adverse clinical outcomes in patients with kidney disease, for example [42, 48, 59, 69, 83, 85–89]. Among patients with hypertension, elevated aldosterone levels were found to be associated with the severity of OSA (five articles) [29, 93–96], lower cerebral blood flow (one article) [60], and new onset metabolic-associated or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, showing a non-linear relationship (one article) [30]. Higher aldosterone (serum aldosterone, ARR) has been associated with insulin resistance, increased risk of diabetes, and worse outcomes (diabetic nephropathy) in patients with diabetes (four articles) [46, 61, 90, 92]. However, a more recent study reported no significant association of plasma aldosterone with the risk of incident diabetes [91]. Three studies reported a strong positive association of aldosterone (serum and plasma) with metabolic syndrome and changes in the risk components of metabolic syndrome [66, 93, 97].

Certain ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) profiles are associated with increased CVD risk, including nocturnal hypertension [62]. The findings from this TLR provide consistent evidence for an association between aldosterone levels and BP (four articles)[26, 27, 59, 62], in particular, nocturnal hypertension [62]. This is also supported by data from a small 24-h ABPM study that did not meet the inclusion criteria for this TLR (because it included patients with primary aldosteronism), which demonstrated an association between high PAC and a low nocturnal BP dipping rate. The study also suggested that adrenocorticotropic hormone stimulation may improve PAC sensitivity as a clinical indicator of nocturnal hypertension [119].

Despite extensive data on aldosterone across cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic diseases, it remains unclear whether aldosterone levels are a causal driver or an effect of these diseases. While there is some evidence for a BP-independent link between aldosterone and elevated BP, this only partially implies causality of aldosterone, and further evidence is needed. One recent study by Inoue et al. (2024) used a two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to identify a causal relationship with primary aldosteronism and coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, and stroke in East Asian and European populations. [120] However, this recent publication was not included in this TLR because it was outside the review timeframe. The studies in this review were not designed to assess causality, and therefore associations reported here should not be interpreted as evidence of causality for aldosterone in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic diseases. Interpretation of the review data may be confounded by an emerging understanding that aldosterone dysregulation may be characterized according to its physiologic drivers, such that renin-independent aldosterone production represents ‘primary aldosteronism’ and renin-dependent aldosterone production or aldosterone dysregulation represents ‘secondary aldosteronism’ [7, 121, 122]. The interpretation of future studies would be enhanced by inclusion of these thoughts, which are used in clinical practice. A key strength of this study was the long, 10-year timeframe. However, it had with several potential limitations, including missing earlier, potentially important articles due to the screening window, although it was assumed that any key studies/milestones published > 10 years ago would be summarized in the more recent publications identified in the TLR. Also, the database search was limited to English language articles and included only publications from the MEDLINE or Embase databases. The search for specific topics may have led to the omission of potentially important information on treatments and outcomes. For instance, no studies of the non-steroidal MR antagonist finerenone were identified from our search as no studies during the review period (2013–2024) reported direct levels or changes in aldosterone when finerenone was administered to humans. While the impact of finerenone on cardiorenal outcomes has been well established in several breakthrough trials [123–125], these are not covered by the search parameters of this review. However, a randomized controlled trial by Hu et al. (2025) demonstrated compensatory increases in aldosterone levels in patients with primary aldosteronism following treatment with finerenone (20 mg or 40 mg) or spironolactone (20 mg or 40 mg) with a reported increase of 79.5 pg/ml (95% CI 35.5–145.0, n = 30) and 22.0 pg/ml (95% CI − 3.0 to 91.4, n = 29) in upright PAC from baseline, respectively [126]. Both non-steroidal and steroidal MR antagonists increased aldosterone levels, with a greater increase observed with finerenone.

There are also limitations associated with using an electronic screening tool, such as a dependence on key words, which may have failed to capture subtle differences in study design and data interpretation; however, the conservative relevance threshold of 0.5, rather than the standard threshold of 0.8, should have mitigated this. Furthermore, the initial search used a broad search string, which returned > 16,000 results. No direct comparisons can be made between studies of aldosterone dysregulation, as data are reported in patients with varying disease severities and multiple comorbidities, and many studies used different outcome measures and time points. Finally, while the study employed a two-pass screening process, with titles and abstracts initially reviewed using an iteratively trained electronic tool, only a single, experienced reviewer undertook the full text review and decided whether an article was relevant or not; there was no subsequent review/validation for relevance by a second reviewer, which could have introduced some selection bias. Moreover, several included studies had small sample sizes, potentially increasing the risk of bias in the reported findings. However, the risk of bias was not formally assessed, as the TLR's goal was to consolidate existing evidence.

Conclusion

This TLR reports on the findings that aldosterone dysregulation (excess or autonomous or inappropriate aldosterone production at the source within adrenal glands) is associated with diseases linked to organ damage (including hypertension, CV, kidney, and metabolic diseases) and adverse patient outcomes [23, 26–29, 31–33, 36, 42, 48, 58–89, 93, 97]. The clinical challenges linked to aldosterone dysregulation highlighted in this TLR underscore the need for effective therapeutic options to enhance patient protection against the adverse outcomes associated with aldosterone dysregulation. Further research is therefore essential to investigate the clinical significance of aldosterone dysregulation, both in cases associated with hypertension and in those with blood pressure within normal ranges. Research into 24-h BP profiles, particularly nocturnal dipping rates, in patients with hypertension and suspected aldosterone dysregulation could provide a more comprehensive picture of BP variability throughout the day/night, enabling risk stratification and more targeted treatment strategies. Additional research is also required to provide further epidemiologic data on aldosterone dysregulation in patients with hypertension.
