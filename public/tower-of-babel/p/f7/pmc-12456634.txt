The effects of tourniquet deflation on optic nerve sheath diameter in lower limb orthopaedic surgeries
Authors: Saraswathi Nagappa, Riya Ravindran, Yathish Sarthavalli Krishnamurthy, Chandrakala Chandrashekar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Pneumatic tourniquets are used in orthopedic surgeries to provide a bloodless surgical area and reduce blood loss. Tourniquet inflation causes metabolic changes, leading to cerebral vasodilatation and increased intracranial pressure (ICP). A new noninvasive method to measure optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) via ultrasonography to detect the raised ICP is reliable, inexpensive, and can be repeated many times.Materials and Methods:In this prospective observational study, 23 patients aged between 16 and 60 years, undergoing elective lower limb orthopedic surgeries under spinal anesthesia were included. After pre-anesthetic evaluation and obtaining informed written consent, an ocular ultrasound was performed to measure baseline ONSD in both eyes. Spinal anesthesia was administered and A pneumatic thigh tourniquet was applied as close as possible to the limb root and inflated to 150 mmHg–250 mmHg above the systolic blood pressure. A total of 5 measurements of ONSD were taken by the investigator (anesthetist): before giving spinal anesthesia, after giving spinal anesthesia, before tourniquet deflation, within 5 min, and after 10 min of tourniquet deflation.Results:In our study, the mean age of subjects was 34.57 ± 13.5 years. The majority were males (83%). The subjects showed an increase in ONSD after pneumatic tourniquet deflation in comparison with the baseline values, in the left eye [0.543 cm ± 0.021, P < 0.001] and the right eye [0.549 cm ± 0.021, P < 0.001], which were statistically significant.Conclusion:The impact of pneumatic tourniquet deflation on ONSD measurements recorded by ultrasound in lower limb orthopedic surgeries was significant and this change occurred with a simultaneous increase in end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2).

Introduction

Pneumatic tourniquets have frequently been used in orthopedic surgeries to provide a bloodless surgical area and reduce blood loss via wrapping around the extremities. While there are many advantages to tourniquet applications, hemodynamic or metabolic changes may be observed in the inflation and deflation of the tourniquet.[1] During the period of tourniquet ischemia, the concerned limb depends on anaerobic metabolism brought about by interrupting the arterial blood supply that carries oxygen. Therefore, the anaerobic metabolites accumulate which, will enter the general circulation resulting in reperfusion syndrome on release of the tourniquet.[2] When the tourniquet is inflated, metabolic changes such as increases in carbon dioxide (CO2), lactic acid, and potassium and reductions in PaO2 and pH are observed in the ischemic extremity. These ischemic products are then secreted into the general circulation following deflation of the tourniquet.[3] Increased PaCO2 leads to cerebral vasodilatation and intracranial pressure (ICP).[4] Although this increase in cerebral blood flow (CBF) is compensated and tolerated in most healthy patients, the increase in cerebral blood volume may cause an increase in ICP in patients with decreased intracranial compliance. Increased intracranial pressure can induce deleterious outcomes, including cerebral ischemia. Therefore, cautious monitoring and thorough management of the ICP is required in patients undergoing lower limb surgery, who have or are at high risk of cerebrovascular disease, using a pneumatic tourniquet.[5]

The current gold standard measurement of detecting raised intracranial pressure is through an invasive procedure by placement of an intracranial catheter into the ventricles or the cerebral parenchyma. Intracranial pressure can be definitively measured and monitored through devices such as an intraparenchymal catheter or an external ventricular drain (EVD) catheter. Bedside sonographic measurement of optic nerve sheath diameter is emerging as a noninvasive technique to detect elevated ICP. Increased ICP is transmitted to the subarachnoid space surrounding the optic nerve, causing optic nerve sheath expansion, and the expansion of this cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space can easily be detected using ultrasound.[6]

This study aims to investigate alterations in the ICP using ultrasonography to measure the ONSD after deflation of the pneumatic tourniquet in patients undergoing lower limb surgery.

Materials and Methods

The study design was a prospective observational study. After obtaining Institutional ethics clearance [CTRI/2024/07/07007106/069423] and informed consent, the study was conducted on 23 patients between August 2023 to February 2024 at our medical college and Research Institute. The demographic data (age, weight, height) and american society of anesthesiologist (ASA) status were noted, and patients were taken into a single group of patients undergoing lower limb orthopedic surgeries under spinal anesthesia. The study’s objectives were to study the impact of pneumatic tourniquet deflation on optic nerve sheath diameter in patients undergoing lower limb surgeries under spinal anesthesia and to correlate the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) change with change in end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2) after tourniquet deflation. Inclusion Criteria include Age between 18 to 60 years, patient willing to give informed consent, ASA grade I and II, and patients undergoing lower orthopedic surgeries with pneumatic tourniquets. Exclusion Criteria were patients with Neuritis, Glaucoma, Optic nerve tumor, Orbital trauma, fracture, and any space-occupying lesion in the brain. After obtaining approval and clearance from the institutional ethics committee, the patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria were enrolled for the study after obtaining informed consent. Routine pre-anesthetic evaluation was done and the patients kept nil per oral for 8 hours before surgery. On arrival at the operation theatre standard anesthesia monitors including non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), electrocardiogram (ECG) pulse oximetry attached, and baseline hemodynamic parameters like heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) and oxygen saturation (SPO2) noted. 18 G intravenous (IV) cannula secured and Ringer lactate started. Spinal anesthesia was administered with 15 mg hyperbaric bupivacaine through 25 Gauge Quincke’s spinal needles. After ensuring sufficient motor and sensory block, tourniquet cuffs were applied to the patients. A pneumatic thigh tourniquet was used as close as possible to the limb root and inflated to 150 mmHg–250 mmHg above the systolic blood pressure. The time the tourniquet inflated was recorded on the perioperative anesthesia follow-up sheet. Measures were taken to ensure that the tourniquet time did not exceed 120 min.

During the examination, the patient was made to lie supine with eyes closed and Tegaderm will be applied to the closed eyelid. Ultrasound imaging of the ONSD was done using a single ultrasound machine’s 6 Hz–13 Hz linear probe (Sonosite). The copious gel was applied to the probe placed on the superior and lateral aspects of the orbit and moved gently from the temporal to the nasal side until the optic nerve was visualized as a linear hypoechoic structure with clearly defined margins posterior to the globe. The images were obtained in the transverse plane. ONSD is measured in both eyes and 3 mm behind the optic globe. A total of 5 measurements were performed by the investigator (anesthetist): before giving spinal anesthesia, after giving spinal anesthesia, before tourniquet deflation, within 5 min, and after 10 min of tourniquet deflation. EtCO2 and MAP values were also measured and noted in the same time intervals.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2016 and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0. (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). To describe the data descriptive statistics frequency analysis, and percentage analysis were used for categorical variables and the mean and SD were used for continuous variables. To find the significant difference in the multivariate analysis for repeated measures the Repeated measures of analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used with Bonferroni correction to control the type I error on multiple comparisons. In all the above statistical tools the probability value of. 05 is considered a significant level.

Discussion

Our study examined the effects of pneumatic tourniquet deflation on ONSD in patients undergoing lower limb orthopedic surgeries under spinal anesthesia. The findings revealed a statistically significant increase in ONSD following tourniquet deflation, accompanied by a concurrent rise in ETCO2 levels. These observations align with previous studies by Besir and Tugcugil,[8] and Cebeci and Canakci,[9] which reported similar increases in ONSD and ICP following tourniquet deflation in orthopedic surgeries. Additionally, our results are consistent with findings by Song et al.,[10] who demonstrated hemodynamic and cerebral oxygenation changes associated with tourniquet deflation in aged patients undergoing knee replacement surgery.

The physiological changes observed in our study can be attributed to the ischemic and metabolic effects induced by pneumatic tourniquet application. During tourniquet inflation, ischemic metabolites such as carbon dioxide, lactic acid, and potassium accumulate in the occluded limb. Upon tourniquet release, these metabolites enter systemic circulation, leading to transient hypercapnia, increased cerebral blood flow, and subsequent elevations in ICP. This phenomenon has been previously documented in studies investigating changes in ETCO2 and arterial blood gas levels after tourniquet release.[11]

ONSD is a well-established, non-invasive surrogate marker for ICP. The optic nerve shares a direct anatomical connection with the brain’s chiasmatic cistern through its subarachnoid space, allowing cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics to influence the optic nerve sheath. Elevated ICP results in CSF displacement into the perineural subarachnoid space, causing dural sheath distension and an increase in ONSD. This mechanism has been validated in experimental studies, including those by Hansen and Helmke,[12] as well as clinical studies investigating ONSD in neurocritical ill patients.[13]

Previous studies have highlighted the reliability of ONSD measurements in detecting elevated ICP. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Dubourg et al.[14] and Ohle et al.[15] confirmed the accuracy of ultrasound-guided ONSD assessment in predicting increased ICP when compared to computed tomography (CT). Additionally, Geeraerts et al.[16] demonstrated the utility of ocular sonography as a non-invasive tool for ICP assessment in neurocritical care patients. Furthermore, studies such as those by Abegao Pinto et al.[17] have reported correlations between intraocular pressure and ONSD, reinforcing its role in intracranial pressure monitoring.

The choice of anesthetic agents may also play a role in ONSD variations. Geng et al.[18] found that the use of sevoflurane and propofol influenced ONSD measurements in laparoscopic gynecological surgery. While our study focused on spinal anesthesia, future research could explore the comparative effects of different anesthetic techniques on ICP-related changes in ONSD. Given the reliability and non-invasive nature of ultrasound-guided ONSD measurements, incorporating this technique into perioperative monitoring may provide anesthesiologists and surgeons with valuable insights into intracranial pressure fluctuations. This could aid in early detection and management of elevated ICP, thereby enhancing patient safety and optimizing surgical outcomes.

Conclusion

The impact of pneumatic tourniquet deflation on ONSD measurements recorded by ultrasound in lower limb orthopedic surgeries was significant. These changes were accompanied by a concurrent increase in ETCO2, suggesting a physiological link between tourniquet release, systemic metabolic alterations, and intracranial pressure fluctuations. Incorporating ONSD monitoring into perioperative care may enhance the assessment of intracranial pressure dynamics, contributing to improved patient safety and surgical outcomes.
