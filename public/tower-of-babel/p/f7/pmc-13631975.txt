Use of multiple sclerosis drugs among pregnant individuals with live-birth deliveries in the FDA’s Sentinel Distributed Database
Authors: Danijela Stojanovic, Lockwood Taylor, Mayura Shinde, Nicole Haug, Mohamed Mohamoud, Leyla Sahin, Amanda Carruth, Rebecca Hawrusik, Jennifer G. Lyons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdministrative claims databases may complement pregnancy registries for post-marketing utilization assessment and safety surveillance.ObjectiveTo assess patterns of utilization of disease-modifying therapies (DMT) indicated for treatment of multiple sclerosis among women with live-birth deliveries across pregnancy and over time.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective administrative claims-based utilization analysis from January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2020 in the Sentinel Distributed Database. We described use across the pregnancy period and over time among pregnancies ending in a live-birth delivery and matched non-pregnant episodes.ResultsAmong 2,142,706 pregnancies resulting in live-birth deliveries, 1,362 pregnancies were exposed to DMTs (< 0.1%). Glatiramer acetate was the most prevalent DMT any time in pregnancy (48.9%), followed by interferon beta 1a (22.4%), dimethyl fumarate (10.7%), and natalizumab (8.4%). The highest use was observed six months prior to pregnancy start, while the lowest use was in the second trimester.ConclusionsIn our analysis, exposure to DMT was low and varied across the pregnancy period. Use among pregnant patients was highest in the six months prior to pregnancy start and remained lower than pre-pregnancy levels after delivery.

Background

Evidence on drug safety in pregnancy often comes from post-marketing studies [1] given historical exclusion of pregnant individuals from clinical trials. If the treatment target population includes females of reproductive potential, post-marketing studies typically include product-specific or multi product disease-based pregnancy registries [2, 3]. Although pregnancy registries remain the first line of evidence in assessing the safety of new drugs in pregnancy, there has been increased use of administrative claims databases and descriptive pregnancy safety studies to capture use in routine care [1].

Over the past decade, safety issues related to pregnancy outcomes have become a concern specifically for patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) because time-to-MS diagnosis and time-to-treatment have significantly decreased while pregnancy rates among individuals with MS have increased [4, 5]. For many individuals with MS, concerns still exist regarding how to safely and effectively treat their condition during the pre-pregnancy, pregnancy, and postpartum periods [6, 7]. In the United States, the current standard of care is to discontinue most disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) before or as soon as pregnancy is identified [6]. However, some individuals may need to remain on treatment during pregnancy to manage their disease. Additionally, as approximately 45% of pregnancies in the United States are unintended, exposure to DMTs in early pregnancy still occurs [8]. The purpose of this analysis is to use the Sentinel Distributed Database (SDD) to assess the patterns of DMT use before, during, and after pregnancy among pregnant individuals with live birth deliveries.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective administrative claims-based analysis using data from the SDD from January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2020. Briefly, the Sentinel System was launched in 2016 by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for active surveillance of medical products [9]. The Sentinel System is a curated data source, comprised of U.S. based Data Partners including large national insurers, integrated delivery care networks, and Medicare fee-for-service, which contribute medical and outpatient pharmacy administrative claims and Electronic Health Record (EHR) data. In the Sentinel System, each Data Partner maintains information on their enrollees and follows the Sentinel Common Data Model, which includes data on enrollment, demographics, medical encounters, diagnoses and procedures, death, outpatient pharmacy dispensing, laboratory test results, and vital signs [10]. Data from six Data Partners contributed to this analysis. Data Partners included large, national, private insurers; regional, integrated delivery systems; and one state-level Medicaid insurer.

We identified singleton, live-birth deliveries among pregnant individuals age 10–49 years using a previously validated algorithm based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis codes indicative of weeks of gestation, and International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) and ICD-10-CM diagnosis or procedures codes for preterm (prior to 37 weeks gestation) and post-term (after 42 weeks gestation) deliveries [11]. Completed pregnancies were assigned a delivery date and pregnancy start was estimated based on these diagnosis and procedure codes. Trimester periods were estimated with first trimester included days 0 to 90 of gestation, the second trimester included days 91 to 180, and the third trimester included days 181 through the day of the hospital admission for live-birth delivery.

Eligible pregnant individuals were required to be continuously enrolled in plans with medical and drug coverage during the entire pregnancy duration plus 183 days prior to estimated pregnancy start date and 183 days after their delivery date, with gaps in coverage of up to 45 days. Each pregnancy was matched 1:1 with replacement, by age (in years) and health plan enrollment status within each Data Partner to a non-pregnant episode from a female health plan member; individuals could contribute multiple pregnancies and non-pregnant episodes across the study period. Valid non-pregnant episodes were defined as periods of health plan enrollment meeting the inclusion criteria defined above with no evidence of a pregnancy. We evaluated baseline demographic and clinical characteristics using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes [12] and identified pregnancies and non-pregnant episodes with a dispensing of any DMT using National Drug Codes (NDC) or procedure codes for injectable drugs. Exposure was assessed as a dispensing with days’ supply from outpatient pharmacy dispensing records that overlapped the specified periods (183 − 91 days and 90 − 1 day) before pregnancy, during pregnancy by trimester, and same time periods after delivery. For pregnancies, we defined trimesters based on the estimated pregnancy start; for matched non-pregnant episodes trimesters were defined based on the matched non-pregnant index date. For each pregnancy period, we estimated the number of exposed pregnancies and calculated the DMT use percent change between different periods. We described use of these products over time and by age group (data not shown).

This analysis used Sentinel distributed analytic tools, including the Cohort Identification and Descriptive Analysis (CIDA) module version 10.2.0 with custom programming. Documentation of the Sentinel tools are publicly available (https://dev.sentinelsystem.org/projects/SENTINEL/repos/sentinel-routine-querying-tool-documentation/browse/files). This Sentinel project is a public health surveillance activity conducted under the authority of the Food and Drug Administration and, accordingly, is not subject to Institutional Review Board oversight.

Results

We identified 2,142,706 pregnant patients with 2,630,485 pregnancies resulting in live-birth deliveries (Table 1). The baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of pregnant and non-pregnant matched cohort in the SDD were comparable. Few pregnancies were exposed to DMTs (1,362; 0.05%) while three times as many in the matched non-pregnant cohort had evidence of DMT exposure (3,875, 0.15%).

Glatiramer acetate was the most prevalent DMT any time in pregnancy (48.9%), followed by interferon beta 1a (IFN beta-1a, 22.4%), dimethyl fumarate (10.7%), and natalizumab (8.4%). In the matched non-pregnant cohort, the exposure to DMTs overall was higher than in the pregnant cohort (3,875; 0.15%). The most prevalent DMTs were glatiramer acetate (31.2%), interferon beta-1a (29.3%), dimethyl fumarate (14.7%), and natalizumab (11.3%; Table 2). DMT use varied across the pregnancy period. Compared to the pre-pregnancy period, the proportion of pregnancies exposed to DMTs decreased during 1st trimester, remained low during the 2nd and 3rd trimester, and increased again in the post-delivery period. From 183 − 91 days pre-pregnancy through 1st trimester, DMT use decreased by 29% and from 1st to 2nd trimester DMT use decreased by 67%. From 3rd trimester to 90 days post-pregnancy, DMT use increased by 204% and continued to increase to almost pre-pregnancy levels. In each period, glatiramer acetate was the most dispensed DMTs (Fig. 1).

DMT utilization increased over time, with the lowest observed use for both cohorts at the beginning of the study period in 2001 (4[0.02%] pregnancies and 22[0.09%] matched non-pregnant episodes). Among the pregnant cohort, the highest observed use was in 2017 with 153 (0.07%) exposed pregnancies, while the highest observed use among the matched non-pregnant cohort was in 2015 with 365 (0.17%) exposed non-pregnant episodes (Fig. 2). Glatiramer acetate remained the most prevalent DMT from 2008 through the end of the study period, followed by interferon beta-1a and dimethyl fumarate (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In our study of more than 2.6 million pregnancies resulting in live-birth deliveries matched 1:1 to non-pregnant episodes exposure to DMTs overall was low, though increasing over time. Use of DMTs was highest among the non-pregnant cohort during all time periods. Among the pregnant cohort the prevalence of DMT use was highest during the 183 − 91 days pre-pregnancy, decreased during pregnancy, and increased close to pre-pregnancy levels following live-birth deliveries, similar to findings in other studies [12–14]. Use of all DMTs was increasing from 2001 to 2020, similar to observed trends of specific DMTs [15, 16].

There are several possible explanations for the observed DMT patterns of use across different pregnancy periods. First, low DMT use in pregnancy may reflect current prescribing guidelines which call for discontinuation of treatment on confirmation of unintended pregnancy or when a woman is trying to conceive. Our study may have captured pregnant individuals with substantial or highly active MS who may benefit from continued DMT treatment, which may outweigh the unknown risk to the fetus [6, 17]. Second, decreased DMT use in pregnancy could be related to decreased MS disease activity during pregnancy [12, 18]. Third, the most used DMTs, glatiramer acetate and interferon beta-1a, are recommended MS treatments until pregnancy is confirmed and continued only if the benefits outweigh the risks. It is possible that pregnant individuals in our cohort were prescribed these DMTs because they have been on the market longer with no identified safety issues and limited evidence for newer DMTs. Lastly, our findings indicate that DMTs are resumed after live-birth deliveries. Given the significant increase in the MS relapse rates following deliveries [12], the choice about whether to resume DMT treatment immediately after delivery is a shared decision between patient and provider, with consideration for patient preferences and disease activity.

This study has several strengths. The Sentinel Distributed Database contains a large number of pregnancies, increasing the precision and validity of our utilization estimates. Further, we identified users of DMTs without respect to a documented multiple sclerosis diagnosis in the health record, potentially including those pregnancies among patients with multiple sclerosis that simply did not have a code during the study period, but had a dispensing or procedure for DMTs.

This study also has several limitations. Although the Sentinel Distributed Database is a large distributed database of eligible pregnancies covered by both private and public insurers across the United States, the current analysis includes Medicaid data from a single state, limiting generalizability to all pregnant populations, as approximately 50% of U.S. pregnancies are insured by Medicaid [19]. Additionally, we estimated pregnancy start date based on a validated claim-based algorithm, so pregnancy start and timing of the trimesters may have been misclassified. As is common with health insurance claims data, information on medication use is based on outpatient pharmacy data and documents the fact that an insurance provider was billed for a prescription, but does not necessarily directly correspond to medication use. This may lead to misclassification of use during pregnancy, especially in the first trimester, where patients may not be aware of their pregnancy status and continue to pick up their medications from the pharmacy. Further, the days’ supplied was used to calculate periods of exposure, which may be misclassified if a patient does not take the medication as prescribed. Also, several DMTs for MS have been approved recently and are not captured in our study due to limited market uptake. Finally, our study captures DMT exposure among pregnant individuals with live-birth deliveries only, and not among pregnant individuals with non live-birth outcomes such as miscarriage or stillbirth.

Conclusion

In this study of pregnant patients dispensed disease-modifying therapies intended to treat multiple sclerosis, we found low use at any point in the six months before pregnancy through six months after pregnancy, compared to a cohort of non-pregnant women. Use was highest prior to pregnancy start, with a decrease in the first and second trimester. However, use of DMTs among pregnant individuals remained lower than that of non-pregnant individuals even in the six months after delivery. Importantly, observed use never matched that of the pre-pregnancy period. The observed pattern of DMT use indicates that pregnant individuals may discontinue treatment prior to becoming pregnant and avoid restarting until many months after delivery.
