Ethical Issues Conducting Research During War and Violent Conflict: A Review
Authors: Dónal O'Mathúna, Emily E Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlobal health engagement increasingly occurs in the context of war and violent conflict. Many health‐related decisions are made which should be guided by evidence. Health research is needed to provide evidence, yet conducting research in conflict settings raises multiple ethical issues. However, the ethical issues involved in conducting research during war and violent conflict have received insufficient attention. The literature suggests that these ethical issues, while similar to those of research conducted in humanitarian crises, are also distinct. This article provides a review of the ethical issues faced by researchers in conflict zones with a goal to inform future research into such issues. The review was organized around an established 8‐principle ethics framework for research in low‐income settings. We conclude with a number of recommendations and call for additional attention by bioethicists to this important area to protect participants and support researchers in conflict settings, whether coming from military or nonmilitary organizations.Keywords: military ethics, research ethics, researcher support, violent conflict, war

Introduction

Global health engagement increasingly occurs during war and violent conflict, raising many different ethical issues for military and nonmilitary actors. This article reviews ethical issues arising in health research conducted in conflict settings, defined broadly to include conventional warfare, modern asymmetric conflicts, political violence or gang‐related criminality. Similar ethical challenges arise regardless of the nature and scale of the conflict.

Conducting research during military engagements and conflict has been questioned, with some even arguing that research should wait until the fighting stops [1]. Concerns include the challenges of adhering to methodological rigor, and whether research is ethically justified given the level of risk to participants and the confidentiality of their data in insecure settings [2]. We start from the argument that while there are concerns about intersectional vulnerabilities of research participants, conducting health research during armed conflict is an ethical imperative [3]. Indeed, “Fundamentally, war is about health” [4]. People are killed and injured during war, requiring treatment and rehabilitation; chronic pain and mental health difficulties impact combatants and civilians, sometimes for many years. Health‐related decisions made during war require evidence, yet a fairly weak evidence base exists for many interventions aimed at improving health during conflict [2]. A critical need exists for research to both document and understand the health effects of war on individuals and populations [4]. If research waits “until the guns fall silent, knowledge and understanding tend to be stuck at the pre‐war level” [1]. Rigorous research data should be collected on the efficacy of humanitarian interventions and policies, including services for military personnel and noncombatants [5, 6, 7]. Additionally, interventions to address physical injuries and psychological trauma may not be as effective during war, or may need to be adapted, requiring further research [8, 9, 10].

Research studies already in progress before war and violence erupt trigger ethical questions about whether and how research should continue. In low‐resource settings, clinical research can be a critical source of care for patients with chronic or terminal diseases [11, 12]. Stopping clinical research may cause significant harm to individuals receiving care within research contexts [13, 14]. If research is suspended or discontinued, what ethical responsibilities exist towards enrolled participants? But if research continues, war likely will affect the balance of risks and potential benefits which requires ongoing ethics assessments. Yet needed real‐time support can be lacking [15]. How those ethical reassessments and ethics reviews can best be carried out during war raises further questions.

History shows that health research during war has produced many benefits. These include progress with public hygiene during ancient Roman military campaigns, developing anesthesia during the US Civil War, introducing penicillin during World War II, and more recently, advancing understanding of and interventions for post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [16]. Rigorous research can be conducted during war, despite methodological and ethical challenges [17]. A shift towards evidence‐based programming in humanitarian settings, including those involving conflict, is happening increasingly [18]. To take just one example, alleviating the burden of mental health crises among those affected by armed conflicts requires a strong evidence base [19]. High quality research is critically needed (i) to document and understand the health effects of war on mental health; (ii) to inform development and testing of interventions to promote mental health; and (iii) to validate models of service delivery [2, 6]. However, conducting research is not generally central to humanitarian organizations’ missions, with a few notable exceptions. (For example, see the International Committee of the Red Cross’ Center for Operational Research (CORE) (https://www.icrc.org/en/operational-research) or Médecins sans Frontières (MSF) Science Portal (scienceportal. msf. org).) A key reason for hesitancy towards research is concerns about how to address the ethical issues such research raises [20].

Specific ethics guidance for implementing research in conflict settings is thin [21], and discussions of ethical challenges and how to resolve them remain sparse compared with other research topics and settings – even research in natural disasters or pandemics [5]. In this paper we review some of the ethical challenges with research in conflict settings with a goal of encouraging further research into understanding these challenges and how they can be addressed by researchers and those providing ethical review and oversight for such research, ideally in collaboration with members of the communities where research takes place.

Research Ethics During War

Despite the impacts of war on health, bioethics as a discipline has been slow to consider ethics during war and conflict [22]. Even when bioethics has examined warfare and health ethics, it has been “in a limited fashion, highlighting mostly obligations of physicians to patients” [23]. Even as war zone research has increased, examination of its ethical challenges has lagged behind [1, 23]. A particular need exists to address research ethics applied to settings of war and violent conflict.

Ethical considerations for research during war are similar to research in other humanitarian emergencies, yet also have their distinctive characteristics [24, 25]. However, literature on the topic is limited. For example, a 2020 systematic literature review of research ethics in humanitarian settings included 52 publications, yet only nine specifically addressed research in armed conflict [5]. While the research ethics literature has given some attention to issues arising in research conducted with soldiers, both military and civilian organizations also conduct research that enrolls civilians and the ethical considerations for civilian participants may differ from those for soldiers.

Distinct ethical considerations for health research conducted during war are varied and have been discussed primarily in expert commentaries and case studies. Clinical trials to address public health needs caused by war raise concerns about double standards and how informed consent is ensured [26]. War creates unique participant vulnerabilities that require additional safeguards, including vulnerabilities from experiencing or witnessing violence (including sexual violence), multiple types of losses, and short‐ and long‐term displacement [5, 27]. War refugees differ from other refugees in ways that influence their ability to provide voluntary informed consent and their trust in researchers [28]. War creates ongoing threats to the safety of researchers, participants, and local communities [8]. Research during war may trigger broader risks, such as compromising humanitarian action or threatening neutrality and impartiality [7]. In conflict zones, “even the simple presence of foreign researchers could pose an additional risk to the local community” [21]. Thus, while research during war can be ethically justified, it may require a higher level of justification that takes into account the unique contextual factors and inherent vulnerabilities of potential participants and their communities [6, 29].

Clear guidance on the ethics of delivering care and conducting research during war is imperative. A qualitative research project that interviewed healthcare clinicians practicing during war in Syria found little relevant guidance from existing ethics guidelines for the ethical challenges faced during war [30]. Similarly, while some ethics frameworks for research during humanitarian crises are instructive [29], few international research ethics guidelines specifically address conflict settings [31]. Resources on health research during humanitarian crises often do not center ethics or only include a few war‐related cases. For example, see “Lessons from the Field: Confronting the Challenges of Health Research in Humanitarian Crises,” https://www.biomedcentral.com/collections/lessonsfromthefield. Existing bioethics scholarship and empirical research suggest the need for more practical guidance and educational resources specific to research in war and other conflict settings [21].

Organizing Framework of the Review

To guide our review of ethical issues conduct research during war, we employed an established framework outlining ethical principles and benchmarks for multinational clinical research in low‐income settings [32]. This framework was developed by bioethicist Ezekiel Emanuel and colleagues from the US National Institutes of Health and outlines eight principles for ethical clinical research in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) that includes benchmarks specifying practical measures for assessing the extent to which each principle is satisfied. This framework builds on their prior work to delineate a coherent framework for evaluating research that incorporates all relevant considerations and reflects established and widely recognized codes, declarations, and relevant literature on the ethics of human research [33]. Their eight principles are: collaborative partnership, social value, scientific validity, fair selection of study population, favorable risk‐benefit ratio, informed consent, respect for recruited participants and study communities, and independent review (see Table 1).

Our framing of researchers’ ethical obligations was supplemented by the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) International Ethical Guidelines for Health‐related Research Involving Humans [31]. CIOMS Guidelines include detailed commentary on how ethical principles should be applied in low‐resource settings which is consistent with the Emanuel et al. framework. Guideline 20 is specific to Research in Disasters and Disease Outbreaks and emphasizes the importance of ensuring that research does not interfere with or otherwise negatively impact disaster response, that the sense of urgency does not interfere with scientific validity nor ethical conduct of research, that research is responsive to the health needs of those affected by the disaster, and that it cannot be otherwise conducted outside the disaster situation [31]. Such issues are also relevant in conflict settings.

An important issue that has received some attention for research in conflict settings but that is missing from the Emanuel et al. framework and only briefly mentioned in CIOMS Guideline 20 is the potential risk to researchers of both physical and emotional harm, as well as moral distress [34]. Such additional relevant issues were considered within the light of the principles in the Emanuel et al. organizing framework. As a result, we subdivided their harm‐benefit principle into two, adding a new subsection focused on protecting and supporting research team members. Our organizing framework linked to the Emanuel et al. framework and summarized in Table 1.

Our review was also informed by a few systematic reviews. A 2016 systematic review identified and compared fourteen published disaster research ethics guidelines [21]. Of these, only one guideline, which is now over 25 years old, focused on research in conflict settings [1]. Qualitative analysis of the fourteen full‐text guidelines identified two core themes: vulnerability and research ethics committee review. The authors also observed that key ethics concepts and terms are used inconsistently, that requirements are stated without much (or any) supporting evidence, and that little practical guidance is provided regarding how to weigh conflicting ethical principles [21]. These findings did not alter our organizing framework and supported the need for such a framework.

Eight Key Ethical Challenges and Considerations With Research During Conflicts

Engaging in Collaborative Partnership

Health research in conflict settings is often conducted by international non‐governmental humanitarian agencies in partnership with academic researchers. Investigators leading the studies may often not be citizens of the country where the research takes place. Ideally, in‐country researchers as well as health ministry personnel, local community leaders and local community members are also engaged in research planning and implementation. Community engagement is now widely recognized as an ethical obligation of any researcher working in low‐income international settings [31]. Satisfying the ethical principle of collaborative partnership requires meaningful engagement of local partners to determine the social importance of the health problem to be studied, ensure that the research is respectful of the community's traditions and values and aware of the current sociopolitical context, develop local research capacity through education and infrastructure‐building, and equitably share the health, financial and other benefits (e.g., publications) of the research [32].

Researchers in conflict settings may encounter heightened mistrust due to the conflict, and participants may be at higher risk of harm from breaches of privacy and confidentiality. This may lead to challenges with engaging local collaborators for research [35]. Community engagement can ensure that research is responsive to community needs, promote understanding of the goals and research design, and address barriers to access or ethical concerns [36]. Engagement can also center the resilience and strengths of communities affected by armed conflict and not just their vulnerabilities [37]. Researchers should assume that communities have the capacity to engage and participate, no matter what circumstances exist in the conflict [38].

Collaboration may include providing training for researchers as part of capacity‐building efforts which should be reasonably proportionate to the magnitude and duration of the planned research project(s). Developing research capacity may be particularly challenging if local academic and research institutions are closed or not fully functioning due to the conflict or if skilled researchers and health professionals have left the country, especially if strategic attacks on health care facilities are a feature of the conflict [39].

Engaging early with local collaborators is critical to minimize the research burden on the community. Researchers from high‐income countries (HICs) should be sensitive to power imbalances and how differential experience and expertise can be significant barriers to equitable, meaningful engagement [40]. Investing in local relationships is critical for adequate protection of research participants and ethical implementation, especially when guidance and/or research ethics review can fall short in providing direction for researchers operating in complex, dynamic situations. Importantly, research should never disrupt health care delivery (e.g., planning for research implementation and ethics review should not delay the delivery of services) [31].

Ensuring Social Value

The social (and scientific) value of generating knowledge that contributes to the protection and promotion of people's health is the primary ethical justification for conducting any research with humans [41]. Social value is also closely tied to researchers’ obligation for community engagement, as researchers working in settings of armed conflict must ensure that research is responsive to community needs and priorities [31]. Although social value is a basic ethical requirement for all research, it is not always reasonable to expect immediate benefit from the results of any one individual study. Thus, engaging in long‐term research collaborations is preferable to “one‐off” studies [32] or “fly‐in, fly‐out” research [42]. In most research settings, when weighing potential harms against potential benefits, producing knowledge that will advance a research agenda is generally of sufficient social value to offset risks if they are minimal. The circumstances of armed conflict, including the burden on study populations, increase researchers’ obligations after the study ends. Plans for post‐trial access to beneficial findings and interventions should be in place, and research findings that do not require approval by a regulatory agency should be implemented as soon as feasible [31].

Findings generated from the research should be shared with the individual participants and the broader community(ies) and disseminated to relevant practitioners, policymakers, and other research teams not only through peer‐reviewed manuscripts but also via other more local and accessible means. This will require investment in local partners, engagement with health ministries, and advocacy to funders – and most importantly, planning to ensure appropriate resources are in place to do all of this. Planning ensures that findings are published in relevant outlets to ensure participants’ investment in the research makes a difference.

Promoting Scientific Validity and Rigor

Research cannot be socially valuable unless it is also scientifically rigorous [32]. When findings are published from research using methods that are not rigorous, this can call results into question and undermine efforts to document and raise awareness about the impact of armed conflicts [6]. In dynamic environments like conflict settings, scientific validity depends upon feasibility. In the worst case, research may be totally abandoned mid‐course, but methodological rigor and adherence to standard protocols may suffer in a variety of other less dramatic ways due to factors that are unpredictable and out of the research team's control. While certainly methodological adjustments can (and sometimes must) be made when research is disrupted for reasons beyond researchers’ control, thoughtful planning and feasibility assessment during design as well as funding and ethics review are key to mitigating threats to scientific validity, promoting adherence to planned protocols, and ensuring data quality [6]. Consultation with others experienced in similar research studies, conflict settings or local conditions can also be useful to assessing project feasibility.

Strict fidelity to data collection and treatment/intervention protocols may be challenging. Validated assessment and data collection tools may not be available or may need to be modified due to language, culture, or local clinical standards [42]. Methodological compromises may also be necessary to protect participants’ privacy and the confidentiality of their data. Researchers who ask about sensitive issues must be creative in data collection. For example, in research asking about the timing of critical events, rather than asking for exact dates of events as these could be linked to specific attacks and other events, participants could be asked about the amount of time elapsed since the event.

In addition to adequate infrastructure and financial resources, competent, well‐trained and well‐supported personnel are the best way to promote scientific rigor. Successful implementation of any research study will likely involve partnership with a humanitarian organization that is familiar with local actors, geography, and customs. However, not all NGOs have research as part of their core mission. NGO partners may have “dual roles” as service providers and researchers, and research rightly may be a lower priority than providing care. Dedicated research personnel can mitigate this threat, but they are not always available; personnel turnover is high in humanitarian settings [43]. Robust safety protocols, psychological and psychosocial support, and opportunities for professional development are critical.

Selecting Study Participants Fairly

Ensuring fair distribution of the burdens and benefits of research requires that research participants are neither included nor excluded solely out of convenience. This not only threatens scientific validity but may also be unjust. At the same time, the study population should be selected to minimize risks and promote social value [31, 32]. Duplicate research or excessive research burden in a particular community should be avoided. Selection or recruitment approaches should be careful not to stigmatize or otherwise negatively label participants [42].

Features of the conflict may prevent researchers from being able to access certain groups of participants, thereby calling into question the generalizability of results. Local partnerships are essential to access participant groups, but partners might control access to specific groups of participants, impacting the representativeness of study populations [42].

Ensuring a Favorable Risk‐Benefit Ratio

As discussed in the section on our Organizing Framework, we divided this principle from Emanuel et al. [32] into two.

Mitigating Potential Harm

Much has been written about the potential vulnerability of certain individuals and groups to increased risk of certain harms in research, challenges comprehending research risks, or an inability to refuse participation [31]. Critiques include that vulnerability as a concept is useless in guiding researchers regarding appropriate safeguards for fair inclusion, can stereotype or stigmatize certain groups of potential research participants, or may lead to unjust exclusion [44]. While potential participants living through armed conflict may appear particularly vulnerable given intersecting challenges such as lack of access to basic resources and services, cognitive deficits, limited education and/or language discordance with researchers, vulnerability is not a reason to not do research. The concept is useful in raising researchers’ awareness regarding their unique responsibilities to protect research participants from certain kinds of risks and take particular care in promoting understanding and voluntariness during the informed consent process [25].

All research poses risks, and even well‐intentioned research can cause harm, or increase the risk of harm, especially in volatile settings [45]. In any research study, potential harms must be identified, their likelihood and magnitude assessed, and these weighed against potential individual and social benefits [32]. During armed conflict, risk of physical harm is ever‐present and unpredictable. Exposure to the existential threat of multiple forms of violence may last years, even decades. Satisfying the ethical principle of a favorable risk‐benefit ratio allows for the assessment of potential harms and benefits in the specific context of the study population. However, the fact that risks to mental and physical health, as well as legal, economic, and other social risks, are omnipresent in conflict settings does not diminish the potential incremental risks of research, and double standards are not acceptable [46]. The additional risks of research, even if minimal, on top of the daily risks, increase researchers’ obligation to provide some direct benefit to individual participants. An important risk that research poses to participants in settings of armed conflict is an unintended breach of privacy or confidentiality which could have serious physical, social, or legal consequences. Privacy/confidentiality risks and safeguards will be discussed in more detail below.

In conflict settings, all researchers, even those conducting studies that do not explicitly seek to document impacts or evaluate treatments for psychological or psychosocial effects of the conflict, will encounter participants reporting mental health concerns. If mental health services are not provided through the research, study protocols should include guidance for local referrals, and data collectors should be trained to provide this information [18]. People who refuse to participate should also receive referrals to psychological and psychosocial services; provision of information or appropriate referral should not be a condition of participation.

Protecting and Supporting Research Team Members

Modern armed conflicts have seen a significant increase in deliberate, strategic attacks on health care facilities [47]. In “When Ethics Survives Where People Do Not,” researcher Ghaith Hussein relays agonizing over the safety of his data collection team in Darfur after they lost contact for 5 days:

And here I am sharing this story with you in the hope that when you come across the next report from a survey conducted during an armed conflict, you will see the people; you will hear the people; you will feel the people—not only those surveyed but also the surveyors. We are all part of a story worth telling [15].

Research team members – especially those who interact with participants and who collect the data but whose names are not on the publications – are often invisible. They face risks to their physical and psychological safety, yet research ethics frameworks and review processes only consider risks to research participants and not risks to researchers.

Researchers may also hear participants share things that may be difficult to hear, including stories of violence, abuse, torture, thoughts of self‐harm and suicidality, and even perpetrating harm to others. Such vicarious trauma may lead to researcher stress, grief, anger, and over‐involvement in participants’ lives [42]. Emotional and moral distress can be particularly high when health impacts are documented but no action is taken to treat individuals or advocate for system changes [48].

Research can also create moral dilemmas, especially when illegal activities or human rights violations are exposed [38]. Researchers working in conflict settings may be particularly troubled if they need to consider engaging or even cooperating with corrupt or violent individuals or groups to gain access to a certain population or secure protection for themselves [38]. Moral distress (MD) (defined as the distress experienced when a person knows the right action to take but is unable to do so due to external constraints) and moral injury (MI) (resulting from repetitive or ongoing moral distress), have been widely recognized in health care providers, but little attention has been paid to its experience in conflict settings [49]. Researchers may experience moral distress when what they perceive as the “right” thing to do is in conflict with what is in the approved protocol [50].

Independent Review: Implications for Research Ethics Review

Ethics review by Research Ethics Committees (RECs) has several goals: independence (ensuring that the research is free from conflicts of interest), competence (ensuring that the individuals conducting the research have the necessary expertise and resources), and transparency to promote public accountability [32]. The primary focus of REC review is risk assessment. RECs reviewing research proposed to take place in conflict settings may see “familiar ethical issues amplified” [51]. They may also see risks everywhere but have little guidance to offer [38]. Lack of specific, evidence‐based safeguards in armed conflict settings could lead to both under‐ and over‐protection (paternalism) in REC review.

The scope of collaboration in conflict research, especially international collaboration, often requires review by multiple RECs which complicates the review process. Partner institutions can have different levels of experience and comfort reviewing conflict‐related research. Dedicated RECs at international humanitarian organizations have extensive experience, knowledge, and guidelines for ethical conduct of research [29, 52], while small NGOs may not have much research experience or even a REC. RECs at academic institutions may not be aware of specific considerations relevant to research in conflict settings because they see very few studies in settings of armed conflict. They may also be unfamiliar with a particular region, the contours of the current conflict, or the daily experience of those living in the conflict, yet they are likely to be driving the review process. Academic RECs may be less flexible than RECs at humanitarian organizations, which were specifically established to review research in complex disaster settings [18].

While local, in‐country review is widely recognized as ethically optimal [31], formal review structures may not exist or may have been destroyed by the conflict. Establishing capacity for research ethics review falls under the responsibilities of researchers from HICs for broad research capacity building [31]. Conflicts of interest can be avoided by ensuring that financial assistance for capacity development is not tied to decisions about specific protocols.

Multiple authors and international working groups have issued recommendations for REC review of research that are relevant to research in conflict settings [53]. International humanitarian organizations have shared their experiences and models for REC review [29, 52]. For studies in dynamic environments that need to be reviewed and implemented quickly, it is recommended that RECs use a flexible, staged process and have pre‐approved protocol templates for standard study designs [18]. RECs with limited experience reviewing research conducted in settings of armed conflict have a responsibility to identify and obtain reviews from appropriate outside expert consultants to inform their decisions [54]. A “shared approach” to ethics review that gives research communities greater influence over decisions is recommended [38].

Supporting Valid, Voluntary Informed Consent

Meaningful, voluntary informed consent is well‐established for all ethical research [32]. Multiple challenges exist to obtaining valid, voluntary informed consent for research during armed conflict, including language discordance, low literacy, and lack of familiarity with research. Privacy is often absent in conflict settings, and ever‐present distractions can threaten participants’ focus and comprehension. It may also be challenging to explain that research is different from other activities, such as history taking for claim of refugee status, when the procedures (e.g., interview questions) may seem similar [42].

Regions affected by armed conflict are some of the most resource‐poor environments. This threatens voluntariness. When invited to participate in research, someone struggling to meet their basic needs could reasonably think they will lose out by refusing to participate or believe that participating will provide access to additional resources. Individuals invited to participate in research may already be dependent on the same organization conducting research for essential services [38]. Efforts by researchers to return benefit to the community may also be mistakenly viewed as unduly influential or even “coercive” by RECs or IRBs [38].

In studies that involve more than a single interaction, informed consent should be an ongoing process. Risks may change over time due to the dynamic environment, changes in the conflict itself, and/or the presence or departure of different military and humanitarian actors [18]. Such changes may significantly alter the risk‐benefit ratio of a study with implications not only for individual participant consent but for study continuation.

Engaging local community partners can also improve informed consent processes [55]. Obtaining permission from community leaders to conduct research in a community is usually done with the intent of adding an additional layer of protection for individual participants who must also provide individual consent [40]. However, community leaders may stand to benefit differently/more from the resources that research brings compared to individual participants [38]. Robust training of data collectors is critical to ensuring valid, voluntary informed consent and should emphasize that invitations to participate in research pose risks to privacy and confidentiality even for those who do not want to participate, are not eligible to participate or ultimately decide not to participate [6].

Respecting Participants and Communities by Safeguarding Participant Privacy and Confidentiality

Identifying and implementing adequate procedures to protect the privacy of participants and the confidentiality of their personal information and data during recruitment, data collection, and reporting is critical to demonstrating respect for participants and study communities [32]. Data collected from participants in research during armed conflict creates a “perfect storm” of ethical challenges. Highly sensitive information about stigmatized or potential illegal behavior may be collected; participants may report human rights violations they witnessed, had committed against them, or may have engaged in themselves. The risk of a breach of privacy or confidentiality is higher than usual due to both unstable environments and bad actors. Additionally, both the likelihood and magnitude of harm that could occur from a breach is higher than usual. Being seen just “talking” to researchers could place individuals or entire communities at significant risk of social, legal, or even physical harm – which is rare in other environments [6, 38]. Therefore, careful attention must be paid to privacy and confidentiality safeguards, and researchers should avoid collecting identifying information or certain specific data points that may create exceptional risk. Whenever possible research participants should remain anonymous [42]. For researchers working in volatile regions, it has been suggested to create a diversionary questionnaire that asks other types of questions such as basic demographics that they can produce should privacy be interrupted or conditions become otherwise unsafe [42].

Protecting privacy and confidentiality may be difficult in conflict settings due to uncertainty and close quarters, especially in refugee facilities [18]. The use of specific research methodologies such as focus groups or reliance on local interpreters may also increase risks of breach of confidentiality and resulting harms [38]. The usual safeguards such as secure locations, password protection, encryption may not be adequate to protect participants – not only due to resource constraints but also because bad actors may physically threaten research staff.

Discussion

The Nuremberg Code, the foundational ethical guidance document for biomedical research, emerged as a result of unethical research conducted during armed conflict [56]. Focused primarily on the abuse and lack of informed consent of prisoners in Nazi concentration camps, the Nuremberg Code did not consider the specific ethical requirements for conflict and post‐conflict research. Almost 80 years later, researchers working in conflict settings continue to face challenges in protecting research participants and their research team members from physical, psychological, and psychosocial harms; ensuring valid, voluntary informed consent; maintaining scientific integrity; and supporting and developing the research capacity of local partners. Particular challenges arise from the increased risk of harm to researchers and participants, from unintended breaches of privacy and confidentiality, and researcher emotional and moral distress. RECs at HIC‐based academic institutions may have limited experience reviewing research in conflict settings, and local review mechanisms may not be available. Research into all these issues, including bioethics research, is needed to ensure proposals, policies and practices are evidence‐based [57].

Funders have an important role to play in establishing, communicating and enforcing ethical standards. Research, research capacity building, and ancillary services should be funded concurrently to promote benefit to research participants and researched communities. Funded programs should be evidence‐based. Research feasibility assessment tools tailored to conflict settings are critically needed.

Given the ethical, methodological, and logistical challenges, research in conflict settings should not be undertaken by inexperienced researchers without direct supervision from researchers experienced with conflict research [1]. Missing from the literature are systematic examinations of the experiences of those on the ground. Ethics guidance should be informed by the challenges faced and lessons learned by researchers and others who have lived in conflict settings. Gathering their stories through mixed‐methods research should be a priority of agencies funding their research. Developing meaningful ethical guidance and support requires listening to those involved in all aspects of health during war to identify and prioritize key issues and concerns [58]. Towards that end, we have conducted qualitative research interviews with health researchers from around the world who conducted research in conflict settings and will publish those findings elsewhere.

Researchers working in conflict settings constantly face a stark tension: on one hand they question whether they should be doing the research, while at the same time they acknowledge the risks of not doing it [11]. They also need guidance on day‐to‐day ethical challenges that require decisions be made quickly in a dynamic environment; for example, whether to continue data collection, weighing safety considerations against data integrity, validity and generalizability. This suggests a benefit to having specialized consultation services to support research ethics in practice [51, 59]. Current communications technologies allow real‐time contact between researchers seeking guidance and experts who can support them.

We believe our framework can help guide researchers during all stages of research project design, conduct and dissemination. Researchers working in conflict and war settings must regularly ask whether they are considering and examining the ethical challenges discussed under all the themes discussed here. Researchers must go beyond obtaining ethics approvals by demonstrating a favorable risk‐benefit ratio, fair recruitment and participant selection processes, adequate consent forms and processes, and methodological rigor. Researchers working in conflict settings should also demonstrate that the proposed research has addressed the challenges discussed here. They can do this by showing that their research: 1) is responsive to community needs; 2) is needed during the conflict; 3) does not interfere with other essential services such as providing medical or life‐sustaining resources; 4) is feasible, with robust contingency plans in place both for participant safety and data integrity; 5) includes clear plans for capacity‐building and provision of community‐level benefits; and 6) has mechanisms in place to monitor safety conditions and provide psychological safety for all research team members. Lastly, researchers should also be reporting on their practices around ethics review and approval, informed consent, participant safety and privacy and confidentiality protections, researcher protections, and other ethical issues so that the research community can learn from each other to inform the development of specific ethics standards and best practices [60, 61]. Journals have a role to encourage this reporting.

Conclusion

The fact that resources are limited and background risks are high in settings of armed conflict does not absolve researchers of their responsibility for upholding ethical standards. Rather, because formal oversight mechanisms offer fairly weak protections in these dynamic environments, researchers working in conflict settings must have exceptionally strong ethical grounding [62]. Merely providing specific guidance or recommendations for technical methodological modifications is insufficient to ensure that participants are respected and protected. Henry Beecher's seminal 1966 article on clinical research ethics noted the importance of informed consent in research but recognized that an even more reliable safeguard to ethical research is the “the presence of an intelligent, informed, conscientious, compassionate, responsible investigator” [63]. Research during armed conflict highlights the importance of research ethics going beyond ethics approvals and regulatory guidelines. Researchers need to develop and exercise core ethical virtues in the context of strong partnerships to ensure that research conducted during armed conflict upholds the highest ethical standards and provides rigorous evidence to guide practice.
