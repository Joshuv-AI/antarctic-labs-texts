Renal and cardiovascular outcomes and safety of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of subgroup-level estimates derived from randomized trials
Authors: Ziyang Bao, Jingjia Hu, Yue Sun, Xianfa Li, Riyang Lin, Caifeng Zhu, Jiazhen Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElderly patients with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease have high cardiorenal risk. SGLT2 inhibitors are considered to potentially provide combined renal and cardiac benefits.ObjectiveTo evaluate renal and cardiovascular outcomes and safety of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease.MethodsRandomized controlled trials from database inception to 2025-7-18 were systematically searched, and trials enrolling non-dialysis chronic kidney disease and reporting a ≥ 65-year subgroup were included. The primary outcome was an adverse renal composite endpoint, and the key secondary outcome was a composite of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization. Safety focused on genital mycotic infections and volume depletion/hypotension, and acute kidney injury (AKI) and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) were summarized descriptively. Random-effects models based on log HR or RR (REML, Hartung–Knapp) were used, pre-specified sensitivity analyses were performed, and the certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.ResultsThree double-blind trials including 5,831 patients aged ≥ 65 years were included. SGLT2 inhibitors reduced the risk of the adverse renal composite endpoint (HR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.52–0.89; I²=29.98%), and the risk of the composite of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization (HR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.64–0.86; I²=35.53%). For safety, the risk of genital mycotic infections was increased (RR = 2.40, 95% CI 1.96–2.94; I²=23.85%), and the risk of volume depletion/hypotension was also increased (RR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.09–1.50; I²=0.00%); no signal of increased AKI was observed; DKA was very rare. GRADE rated the certainty of evidence as high for the renal outcome and moderate for the heart failure-related outcome.ConclusionIn patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, SGLT2 inhibitors added to standard therapy achieve dual renal and cardiovascular benefits with a manageable safety profile, supporting their structured use with stratified monitoring. It is recommended that SGLT2 inhibitors be routinely included in treatment.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, SGLT2 inhibitors, Elderly, Meta-analysis

Introduction

In elderly populations, the burden of chronic kidney disease is substantial, and the disease course is intertwined with metabolic comorbidities, blood pressure abnormalities, and difficulties in volume management, with renal outcomes and cardiovascular events mutually driving each other [1, 2]. In patients aged ≥ 65 years in the non-dialysis stage, it is necessary both to delay the progression of kidney function and to reduce the burden related to heart failure [3]. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors act through proximal tubular mechanisms to influence glomerular hemodynamics and improve volume and metabolic status, and have shown dual renal and cardiovascular protective signals in multiple populations [4, 5]. The clinical focus most relevant to elderly patients with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease is whether reproducible, age-compatible net clinical benefit can be achieved on top of standard kidney-protective therapy. Evidence centered on time-to-event endpoints is relatively sufficient in the overall population, whereas systematic evaluation targeting the ≥ 65-year subgroup remains insufficient [6]. Most existing studies are based mainly on the overall sample, with elderly subgroup analyses often appearing in supplementary materials, and the reporting pathways and endpoint definitions are inconsistent, making it difficult to directly compare effects and safety across trials [7]. The confirmation thresholds and components of renal composite endpoints differ among trials, the definitions of heart failure outcomes vary between composite endpoints and hospitalization, and elderly-specific volume-related adverse events and genital infections lack structured presentation [8]. In clinical decision-making, a quantitative evidence framework specifically for patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease is still lacking to determine whether SGLT2 inhibitors should be used as foundational therapy [9] and to plan monitoring and combination strategies accordingly. A rigorous synthesis of elderly subgroups in randomized controlled trials is therefore needed, with harmonized outcome definitions and effect size scales, to provide a practical profile of dual renal and cardiovascular benefits and risks. This study focuses on the ≥ 65-year subgroup in dedicated randomized controlled trials of chronic kidney disease, prespecifies clear research questions and outcome definitions, uses random-effects meta-analysis of log hazard ratios with Hartung–Knapp correction, and combines the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) to assess the certainty of evidence, in order to quantify the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on the adverse renal composite endpoint and heart failure-related endpoints, and to delineate a safety profile centered on genital mycotic infections and volume depletion or hypotension. The results suggest that dual renal and cardiovascular benefits coexist with manageable risks in elderly patients with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, providing a basis for incorporating this drug on top of standard therapy and implementing stratified monitoring.

Materials and methods

Study design and reporting standards

This study was a systematic review and meta-analysis subgroup-level estimates derived from randomized trials, with the study protocol prespecified and the analysis plan fixed before data extraction began. Subgroup analyses in meta-analyses are inherently limited and should be interpreted with caution. First, subgroup comparisons are observational and not based on randomized grouping, which precludes definitive causal inference. Second, conducting multiple subgroup comparisons increases the risk of type I error inflation due to repeated statistical testing. Third, subgroup meta-analyses generally yield lower statistical credibility and precision, as residual heterogeneity and reduced sample size can destabilize effect estimates. The reporting of the study followed the PRISMA 2020 Statement. The study population was restricted to patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, the intervention was SGLT2 inhibitors, and the control was placebo or standard therapy.

Literature search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science Core Collection, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The search period was from database inception to July 18, 2025. No language restrictions were applied. The reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were searched retrospectively to identify additional records. The search strategy was constructed based on three conceptual groups of subject headings and free-text terms: drug (SGLT2 inhibitors), disease (chronic kidney disease), and study type (randomized controlled trial), with an additional restriction to elderly populations. Using PubMed as an example, the core search string was: ((“Sodium-Glucose Transporter 2 Inhibitors“[Mesh] OR “sodium-glucose cotransporter 2” OR SGLT2 OR dapagliflozin OR empagliflozin OR canagliflozin OR ertugliflozin) AND (“Kidney Diseases, Chronic“[Mesh] OR “chronic kidney disease” OR CKD OR “renal insufficiency, chronic” OR albuminuria OR proteinuria) AND (randomized OR randomised OR “Randomized Controlled Trial“[Publication Type]) AND (Aged[Mesh] OR elderly OR older OR “65 years”)). Search strings for each database were adapted in terms of fields and syntax within the same conceptual framework, and were calibrated for recall and accuracy through pilot searches and inspection of the results.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: ① randomized controlled trials; ② adult patients with chronic kidney disease who had not received dialysis at enrollment, with chronic kidney disease defined according to the criteria of the trial protocol; ③ the intervention was a single SGLT2 inhibitor plus background standard therapy, and the control was placebo or background standard therapy; ④ reported risk ratios for efficacy outcomes in the ≥ 65-year population or provided sufficient data to calculate an effect size; ⑤ a minimum follow-up duration of no less than 6 months; ⑥ reported at least one prespecified primary or key secondary outcome. Exclusion criteria: ① non-randomized studies, crossover trials, or single-arm studies; ② populations on maintenance dialysis or with kidney transplantation at baseline; ③ trials using dual SGLT1/2 inhibitors; ④ trials that did not report results for the ≥ 65-year subgroup and from which an effect size could not be derived; ⑤ trials that lacked prespecified outcomes or reported only incompatible measures; ⑥ duplicate publications, for which the report with the most complete data and the longest follow-up was used.

Study outcomes and definitions

Primary outcome

The primary analysis used the adverse renal composite endpoint as prespecified in each trial. This composite endpoint consisted of one or more of the following components: sustained decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate reaching or exceeding a prespecified magnitude, doubling of serum creatinine, initiation of renal replacement therapy (hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation), and kidney-related death. The confirmation interval for the component of “sustained” was defined according to each trial protocol. Sensitivity analysis was restricted to definitions that included renal replacement therapy or kidney-related death.

Key secondary outcome

Priority was given to the composite of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization. If a trial reported only heart failure hospitalization, it was included in the pooled analysis of heart failure hospitalization, and the impact of different definitions was examined in sensitivity analyses.

Safety outcomes

Safety outcomes were expressed as risk ratios calculated from the number of participants with adverse events and the total number of participants. Genital mycotic infections were identified according to investigator judgment or the corresponding terms in the adverse event coding system. Volume depletion or hypotension was determined by investigator judgment and aggregated using prespecified adverse event terms. Acute kidney injury preferably followed the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) creatinine criteria; if a trial used different diagnostic criteria, the definition in that trial report was used. Diabetic ketoacidosis was defined based on confirmation of events by the trial team.

Study screening and data extraction

Two researchers independently performed an initial screening of titles and abstracts, followed by full-text rescreening, using reference management software for de-duplication with manual verification. Reasons for exclusion were recorded item by item in the PRISMA flow diagram. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher. Two researchers independently extracted data using a standardized prespecified form and cross-checked the results. The extracted items included basic trial characteristics, baseline data of participants, intervention and control regimens, sample size and number of events in the ≥ 65-year subgroup, risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals for each prespecified outcome, follow-up duration, descriptions of analysis sets, and risk of bias information. For time-to-event outcomes, hazard ratios and their 95% confidence intervals were uniformly extracted and converted to log hazard ratios and standard errors (standard errors were calculated as (ln upper limit − ln lower limit)/3.92) [10]. If a study did not provide a hazard ratio for the ≥ 65-year subgroup and an effect size could not be derived by appropriate methods, that study was not included in the quantitative synthesis for that outcome. For multi-arm trials with multiple SGLT2 inhibitor doses, the relevant intervention arms were first combined into a single intervention arm within the study to avoid double-counting.

Quality assessment and risk of bias judgment

The Cochrane RoB 2 tool was used at the outcome level to assess five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting, with judgments of low risk, some concerns, or high risk [11]. Two researchers independently performed the assessments and reached agreement, with a third researcher adjudicating when necessary. The results of the risk of bias assessment were used in sensitivity analyses to test robustness.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3), with the main packages including metafor and meta, and graphical outputs included the PRISMA flow diagram, forest plots, and risk of bias summary plots. Publication bias analyses and funnel plots were not presented because the number of studies was less than 10. The certainty of evidence for the primary outcome and heart failure-related outcomes was assessed using the GRADE approach [12], grading across five domains (limitations of studies, inconsistency of results, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias), and a summary table was prepared to present the evidence level and reasons for downgrading.

Effect size selection and pooling methods

For the primary outcome and heart failure-related outcomes, hazard ratios were used as the effect measure and were pooled using inverse-variance–weighted random-effects models based on log hazard ratios, with variance estimated by restricted maximum likelihood and confidence intervals adjusted using the Hartung–Knapp method. For safety outcomes, risk ratios were used as the effect measure and were pooled using random-effects models. When a study had a single zero event in one arm for a given outcome, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied; studies with zero events in both arms for an outcome were excluded from the pooled analysis for that outcome [13]. All tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold set at 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals and 95% prediction intervals were reported. Because the number of studies was 3, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were not performed.

Heterogeneity assessment and handling

Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochrane’s Q statistic, τ², and I², and was presented in forest plots. Exploration of the sources of heterogeneity was preferentially conducted through prespecified sensitivity analyses. I² and τ² were interpreted as descriptive indicators and were not used as threshold values to replace clinical judgment.

Sensitivity analyses and narrative synthesis strategies

Two sensitivity analyses were prespecified in the protocol: ① a narrow-definition analysis of the adverse renal composite endpoint, including only studies whose composite endpoint contained renal replacement therapy or kidney-related death; ② repeating the pooling after excluding studies judged to be at high risk of bias for that outcome. For heart failure-related outcomes, when there were inconsistencies across studies between the definitions of the “composite of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization” and “heart failure hospitalization”, the primary analysis prioritized the composite endpoint, and in sensitivity analyses, studies that reported only heart failure hospitalization were excluded. For outcomes that did not meet the quantitative pooling condition of “at least two studies with consistent effect measures”, a structured narrative synthesis was conducted, reporting the direction of effect, point estimates, and ranges of uncertainty, and explaining the reasons for not performing a meta-analysis.

Results

Literature search and study inclusion

A total of 823 records were identified. After de-duplication using software and manual checking, 644 records entered title and abstract screening. In the initial screening, 563 records were excluded, and 81 articles entered full-text assessment, of which 78 were excluded because of inappropriate study design, populations not consisting of non-dialysis CKD, no reporting of ≥ 65-year subgroup or prespecified outcomes, use of dual SGLT1/2 inhibitors, or duplicate publication. Finally, only 3 randomized controlled trials (Heerspink HJL 2020, PMID: 32970396; Herrington WG 2023, PMID: 36331190; Perkovic V 2019, PMID: 30990260) were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies and populations

Three randomized, double-blind, controlled trials were included, with a total of 5,831 patients aged ≥ 65 years. The mean age was approximately 71 years, and about one-third were women. Baseline eGFR was mostly in the range of 36–46 mL/min/1.73 m², UACR was generally elevated, and the proportions of type 2 diabetes and use of renin–angiotensin system inhibitors were both high, indicating a substantial overall burden of renal and cardiovascular risk in the population (Table 1).

Risk of bias assessment

According to the RoB 2 algorithm, the overall risk of bias for the renal composite outcomes in the three studies was judged to be low risk. For heart failure outcomes, the presence of some concerns in the domain of “selective reporting” led to an overall judgment of some concerns, while all other domains besides “selective reporting” were judged to be low risk (Fig. 2).

Risk of bias (RoB 2) traffic-light plot (by outcome)

Primary outcome

Using inverse-variance–weighted random-effects models based on log HRs, the analysis showed that in patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, SGLT2 inhibitors significantly reduced the risk of the adverse renal composite endpoint, with a pooled HR of 0.68 (95% CI 0.52–0.89) and low heterogeneity (I²=29.98%) (Fig. 3). Using REML random-effects models with Hartung–Knapp correction, the pooled HR under the narrow definition was 0.66 (95% CI 0.49–0.89; I²=22.71%), and after excluding studies at high risk of bias, the pooled HR was 0.68 (95% CI 0.52–0.89; I²=29.98%), indicating robust results (Table 2).

Key secondary outcome

Using inverse-variance–weighted random-effects models based on log HRs, the analysis showed that in patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, SGLT2 inhibitors reduced the risk of the composite of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization, with a pooled HR of 0.74 (95% CI 0.64–0.86) and moderate heterogeneity (I²=35.53%) (Fig. 4). Safety outcomes

In patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, inverse-variance–weighted random-effects models based on log RRs showed that the risk of genital mycotic infections was significantly increased in the SGLT2 inhibitor group, with a pooled RR of 2.40 (95% CI 1.96–2.94, I²=23.85%) (Fig. 5A). The risk of volume depletion or hypotension-related adverse events was also increased, with a pooled RR of 1.28 (95% CI 1.09–1.50, I²=0.00%) (Fig. 5B).

Safety outcomes of SGLT2 inhibitors. A: Meta-analysis forest plot of genital mycotic infections. B: Meta-analysis forest plot of volume depletion or hypotension-related adverse events

For the outcome of AKI, only DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY reported data for the ≥ 65-year subgroup based on KDIGO creatinine criteria. In DAPA-CKD, AKI occurred in 38/820 patients in the SGLT2 group and 53/817 patients in the control group, with an RR of 0.71 (95% CI 0.48–1.07). In EMPA-KIDNEY, AKI occurred in 61/1241 patients in the SGLT2 group and 74/1240 patients in the control group, with an RR of 0.82 (95% CI 0.59–1.15). In both trials, the direction of effect was consistent and no increased risk of AKI was observed, suggesting a possible protective effect, although the confidence intervals all crossed 1. CREDENCE did not separately report AKI events for the ≥ 65-year subgroup, so a study-level RR could not be estimated and it was not included in quantitative pooling. For DKA, all three trials had no analyzable events or events were extremely few in the ≥ 65-year subgroup, so DKA can only be considered extremely rare among elderly patients with non-dialysis CKD; this study could not quantitatively assess its relative risk, and no clear safety signal was observed.

Assessment of certainty of evidence

Assessment using the GRADE approach showed that the level of evidence for the adverse renal composite endpoint was high, with no downgrading in any domain across the three studies including a total of 5,831 patients aged ≥ 65 years. The level of evidence for the heart failure-related composite endpoint was moderate, mainly because the risk of bias domain was downgraded by one level due to most subgroup results being reported in supplementary materials (Table 4).

Discussion

In patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with a reduced risk of the adverse renal composite endpoint, and the pooled effect indicated a clear reduction in relative risk with low heterogeneity. The results remained consistent when using a narrow definition of the endpoint or excluding studies at high risk of bias, and the GRADE assessment rated the level of evidence as high, indicating that the conclusions are stable and generalizable. Physiologically, inhibition of glucose and sodium transport in the proximal tubule increases distal chloride delivery, reactivates tubuloglomerular feedback, increases afferent arteriolar tone, reduces intraglomerular pressure, and slows the long-term rate of decline in filtration rate, accompanied by reductions in proteinuria [14–18]. At the same time, moderate osmotic diuresis, mild reductions in volume and blood pressure, decreases in uric acid, and attenuation of inflammatory and fibrotic signaling occur, forming a kidney-protective effect centered on hemodynamics and accompanied by improvements in metabolic and inflammatory status [19]. The early decline in filtration rate reflects hemodynamic resetting rather than structural kidney injury, which is consistent with the safety finding that no increased risk of acute kidney injury was observed [20]. Neuen et al. indicated that the renoprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on slowing chronic kidney disease progression were consistent across all eGFR subgroups, and the corresponding HRs as follows: HR = 0.61 for eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m², HR = 0.57 for eGFR of 45 to < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², HR = 0.64 for eGFR of 30 to < 45 mL/min/1.73 m², and HR = 0.71 for eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m², with a P value for trend of 0.16 [21]. Large-scale data encompassing more than 27 million participants from the CKD Prognosis Consortium further revealed that individuals with stage 4 CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²) carry a 110- to 580-fold higher risk of kidney failure compared with patients with preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²) and normal albuminuria, with risk increments stratified by albuminuria severity [22]. These findings strengthen the guideline recommendations from major cardiovascular and nephrology societies for initiating SGLT2 inhibitor treatment in patients with CKD and an eGFR of ≥ 20 mL/min/1.73 m², independent of diabetes mellitus status [23, 24]. Compared with younger individuals, older patients generally have diminished baseline renal reserve, slower age-related physiological eGFR decline, and a higher burden of comorbidities, which render renal outcomes highly sensitive to the threshold definition adopted. Such methodological heterogeneity across studies may distort between-study comparisons of SGLT2 inhibitor efficacy in elderly CKD cohorts. In recognition of this issue, we acknowledge that standardized renal endpoint definitions are essential to enhance the consistency and generalizability of future geriatric renal trials. Compared with the overall populations in previous CKD randomized controlled trials, the magnitude of the relative effect was similar, suggesting that age itself is not a major effect modifier [25]. In the high-risk elderly population included in this study, lower baseline filtration rates and higher levels of proteinuria correspond to higher event rates, and greater absolute numbers of events avoided can be expected. The prediction interval was close to but did not cross the line of no effect, indicating variability in effect size across different clinical settings but a consistent overall direction. This is in line with the renal protective trends previously observed across different levels of kidney function and proteinuria, and supports the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in combination with standard kidney-protective therapy to further reduce the risk of renal endpoints in elderly patients with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease.

In patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, the incidence of the composite of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization was observed to decrease, and the effect remained stable with reduced heterogeneity after harmonization of endpoint definitions, indicating the reproducibility of the results and methodological robustness. A recent study by Mariani et al. revealed that the use of SGLT2i induced a significant reduction of atrial and ventricular arrhythmias events in HFrEF carriers of implantable cardioverter defibrillator or cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator [26]. Indeed, several clinical trials, including landmark studies such as EMPA-REG OUTCOME, CANVAS Program, and DECLARE-TIMI 58, have demonstrated robust reductions in cardiovascular events, particularly heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality, among patients treated with SGLT2i. However, subsequent trials showed that SGLT2i benefits extend beyond the diabetic population, encompassing individuals with and without diabetes. Additionally, SGLT2i exhibit nephroprotective effects, manifesting as a slowing of the progression of chronic kidney disease and a reduction in the risk of end-stage kidney disease. Another review showed that multiple clinical trials have clearly demonstrated substantial reductions in cardiovascular events, especially heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality, among patients treated with SGLT2i. Moreover, SGLT2i show nephroprotective effects, which are manifested as a deceleration of the progression of chronic kidney disease and a decrease in the risk of end-stage kidney disease [27]. Beyond their well-established cardioprotective effects, SGLT2 inhibitors exert substantial improvements in endothelial function among individuals with diabetic cardiomyopathy, highlighting additional vasculoprotective benefits of this drug class [28]. The physiological basis can be explained by mild natriuresis and osmotic diuresis [29], with preferential mobilization of interstitial fluid, reductions in venous return and preload, improvement in pulmonary congestion and ventricular filling pressure, and alleviation of renal venous congestion [30], accompanied by modest reductions in blood pressure and body weight, decreased uric acid levels, and more efficient myocardial substrate utilization, thereby optimizing cardiorenal interaction from both volume and metabolic perspectives [31]. Elderly patients with chronic kidney disease have greater sodium sensitivity and higher venous pressure load, and small changes in volume management are more likely to elicit responses at the endpoint level, which is consistent with the observed decrease in the composite endpoint [32]. Compared with evidence from previous randomized trials in overall populations with heart failure or chronic kidney disease, the feature that benefits are mainly driven by heart failure-related events was reproduced, with the magnitude of effect falling within the previously reported range and no age-related reversal of effect direction observed [33]; after adopting stricter endpoint definitions, between-study variability was reduced, suggesting that differences are more likely attributable to endpoint definitions and the composition of events rather than inconsistency in drug effects [34]. Given the higher baseline risk and event density in elderly populations, the above relative effects are more likely to translate into greater absolute benefits, supporting the incorporation of SGLT2 inhibitors into treatment pathways aimed at reducing heart failure-related burden on the basis of optimized natriuresis, diuresis, and blood pressure control, and the use of individualized fluid management to reduce volume-related adverse events.

Regarding safety, the risk profile of SGLT2 inhibitors shows predictable and overall manageable characteristics. Genital mycotic infections and volume depletion- or hypotension-related adverse events occur more frequently and represent clear pharmacological extensions, and in clinical practice they mostly present as mild to moderate events that can be prevented and managed by reinforcing local hygiene, early recognition of pruritus or changes in secretions, assessment of changes in blood pressure and body weight, and individualized adjustment of diuretic and antihypertensive regimens. Considering the potential adverse effects of SGLT2 inhibitors, including genital infection and hypotension, targeted clinical management is essential. Clinicians should carry out systematic patient education to help patients recognize early symptoms of infection and hypotension. Meanwhile, the frequency of follow-up visits should be appropriately increased during treatment, so as to timely identify and intervene relevant adverse reactions. No increasing trend in the risk of acute kidney injury was observed, and the direction of effect even tended to be risk reduction, which is consistent with the understanding that the early modest decline in eGFR mainly reflects resetting of intraglomerular pressure rather than acute nephron injury [35], and is also in line with the direction of benefit on the adverse renal composite endpoint. Events of diabetic ketoacidosis in the existing ≥ 65-year subgroup were rarely recorded and are difficult to assess quantitatively, but at least no recognizable persistent safety signal has emerged, suggesting that in elderly populations mainly consisting of non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, this complication is more likely to be confined to special situations such as severe stress, insulin therapy, or severe dehydration [36]. Previous randomized trials in populations with different underlying diseases and age groups have consistently reported an increase in fungal infections and volume-related events, without confirming a systematic increase in AKI [37], and the findings of this study are consistent with this pattern. Against the background of clear benefits in both renal and heart failure outcomes, this risk profile, dominated by local infections and volume-related events, supports achieving dual renal and cardiovascular protection for elderly patients with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease in clinical practice through standardized patient education, close monitoring, and careful management of concomitant medications, without substantially increasing irreversible serious adverse events.

Several key limitations inherent to the present meta-analysis should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was restricted to only three eligible trials, and the reliance on aggregated study-level data precluded adjustment for individual-level heterogeneity, thereby introducing potential selective reporting bias. Second, incomplete reporting of several prespecified subgroup indicators across the included studies limited our ability to evaluate the impacts of advanced age (≥ 75 years), severely reduced eGFR, frailty status, and concomitant diuretic dosage and blood pressure targets on therapeutic efficacy and safety outcomes. Such data insufficiency may result in unstable confidence intervals, less reliable prediction intervals, and low precision of heterogeneity estimates, which compromise the robustness and comprehensiveness of our subgroup findings. Third, inconsistent reporting of composite heart failure endpoints and the predominant presentation of elderly subgroup data in supplementary materials further increase the risk of selective reporting uncertainty. In addition, the low incidence of diabetic ketoacidosis and certain safety events, together with the lack of age-stratified data for patients aged ≥ 65 years, prohibited rigorous quantitative subgroup evaluation. Finally, all subgroup comparisons were observational in nature without within-subgroup randomization. Collectively, these methodological limitations necessitate cautious interpretation of the current subgroup results, and future studies with more complete individual-level and stratified data are warranted to validate our findings.

Also, the median follow-up duration was approximately 24–30 months, which is not sufficient to evaluate very long-term hard endpoints and rare adverse events. To improve the usability of the evidence, it is recommended to conduct individual participant data meta-analyses centered on patients aged ≥ 65 years, with harmonized prespecified definitions of renal composite and heart failure composite endpoints, and stratification by age and frailty, eGFR and UACR, history of heart failure, and diuretic intensity, reporting absolute risks, NNT, and NNH; to establish prospective registries for elderly CKD, standardize safety terminology and event adjudication, and fully report elderly strata; to embed strategies for coordinated volume management and blood pressure lowering in real-world studies and pragmatic trials, in order to verify net clinical benefit and optimal monitoring frequency across different baseline risk strata; and to improve data sharing and preregistration mechanisms, prespecify narrow renal composite endpoints and endpoint-consistency sensitivity analyses, and collect quality control indicators such as methods of event adjudication and loss to follow-up, so as to enhance cross-study comparability and the external validity of conclusions.

Conclusion

In patients aged ≥ 65 years with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease, SGLT2 inhibitors on top of standard therapy provide clear renal benefits and reduce heart failure-related events, and the effects remain robust across different endpoint definitions and methodological tests. Overall safety is acceptable, with the main risks being genital mycotic infections and volume depletion or hypotension, with no increase in acute kidney injury and rare diabetic ketoacidosis. Across trials with different proportions of participants with type 2 diabetes, the direction of benefit was generally consistent; however, effect modification by diabetes status could not be formally evaluated. It is recommended that SGLT2 inhibitors be routinely incorporated into treatment after assessment of volume status and concomitant medications, with close monitoring for genital infection and hypotension is required during medication, and optimization of diuretic and RAS inhibitor doses.
