Artificial intelligence-powered chatbots’ responses to orthodontic questions from the dentistry specialization examination: Accuracy and source evaluation
Authors: Berrak Çakmak, Tevhide Sökmen, Burcu Baloş Tuncer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purpose: The use of artificial intelligence (AI) powered chatbots in dental education is becoming increasingly widespread. Evaluating their performance and the reliability of their sources is essential to understand their educational value. The aim of this study was to evaluate the performance of AI-powered chatbots in addressing orthodontic questions from the Dental Specialty Exam (DUS) and to assess the accuracy and reliability of the information sources on which they rely.Materials and methodsA total of 129 orthodontic questions from the exam administered between 2012 and 2021 were categorized according to Bloom’s taxonomy. Each question was individually entered into ChatGPT-5, Claude 3.7, and Copilot, and their performances were comparatively evaluated. The sources referenced by the chatbots while generating their answers were also assessed. The data were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared test.ResultsChatGPT-5, Claude 3.7, and Copilot achieved accuracy rates of 82.2 %, 83.7 %, and 85.3 %, respectively. Copilot performed best on scenario-based questions (100 %) but performed worst on visual analysis questions (33.3 %). Citation analysis showed that, ChatGPT-5.0 used reliable academic sources, whereas Claude cited few and less credible references, and Copilot relied mainly on moderately reliable materials.ConclusionChatbots exhibited strong text-based reasoning abilities but limited visual interpretation skills. While ChatGPT-5.0 provided more reliable and well-referenced responses, other models showed weaker citation practices. These underscored both the potential and the current limitations of AI-based systems in orthodontic education and clinical practice.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Bloom’s taxonomy, Chatbots, Dental specialty exam, Orthodontics, Source evaluation

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are designed to develop algorithms capable of simulating human cognitive processes through numerical models. One of the most common applications include chatbot systems.1,2 Pre-trained large language models (LLMs) have enabled the development of chatbots that can interpret a variety of patient queries in the healthcare context, retain contextual information across interactions, and produce relevant medical responses.3,4

Versions of chatbots are being improved over time to provide higher quality and fewer mistakes in many tasks, necessitating timely evaluation of these models.5 The most widely used and accessible ones include ChatGPT, Claude, and Copilot. Introduced in November 2022, ChatGPT has undergone rapid development, beginning with the release of GPT-4 in March 2023 and its subsequent enhancements through GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4.1 during late 2023 and 2024, and was ultimately updated to ChatGPT 5.0 on August 7, 2025.6 Claude, launched in March 2023, reached its Claude 3.7 version on February 24, 2025, improving contextual comprehension and reasoning capacity.7 Copilot, also introduced in March 2023, received a significant update in August 2025, integrating GPT-5.0 and advanced productivity features.8 These advancements are particularly relevant to medicine and dentistry, where data are frequently derived from visual representations such as clinical photographs or radiographic scans.9 Although earlier versions of GPT, Claude, and Copilot had limited ability to interpret visual data,10,11 the latest versions may offer enhanced clinical utilities in interpreting radiographic images, detecting lesions, and analyzing clinical data in integrated visual and textual contexts.12,13

Recently, the demand for specialization in dentistry has increased, as in medicine. In Turkey, the pathway for dental graduates to pursue specialization is by succeeding in the Dentistry Specialization Examination (DUS), a centralized exam administered by the national authorities. This examination consists of multiple-choice questions (MCQs) that assess knowledge in clinical dentistry and basic sciences. Studies declared that AI-powered chatbots demonstrate a high potential for accurately responding to MCQs.9,14 Dental education requires not only factual knowledge but also analytical thinking, diagnostic reasoning, and a comprehensive understanding of clinical principles. The DUS administered in Turkey goes beyond general dental licensure examinations and functions as a rigorous standard for evaluating higher-order cognitive competencies.

Critical thinking is a fundamental skill for achieving the goals of postgraduate orthodontic education. To successfully manage various malocclusions, complex clinical presentations, multidisciplinary cases, and a wide variety of etiologies, residents must develop skills regarding critical thinking, visual and spatial reasoning in orthodontics, all to assist with problem-solving strategies from the beginning of their trainings.15 Therefore, orthodontics is a field which relies on these skills, revealing it as an ideal domain for testing LLMs’ multimodal capabilities. In this regard, the study focused on DUS questions in the field of orthodontics rather than other branches of dentistry. However, research on AI-powered chatbots focusing on orthodontics is limited.16 Previous studies have assessed earlier versions of ChatGPT, Claude, and Copilot, but recent advances in AI have broadened their potential use in medicine and dentistry.9,14,16,17 Additionally, few studies have examined the reliability of the sources these models use when generating responses, despite its importance in health sciences education.18 As DUS questions become increasingly visual and clinically oriented, updated evaluations of current AI-powered chatbots are needed. The exclusion of visual questions in earlier studies further emphasizes the need to investigate how existing models handle cognitive tasks.19–23 Therefore, this study aimed to analyze both the accuracy of responses generated by AI-powered chatbots (ChatGPT-5.0, Claude 3.7, and Copilot-GPT-5.0 integrated) regarding orthodontic questions, and the nature of the sources they use, to assess the extent to which these models can reflect expert-level cognitive and reasoning abilities.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval was not required as the research was based solely on open-source digital data and AI-generated outputs. A total of 130 DUS orthodontic questions from 13 examinations conducted between 2012 and 2021 were obtained from the database of the Assessment, Selection and Placement Center (ÖSYM), the official institution of the Republic of Turkey responsible for conducting the centralized examinations. Questions after 2021 were excluded, as ÖSYM officially ceased publishing questions afterwards. One question was also removed from the analysis because it was officially cancelled. Therefore, a total of 129 questions were evaluated. Since the study focused solely on evaluating LLM performance within the scope of orthodontic knowledge and reasoning, all questions from dental specialties other than orthodontics were automatically excluded.

The latest subscribed versions of ChatGPT-5.0 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) and Claude 3.7 (Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA) were used, along with the latest free version of Copilot (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). All interactions and questions asked were conducted in the Turkish language. Orthodontic questions were entered into the chatbots separately on September 17, 2025. The question entry process was managed by a single operator (T.S.), without any guidance. The chat session was a new chat before submitting each MCQ to avoid any influence from prior interactions.

Two orthodontists with at least two years of professional experience (B.Ç. and T.S.) independently verified the responses using the official answer keys. Additionally, the questions were independently classified by the same orthodontists according to Bloom’s taxonomy and the types of sources used by AI-powered chatbots when generating answers. Any discrepancies were resolved through re-evaluation of the sources and questions, followed by discussions among the experts until consensus was reached.

Following each response, the AI-powered chatbots were instructed to display their sources using the command, “Please provide the sources you used to answer this question” The responses were categorized as textbook, Turkish article, English article, educational material (open-access undergraduate lecture notes of dentistry faculties), or clinical/commercial websites of private dental clinics.

Bloom’s taxonomy classifies learning objectives into six categories: remembering, understanding, applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating.24 The questions in this study were categorized into four types as; knowledge-based, scenario-based, evaluative, and visual analysis questions. The knowledge-based and scenario-based question categories were classified as lower-order cognitive levels. The latter categories, evaluative and visual analysis questions, were considered to be higher-order cognitive levels. According to Anderson and Krathwohl’25 widely adopted revision, “understanding” and “applying” represent lower-order cognitive processes, whereas “analyzing,” “evaluating,” and “creating” constitute higher-order skills. Although scenario-based questions may appear more complex, their cognitive demands in the DUS exam primarily fall within the ‘understanding’ and ‘applying’ categories of the revised Bloom’s taxonomy, both classified as lower-order cognitive levels.25

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) Software, Version 31.0. The sample size calculation was performed using the G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.4; Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), based on a medium effect size (effect size = 0.5), α = 0.05, and a power level of 80 %, which indicated that a minimum sample size of 97 questions was required. To assess the inter-rater reliability, Cohen’s Kappa coefficients were calculated. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). The Pearson Chi-square test was used to test the relationship between categorical variables. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was calculated as 1.00 for validating the official answer key, 0.89 for categorizing questions according to Bloom’s taxonomy, and 0.92 for source classification, indicating an excellent level of agreement. ChatGPT-5.0, Claude 3.7, and Copilot demonstrated similar performances in answering the questions. The accuracy levels achieved were 82.2 % for ChatGPT-5.0, 83.7 % for Claude 3.7, and 85.3 % for Copilot, respectively. When analyzed by question category, all AI-powered chatbots showed similar performance levels (Table 1). While ChatGPT-5 and Claude performed similarly across all categories, Copilot was more successful in answering scenario-based questions (100 %), compared to knowledge-based (86.2 %) and evaluation questions (86.8 %), but performed worse in visual analysis questions (33.3 %) (Table 2). Of the 129 exam questions, 82 (63.6 %) were high-level questions. The performance of the chatbots according to taxonomy levels was statistically similar in both categories (high-level and low-level). All declared decreased accuracy rates as the Bloom’s taxonomy level of the questions were increased (Table 3). According to Table 4, notable differences were observed in citation patterns among the chatbots. ChatGPT-5.0 cited the highest number of sources (n = 342), most of which were textbooks (79.5 %) and English-language articles (14.6 %). Claude provided citations only for 11 questions, among which, seven were clinical or commercial websites. It did not cite sources for 91.6 % of the questions. In contrast, Copilot cited 341 sources, including 43.7 % (n = 149) from Turkish-language articles, 26.1 % from educational materials, and 24.0 % from clinical or commercial websites.

Discussion

The current results showed that the chatbots presented high and similar accuracy rates. However, chatbots exhibited distinct limitations and performance differences, particularly on questions related to visual analytics. Notable differences were observed in the types and reliability of the sources cited by the chatbots.

In this study, subscribed versions of ChatGPT-5.0 and Claude 3.7 were used to avoid interaction limits. Copilot’s free version already provides unlimited responses, but the Microsoft 365–integrated paid version was excluded because it can access user documents and sensitive data. Since chatbots may generate varying responses to repeated questions,26 each question was asked only once.

LLMs, as ChatGPT, Claude, and Copilot were not specifically developed to address dentistry-specific questions. They lack the contextual understanding and domain-specific expertise required to fully comprehend the complex relationships among various dental problems and treatment approaches.27 However, previous studies have reported that AI-powered chatbots can perform successfully on dental licensing examinations and even on questions requiring advanced professional knowledge.28–30 Chau et al.28 stated that ChatGPT achieved an 82 % accuracy rate in prosthodontic and restorative dentistry questions, while Mahmoud et al.29 found accuracy levels between 70 % and 85 % in oral and maxillofacial surgery board exams. In periodontology assessment, Ramlogan et al.30 observed a 73 % correct response rate, and Bulut et al.31 reached over 75 % accuracy, comparable with general practitioners. Similarly, Ali et al.5 reported that ChatGPT provided approximately 90 % accuracy in multiple-choice dentistry questions. These are consistent with our results and suggested that LLMs demonstrate substantial competence in dental knowledge assessments, achieving accuracy rates ranging from 70 % to 90 %, thereby underscoring their growing reliability as educational and evaluative tools in dental training.

Bloom’s taxonomy was employed in this study to provide a structured and objective framework for evaluating the cognitive complexity of orthodontic questions. Taxonomy is valuable in chatbot performance analysis, enabling identification of whether models demonstrate competence only in lower-level recall or also engage in higher-order reasoning akin to human problem-solving.24 The present study aligns with the previous investigations, demonstrating that the accuracy rates of the chatbots decreased as the taxonomy level of the questions increased.9 Although higher-order cognitive items were expected to reveal greater performance differences among the models, the lack of statistically significant variation may indicate that present higher-order questions still rely largely on text-based application of foundational knowledge rather than complex analytical reasoning. Another plausible explanation is the relatively small number of high-level cognitive items, which may have limited the statistical power to detect true differences in model performance.

The exam utilized in this study has been administered in Turkey since 2012, and comprises MCQs. Although the exams conducted between 2012 and 2017 included no visual orthodontic questions, a total of nine questions directly requiring visual analysis were introduced between 2018 and 2021. Visual interpretation is inherently crucial in orthodontics, therefore examination content has become increasingly visual over time. ChatGPT-5.0 and Claude 3.7 achieved 55.6 % accuracy on visual items, whereas Copilot reached 33.3 %, indicating ongoing limitations in interpreting image-based clinical information. Similarly, McNulty et al.10 and Sau et al.11 found that multimodal LLMs showed inadequate visual interpretation and poor diagnostic validity on neurosurgery board questions. Wang et al.12 likewise emphasized the need to strengthen multimodal reasoning in ChatGPT-5.0. In dentistry, Mine et al.32 reported that four LLMs performed well on text-based items (80–92 %) but showed substantially lower accuracy on image-based questions (45.6–67.8 %), with even poorer results in visually intensive areas such as orthodontics (36.4–66.7 %). Fukuda et al.33 also observed low accuracy for GPT-4V (35.0 %) and Gemini Pro (28.1 %), noting further declines as visual complexity increased. Nguyen et al.34 similarly documented strong performance on text-based items but weak results on image-dependent tasks. In line with these findings, Copilot’s near-perfect accuracy on scenario-based items in our study indicates that LLMs remain effective in text-based reasoning yet continue to struggle with visual-spatial analysis. Currently multimodal LLMs are trained predominantly on text-dominant datasets and therefore have limited exposure to diverse malocclusions, structured radiographic or orthodontic imageries. Their visual encoders struggle with fine-grained pattern recognition, spatial feature integration, and biomechanical interpretation. These multimodal constraints likely contributed to the reduced accuracy in visual-analysis items.

As for sources, ChatGPT-5.0 provided the most reliable and verifiable references, citing mostly internationally recognized textbooks or peer-reviewed English literature. Copilot tended to rely predominantly on Turkish-language sources, like local academic articles or lecture notes. Conversely, Claude 3.7 reported no need to require external sources, asserting that the relevant information was already embedded within its system. These distinctions highlighted differences in model design, source accessibility, and response generation strategies among the chatbots. Dursun and Bilici Geçer35 and Alkhamees36 examined the accuracy and evidence-based nature of chatbot responses, while Hassan et al.37 analyzed their reliability and readability. All three studies reported concerns regarding potential inaccuracies in the responses generated by chatbots. These studies evaluated only the accuracy of the information without classifying the types of sources. The present study, however, deepens the reliability assessment by systematically classifying the sources used by chatbots. A unique aspect of this study is that it is the first study to perform visual assessment and source reliability assessment specifically in the field of orthodontics.

A limitation of this study is the continuous evolution of AI chatbots, indicating that the current versions may not accurately represent the recent iterations available at the time of publication. Furthermore, these systems were not specifically designed for orthodontics, and future model improvements may yield different outcomes. Another limitation lies in the exclusion of orthodontic questions from the past three years (2022–2025), which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the limited number of visual-analysis questions and the exclusive use of multiple-choice formats may not fully capture the models’ potential performance across different exam structures or within a more diverse set of visual data. The exam used in the study is not internationally valid; however, the questions were prepared by orthodontic expertises and each question was reviewed by other faculty members to minimize potential disagreements. A further limitation is that the study was conducted entirely in Turkish. This linguistic context may have influenced the sourcing patterns and the model’s performance. Thus, language effects should be considered when interpreting the comparative outcomes.

In conclusion, current AI-powered chatbots achieved high accuracy regarding orthodontic questions, but showed markedly lower performance on visual interpretation skills. ChatGPT-5.0 provided the most reliable references, primarily textbooks and English-language articles. Copilot cited more Turkish sources, and Claude 3.7 cited almost none. These demonstrated both the potential and shortcomings of AI chatbots; therefore, AI systems should be viewed as complementary aids in orthodontic education, not as primary diagnostic or assessment tools.
