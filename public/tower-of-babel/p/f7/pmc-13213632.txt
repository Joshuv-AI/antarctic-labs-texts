Adrenal neuroblastoma presenting as lameness in a six-year-old boy: a case report
Authors: Amina Karray, Yassmine Houas, Abir Bousetta, Elhem Jbebli, Faouzi Nouira, Said Jlidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most lethal extracranial solid malignancy in children and presents with diverse clinical manifestations. Extra-abdominal symptoms may delay diagnosis. This case involves a previously healthy 6-year-old boy who presented with a one-month history of isolated lameness. Initial clinical examination and pelvic radiography were unremarkable. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated bilateral bone metastases, while abdominal imaging revealed a right adrenal mass. Surgical biopsy and histological analysis confirmed a non-amplified NMYC adrenal NB. The patient received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, complete surgical resection, and adjuvant chemotherapy. Histopathological examination confirmed a differentiating NB. The postoperative course was uneventful.

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most common and deadliest extracranial solid malignancy in children.1 More than 50% of patients present with metastatic disease at diagnosis.1 Bone metastases occur in approximately 56% of cases of metastatic NB.2 Over half of NB cases originate in the adrenal glands and frequently present with non-specific symptoms. These misleading clinical presentations, together with the critical need for accurate diagnosis, complicate NB management. This case highlights a potential diagnostic pitfall and emphasizes the challenges in the clinical recognition and management of adrenal NB.

Case report

A previously healthy six-year-old boy presented with a one-month history of isolated, non-traumatic lameness that progressively worsened, leading to hospital admission for further evaluation. Upon admission, his general condition remained stable. Palpation of the left sacroiliac joint produced pain on the right side. Examination of the spine and hips revealed no abnormalities, and abdominal examination was unremarkable. Pelvic X-ray findings were normal. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed findings suggestive of bilateral bone metastases (Fig. 1). To identify the primary tumor, an abdominal ultrasound was conducted, revealing a right retroperitoneal mass. Further characterization with abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a heterogeneous right adrenal mass measuring 56 × 44 × 48 mm, not crossing the midline, with central macrocalcifications and areas of necrosis (Fig. 2A and B). Adrenal neuroblastoma was strongly suspected. Urinary catecholamine metabolite levels were within normal limits. Surgical biopsy and histological analysis, including evaluation of the NMYC oncogene, revealed a non-amplified NMYC adrenal neuroblastoma. The patient received seven cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which were well tolerated. Follow-up CT imaging demonstrated tumor regression to 39 × 23 × 36 mm (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, an en bloc resection of the right adrenal gland was performed, achieving complete macroscopic resection (Fig. 3). Histopathological examination confirmed a differentiating neuroblastoma. The postoperative course was uneventful. Discussion

NB is an embryonal neuroendocrine tumor originating from neural crest progenitor cells and can develop anywhere along the sympathetic nervous system. It most frequently occurs in the adrenal glands and is the most common abdominal tumor in childhood.3 Clinical presentations of NB vary widely, ranging from widespread metastatic disease to spontaneous regression. Early diagnosis is generally straightforward when symptoms relate to the primary tumor, such as abdominal distension, pain, or an asymptomatic palpable mass. However, adrenal NB can grow substantially while remaining clinically silent, with initial presentations sometimes occurring outside the abdomen. In such cases, diagnosis is more challenging due to non-specific signs, including bone marrow involvement, bone metastases, or paraneoplastic syndromes.1 More than 50% of NB patients present with metastatic disease at diagnosis.4 NB disseminates rapidly and extensively via lymphatic and hematogenous routes. Adrenal NB commonly involves regional lymph nodes and, although it rarely invades the aorta or inferior vena cava, it may adhere closely to these structures. Hematogenous spread most frequently affects the bone marrow, bones, and liver. Several reports describe adrenal NB presenting orthopedic symptoms. Orthopedic presentations account for approximately 22% of NB cases.5 Patients with bone metastases often present with bone pain, limping, or irritability,1 and in some cases, metastases are revealed by pathological fractures. Some authors recommend abdominal ultrasonography for children presenting with limping or suspected septic arthritis of the hip. This simple, rapid, and cost-effective imaging modality may help prevent delays in diagnosing adrenal NB.5 More advanced imaging techniques, such as CT scans or, when available, MRI, should also be considered. MRI is particularly effective for detecting bone lesions. When clinical suspicion of malignancy is high, imaging fails to identify a primary tumor, bone marrow biopsy is recommended. Cases of metastatic NB without an identifiable primary mass have been reported, possibly reflecting dissemination from a regressed or fully matured primary tumor.4

Conclusion

In any child presenting with limping, a neoplastic cause, particularly adrenal NB, must be included in the differential diagnosis. Abdominal imaging is recommended when uncertainty exists to enable prompt initiation of appropriate management. Early recognition and intervention are critical to improving the prognosis of adrenal NB.
