Social relations and mental health among engineering students
Authors: Syed Inshaallah Tahir, Tasleema Jan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEngineering programmes are frequently hard and stressful, which puts students under a lot of pressure to perform well academically, which can be harmful to their mental health.MethodsThis study was conducted to compare male and female engineering students on Social Relations and Mental Health. The study sample consisted of 600 engineering students (364 male and 236 female) selected randomly from different institutions offering B.Tech. engineering courses. An independent t-test was conducted to compare the two groups on these variables, while linear regression analysis was used to examine the impact of social relations on mental health.ResultsThe results reveal that female engineering students have high social relations and good mental health in comparison to their male counterparts. To discover gender-specific demands and obstacles within the intense academic environment of engineering, a gender-based comparison of these variables is imperative. The linear regression analysis revealed significant impact of social relations on mental health.ConclusionsThe conclusions of this study support the recommendation that educational practices that create more equal and encouraging learning environments be supported, since they will produce well-rounded engineers with high social skills and emotional resilience.Keywords: Social relations, Mental health, Engineering students, Academic environment, Social skills

Introduction

Social relations and education

Every individual has the urge to fit in with their social group, get acceptance, and have a certain role in society. The educational institution is an epicentre of unparalleled human relationships [1]. Friends, neighbours, colleagues, and other acquaintances are all considered part of social relations. These relationships are impacted by both positive factors, such as emotional support, and negative factors, such as stress and conflict [2]. Classmates, teachers and parents are the three main sources of relationships that have an impact on students’ educational and personal lives [3]. Each has a substantial bearing on the development and good functioning of learners. Social relationships give people the confidence to open up about their innermost thoughts and feelings [4]. In higher education, social connections are essential for stress management and better adjustment [5]. Families should foster a trusting atmosphere where children can freely communicate their ideas and emotions [6]. In addition to significantly influencing students’ academic drive and involvement, interpersonal ties are also crucial to theories concerning educational activities and motivation [7]. Mental growth of adolescents and young adults’ is significantly influenced by their social ties. Among the most significant social relationship contexts in the lives of teenagers and young people are those with family, friends, teachers and relatives. The social and academic growth of students are enhanced by social connection factors [8].

Young people’s involvement in educational institutions and their decision about whether to continue or quit are significantly influenced by their interactions with their families, peers, and teachers [7]. According to Croninger and Lee [9], students’ likelihood of dropping out of school appears to be halved when they feel their teachers actually care about them. Higher education involves a lot of stress and obstacles that can cause a person to have a range of social, physical, and mental issues [10].

Mental health concerns in students

Depression and feelings of anxiety are the two mental health conditions that students experience most frequently and as reported by Pedrelli et al. [11], because many people do not seek therapy or follow their treatment programmes, mental health disorders often persist for a long period. Research has also indicated that the prevalence and severity of mental health issues among college students are rising [12]. Students at college go through high levels of anxiety, moderate levels of stress, and depression [13]. Psychological issues can have a significant effect on students’ academic performance since depression affects learning even outside of depressive episodes by impairing memory, attention, and decision-making [14]. The frequency of mental health problems has a substantial impact on students’ educational, professional, and personal outcomes. University students, especially those in engineering, face particular pressures and have a lower propensity to seek help, which can make their mental health problems worse and last longer [15]. The likelihood of male students using the services is lower than female students [16]. Male students report receiving less assistance [17]. Students often exhibit reluctance in seeking help due to the perception that mental health challenges signify weakness, potentially hampering their professional growth and career advancement [18]. Untreated mental health issues can make it difficult for a person to handle challenges, but they do not define who they are; instead, they are a natural response to difficult situations [19]. A balanced emotional and personal development is necessary for mental health to effectively handle stress, produce quality work, and make a meaningful contribution to the community [20].

Mental health concerns in engineering students

Students looking for demanding and fascinating career prospects may find engineering degrees appealing since they are frequently linked to innovation and a variety of job possibilities in public as well as private sectors [21]. It is believed that engineering identity is linked to perseverance in both academic and professional settings [22]. Jensen et al. [23] mentioned that an essential area of attention for engineering educators is the mental health of engineering students. Tait et al. [24] opined that engineering students face a difficult academic environment and are more susceptible to mental health issues. According to them, they are also less likely to seek assistance while experiencing these issues. These results are consistent with the results reported by Wright et al. [25] who have observed that in comparison to other students, the likelihood that engineering students with psychological issues will seek professional help is significantly lower. According to Sánchez-Peña et al. [26], engineering students’ mental health issues are exacerbated by financial difficulties, career concerns, and academic stress. The stigma keeps students from asking for help, making it even more difficult to continue, and the high workload increases the likelihood of dropping out. Jensen and Cross [27] noted that engineering students perceive their academic programmes as unique, and they frequently attribute this distinction to the higher stress levels they experience compared to students in other fields. The idea that engineering education must involve hardship may lead to or worsen negative attitudes toward stress and mental health in the field [23]. The difficulties unique to the engineering field may cause students to experience higher stress levels [28]. Supporting the same argument Wright et al. [25] highlighted that an unsupportive setting for engineering training leads to stress. Hargis et al. [29] reported that stress and worry have become so normalised in the profession of engineering that students are unaware of the need for professional care, which could lead to a higher risk of undetected mental health disorders.

Relation between mental health and social relations

Santini et al. [30] reported that poor social connections have a harmful impact on mental health. People in good mental health are better able to establish and sustain relationships, carry out culturally-specific social duties, adapt to change, and communicate and manage their emotions efficiently. It gives them a feeling of value, autonomy, and comprehension of their experiences inside and outside themselves [31]. In order to support young people in maintaining their mental health, friendship and interpersonal connections are important [32]. Relationships can be impacted by mental health; for example, symptoms of depression like pessimism might lower interest or drive in long-term objectives like completing a degree [33]. Aside from other focused therapies, implementing programmes modifications might also increase student happiness, reduce stress, and promote mental health in general [34].

Social support and individual wellbeing

Social support is the assistance that people anticipate or get from others, whether through formal support organizations or unofficial personal relationships [35]. Often called social support, this support is a psychosocial coping mechanism that enhances individuals’ own resources, including self-efficacy and self-esteem, and mitigates the adverse impacts of stress [36]. Kawachi and Berkman [37] emphasized that social support plays a central role in shaping mental health and well-being through multiple pathways. Cohen and Wills [38] suggested through the stress-buffering theory that perceived social support reduces the damaging effects of stressful life events by providing emotional and practical support. Strong social ties help students cope with stress and safeguard their psychological equilibrium, but a lack of support exacerbates feelings of depression and anxiety [21]. Friends, relatives, and significant others might offer emotional, practical, or informational support [39]. According to some research, students in middle school who feel more supported by their peers and parents are less likely to experience depression [40–42]. According to Reid et al. [43], students who have been bullied in the past may have less anxiety during their first year of college if they perceive that their families are providing them with social support. Good mental health is regularly associated with perceived support, which is generally attributed to objectively supportive behaviours that reduce stress [44]. Building healthy relationships with family and friends provides a sense of support, reduces anxiety and promotes emotional stability [45]. Reliable social support, good interpersonal relationships across ethnic groups, and effective communication skills can all help shield people from mental health issues like depression [46]. Gottlieb and Bergen [35] assert that sources and forms of support are interrelated, with research demonstrating that stronger bonds between various forms of support are caused by sentiment override, in which we view close people as offering all kinds of support. Thoits [47] reported that a person’s behaviour patterns and psychological and physical well-being are influenced by their social support system. Presence of supportive relationships whether emotional, informational, instrumental, or appraisal-based buffers individuals against stress, enhance resilience, and improves somatic and mental health outcomes [48–50]. Social interactions benefit students in handling university stress, yet strong home attachment can induce homesickness, and social pressure may impair personal and academic advancement [5]. In addition to being a major component of learning and motivation theories, relations with others are crucial to students’ academic engagement and drive to learn [7]. Individuals with strong social connections experience better mental health, reduced stress, and improved overall functioning [17, 51]. A graduate student’s mental health and determination to continue their education are greatly influenced by the social support they receive from their relationships [15].

Rationale of the study

Concern over mental health issues among undergraduate students is on the rise worldwide, especially in rigorous academic disciplines like engineering [23]. While prior studies have examined the specific effects of academic pressures and support networks on students’ well-being in great detail [39–41, 41], social relationships, including its domains like peer relationships, familial support, and interactions with faculty, have recently drawn more attention. However, few studies have examined the direct relation or interaction between social relations and mental health outcomes among engineering students, a group uniquely positioned in a high-pressure learning environment [27]. Furthermore, empirical research that considers both of these variables, i.e., social relations and mental health, including their respective dimensions, at the same time, is notably lacking [15, 23], especially in non-Western contexts like Kashmir, India. By investigating the relationship between engineering students’ social relationships and mental health, this study aims to close this knowledge gap and offer insights that are both culturally and educationally relevant.

Moreover, Students’ mental health and social interactions are significantly impacted by their gender. Studies have indicated that there are significant differences between male and female students in terms of how they feel and react to psychological stress, as well as how they establish and use social support networks [16, 17, 25]. In engineering education, a traditionally male-dominated sector [25], where social dynamics and coping methods can differ greatly across genders, these gender-based inequalities are especially pertinent. Thus, analysing gender disparities offers a more profound understanding of how social relationships and mental health interact in this particular academic setting.

Operational definitions of the variables

Social relations: The connections between individuals who engage in frequent encounters that the participants believe hold personal significance are referred to as social relationships [52].

Social Relations is operationally defined by the dominant set of scores obtained by sample subjects on Social Relationship Scale by Kumar et al. [53].

Mental health: “Mental health is a state of well-being in which an individual realizes his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and is able to make a contribution to his or her community” [54].

Mental Health is operationally defined by the dominant set of scores obtained by sample subjects on the Mental Health Checklist by Kumar [55].

Method and sample

There are only five institutions in Kashmir offering B.Tech. programme. The study was conducted on engineering students pursuing B.Tech. courses in four institutions of Kashmir namely (1) IOT, Zakura, (2) IUST, Awantipora, (3) SSM, Pattan and (4) GCET, Safapora. The sample for this study consists of 600 engineering students selected randomly from different engineering courses (Civil, Mechanical, Electrical and Electronics & Communication Engineering).

The study involved B.Tech. 5th semester engineering students. The B.Tech. (Bachelor of Technology) programme commonly pursued in countries like India, is a four-year undergraduate (bachelor’s degree) degree in engineering, consists of a total of eight semesters, with two semesters per academic year. It is equivalent to a Bachelor of Engineering (B.E.) in many other countries and involves rigorous coursework and practical training in various engineering disciplines.

The reason for the inclusion of 5th semester students only was to control the effect of being new to the engineering field in the institutions and being more senior in the same field. So, the researcher decided to select students from the middle of their journey in engineering education. The sample was stratified based on Gender and therefore, the sampling technique employed is Stratified Random Sampling Technique. The breakup of the sample is given in Fig. 1 below.

Sample breakup

The binary classification of male and female, served as the basis for defining gender in this study. In the demographic section of the tools used, participants self-report their gender and none of the participants was identified as third gender or any other non-binary gender.

Data collection

The variables Social Relations and Mental Health were assessed among engineering students with the help of standardised scales. The social relationships scale by Kumar et al. [53] was used to evaluate the level of social relations among engineering students. The scale has seven dimensions: Parents, Siblings, Friends, Relatives, Classmates, Teachers, and Strangers. The scale showed strong reliability, with a test–retest coefficient of 0.93.

Kumar’s [55] Mental Health Checklist was used to assess mental health. The Checklist has two dimensions viz., Mental and Somatic. The reverse scoring procedure of the Checklist indicate that higher the score poor the mental health and vice versa. The dimensions of the scales are shown in Fig. 2. The scale’s test–retest reliability was measured at 0.81.

Dimensions of social relationships scale and mental health checklist

Analysis procedures

The collected data were analysed using both descriptive and inferential statistical techniques, including graphical representations (line charts, histogram and PP Plot) were used. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare male and female engineering students on the variables of social relations and mental health. The study employed simple linear regression analysis to examine the influence of social relations on mental health. This analysis assessed the predictive relationship between the independent variable (social relations) and the dependent variable (mental health). All statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences), and significance was determined at the conventional alpha level of 0.05. The normality of data was checked through skewness and kurtosis. The skewness and kurtosis values of the variables viz. Social Relations and Mental Health as reflected in Tables 1 and 2 indicate that the data is fairly symmetrical as the skewness values of the variables range from -0.5 to 0.5 respectively.

The relation between social relationships and mental health was investigated using regression analysis. The model summary in Table 5 indicates a strong connection between these factors. The model explains 54% of the variance in mental health, with a R Square value of 0.540, indicating a strong explanatory power of the independent variable i.e. Social Relations. The statistical significance of the model (F (1, 598) = 703.200, p < 0.001) is confirmed by the ANOVA results, indicating that social relations is a significant predictor of mental health. The unstandardized coefficient signifies that for every unit of increase in social relations score, mental health score decreases by 0.179 units indicating improvement in mental health (lower mental health score indicates good health). The model’s statistical significance is evidenced by a t-value of 26.51 and a p-value of 0.000. The coefficient was estimated with precision, as evidenced by the standard error of 0.007. Social relations and mental health are strongly correlated, as indicated by the standardized beta value of − 0.73. Mental health score decreases by 0.73 standard deviations for every 1 standard deviation increase in Social Relations. This implies that students’ mental health reflects a noticeable and significant change in tandem with the change of their social relationships. This coefficient’s strength emphasizes how important social relationships are in determining students’ mental health.

Discussion

In analysing the research question, “Do male and female engineering students differ significantly in their levels of social relations?”, the analysis revealed that the mean difference of 3.37 between male and female engineering students on social relations was statistically significant. Therefore, based on these findings, the null hypothesis (H01) is rejected. Significant difference at 0.05 level was found between male and female engineering students on the different dimensions of social relations including Parents, Siblings/Cousins, Peers/Classmates and Teachers dimension where mean score favours female students therefore, they were found to be more socially related to them as compared to their male counterparts. On the other hand, in the Friends and Stranger dimension a significant mean difference was found where males were more socially related to friends and strangers than their counterparts. However, the analysis showed no significant difference between the groups on the Relatives dimension, suggesting that both groups have comparable social relationships with their relatives. The findings lead to the conclusion that female engineering students have high social relations than their male counterparts. This research lends credence to the belief that women prioritize and uphold stronger social ties [35, 56]. These dynamics might be influenced by the engineering school atmosphere, which men have historically dominated. To deal with the difficulties, they encounter in these settings, female students frequently build strong relationships (with ‘peers,’ ‘mentors’ and ‘teachers’) that encourage them and somewhat ease the burden of their education [57]. Success in school may hinge significantly on the quality of relationships with teachers [58]. Positive peer and teacher relationships increase school satisfaction, while difficulties with peers and a lack of mutual respect and support from parents decrease it [59]. The results counter the notion of García et al. [60] that boys feel higher support from parents than girls.

When examining the research question, “Is there a significant difference in mental health status between male and female engineering students?”, the results revealed a significant difference in the average mental health scores of male and female engineering students. The mean score indicates that female students exhibit good mental health status than their male counterparts as depicted by their mean scores. Thus, magnitude of the mean differences (0.70) was found to be significant. The Somatic Health of male and female engineering students is comparable as revealed by the analysis results, probably because they participate in the same level of classwork and lab-work activities in engineering institutions. This includes headache, tiredness, disturbed sleep, indigestion and acidity. Hence, in the light of these findings the null hypothesis (H02) stands rejected. This allows the conclusion that, in comparison to male students, female engineering students have good mental health. Female students may have improved psychological health outcomes because they are more likely to ask for assistance, discuss their issues with parents and friends, and utilize the mental health resources that are accessible. On the other hand, gender norms and cultural expectations that place emphasis on emotional control and self-reliance may discourage male students from reaching out for social assistance. This is in contradiction with the results of Jadal [61]. Similarly, the findings did not agree with the evidence provided by the researchers [27, 62]. The state of mental health, anxiousness and depressive disorders did not differ substantially by gender [63, 64].

In exploring the research question, “How do social relations influence the mental health of engineering students?” the regression analysis demonstrated that social relations is significantly related with mental health. Since every Cook’s Distance value is less than 1 (min 0.007 to max 0.027), there are no significant outliers in the data that might affect the reliability of the regression analysis [65]. The P-P Plot (Fig. 6) supports the validity and robustness of the regression model, increasing confidence in the findings i.e., relationship between social relations and mental health. In the light of the results the null hypothesis (H03) stands rejected. It indicates that good social relations (including relations with parents, siblings/cousins, teachers, classmates, and friends) contribute to good mental health.

The findings from the regression analysis shed important light on how different dimensions of social relationships and mental health are related. Peer and sibling relationships have relatively significant standardized coefficients, indicating that they are the strongest predictors in the model. The significance of strong, encouraging bonds with family members and peers in promoting mental health is highlighted by this finding. Peers in particular may have a major influence because they are frequently the main source of emotional support, especially in adolescence and young adulthood.

Similarly, siblings and cousin relationships, which are frequently marked by shared experiences and enduring attachments, offer a feeling of emotional stability and belonging, both of which improve mental health. Although they have a relatively lesser impact, other important predictors also have an impact on mental health, including relationships with parents, strangers, friends, and teachers. Supportive relationships with parents are often linked to emotional stability and resilience, while supportive relations with teachers provide guidance and encouragement, particularly in educational contexts. Interestingly, there is a strong correlation between mental health and even relationships with strangers. This could be a reflection of the wider influence that social contacts and community connectedness have on psychological well-being. Relationships with relatives are the only predictor that falls short of statistical significance. This could be due to the varying nature of these relationships, which may range from highly supportive to more distant or strained.

The study emphasizes how important social relations are for improving mental health. Good relationships are essential for promoting a sense of wellbeing and belonging because they lower stress levels and offer emotional support. These results demonstrate how critical it is for academic institutions and decision-makers to foster student socialization situations. In this approach, students’ mental health may be significantly improved, which will improve their overall quality of life and learning. Tahir and Jan [4] in their study stated that mental discomfort is more likely to occur when there are minimal social interactions. According to Dave [66], people who disregard crucial facets of their lives, such in-person relations, are more likely to suffer from mental illnesses. By taking care of these things, people can reduce their chance of experiencing mental health problems. Mori et al. [67] reported that in low-income nations in particular, informal support networks comprising friends, family, and teachers are essential in addressing the lingering need for mental wellness. Peer interactions are essential for promoting mental health and perception of self among youngsters, and they differ from the support that comes from parents and teachers [19]. Through friendships marked by admiration and affection for one another, students can meet their requirements for personal development [68]. Policymakers can help improve the academic outcomes of engineering students by addressing the causes of their psychological stress and creating a more supportive learning environment [69].

Conclusion and recommendations

This analysis leads to the conclusion that social relations are positively correlated with mental health and has a strong impact on mental health. Consequently, it is recommended that educators and parents cultivate positive relationships with their students/children, as this is critical to their mental health. These findings highlight the critical role of social relationships in shaping mental health outcomes. It is suggested that interventions aimed at improving mental health should consider fostering positive and supportive relationships across different social contexts, including family, peers, friends and community networks. Students are also recommended to build relationships with family members and teachers and to express their emotions, as this can help them cope with stress and anxiety and achieve a more balanced life. Teachers may create inclusive and respectful learning environments, encourage positive peer relationships, and address issues that impede development by having a thorough understanding of these social relations. In the end, our research contributes to the importance of developing learning environments in which every student can succeed intellectually, socially, and emotionally. Because mental health has an impact on one’s overall quality of life, it’s important to promote well-being and avoid major difficulties by identifying and addressing students’ problems early.

Limitations

While the present study provides valuable insights into the relationship between social relations and the mental health of engineering students of Kashmir, it is not without limitations. One key limitation is that the sample was restricted to students from 5th semester only. This limitation reduces the generalizability of the findings to engineering students at different stages of their academic journey. Students in their first semester, for example, may experience different levels of social relations compared to those in later semesters, which could impact the broader applicability of the results. Including students from different academic years in to future studies might help provide a more thorough understanding of the processes at work during an engineering student’s whole academic journey. Additionally, the variables (such as gender, stream of engineering, family background, prior academic exposure and socio-economic status) were not explored in depth beyond the scope of the primary objectives, which could have an influence on the results. This may limit the generalizability of the findings across different demographic groups. Future research should examine these demographic dimensions more comprehensively.
