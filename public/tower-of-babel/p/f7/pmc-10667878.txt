Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide [1]. Common sites of metastatic lung disease include the brain, liver, adrenal glands, and bones. Gastrointestinal (GI) metastasis of primary lung adenocarcinoma is extremely rare, with a clinical incidence of <1 % [[2], [3], [4]]. The presence of an isolated GI metastasis is an ever-rarer entity, with majority of patients with GI metastasis displaying metachronous disseminated metastatic disease [3,4]. The most common site for GI metastatic disease in lung cancer is the small intestine [5]. Those typically with the presence metastatic disease to the GI tract have poorer prognosis and deemed a finding of advanced disease [3]. Given this, surgical management of metastatic disease to the GI tract can be controversial, however, should be considered in individual cases. We present a rare case of primary lung adenocarcinoma metastasis to the small bowel in a 60-year-old female. This case is reported in line with the SCARE criteria and informed consent was obtained from patient for publication of case report [6].

Presentation of case

A 60-year-old female was referred with a small bowel mass found after workup for colicky abdominal pain and intermittent diarrhoea for three months. She denied symptoms of rectal bleeding, weight loss, vomiting, persistent cough, haemoptysis or carcinoid type symptoms. Her significant medical/surgical history included a 40-pack year smoking history and a normal colonoscopy two years prior. Her Drug and social history was unremarkable. Her abdominal examination was benign with no palpable masses.

Initial computed tomography (CT) imaging demonstrated a non-obstructing distal small bowel mass with adjacent lymphadenopathy, concerning for a neoplastic aetiology (Fig. 1). CT chest and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level were performed to complete staging for presumed small bowel malignancy. CT chest revealed a mediastinal soft tissue mass measuring 4.8 × 3.1 cm in maximal axial dimension (Fig. 2). CEA level was 271 ng/mL. Further investigation via bronchoscopy was performed which confirmed a large conglomerate mediastinal mass. Cytology results demonstrated poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, positive for CK7 and TTF1, favouring primary lung adenocarcinoma. FDG PET scan demonstrated a PET avid mass in the chest as well an abdominal mass with associated lymphadenopathy (Fig. 3).

Due to developing obstructive symptoms, an elective small bowel resection was performed. Intra-operative findings included an obstructing cicatrised lesion in the mid small bowel with an adjacent enlarged lymph node. No peritoneal disease or other lesions were identified. Histopathology confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma, arising from the subserosa. One out of sixteen lymph nodes were positive. Immunohistochemistry staining was consistent with primary lung adenocarcinoma (eGFR, CK20, GATA3, PAX8, CD10 negative, CK7 and TTF1 positive). The patient was discharged on post-operative day three. She received chemoradiotherapy for the lung lesion and remains well 3 years later.

Discussion

The presence of GI metastatic disease is considered a finding of advanced disease and associated with a poor prognosis as they are typically metachronous, and are often multiple/disseminated [7]. In previous case series, most patients died within three months of diagnosis of GI metastatic disease [[3], [4], [5]]. Only one case was identified where a patient with isolated small bowel metastatic disease was resected and was alive 5 years post resection [7]. As a result of this prognostic data, the role of surgical intervention remains controversial and needs to be carefully considered and individualised to each patient. Current evidence suggests that surgical resection in symptomatic patients has therapeutic benefit and is associated with improved overall survival. [4,5] Further evidence suggests that resection should be considered even in palliative patients to prevent complications such as bowel obstruction, haemorrhage and perforation [8].

Autopsy data suggests the presence of small bowel metastasis is higher than what is observed clinically due to the volume of asymptomatic patients [2]. It is likely that with advances in screening and medical imaging, more GI metastasis in lung cancer patients will be identified and the role of surgical intervention will continue to be explored [9]. Current evidence provided through previous case series supports a role for surgical resection for select patients, particularly for those who are symptomatic or who have an isolated GI metastasis on imaging [4,5].

Of particular interest in this case is the extremely high CEA level of 271 ng/mL (normal range < 3.0 ng/mL) at the time of diagnosis. On review of the available literature, the authors were unable to identify another case where metastatic lung adenocarcinoma was associated with a CEA of >200 ng/mL. It remains unclear if the degree of elevation has implications for overall survival or further metastasis, however monitoring the decline in CEA level with treatment and during surveillance for recurrent disease will be important. In relation to lung cancer, a CEA value >2.57 ng/mL seems to correlate with increased risk of cancer recurrence with a sensitivity of 71.4 % and specificity of 72.3 % [10]. One possible explanation as to why the absolute CEA value is not necessarily correlated with outcomes is that CEA levels may not necessarily be due to tumour volume or site of metastasis but rather due to the individual metabolic characteristics and mutations of individual tumours and patients [11]. The involvement of small bowel in this case may also have contributed to high CEA levels, however this is only speculation as no research has been done to study this correlation. Further research in this area is required to further define the role and value of CEA in lung cancer, specifically in the setting of GI metastatic disease.

Conclusion

This case highlights a very uncommon scenario of lung adenocarcinoma presenting as an isolated, symptomatic small bowel metastasis. It was successfully surgically resected, followed by treatment of the primary. GI metastatic disease usually presents late, is associated with advanced disease, and subsequently a very poor prognosis. Literature supports surgical resection for select symptomatic patients and/or patients who have isolated GI metastasis. The role of CEA in lung cancer and GI metastatic disease requires further research and validation.
