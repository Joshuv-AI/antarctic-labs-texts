Evaluation of PISA, serum IL-12 and IL-18 in stage III periodontitis with and without type 2 diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Shefali Mangrolia, Rajashri Abhay Kolte, Abhay Pandurang Kolte, Shahabe Saquib Abullais, Saad AlQahtani, Aishwarya Ikhar, Ritika Rakesh Gattani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have shown a positive association between periodontitis and diabetes mellitus. However, there is a lack of comprehensive data regarding their combined effect on Periodontal inflamed surface area (PISA), serum Interleukin-12 and Interleukin-18. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess PISA, along with serum IL-12 and IL-18 levels, in individuals diagnosed with Stage III Periodontitis, both with and without type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).Methods79 participants categorized into: Group I comprising 19 periodontally healthy individuals while Groups II and III included 30 patients each with stage III periodontitis without and with T2DM, respectively. Clinical assessments of probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), bleeding on probing (BOP) and periodontal inflamed surface area (PISA) were performed alongside evaluation of serum IL-12 and IL-18 using enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Additionally, blood investigations included fasting blood glucose, 2-hour postprandial glucose, and HbA1c levels were recorded.ResultsPatients in Group II and III demonstrated elevated values for all clinical measurements with significant differences between mean PISA values in Group II (254.21 ± 229.21 mm2) and Group III (401.93 ± 517.61 mm2). Additionally, serum IL-12 and IL-18 were found to be comparatively higher in Group III than in Group II.ConclusionThe findings suggest significantly elevated PISA values for Group III patients and greater severity of periodontal disease as compared to the others. Further research is necessary to better understand the interplay between periodontitis and T2DM, particularly focusing on inflammatory biomarkers and PISA.CTRI NumberThe trial was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2023/06/053653) on 08/06/2023.Keywords: Stage III periodontitis, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Interleukin-12, Interleukin-18, Periodontal Inflamed Surface Area

Introduction

Periodontal diseases refer to a range of conditions that impact the structures supporting the teeth, such as the gingiva, periodontal ligament, root cementum, and alveolar bone. The most prevalent types- gingivitis and periodontitis- are initiated by microbial activity, which trigger host immune responses that contribute to the destruction of periodontal tissues [1]. Periodontitis adds to the overall systemic inflammatory load by allowing the passage of bacteria, cytokines, and other inflammatory agents through the ulcerated lining of periodontal pockets into the circulatory system [2]. Tonetti et al. [3] introduced a classification system for periodontitis that includes staging and grading. Stage III involves severe attachment loss, deep pockets, and complex treatment needs. Grading (A-C) reflects disease progression and is influenced by risk factors like smoking and diabetes.

Type 2 Diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is characterized by a progressive reduction in insulin secretion, often accompanied by insulin resistance. A bidirectional inflammatory relationship exists between diabetes and periodontitis, where each condition may intensify the severity of the other [4]. The association between diabetes and periodontal disease was first proposed over fifty years ago, and numerous global studies have since reinforced this connection. As a result, periodontitis is now recognized as the ‘sixth complication’ of diabetes. Inadequate glycemic control has been consistently linked to heightened gingival inflammation in individuals with T2DM [1].

To effectively evaluate the role of periodontitis in contributing to systemic diseases, it is essential to quantify the extent of inflamed periodontal tissue. The periodontal inflamed surface area (PISA) was developed for this purpose, representing the surface area (in square millimeters) of bleeding pocket epithelium. As a specific marker of inflamed periodontal tissue, PISA provides a direct measure of the systemic inflammatory loas associated with periodontitis. It is derived using common periodontal parameters, including clinical attachment loss (CAL), bleeding on probing (BOP), and gingival recession. A strong positive correlation has been observed between elevated PISA values and increased HbA1c levels, indicating poor glycemic control [5].

Cytokines are critical in modulating immune response during periodontal inflammation. Interleukin-12 (IL-12) plays a pivotal role in early immune defense by promoting the differentiation of naïve T lymphocytes into Th1 cells and inducing interferon-gamma (INF- γ) production by natural killer (NK) cells and T cells [6]. In 1995, Okamura et al. [7] identified IL-18, initially known as an INF-γ-inducing factor. IL-18, a member of the IL-1 family, is a pro-inflammatory cytokine with antitumor properties. Its effects are significantly amplified in the presence of IL-12, resulting in a robust Th1 immune response and elevated levels of INF- γ, TNF- α, IL-1β, matrix metalloproteinases (like MMP-9), chemokines, adhesion molecules, and nitric oxide- all contributing to chronic periodontal inflammation [7, 8]. Yamada et al. in 2002 [9], demonstrated that IL-12 enhances the expression of IL-18 receptors, increasing cellular responsiveness to IL-18 and promoting synergistic INF-γ secretion. This interaction plays a key role in both inflammation and bone metabolism associated with periodontal disease [7–9].

Since there is a lack of clarity and no prior research has collectively assessed these parameters, the present study aimed to evaluate the PISA, along with serum levels of IL-12 and IL-18, in individuals diagnosed with Stage III periodontitis patients, both with and without T2DM. The null hypothesis formulated was “There is no significant difference in PISA values and serum IL-12 and IL-18 levels among periodontally healthy individuals, patients with Stage III periodontitis, and patients with Stage III periodontitis with type 2 diabetes mellitus.”

Materials & methods

This cross-sectional study was carried out in the Department of Periodontology, following the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1975) and its 2013 revision. The study was registered under the Clinical Trial Registry-India (CTRI) (CTRI/2023/06/053653), a primary registry of the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. Ethical clearance was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the same institution (IEC/VSPMDCRC/9 /2023), and informed written consent was obtained from all participants.

The sample size was determined based on an effect size of 0.35, 80% statistical power, and an allocation ratio of 1:1:1, resulting in the recruitment of 79 participants (both males and females). The study included participants ranging in age from 20 to 60 years. Patients were categorized into three groups: Group I included 19 periodontally healthy individuals, while Group II and Group III consisted of 30 patients each diagnosed with Stage III periodontitis and Stage III periodontitis with T2DM, respectively. Stage III periodontitis was selected because it represents a clinically significant level of periodontal destruction with deeper periodontal pockets and greater inflammatory burden, allowing better evaluation of PISA and systemic inflammatory biomarkers. Selecting a uniform stage also helped reduce heterogeneity in disease severity. Periodontal diagnoses were established according to the 2017 classification guidelines jointly presented by the American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) and the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) [3].

Inclusion criteria

Group I

Individuals with healthy periodontium possessing a minimum of 20 natural teeth, exhibiting a Gingival index (GI) of 0, probing pocket depth (PPD) of ≤ 3 mm, no CAL, and no radiographic evidence of alveolar bone loss.

Group II

Patients diagnosed with Stage III periodontitis possessing a minimum of 20 natural teeth, characterised by a GI score greater than 1, PPD ≥ 6 mm, and CAL ≥ 5 mm. Participants were confirmed to be non-diabetic based on fasting blood glucose, 2-hour postprandial glucose levels, and HbA1c values within the normal diagnostic range.

Group III

Patients with Stage III periodontitis possessing a minimum of 20 natural teeth and coexisting T2DM, showing GI > 1, PPD ≥ 6 mm, CAL ≥ 5 mm, and HbA1c levels ranging between 5% and 7%.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals who had received periodontal therapy within the last six months, exhibited signs of acute illness, were undergoing immunosuppressive treatment, had taken systemic anti-inflammatory medications in the previous three months, or were on drugs known to influence periodontal tissues, as well as pregnant and lactating women, were excluded from the study.

Clinical examination

A comprehensive case history was documented, followed by clinical and hematological assessments for all selected participants. Diagnosis of T2DM was confirmed through blood investigations, after which patients with Stage III periodontitis were categorized into groups with and without T2DM [4]. The intraoral clinical examination was done using UNC-15 periodontal probe1. The clinical parameters assessed included PPD, CAL, Plaque index (PI) [10], Gingival index (GI) [11], and papillary bleeding index (PBI) [12]. PISA was calculated using a Microsoft Excel-based tool2. BMI was calculated using formula weight in Kg upon height in cm2. The intraoral examination was performed on 10 patients before the commencement of the study by a single examiner (SM) to calibrate the examiner for assessment of periodontal parameters. Readings were repeated by the same examiner (SM) two hours after the first measurement in order to perform intra-observer reproducibility analysis. Intraclass correlation (ICC) coefficient with a two-way mixed‐effects model was obtained for each periodontal parameter. The ICC ranged between 0.92 and 0.99 in the groups (P < 0.0001), indicating excellent intra-observer reliability.

Collection of serum samples

A total of 10 ml of venous blood was drawn from the antecubital region using a 20-gauge needle. The samples were centrifuged at 1500 RPM for 10 min, and the separated serum was transferred into sterile tubes and stored at -20 °C until further biochemical evaluation. Serum concentrations of IL-12 and IL-18 were determined using commercially available ELISA kits3. Glycemic parameters such as HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, and 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose was also measured. The concentrations of IL-12 and IL-18 were determined using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The detection range for IL-12 was 3.1–200 pg/mL with a sensitivity of 2.1 pg/mL, while the detection range for IL-18 was 31.25–2000 pg/mL with a sensitivity of approximately 18 pg/mL.

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test along with descriptive statistics. The mean, median, and 5% trimmed mean were comparable across variables, indicating absence of substantial skewness or outliers. Therefore, parametric statistical tests were applied for the analysis of continuous variables. Continuous variables were summarized as a mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare clinical (GI, PI, PPD, PESA), biochemical (Fasting, 2-h postprandial, HbA1c, IL-12, IL-18), and anthropometric (BMI) parameters among Groups I, II and III. Independent t-test was used to compare CAL, BOP, and PISA between Groups II and III. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS software version 214. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Pearson’s Correlation test was applied for correlation of HbA1c with periodontal parameters across study groups.

Discussion

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory condition that comprises the supporting structures of the teeth. Diabetes mellitus is a major global oral health burden, affecting more than half of the population worldwide and is considered the sixth most common disease globally [13, 14]. Traditional classification models for periodontal disease do not adequately reflect the extent of active inflammation within periodontal tissues [15].

To address this limitation, the PISA was introduced as a quantitative tool that estimates the surface area of inflamed periodontal tissue, thereby providing a more accurate measure of the host’s current inflammatory burden [5]. Concurrently, advancements in molecular diagnostics have enabled the incorporation of biomarkers, particularly proinflammatory cytokines, into periodontal disease evaluation. As the cascade of inflammatory responses triggers the release of various pro-inflammatory cytokines, which contribute to the breakdown of connective tissue and alveolar bone, ultimately resulting in tooth loss [16]. The variables such as age, gender and BMI may potentially act as confounding factors because increasing age and higher BMI have been associated with elevated systemic inflammatory responses. However, in the present study, BMI differences among the groups were not statistically significant, and gender distribution was relatively balanced in Groups II and III. The age range of 20–60 years was selected to ensure adequate participant recruitment while representing a broad adult population affected by periodontitis and diabetes. Although age differences existed among groups, this reflects the natural epidemiological trend in which T2DM and severe periodontitis are more prevalent in older individuals. The biomarkers assist in detecting periodontitis and help assess the degree of inflammation and its possible systemic implications. Tools such as PISA and cytokine profiling offer a more integrated and biologically meaningful strategy for the diagnosis and monitoring of periodontal disease [5, 15].

A well-established bidirectional link exists between diabetes and periodontal disease: uncontrolled glycemic levels can aggravate periodontal inflammation, while periodontal infection can negatively influence glycemic control [5, 17].

A major limitation in evaluating the relationship between systemic conditions and periodontitis has been the inconsistency in classification systems and lack of a uniform inflammatory index. PISA addresses this gap by offering a standardized and quantifiable measure of periodontal inflammation, facilitating more accurate assessments of its systemic implications. As originally outlined by Hujoel et al. [18] PISA is calculated using CAL, gingival recession, BOP recorded at six sites per tooth which automates the calculation process, thereby simplifying its application in clinical research [5, 17].

The integration of PISA into periodontal studies improves the evaluation of periodontal disease as a risk indicator for systemic disease. Another parameter, the PESA estimates the surface area of the pocket epithelium and is especially useful when the gingival margin is located at or apical to the CRJ, providing a more precise reflection of tissue involvement [5].

In the present study, PISA values were not assessed in Group I. The mean PISA in Group II was 254.21 ± 229.21 mm2, whereas Group III exhibited greater mean PISA values of 401.93 ± 517.61 mm2. Although the inflammatory burden was elevated in Group III compared to Group II, the difference was not statistically significant. This trend may be attributed to the systemic inflammatory influence of T2DM, wherein fluctuations in the glycemic status may also reflect on the inflammatory burden within the local tissues [17].

Previous studies have reported that individuals with both T2DM and periodontitis tend to have significantly higher PISA scores than T2DM individuals without periodontitis. Moreover, PISA has been positively correlated with HbA1c levels, suggesting that an increased inflammatory burden from periodontitis may be associated with poorer glycemic control [16]. Sakthi Devi et al. [17] demonstrated a linear relationship between elevated PISA values and higher HbA1c levels in patients with T2DM. Similarly, Nesse et al. [19] found the greater PISA was linked with worsened glycaemic status, supporting the association between periodontal inflammation and metabolic control in diabetes.

Ahuja et al. [20] demonstrated elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly TNF- α, in individuals with periodontitis. Their findings suggested that managing periodontal inflammation could potentially support glycaemic control by enhancing insulin sensitivity or delaying diabetes-related complications. Nonetheless, it was also noted that HbA1c levels often reverted to baseline over time considering the increased infection risk and potential changes in dietary or pharmacologic regimens, the present study included only individuals with well controlled diabetes.

In the present study, the men PPD in Group I was 1.17 ± 0.26 mm. Among the groups, the highest mean PPD was noted in Group III followed by Group II. This pattern aligns with the previous findings, where diabetic individuals exhibited elevated PPD values. Additionally, PPD has shown to vary with glycemic control: 2.40 ± 0.097 mm in normoglycemic individuals, increasing progressively to 3.79 ± 0.073 mm in those with poor glycemic control [21]. A similar trend was observed in the current study, with Group II showing mean PPD of 3.98 ± 0.46 mm and Group III recording an even higher value of 4.62 ± 0.32 mm.

CAL was not assessed in Group I. However, Group II had a mean CAL of 3.23 ± 083 mm, while Group III demonstrated a significantly higher mean CAL of 4.17 ± 0.79 mm, suggesting more severe periodontal destruction in individuals with T2DM. These findings are in agreement with previous literature reporting mean CAL values of 2.48 ± 0.16 mm (normal glycemic control), 2.77 ± 0.14 mm (good), 3.40 ± 0.12 (moderate), and 3.97 ± 0.091 mm (poor glycemic control). The significant increase in CAL in poorly controlled diabetics underscores the association between deteriorating glycemic status and worsening periodontal parameters. Furthermore, a systematic review concluded that individuals with T2DM exhibited significantly compromised periodontal health compared to non-diabetic counterparts, reflected by an average increase of 0.61 mm in PPD and 0.89 mm in CAL [22].

Correlational analysis performed between periodontal parameters and HbA1c in the present study suggests that worsening glycaemic status may be modestly associated with reduced epithelial surface area and attachment loss in Group II and Group III.

These observations reinforce the interplay between T2DM and periodontal disease severity.

Recent research has increasingly focused on identifying molecular biomarkers that facilitate early detection, monitor disease progression, and evaluate longitudinal changes. Periodontitis is characterised by a host-mediated immune-inflammatory reaction triggered by pathogenic bacteria [23, 24]. Among the key mediators of this response are proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 and IL-18, which are predominantly released by monocytes and macrophages upon exposure to bacterial components like lipopolysaccharides from gram-negative organisms. IL-12 is instrumental in initiating a Th1 type immune response, while IL-18 amplifies this effect by acting synergistically with IL-12 to induce IFN- γ production by T cells and natural killer (NK) cells [25].

Sánchez-Hernández et al. [6] reported increased serum IL-12 levels in patients with aggressive periodontitis, indicating its potential involving in gingival tissue destruction and its reflection at the systemic level. Consistent with prior findings, the present study observed a significant elevation in IL-18 concentrations across the study groups. Group I showed the lowest mean IL-18 level at 152.63 ± 38.27 pg/mL, while Group II exhibited a marked increase to 344.17 ± 45.85 pg/mL. The highest levels were observed in Group III with a mean value of 581.57 ± 119.01 pg/mL. This progressive increase suggests a direct relationship between IL-18 levels and periodontal disease severity, with further amplification in the presence of systemic conditions such as Type 2 diabetes. These findings support the potential of IL-18 as a biomarker of periodontal inflammation, particularly in metabolically compromised individuals. Previous studies have similarly reported elevated serum IL-18 levels in patients with chronic periodontitis compared to healthy controls, reinforcing its role in periodontal pathogenesis [23].

Additionally, a positive correlation between IL-18 and HbA1c levels has been documented, independent of age and sex, indicating that individuals with higher HbA1c tend to exhibit increased IL-18 concentrations [26]. The present study findings align with this association, further supporting a link between systemic glycaemic status and IL-18 driven inflammatory responses. These cytokines has been linked to insulin resistance in individuals with metabolic syndrome [27].

This study has certain limitations like the relatively small sample size which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally in the present study there is lack of adjustment for potential confounding variables such as age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) and use of anti-diabetic medications in Group III patients which may influence periodontal and metabolic parameters. Moreover, only serum levels of IL-12 and IL-18 were evaluated, while their concentrations in GCF or saliva were not assessed. Future studies with larger sample sizes and multivariable analysis are warranted to account for these potential confounders.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that PISA, along with serum IL-12 and IL-18 levels, were markedly elevated in patients with stage III periodontitis with T2DM, reflecting an increased systemic inflammatory load. Additionally, clinical parameters such as PPD, CAL, and BOP were also significantly higher in diabetic periodontitis patients compared to non-diabetic counterparts, indicating more severe periodontal destruction. The findings support the use of PISA and systemic inflammatory markers for better assessment and personalized care in periodontitis, particularly in diabetic patients.
