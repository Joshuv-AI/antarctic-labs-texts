Healing or Harming? Navigating Resistance and Rationality in Diabetic Foot Ulcer Antibiotic Therapy: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Maria Infant Majula Shifani Mahendran, Vinoj Gopalakrishnan, Vaijayanthi Saravanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent a major complication in patients with diabetes mellitus, resulting in reduced quality of life, frequent hospitalizations, and increased healthcare costs. Our study analyzed the clinical records of 200 patients with DFU selected from a pool of medical records from a tertiary care hospital in India. Our study assessed patterns of drug prescription, determined pathogen profiles and antimicrobial susceptibility, evaluated economic burden, and identified the prevalence and severity of drug–drug interactions (DDIs) among patients with DFUs. Antibiotics were the most commonly prescribed drugs (18.5%) with the highest incidence of serious DDIs (28.5%). These results underscore the importance of optimizing antibiotic stewardship and rational prescribing practices to improve clinical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are the most challenging complication of diabetes and often lead to prolonged hospitalization, amputation, and death.[1] Globally, the prevalence of DFUs was 6.3%, and it is estimated that up to 25% of patients with diabetes will develop foot ulcers in their lifetime.[2] Infected DFUs are often polymicrobial, involving aerobic Gram-positive cocci, Gram-negative bacilli, and anaerobes, which complicates the treatment strategies.[3] The increased prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms and biofilm formation worsens the prognosis of DFU.[4] Moreover, irrational drug prescriptions and drug–drug interactions (DDIs) further complicate the management of DFU.[5] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), prescribing indicators in different ways that lead to an irrational use of medicines, including polypharmacy and antibiotic misuse, result in 50% of pharmaceutical waste.[6] In DFU care, such practices can drive antimicrobial resistance and increase costs. It highlights the importance of antibiotic stewardship, evidence-based prescribing, and integrated pharmacological oversight in DFU management. In India, DFU management generally follows a multidisciplinary approach involving wound debridement, glycemic control, and empiric or culture-guided antibiotics such as amoxicillin–clavulanate, clindamycin, or piperacillin–tazobactam.[7] However, resource constraints often necessitate empirical treatment without culture support, and advanced wound therapies remain underutilized. To address these issues, we conducted a comprehensive pharmacoepidemiological and microbiological evaluation of DFU cases. According to the WHO, irrational use of medicines, including unnecessary polypharmacy and antibiotic misuse, results in up to 50% of pharmaceutical waste. This study combined analysis of prescription patterns and DDIs (pharmacoepidemiology) with pathogen identification and susceptibility testing (microbiology). We aimed to identify the prevailing pathogens and resistance patterns, characterize the medication use and DDI risks, and thereby inform more rational, evidence-based therapy strategies for DFU patients. This study highlights a previously underexplored intersection between microbiological resistance and pharmacological safety in DFU management. The dual burden of multidrug resistance and high-risk drug interactions necessitates an integrated care model that combines microbiological diagnostics, rational prescribing, and proactive pharmacovigilance. Establishing regionally adapted, resistance-informed treatment protocols may significantly reduce adverse outcomes in DFU patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital in India. We reviewed the records of patients with type 2 diabetes admitted between January 2021 and December 2024. Of 1000 screened cases of diabetes mellitus, 200 patients with documented DFUs were included. Patients were eligible if they were ≥ 18 years old with confirmed type 2 diabetes and a clinically diagnosed DFU. Patients were diagnosed with DFUs based on the clinical documentation available in their medical records. Only records with a clearly documented diagnosis of DFU were included. Patients with incomplete medical records, type 1 diabetes, or nondiabetic causes of foot ulceration were excluded. The data were manually extracted into structured case report forms. Prescribed medications were categorized (antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs]/analgesics, antidiabetics, and antihypertensives) and their routes (oral, intravenous, and subcutaneous) recorded. Antidiabetic therapy was noted as oral hypoglycemic agents (OHAs), insulin, or a combination. DDIs were evaluated using the Lexicomp and Micromedex databases and classified by severity. We also recorded the length of hospital stay and the total cost of treatment.

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). We used descriptive statistics (means and percentages) to summarize patient characteristics, prescription patterns, and microbiological findings.

RESULTS

This study included 200 eligible patients with DFU [Table 1]. The majority (63%) of the patients were male, and most (42%) of the patients belonged to the 50–60 years of age group, followed by those aged 60–70 years (23.5%) and 40–50 years (20%). Antibiotics were prescribed at the highest frequency (18.5%). NSAIDs/analgesics and antidiabetics accounted for 14% of patients. Antihypertensives were used in 10.1% of patients, and proton-pump inhibitors were used in 7.5% of patients [Table 2]. Medications related to mental health conditions included antidepressants (3%), antipsychotics (2.5%), and antiepileptics (2.76%). Most treatments (72.26%) were administered orally. Intravenous injections (31.89%) and subcutaneous insulin (6.6%) were also administered for targeted therapy [Figure 1]. Antidiabetic therapy included both insulin (short- and long-acting) and OHAs such as metformin, sitagliptin, and sulfonylureas. Among the patients, 31.5% received only OHAs, while 18% received a combination of insulin and OHAs [Figure 2]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was the most common comorbidity, present in 47.3% of patients. Other comorbidities included hypertension (23.0%), coronary artery disease (2.4%), chronic kidney disease (CKD) (6%) and cerebrovascular accident (2.4%), and Diabetic retinopathy (2.4%) [Supplementary Figure 1].

The most frequently used antibiotics were injectable clindamycin (24.5%), piperacillin–tazobactam (17.0%), meropenem (1.5%) and ceftriaxone (0.5%). Other antibiotics, including amoxicillin–clavulanate, ciprofloxacin, and linezolid, were each used in <10% of cases [Supplementary Table 1]. DDIs were observed in all patients, with 33.3% of interactions classified as intermediate in severity and 66.7% as moderate to severe [Supplementary Table 2]. Notable drug-related problems (DRPs) included hypoglycemia from dapagliflozin–glimepiride combinations (30% of DRPs) and hypotension from amlodipine–nitroglycerin (46.7% of DRPs) [Supplementary Table 3]. A Lexicomp® database antimicrobial interaction assessment identified 38 potential antimicrobial drug–drug interactions (pDDIs). Of these, 34.2% of patients were classified as Category D (therapy modification recommended), 5.3% as Category X (avoid combination), and 60.5% as Category C (monitor therapy). In terms of severity, 13.2% of the pDDIs were minor, 28.9% were major, and 57.9% were moderately severe. The most common interactions involved linezolid with serotonergic agents and fluoroquinolones with corticosteroids. Bacterial culture reports showed that Escherichia coli (36%) was the most common pathogen, followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (21.2%), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (15.2%), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (15.2%). These pathogens exhibited mixed antibiotic susceptibility to meropenem, piperacillin–tazobactam, amikacin, and linezolid [Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 4]. Finally, the usage rates of antibiotics (79.9%) and injectable medications (49.5%) exceeded the WHO recommendations [Supplementary Table 5]. The cumulative duration of therapy corresponded to a total hospital stay of 138 days, during which patients received both intravenous (IV) and oral (PO) antibiotic formulations. IV antibiotics were administered for a mean duration of 66 days, while PO antibiotics were prescribed for 72 days, indicating a transition from parenteral to oral therapy as clinical improvement permitted. Economic analysis revealed that IV formulations contributed to 45.6% of the total antimicrobial expenditure. In contrast, PO antibiotics accounted for a slightly higher proportion (54.4%) [Supplementary Table 6].

DISCUSSION

In our study, antibiotics accounted for 18.5% of the total prescriptions for DFU cases, which is slightly lower than the 22.2% of prescribed antibiotics reported by Varun et al.[8] In the present study, E. coli (36%) was the culture-positive organism most commonly isolated from DFU wounds. Similarly, Alkhatieb et al., also reported a significant prevalence of E. coli infection in DFU.[9] In line with the findings of Santella et al., K. pneumoniae in our study remained sensitive to meropenem and piperacillin/tazobactam.[10] The prevalence of MDR Acinetobacter baumannii and S. aureus highlights the increasing burden of antimicrobial resistance, reinforcing the need for susceptibility-guided antibiotic therapy. Prescription pattern in this study was the diversity of prescribed medications addressing comorbid conditions, including CKD (6%), which reflects the systemic burden of diabetes and its complications. Injectable antibiotics, including clindamycin (24.5%), meropenem (1.5%), highlight the importance of high-potency treatment in severe cases. Moreover, the study’s evaluation of DDIs identified 30% of cases in the severe (“D” risk) category and 46% in the moderate (“C” risk) category, emphasizing the importance of vigilant therapeutic monitoring. Documentation of DDIs further highlights the need for integrated pharmacological oversight in the management of DFU. Patient population in our study was at significant risk, with 33.33% of identified DDIs being classified as intermediate, and 66.66% of the cases were moderate to severe. DRPs included interactions between dapagliflozin and glimepiride, which were associated with a 30% incidence of hypoglycemia. In addition, interactions between amlodipine and nitroglycerin were linked to hypotension in 46.67% of the cases. The study reported a critical need for careful monitoring and adjustment of medications, particularly in polypharmacy scenarios, to mitigate the risks associated with interactions. The interacting medications reported in this study, such as amlodipine and nitroglycerin or dapagliflozin and glimepiride, were not prescribed as fixed-dose combinations. Rather, these agents were administered concurrently as part of disease-specific, multidrug regimens, often targeting comorbid conditions such as hypertension or T2DM. The clinical concern arises from their pharmacodynamic or pharmacokinetic interactions during coadministration, which may alter therapeutic efficacy or increase adverse event risks. The coprescription of dapagliflozin and glimepiride is associated with increased risk of hypoglycemia due to additive glycemic-lowering effects, while combined administration of amlodipine and nitroglycerin can potentiate vasodilation, leading to symptomatic hypotension. These interactions reflect the complex therapeutic needs in DFU patients and underscore the necessity of integrated DDI screening in antimicrobial stewardship frameworks.

CONCLUSION

Our pilot study effectively identified prevalent pathogens and their antibiotic sensitivity patterns. We observed a high rate of antibiotic and injectable use, frequent DDIs, and the presence of resistant organisms. Deep pharmacological surveillance and evidence-based prescribing are essential to balance the benefits of therapy against the risks of resistance and adverse interactions.
