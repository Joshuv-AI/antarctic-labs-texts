Factors influencing surgical management and outcomes of colonic trauma: a prospective observational study at three hospitals in Sana’a City, Yemen
Authors: Ali Lotf Al-Amy, Mohaned Yahia Al-Ajaly, Haitham Mohammed Jowah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColonic trauma is a major clinical challenge particularly in resource-constrained conflict settings. The optimal surgical management remains debated. This study evaluated the factors influencing the surgical approach and the associated outcomes of colonic trauma in Yemen.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational cohort study from May 2020 to April 2021, enrolling 90 consecutive patients with traumatic colonic injuries at three hospitals in Sana’a City. We collected data on patient demographics, injury characteristics (including AAST Colon Injury Scale grade), and postoperative outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed to compare patients managed with primary repair and colostomy.ResultsPrimary repair was performed in 74 patients (82.2%) and colostomy was performed in 16 patients (17.8%). The decision to perform colostomy was significantly associated with gunshot wounds (P = 0.002), severe fecal contamination (P < 0.001), and high-grade AAST injuries (P < 0.001). Although there was no statistically significant difference in the overall mortality (5.4% for primary repair vs. 12.5% for colostomy; P = 0.279), procedure-specific morbidity was profound. The anastomotic leak rate in the subgroup that underwent resection with primary anastomosis was 55.6%. Furthermore, stoma-related complications affected 50% of patients in the colostomy group.ConclusionPrimary repair is the predominant strategy for treating colonic trauma in this conflicting setting, and colostomy is reserved for high-risk patients. However, our findings revealed two critical context-specific dangers: an alarming failure rate for primary anastomosis in severe injuries, and a substantial morbidity burden from stoma formation. These results suggest that when a simple primary suture is not feasible, a damage-control approach with an end colostomy may be the safest option in an austere surgical environment.Keywords: Colonic trauma, Primary repair, Colostomy, Stoma complications, Observational study, Conflict surgery, Trauma, Injury severity

Introduction

Colonic injuries constitute a formidable challenge in contemporary trauma surgery and are characterized by significant morbidity and mortality risks attributable to the inherent septic potential of the colonic contents. The historical trajectory of colonic trauma management reflects a dramatic evolution in surgical philosophy and outcomes. During World War I, mortality rates approached 60%, establishing a paradigm of extreme caution that has persisted for decades [1]. Although modern advances in surgical techniques, critical care, and antimicrobial therapy have substantially reduced mortality, associated morbidity, particularly infectious complications, remain a persistent concern, with abdominal sepsis rates of approximately 20% in contemporary series [2, 3].

The fundamental surgical management paradigm encompasses two distinct approaches: primary repair, which includes simple suture closure or resection with primary anastomosis; and fecal diversion, which typically involves colostomy with or without resection (such as Hartmann’s procedure). This dichotomy has generated decades of scholarly debate and clinical controversy [4–6]. While early military experience strongly favored mandatory diversion, evidence accumulating since the late 1970 s, particularly from civilian trauma centers, has increasingly demonstrated the safety and efficacy of primary repair in appropriately selected patients [7, 8]. Despite the emerging consensus favoring primary repair for many injuries, the debate persists, with some authorities cautioning against dogmatic applications, especially in high-risk clinical scenarios [5].

Multiple interconnected factors influence surgical decision-making and subsequent patient outcomes. These include hemodynamic stability at presentation, degree of peritoneal fecal contamination, injury severity and mechanism (commonly classified using the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) Colon Injury Scale) [9], the presence and severity of associated injuries (reflected in composite scores, such as the Penetrating Abdominal Trauma Index), and temporal factors, including delay from injury to surgical intervention [7]. High-energy destructive injuries, significant contamination, hemodynamic instability requiring massive transfusion, and high overall injury burden have traditionally been considered as relative indications for fecal diversion [10].

Although international literature provides extensive data on colonic trauma management, there is a paucity of published information detailing current practices, decision-making factors, and outcomes specific to Yemeni healthcare settings. Understanding the clinical decision-making processes and outcomes of major trauma-receiving centers is crucial for evaluating the current standards of care and identifying opportunities for quality improvement in this unique clinical environment.

This prospective observational study was designed to address the three primary objectives.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted across three major trauma-receiving hospitals in Sana’a City, Yemen: Al-Thawra Modern General Hospital (TMGH), 48 Model Hospital, and Typical Police Hospital. These institutions represent the principal public and government health care facilities in the region that manage surgical emergencies. The study enrollment period was extended from May 2020 to April 2021, encompassing a complete 12-month cycle to account for potential seasonal variations in trauma patterns.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Al-Thawra Modern General Hospital, Sana’a, Yemen (Reference Number: IRB-TMGH-2020-04), and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Further details on ethical approval and consent are provided in the declaration of section.

Surgical expertise and hospital resources

All surgical procedures were performed by or under the direct supervision of senior general surgeons with extensive experience (> 10 years) in trauma surgery at high-volume centers. However, it is important to note the resource-constrained environment of this conflict setting. Specialized stoma care was limited to all the participating hospitals. There are no dedicated enterostomal therapy nurses. Surgical residents and general nursing staff primarily handle postoperative stoma management and patient education, with limited access to modern stoma appliances and skin barrier products.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

All patients aged ≥ 8 years (based on the youngest patient enrolled) who presented during the study period, underwent emergency laparotomy, and were intraoperatively confirmed to have sustained a traumatic colonic injury requiring surgical intervention (repair or diversion) were enrolled.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they (1) died in the emergency department prior to surgical intervention, (2) were managed nonoperatively for abdominal injuries, or (3) had significant pre-existing colonic pathology (including colorectal malignancy or inflammatory bowel disease) that would confound the management of acute traumatic injury.

Patient recruitment

Eligible patients were consecutively identified by attending surgical teams at the time of surgery, and data were prospectively entered into a dedicated study registry to ensure comprehensive capture and minimize selection bias.

Data collection and variables

Data were prospectively collected using a standardized data-collection instrument specifically designed for this study. Information was obtained from multiple sources, including direct patient assessments, emergency department records, operative reports, anesthesia records, and comprehensive inpatient medical charts.

The intraoperative decision to perform simple suture repair versus resection with anastomosis was based on the surgeon’s judgment. Generally, factors favoring simple suture repair include injuries involving less than 50% of the colonic circumference, minimal tissue devitalization, and adequate blood supply to the injured segment. Resection with primary anastomosis is considered for more destructive injuries, segmental tissue loss, or when the viability of the remaining colon edges is questionable.

Wound management and antibiotic protocol

All patients received preoperative intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis, typically third-generation cephalosporin and metronidazole, administered prior to the skin incision. Postoperative antibiotics were continued based on the surgeon’s assessment of contamination and patient’s clinical progress. For wound management, the fascia was closed in all cases. In patients with significant fecal contamination, the skin and subcutaneous tissues are frequently managed by delayed primary closure or packed, and left to heal by secondary intention. Specialized resources, such as wound protection sleeves or vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) devices, were not available in the participating centers.

Statistical analysis

A priori power analysis was not performed given the prospective, observational nature of the study enrolling consecutive patients over a defined period. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages, whereas continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with ranges, as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Given the relatively small sample size and the risk of model overfitting, we did not perform multivariate analyses.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

During the 12-month study period from May 2020 to April 2021, 90 patients with surgically managed colonic injuries were prospectively enrolled across the three participating hospitals. The cohort comprised 69 men (76.7%) and 21 women (23.3%), with a mean age of 35 years (range: 8–73 years) (Table 1). Complete follow-up through hospital discharge was achieved for all patients, and no patients were lost to follow-up.

Injury characteristics and baseline demographics

Penetrating injuries were the predominant mechanism, accounting for 75.5% of cases (n = 68), whereas the remaining 24.5% (n = 22) were due to blunt trauma. The anatomical location of the colonic injuries was distributed across the right colon (31.1%, n = 28), transverse colon (28.9%, n = 26), and left colon (40.0%, n = 36).

The severity of colonic injury was graded using the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) Colon Injury Scale, with most patients presenting with Grade I-II injuries (Table 1). Significant fecal contamination (moderate or severe) was observed in 24.4% (n = 22) of patients. Baseline demographics and key injury characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Surgical management patterns

Primary repair without diversion was the most common surgical strategy, performed in 74 patients (82.2%). This group included 65 patients (87.8%) who underwent simple suture closure and nine patients (12.2%) who required resection with primary anastomosis.

Fecal diversion was performed in 16 patients (17.8%). This group consisted of 11 patients who underwent end colostomy (Hartmann’s procedure) and 5 patients who underwent loop colostomy. To elaborate on the loop colostomy procedures, two were performed as diverting stomas proximal to a primary suture repair of the colonic injury, whereas in the other three cases, the injured segment of the colon was used to create the loop colostomy (Table 2).

Factors influencing surgical decision-making

Several key factors were significantly associated with the choice of surgical management.

Injury mechanism

Gunshot wounds were significantly more likely to require colostomy than other mechanisms (25.0% vs. 8.3% for stab wounds and 4.5% for blunt trauma; P=0.002, chi-square test).

AAST injury grade

A strong correlation existed between injury severity and management strategy (Fig. 1). While 95.5% of Grade I-II injuries underwent primary repair, 62.5% of high-grade (Grade IV-V) injuries required colostomy (P<0.001).

Physiological status

Patients who presented with hemodynamic shock were significantly more likely to undergo colostomy than those who were stable (P=0.001).

Anatomical location

The rate of primary repair was higher for injuries to the right colon (93.0%) and transverse colon (88.0%) than for injuries to the left colon (69.0%), where colostomy was more common (P=0.012, chi-square test).

Procedure-specific complications

In the primary repair group (n=74), procedure-specific complications included an anastomotic leak rate of 55.6% (5 of 9 patients) in the high-risk resection subgroup and a fecal fistula rate of 6.2% (4 of 65 patients) from simple suture lines. Most significantly, stoma-related complications affected eight of the 16 (50.0%) patients who underwent colostomy. These were stratified into minor complications, such as skin excoriation (n=3), and major complications requiring significant medical intervention or potential reoperation, including stomal prolapse (n=2), parastomal hernia (n=2), and stomal necrosis (n=1) (Table 4).

Mortality and recovery metrics

As shown in Table 5, in-hospital mortality was higher in the colostomy group (12.5% vs. 5.4%), but the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.279). The 30-day reoperation rate was also comparable between groups (10.8% vs. 12.5%; P=1.000). Interestingly, colostomy patients achieved earlier oral feeding (P<0.001), while the length of hospital stay remained similar.

Discussion

This prospective observational study, conducted during an active conflict period across three major trauma centers in Yemen, provides valuable insights into colonic trauma management in resource-constrained environments. Our findings demonstrate that primary repair emerged as the predominant surgical strategy employed in 82.2% of the cases, with colostomy selectively reserved for high-risk presentations in 17.8% of the cohort.

The significant associations between colostomy selection and specific injury characteristics—penetrating gunshot wounds (P=0.002), severe fecal contamination (P<0.001), and high-grade AAST injuries (P<0.001)—align closely with established trauma surgery principles and contemporary guidelines [11]. These findings validate the surgeons' clinical decision-making process in this challenging environment and demonstrate adherence to evidence-based practice despite the resource constraints.The predominance of primary repair (82.2%) in our cohort reflects the evolution of colonic trauma management over recent decades, a shift that began after landmark studies challenged the necessity of routine diversion [12]. This rate is consistent with the patterns of contemporary practice reported in recent literature, where the primary repair rates typically exceed 70% [13, 14]. This alignment suggests that surgical decision-making in Yemen follows established international standards, despite the challenging clinical environment. Our observed colostomy rate of 17.8% is considerably lower than the rates reported in other modern military conflicts, which have been as high as 40% [15–17], suggesting an evolving confidence in primary repair even in austere scenarios.

The strong association between gunshot wounds and colostomy requirements (25.0% vs. 8.3% for stab wounds, and 4.5% for blunt trauma) reflects the high-energy nature of these injuries and their propensity for extensive tissue destruction. This finding is particularly relevant in the context of Yemen's ongoing conflict in which penetrating injuries are predominant. The 2019 Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) guidelines specifically recognize high-energy penetrating injuries as important considerations in surgical decision-making [11], supporting our observed patterns.

Perhaps the most clinically significant finding of this study is the exceptionally high rate of stoma-related complications, affecting 50% of the patients who underwent colostomy. This represents a substantial morbidity burden extending beyond the immediate perioperative period. While this included major complications requiring reoperation, such as stomal necrosis (12.5%), a significant proportion of this morbidity stemmed from issues such as skin excoriation. This finding is consistent with the broad range of 20–70% reported in the literature, with higher rates typically observed in emergency settings [18–20]. However, in the context of a resource-constrained conflict environment, these complications assume greater significance because of (1) the limited specialized stoma care resources and supplies now detailed in our methods, (2) challenges in providing adequate patient education and follow-up, (3) economic burden on patients and families, and (4) potential difficulties in accessing reversal procedures, which carry a significant risk of morbidity [21].

Equally alarming was the 55.6% anastomotic leak rate observed in a small subgroup of patients (n=9) who underwent resection with primary anastomosis. Although this finding was based on a small number of patients, it is a critical safety concern. By definition, these patients were in the high-risk group for whom simple sutures were deemed insufficient. This result strongly suggests that in our specific clinical setting, the threshold for performing safe primary anastomosis is extremely high. This provides compelling evidence that, when simple repair is not feasible, a damage-control approach with resection and end colostomy may be a safer pathway to avoid the catastrophic consequences of anastomotic leaks.

Interpretation of outcome comparisons between the primary repair and colostomy groups requires careful consideration of the fundamental selection bias inherent in this observational study. Patients who underwent colostomy had significantly more severe baseline injury characteristics including higher rates of shock (50.0% vs. 39.2%), severe contamination (75.0% vs. 13.5%), and high-grade injuries (62.5% vs. 4.1% for AAST grades IV-V). This confounding by indication prevents the direct causal attribution of outcomes to the surgical procedure itself.

However, despite these limitations, several observations require further investigation. The significantly higher wound infection rate in patients who underwent colostomy (31.2% vs. 8.1%; P=0.015) likely reflects both the severity of the underlying injuries and the risk of contamination associated with the presence of a stoma. This finding is consistent with previous reports and highlights the importance of meticulous wound management in this population [22]. Although in-hospital mortality was higher in the colostomy group (12.5% vs. 5.4%), this difference was not statistically significant and must be interpreted in the context of significantly worse baseline injury severity in the colostomy cohort.

Our findings have several important clinical implications in the management of colonic trauma, particularly in resource-constrained settings. First, this study reinforces the principle that, when clinical conditions permit, primary repair remains the preferred strategy for most colonic injuries. The selective application of colostomy is appropriate for specific high-risk scenarios, particularly those involving high-energy penetrating injuries with severe contamination, hemodynamic instability, or damage control [23]. Furthermore, the substantial burden of stoma-related complications, which affected 50% of colostomy patients in our series, underscores the critical importance of pursuing primary repair whenever safe and feasible to avoid this significant morbidity. Finally, given the elevated risk of infection, our data highlight the need for enhanced wound management protocols for patients who require colostomy.

This study has several notable strengths, including its prospective observational design and systematic data collection, which minimized recall bias. We used a standardized injury severity classification with a validated AAST grade [9, 24] and our comprehensive outcome assessment included a specific focus on stoma-related complications. A key strength is the presentation of unique data from underrepresented geographic regions and conflict settings, which offer valuable real-world insights. Finally, complete follow-up was achieved for all patients through hospital discharge, and no patients were lost to follow-up.

However, this study has several limitations. The primary limitation is the inherent confounding by indication, which prevents any causal claims regarding outcomes. Other limitations include the relatively small sample size, particularly in the colostomy group (n=16), which limited our statistical power; the lack of a statistically significant difference in mortality, for instance, may represent a Type II error. Furthermore, this study was limited by the lack of long-term follow-up data to assess stoma reversal outcomes, absence of standardized polytrauma severity scores (e.g., ISS), and exclusion of patients with extraperitoneal rectal injuries. Finally, our analytical approach grouped simple repair with resection and anastomosis; a more granular comparison could be the focus of future research.

This study identified several important areas for future research. Multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to better define specific risk factors that should guide the choice between primary repair and colostomy. Long-term follow-up studies are essential to track stoma reversal rates, complications, and patient-reported quality of life, thereby capturing the true total burden of fecal diversion. Future studies should employ risk-adjusted analyses that incorporate comprehensive injury severity scores and physiological parameters to disentangle the effects of surgical approach on the severity of the underlying injury.

Conclusion

This prospective study on colonic trauma management in a conflict setting demonstrated that primary repair is the predominant surgical strategy, with colostomy appropriately reserved for high-risk presentations. While inherent selection bias limits direct outcome comparisons, the most significant contribution of this study is the identification of two critical, context-specific risks: an alarming failure rate for primary anastomosis in severe injuries, and a substantial morbidity burden affecting 50% of colostomy patients.

These findings support a surgical philosophy that prioritizes simple primary suture repair, whenever clinically feasible. However, for more destructive injuries, the decision-making process is fraught with peril. An exceptionally high anastomotic leak rate serves as a warning against high-risk anastomosis in this setting. Conversely, the significant stoma-related complication rate, exacerbated by limited resources, underscores that fecal diversion is not a benign alternative.

Ultimately, surgeons in austere environments must weigh the catastrophic risk of failed anastomosis against the profound long-term challenges of managing a stoma. Our data suggest that, when simple repair is not possible, a damage-control approach with resection and end colostomy may be the safest path forward. Future research should focus on developing better risk stratification tools to guide this critical decision and optimize outcomes for trauma patients in the world’s most challenging settings.
