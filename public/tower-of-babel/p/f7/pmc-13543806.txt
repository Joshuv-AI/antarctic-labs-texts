Prognostic impact of age and MDS-associated mutations in NPM1-mutated AML
Authors: Vivian M. Liu, Megan Othus, Jasmine Naru, Rhonda E. Ries, Era L. Pogosova-Agadjanyan, Frederick R. Appelbaum, Thomas R. Chauncey, Eliana Dietrich, Harry P. Erba, John E. Godwin, Matthew P. Fitzgibbon, Min Fang, Stanley C. Lee, Anna Moseley, Mary-Elizabeth Percival, Guangrong Qin, Jerald P. Radich, Suravi Raychaudhuri, Cheryl L. Willman, Soheil Meshinchi, Derek L. Stirewalt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•MDSm+ in favorable-risk NPM1-mutated AML do not independently predict prognosis after adjusting for age.•Patients with NPM1-mutated AML who are aged ≥65 years have inferior outcomes regardless of MDSm+.

Introduction

Nucleophosmin-1 mutations (NPM1+) are one of the most common genomic alterations in adult acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and are generally associated with chemosensitive disease and favorable outcomes, especially when there is not a concurrent fms related receptor tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3)-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD−). Currently, AML risk stratification is based on cytogenetic changes and genomic mutations per the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) model, updated most recently in 2022 (ELN2022).1 Recent updates to the ELN2017 and ELN2022 include the integration of genes frequently mutated in myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), defined as mutations in ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, RUNX1, U2AF1, and ZRSR2 (MDS-associated mutations [MDSm+]), as an adverse risk factor. It is unclear whether MDSm+ alters the favorable-risk characteristics of NPM1+/FLT3-ITD− AML, and whether MDSm+ renders otherwise favorable-risk NPM1+ AML to adverse-risk disease. We have previously shown that older patients with NPM1+/FLT3-ITD− AML, particularly age ≥65 years, have a poor prognosis (2-year overall survival [OS] for age 55-65 years vs >65 years was 70% vs 27%; P < .001).2 Given that the frequency of MDSm+ increases with age, the strong correlation between age and inferior outcomes may be partially attributable to the MDSm+ in older patients with NPM1+ AML.

Previous studies on the impact of MDSm+ in patients with NPM1+ AML have reported conflicting results.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Some have shown inferior outcomes in patients with favorable-risk NPM1+ with MDSm+.3,4 In particular, Chan et al reported that an inferior OS in NPM1+/MDSm+ disease is driven by older patients (≥60 years), although treatment was heterogeneous and included nonintensive therapies or no treatment in 24%.3 They also showed that MDSm+ was not associated with adverse outcomes for younger patients.3 Mrózek et al demonstrated a significant difference in complete remission (CR) rate, disease-free survival, and OS in patients with NPM1+/FLT3-ITD− with and without MDSm+, although they did not stratify their cohort by age.4 In addition, a meta-analysis of 14 studies demonstrated worse OS for MDSm+ in only favorable-risk NPM1+ AML (though some of these studies used ELN2017, whereas others used the ELN2022 classification).8 Other studies, including a large study of >1300 patients with NPM1+ in the United Kingdom,5 did not find MDSm+ were significantly associated with adverse outcomes in patients with NPM1+ as a whole5,7 or in patients with ELN2022 favorable-risk NPM1+.5,6 Because most studies did not address the association between increasing age and prevalence of MDSm+, we evaluated the impact of MDSm+ on outcomes for adults with NPM1+ AML and favorable-risk disease, with particular attention to the impact of age.

Methods

We performed a retrospective analysis of 271 pretreatment diagnostic specimens from 3 cohorts of patients with NPM1+ AML: (1) 119 patients enrolled into SWOG clinical trials (SWOG-9031, SWOG-9333, S0106, and S0112),9, 10, 11, 12 (2) 94 locally treated patients (Fred Hutch), and (3) 57 patients from the Beat AML study.13 All patients were treated with curative-intent intensive chemotherapy regimens (supplemental Table 2), and had documentation of cytogenetic and long-term outcome data. Clinical outcomes and correlative studies have previously been reported for all patients from SWOG and Beat AML. All studies for these analyses were performed according to the Institutional Review Board-approved protocols and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

DNA was extracted from Fred Hutch and SWOG specimens with targeted genomic sequencing performed as previously described (supplemental Methods).14 Genomic mutations for Beat AML were obtained from previously published data.13 Morphologic CR rates were tabulated and compared between groups using logistic regression models stratified by cohort. Event-free survival (EFS) and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using Cox regression models stratified by cohort. Among participants who achieved a CR, cumulative incidence to time to relapse and transplant (both with death without relapse or transplant, respectively), analyzed as a competing event was estimated nonparametrically and compared using Gray’s test (transplant) and cause-specific hazard models stratified by cohort (relapse).

Results

Patient characteristics and co-occurring mutations are shown in supplemental Table 1 and supplemental Figure 1. MDSm+ occurred in 17% of patients, with the most common mutations being in SRSF2 (7%) and SF3B1 (3%; supplemental Figure 1). There were no significant differences between baseline characteristics of each of the 3 cohorts (supplemental Table 1). All patients were treated with intensive chemotherapy regimens for induction (supplemental Table 2).

To assess the impact of MDSm+ on outcomes for patients with NPM1+ AML, we compared clinical outcomes between patients stratified into the following groups using ELN2022 criteria: (1) favorable-risk/MDSm− (n = 116), (2) favorable-risk/MDSm+ (n = 33), (3) intermediate-risk (n = 107), and (4) adverse-risk (due to adverse-risk/complex karyotype or mutated TP53, n = 15). Favorable/MDSm+ patients had a significantly worse OS (hazard ratio [HR], 2.0 [95% confidence interval, 1.1-3.3]; P = .008) compared with favorable/MDSm− and similar OS to intermediate- and adverse-risk groups (Figure 1; Table 1). However, no significant difference was identified in EFS, time to relapse, or CR rates between patients with favorable/MDSm+ and favorable/MDSm− (Figure 1; supplemental Figure 2). Age is a potential confounder as it is both associated with the development of MDSm+ and adverse outcomes for patients with NPM1+.2,15 Therefore, we further stratified patients with NPM1+ based on age, using a cut-off threshold previously defined as being associated with adverse risk (<65 years vs ≥65 years).2 Subsequent analyses showed that there was no longer a significant difference in outcomes between favorable/MDSm+ and favorable/MDSm− in both younger and older patients (Figure 1; Table 1). The older favorable-risk patients (≥65 years) had a significantly inferior OS and EFS compared with younger patients, regardless of the presence of MDSm+, with older patients with MDSm+ having the worst OS and EFS (Figure 1; Table 1). These results suggest that much of the previously identified adverse effect of MDSm+ could be due to the confounding variable age, and after controlling for age this effect is mitigated. In the Fred Hutch cohort, the cumulative incidence of allogeneic stem cell transplant (allo-SCT) was significantly higher among younger patients with NPM1+ compared with older patients (50% vs 17%, P = .007; supplemental Figure 3). There was no significant difference in OS between those who did and did not receive an allogeneic transplant (time-dependent HR controlling for age, 1.05 [95% confidence interval, 0.61-1.82]), suggesting that allo-SCT was able to rescue outcomes for patients who had reasons for transplant, such as higher risk disease, presence of minimal residual disease (MRD), and relapsed disease.

Discussion

We and others have previously shown that the NPM1+/FLT3-ITD− genotype is a favorable mutational profile in patients aged <65 years, but no longer favorable in patients aged ≥65 years.2,15 Similarly, in this study, we found that patients with NPM1+ AML aged ≥65 years have adverse clinical outcomes, even in those that are ELN2022 favorable-risk without MDSm+. Although patients with NPM1+ AML harboring MDSm+ have significantly worse OS compared with MDSm− favorable-risk NPM1+ AML, this difference is likely driven more by age than the MDSm+. We also found that the cumulative incidence of allo-SCT was significantly higher in younger patients compared with older patients, which is potentially a contributing factor to the inferior OS in older patients, particularly because post-relapse outcomes of older patients with NPM1+ can be salvaged with allo-SCT in second remission.16

Increasing age is also highly correlated with increased prevalence of MDSm+,15 suggesting that differences among published studies may stem from differences in cohort age, which has not been adequately addressed by previous studies. Chan et al found significantly worse OS in NPM1+ AML with MDSm+ in both their full cohort and ELN2017 favorable-risk patients. When patients were stratified by age, this effect was driven by patients who were older (defined as aged ≥60 years).3 However, their cohort included 56 patients (24.1%) who either received lower intensity therapy or no therapy, which were significantly enriched in the MDSm+ population (37.2% vs 17.9%, P = .008). Thus, the findings of the previous study may potentially be confounded by differences in intensity of treatment, whereas all patients in the current, larger cohort were treated with intensive induction chemotherapy. Of note, other analyses that have not found a significant difference between NPM1+ AML with and without MDSm+ have a lower median age in their study population. For instance, Othman et al did not find a significant difference in NPM1+ AML with and without MDSm+, but the median age was 53 years (range 6-76) with only 20% of patients aged ≥60 years.5 Similarly, Ruhnke et al had a low median age in the ELN2022 favorable-risk group of 51 years.6 Therefore, it is possible that the discrepancy in outcomes between various studies is driven by the difference in median age of different cohorts, with cohorts with a higher median age more likely to find a statistically significant difference.

Although this study examines a large overall cohort of 271 patients with NPM1+, the numbers remain limited for subset analyses. For instance, there was a potential trend toward decreased OS in older patients with favorable/MDSm+, but the trend did not reach statistical significance (HR, 1.42; P = .33). We were also unable to evaluate the prognostic impact of mutations for individual MDSm+ genes given small subset numbers. In addition, data regarding allogeneic transplant, presence of MRD after induction, and treatment after relapse were not available in many patients (eg, SWOG, Beat AML), which limits our ability to fully assess the potential impact of MRD positivity and subsequent therapies as a confounding variable.

Our study demonstrates that age is a strong predictor of outcomes in NPM1+ AML, even in ELN2022 favorable-risk disease. Older patients with ELN2022 favorable-risk NPM1+ AML have worse outcomes than their younger counterparts, regardless of MDSm+, suggesting that these patients should be treated more aggressively and considered for upfront allogeneic transplant if they are physically fit. The finding of MDSm+ in patients with NPM1+ AML is not independently associated with adverse risk after adjusting for age. Therefore, further comprehensive studies investigating the effect of age in NPM1+ AML are warranted to develop more precise risk-stratification guidelines.
