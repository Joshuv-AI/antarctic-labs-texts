Drug-Resistant Tuberculous Meningitis (DR-TBM) in a Low-Incidence Setting: Two Cases and an Evidence Review Highlighting Key Advances and Knowledge Gaps in Clinical Management
Authors: Karol F Boschung, Patrick H P Wong, Denise Werry, Inna Sekirov, Shannon L Turvey, Victoria J Cook, James Johnston, William J A Connors
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report on 2 patients with drug-resistant tuberculous meningitis diagnosed and treated in a high-resource, low-incidence setting. Both patients were treated with multidrug regimens including bedaquiline, a nitroimidazole, cycloserine, and linezolid. Drug selection was informed by molecular diagnostics, susceptibility testing, and pharmacokinetic and clinical data. Adverse events included suicidal ideation, cytopenias, ototoxicity, and neuropathy. There was discordance between phenotypic (susceptible) and genotypic (resistant) drug susceptibility testing for rifampin. After treatment, both patients had excellent clinical outcomes, with at least 12 months of post-treatment follow-up.These clinical cases provide an opportunity to explore the role of molecular diagnostics in the diagnosis of drug-resistant tuberculous meningitis, drug regimen selection in the era of novel oral regimens, and clinical decision-making in the setting of discordance between genotypic and phenotypic drug susceptibility testing.Keywords: Tuberculous Meningitis, Tuberculosis, Central Nervous System, Multidrug-Resistant Tuberculosis, Pre-Extensively Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis

Two patients with drug-resistant tuberculosis meningitis (DR-TBM) were encountered in British Columbia, Canada. Provincial tuberculosis (TB) incidence is ∼6/100 000, and 17/100 000 among newcomers to Canada. The rate of drug resistance is 7.6% [1].

CASE 1

A healthy, 20-year-old female student without HIV-1 (HIV) presented to the emergency room with a 1-month history of cough and fever. She had no neurologic findings. Originally from India, she had been in Canada for 8 months and had a prior TB contact (out-of-country, details unverified). Five days prior, an outpatient physician had prescribed prednisone. Chest x-ray demonstrated miliary opacities, and rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol were started for empiric treatment of miliary TB. She developed rapidly progressive respiratory failure, was admitted to the intensive care unit, and was intubated. Initial expectorated sputum acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear and GeneXpert Ultra MTB/RIF (Xpert Ultra; Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) were negative, but a subsequent endotracheal tube aspirate demonstrated 1+ AFB. Xpert Ultra on this sample was positive and detected an rpoB mutation. Empiric moxifloxacin and linezolid were added (see Figure 1 for treatment summary).

Graphical case and treatment summary.

There was slow clinical improvement in respiratory status and level of consciousness, and after 2 weeks she remained sedated on mechanical ventilation. There was some delay in availability of specialist advice, and a lumbar puncture (LP) was performed 2 weeks into her admission. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) AFB smear was positive. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated nodular leptomeningeal enhancement. She was diagnosed with Medical Research Council (MRC) Grade 1 TBM based on clinical status at presentation, and corticosteroids and acetylsalicylic acid were added. Sequencing of the endotracheal sample demonstrated mutations associated with resistance to rifampin, isoniazid (inhA and katG), pyrazinamide, and fluoroquinolones (Figure 2). Genotypically resistant medications were stopped, and meropenem/amoxicillin-clavulanate, amikacin, bedaquiline, clofazimine, delamanid, and cycloserine were added as approved and available. After 1 month, meropenem/amoxicillin-clavulanate was discontinued and linezolid was dose-reduced due to neutropenia. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) confirmed adequate linezolid levels. Phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (DST) results confirmed pre-XDR TB, and moxifloxacin was stopped.

Summary of drug susceptibility testing for cases 1 and 2.

Sputum and CSF culture conversion were documented at week 9 of therapy. After 4 months, subjective hearing loss was noted; audiometry confirmed objective deficits, and amikacin was stopped. After 5 months, the patient was discharged, with monthly lab monitoring for adverse effects. One month later, lactatemia and symptoms suggestive of peripheral neuropathy were noted. Linezolid was dose-adjusted to 600 mg 3 times a week, the lactatemia resolved, and the neuropathy was monitored without progression. MRI brain at 7 months was normal. She received 18 months of MDR-TB therapy, and 1 year after treatment completion, she had returned to school with improved but persistent peripheral neuropathy.

CASE 2

A 28-year-old HIV-negative female born in the Philippines, living in Canada for 4 years, presented with 1 month of progressive headaches and fevers. Over the course of several presentations to the emergency department, she was treated for a tension headache and then a viral illness. An LP was performed, and the CSF demonstrated a lymphocytic pleocytosis, which prompted empiric therapy for viral meningitis. There was no symptomatic improvement. CSF was not sent for MTB culture or PCR. A new sixth cranial nerve palsy prompted an urgent MRI brain, which demonstrated nodular leptomeningeal enhancement. A repeat LP was performed, and CSF was AFB smear negative, with Xpert Ultra detecting rifampin-resistant TB. She was diagnosed with DR-TBM, MRC Grade 2. Empiric treatment for DR-TBM (including corticosteroids and acetylsalicylic acid) was initiated and modified as drugs were approved and available (Figure 1).

CSF culture grew Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBc), and phenotypic DST demonstrated isoniazid and ethambutol resistance but susceptibility to rifampin (breakpoint of 1.0 µg/mL). Sequencing identified an inhA mutation associated with isoniazid resistance and the rpoB mutation His445Asn, a mutation classified as “Group 1 - associated with resistance” [2]. Subsequent nonstandardized DST demonstrated susceptibility to all rifampin concentrations tested, down to 0.09 µg/mL. Due to links between phenotypically occult rpoB mutations and treatment failure [3–5], MDR-TB treatment with an all-oral regimen was pursued. Linezolid, bedaquiline, and pretomanid were continued throughout. Cycloserine was stopped after 11 days due to a suicide attempt. After 4 months, levofloxacin was switched to moxifloxacin due to myalgias. CSF culture conversion was demonstrated at week 4. Headache and diplopia resolved over several months.

After 48 days, the patient was discharged, with monthly lab monitoring for adverse effects. She had ongoing anticipatory nausea associated with medications, which resolved upon treatment completion. She was treated for 12 months with radiographic improvement on MRI, and at last follow-up 12 months post-treatment she had no sequelae.

DISCUSSION AND LITERATURE REVIEW

TBM is the deadliest form of TB, and there is no standard of care for DR-TBM. The above vignettes demonstrate the need for decision-making that integrates evidence regarding molecular diagnostics and the potential role of novel agents.

Molecular Testing: TBM and Drug Resistance

DR-TBM often suffers from delayed diagnosis and inadequate initial therapy (as in Case 2), resulting from the low yield of standard techniques and long turnaround times of culture and DST [6–9]. Molecular diagnostics could accelerate TBM diagnosis and allow earlier treatment optimization [10–12].

A 2025 Cochrane review found that, compared with a microbiologic reference standard, Xpert Ultra summary sensitivity is 88.2% (95% CI, 83.7%–91.6%) with CSF [13]. Concentration of the CSF before testing is required to achieve this performance. While the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends Xpert Ultra for first-line TBM testing, its negative predictive value is insufficient to rule out TBM (61.1%–92.7%) [11, 14]. Importantly, while CSF culture is often the reference standard for TBM diagnosis, Xpert Ultra can detect MTB in culture-negative cases, with 1 study noting that 6 of 21 Xpert Ultra-positive TBM cases were CSF culture negative [12, 14].

The GeneXpert MTB/XDR (Xpert XDR; Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) and the Genotype MTBDRsl Line Probe Assay (MRBDRsl; Hain, Nehren, Germany) were developed to diagnose DR-TB. MTBDRsl delivers sensitivities ranging from 95% for fluoroquinolone resistance to 89% for capreomycin, while Xpert XDR has 93.2% sensitivity for fluoroquinolone resistance, but falters in detecting resistance to amikacin (86.1%) and capreomycin (61%–70%) [10, 15]. There are no CSF-specific performance data for these assays.

An important gap in available genotypic DST includes novel and repurposed agents such as bedaquiline. Assays currently under development to address this include Deeplex Myc-TB (Deeplex; GenoScreen, Lille, France) and Truenat (Molbio, Goa, India). Deeplex has reported a sensitivity for bedaquiline resistance of 100% in CSF, while Truenat provided results similar to phenotypic DST and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) [16, 17]. However, research remains hampered by limited data linking genotypic variants to phenotypic resistance and clinical outcomes [18]. Therefore, despite its limitations, phenotypic DST remains essential. In the cases above, Xpert Ultra and WGS provided initial genotypic DST, while phenotypic DST was utilized for confirmation and susceptibility testing for novel agents.

Genotypic/Phenotypic Drug Susceptibility Discordance

Molecular tests may be used for rapid TBM diagnosis and DST, but interpretation can be challenging in cases of discordance between platforms. Rifampin susceptibility discordance rates of 14.5% have been found between Xpert and MTBDRplus [19]. Across various first-line drugs, 6%–34% of isolates test susceptible on rapid genotypic DST, while demonstrating resistance on phenotypic DST [20–22]. In a study of 25 theoretical regimens for MDR-TB constructed using Xpert, MDRTBplus, and WGS, genotypic DST had variable agreement with phenotypic results: 49% agreement for Xpert and 63% for MTBDRplus. Only WGS-informed regimens contained no drugs to which phenotypic DST showed resistance [23].

Clinical application of discordant results can be challenging. Phenotypically occult rpoB mutations are a more common example. In 1 study, the His445Asn mutation (seen in Case 2) was commonly associated with such discordance [24]. In a review of cases of isoniazid-monoresistant TB, strains with an occult rpoB mutation had higher treatment failure rates than those without mutations (100% vs 4%), while other studies found failure rates of 31%–55% when using rifampin-based therapy in patients with occult rpoB mutations [3–5]. This impacted decision-making in Case 2. To assist in genotypic DST interpretation, the WHO has developed a reference standard for the interpretation of mutations conferring resistance [2].

Novel and Repurposed Agents

Fluoroquinolones

Fluoroquinolones have high central nervous system (CNS) penetration and excellent early bactericidal activity, with a demonstrated mortality benefit in isoniazid-monoresistant TBM when combined with high-dose rifampin (15 mg/kg) [8]. However, a phase II RCT of pediatric drug-susceptible TBM (DS-TBM) found no effect of high-dose rifampin (30 mg/kg) and levofloxacin on neurologic outcome, measured by modified Rankin Score (mRS) [25]. The SURE and INSHORT trials (currently underway) will also evaluate fluoroquinolones in pediatric and adult TBM, respectively [26, 27].

A pharmacokinetic (PK) analysis revealed a U-shaped dose–response curve in TBM, with worse outcomes at the highest and lowest levels of CSF fluoroquinolone exposure [28].

Linezolid

Linezolid drug radiolabeling studies demonstrate preferential lung vs brain penetration [29]. Other PK studies found CSF:plasma concentration ratios of ∼0.6, and higher levels in CSF than in brain parenchyma [30–32].

Two retrospective studies demonstrated good outcomes using linezolid in DR- and DS-TBM: 88.9% (vs 70%) favorable outcomes in pediatric TBM and 0% (vs 60%) mortality in a small group of MDR-TBM [33, 34]. However, prospective studies assessing linezolid in TBM have faced recruitment challenges, and their results have been equivocal.

The LASER-TBM trial (n = 52) found no significant effect on mortality with 35 mg/kg of rifampin and linezolid (1200 mg/d, reducing after 28 days to 600 mg/d) in HIV-associated DS-TBM [35]. The ESCALATE pilot trial (n = 29) added linezolid (600 mg twice daily for 28 days) to standard therapy for DS-TBM and demonstrated significantly improved mRS [36]. The ALTER trial (n = 40, 98% with HIV) suggested a nonsignificant trend toward improvement in both survival and mRS with the addition of 1200 mg/d of linezolid for 4 weeks for treatment of DS-TBM [37]. No difference in safety outcomes was observed in any trial. The INTENSE-TBM and IMAGINE-TBM RCTs are ongoing and will hopefully provide further clarity on the use of linezolid in TBM [38, 39]. Two newer oxazolidinones (sutezolid and delpazolid) have shown promise in pulmonary TB, but not yet TBM [40, 41].

Linezolid has a narrow therapeutic window, significant pharmacokinetic variability, and high rates of discontinuation, especially with the prolonged courses often required for TB treatment [42–44]. TB guidelines also recommend linezolid TDM, and it was used in the cases above [45]. A prospective, open-label study found that when TDM was successfully used to achieve desired trough levels, the incidence of linezolid-induced thrombocytopenia decreased significantly (10.7% vs 75%) [46]. Retrospective data also suggest that TDM mitigates the link between extended linezolid treatment and toxicity [47]. There are few data on the utility of TDM in TBM.

Novel Agents (Bedaquiline, Pretomanid, Others)

Clinical data on the use of novel agents such as bedaquiline, delamanid, and pretomanid for TBM are scarce. A 2021 report describes a patient treated for pre-XDR pulmonary TB who was subsequently found to have a sterilized tuberculoma (biopsy-proven) without recurrence at 18-month follow-up. This may represent the first case of pre-XDR CNS TB cured with the 26-week BPaL regimen [48].

Pharmacokinetic studies shed light on the CNS penetration of novel agents. Bedaquiline radiolabeling studies demonstrate preferential lung penetration [49]. PK modeling predicts that M2, bedaquiline's active metabolite, will achieve 7% of serum levels in the brain [50], while drug radiolabeling in a murine TB model found a 0.15 brain parenchyma:serum ratio [49]. However, mass spectrometry of homogenized rat brain tissue estimated that bedaquiline levels surpassed MTBc minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) within 4 hours. This study also analyzed brain slices, finding extensive parenchymal drug distribution [51]. Overall, bedaquiline appears to preferentially distribute to brain parenchyma vs CSF. The data for delamanid suggest a similar PK profile: CSF concentrations are 10-fold lower than serum, but brain tissue concentrations are 5-fold higher than plasma in rabbits with TBM [52]. The PK effect of brain inflammation is unclear.

Human radiolabeling and animal studies both demonstrate preferential brain penetration of pretomanid [30, 53, 54]. A positron emission tomography–facilitated PK modeling study developed several pretomanid-based regimens that demonstrated improved bactericidal activity in a murine model of TBM [53]. A similar regimen was tested in the SimpliciTB trial, which found that bedaquiline, pretomanid, moxifloxacin, and pyrazinamide effectively cleared sputum cultures. However, this was associated with hepatotoxicity, warranting caution with combining pyrazinamide and pretomanid [55].

Limited human clinical data on cycloserine demonstrate a CSF:serum concentration ratio of 0.66 [31, 32]. A TBM case series found undetectable CSF clofazimine levels, and clofazimine was not seen in brain tissue during a 1974 autopsy study of a patient with leprosy [32, 56]. However, mouse studies have found widespread clofazimine in brain tissue, and clofazimine administration was found to be protective against brain dissemination of MTB in an aerosol challenge study [57, 58].

In light of the above data, regimens containing novel and repurposed agents were used in the treatment of these cases, but priority was given to agents with clinical data and experience supporting their use in TBM (ie, fluoroquinolones and linezolid). In Case 1, linezolid was continued (with TDM assistance) despite adverse effects.

CONCLUSIONS

In DR-TBM treatment, clinical data are largely limited to linezolid and fluoroquinolones, but successful treatment with novel and repurposed agents has been reported. In cases of genotypic/phenotypic DST discordance, clinicians should consider treating occult rpoB mutations as rifampin-resistant. Further study into optimal DR-TBM management is urgently needed.
