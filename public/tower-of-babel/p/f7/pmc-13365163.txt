Physician Consultation Time and Perceived Quality of Outpatient Care for New Cancer Patients in Korea: An Experimental Vignette Survey of the General Public and Physicians
Authors: Ji Sun Park, Moo Hyuk Lee, Sun Young Kim, Young Kyung Do
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the Republic of Korea, the limited outpatient consultation time for serious illnesses has been widely cited as a major concern for quality of care, often referred to as the “3-minute consultation.” Short consultations hinder essential clinical functions, weaken physician–patient communication, and may lead to inefficient healthcare utilization. However, little research has been conducted on whether consultation time affects the perceived quality of outpatient care, particularly from both the patient’s and the physician’s perspectives.MethodsAn experimental vignette-based survey was conducted with 2,023 individuals from the general public and 324 junior physicians between August 5 and 26, 2024. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four consultation scenarios (3, 5, 10, or 15 minutes), each depicting an initial outpatient visit for a cancer patient at a tertiary hospital. Perceived quality of care was assessed using a 13-item questionnaire that covered key aspects of the patient experience, likely consequences of the consultation, and overall perceptions. Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 for strongly disagree to 5 for strongly agree), except for the final item on overall rating. For each item, trend analysis was conducted to test whether a greater amount of consultation time is associated with higher perceived quality of care. Differences between the general public and physician samples were also examined.ResultsLonger consultation times were associated with higher perceived quality of care across almost all items and groups (P for trend < 0.001). Scores for respect and courtesy increased from 1.93 (general public) and 2.06 (physicians) at 3 minutes to 4.16 and 4.59 at 15 minutes, respectively, with similar trends for attentiveness, information provision, explanation clarity, and patient participation. Trust in the physician and anxiety reduction also improved with longer consultations, while likelihoods of second-opinion visits and unnecessary testing decreased, though to a lesser extent. Physicians tended to rate the quality of care more favorably than the general public.ConclusionLonger consultation times are likely to improve the quality of care in tertiary oncology outpatient settings, from both the patient and physician perspectives.

INTRODUCTION

Physician consultation time is a critical determinant of the quality of care, shaping the interactions between patients and physicians. Insufficient consultation time not only hampers essential clinical functions such as physicians’ information gathering, explanation, and shared decision-making,12 but also negatively impacts patient experiences, including respect and courtesy, active listening, and anxiety reduction.34567 Beyond these immediate consequences, inadequate consultation time may erode trust in physicians, lead to an increase in unnecessary diagnostic testing, and heighten the likelihood of patients seeking second opinions or revisiting other hospitals, ultimately contributing to inefficiencies and wasted healthcare resources.8910 While concerns regarding the negative impact of limited consultation time may vary from country to country, they are particularly pronounced in Korea, where outpatient visits are among the shortest globally.

Surveys indicate that the average consultation time is 11.81 minutes for initial visits and 6.43 minutes for follow-ups.11 However, these figures are based on self-reported data across various healthcare settings and encompass the entire care process, including history-taking, physical examination, and documentation. As such, there may be a considerable discrepancy between these reported durations and the consultation time actually perceived by patients in tertiary hospital outpatient settings. In-depth interview findings suggest that patients often perceive the consultation time as insufficient, making encounters feel rushed and impersonal and contributing to growing dissatisfaction.12 To address this issue, the government launched an in-depth consultation pilot program in December 2017 (often referred to as the ‘15-minute consultation’) to ensure adequate consultation time for patients with serious illnesses visiting tertiary hospitals.

Although prior research suggests that extended consultation time may be associated with improved quality of care, this relationship remains a subject of ongoing debate.1314 This uncertainty is largely attributable to methodological challenges in accurately assessing the link between consultation time and quality of care. First, directly observing and measuring consultation time in real-world clinical settings is difficult. Many studies have relied on self-reported estimates from physicians or patients, which are vulnerable to recall bias. Additionally, calculating consultation time as the total consultation duration divided by the number of patients seen may lead to overestimation by incorporating non-clinical periods.2 Moreover, numerous confounding factors, such as whether the visit is an initial consultation, the complexity of the medical condition, diagnostic uncertainty, physician workload, and the presence of team-based care, further complicate the interpretation of findings.15 Without adequately accounting for these variables, isolating the true impact of consultation time on quality of care becomes challenging. One potential approach to addressing these methodological limitations is the use of vignettes. Vignettes are short, carefully constructed descriptions of a person, object, or situation, representing a systematic combination of characteristics.16 Experimental vignette studies allow for the manipulation of consultation time at various levels and enable a rigorous and unbiased estimation of its causal effects on the quality of care.17 Furthermore, existing research has predominantly focused on the association between consultation time and quality of care in primary care settings, with limited exploration of this relationship in the context of cancer care. Sufficient consultation time may be even more critical in cancer care due to the seriousness of the disease, complexity of treatment decision-making, greater emotional dimensions, and its profound effect on patients’ health and healthcare utilization.8 While ensuring adequate consultation time is essential for quality cancer care, it remains unclear how patients and physicians perceive consultation time and what its implications might be.

This study employs an experimental vignette methodology to examine how varying consultation times influence perceptions of quality of care in initial outpatient visits for cancer patients. Through a vignette survey administered to both the general public and physicians, this study aimed to identify consistent patterns in perceptions of care quality across varying consultation times and to explore potential differences between these groups.

METHODS

Study setting and participants

This study utilized a common module on consultation time from two separate surveys targeting the general public and junior physicians, conducted as part of a larger research project aimed at examining the general public’s and physicians’ perceptions of the healthcare system and factors influencing their healthcare decision-making.

A total of 2,023 members of the general public and 324 junior physicians participated in the surveys. The general public sample was drawn from a nationwide representative panel using proportionally stratified sampling by gender, age group, region, and monthly household income. The regions were intentionally stratified in a non-uniform manner, with 35% of the sample from the Seoul Metropolitan Area (SMA) and 65% from non-SMA regions. For the physician sample, the target population was junior physicians in Korea (defined as residents who were or would have been in their third or fourth year of training, and specialists within ten years of completing residency). The sample was proportionally stratified according to regional physician distribution, based primarily on resident quotas. The accessible population comprised physicians who could be reached during the large-scale walkout of junior physicians at the time of the survey. Study participants were recruited through thirteen nationwide recruiters with extensive networks among early-career physicians. Alternative probability-based sampling methods were deemed infeasible due to the large-scale walkout of junior physicians at the time of the survey. Data collection was conducted online by two professional research agencies (Hankook Research for the general public sample and Research & Research for the physician sample) from August 5 to 26, 2024. In this study, all responses were included in the final analysis.

Study design

This study employed an experimental vignette-based survey design. Participants from the general public and physician samples were randomized within their respective groups to one of four consultation time scenarios and completed the survey accordingly. Randomization was implemented by the online research agencies using their predetermined algorithms, independent of the research team.

Vignettes

Vignettes were developed to simulate initial outpatient consultations for a cancer patient visiting a tertiary hospital. The initial draft scenarios were created by a multidisciplinary team to reflect realistic interactions at each consultation length and were refined through expert review by one of the authors, a medical oncologist at a tertiary hospital. Physician–patient interactions were systematically varied across four consultation time conditions: 3, 5, 10, and 15 minutes. Depending on the consultation time, the vignettes varied in key factors such as eye contact, the amount of information provided, opportunities for the patient to ask questions, and the follow-up plan. For example, in the 3-minute consultation scenario, the physician made minimal eye contact with the patient, provided only basic information, and left little to no opportunity for the patient to ask questions. In contrast, in the 15-minute consultation scenario, the physician frequently made eye contact, asked detailed questions about the patient’s health, provided comprehensive explanations, and discussed a clear follow-up plan. The vignettes were not necessarily intended to depict actual clinical practice in the current environment for each consultation length, but rather to illustrate how consultation time can serve as a critical structural precondition that would possibly enable physician–patient interactions. Full details of the scenarios are available in the Supplementary Methods (Supplementary Data 1).

Although 3- or even 5-minute consultation may appear unrealistic for a first visit of a cancer patient at a tertiary hospital, the inclusion of these short consultation times in our vignettes can be justified on several grounds. First, the primary aim of this vignette-based study was not necessarily to replicate real-world situations with substantial frequency, but rather to explore how perceptions of care quality vary with consultation length. This includes examining less common scenarios such as 3-minute and 15-minute consultations. In this context, the 3-minute consultation was not intended to reflect typical clinical practice, but to represent a highly time-constrained interaction. Second, although relatively uncommon, 3- or 5-minute consultations do occur in overcrowded outpatient care settings and reflect ongoing concerns within the Korean healthcare system. This is particularly relevant for tertiary hospitals, where physicians often review patient information in advance and support staff may collect patient history and test results beforehand. From the patient’s perspective, however, these behind-the-scenes efforts may not be visible, and the consultation itself may feel brief. Third, while we acknowledge recent policy developments, such as the in-depth consultation pilot program at tertiary hospitals and the reimbursement of multidisciplinary cancer care, adoption at the individual physician and patient levels remains limited.

Outcome measures

The survey consisted of 13 items assessing perceived quality of care. The first five items were adapted from the questions that are directly pertinent to physician-patient interactions in the Patient Experience Assessment questionnaire currently administered in Korea (albeit in inpatient care settings).18 Participants were asked whether the physician treated the patient with respect and courtesy (Q1), listened attentively (Q2), provided sufficient information (Q3), explained medical information in a clear and understandable manner (Q4), and involved the patient in shared decision-making regarding tests or treatments (Q5).

The next five items were developed based on a literature review and discussions among the research team, considering healthcare utilization patterns in Korea, to capture additional aspects of perceived quality of care focused on the possible positive and negative consequences of inadequate consultation time.819 These items assessed whether the patient was likely to experience reduced anxiety following the consultation (Q6), would prefer to seek care at another tertiary hospital (Q7, reverse coded), had increased trust in the physician (Q8), and whether the consultation would lead to an appropriate treatment plan (Q9) and reduce unnecessary tests (Q10).

The remaining three items were designed to assess overall perceptions of the consultation. These included whether the consultation time was perceived as sufficient (Q11), whether the presented consultation scenario was desirable overall (Q12), and an overall rating of the outpatient consultation experience (Q13). Responses to Q1–Q12 were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 for Strongly Disagree to 5 for Strongly Agree), while Q13 was rated on a 0–10 scale (0 for Worst and 10 for Best). These items primarily reflect two dimensions of perceived quality of care, patient-centeredness (Q1–Q6, Q8–Q9) and efficiency (Q7, Q10).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations. Homogeneity of baseline characteristics across the four consultation time groups within each sample was assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables.

For each item of Q1–Q10, trend analysis was conducted using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test to assess whether a greater amount of consultation time (i.e., from 3 to 5, 10, and 15 minutes) is associated with higher perceived quality of care. For all items (Q1–Q13), differences between the general public and physician samples were examined using t-tests. Given the ordinal nature of Likert-scale responses, we also examined skewness and reported medians, first and third quartiles, and Mann-Whitney U test results as a robustness check. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital and the requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB approval number: C-2201-045-1288).

Among the participants, 52.8% were married, 66.6% resided in non-SMA areas, and there was considerable variation in average monthly household income. In the physician group, the mean age was 33 years, with 59.3% identifying as male and 39.2% as female. The proportion of physicians who were married (48.8%) was comparable to those who were not (51.2%), and 60.2% practiced in the SMA. Across almost all survey items, longer consultation durations (3, 5, 10, and 15 minutes) were consistently associated with more positive evaluations of quality of care (P for trend < 0.001; Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 2). For instance, in the item assessing whether the physician treated the patient with respect and courtesy (Q1), the mean scores of responses of the general public and physicians for the 3-minute consultation were 1.93 and 2.06, respectively, increasing to 2.54 and 3.05 for 5 minutes, 3.70 and 4.35 for 10 minutes, and 4.16 and 4.59 for 15 minutes. A similar pattern was observed for items evaluating attentiveness to the patient’s concerns (Q2), provision of sufficient information (Q3), clarity of explanations (Q4), and opportunities for patient participation in decision-making (Q5), all of which exhibited a progressive increase in positive assessments as consultation time lengthened. In addition, reductions in post-consultation anxiety (Q6) and trust in the physician (Q8) showed a clear upward trajectory, indicating that extended consultation time was associated with greater perceived quality of care.

The rate of improvement was less pronounced for items assessing the likelihood of seeking care at another tertiary hospital (Q7, reverse coded) and the potential for reducing unnecessary tests (Q10). Specifically, in Q7, the mean scores for the 3-minute consultation were 2.60 (general public) and 2.20 (physicians), increasing only to 3.14 and 3.32, respectively, for the 15-minute consultation. Similarly, in Q10, the scores rose from 2.12 (general public) and 2.61 (physicians) in the 3-minute consultation to 3.89 and 3.76 in the 15-minute consultation, respectively. These were the only two items whose mean scores were below 4 even in the 15-minute consultation time.

When overall perceptions were compared between the two samples, physicians provided more favorable ratings than the general public across consultation duration (Table 2). For instance, in Q12, which assessed whether the presented consultation scenario was desirable overall, the 10-minute consultation was rated 3.60 by the general public and 3.86 by physicians, with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.014). Similarly, in Q13, which evaluated the overall outpatient consultation experience on a scale of 0 to 10, physicians assigned significantly higher scores than the general public for the 5-minute, 10-minute, and 15-minute consultations (P < 0.001). This pattern was also observed in other measures, particularly in the 10- and 15-minute consultation time (Supplementary Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study suggests that longer consultation durations have the potential to improve quality of care, as perceived by both the general public and physicians. Our findings indicate that longer consultation time may improve patient experience and the quality of physician–patient communication, while also potentially reducing unnecessary diagnostic testing and second-opinion visits, ultimately promoting more efficient healthcare utilization. These results align with previous studies suggesting that increasing consultation time improves quality of care.262021 Shorter consultations reinforce cognitive biases such as availability bias.1 They have also been associated with efficiency-driven communication patterns, where physicians exert greater control over conversations and limit patient participation.7 Furthermore, time constraints may encourage physicians to substitute quicker, low-value diagnostic testing for time-consuming consultations.21 Extending consultation time may have mitigated these issues by improving the quality of physician–patient interactions, fostering greater trust in physicians, and reducing unnecessary medical utilization. Interestingly, the greatest “marginal” improvements were observed for the change from 5- to 10-minute consultations. As expected, the most positive perceptions were observed at 15 minutes. These findings are consistent with prior research, which has reported that consultation times between 10 and 20 minutes are associated with substantial improvements in communication quality and diagnostic accuracy.2

Not all aspects of perceived care quality improved equally. The relatively smaller improvements observed in second-opinion visits and reduced unnecessary tests may be attributed to the fact that these items assess possible downstream outcomes rather than the consultation process described in the vignettes. Since the vignettes did not explicitly indicate these consequences, the perceived effects of consultation time on such outcomes were likely secondary and inferred, relying on the respondents’ interpretation and imagination, thereby leading to smaller perceived changes. Moreover, these outcomes are linked to long-standing perceptions and healthcare utilization habits embedded in the environment and thus less amenable to change through consultation time alone.3 The entrenched practice of “doctor shopping”—where patients visit multiple physicians to obtain additional opinions or prescription, often driven by a consumer-oriented view of healthcare—may limit the anticipated impact of longer consultations on reducing second-opinion visits.

This study also found that across most of the survey items, physicians provided similar or more favorable ratings than the general public for the same consultation durations, with differences more pronounced on certain items for the 10- and 15-minute consultations than for the shorter ones. One possible explanation is that physicians are more likely than patients to view these relatively longer consultations as being already “good enough,” and even somewhat idealized, given the constraints of real-world practice. This may also reflect a sense of fatigue with time-limited settings and a desire for improved care environments. Nonetheless, even a 15-minute consultation may still be insufficient for certain complex or emotionally demanding cases, especially in oncology care.

This study underscores the critical role of adequate consultation time in improving quality of care, highlighting the need for policy investments to achieve this goal. The current reimbursement system fails to adequately compensate for sufficient consultation time, contributing to time constraints in outpatient care.22

The ability of physicians to observe patients more carefully, engage in thorough clinical reasoning, and foster meaningful discussions should be valued at least as much as, if not more than, the volume of tests performed.23 In this regard, ongoing initiatives such as the in-depth consultation pilot program in tertiary hospitals and the multidisciplinary care reimbursement represent meaningful policy efforts. Our findings show that perceived quality of care improves with longer consultation time, with the most substantial improvements observed in the 10- to 15-minute, suggesting that a certain minimum duration may be necessary for meaningful physician–patient interaction. However, these programs remain limited in scope. This study provides empirical support for expanding such initiatives in a broader and more sustainable manner, particularly for patients with serious conditions who require sufficient consultation time. Moreover, structural reforms aimed at reducing patient load per physician are imperative for sustainable improvements in consultation time. A comprehensive approach integrating workforce strengthening, payment reform, reinforcement of the referral system, and system-wide support for extended consultations will be essential. At the same time, medical education should place greater emphasis on communication training to equip future physicians with the skills needed to make effective use of extended consultation time.

The main strength of this study lies in its comprehensive analysis of the potential impact of consultation time on perceived quality of care while also comparing perceptions between the general public and physicians. Unlike previous studies that have primarily focused on specific aspects of healthcare quality or restricted their analysis to particular patient groups,6819 this study adopts a broader perspective by incorporating diverse groups and assessing multiple dimensions of perceived quality of care. Furthermore, the use of an experimental vignette methodology allowed for a systematic evaluation of the causal relationship between consultation duration and perceived quality of care, addressing methodological limitations encountered in prior research. In particular, the vignette design was tailored to reflect the unique outpatient consultation environment in Korea through the development of realistic scenarios, strengthening the practical implications and policy relevance of the findings.

This study has several limitations. First, hypothetical, scenario-based vignettes may not fully capture the complexity and nuance of real-world clinical interactions relevant to time constraints. Although we constructed the vignettes based on the literature regarding key elements of quality of care associated with physician consultation time, as well as years of clinical experience, it remains uncertain whether they fully capture the complex and time-relevant realities of the clinical encounter, such as pre-consultation work and the availability of assistant personnel. Second, because the scenario was designed specifically for a new cancer patient in an outpatient setting at a tertiary hospital, the findings may not be generalizable to other clinical contexts. While we selected the scenario for its relevance in popular discourse and from a societal standpoint, future research could examine more diverse settings, considering both the clinical and patient perspectives. Third, although we selected the outcome measures based on existing literature and their relevance to our study, these measures were not derived from a validated instrument. For greater generalizability, a validated set of questions, ideally in the form of an instrument, is needed. Fourth, the physician sample is not representative of the Korean physician workforce, as it consisted of junior physicians (i.e., excluding more experienced practitioners) and relied on recruiter-based sampling rather than a fully proportionate, quota-driven approach. Therefore, the results of the comparison between the general public and physician samples should be interpreted as suggestive and exploratory. Fifth, our study was conducted amidst the healthcare standoff of 2024 and involved a sample of junior doctors, many of whom were no longer at their previous workplace, as well as individuals from the general public, who might have had different perceptions if surveyed at other times. However, it is unclear whether this peculiar survey setting had any significant consequences on our study.

Despite such limitations, this study suggests that longer consultation durations may lead to more positive perceptions of care quality, among both the general public and physicians. In particular, 10- and 15-minute consultations for new cancer outpatients in tertiary hospitals may offer notable improvements over shorter consultations, underscoring the need to secure increased consultation time for patients with serious illnesses.
