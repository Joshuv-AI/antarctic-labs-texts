Isolated third ventricular hydatid cyst at the foramina of Monro presenting with obstructive hydrocephalus: A case report
Authors: Injam Ibrahim Sulaiman Rowandizy, Ahmed Adnan Al-Juboori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Hydatid cysts are uncommon parasitic lesions of the central nervous system (CNS). Among these, intraventricular involvement is exceptionally rare, with only a handful of cases reported in the literature. Occurrence within the third ventricle is particularly unusual.Case Description: We present the case of a 51-year-old male from an endemic region in Iraq who developed a third ventricular hydatid cyst presenting with hydrocephalus. The patient exhibited symptoms of obstructive hydrocephalus, including headaches, vomiting, and gait instability. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a sharply marginated, cerebrospinal fluid-isointense, non-enhancing cystic lesion measuring approximately 2.8 × 2.5 × 2.3 cm within the third ventricle, obstructing the foramina of Monro, and resulting in triventricular hydrocephalus. Serological testing confirmed Echinococcus granulosus infection. The cyst was surgically removed intact through a transcortical-transventricular approach using the Dowling technique, avoiding rupture-related complications. Postoperative recovery was smooth, and adjunct albendazole therapy was administered.Conclusion: This case highlights the importance of high clinical suspicion, precise imaging interpretation, and meticulous surgical technique when managing rare CNS hydatid cysts, especially in endemic areas. To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of the very few reported cases of a third ventricular hydatid cyst worldwide. The report highlights both the extreme rarity of this localization and the importance of meticulous surgical removal and adjunct antiparasitic therapy in preventing recurrence, offering valuable insights for neurosurgeons practicing in endemic regions.

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of intracranial hydatid disease represents approximately 1–2% of all reported cases of hydatid disease.[1] Hydatid disease is a zoonotic infection caused by the cestodes Echinococcus multilocularis and Echinococcus granulosus, which are endemic in certain regions. Humans act as intermediate hosts, typically acquiring the infection through ingestion of food or water contaminated with parasite eggs from definitive hosts such as dogs.[6]

Hydatid disease has a worldwide distribution but remains highly endemic in specific regions, including Mediterranean countries, the Middle East, India, Australia, and parts of Latin America.

The liver is the most frequently involved organ (approximately 75%), followed by the lungs (15%), and less commonly the central nervous system (CNS), which accounts for only 0.6–2% of cases. Cerebral hydatid disease is sporadic and tends to occur more often in children and young adults.[4,5,7]

Among CNS presentations, intraventricular hydatid cysts are exceptionally uncommon. To date, only 47 cases have been reported in the literature. Even more rarely, and to the best of our knowledge, only three cases of third ventricular hydatid cysts have been reported.[6] This case report describes a rare instance of a third ventricular hydatid cyst in an adult patient from Iraq, presenting with obstructive hydrocephalus. This report aims to highlight the diagnostic challenges and surgical considerations associated with this unusual presentation, particularly in endemic regions where such parasitic infections may be overlooked.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 51-year-old male presented with a 3-month history of progressively worsening early-morning headaches, intermittent episodes of projectile vomiting, transient visual obscurations, and new-onset gait instability. The patient denied focal motor or sensory deficits, seizures, or altered consciousness. On neurologic examination, bilateral papilledema was observed, without evidence of cranial nerve palsies, hemiparesis, or sensory level. Cerebellar signs were absent, though the patient exhibited a wide-based, unsteady gait.

Contrast enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed a sharply demarcated, cerebrospinal fluid-hyperdense spherical lesion measuring approximately 2.8 × 2.5 × 2.3 cm located within the third ventricle. The lesion was causing near-complete obstruction of both foramina of Monro, leading to upstream dilation of the lateral ventricles and resulting in obstructive hydrocephalus. There was no evidence of perilesional edema or contrast enhancement, suggesting a cystic etiology [Figures 1 and 2].

Serologic testing for parasitic infections was positive for E. granulosus, supporting the diagnosis of a third ventricular hydatid cyst. The patient had no prior history of hydatid disease and reported no contact with definitive hosts such as dogs or sheep, although he resided in an endemic rural area.

Given the cyst’s precarious location within the third ventricle and the high risk of intraoperative rupture, which could lead to anaphylaxis or dissemination, a meticulously planned neurosurgical intervention was undertaken. Through a right frontal craniotomy and transcortical-transventricular approach, the cyst was successfully removed intact using the Dowling technique, employing gentle irrigation and hydrodissection to avoid rupture.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with resolution of papilledema and improvement in gait. Follow-up imaging showed decompression of the ventricular system and no residual cyst [Figure 3]. No neurological deficits were noted at the time of discharge. He was discharged on the 7th postoperative day in stable condition and was prescribed albendazole as adjunct antiparasitic therapy.

DISCUSSION

Hydatid disease of the CNS is a rare occurrence, accounting for <2% of all hydatid disease cases.[6,8] Intraventricular involvement, especially within the third ventricle, is exceedingly uncommon and poses significant diagnostic and surgical challenges. Our case of a third ventricular hydatid cyst adds to a small but growing body of literature on this rare entity.

Aydin et al.[2] reported a highly unusual case involving four hydatid cysts within the lateral ventricle, which were removed intact using custom-designed ellipsoid forceps. Their case highlighted both the technical complexity of intraventricular surgery and the necessity for precise instrumentation to prevent cyst rupture and subsequent complications. Similarly, Guzel et al.[4] described a giant primary intraventricular hydatid cyst in a pediatric patient, highlighting that such cysts can remain asymptomatic for extended periods due to the accommodating nature of pediatric cranial anatomy. In their report, the cyst was successfully excised using Dowling’s technique, and its intraventricular location was confirmed intraoperatively, underscoring the diagnostic limitations of preoperative imaging.

Consistent with those findings, our adult patient presented with symptoms of obstructive hydrocephalus, including headache, vomiting, and gait disturbance. MRI findings were characteristic of a cystic lesion without perilesional edema or enhancement, aiding the diagnosis. However, as both cited cases illustrate, even with modern imaging, definitive diagnosis often relies on intraoperative findings and histopathology.

Our findings align with the broader literature on intracranial hydatid disease. Duishanbai et al.[3] reported a large pediatric series of 30 cases, emphasizing that increased intracranial pressure, headache, and vomiting were the most common presentations, while intact surgical removal using Dowling’s technique resulted in excellent outcomes in the majority of patients. Similarly, Panagopoulos et al.[7] described a solitary cerebral hydatid cyst in an 11-year-old child and highlighted the critical role of MRI in differentiating hydatid cysts from other cystic lesions, as well as the importance of en bloc excision without rupture to avoid dissemination and anaphylaxis. Both studies reinforce that while clinical and radiological features may be nonspecific, early recognition and careful surgical planning are essential to achieving favorable outcomes.

The intact surgical removal of the cyst using the Dowling technique in our case was crucial in preventing rupture, which can lead to anaphylaxis, meningitis, or local recurrence. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and adjunct albendazole therapy was administered to minimize recurrence risk.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the diagnostic and surgical challenges of managing a rare third ventricular hydatid cyst. Careful imaging evaluation, combined with meticulous surgical technique, enabled successful treatment without complications. Such cases, though uncommon, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of intraventricular cystic lesions, particularly in endemic regions.
