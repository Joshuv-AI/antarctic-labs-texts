Effect of ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction on functional properties and flavor characteristics of Oyster protein enzymatic hydrolysates
Authors: Zilong Zhao, Wenduo Wang, Jin Chen, Jianxu Chen, Jinying Deng, Guixian Wu, Chunxia Zhou, Guili Jiang, Jingjing Guan, Donghui Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the delamination phenomenon during storage and flavor characteristics of Oyster protein hydrolysates (OPH). In this study, xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) were selected to covalently graft with OPH through ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction, and the effect of ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction on the structure, functional properties, and flavor characteristics of OPH were investigated. The results revealed that the ultrasound treatment led to a 1.46-fold increase in the degree of grafting compared with the conventional wet-heat Maillard reaction methods. Structural analyses at various levels indicated substantial alterations in the OPH structure following the ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction. More ordered α-helical secondary structures were shifting to random coiling, the tertiary structure showed more stretching changes, and the surface structure was characterized by loose and porous features. Compared with OPH, the solubility of the ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction products (OPH-U-M) increased from 54.67% to 70.14%, leading to a notable enhancement in storage stability. Flavor profile analysis demonstrated a decrease in unsaturated aldehydes and ketones presenting fishy and bitter aromas, while an increase in presenting meat aroma compounds was observed in OPH-U-M. Furthermore, OPH-U-M exhibited superior antioxidant properties with DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging abilities enhancing 46.05% and 42.09% in comparison with OPH, respectively. The results demonstrated that covalently binding with XOS under ultrasonication pretreatment endowed OPH with superior functional properties (including solubility, storage stability, and antioxidant activity), and the improvement of flavor profile. This study can provide theoretical guidance and practical implications for promoting the processing applications of oyster protein.Keywords: Oyster protein hydrolysate, Ultrasonication pretreatment, Maillard reaction, Functional properties, Flavor Characteristics

Introduction

Oysters, specifically Crassostrea gigas, are one of the shellfish with the most economically important in China, due to their high protein content, exceeding 50% by dry weight, making them a high-quality protein source [1]. Oyster protein hydrolysates (OPH) are derived from oysters through protease hydrolysis and have rich biological activities (including antioxidant, anti-aging, and anti-inflammatory properties), so it is widely applied in the preparation of functional foods [2], [3]. Although OPH has abundant beneficial functional properties, its poor storage stability and heavy fishy odor greatly limit the application of OPH in the food industry [2], [4], [5].

Maillard reaction is a non-enzymatic browning reaction between free amino residues on proteins/peptides and the carbonyl groups of reducing saccharides, which occurs spontaneously without additional chemical reagents and is regarded as a green means of protein modification [6]. Maillard reaction can covalently graft saccharide molecules rich in hydrophilic groups onto proteins, thus conferring better solubility to proteins [7], as well as the conformational flexibility and stretchability of proteins by exposing more internal groups to the surface of the structure, resulting in better emulsification and antioxidant properties [8], [9]. A previous study reported that casein's solubility, thermal stability, and antioxidant activity were significantly increased after the glycosylation modification of casein with arabinogalactan [10]. Moreover, Maillard reaction products (MRPs) also contribute unique umami textures and meat aromas to food products [11], [12]. De Sousa Fontes et al. discovered that the MRPs of goat protein hydrolysate with a strong roasted and cooked meat aroma [13]. According to the content number of monosaccharides, carbohydrates can be divided into monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides, among which oligosaccharides have attracted the attention of researchers because of their rich functional activities (such as good water solubility, regulation of intestinal flora, etc.). Xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) is a representative functional oligosaccharide with high water-solubility and regulation of human intestinal health, which has great potential application in the Maillard reaction [14].

Ultrasonication is an energy-efficient physical treatment that expedites intermolecular movements by augmenting the efficiency of heat and mass transfer within the system via mechanical and cavitation [15]. Ultrasound pretreatment can enhance the binding of free amino active sites with reducing saccharides, thereby increasing the rate of the Maillard reaction. Therefore, the application of ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction to improve efficiency and functional properties of protein-glycan coupling has become one of the hot spots in recent years [16], [17]. Previous studies have found that the glycosylation of peanut protein isolates with maltodextrin under ultrasonication pretreatment conditions for 40 min can achieve the same degree of graft under conventional wet heating treatment for 48h [18]. In addition, the ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction requires less activation energy than the conventional wet-heating methods, which contributes to the formation of more aromatic compounds [19]. For instance, it has been reported that the variety and content of roasted meat aroma compounds in fish protein hydrolysates increased, along with significant structural changes and enhanced antioxidant capacity after ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction [20].

However, there are few studies on the improvement of functional and flavor characteristics of OPH by ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction. Therefore, this study selected XOS as the oligosaccharide model to study how the structural changes of OPH affect its function and flavor characteristics under the condition of ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction between OPH and oligosaccharides. The structure of OPH was characterized by the degree of graft (DG), fluorescence spectrum, Fourier infrared spectrum, circular dichroism, and scanning electron microscopy. The effects of solubility, thermal stability, storage stability, and antioxidant capacity on its functional properties were investigated. Finally, the flavor characteristics of OPH were analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS). The results of this study are expected to provide theoretical guidance and reference for promoting the processing and application of oyster protein.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemical reagents

The fresh oysters were purchased from a local aquatic market (Yangjiang, Guangdong, China). Pancreatin, XOS (purity ≥95 wt%), and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl hydrazine (DPPH) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Flavourzyme (1.5×106 U/g from Aspergillus oryzae), Ortho-phthalaldehyde (OPA), 8-Phenylamino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS), β-mercaptoethanol, 2,2′-Azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). All other reagents used in this study were analytical grade.

Preparation of OPH and Maillard reaction products (MRPs)

Preparation of OPH was referred to the method of Zhang et al with slight modification [21]. The oyster homogenate was obtained by combining 20.0 g with distilled water at a mass ratio of 1:3 (w/v). The mixture was then homogenized for 2 min using a high-speed disperser (T18, IKA, Germany). Two enzyme mixtures (Flavourzyme and Pancreatin) were added to the homogenate at a mass ratio of 1:2 (w/w) after adjusting the pH value of the homogenate to 7.5. The enzymolysis was conducted for 4h at 50 °C and 150 g in a shaking water bath. Following the completion of the enzymolysis, the OPH was placed in a boiling water bath for 10 min for inactivation, Subsequently, the supernatant was obtained by centrifuging at 10,000 g for 10 min and stored at 4 °C.

The OPH was mixed with 3% XOS in equal proportions with the pH value adjusting to 9.0, under the condition of 300 W ultrasonic processing for 20 min (JY96-IIN, SCIENTZ, China) at 90 ℃ wet-heating for 1.5h, conventional MRPs without ultrasound treatment. After the reaction, the mixture was cooled in an ice bath to terminate the reaction, centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was taken for further experiments.

Measurement of browning intensity

The absorbance of the samples at 294 nm and 420 nm was measured as an indicator of the Maillard reaction intermediates and the browning intensity using a UV spectrophotometer (UV-1900i, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) after samples were diluted 50-fold and 20-fold with distilled water, respectively.

Determination of the degree of graft (DG)

Referring to the method of Yang et al [22], the DG of MRPs was determined using the OPA method. Characterization of OPH and OPH-XOS conjugates

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy of samples (0.1 mg/mL) was recorded on the fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF6000, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan). The excitation wavelength (λex) was set as 280 nm with an emission wavelength (λem) of 310–420 nm, and the slit width was 5 nm.

The λex was set as 347 nm with λem of 360–550 nm, and the scanning speed was 1200 nm/min to observe the changes of fluorescent compounds in MPRs.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

The freeze-dried samples were combined with potassium bromide powder in a ratio of 1:100 (w/w). The mixture was then compressed to form sheets for analysis utilizing a Fourier infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). FT-IR spectra were collected in the wavenumber range from 4000 to 400 cm−1, by scanning the samples 32 times and employing a blank potassium bromide sample as the background spectrum.

Far-UV circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

The CD spectra of each sample were determined using a circular dichroism spectrometer (Chirascan V100, Applied PhotopHysics Ltd, Surrey, UK) with the following parameters: The wavelength range was detected from 190 to 260 nm, the sample (0.1 mg/mL) volume was 200 μL, the response time was 0.25 s, and ultrapure water was used as a blank under nitrogen flow protection. Subsequently, the CDNN program (version 4.0) was used to calculate the secondary structural content of the samples.

Surface hydrophobicity index (H0)

The surface hydrophobicity of samples was evaluated utilizing ANS fluorescent probe based on the method of Wang et al with slight modifications [6]. In brief, 20 μL of ANS solution (8.0 mmol/L) was respectively added to 4 mL of the different concentrations of sample solution (0.005–0.1 mg/mL) and mixed well in the dark for 15 min. The fluorescence intensity of the sample was recorded at 390 nm (λex) and 470 nm (λem) using a fluorescence spectrophotometer, and the fluorescence intensity was plotted against the protein concentration, the slope of which was taken as an indicator of H0 of the protein.

The content of the free sulphydryl (−SH) group

Particle size and zeta potential

To avoid multiple scattering, the sample solution was diluted 200 and 500 times with distilled water before measurement, and 1.5 mL of the sample was taken to determine the mean particle size and zeta potential of the sample using a laser particle size and zeta potential meter (NanoBrook Omni, Bruker, NY, USA) at room temperature.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface structure of the samples was determined using scanning electron microscopy (JSM-7610-F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The freeze-dried samples were placed on stage and then plated gold onto the surface of the sample in a vacuum environment. The surface morphology of the samples was observed at an appropriate working voltage.

The molecular weight (MW) distribution

The MW distribution was applied by the method according to Sun et al with appropriate modification [24]. High-performance gel-filtration chromatography (1260, Agilent, CA, USA) equipped with a TSK gel filtration column (30 cm × 7.8 mm I.D.) was employed, and the mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile: water: trifluoroacetic acid (20:80:0.1) at the flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The temperature was 25 °C, the sample volume was 20 μL, and the UV detection was monitored at 220 nm. The standards were prepared with insulin (MW: 5778 Da), bacitracin (MW: 1422 Da), Gly-Gly-Tyr-Arg (MW: 451 Da), and Gly-Gly-Gly (MW: 189 Da). Afterward, The MW distribution of the samples was evaluated by building the calibration curve from the corresponding retention time of the standards versus MW.

Results and Discussion

Analysis of browning intensity

The formation of the intermediate product (Amadori compound) and brown polymer (melanoidin) were correlated with browning in the system during the Maillard reaction, displaying pronounced strong absorption peaks at UV wavelengths of 294 nm and 420 nm, respectively [8]. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the absorbance of intermediates and browning intensity in OPH-U-M were significantly increased compared to those of OPH and OPH-M, indicating that ultrasonication can accelerate the Maillard reaction process. This finding is consistent with previous reports by Ye et al [29], which proposed the mechanical force generated by ultrasound enhances the collision frequency between substrate and saccharide molecules, resulting in increased formation of intermediate products. These intermediates may further convert into melanoidins, thereby intensifying the browning degree. Moreover, the transient high-temperature and high-pressure conditions induced by ultrasound reduced the activation energy required for the generation of intermediates and melanoidins compared to the conventional Maillard reaction, deepening the browning degree [19].

Degree of graft (DG)

The degree of graft (DG) of the Maillard reaction can be indicated by the changes in free amino acid content in proteins. In Fig. 1B, the OPH-U-M exhibited the highest DG value (28.59%), which was 1.46 times higher than that of OPH-M (19.56%). This variation might be attributed to the shear force generated by ultrasound, which unfolded the peptide chain and promoted the conjugation reaction ratio between the free amino group of OPH and the XOS carbonyl group [30], thus leading to an increase in the degree of graft. This finding is consistent with a previous study, suggesting that ultrasonication exposed more free amino groups of soybean protein isolate to couple with Lentinan polysaccharides, resulting in an increased degree of grafting [31].

Structural characterization analysis

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Tryptophan (Trp) residues with fluorescence emission properties are typically enclosed inside the protein structure. The shift in the maximum emission wavelength (λmax) of Trp fluorescence spectra can be utilized to monitor alterations in the microstructural environment of the proteins and applied to verify changes in the protein tertiary structure [28]. As shown in Fig. 2A, the λmax of OPH-M shifted from 340 nm to 351 nm, representing a “red-shift” phenomenon which indicated hydrophobic amino acids (such as Trp) were more exposed onto the surface of the protein structure, showing the protein structure from dense to stretched state. In comparison to OPH-M, the λmax of OPH-U-M shifted from 340 nm to 353 nm which might be attributed to the structure of OPH becoming more stretched and looser due to the ultrasonication pretreatment. Additionally, all MRPs exhibited a decrease in fluorescence intensity compared to OPH, which is correlated to the “masking” effect caused by the covalent grafting of XOS to the surface structure of OPH [32]. The lowest fluorescence intensity observed for OPH-U-M indicated that ultrasonication pretreatment promoted covalently grafting of XOS to OPH, which aligned with the report of Cui et al stating that soybean protein isolate coupled with glucose experienced decreased fluorescence intensity with increasing sonication amplitude [30].

The initial stages of the Maillard reaction could form ketoaldehydes fluorescent compounds serving as precursors to browning compounds and are detectable at the excitation wavelength of 347 nm [33]. As depicted in Fig. 2B, OPH-M, and OPH-U-M exhibited strong absorption peaks at 400–450 nm compared to OPH, with OPH-U-M showing the highest fluorescence intensity, which can be attributed to ultrasonication pretreatment exposing more free amino groups in OPH and enhancing the intermolecular movement with XOS which promoted the process of the Maillard reaction, consequently increasing the fluorescence intensity. Habinshuti et al also found that the fluorescence intensity in ultrasound-assisted preparation of sweet potato protein hydrolysate-xylose conjugate was higher than in conventional wet-heating Maillard reaction samples [34].

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

FT-IR spectra were frequently utilized to reflect the changes in functional groups of OPH after glycosylation modification [35]. The FT-IR spectra of samples were depicted in Fig. 2C. In the FT-IR spectrum of OPH, two characteristic absorption peaks appeared at 1700–1600 cm−1 and 1550–1500 cm−1, corresponding to the amide I band (C=O stretching vibration) and amide II band (N-H bending vibration), respectively [33]. A comparison with OPH revealed that the absorption peak of the amide I band in OPH-M shifted from 1645 cm−1 to 1630 cm−1, indicating that the secondary structure of OPH was changed while the absorption intensity of the amide II band significantly decreased due to covalent binding between the amino group of OPH and XOS's carbonyl group, and the loss of –NH2. Moreover, the broadening of the absorption peak at 3500–3000 cm−1 in OPH-M indicated enhanced –OH stretching vibration, which might be due to the introduction of XOS's –OH group strengthening hydrogen bonding. This finding is consistent with a previous report, suggesting that the absorption band from 3250 cm−1 to 3400 cm−1 of myofibrillar protein peptides was broadened and increased in free hydroxyl groups after glycosylation [15]. Characteristic peaks within the 1180–950 cm−1 range were typically referred to as the “saccharide” bands, reflecting covalent binding between saccharide molecules and proteins [36], with higher absorption intensity observed for OPH-M compared to OPH further implying the formation of covalent bonds between OPH and XOS. Additionally, these variations at the 3500–3000 cm−1 and 1180–950 cm−1 became more obvious in the OPH-U-M, which signified more XOS were induced to covalently bond with OPH under ultrasonication treatment [8]. This observation is consistent with the DG results, which further confirm the ultrasonication treatment facilitated the Maillard reaction.

Far-UV CD spectra (190–260 nm) can reflect the changes in the protein secondary structure after the Maillard reaction [6]. In Fig. 2D, OPH displayed two negative peaks at 208 and 224 nm, which were the characteristic peaks of α-helices, along with a negative absorption band at 217 nm and a positive absorption peak at 195–198 nm revealed the existence of β-sheet structure [37]. The analysis of the secondary structure content indicated a decrease in α-helix content (from 20.02% to 15.28%), while an elevation both in the β-sheet content (from 39.98% to 44.38%) and random coil content (from 25.35% to 27.06%) in OPH-M, compared with OPH. These alterations were likely due to the covalently binding of reactive amino groups distributed in the α-helix region to the carbonyl group of XOS, causing the disruption of intramolecular forces and the rearrangement of intermolecular forces within proteins, which transformed more α-helix to the β-sheet, Simultaneously, the molecular structure of OPH was unfolded increasing the content of random coil through Maillard reaction [36]. Furthermore, the random coil content increased to 29.30% in OHP-U-M, which might be attributed to the cavitation effect of ultrasound that destroyed the protein–protein interactions (particularly α-helix) and exposing more amino sites to expedite the Maillard reaction process, making the molecular structure looser [38]. These outcomes align with a previous study that the content of α-helix was decreased and the random coil content was elevated in soybean protein isolate-Lentinan polysaccharides conjugate by ultrasonication [31].

Surface hydrophobicity index (H0) and free sulfhydryl content

Protein hydrophobic residues are typically encapsulated inside proteins and the H0 indicates the distribution of hydrophobic residues on the protein surface, which in turn reflects the conformational changes in protein [15]. As can be seen in Fig. 3A, the values of H0 in OPH, OPH-M, and OPH-U-M were 416.90, 211.77, and 153.84 respectively, OPH had the highest value of H0, which might be attributed to the forces that maintain protein conformation were disrupted (e.g., van der Waals forces, intramolecular hydrogen bonds) during the heating and the internal hydrophobic groups were exposed, hydrophobic interactions was enhanced, resulting in the observed increase in H0 [25]. Since the presence of ample hydrophilicity hydroxyl groups in XOS, along with covalent binding with OPH, the H0 value of OPH-M and OPH-U-M was significantly decreased, regulating the hydrophilic/hydrophobic balance on the surface of OPH and preventing the inter- and intra-molecular hydrophobic interactions between conjugate molecules [8]. In addition, the decrease of H0 could also be due to the shielding effect of saccharide molecules inhibiting ANS attachment to hydrophobic residues in OPH [36], which is consistent with the endogenous fluorescence analysis. Yang et al proposed that the H0 value of the conjugate of casein (CP) with arabinogalactan (AG) was significantly decreased compared to CP, which also represented an improvement of the protein solubility [10]. The lowest H0 value observed in OPH-U-M was attributed to that more polypeptide chains of OPH were unfolded by the ultrasonication force, allowing more hydrophilic hydroxyl groups in XOS to be introduced to the OPH structure surface, resulting in decreased surface hydrophobicity”.

Free sulfhydryl groups are susceptible to oxidation and thus form intermolecular disulfide bonds, which cause protein aggregation and affect the functional properties of proteins [30]. Despite the high-temperature conditions promoting the conversion of free sulfhydryl groups into disulfide bonds, the steric hindrance provided by saccharide molecules inhibited the formation of disulfide bonds [32]. In Fig. 3A, the free sulfhydryl content of OPH was the lowest (2.60 μmol/g), which might be due to the oxidation of the sulfhydryl groups and the conversion to disulfide bonds during the heating, possibly leading to polymerization reactions and delamination phenomenon. The free sulfhydryl content of OPH-M increased notably compared to OPH, implying more free sulfhydryl groups of OPH were released during the Maillard reaction, and the formation of disulfide bonds was inhibited by steric hindrance provided by XOS. OPH-U-M exhibited the highest content of free sulfhydryl groups (10.71 μmol/g), which was attributed to the ultrasonic cavitation effect that loosens and stretches OPH, accelerating the process of Maillard reaction, while enhancing the spatial resistance, consequently increasing the free sulfhydryl group contents.

Particle size and zeta potential

The mean particle size of protein could reflect the aggregation status of protein particles [39]. The particle size of different samples are shown in Fig. 3B. The order of mean size was OPH > OPH-M > OPH-U-M, which was attributed to the introduction of XOS enhancing steric repulsion, and thus diminished the protein aggregation tendency of OPH to some extent. Additionally, ultrasonication could disintegrate the internal aggregates of proteins and magnify the grafting efficiency with XOS, resulting in the smallest mean particle size for OPH-U-M. Li et al found that under ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction conditions, the highly ordered myofibrillar protein structure was cleaved into smaller sub-filament structures, increasing the DG, and thus decreasing the particle size [38], which is consistent with the free sulfhydryl groups analysis.

Zeta potential can reveal the surface charge of protein molecules in an aqueous medium, predict interactions with protein surfaces and the stability of its solution, and when the absolute value of zeta potential is high, it indicates that the electrostatic repulsion between protein moleculars is high, which can effectively prevent molecular aggregation [40]. As shown in Fig. 3B, all the sample solutions exhibited negative charges, and the absolute value of zeta potential of both OPH-M and OPH-U-M being larger than that of OPH (from −18.48 mV of OPH to –23.89 mV and −29.13 mV of OPH-M and OPH-U-M, respectively). This was due to the covalent grafting of XOS with OPH, introducing a large number of negatively charged hydroxyl groups to the surface of OPH, increasing the negative potential of OPH and maintaining the stability of solution system. This variation was obvious at OPH-U-M, which was possibly due to ultrasonication inducing the unfolding of the peptide structure, exposing more amino sites, according to the mutual attraction of heterologous charges, the exposed positively charged amino acid residues are more likely to covalently bind with XOS, thus increasing the negative potential. Yan et al. also found that after glycated with the monosaccharides (glucose, galactose, and xylose) the zeta potential of OVA (ovalbumin) showed a decrease in comparison with the original state, which might be attributed to the glycation allowed more hydroxyl groups of saccharides to the protein surface, thus enhancing the electrostatic repulsion between the protein molecules and maintaining the stability of the proein solution system [41].

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The SEM results of all samples are shown in Fig. 4. OPH exhibited an uneven distribution with numerous fragments surface, while the surface structure of OPH-M displayed fewer fragments and sparse pores, and the OPH-U-M surface structure appeared even less dense with widened pore sizes. These observations might be related to the following reasons: (1) The high-frequency shear force generated by ultrasound induces changes in the OPH structure, resulting in broadening the pore size of the surface structure [15]. (2) During the Maillard reaction, the OPH structure was unfolded, making the surface structure loose [35].

The molecular weight (MW) distribution

The results for MW distribution of all samples are illustrated in Table 1. In comparison to OPH, the content of >3 kDa in MRPs was decreased, suggesting that small MW peptides were provided from the degradation of large MW peptides. Moreover, the content of <0.2 kDa was decreased, while the content of 0.2–3 kDa was increased in MRPs, implying XOS could cross-link with peptide degradation products to generate higher MW peptides [33]. Whereas, OPH-U-M contained a higher content of 0.5–1 kDa peptides compared to OPH-M, demonstrating that ultrasonication would accelerate the cross-linking of <0.2 kDa peptides with XOS during the Maillard reaction. Meanwhile, previous studies have confirmed low molecular weight peptides are associated with antioxidant activity. Habinshuti et al discovered that ultrasonication increased the molecular weight content and enhanced the antioxidant activity of MRPs <1 kDa peptides derived from sweet potato protein hydrolysate [34].

Functional properties

Protein solubility

The solubility of proteins is a fundamental functional property that impacts other functional properties [35]. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the solubility of OPH-M and OPH-U-M significantly elevated compared with that of OPH (from 54.67% of OPH to 64.80% and 70.14% of OPH-M and OPH-U-M, respectively). After heating, the internal hydrophobic groups in OPH were exposed and hydrophobic interactions were enhanced, promoting protein aggregation which decreased the protein solubility. Glycated with XOS through Maillard reaction could increase the protein solubility of OPH, which might be attributed to the following reasons: (1) numerous hydrophilic hydroxyl groups of XOS were bound with OPH, which enhanced the affinity for water molecules and increased the OPH solubility; (2) during the Maillard reaction, the presence of XOS saccharide chains prevented the degradation of protein solubility due to aggregation. OPH-U-M exhibited the highest solubility, possibly due to the cavitation effect of ultrasound inducing the peptide chains of OPH to form a more loose and porous surface structure (Fig. 4D), enabling them to bind more water molecules, corresponding to the result of OPH-U-M has the highest DG value [25]. This is in agreement with the results of Li et al, who discovered that the solubility of goat's milk protein and pectin coupling prepared by ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction had the highest solubility [39].

Solubility of samples with different treatments.

Storage stability

The protein sedimentation rate can reflect the degree of protein aggregation during the storage period [5]. Table 2 and Fig. 6 present the protein sedimentation rate and macroscopic changes during storage, respectively. OPH exhibited evident delamination after 5 days of storage, with the protein sedimentation rate increasing significantly from 8.74% to 40.41% within 30 days. Conversely, OPH-M improved the delamination phenomenon with a decrease in protein sedimentation rate by 11.33% after 30 days, which might be related to the number of hydrophilic groups that were increased in the conjugate, enhancing solubility and suppressing the aggregation effect induced by hydrophobic interactions. Furthermore, XOS provides more negative charges, increasing the intermolecular electrostatic repulsion of OPH and promoting the stability of the solution system (Fig. 3B) [42]. Meanwhile, OPH-U-M showed the lowest protein sedimentation rate (20.49%) after 30 days. These results might be related to the following reasons: (1) The interfacial waves generated by ultrasonication inhibited the refolding of the peptide chains, and the structure of OPH became more stretched and looser, therefore introducing more hydrophilic groups and enhancing the solubility. (2) Ultrasonication dissociated the internal aggregates of OPH, exposing more amino sites and magnifying the grafting efficiency with XOS which decreased the particle size and enhanced the intermolecular electrostatic repulsion to suppress the delamination phenomenon.

Thermal stability

High-temperature thermal reactions can disrupt the natural conformation of proteins, causing protein flocculation. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) is utilized to assess the thermal stability of proteins by calculating the denaturation temperature (Td), where higher Td values represent better thermal stability [42]. In Fig. 7, DSC spectra of all samples showed broad peaks between 60–90 °C. MRPs indicating higher Td values and improved thermal stability were extrapolated from the absorption peaks of MPRs that shifted to the right compared with OPH (from 71.84 ℃ of OPH to 77.07 ℃ and 81.56 ℃ of OPH-M and OPH-U-M, respectively). These might be due to the destruction of non-covalent interactions (e.g., van der Waals forces, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bonds) in the tertiary structure of OPH after glycosylation and the introduction of XOS in which enhanced steric hindrance and protein solubility and therefore effectively suppressed the aggregation due to heat-induced of protein, Corresponding to the results of free sulfhydryl groups [43]. OPH-U-M had the highest Td value, possibly as its maximum DG, aligning with the discovery that ultrasonication enhances the thermal stability of pea protein isolate −XOS conjugate [14].

Flavor characterization

The composition and content of volatile compounds are closely correlated to the flavor profile. A total of 78, 105, and 102 volatile compounds were detected and relatively quantified from OPH, OPH-M, and OPH-U-M, respectively. Among these, 47 new compounds were generated by both two MRPs (Fig. 8A), containing 2 aldehydes, 1 alcohol, 2 esters, 9 ketones, 3 alkanes, 26 heterocyclic compounds, 2 sulfide-containing compounds, and 2 others.

The Venn diagram (A), the contents of flavor compounds (B), the PCA (C), the heat map of MRPs (OPH-M, OPH-U-M), and OPH(D) with different treatments.

As shown in Fig. 8B, aldehydes were the predominant components of OPH volatile compounds (relative content was 46.98%), arising from the oxidation of proteins and lipids, generating short-chained aldehydes and unsaturated aldehydes, contributed undesirable odors like green grassy, ichthyic, and fatty odors due to its low odor threshold. The number of aldehyde species (from 17 to 12 species) and their relative content dramatically decreased in OPH-M (from 46.98% to 15.10%), and the relative content of aldehydes in OPH-U-M was only 12.28%, which was the lowest percentage. This decline is attributed to the degradation of reactive carbonyl compounds, particularly unsaturated and dibasic aldehydes, through Strecker degradation, with ultrasonication expediting this process and reducing the aldehyde content [44].

Alcohols are primarily formed through fatty acid degradation and have a high odor threshold and minimal impact on flavor [45]. Among OPH, 1-octen-3-ol with mushroom and fatty flavor has the highest content in alcohols and is an important flavor contributor among alcohols [46]. Conversely, its content was decreased in OPH-M and OPH-U-M.

Ketones, generated through the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids or amino acids degradation, have a low odor threshold and impact floral and fruity aromas that considerably improve flavor [45]. The relative content of ketones in OPH-M was increased (from 5.88% to 10.96%), compared to OPH, especially the straight-chain ketone with the fruity and floral aroma. Interestingly, unsaturated ketones contributed to undesirable odors like fishy, mushroomy, and fatty odors were decreased. Furthermore, three new ketones (2,3-heptanedione, 3-hepten-2-one, and 2-pentadecanone) were newly formed in OPH-U-M, contributing buttery, floral, and fruity aromas that positively affected the flavor of OPH [46].

The newly formed heterocyclic compounds (e.g., pyrazine, furan, thiazole) and sulfide-containing compounds in MRPs with nutty and roasted meat cooked aroma had a positive effect on OPH flavor [47]. The content of pyrazine in OPH-U-M was increased compared to OPH-M, which is attributed to the reduction of activation energy for pyrazine synthesis due to the transient high-temperature and high-pressure conditions induced by ultrasonication treatment [29]. Meanwhile, it has been suggested that peptide-terminal amino acids in the range of 0.5–1 kDa are more active than free amino acids and large peptides in forming higher levels of meat flavors (particularly pyrazine), which is consistent with the highest content of the <1 kDa fraction of OPH-U-M in this study [16], [24], [48].

The results of the principal component analysis (PCA) are shown in Fig. 8C, the total contribution of PC1-PC2 was 96.3%, and the samples were distributed in different quadrants, indicating flavor differences. Meanwhile, to further investigate the contribution of volatile components, The OAV value can be utilized to assess the impact of volatile compounds, it was generally considered that OAV ≥ 1 was of great significance to the overall aroma contribution, with the 26 volatile compounds exhibiting OAV ≥ 1 identified based on the content and threshold of volatile compounds in Table S2 (including 9 aldehydes, 2 alcohols, 5 ketones, 8 heterocyclic compounds, and 2 sulfide-containing compounds) which were serving as variables for a heat cluster diagram, which visually reflected the differences of volatile compounds among samples [26], and the results are depicted in Fig. 8D. The contents of unsaturated aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones with ichthyic and fatty flavors were diminished in the MPRs, whereas the contents of heterocyclic compounds with nutty, sulfide-containing compounds with meaty flavors, and floral and fruity straight-chain ketones were significantly increased, which improved the overall aroma of the OPH. In addition, the presence of double bonds in the heterocyclic compounds like pyrazine, thiophene, and furan enhanced the antioxidant capacity and formed the roasted meat aroma of OPH. Moreover, compounds of 2-Ethyl-6-methyl-pyrazine, Trimethyl-pyrazine, and Dimethyl trisulfide with OAV ≥ 100 due to their low threshold, which imparts roasted and meaty flavors not present in OPH, are identified as the primary key flavor-active substances in MRPs. Interestingly, the production of pyrazine and sulfide-containing compounds might be associated with <1 kDa peptides which may be attributed to small MW peptides being more active than free amino acids and large peptides in the Strecker degradation during the Maillard reaction [16], [24], [48]. Compared with OPH-M, OPH-U-M not only had more meaty compounds but also increased the content of floral and fruity compounds, which enriched the overall aroma of OPH. In summary, ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction could effectively improve the overall flavor characteristics of OPH.

ME (matrix effect) could affect and pose an obstacle to the detection and quantification of target compounds in complex matrices, which are caused by different matrix components in the system [27]. Matrix-matched calibration was usually utilized to minimize or eliminate the matrix effect which could prevent ME-related analyte quantification errors [49]. The MEs of seven shared key odor compounds were < |20|% and within the tolerable limit of ME in OPH, OPH-M, and OPH-U-M (Table 4). Furthermore, seven shared key odor compounds with high concentrations (OAV ≥ 1) were detected and quantitatively analyzed in combination with a quantitative external standard method. The content of aldehydes with ichthyic, green, and fatty flavors was decreased in both two MRPs, and the content of 2-Nonanone with floral and fruity flavors was increased, which was possibly due to the reduction and dehydrogenation of aldehydes to transform ketones [4]. These results clearly proved that the covalent grafting with XOS through the Maillard reaction can contribute to the improvement of the flavor properties of OPH.

Antioxidant properties

The antioxidant capacities can be reflected by DPPH, ABTS radical scavenging rate, and FRAP-reducing power [50]. As shown in Fig. 9A, it is evident that the DPPH radical scavenging activities were significantly increased after glycosylation (from 35.50% of OPH to 71.53% and 81.55% of OPH-M, and OPH-U-M, respectively). The ABTS radical scavenging activities displayed an identical trend (48.36% of OPH to 76.30% and 90.44% of OPH-M, and OPH-U-M, respectively). In Fig. 9B, the FRAP values of the MRPs were significantly higher than those of OPH, suggesting that the MRPs provide more hydrogen atoms to disrupt free radical chains and expedite the conversion of Fe3+ to Fe2+. The antioxidant capacity of OPH-U-M was greater than OPH-M, it can be inferred from ultrasonication stretched the structure of OPH and exposed more aromatic amino acids, which can be directly involved in the electron transfer, thus increasing the antioxidant activity. Previous studies suggested that fluorescent compounds of MPRs could provide hydrogen atoms, which had potential antioxidant activity [33], [47]. This is consistent with the analysis of λmax of endogenous fluorescence spectra and the fluorescence intensity of fluorescent compounds results described above. Fu et al discovered that ultrasonication could enhance the grafting of glucose with the Chinese giant salamander enzymatic hydrolysate, generating more fluorescent compounds and alternating the conformations around the Trp and Tyr residues, consequently enhancing the antioxidant activity of the conjugate [16].

Correlation analysis

To further illustrate the effect of the Maillard reaction on the structural, and functional properties of OPH, the structure characterization indexes (such as DG, particle size, Zeta-potential, H0, peptide molecular weight) and physicochemical indexes (such as solubility, thermal denaturation temperature, and antioxidant capacities) were selected to construct a correlation analysis. As shown in Fig. 10, the correlation index value of DG with solubility, thermal denaturation temperature, and antioxidant capacity was all close to 1.00, indicating a significant positive correlation between them. However, it was negatively correlated with particle size, potential, H0, and >3 kDa peptides. It was thus further confirmed that the large MW peptides of OPH were degraded to small MW peptides after the Maillard reaction, making OPH loosely stretched. In addition, the introduction of XOS with a large number of hydrophilic and negatively charged hydroxyl groups enhanced the affinity for water molecules and steric hindrance, which contributed to the improvement of solubility, resulting in the stabilization of the solution system.

The closer the correlation coefficient is to 1.00, the more significant the positive correlation between the two is, and vice versa).

Conclusion

In this study, the effects of XOS through ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction on the structure, functional properties, and flavor characteristics of OPH were investigated. The results demonstrated that the ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction could increase the protein solubility, storage stability, the antioxidant properties of OPH. Otherwise, the flavor characteristics of OPH were also improved. Ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction significantly enhanced the solubility (from 54.67% of OPH to 70.14% of OPH-U-M sample), and improved the storage stability (protein sedimentation rate decreased from 40.41% of OPH to 20.49% of OPH-U-M). In addition, the antioxidant properties of OPH were significantly enhanced after ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction (DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging abilities increased from 35.50% of OPH to 81.55% of OPH-U-M, and from 48.36% of OPH to 90.44% of OPH-U-M, respectively). Furthermore, Ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction positively influenced the flavor attributes of OPH, with OPH-U-M exhibiting reduced levels of unsaturated ketoaldehydes with fishy and fatty while displaying increased presence of compounds with floral, fruity, and roasted meat aromas. These phenomena might be attributed to the influence that ultrasonic preprocessing can unfold the OPH structure and improve the degree of glycosylation grafting of XOS and OPH, and the covalently binding between XOS and OPH could modification the conformational flexibility, surface charge, balance of hydrophilic/hydrophobic amino groups, which effectively improved the solubility, antioxidant capabilities, and the flavor characterization of OPH. The present study indicated that ultrasound-assisted Maillard reaction as a novel processing method could provide a theoretical reference and guidance for improving the functional values of oyster protein in the marine food industry.
