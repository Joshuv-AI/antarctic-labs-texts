Transatrial Balloon-Expandable Valve Implantation for Severe Mitral Annular Calcification With Native Leaflet Sealing
Authors: Omar Saleh, Rishab Agarwal, Israa Saleh, Nicholas Valle, Joshua Dolsen, Raymond L Benza, Deepak R Talreja, Clinton D Kemp, Matthew R Summers, Christopher M Sciortino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere mitral annular calcification (MAC) limits mitral intervention options. Transseptal valve-in-MAC (ViMAC) transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) risks neo–left ventricular outflow tract (neo-LVOT) obstruction and paravalvular leak, while surgical replacement requires extensive annular reconstruction.Case SummaryWe report 3 high-risk patients with severe MAC (computed tomography [CT]–based MAC scores 7-10) who underwent open transatrial valve-in-MAC TMVR via a balloon-expandable valve after CT-based simulation and multidisciplinary heart review identified increased risk of valve malapposition or neo-LVOT obstruction precluding transseptal approach. Native anterior mitral leaflet tissue was preserved, everted, and fixed to the bioprosthetic valve cuff, creating a seal to address frame-annulus gaps.DiscussionAll patients achieved stable prosthesis apposition with no paravalvular leak, stable LVOT gradients, and significantly improved functional status at their 3-month follow-up. Two underwent concomitant aortic root replacement; one underwent concomitant septal myectomy and tricuspid repair.Take-Home MessagesCT-based simulation facilitates identification of patients who face increased risk of complications with transseptal TMVR. This series demonstrates reproducibility of native anterior mitral leaflet fixation to address frame-annulus gaps in transcatheter mitral replacement.Key Words: hybrid procedure, mitral annular calcification, structural heart disease, transcatheter mitral valve replacement, valve-in-MAC

Severe mitral annular calcification (MAC) complicates both surgical and transcatheter mitral intervention. MAC is an inflammatory and degenerative process of the mitral annulus that is often exacerbated by renal dysfunction. Transseptal valve-in-MAC (ViMAC) transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) carries risk of neo-left ventricular outflow tract (neo-LVOT) obstruction and paravalvular leak (PVL), while surgical replacement often requires extensive decalcification with longer procedural times, atrioventricular disruption risk, and may result in systemic inflammation secondary to the release of calcium-associated cytokines.1, 2, 3 Approaches that preserve MAC integrity may therefore offer a meaningful reduction in procedural risk.

In severe mitral annular calcification, leaflet-skirt fixation provides a simple, tissue-based method to simultaneously address both neo-LVOT obstruction and paravalvular leak risk while reducing surgical complexity and cardiopulmonary bypass time compared with alternative methods.

We present the first case series describing our center’s open transatrial mitral valve replacement technique that preserves the anterior mitral leaflet (AML) and fixes it to the prosthesis cuff, creating a seal that addresses frame-annulus gaps and displaces native mitral tissue away from the LVOT, simultaneously mitigating both neo-LVOT obstruction and PVL secondary to malapposition. This series extends our previously reported surgical technique4 to a 3-patient cohort, detailing patient selection, procedural execution, and 3-month outcomes across distinct MAC distributions and concomitant cardiac pathology.

A balloon-expandable valve was selected over a self-expandable valve for several reasons. First, our technique requires reverse-orientation deployment; self-expanding valves have an asymmetric frame and cannot be reverse-mounted on their delivery system, precluding this configuration. Second, the SAPIEN platform (Edwards Lifesciences) offers a shorter frame height, reducing ventricular protrusion into the neo-LVOT. Third, balloon-expandable valves generate higher radial force advantageous for sealing against rigid MAC, and their single-step deployment facilitates precise control under direct visualization. Computed tomography (CT)–based simulation informed patient selection by identifying features that complicate percutaneous approaches (Table 1).

Patient 1

A 74-year-old woman presented with acute on chronic NYHA functional class III-IV heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was notable for severe aortic stenosis (mean gradient 55 mm Hg), severe mitral stenosis with circumferential MAC (peak gradient 16 mm Hg, mean gradient 8 mm Hg, and mitral valve area 2.0 cm2), and reduced ejection fraction (35%). CT confirmed heavy anterior and posterior MAC; CT-based MAC score was 10/10 (severe).

DASI predicted elevated coronary obstruction risk for transcatheter aortic valve replacement and incomplete prosthesis apposition for transseptal TMVR due to rigid, irregular calcification. The heart team deemed both percutaneous and conventional surgical approaches high risk.

After median sternotomy and initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass, the calcified mitral valve was exposed via left atriotomy. Primary chordae were resected to mobilize the anterior leaflet, which was divided at A2 and everted into the atrium with pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures. A 29-mm SAPIEN 3 Ultra was crimped onto the Certitude delivery system over a Safari wire and deployed in reverse orientation at approximately 80% atrial depth, flowerpotting the prosthesis. Target expansion was set to a CT-derived cross-sectional area, with pressure-expansion relationships modeled preoperatively using DASI. The everted anterior leaflet was fashioned as a skirt and circumferentially sutured to the valve’s atrial cuff. Because of her increased coronary obstruction risk with transcatheter aortic valve replacement, the patient underwent concomitant aortic root replacement.

Postprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed normal prosthetic function, absent PVL, and an LVOT gradient of 3 mm Hg (Figure 1). At 3-month follow-up, her echocardiogram was notable for left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) increasing to 59%, and the mitral valve mean gradient was 5 mm Hg (peak 10 mm Hg), with LVOT gradients remaining normal (3.7 mm Hg and 2.5 mm Hg for peak and mean, respectively), without evidence of PVL. The patient reported significantly improved functional symptoms (NYHA functional class I-II), without evidence of systemic inflammatory response observed perioperatively or during subsequent postoperative follow-up at 30 days and 3 months (Table 2).

Patient 2

An 80-year-old woman presented with progressive dyspnea. TTE revealed moderate-to-severe aortic stenosis (aortic valve area 0.96 cm2, mean gradient 18 mm Hg), moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation and stenosis (peak gradient 13 mm Hg, mean gradient 6 mm Hg), and a severely enlarged left atrium. CT showed circumferential, caseous MAC (annulus perimeter 141 mm, area 13.7 cm2) (Figure 2) with a CT-based MAC score of 10/10 (Table 1).

The heart team concluded that solely percutaneous intervention carried prohibitive risk, and conventional replacement would require aggressive decalcification (Figure 3).

After median sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass, a 50-mm AtriClip was placed on the left atrial appendage. The valve showed circumferential heavy MAC with immobilized leaflets. A 29-mm SAPIEN 3 was crimped onto the Certitude delivery system over a Safari wire, advanced through the atriotomy, and deployed in reverse orientation at 90% atrial depth. Despite deployment at nominal pressure, commissural gaps persisted due to annular rigidity; the valve was postdilated with an additional 5 cc.

The native AML was then fashioned as a sealing skirt and sutured to the valve's atrial cuff, with subsequent reinforcement with pledgeted sutures at the commissural gaps to address residual defects (Figure 4). A 19-mm Epic valve within a 24-mm Valsalva graft replaced the aortic root. The patient required temporary intra-aortic balloon pump support.

Intraoperative TEE confirmed well-seated prostheses with trace PVL and unobstructed LVOT (Figure 5). At 3-month follow-up, the LVEF was 60%, the mitral mean gradient was 3 mm Hg (peak 5 mm Hg), LVOT gradients remained normal (peak/mean 3/2 mm Hg), and there was no PVL. No evidence of a systemic inflammatory response was observed postoperatively nor on subsequent 30-day and 3-month follow-up visits (Table 2). The patient also reported significantly improved functional status.

Patient 3

A 74-year-old woman with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy presented with NYHA functional class II-III heart failure symptoms and progressive dyspnea on exertion with bilateral pleural effusions. TTE demonstrated an LVEF of 40%, severe mitral regurgitation, and moderate mitral stenosis in the setting of MAC with a resting LVOT peak gradient of 6 mm Hg. TEE confirmed severe MAC with restrictive leaflet motion, severe (4+) mitral regurgitation, and a mitral valve area of 1.6 cm2, along with moderate tricuspid regurgitation due to annular dilation (Video 1). The CT-based MAC score was 7/10 (severe), with predominantly posterior calcification.

Cardiac magnetic resonance demonstrated asymmetric basal septal hypertrophy with turbulent flow through the LVOT. Left heart catheterization revealed no obstructive coronary artery disease and low left ventricular filling pressures. Given the presence of severe mixed mitral valve disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy requiring septal reduction, and concomitant tricuspid regurgitation, the multidisciplinary heart team determined that a combined open surgical approach would allow comprehensive management while mitigating the risks of neo-LVOT obstruction (Figure 6) and PVL associated with transseptal ViMAC TMVR.

After median sternotomy and initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass, a 45-mm AtriClip Mini was placed on the left atrial appendage. Transaortic septal myectomy was performed via aortotomy, resulting in a patent LVOT. Through a left atriotomy via Sondergaard's groove, the mitral valve was exposed, revealing severe posterior annular calcification with immobile leaflets. The AML chordae were resected to mobilize the leaflet, which was preserved and subsequently used as the autologous sealing skirt (Figure 4).

A 29-mm SAPIEN 3 was crimped onto the Certitude delivery system over a Safari wire and deployed in reverse orientation at 90% atrial depth with nominal +3 cc expansion under direct visualization. The preserved AML was everted and secured to the prosthesis cuff to both displace tissue away from the neo-LVOT and buttress areas of predicted frame-annulus malapposition. Tricuspid valve repair was then performed using a 32-mm MC3 annuloplasty ring.

Postoperative TEE demonstrated a well-seated mitral prosthesis with trace PVL and favorable transvalvular gradients (peak/mean 7/3 mm Hg) (Video 2). There was no residual neo-LVOT obstruction after myectomy, and the LVEF recovered to 60%. At 3-month follow-up, the LVEF was 61%, the mitral mean gradient was 4 mm Hg (peak 9 mm Hg), LVOT gradients remained normal (peak/mean 3/2 mm Hg), and there was no PVL. Further, the patient reported significant symptomatic improvement (NYHA functional class I-II) (Table 2).

Discussion

This case series demonstrates the feasibility of a novel, modified open transatrial approach to ViMAC TMVR in high-risk patients who are candidates for neither conventional surgical mitral valve replacement nor transseptal TMVR. Early ViMAC TMVR experience reported 30-day mortality approaching 40% with transseptal approaches, driven largely by neo-LVOT obstruction and PVL. The advantages of this approach are 4-fold: 1) reducing the extent of calcium debridement required in severe MAC;3 2) facilitating controlled valve positioning; 3) enabling concomitant surgical procedures;5,6 and 4) using native patient tissue to address both frame-annulus malapposition and neo-LVOT obstruction risk. All patients survived beyond 3-month follow-up with none-to-trace PVL, preserved LVOT patency, and significant functional improvement.

Advanced computational simulation played a central role in procedural planning. DASI informed patient selection by predicting postdeployment anatomical relationships, including coronary obstruction risk, neo-LVOT geometry, valve apposition risk, and pressure-expansion relationships to guide balloon-sizing strategy.7 These predictions allowed our multidisciplinary heart team to conduct a more personalized patient risk assessment that directly informed the use of this hybrid surgical strategy. Current simulation platforms model pre- and postdeployment valve geometry but do not yet simulate the effect of AML manipulation on frame sealing or LVOT geometry; incorporation of such modeling would further strengthen procedural planning in future cases.

The principal technical modification in this series is the preservation, eversion, and fixation of the native AML to the prosthetic valve cuff rather than resection. Standard surgical techniques excise the AML to mitigate LVOT obstruction;8 however, neo-LVOT obstruction remains a recognized complication after both surgical mitral valve replacement and ViMAC TMVR.9 Kawano et al,10 for example, reported LVOT obstruction in 18% of 11 high-risk transatrial ViMAC patients treated with leaflet resection and felt reinforcement.10 By leveraging the patient's native tissue without the use of additional foreign material, our technique yielded none-to-trace PVL in each patient alongside no occurrence of neo-LVOT obstruction, with LVOT peak gradients remaining below 4.0 mm Hg across all patients at 3-month follow-up.

Importantly, this approach preserves the integrity of the calcified annulus, which is particularly relevant in caseous MAC, where mechanical disruption carries the risk of embolization of calcified contents and exposure of underlying inflammatory tissue. Within our center's experience, no patient undergoing transatrial TMVR has yet demonstrated signs of significant systemic inflammatory response postoperatively. This is notable given that systemic inflammatory response secondary to cytokine release has been reported as a complication of surgical MAC debridement. In reducing total necessary calcium debridement, our technique may attenuate the risk of subsequent inflammation. A transient rise in peripheral white blood cell count was observed in all patients, consistent with the expected nonspecific leukocytosis after cardiac surgery and cardiopulmonary bypass. Because this response is not specific to MAC-related inflammation, biomarkers such as C-reactive protein may better characterize any MAC-attributable inflammation. Prospective monitoring with specific inflammatory markers is warranted.

Avoiding extensive decalcification additionally shortens the patient duration on cardiopulmonary bypass, permitting concurrent management of polyvalvular pathology. In this series, 2 patients underwent aortic root replacement, and 1 underwent septal myectomy with tricuspid valve repair. This versatility is particularly relevant for patients who require concomitant cardiac procedures that cannot be performed percutaneously, and for whom redo sternotomy would carry substantial additional risk.

This report is limited by its single-center design, small sample size, and short follow-up. The durability of leaflet-to-cuff fixation and its impact on late PVL, risk of explant or future revisions, and bioprosthetic valve function require longer surveillance in larger cohorts.

Conclusions

In selected patients with severe MAC who are high-risk surgical candidates unsuitable for transseptal ViMAC TMVR, open transatrial ViMAC TMVR with an autologous leaflet sealing skirt is feasible and was associated with minimal PVL and no clinically evident neo-LVOT obstruction at 3-month follow-up. This approach saves cardiopulmonary bypass time by avoiding extensive annular decalcification and enables surgical management of coexisting pathology in a single sternotomy.
