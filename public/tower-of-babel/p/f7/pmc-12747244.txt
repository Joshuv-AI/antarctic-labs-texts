Whole genome sequencing dataset of Kosakonia sp. PSU31 isolated from clinical patient in Southern Thailand
Authors: Manaschanan Saivaew, Thunchanok Yaikhan, Arnon Chukamnerd, Sarunyou Chusri, Rattanaruji Pomwised, Monwadee Wonglapsuwan, Komwit Surachat, Kamonnut Singkhamanan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset presents a comprehensive genomic analysis of Kosakonia sp. PSU31, isolated from the nasopharynx of a patient in Satun Hospital, Southern Thailand. The data collection involved the extraction of bacterial DNA from a nasopharyngeal swab sample, followed by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) performed using MGISEQ technology. The genome of Kosakonia sp. PSU31 was annotated to identify key genomic features, including mobile genetic elements (MGEs), antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), virulence factors, bacteriocin production genes, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis clusters. The dataset includes detailed genomic sequence, gene annotations, and associated metadata, which are available for further analysis and research. This dataset offers insights into the genetic composition of Kosakonia sp., a bacterium of clinical interest, highlighting its potential roles in antimicrobial resistance and pathogenicity. Researchers can leverage this data to advance bacterial pathogenesis, antimicrobial resistance, and the characterization of secondary metabolites. Overall, this dataset contributes valuable information on microbial dynamics and resistance profiles, ultimately supporting efforts to improve diagnostics, antimicrobial stewardship, and infection control in hospital environments.Keywords: Whole genome analysis, Bacteriocins, Virulence factors, Antimicrobial resistance

Background

The Kosakonia sp. PSU31 was isolated from the nasopharynx of a 89-year-old female patient hospitalized in the intensive care unit (ICU) at Satun hospital, Thailand. Initially, the isolate was identified as Salmonella spp., but due to the limitation of the standard of detection, WGS and comparison with NCBI databases with genomics data confirmed its identity as Kosakonia sp., a bacterium considered rare in clinical settings. Existing literature supports the pathogenic potential of Kosakonia sp., with documented cases including bloodstream infections in Austria [1], France [2], and cases of intra-abdominal infections [3]. These findings underscore the emerging clinical significance of Kosakonia sp., particularly in immunocompromised patients, due to their potential for multidrug resistance and involvement in serious infections, including bloodstream and intra-abdominal infections, as described previously. Notably, the antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed resistance in various antibiotic groups, including third-generation (ceftriaxone), fluoroquinolone (Levofloxacin), Aminoglycoside (Amikacin), and Carbapenem (Ertapenem), indicating potential multidrug resistance, like previous isolates such as Kosakonia sp. PSU27 from Thailand [4].

The dataset was compiled to investigate the genomic characteristics of Kosakonia sp. PSU31, isolated from a clinical source in Southern Thailand. The study aimed to identify virulence factors and secondary metabolite gene clusters, such as bacteriocins, using bioinformatics tools. This work was motivated by the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria and the need to better understand the genetic mechanisms underlying bacterial pathogenicity and resistance. Advanced sequencing technologies and annotation pipelines allowed the identification of genes with potential applications in antimicrobial research. The dataset enriches the understanding of Kosakonia sp., which are emerging pathogens with diverse ecological and clinical significance.

Data Description

Genome information

The dataset comprises the WGS data of Kosakonia sp. PSU31, a clinical isolate. The genome was assembled into 85 contigs, with a total length of 4927,448 base pairs. The assembly exhibits 99.9 % completeness, 0.2 % contamination, and a GC content of 54 %. The annotated genome includes 4643 coding sequences (CDS), 4 rRNA genes, 87 tRNA genes, and 1 tmRNA gene, reflecting its potential for diverse metabolic and cellular processes (Table 1).

Secondary metabolite production and function

The genome of Kosakonia sp. PSU31, isolated from a hospital environment, was analyzed using antiSMASH 7.0.0 to identify secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs). This analysis revealed five distinct BGCs encoding secondary metabolites with potential bioactive properties (Fig. 1). These include a polyketide synthase (PKS) cluster responsible for arylpolyene production, two non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) clusters, and two ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptide (RiPP) clusters, including one associated with thiopeptide biosynthesis.

Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) identified in the genome of Kosakonia sp. PSU31 using antiSMASH. The regions represent five distinct BGCs: Region 1 encodes a polyketide synthase (PKS) cluster for arylpolyene production; Region 2 contains a ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptide (RiPP) cluster for thiopeptide synthesis; Region 3 and Region 4 represent non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) clusters, including one producing NRPS-metalophore hybrids; and Region 5 contains a RiPP-like cluster. Core biosynthetic genes, additional biosynthetic genes, transport-related genes, regulatory genes, resistance genes, and other related features are annotated.

As bacteriocins are a class of secondary metabolites, we identified bacteriocin-associated gene clusters in Kosakonia sp. PSU31 using BAGEL4 version 1.2. The analysis revealed two gene clusters encoding potential bacteriocin products (Fig. 2).

Genomic organization of bacteriocin biosynthetic gene clusters identified in Kosakonia sp. PSU31. Genes with no function determined are shown in white. These clusters indicate the strain's potential for producing antimicrobial peptides with roles in microbial competition and survival in hospital environments.

Prophage analysis and functional characterization

The analysis conducted with Phigaro 2.4.0 identified a potential prophage region within the Kosakonia sp. PSU31 genome, spanning coordinates 1435,765 to 1472,784. Taxonomic classification categorizes this region as belonging to the Siphoviridae family. This genomic segment encompasses genes that encode key phage-associated functions, including terminase, replication, tail, assembly, and portal, along with other structural or accessory components (Fig. 4).

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Bacterial isolation and identification

The bacterial strain used in this study, Kosakonia sp. PSU31, was isolated in 2019 from the nasopharynx of an 89-year-old female patient admitted to the ICU of a hospital in Southern Thailand. The patient had been hospitalized for over six months and exhibited a carbapenem-resistant infection. The isolation process involved culturing clinical samples on MacConkey agar supplemented with 2 µg/mL imipenem to select for carbapenem-resistant bacteria. Initial species identification was performed using biochemical tests in accordance with Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology. Further confirmation of the bacterial species was achieved using a Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI-TOF) Mass Spectrometer (MS). DNA extraction and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and quality were assessed using a NanoDropTM 2000/2000c spectrophotometer, while the integrity of the DNA was verified through 1.5 % w/v agarose gel electrophoresis. The extracted DNA was subjected to WGS using the MGISEQ platform at the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI) with an external provider, where the specific library preparation kit is not available. Sequencing was performed with 150 bp paired-end reads to ensure high-resolution genomic analysis.

Bioinformatics and genomics analysis

The raw sequencing data were analyzed using multiple bioinformatics tools with default parameters. The raw FASTQ reads of Kosakonia sp. PSU31 was processed using the Bacseq (v1.0) pipeline [5], which included quality assessment with FastQC (0.12.0) and MultiQC (1.0.1), followed by read trimming with Fastp (0.23.4). Genome assembly was performed using SPAdes (3.15.5), and assembly quality was evaluated with QUAST (5.3.0+galaxy0). Genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO (5.8.0+galaxy1), and annotation was carried out using Prokka (v1.14.5).

To determine bacterial identity and confirm species-level similarity, the assembled genome was compared against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLAST (v1.3.1). Genome mapping and visualization were conducted using Proksee (v1.3.0). Contamination and completeness metrics were further evaluated using CheckM2 (v1.0.2+galaxy1) on the Galaxy platform.

Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) were identified using the CARD (v6.0.3) and ResFinder (v4.7.2) databases. Secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were predicted using BAGEL4 (v1.2) and antiSMASH (v7.0.0). Virulence-associated genes were identified using the Virulence Factors of Pathogenic Bacteria Database (VFDB, v2.0). Mobile genetic elements (MGEs), including plasmids, transposons, and integrons, were annotated using ISFinder and PlasmidFinder (v2.1.6). Prophage regions were detected using Phigaro (v2.4.0) to identify potential phage elements within the genome.

Limitations

The primary limitations of this work include the dataset size, as it focuses on a single isolate, Kosakonia sp. PSU31 may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other strains or species within the Kosakonia genus. Additionally, the identification of bacteriocins and virulence factors relies on BAGEL4 and VFDB databases, which may not comprehensively capture all relevant genes or pathways due to potential database incompleteness or annotation errors. Lastly, the dataset is based solely on in silico predictions, without experimental validation such as functional assays or gene expression studies to confirm the identified genes or their predicted roles. These limitations emphasize the need for further studies involving additional isolates and experimental approaches to validate and expand these findings.
