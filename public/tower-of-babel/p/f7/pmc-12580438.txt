Early postpancreatectomy hemorrhage: is an update of the ISGPS definition required?
Authors: Gao Yong, Wang Yazhou, Dai Yuran, Zhang Kai, Yan Han, Lu Zipeng, Chen Jianmin, Guo Feng, Xi Chunhua, Gao Wentao, Wu Junli, Jiang Kuirong, Miao Yi, Wei Jishu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostpancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH) is a severe complication in pancreatic surgery. This study focused on early PPH (E-PPH), aiming to identify its characteristics, evaluate the existing grading criteria by the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS), and explore effective treatment strategies.MethodsPatients undergoing pancreatic surgery between March 2020 and January 2024 in two institutions were screened from prospectively maintained databases. Patients with E-PPH were divided into intervention group and the conservative group. The sites of hemorrhage were determined and categorized. Clinical presentation and outcomes were compared among different grades and interventions.ResultsAmong 4062 patients who underwent pancreatic surgery, 113 cases of E-PPH were identified, with an incidence of 2.8%. E-PPH was more concentrated within 24 h (76.2%) and occurred more extraluminally (78.2%). The intervention group had a higher proportion of hemodynamic instability (40.9%) and ICU stays (54.5%). E-PPH in the mesenteric region was more common in pancreaticoduodenectomy (81.9%) and open surgery (90.9%). Branches of the common hepatic artery and superior mesenteric vessel were the majority responsible vessels. Appropriate E-PPH treatment was effective, with successful hemostasis in all intervention cases. The occurrence of ICU admission, the length of ICU and postoperative hospital stay and 90-day mortality were not significantly different between different grades with intervention.ConclusionAppropriate therapy for E-PPH could lead to a favorable prognosis. The current definitions and grades for PPH are inadequate and require further modification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03896-1.Keywords: Pancreatic surgery, Postpancreatectomy hemorrhage, Early hemorrhage, ISGPS grade reevaluation, Prognosis

Introduction

Postpancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH) might not be the most common complication of pancreatic surgery, with an incidence ranging from approximately 1% to 8% [1–3]. However, it can be the most serious and fatal and accounts for 11% to 38% of overall mortality. The International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) has established definitions and grades for PPH based on onset, location, and severity [3]. Numerous studies have focused on late PPH owing to its complex etiology and association with other comorbidities such as fistula and infection [4, 5]. Consequently, the clinical manifestation of early PPH have not received adequate attention. Therefore, it is imperative to explore the features of early PPH to reduce the incidence in this cohort. Notably, some PPH occurring more than 24 h exhibits a comparable prognosis with PPH occurring within 24 h [1, 6, 7]. This raises the question of whether the factors and cut-offs used to determine the grading of PPH need to be reevaluated.

In this study, we retrieved data on patients with PPH from prospectively maintained databases at two institutions, mainly focused on identifying the site, management, and prognosis of PPH in early period, and evaluated the criteria for grading hemorrhage.

Methods

Patient population and data collection

All patients undergoing surgical procedures between March 2020 and January 2024 were screened from the pancreas centers of the First Affiliated Hospital and Benq Hospital with Nanjing Medical University. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pancreaticoduodenectomy or pylorus preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy, distal pancreatectomy, total pancreatectomy, middle segment pancreatectomy, enucleation and chronic pancreatitis surgery. (2) With reference to the ISGPS definition criteria, postoperative complications included PPH. (3) PPH occurring within 48 h of the conclusion of the primary procedure was considered as PPH in early period (E-PPH) in this study. All patients provided written informed consent at the time of admission. Demographic data, intraoperative parameters and postoperative variables were extracted from our prospectively recorded electronic database. Postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) was assessed and graded based on the criteria established by ISGPS. Hemodynamic instability was defined as non-invasive upper limb blood pressure lower than 90/60 mmHg, heart rate above 100 bpm, or the need to use vasoactive drugs to maintain blood pressure above 90/60 mmHg or heart rate below 100 bpm. The site of hemorrhage was identified from the surgical records or interventional notes or endoscopic reports. The hemorrhage sites were categorized as mesenteric region including omentum or transverse mesocolon, artery-related region including branches of the celiac trunk and mesenteric vessels, and pancreas region including pancreatojejunostomy anastomosis or the stump of the pancreas (Supplementary Figure). The study was complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University.

Pancreatectomy procedure

The protocols for the surgical techniques have already been mentioned [8]. Briefly, the surgical process contained the following distinct phases: the resection preparation phase including Kocher maneuver, amputation of the gastrocolic ligament, and mobilization of the colon and mesocolon, the specimen resection phase including dissection of organs or blood vessels and digestive tract reconstruction phase. During the surgery, harmonic and bovie were used for detachment of mesenteric membrane and omentum. Non-absorbable suture was employed to ligate right gastric artery, right gastroepiploic artery, inferior pancreatic artery, dorsal pancreatic artery and corresponding veins or finer vessels. The major arteries, including gastroduodenal artery, inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery, first jejunal artery or splenic artery, were managed through ligation with non-absorbable plus polypropylene suture. The artery at transection of remnant pancreas was sutured by 5 − 0 polypropylene suture. No bleeding was observed prior to closing the abdominal cavity.

PPH treatment

The treatment of E-PPH was determined by location of hemorrhage and severity. Briefly, vital signs and hemoglobin levels were assessed upon detection of E-PPH. Intraluminal E-PPH was managed with proton pump inhibitors and 150 ml of topical 8% norepinephrine diluted with iced saline each time via nasogastric tube followed by 30 min of valve closure and repeat every 4 to 6 h as needed, and endoscopic treatment was conducted if the aforementioned medication failed. Whereas extraluminal E-PPH experienced relaparotomy or digital subtraction angiography (DSA) in the presence of hemodynamic instability, a significant decline in hemoglobin levels within a brief timeframe, or persistent bleeding despite pharmacological and transfusion therapy. Relaparotomy or DSA decision was determined by the chief surgeon or senior doctor on duty (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 on a personal computer. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages whereas continuous variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges. The non‑parametric test was employed to compare continuous parameters, while the chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare the categorical parameters. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of E-PPH

Between March 2020 and January 2024, a total of 4062 patients experienced pancreatic surgery in two institutions. After applying the inclusion criteria, 113 patients with E-PPH were identified, representing an incidence of 2.8% (Table 1; Fig. 2). Twelve patients were excluded from the further analysis due to delayed hemorrhage after E-PPH during hospitalization. The patients were divided into two groups according to the treatment received (Table 2). The intervention group included 22 patients (21.8%) undergoing DSA, endoscope, or laparotomy. The conservative group included 79 patients (78.2%) treated with intravenous medication without any additional interventions. The conservative group exhibited a greater prevalence of combined venous resection (16.5% vs. 4.5%) and malignant lesions (69.6% vs. 54.5%), but a lower incidence of combined sub-adventitial divestment technique (SDT) (1.3% vs. 13.6%). The two groups exhibited similar characteristics regarding the composition of surgical procedures, operation duration (250 min vs. 231 min) and estimated blood loss (200 ml vs. 200 ml). Hemorrhage characteristics and clinical presentation of E-PPH

Data shown in Table 3 indicated that E-PPH was more concentrated within 24 h (n = 77, 76.2%) and occurred more extraluminally (n = 79, 78.2%), with no significant difference between the intervention group and the conservative group. More patients in the intervention group exhibited hemodynamic instability (40.9% vs. 2.5%) and a higher occurrence of ICU stay (54.5% vs. 30.4%). The length of ICU stays and postoperative hospital stays was longer in the intervention group, though this difference was not statistically significant.

Characteristics of intervention group

Each patient in the intervention group received only a single intervention. Among them, 19 patients underwent relaparotomy, 2 patients underwent DSA with transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) and 1 patient underwent endoscopy for hemostasis (Table 4). The hemorrhage observed in patients with endoscopic hemostasis was caused by mucosal laceration at the cardia probably due to the insertion of nasogastric tube during surgery. In DSA cases, gastroduodenal artery hemorrhage was detected. All hemostasis were successful in intervention group. Unfortunately, one patient died of infection at postoperative day 47. Another patient was dead of cardiac arrest after hemostasis.

Discussion

The incidence of early hemorrhage varies, with most reporting approximately 2%−5% [6, 7, 9–11] and up to 10% [12]. In comparison, the incidence of our E-PPH is lower. This cohort found that nearly 80% E-PPH received conservative treatment and the rest E-PPH required intervention. Early PPH is mainly caused by failure of surgical hemostasis [13] with perioperative antithrombotic treatment emerging as an additional factor [14]. The sites of E-PPH requiring reintervention are listed in detail and should be attached more importance and given adequate hemostasis during operation. The E-PPH sites were categorized as mesenteric region, artery-related region, and pancreas region in this study, and the findings revealed half of the E-PPH originated from the mesenteric region, with a high proportion in open surgery. This may be attributed to the laparoscopic procedure dissecting from the middle of the gastrocolic ligament, which facilitates the identification and ligation of blood vessels, as well as the fact that the entire dissections are performed with harmonic. It is evident that E-PPH in mesenteric region might be entirely avoided with more meticulous intraoperative hemostasis. In addition, E-PPH might be associated with several risk factors, which need to be further analyzed [12, 15, 16].

The extant definitions and grades for PPH established by ISGPS are inadequate as follows. First, the two objective factors of mild PPH, decrease in hemoglobin concentration < 3 g/d and volume of blood transfusions, might result in a significantly higher proportion of cases meeting the definition [12]. Notably, a reduction in hemoglobin concentration is not the same as hemorrhage. Fluid resuscitation in the early postoperative phase leading to a drop in hematocrit, as well as the heterogeneity of blood transfusion indications in different institutions might be the potential cause [1]. This may result in an overestimation of the PPH grade, potentially leading to a lower observed incidence of grade A PPH, even in the presence of a clearly imbalanced ratio [17]. In some studies, even no grade A hemorrhage was found [6, 9]. In addition, ISGPS definition has limited utility in stratifying early PPH outcomes. Grade A PPH might have no significant clinical impairment, showing no significant differences in mortality, length of hospital stay or readmission rate compared to patients without PPH [7]. For grade B, early severe PPH and mild late PPH are both included in this grade. Although we did not compare the two in this study, prior research demonstrated no homogeneous between these two groups. For instance, the number of transfusions is higher in severe early hemorrhage than in late mild hemorrhage [17]. Bassi’s data showed a notable surge in the necessity for ICU admission of severe PPH due to resuscitation in early hemorrhage. Also, there is notable difference in PPH management between the two groups [7]. Grade C PPH has the potential to result in life-threatening conditions and its prognosis might be the most unfavorable. However, our data showed no significant difference in length of ICU stay, postoperative hospitalization days and 90-day mortality in grade C PPH compared to grade B PPH with reference to ISGPS 2007 definition. This is due to that the time threshold for the onset of hemorrhage is set at 24 h, resulting in the division of patients belonging to a uniform classification into different grades. Our findings revealed that PPH requiring intervention between 24 h and 48 h remained a favorable prognosis, which differed from grade C PPH resulting from POPF or abdominal infection. Accordingly, the use of a 24-hour assessment threshold is unduly inflexible. PPH from failed intraoperative hemostatic could still persist beyond 24 h. Therefore, 24-hour threshold should be extended [1, 6]. To note, time of onset of hemorrhage is just an indirect criterion. The primary determinant of prognosis might be the underlying cause of the hemorrhage and the associated pathophysiologic processes. Inclusion of the etiology of hemorrhage in the grading criteria may facilitate the classification of individuals with a similar prognosis. For example, Bassi et al. recommended to use concomitant POPF instead of time of onset for PPH [7]. Overall, more relevant and decisive factors need to be considered in future revision of the ISGPS definition was proposed.

In the case of E-PPH, different treatments are applied for intraluminal and extraluminal hemorrhage. For intraluminal hemorrhage, most are observed to stabilize following application of a conservative treatment [18]. Endoscopy is the first recommended if intervention is required [11, 15]. Bleeding site of gastrointestinal tract and bilioenteric anastomosis could be detected and treated by endoscopy. Nevertheless, endoscopy might prove inadequate for identifying the source of hemorrhage due to the inherent intracavitary view of the endoscope and the potential for minimal bleeding [9]. It is to be noted that while the clinical presentation is intraluminal, the cause of the hemorrhage might also be extraluminal blood entering the cavity through the anastomosis or pancreatic stump [17]. Therefore, DSA is another preferred treatment option [17]. In this cohort, intraluminal hemorrhage occurred in ten patients and conservative treatment was effective in eight patients. Endoscopy was performed in one patient, revealing mucosal bleeding in the cardia due to intraoperative gastric tube injury. In another case, DSA was conducted, allowing the identification of hemorrhage in a branch of the superior pancreaticoduodenal artery. Hemostasis was successful in these two cases. For extraluminal E-PPH, relaparotomy is prioritized if persistent blood is drained percutaneously or via drainage tube immediately after surgery or clinically significant impairment is detected [3, 19]. Relaparotomy is more effective than DSA due to its risk of failure [6, 9]. In Lou’s cohort, three patients with early PPH underwent DSA and one failed [20]. In another cohort comprising five early PPH, two underwent TAE and received surgical treatment subsequently due to rebleeding [19]. In this study, successful hemostasis was achieved in all surgical and DSA cases for extraluminal E-PPH. With appropriate treatment regimens, the causes of E-PPH could be effectively identified and rectified. Thus, outcomes in patients with E-PPH could be favorable [6, 9, 18, 21].

Conclusion

Appropriate interventional therapy for E-PPH could correct the cause and lead to an overall favorable prognosis. The current definitions and grades for PPH established by ISGPS are inadequate and require further modification.
