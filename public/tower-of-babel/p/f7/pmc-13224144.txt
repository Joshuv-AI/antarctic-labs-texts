Astaxanthin-metformin conjugate nanomicelles-loaded hydrogel reprograms macrophage metabolism via mitochondrion-targeted regulation for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Xin-Nan Teng, Hui Wang, Yue Zhu, Shu-Chang Wang, Run-Long Pan, Chen Du, Run-Fang Song, Shuai-Shuai Zhang, Jun-Rong Li, Song-Bai Zhang, Feng Xu, Qian Zhang, Min Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractsDiabetic wounds struggle to heal because of ROS-mediated immune dysfunction, in which the mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming of macrophages is critical for inflammation resolution and tissue repair. Here, we innovatively combine the antioxidant astaxanthin (Ast) and the metabolic activator metformin (Met) via pH-responsive dynamic Schiff base linkages to form amphiphilic Ast-PEG-Met conjugates, which self-assemble into nanomicelles (NMs). This strategy effectively improves the solubility of Ast and enables synergistic delivery of the two complementary drugs. NMs target macrophage mitochondria, where Ast scavenges mtROS and Met activates energy metabolism pathways in a synergistic manner. The two drugs synergistically activate the AMPK-PPARγ axis and promote mitophagy, thereby restoring mitochondrial function and regulating immunometabolic reprogramming. For on-demand release, the NMs were further encapsulated into a ROS-responsive self-healing hydrogel crosslinked by dynamic phenylboronate ester bonds. This system responds to high levels of ROS and an acidic wound microenvironment with favorable biocompatibility. In a diabetic full-thickness skin defect model, this integrated platform markedly accelerated wound healing, promoted collagen deposition and angiogenesis, and alleviated chronic inflammation. This work provides a promising combined strategy involving antioxidant–metabolic regulation for chronic diabetic wound therapy via a dual-responsive hydrogel drug delivery system.

Introduction

Chronic diabetic wounds severely impair the lives of affected patients [1]. Normal wound healing occurs through an ordered cascade of inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [2]. In diabetic wounds, however, the hyperglycemic microenvironment induces excessive accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), overactivation of the polyol pathway, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which collectively lead to the rapid accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [3,4]. Thus, scavenging excessive ROS is the most direct strategy for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and promoting wound healing [5]. Researchers have therefore developed a variety of antioxidant wound dressings that load redox enzymes, enzyme mimics, or natural antioxidant components to eliminate free radicals and alleviate oxidative damage [6,7]. Among them, natural antioxidants have attracted extensive attention because of their wide availability, excellent biocompatibility, and high safety, making them more suitable for long-term chronic wound repair. Curcumin, quercetin, and other molecules have been widely explored in wound healing research [[8], [9], [10]]. Astaxanthin (Ast) is widely recognized as the most potent natural antioxidant, with an antioxidant capacity far exceeding that of vitamin C and other carotenoids [11]. It can efficiently quench singlet oxygen, scavenge diverse free radicals, and inhibit lipid peroxidation [12]. More importantly, Ast directly protects the mitochondrial membrane, maintains mitochondrial homeostasis, and enhances mitochondrial activity by regulating related genes at the transcriptional level [13].

In addition, excessive ROS not only directly damage local tissues but also act as critical second messengers to abnormally activate inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB and NLRP3, thereby causing immune cell dysfunction [5]. In particular, macrophages are prone to remain in a persistent proinflammatory state, ultimately resulting in severe immune microenvironment disorders [14]. Such ROS-mediated immune dysfunction represents the core mechanism that causes diabetic wounds to become trapped in a chronic inflammatory state. Therefore, restoring immune homeostasis, especially by correcting the proinflammatory polarization shift of macrophages, is crucial for diabetic wound repair [15]. In terms of immunomodulation, Ast promotes the polarization of microglia toward the M2 phenotype and suppresses inflammatory responses, functions with promise for in alleviating immune disorders in diabetic wounds [16,17]. Despite the prominent advantages of Ast, its application in skin wound repair remains severely limited, mainly because of its extremely poor water solubility and chemical stability. Most existing studies formulate Ast as oils, creams, films, and other formulations for direct administration, which fail to achieve long-term retention and precise delivery at the lesion site [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Hydrogels have become a research focus in wound repair dressings because of their excellent moisture retention, tissue adaptability, and biocompatibility, which can effectively compensate for the shortcomings of traditional formulations [22]. However, studies integrating Ast into hydrogel systems remain scarce, and most rely on simple physical blending or solvent loading without effective molecular engineering strategies to achieve stable encapsulation and controlled release [23,24].

Nevertheless, macrophage dysfunction is not solely caused by oxidative damage, and simply scavenging ROS cannot completely reverse the pathological polarization of macrophages in diabetic wounds [25]. Abnormal energy metabolism also plays a key role in this pathological process [26]. Studies have confirmed that the diabetic microenvironment impairs mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in macrophages, shifting cellular energy metabolism from efficient oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis dominance [27]. This metabolic reprogramming further strengthens the proinflammatory M1 phenotype while inhibiting the polarization of macrophages toward the pro-repair M2 phenotype [28]. Therefore, in addition to ROS scavenging, restoring mitochondrial metabolic function and activating cellular energy-sensing pathways represents another critical strategy to correct macrophage dysfunction and rebuild immune homeostasis. As a classic hypoglycemic drug and a well-established agonist of central cellular energy sensors, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and metformin (Met) have the potential to regulate energy metabolic reprogramming, restore mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [29,30]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the topical administration of Met can attenuate inflammation and accelerate skin wound healing by activating AMPK signaling [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. If the metabolic reprogramming of mitochondria within macrophages can be effectively regulated through the delivery of Met, it is expected to increase the likelihood of immune homeostasis reconstruction in diabetic wounds, from the perspective of restoring mitochondrial metabolic function. How to efficiently and simultaneously deliver Ast and Met, two drugs with complementary functions but significantly different solubilities through a single delivery system to achieve synergistic mitochondrial targeting in macrophages and exert combined antioxidant and metabolic regulatory effects remains an urgent challenge.

In this study, an integrated intelligent therapeutic strategy from molecular engineering to material design was developed. At the molecular level, we synthesized amphiphilic Ast-PEG-Met conjugates via a two-step controllable coupling reaction: esterification was first used to improve the hydrophilicity of Ast, followed by the precise conjugation of Ast and Met via a pH-responsive dynamic Schiff base bond. The conjugates can self-assemble into nanomicelles (NMs) in aqueous solution, significantly improving its chemical stability while achieving the codelivery of two functional drugs. At the delivery system level, these NMs were further encapsulated into a ROS-responsive self-healing hydrogel constructed with dynamic phenylboronate ester bonds. This hydrogel exhibits favorable mechanical strength, self-healing performance, and tissue adhesiveness, and can degrade specifically in the high-ROS wound microenvironment to achieve spatiotemporally controlled drug release.

We hypothesize that this delivery system can respond to the diabetic wound microenvironment and codeliver Ast and Met into macrophages, where they function in a synergistic manner. Ast scavenges mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) and improves the cellular redox state, whereas Met activates AMPK signaling to regulate energy metabolism. The coordinated action of the two agents repairs mitochondrial function, promotes the metabolic reprogramming of macrophages, and drives their polarization from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the pro-repair M2 phenotype. Reprogrammed macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory factors and pro-angiogenic factors, thereby remodeling the wound immune microenvironment and disrupting the cycle of chronic inflammation. Materials and methods

Materials

Ast (purity ≥98%), Met hydrochloride (purity ≥97%), 4-carboxybenzeneboronic acid (BA), dopamine hydrochloride (DA, purity ≥98%), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) and streptozotocin (STZ) were purchased from Aladdin (China). Carboxymethyl-PEG1000-benzaldehyde (COOH-PEG-Bz) was obtained from Kaixin Biotech (China). Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS, degree of substitution >80%) was obtained from Macklin (China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Escherichia coli 055:B5) was purchased from Solarbio (China). All cell culture media and related reagents were obtained from Gibco (USA). The mouse fibroblast line L929, the mouse macrophage line RAW 264.7, and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were acquired from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (China). Male Sprague‒Dawley (SD) rats were provided by the Laboratory Animal Center of Air Force Medical University (China). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethical Committee, the Third Affiliated Hospital, Air Force Medical University (Ethics Approval No.: 2025-kq-034).

Synthesis and characterization of Ast-PEG-Met conjugates and NMs

Ast-PEG-Met conjugates were synthesized using a two-step coupling strategy. First, 90 mg of COOH-PEG-Bz and 450 mg of a mixture of EDC and DAMP were dissolved in 18 mL of anhydrous dichloromethane with magnetic stirring. Subsequently, 24 mg of Ast was added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed under light-protected conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. The intermediate (Ast-PEG) was purified via dialysis (MWCO: 1000 Da) against deionized water in the dark for 72 h, followed by lyophilization. In the second step, lyophilized Ast-PEG and an equimolar amount of Met were dissolved in 9 mL of methanol and stirred at room temperature for 12 h to form the dynamic Schiff base. The solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator at 37 °C to yield the Ast-PEG-Met conjugates. To prepare the NM, 10 mg of the conjugate was dispersed in 10 mL of PBS and subjected to ultrasonication for 10 min.

The reaction mixture was preliminarily analyzed by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using a chloroform–methanol (5:1, v/v) mixture as the developing solvent, with visualization under both natural and UV light (254 nm). The chemical structure was characterized by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR; Bruker AVANCE NEO 600, USA) in deuterated solvents (Chloroform-d and Methanol-d4), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Thermo Fisher Nicolet IS50, USA), and mass spectrometry (MS; AB Sciex X500R, USA) in positive ion mode. UV–Vis absorption spectra were recorded using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy LX, USA). The solubility of the Ast-PEG-Met conjugates in water, PBS, DMSO, Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), and serum were assessed. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the Ast-PEG-Met NMs in PBS were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Anton Paar Litesizer 500, Austria). The morphology of the NMs was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Thermo Fisher FEI Tecnai G2, USA) after negative staining with 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid.

Assessment of ROS scavenging and antioxidant activity

The free radical scavenging capacity was evaluated using DPPH and ABTS assays [35]. For the DPPH assay, stock solutions of free Ast and Ast-PEG-Met conjugates (20 μg/mL in DMSO) were mixed with an equal volume of DPPH solution and incubated in the dark for 30 min, after which the absorbance at 517 nm was measured. For the ABTS assay, gradient concentrations of Ast-PEG-Met conjugates (0–25 μg/mL in PBS) were mixed with freshly generated ABTS+• solution, and the absorbance at 734 nm was recorded after 10 min. In both assays, DMSO or PBS served as the blank, and 0.1 mM Trolox was used as the positive control.

Cellular antioxidant and cytoprotective effects were assessed using L929 fibroblasts. To determine the optimal concentration for Ast-PEG-Met NMs, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cotreated with 500 μM H2O2 and different concentrations of NMs (0, 2, 4, 8, and 16 μg/mL) for 24 h. Cell viability was measured using a cell counting kit-8 assay (CCK-8; InCellGenE IC-1519, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration that resulted in the highest cell viability without cytotoxicity (8 μg/mL) was selected for subsequent experiments. For the oxidative stress protection assay, cells were cotreated with 500 μM H2O2 and either free Ast (2.4 μg/mL, 4 μM) or Ast-PEG-Met NMs (8 μg/mL, delivering 4 μM Ast) for 24 h, and cell viability was assessed. Cells treated with fresh medium without H2O2 served as the normal control. For the detection of intracellular ROS, L929 fibroblasts seeded on 6-well plates were cotreated with 200 μM H2O2 and the respective drugs (free Ast or Ast-PEG-Met NMs at an equivalent concentration of 4 μM Ast) for 24 h. The cells were then washed with PBS, stained with DCFH-DA (Beyotime S0033S, China) at 37 °C for 30 min, and immediately imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE Ts2, Japan).

For the biochemical analysis of oxidative stress markers, L929 fibroblasts were seeded in 6-well plates. After overnight attachment, the cells were cotreated with 200 μM H2O2 and the respective drugs (as above) for 24 h. The cells were then washed, lysed with the corresponding assay lysis buffers, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The total protein concentration in the supernatant was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime P0010, China). The levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH) and the activities of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were subsequently measured using a lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (Beyotime S0131S, China), a total glutathione assay kit (Beyotime S0052, China), a catalase assay kit (Beyotime S0051, China), and a total superoxide dismutase assay kit with WST-8 (Beyotime S0101S, China), respectively, following the manufacturers’ protocols. Absorbances for all endpoint measurements were determined.

Analysis of macrophage polarization and cytokine secretion

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded into confocal dishes or 6-well plates. After overnight culture, the cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS for 24 h to induce M1 polarization. The medium was subsequently replaced with fresh medium containing either free Met (0.52 μg/mL, 4 μM) or Ast-PEG-Met NMs (8 μg/mL, delivering 4 μM Met) for an additional 24 h. Cells stimulated with LPS alone served as the control.

For immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed with precooled methanol, blocked, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the macrophage marker CD68 (1:50; Abcam ab53444, UK) together with M1 markers (iNOS, 1:200; Proteintech 22226-1-AP, China; CCR7, 1:100; Proteintech 25898-1-AP, China) or M2 markers (Arg-1, 1:200; Proteintech 16001-1-AP, China; CD206, 1:500; Proteintech 83485-1-RR, China). After being washed, the cells were incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG and FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:200; Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus FV3000, Japan).

For the detection of cytokine secretion, cell culture supernatants were collected after centrifugation. The levels of TNF-α and IL-10 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Bosterbio, EK0527 for TNF-α; EK0417 for IL-10, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

After treatment as described in Section 2.4, total RNA was extracted from the macrophages using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, USA). The RNA quality was assessed, and sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using FeatureCounts (v2.0.6). Differential expression analysis between the Ast-PEG-Met NMs group and the LPS control group was performed with DESeq2 (v1.42.0), with thresholds of |log2(fold change)| ≥1 and adjusted P-value <0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the differentially expressed genes were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.8.1).

Analysis of mitochondrial function

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in confocal dishes or 6-well plates and treated as described in Section 2.4. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and subjected to the following assays according to the manufacturers’ protocols: mitochondrial superoxide was detected using MitoSOX™ Red (Beyotime S0061S, China); mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed with a JC-1 probe (Beyotime C2006, China); mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) opening was monitored via calcein-AM/CoCl2 quenching assay (Beyotime C2009S, China); and intracellular ATP levels were determined using a luciferase-based ATP assay kit (Beyotime, S0026, China). For mitophagy blockade experiments, RAW 264.7 macrophages were pretreated with 5 μM Mdivi-1 (Ambeed A252480, USA) for 2 h, followed by coincubation with Ast-PEG-Met NMs for an additional 24 h. Control groups were treated with an equivalent volume of DMSO.

Western blot analysis

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as described in Section 2.4. Cells were lysed in precooled RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime P0013B, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein concentration was determined. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12% SDS‒PAGE (ACE ET12012Gel, China) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-AMPK (Thr172, 1:2000; Proteintech 80209-6-RR, China), total AMPK (1:5000; Proteintech 10929-2-AP, China), PPARγ (1:2000; Proteintech 16643-1-AP, China), PINK1 (1:2000; Proteintech 23274-1-AP, China), Parkin (1:4000; Proteintech 14060-1-AP, China), LC3 (1:5000; Proteintech 14600-1-AP, China), or GAPDH (1:20000; Proteintech 10494-1-AP, China). After being washed, the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000; Proteintech SA00001-2, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (InCellGenE IC-5009, USA) and imaged with a western-light chemiluminescent detection system (VILBER FUSION FX SPECTRA, France). The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to those of GAPDH.

Fabrication and characterization of the ROS-responsive hydrogel

CMCS grafted with BA was synthesized according to a published method with modifications [36]. Briefly, 0.83 g of BA was dissolved in 60 mL of DMSO. Afterward, 0.96 g of EDC and 0.58 g of NHS were added to activate the carboxyl groups of BA at room temperature for 4 h. Separately, 6 g of CMCS was dissolved in 800 mL of deionized water. The activated BA solution was added dropwise to the CMCS solution under stirring. The reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for 12 h. The product was subsequently purified by dialysis (MWCO: 8000–14000 Da) against deionized water for 7 days and freeze-dried to obtain CMCS-BA. For hydrogel formation, 0.4 g of CMCS-BA was dissolved in 10 mL of PBS (pH 7.4). Afterward, 0.5 mL of a mixed solution containing DA (3 wt%) and Ast-PEG-Met NMs (1 wt%) was added. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and allowed to stand for 6 h to form the Ast-PEG-Met NMs-loaded hydrogel (termed Ast-PEG-Met Gel). A control hydrogel without NMs (termed Gel) was prepared by the same procedure.

The chemical structures of CMCS-BA and the hydrogels were confirmed by 1H NMR in deuterium oxide (D2O) deuterium oxide and FT-IR. The microstructural morphology of the lyophilized hydrogels was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi S-4800, Japan). Rheological properties, including the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″), were measured using a rotational rheometer (Anton Paar MCR102e, Austria) under oscillatory strain sweep (0.1% to 1000%) and alternating step-strain (1% and 500%) tests to evaluate the mechanical strength and self-healing behavior. The swelling ratio of the hydrogels was determined gravimetrically after immersion in PBS at 37 °C until equilibrium. The in vitro drug release profile was studied by incubating the Ast-PEG-Met Gel in PBS at pH 7.4 or 6.5, with or without 1 mM H2O2, at 37 °C under gentle shaking. At predetermined time intervals, the release medium was sampled and replenished. The amounts of Met and Ast released at their characteristic wavelengths were quantified by UV‒Vis spectroscopy.

Assessment of in vitro biocompatibility and bioactivity

For the evaluation of cytotoxicity, sterilized blank hydrogel (Gel) and Ast-PEG-Met Gel were extracted in complete cell culture medium at 37 °C for 24 h. The extracts were subsequently centrifuged to collect the supernatant. L929 fibroblasts, RAW 264.7 macrophages, and HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates. After cell attachment, the culture medium was replaced with undiluted hydrogel extracts. After 72 h of incubation, the cell viability of L929 fibroblasts was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. A live/dead staining assay was performed using a calcein-AM/PI double-stain kit (Beyotime C2015M, China) after 48 h or 72 h of exposure to the extracts. Cells cultured in normal medium were used as the control. Hemocompatibility was evaluated using a hemolysis assay. Briefly, fresh rat blood was collected and centrifuged to isolate red blood cells (RBCs), which were subsequently resuspended at 2% (v/v) in isotonic saline. Gel and Ast-PEG-Met Gel samples were immersed in the RBCs suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm. PBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 served as the negative and positive controls, respectively.

To assess paracrine bioactivity, conditioned medium (CM) was collected from RAW 264.7 macrophages. Briefly, macrophages were treated with hydrogel extracts (from Gel or Ast-PEG-Met Gel) for 24 h. The culture supernatant was then collected, centrifuged to remove debris, and used as CM. CM from untreated macrophages served as the control. For the scratch wound healing assay, L929 fibroblasts were seeded into 24-well plates to form a confluent monolayer. A straight scratch was created using a 200 μL sterile pipette tip. The detached cells were washed away, and the cells were then incubated with a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of serum-free DMEM and the collected CM. Images of the scratches were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h using a microscope, and the wound area was quantified using ImageJ software. For the tube formation assay, 80 μL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) was polymerized in a precooled 48-well plate at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs were seeded onto the Matrigel and cultured in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of serum-free endothelial cell medium and the collected CM. After 6 h of incubation, capillary-like tube structures were photographed under a microscope, and the total number of nodes per field was counted for quantification.

Evaluation of in vivo therapeutic efficacy

Male SD rats (6 weeks old, 180–200 g) were fasted for 9 h and then injected intraperitoneally with STZ (55 mg/kg in citrate buffer, pH 4.5) to induce diabetes. Rats with stable blood glucose concentrations ≥16.7 mmol/L one week after injection were considered diabetic and used for the study. Diabetic rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, after which the dorsal hair was removed. A full-thickness circular wound (10 mm in diameter) was created on the dorsum using a sterile biopsy punch. The rats were randomly divided into three groups: Blank (no hydrogel treatment), Gel and Ast-PEG-Met Gel. The corresponding hydrogel was applied to cover the wound bed immediately after surgery. Digital photographs of the wounds were taken on Days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 post-surgery. The wound area was measured using ImageJ software, and the percentage of wound closure was calculated. Blood glucose levels and body weight were monitored throughout the study.

On Days 7 and 14 after surgery, the rats were euthanized, and the wound tissues with surrounding skin were harvested. For histological analysis, the tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 6 μm thick sections. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Masson's staining to evaluate tissue architecture, inflammation, and collagen deposition. For ROS detection on Day 7, fresh wound tissues were embedded and snap frozen. Cryosections (10 μm thick) were prepared. To detect superoxide anions, the sections were incubated with 5 μM dihydroethidium (DHE; Beyotime S0063, China) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, counterstained with DAPI and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. For immunofluorescence, paraffin-embedded sections from Day 7 were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against F4/80 (1:5000; Proteintech 28463-1-AP, China), CD206 (1:10000; Proteintech 83485-1-RR, China), TNF-α (1:500; Servicebio GB115701), IL-10 (1:500; Servicebio GB11534), CD31 (1:200; Servicebio GB120008), and α-SMA (1:1000; Servicebio GB111364). After being washed, the sections were incubated with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged using a digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary).

Statistical analysis

All the quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The number of biological replicates is indicated by n, and experiments were conducted at least three times unless otherwise specified. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, indicated by the following asterisks: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Ast-PEG-Met conjugates and NMs

To enhance the bioavailability of Ast and achieve synergistic codelivery with Met, Ast-PEG-Met conjugates were designed using COOH-PEG-Bz as the spacer (Fig. 1A). The reaction progress and product formation were systematically confirmed. TLC results of the first-step reaction mixture revealed two new spots with intermediate polarities between those of free Ast and COOH-PEG-Bz, confirming the generation of mono- and di-substituted Ast-PEG products (Fig. 1B) [37]. FT-IR spectroscopy provided direct evidence of chemical bond formation. The carboxyl C=O stretch vibration of the COOH-PEG-Bz at ∼1700 cm−1 disappeared after esterification, and a new ester C=O stretching peak emerged at 1759 cm−1 in the spectrum of the Ast-PEG intermediate (Fig. 1C) [38]. Subsequent conjugation with Met was confirmed by the attenuation of the aldehyde peak (1659 cm−1) and the appearance of a characteristic imine (C=N) stretching signal at 1637 cm−1 in the Ast-PEG-Met spectrum (Fig. 1D) [39]. Further structural confirmation was obtained by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 1E). The intermediate Ast-PEG spectrum retained the characteristic proton signals of each moiety: the olefinic proton signals of Ast at δ 6.36–6.97 ppm, the methylene proton signal of PEG at δ 3.64 ppm, as well as the aromatic protons (δ 7.57–8.08 ppm) and aldehyde protons (δ 10.07 ppm) from the benzaldehyde group. A distinct chemical shift of the carboxyl-adjacent methylene protons from δ 4.14 ppm in COOH-PEG-Bz to δ 4.55 ppm confirmed successful esterification (Fig. S1, Tables S1–3) [40]. The two-step covalent reactions were further confirmed by MS. As shown in Fig. S2, all three spectra showed the characteristic normal distribution of polydisperse PEG, with adjacent peaks spaced by 44 Da [40]. The measured molecular weight of COOH-PEG-Bz was 1277.7250 Da ([M+2H]2+ at m/z 639.8625), corresponding to an average degree of polymerization of approximately 23 (Fig. S2A). After stepwise conjugation with Ast and Met, the measured molecular weights of the intermediate Ast-PEG and final Ast-PEG-Met conjugates were 1859.0966 Da ([M+2H]2+ at m/z 930.5483) and 1967.1332 Da ([M+2H]2+ at m/z 984.5666), respectively (Fig. S2B and C). These data confirmed the successful stepwise synthesis of the Ast-PEG-Met conjugates, which was consistent with the 1H NMR and FT-IR results. UV–Vis spectroscopy revealed distinct changes in characteristic absorption. The Ast-PEG intermediate displayed the typical absorption of Ast at 490 nm and a benzaldehyde-related peak at ∼320 nm. In contrast, the final Ast-PEG-Met conjugates exhibited a broadened absorption band ranging from 330 to 380 nm, which was attributed to the newly formed imine bond (Fig. 1F).

Compared with that of the hydrophobic free Ast, the aqueous solubility of the Ast-PEG-Met conjugates was dramatically improved, as demonstrated in various physiological media (Fig. 1G). In aqueous solution, the conjugates self-assembled into NMs. DLS analysis revealed that Ast-PEG-Met NMs had a hydrodynamic diameter of 238.0 ± 41.7 nm, and were more monodisperse and compact than the intermediate Ast-PEG assemblies (386.7 ± 74.3 nm) (Fig. 1H). Conjugation with Met also endowed the NMs with a highly positive surface charge (+28.4 ± 2.2 mV), which was attributed to the protonated amine groups of Met at the micelle–water interface (Fig. 1I) [41]. TEM results further verified the formation of spherical and monodisperse NMs (Fig. 1J). The proposed core-shell structure, consisting of a hydrophobic Ast core and a hydrophilic PEG corona with pH-labile, surface-exposed Met, is illustrated in Fig. 1K. This structure maintains the stability of the conjugates and enables the acid-triggered release of water-soluble Ast and Met in specific microenvironments, such as wound sites or intracellular regions.

The design of Ast-PEG-Met NMs provides an advanced strategy to codeliver hydrophobic and hydrophilic agents. Covalent conjugation of Ast and Met is necessary because of their opposing solubilities. Ast exhibits strong hydrophobicity, whereas Met is highly hydrophilic. Simple physical mixing in aqueous media leads to the precipitation of Ast and rapid diffusion of Met, thereby failing to achieve synchronized codelivery. By self-assembling into core–shell nanostructures, the amphiphilic conjugates effectively address this issue, with Ast encapsulated within the hydrophobic core and PEG–Met distributed on the hydrophilic corona. Compared with polysaccharide-based carriers, the present system possesses multiple advantages [38]. The flexible PEG spacer improves the solubility of Ast and facilitates the introduction of stimuli-responsive linkages. The pH-sensitive Schiff base further enables on-demand drug release under acidic microenvironments. Meanwhile, the positively charged surface of NMs promotes cellular uptake [42]. This strategy can be extended to other diseases characterized by local acidosis and inflammation.

Assessment of ROS scavenging and cytoprotective antioxidant activity

Ast possesses unique antioxidant capacity attributed to its special molecular structure, which contains two terminal ionone rings and a central conjugated carbon‒carbon bonds. This structural feature allows Ast to scavenge high-energy electrons and delocalize electronic energy along the carbon chain [11]. Therefore, it is necessary to verify whether structural modification and self-assembly compromise its antioxidant capacity. After modification, Ast-PEG-Met exhibited potent and unaltered DPPH scavenging ability compared with free Ast at an equivalent Ast concentration (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2B, Ast-PEG-Met exhibited a dose-dependent ABTS+• scavenging activity. At the highest tested concentration (25 μg/mL), the ABTS radical scavenging activity showed no significant difference compared with Trolox. The photo of the decolorization reaction provides direct evidence of this dose‒response relationship (Fig. 2C). As the concentration of Ast-PEG-Met increased, the blue‒green ABTS radical was gradually reduced to its colorless form; the initially vivid blue color of the solution therefore faded and was replaced by the characteristic orange hue inherent to Ast [43]. The cytoprotective efficacy of Ast-PEG-Met NMs was evaluated in a cellular oxidative stress model. As shown in Fig. S3, Ast-PEG-Met NMs increased the viability of L929 fibroblasts in a concentration-dependent manner, with a plateau effect observed at 8 μg/mL. Using this concentration (delivering 4 μM Ast), the protective effect of the NMs was further investigated. Although free Ast provided partial protection, the NMs completely restored cell viability to a level indistinguishable from that of the unstressed normal group (Fig. 2D). This superior protective effect highlights a key advantage of the nanoformulation: compared with free, hydrophobic Ast, Ast-PEG-Met NMs enhance the cellular delivery and bioavailability of the antioxidant. A comprehensive biochemical analysis further revealed the cellular response (Fig. 2E–H). H2O2 treatment led to a significant decrease in the activity of the key antioxidant enzymes CAT and SOD [44]. Both drugs counteracted this effect, with Ast-PEG-Met NMs elevating CAT and SOD activities back to baseline levels (Normal group), whereas free Ast activity was significantly but incompletely restored (Fig. 2E and F). These findings suggested that the NMs potentiated the endogenous enzymatic defense machinery more effectively. MDA is the final product of lipid peroxidation and serve as a critical marker of oxidative damage to cell membranes [44]. H2O2 stimulation significantly increased the level of MDA, whereas both treatments differentially reversed this change (Fig. 2G). Compared with free Ast, Ast-PEG-Met NMs showed a stronger trend, indicating that the NMs effectively mitigated membrane lipid peroxidation. GSH, a major intracellular redox buffer, was strongly depleted by H2O2-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 2H) [44]. Both Ast and Ast-PEG-Met NMs treatment significantly restored intracellular GSH levels, demonstrating their ability to alleviate the oxidative burden and promote redox homeostasis. The slightly lower, but statistically insignificant GSH level in the Ast-PEG-Met group was attributed to the more robust activation of CAT and SOD, which reduced the reliance on GSH for direct ROS scavenging [45]. Imaging using the ROS-sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA further revealed that H2O2 treatment caused a substantial increase in intracellular ROS level (Fig. 2I and J). Both free Ast and Ast-PEG-Met NMs treatments markedly attenuated this ROS overaccumulation, with the NMs showing a more pronounced reduction. These results indicated that Ast-PEG-Met NMs demonstrate superior cytoprotection over free Ast at an equivalent dose. The enhanced performance of the NMs is attributable to their improved aqueous dispersibility and cellular delivery, which ensures more efficient intracellular accumulation of the active antioxidant compared with the poorly soluble free Ast. This multifaceted antioxidant defense, including direct radical scavenging, enzymatic defense support and membrane protection, establishes Ast-PEG-Met NMs as a potent nanotherapeutic candidate against oxidative stress, which is a foundational pathology of diabetic wounds.

Analysis of macrophage reprogramming from the M1 to M2 phenotype

Given the central role of dysregulated macrophage polarization in sustaining chronic inflammation in diabetic wounds, we next evaluated the immunomodulatory effect of Ast-PEG-Met NMs on LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. As shown in Fig. 3, LPS stimulation successfully induced a classical M1 phenotype, as evidenced by high expression of the M1 marker enzyme iNOS and the chemokine receptor CCR7, as well as low expression of M2 marker enzyme Arg-1 and the mannose receptor CD206. Treatment with free Met or Ast-PEG-Met NMs (delivering an equivalent dose of Met) significantly reversed this trend. Both formulations markedly downregulated iNOS expression, with the NMs exhibiting a more potent inhibitory effect (Fig. 3A and B). A similar trend was observed for CCR7, where the NMs also showed a stronger suppressive effect, although not significantly different from that of Met (Fig. 3C and D). Concurrently, the expression of the M2 markers was robustly upregulated by both formulations, with Ast-PEG-Met NMs exerted a significantly stronger upregulatory effect on both Arg-1 and CD206 than free Met (Fig. 3E–H). The superior phenotypic reprogramming effect of Ast-PEG-Met NMs was further confirmed by profiling the levels of secreted inflammatory cytokines. ELISA results revealed that both free Met and Ast-PEG-Met NMs significantly suppressed the secretion of the proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α and increased the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 3I and J). AMPK has been identified as a key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, and its activation provides the most critical biological energy to support M2 polarization. Met can activate the AMPK pathway by inhibiting mitochondrial complex I, and this effect has been confirmed in various cell types, such as macrophages, liver cells, and skeletal muscle cells [50]. In addition, PPARγ can also promote the M2 polarization [14]. Ast acts as a selective PPARγ modulator and can inhibit inflammation by upregulating PPARγ expression [[51], [52], [53], [54], [55]]. Liang et al. confirmed that Ast promotes the polarization of microglia from the M1 to M2 phenotype by upregulating PPARγ expression, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects [17]. As an upstream signal, AMPK activates downstream PPARγ through direct phosphorylation and metabolic regulation. Multiple studies have shown that AMPK-PPARγ signaling is involved in the promotion of M2 macrophages in LPS-induced macrophages [56,57]. The co-enrichment of autophagy and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis pathways suggests the use of selective autophagy, potentially mitophagy-the clearance of damaged mitochondria via PINK1/Parkin-dependent ubiquitination [58]. AMPK activation can phosphorylate ULK1 to initiate autophagy and phosphorylate PINK1 to activate PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy [59]. Conversely, after the elimination dysfunctional mitochondria, mitophagy reduces mitochondrial ROS leakage and restores ATP production, thereby altering the AMP/ATP ratio and redox status, which in turn affects AMPK activity [60]. This crosstalk, together with PPARγ-driven metabolic reprogramming, provides a transcriptomic basis suggesting that NMs may promote M2 macrophage polarization through synergistic upregulation of AMPK-PPARγ activity and mitophagy.

Functional validation of mitochondrial rescue and enhanced bioenergetics

We first evaluated the protective effects of Ast-PEG-Met NMs on mitochondrial function in LPS-stimulated macrophages. Compared with the LPS control, NMs significantly decreased mitochondrial superoxide levels (Fig. 4E and F); inhibited MPTP opening (Fig. 4G and H); restored the mitochondrial membrane potential, as reflected by the JC-1 red/green ratio (Fig. 4I and J); and increased the intracellular ATP content (Fig. 4K). These results indicated that NMs effectively repair LPS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. To verify pathway activation at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed (Fig. 4L–P). NM treatment significantly increased the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio (Fig. 4L) and PPARγ protein expression (Fig. 4M), confirming the activation of the AMPK-PPARγ axis. Concurrently, NMs upregulated PINK1 (Fig. 4N), Parkin (Fig. 4O) and LC3-II expression (Fig. 4P), indicating specific activation of the mitophagy pathway.

To determine whether mitophagy contributed to the observed protective effects, a blockade experiment was performed using the mitophagy inhibitor Mdivi-1. Upon Mdivi-1 treatment, the regulatory effects of Ast-PEG-Met NMs on inflammatory factor secretion were significantly attenuated, along with weakened reduction of TNF-α and diminished upregulation of IL-10. Meanwhile, the mitochondrial protective effects of Ast-PEG-Met NMs were also impaired, as reflected by altered levels of mtROS and ATP, as well as changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. S4). These findings demonstrate that mitophagy serves as a crucial pathway by which Ast-PEG-Met NMs exert mitochondrial protection and anti-inflammatory effects. These results demonstrate that Ast-PEG-Met NMs reprogram macrophages from a glycolysis-driven pro-inflammatory state to an oxidative phosphorylation-dominated pro-repair state through mitochondrial-targeted activation of the AMPK-PPARγ axis and PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy. The partial reversal of the effects upon Mdivi-1 treatment suggests that mitophagy-independent mechanisms, such as direct ROS scavenging and inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway by Ast and Met, may also contribute synergistically [46,61].

Fabrication and characterization of the ROS-responsive self-healing hydrogel

Successful grafting of CMCS-PBA was confirmed by 1H NMR, which showed the characteristic proton peak of the phenylboronic acid moiety at 7.83 ppm (Fig. 5B) [63]. FT-IR spectra further verified the structure and subsequent crosslinking (Fig. 5C). The Ast-PEG-Met Gel displayed a distinct orange color owing to the encapsulated NMs (Fig. 5D). Both hydrogels demonstrated macroscopic self-healing capability (Fig. 5E). The drug-loaded hydrogels exhibited strong tissue-adhesive properties conforming to various surfaces (Fig. 5F). SEM observation revealed a lamellar and densely interconnected microstructure with a reduced pore size in the Ast-PEG-Met Gel, where spherical nanostructures were distributed on the matrix (Fig. 5G). Rheological analysis revealed viscoelastic and self-healing properties. Both hydrogels exhibited solid-dominant behavior, with a storage modulus (G′) of approximately 900 Pa and a loss modulus (G″) of approximately 300 Pa at low strain, indicating the formation of a stable, crosslinked network (Fig. 5H). The intersection of G′ and G″ occurred at 256% strain indicating preferable elastic dominance, structural stability, and high yield strain, which were attributed to the synergistic reinforcement of the double-network structure. With respect to self-healing performance (Fig. 5I), the G′ and G″ of the Ast-PEG-Met Gel remained stable above 80% of their initial values after 4 stress cycles, demonstrating superior self-healing capacity and structural recoverability, originating from the reversible breakage and recombination of dynamic boronate ester bonds and noncovalent interactions [65]. Owing to the abundant hydrophilic groups and porous three-dimensional network, the hydrogels showed high swelling capacity, which is essential for exudate management (Fig. 5J).

The core functionality of the hydrogel is its dual-stimuli-responsive drug release behavior (Fig. 5K and L). Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4, no H2O2), Ast and Met release was minimal (44.30% and 55.29%, respectively, at 240 h). The introduction of 1 mM H2O2 significantly accelerated the release to 69.44% and 73.31%, respectively, demonstrating the ROS-triggered degradation of phenylboronate ester linkages. The most rapid and extensive release (76.77% and 82.05%, respectively) occurred under combined acidic and oxidative conditions (pH 6.5+H2O2). In this environment, ROS triggered the dissolution of the hydrogel to release Ast-PEG-Met NMs, while the acidic pH induced drug release from the released NMs. Besides, the release experiment was performed under sink conditions with a large buffer volume, which accelerates hydrolysis more than the actual storage or physiological state does; therefore, the Schiff base bond is expected to be even more stable under actual storage and physiological conditions. This two-stage release sequence is illustrated in Fig. 5M.

Intelligent hydrogels with self-healing, stimulus-responsive, and adhesion properties have recently become a research frontier for diabetic wound care [[66], [67], [68]]. They also serve as immunomodulatory platforms to regulate the chronic wound microenvironment through inherent properties or the delivery of bioactive components [[69], [70], [71], [72], [73]]. In this study, Ast-PEG-Met NMs-loaded hydrogel shows strong cohesion, shape stability and self-healing ability, thereby enabling stable retention in the wound. It remains stable under physiological conditions but rapidly releases drugs in response to high ROS levels and acidic pH, forming an inflammation-triggered feedback loop. This system offers a controllable platform for the spatiotemporal delivery of therapeutic NMs to diabetic wounds.

We further investigated whether the drug-loaded hydrogel could generate a pro-healing microenvironment through interaction with immune cells. CMs were harvested from RAW 264.7 macrophages cultured in the presence of hydrogel extracts. As shown in Fig. 6G and H, the Ast-PEG-Met Gel CM dramatically accelerated the migration of L929 fibroblasts in a scratch wound assay. Fibroblast migration is critical for wound contraction and re-epithelialization, and together with angiogenesis it reflects the effectiveness of wound healing [22]. Studies showed that M2 macrophages secrete proangiogenic factors such as VEGF and PDGF, as well as growth factors such as CTGF and TGF-β to regulate endothelial cell and fibroblast function through paracrine signaling [28,74,75]. In this study, the Ast-PEG-Met Gel reprograms macrophages into the M2 phenotype, and the CM from macrophages treated with the Ast-PEG-Met Gel extract significantly promoted fibroblast migration and endothelial tube formation, suggesting that paracrine factors derived from macrophages may mediate these effects.

Evaluation of therapeutic efficacy and mechanism in a diabetic wound model

The therapeutic potential of the Ast-PEG-Met Gel was evaluated in a STZ-induced diabetic rat model with full-thickness dorsal wounds (Fig. 7A). The wound closure results revealed that the Ast-PEG-Met Gel significantly accelerated wound healing (Fig. 7B–D). By Day 7, this group achieved nearly 80% of its total 14-day healing, whereas the percentages of the Blank and Gel groups were approximately about 65% and 64%, respectively, suggesting that the intervention most critically affected the inflammatory and early proliferative phases. As the systemic diabetic parameters remained uniformly and severely dysregulated across all groups, the benefit was local and specific, confirming that healing was uncoupled from gross metabolic control (Fig. 7E and F). Histological analysis provided structural evidence of differential healing trajectories. Compared with the two control groups, the Ast-PEG-Met Gel-treated group exhibited significantly enhanced granulation tissue formation, reduced inflammatory infiltration, and more robust collagen deposition (Fig. 7G and H). By Day 14, this early advantage culminated in mature tissue regeneration (Fig. 7I and J). Wounds in the Ast-PEG-Met Gel group displayed re-epithelialization, organized collagen bundles, and restored skin appendage structures in some samples, which closely resembled normal skin architecture. Conclusions

In summary, we developed an integrative therapeutic system for chronic diabetic wounds by combining a mitochondrion-targeted NM formulation with a ROS/pH-responsive hydrogel. The resulting rationally designed Ast-PEG-Met amphiphilic conjugates effectively improved the solubility and antioxidant activity of Ast, enabling the synergistic deployment of antioxidant activity and metabolic modulation. By targeting macrophage mitochondria and scavenging mtROS, this system activates the AMPK-PPARγ pathway and promotes mitophagy to restore mitochondrial homeostasis, thus effectively reprogramming macrophage immunometabolism and resolving persistent inflammation. When incorporated into a self-healing, dual-responsive hydrogel, the NMs enable on-demand payload release within the diabetic wound microenvironment. This combined platform significantly promotes tissue regeneration by accelerating wound closure, promoting angiogenesis, and facilitating collagen maturation. This work presents a mechanistically grounded strategy for immunometabolic regulation, offering a promising and translatable approach for the treatment of diabetic wounds and other inflammatory tissue disorders.
