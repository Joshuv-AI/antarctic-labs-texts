Sleep quality and sleep hygiene among medical undergraduates in Himachal Pradesh (North India): A questionnaire-based study
Authors: Punam Verma, Hitesh Jani, Priti Bhanderi, Bhoopendra Patel, Rupali K. Parlewar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Medical students are particularly susceptible to poor physical and mental health due to inadequate sleep.Aim:This study aimed to evaluate sleep behaviours such as sleep quality, sleep hygiene awareness, and practices among medical students at an institution in Himachal Pradesh.Methodology:The study encompassed all MBBS students enrolled at the institute during the study period. It examined the association between sleep quality and sleep hygiene awareness and practices. Validated tools such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and Sleep Hygiene Awareness and Practice Scale (SHAPS) were used for assessment.Results:Participants from each academic year were included, and the median global PSQI score was 7.91, with scores above 5 indicating poor sleep quality. A total of 241 students (76.75%) were identified as poor sleepers, while 73 students (23.24%) were classified as good sleepers. Sleep hygiene practice (SHP) score (14.39) was higher in poor sleepers in comparison with good sleepers (8.72), while sleep hygiene awareness (SHA) score (21) was similar in both groups.Discussion:Poor sleep hygiene practices were significantly associated with poor sleep quality, whereas no significant relationship was observed between sleep quality and SHA. Additionally, there was no significant difference in sleep behaviours between genders. However, first-year and fourth-year students exhibited significantly poorer sleep quality and sleep hygiene practices compared to students in other academic years.Conclusion:Findings highlight the importance of addressing sleep health alongside academic demands to improve students’ overall well-being and academic performance. Interventions like awareness campaigns and the creation of a supportive learning environment that prioritizes student well-being should be considered.

Introduction

Medical students go through an intensive course before and after entering the clinical wards and have contact with patients. This group of the population is vulnerable to poor sleep. Unfortunately, sleep deprivation influences their physical, psychological, and cognitive functioning, thereby affecting their academic performance. Limited research investigated the sleep behaviour of this group, which could have both health and academic implications. The high prevalence and implications of sleep difficulties among young adults warrant more exploration into underlying factors contributing to such problems. Studies from various countries have documented a high prevalence of sleep disturbances among medical students, including sleep deprivation, poor sleep quality, and excessive daytime sleepiness.[12]

Academic years of medical education, characterized by intense academic pressure, can significantly impact students’ physical and mental health, particularly their sleep patterns. Numerous studies have highlighted the prevalence of sleep disturbances among medical students, with estimates suggesting that up to 50% experience daytime sleepiness against 36% in general population[3] and 40.6% suffer from poor sleep quality.[4] Sleep disorders among young adults have emerged as a significant global health issue, ranking as the second most common mental health problem.

Psychological stressors like anxiety, depression, and academic stress among higher education students can exacerbate sleep problems.[56] Studies have also reported poor sleep quality in females in comparison with males, tea consumption before going to bed, older age, and single individuals.[78]

Sleep Hygiene, a set of practices aimed at improving sleep quality and quantity. Among university students, poor sleep hygiene is associated with environmental factors: Increased academic pressure (stress of frequent exams and coursework), increase time demands to balance studies and other activities of the institute, reduced parental guidance, i.e. freedom to choose sleep schedules, noisy and distracting environments of hostels,[910] change in lifestyle behaviours such as excessive alcohol consumption, caffeine intake, and late-night technology use can disrupt sleep patterns.[11121314] These factors contribute to both shorter sleep duration and poorer sleep quality.[15]

Poor sleep quality can lead to a host of negative consequences, including physical health risks like increased risk of diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and other chronic conditions[161718]; mental health issues like elevated risk of depression, anxiety, and cognitive impairment[111920] and impaired academic achievements and absenteeism.[152122]

A controversial association between SHA and sleep quality has been reported. Some studies have suggested a positive correlation of SHA with regular sleep habits, good sleep quality, and better sleep efficiency [2324]; others have found no significant link.[2526]

Research Question

Is there any association between sleep quality and sleep hygiene practices among medical students?

Aim

To assess sleep quality and sleep hygiene and their correlation in medical students of the medical institution of Himachal Pradesh, India.

Objective

To assess sleep hygiene practices and sleep quality of medical students using the Sleep Hygiene Awareness and Practice Scale (SHAPS) and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

To examine the association of sleep hygiene and quality with age and year of medical study.

Methodology

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on medical students (17–25 years of age) enrolled in one of the medical institutes of Himachal Pradesh at the time of study. The study period was from December 2023–September 2024. Students having a chronic disease, current or past history of schizophrenia, bipolar, or any other psychotic disorders, memory problems, and taking any neuro-psychotic medications, and who did not give consent were excluded from the study.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol and data collection instruments were reviewed and approved by the Institute Scientific Advisory Committee, followed by approval by the Institute Ethics Committee vide office order no AIIMS/BLS/IEC-BHR/24-08/CERT/10.

Data collection and instruments

Study objectives were explained to the participants, and informed consent was obtained from each participant. They were assured of anonymity and confidentiality of the information provided during the study. Medical students were not given any incentives or financial compensation to participate in the study. Participants were free to withdraw from the study at any time.

A self-administered, structured questionnaire was developed in English and shared with students. The questionnaire was comprised of three sections (A, B, and C). All the items of validated PSQI and SHAPS freely available online[2728] were used to evaluate students’ sleep quality and sleep hygiene awareness and practice, respectively. Time for the distribution of the questionnaire was chosen carefully, paying attention to the exam schedule of the students, such that they will not be having exam over one month before and after filling out the questionnaire. After the collection of questionnaires, erroneous questionnaires were eliminated, and only properly completed questionnaires were considered for further analysis. Students were communicated about the results after analysis.

Section A

In this section, items were to get baseline information: age, gender, and year of the study, and the comorbid medical conditions (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/diabetes mellitus/hypertension/neurological/psychiatric), use of any medication.

Section B: Assessment of Sleep Quality

The validated questionnaire PSQI[29] was used to evaluate students’ sleep quality. This evaluates sleep quality during a preceding month and consists of 19 items subgrouped into seven components: sleep duration, sleep latency, sleep efficiency, subjective sleep quality, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction. The PSQI has a high degree of internal consistency with an overall reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) of 0.83 in the original article. The global cutoff score of 5 for distinguishing poor from good sleep quality has a sensitivity of 86.9% and a specificity of 86.5%.[30]

Scoring of PSQI

The score was calculated by using the scale provided together with the standardized PSQI questionnaire. One question was omitted because of many missing data. Each item was scored on a 4-point scale. To get a global PSQI score, the scores of all components were added up. The total score ranges from 0 to 21; 0 indicates the best sleep quality, and 21 indicates the worst sleep quality. The cutoff point for interpretation is 5; if the total score is equal to or less than 5, it indicates good sleep quality, and scoring more than 5 indicates poor sleep quality.

Section C: Assessment of sleep hygiene

The validated SHAPS[3031] questionnaire was used to evaluate students’ sleep hygiene knowledge and practice. It was divided into two parts: (1) SHA and (2) sleep hygiene practice (SHP)

Sleep hygiene awareness (SHA)

This contains 13 items assessing general sleep hygiene awareness, whether specific activities are beneficial to sleep, disruptive to sleep, or have no effect. All these actions are known to have an effect on sleep and include behaviours as napping, regular use of sleep medications, waking at the same time every day, drinking alcohol in the evening and smoking.

Scoring of SHA

Item numbers 1–6, 9, and 13 were disruptive of sleep, while item numbers 7, 8, 10, 11, and 12 were beneficial to sleep. The responses were collected on 7 7-point Likert scale [(1) very beneficial; (2) moderately beneficial; (3) mildly beneficial to sleep; (4) no effect on sleep; (5) mildly disruptive; (6) moderately disruptive; (7) very disruptive]. Responses 1, 2, and 3 to these questions were scored as correct, while responses 4, 5, 6, and 7 were scored as incorrect. Correct responses were given 1 point; item omitted was given 2 points; incorrect responses were given 3 points.

The scale ranges from 12–36. Higher scores suggested a poor awareness of behaviours and actions that negatively impact sleep.

Sleep hygiene practice (SHP)

SHP student’s response was obtained as the number of days per week that they were engaged in practicing the included 15 sleep behaviours. The total score was the sum of the answers to all items. The score ranges from 0–133. A higher score indicated less healthy SHP.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done using the SPSS software version (22, IBM Corporation), and P values less than 0.05 were considered significant. Descriptive statistics were applied to explore whether the data were normally distributed or not.

Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis test were used to compare to assess the differences between two or more than two independent groups, respectively. Sleep hygiene knowledge and practice scores were compared between students with good and poor sleep quality. Spearman correlation rank test was applied to assess the association among the variables. The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables.

Results

There were 400 students enrolled in the institute at the time of study. All students were approached. A total of 328 (82%) students filled out the questionnaire, and after removing the missing data, 314 (78%) questionnaires were analyzed, including 165 (52.54%) males and 149 (47.457%) females. Of these, 96 (30.57%) were first-year, 88 (28%) second-year, 84 (26.75%) and 46 (14.64%) were fourth-year students, ranging from 17 to 25 years.

Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for the comparisons of sleep behaviours.

Comparative analysis by using the Mann–Whitney U test was done to see the difference in sleep behaviours between male and female students. Overall sleep quality was poor among students (global PSQI score > 5; median score: 7.91 (4–10); but the difference between the groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The median score of SHA: 21 (21–22) were similar in both groups; while that of SHP, all subjects were 11 (7–17), and it was higher among male students (12) (7–18) in comparison with female students 10 (7–15) although these two sleep behaviours were not statistically significantly different in both the groups. Spearman correlation showed a statistically significantly positive correlation of sleep quality with the SHP in both groups (rmale = 0.367; P = 0.001; rfemale = 0.376, P value 0.000), but no significant correlation was found with SHA (rmale = −0.003.0 P = 0.977; rfemale = 0.020; P value 0.838) in both male and female students. No association significant was found between SHP and SHA in both groups [Table 1].

Discussion

Good sleep health is crucial for both overall well-being and academic success. By using validated assessment tools, sleep behaviours can be better understood. This understanding helps us to develop effective interventions to promote healthy sleep habits and better general and mental health and academic outcomes.

This study was aimed to assess sleep behaviours in medical students by using validated assessment tools. Sleep quality was assessed by using PSQI and compared the global PSQI score between the gender, across different age groups, and their year of study. The study found a high prevalence (76.75%) of poor sleep quality (global PSQI >5) in medical students with a statistically significant difference in both genders and in the academic year of study.

The prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students in the present study is similar to the results of previous studies in medical students[32] and higher than other studies.[273334]

The average global score of the PSQI in this study was more than 5 (7.26), i.e. indicative of the presence of clinically significant sleep difficulties.[27] Differences in the proportion of sleep quality across different studies can be due to various factors like population demographics, use of different assessment tools, cultural factors and lifestyle habits, and environmental conditions which play an important role in a crucial role in shaping sleep patterns.[28]

The current study found a statistically significant difference in global PSQI scores among students of different phases (0.0037). The poorest sleep quality in first-year and fourth-year students is similar to the findings of other studies of North India.[35] The reason for the poor quality of sleep in these students could be new schedules, academic demands, unfamiliar environments, lack of social life, night time internet surfing, and improper eating habits for first-year medical students and for fourth-year and third year students, in addition to these clinical rotations, night shifts, frequent examinations, and worries for future academic challenges and sometimes financial distresses.[1636373839]

Although the recommended sleep duration in young adults is 7 h, but in the current study, more than 70% of students in each year have slept less than 7 h and the majority having less than 5 h which is in consistence with the other studies.[38] Sleep deprivation can result in more academic errors, accidents, and most importantly decrease in academic performance.[40] Sleeping less than 7 h is associated with poor general health and an increased risk of developing diseases.[39] Less sleep duration may be due to increased sleep latency. Predictors of sleep latency in medical students were found as stress, anger, worry, cognitive hyperarousal, and hypervigilance.[39] Many studies have reported stress among medical students.[16]

The present study did not find significant sleep disturbance in medical students, which is in contrast to other studies that reported higher sleep disturbances in medical students.[3441] Sleep disturbances may result in poor academic performance, cognitive skills, and risk of development of psychiatric problems.[3840]

The present study also found significant daytime dysfunctions in the student.[28] Medical errors and decreased academic performance are also associated with this component of sleep.[40]

Although in the current study, there is a high prevalence of poor sleep quality but more than 73% of overall students reported subjective sleep quality as very good to fairly good, which is consistence with other findings of other studies in India and other countries.[42] Surprisingly, in the current study, sleep efficiency is not found to be a contributing factor to poor sleep quality, which is in contrast to the findings of other studies.[27]

Another component of the sleep quality assessment scale, i.e. use of sleep medication, was also not found as a contributing factor for the poor sleep in these students, which was reported by studies of other country.[3343]

Difference in the proportion of sleep quality across different studies can be due to various factors like population demographics, use of different assessment tools, cultural factors and lifestyle habits, and environmental conditions which play an important role in a crucial role in shaping sleep patterns.[28]

Prevalence of poor sleep quality in male students is 89%, and in female students, it is 84% in the present study, but the difference in PSQI score of both was not statistically significant which is very much higher than the other study which reported poor sleep quality prevalence among male and female students as 39.8% and 48.2%, respectively.[44]

Contrary to our findings, some studies have reported poor sleep quality in females in comparison with males These studies suggest that factors beyond biology, such as family roles, lifestyle habits (e.g. diet, exercise, substance use), stress response, and cognitive differences, may contribute to poorer sleep quality in females.[45]

The present study did not find a significant difference in sleep quality across different age groups. This discrepancy with previous research, which linked older age to poorer sleep quality, may be due to the relatively narrow age range of our participants.[11] However, our findings align with other studies that suggest older students may have a better understanding of sleep hygiene practices.[21]

Sleep hygiene plays a significant role in determining the quality of sleep, especially for medical students. Previous research has linked poor sleep hygiene to poor sleep quality.[1146]

Our study found no significant difference in the awareness of sleep hygiene practices between students with good and poor sleep quality. However, students with poor sleep quality exhibited poorer sleep hygiene practices as compared to those with good sleep quality. This finding aligns with previous research.[21]

The lack of difference in awareness might be attributed to the challenges faced by students due to increased academic workload and study pressures, which may limit their ability to implement healthy sleep practices.[21]

This study found significantly better sleep hygiene practices in students with good sleep quality. Previous studies have reported a significant effect of day time nap, smoking more than one packet in a day, drink beverages containing caffeine (e.g. coffee, tea, colas) within 4 h of bedtime on the sleep quality of the students.

Limitations

Participants of the study are only from one medical college so findings cannot be generalized to all medical students. Objective assessment of sleep quality by using PSG can further strengthen the results.

Conclusion

Our study revealed suboptimal sleep quality among medical students, as indicated by the PSQI score of 7.26. While sleep hygiene practices were significantly associated with sleep quality, awareness of these practices alone was not sufficient. This highlights the importance of practicing good sleep hygiene habits to improve overall sleep quality.

Given the potential negative impact of poor sleep on future healthcare professionals, it is crucial to implement strategies to address this issue. These strategies may include educational interventions, awareness campaigns, and creating a supportive learning environment that prioritizes student well-being.
