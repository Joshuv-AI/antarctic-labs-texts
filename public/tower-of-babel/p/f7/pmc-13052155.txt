Bladder Outlet Obstruction Secondary to a Prostatic Cyst in a Young Adult: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Suraj K C, Sunit Tandon, Samiksha Lamichhane, Shritik Devkota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBladder outlet obstruction secondary to an obstructing prostatic cyst is a rare clinical entity. Incomplete regression of the Müllerian duct is supposed to be the cause of such cysts. Clinical features can range from acute bladder outlet obstruction to chronic urinary retention, oligospermia, and prostatitis‐like symptoms. Diagnosis is challenging and requires ultrasonography of the abdomen and pelvis, transrectal ultrasound, and even magnetic resonance imaging. Transurethral resection or unroofing of a prostatic midline cyst near the urethra has been a safe and effective method of managing prostatic cysts. This article describes a rare case of bladder outlet obstruction caused by a prostatic midline cyst without an enlarged prostate. The patient was treated with urethral catheterization, diagnostic cystoscopy, and cyst deroofing. This case adds valuable insights to the literature.Keywords: bladder, oligospermia, prostatic cyst, transrectal, transurethral

Introduction

Bladder outlet obstruction is a term endorsed by the International Continence Society (ICS) as a condition characterized by increased detrusor pressure and decreased urine flow rate due to obstructive factors during voiding. In men, benign prostatic hyperplasia is the leading cause of bladder outlet obstruction and lower urinary tract symptoms [1]. Prostatic midline cysts are believed to result from incomplete regression of the Müllerian duct remnant. These cysts originate from the caudal end of the fused Müllerian duct at the level of the verumontanum and extend medially and posteriorly above the prostate gland [2]. Enlarged prostatic cysts can compress adjacent structures such as the posterior urethra, bladder neck, or seminal vesicles, causing obstructive or irritative voiding symptoms, recurrent urinary tract infections, epididymitis, chronic pelvic pain syndrome, hematospermia, low semen volume, or infertility. While most patients are asymptomatic, some may experience these complications [3, 4, 5].

In this article, we present a case of bladder outlet obstruction secondary to a prostatic midline cyst in the absence of an enlarged prostate. The patient initially underwent urethral catheterization, followed by diagnostic cystoscopy and deroofing of the cyst. This presentation is rare in our clinical practice and may provide valuable insights for the literature.

Case Presentation

Case History

A 32‐year‐old gentleman with a history of feeling of incomplete voiding, increased frequency, and poor stream of urine for the past 4 months presented with an acute onset of pain over the suprapubic region for 4 hours. Upon further inquiry, he reported decreased frequency of micturition and amount of urine for the past 24 h. He denied a history of fever, burning micturition, use of any drugs, or fall injury. He had no other significant medical history.

General Physical Examination

A well‐built man with intact consciousness. The patient had a pulse rate of 110 beats/min regular. Blood pressure was 110/90 mmHg and the rest of the hemodynamic parameters were within the normal range.

Systemic Examination

Abdominal examination showed pain over the suprapubic region, which on palpation revealed a globular lump that did not move with respiration. The superior border was palpable and regular.

Methods and Differential Diagnosis

With the above clinical history and palpable bladder per abdomen, we kept a differential diagnosis of bladder outlet obstruction secondary to enlarged prostate or calculi at the bladder neck or stricture at the urethra and prostate cyst.

Investigations

Initial tests revealed a normal complete blood cell counts. Renal function tests and liver function tests were normal. The patient had a normal urine routine and microscopic examination. His prostate‐specific antigen was 2 ng/dL(normal).

Imaging

The patient underwent an ultrasound of the abdomen and pelvis, which showed a prostatic cyst along the median lobe with an intravesical component bulging into the bladder neck, causing bladder neck obstruction. The pre‐void volume was 450 cc, and the post‐void volume was 350 cc, respectively (Figure 1).

Transabdominal ultrasound images where (A, B) show the Pre‐void phase and (C, D) show post‐void images of the pelvis showing a well‐defined prostatic cyst with intravesical extension (arrows) and significant post‐void residual volume in a transverse plane (axial plane).

Management/Treatment

The patient was managed with perurethral catheterization in the emergency department and admitted to the ward for further evaluation. Routine basic investigations, including complete blood counts, renal function tests, chest X‐ray, and electrocardiogram (ECG), were performed and found to be normal. On the 3rd day of admission, the patient underwent a cystoscopic examination to confirm the cause of bladder outlet obstruction. A cyst arising from the prostate along the medial lobe was identified as the cause of the obstruction of the bladder neck. The cyst wall was deroofed using a transurethral approach with electrocautery (Figure 2).

Intraoperative image showing deroofing of the cyst, which was arising from the median lobe. The arrow represents the plane of deroofing of the cyst, which includes the cyst wall only without involving prostatic tissues.

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

Following the surgery, the patient was transferred to the ward. The peri‐ and postoperative period was uneventful, so the Foley catheter was removed on the 3rd postoperative day. After the Foley catheter was removed, the patient was discharged on the same day. On the 8th postoperative day, we measured the pre‐void and post‐void residual volumes, which were 320 mL and 45 mL, respectively. The patient is currently under observation and is recovering well. The collected cyst wall/fluid was sent for histopathological examination, which showed a simple cyst wall with a flattened simple cuboidal lining epithelium (Figure 3).

Microscopic image showing a cyst wall with a flattened simple cuboidal lining epithelium marked with an arrow.

Discussion

Prostatic cysts have a rare prevalence, close to 1%, and can be categorized based on their size, relation with the urethra or seminal vesicles, and the presence or absence of semen within the cyst [6]. There has been confusion between “Müllerian duct cyst” and “prostatic utricle cyst,” which are often used interchangeably for a midline cyst of the prostate; however, they refer to different entities [2]. Improved imaging techniques have increased the diagnosis and prevalence of midline prostatic cysts (MPCs) in adult males, estimated to be 5%–14% [7]. Most frequently, the cyst is located in a posterior position, while it is unusual for cysts to be in the intraprostatic midline [6]. The majority of prostatic cysts are asymptomatic, originating from Müllerian ducts and the utricle as embryological remnants [7].

In 2009, Galosi et al. classified prostatic cysts into six distinct types based on transrectal ultrasonography and pathological correlation, which include isolated medial cysts, cysts of the ejaculatory duct, simple or multiple parenchymal cysts, complicated cysts (infectious or hemorrhagic), cystic tumors, and secondary cysts related to parasitic disease [8]. The majority of prostatic cysts are asymptomatic but can become symptomatic when the cyst's presence is accompanied by infection or if its size and anatomical relationships affect adjacent structures [9]. In our case, the intravesical extension of the prostatic cyst caused bladder outlet obstruction, which was the reason for clinical presentation. The presence of MPCs alone does not predict the development of moderate to severe LUTS, unless there are anatomically projecting to the bladder outlet and subsequently cause outlet obstruction [10] Clinical factors range from painful ejaculation, hemospermia, infertility, urinary tract infections, chronic pelvic pain, and prostatitis‐like syndrome symptoms, rarely resulting in obstructive and irritative urinary tract symptoms if located close to the bladder neck or posterior urethra [11]. Our patient developed acute retention of urine and bladder outlet obstruction due to an underlying prostate cyst, making it a rare presentation.

Tambo et al. analyzed 34 patients with symptomatic prostatic cysts, showing that 40% of the patients suffered from obstructive urinary symptoms, 33% from urinary retention, 9% from urodynia, and 6% from infertility [12]. The differential diagnosis of cystic prostatic lesions near the midline location includes cysts arising from the ejaculatory duct, vas deferens, transurethral resection of the prostate defect, and prostatic abscess [2]. Diagnosis can be challenging so imaging technology such as ultrasound, CT, and MRI could be used. The advantage of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is that it could demonstrate the fluid content of prostatic cysts [13]. Prostatic cysts are generally asymptomatic and do not require treatment. Symptomatic cases can be managed by transrectal aspiration, transurethral incision, deroofing or marsupialization [14] Various surgical techniques are used for management which include open surgical approaches such as suprapubic excision, transperineal or transrectal drainage, posterior sagittal transanal approaches, and various transtrigonal techniques. Open techniques have limited anatomical visualization and potentially significant morbidity from iatrogenic damage to adjacent structures, making it difficult surgery. Minimally invasive procedures such as ethanol sclerotherapy (transperineal or transrectal) and endoscopic techniques have overcome the drawbacks of open surgery. Transurethral resection or unroofing of a prostatic midline cyst near the urethra has been a safe and elegant alternative to all other methods, with improved visualization of the structures and potentially reducing morbidity from iatrogenic injury [2]. Laurenzo Milone et al. found that a partial resection of the cyst spared enough of the bladder neck structures to preserve sexual function, emphasizing the need for personalized surgical approaches in young patients to prevent compromising their sexual health [15]. In this case, we performed a deroofing of the cyst wall to preserve sexual health successfully.

Conclusion

Prostatic midline cysts are believed to result from incomplete regression of the Müllerian duct remnant. Improved imaging techniques have increased the diagnosis and prevalence of midline prostatic cysts. Most of the patients are asymptomatic, while very few present with symptoms of bladder outlet obstruction, from painful ejaculation, hemospermia, infertility, urinary tract infections, chronic pelvic pain, and prostatitis‐like syndrome. Transurethral resection or unroofing of a prostatic midline cyst near the urethra has been a safe and effective method of management of a prostatic cyst.
