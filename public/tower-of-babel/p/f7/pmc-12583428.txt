Elevated NUF2 and CD52 expression predict platinum-based chemoresistance and poor prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Bo Liu, Zhansheng Lu, Jing Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the clinical significance of the cytokinesis-associated gene NUF2 and the immune-related gene CD52 in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with a focus on their association with platinum-based chemoresistance and patient prognosis.MethodsCancer and matched adjacent non-tumorous tissues were obtained from 72 NSCLC patients who underwent surgical resection. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed to assess the expression of NUF2 and CD52. Patients were stratified based on combined staining intensity and extent scores. All patients received gemcitabine plus cisplatin chemotherapy. Associations between gene expression and chemotherapy response, clinicopathological features, and overall survival were evaluated. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox regression modeling were used to identify independent prognostic factors.ResultsNUF2 and CD52 showed significantly higher expression in NSCLC tissues compared to adjacent controls (66.67% and 62.50% positivity, respectively; P < 0.05). Positive expression of both genes was significantly associated with advanced TNM stage, poor differentiation, lymph node metastasis, pleural invasion, and vascular thrombus formation (P < 0.05). Post-chemotherapy non-remission rates were significantly elevated in NUF2- and CD52-positive patients (P < 0.05). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed markedly reduced median survival in patients with positive NUF2 and CD52 expression (P < 0.05). Multivariate Cox analysis identified platinum resistance, lymph node metastasis, pleural invasion, and positive expression of NUF2 and CD52 as independent predictors of poor prognosis.ConclusionHigh expression of NUF2 and CD52 is closely linked to platinum resistance and aggressive tumor behavior in NSCLC, and serves as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes. These markers may hold potential as prognostic indicators and therapeutic targets in chemoresistant NSCLC. These findings, while promising, are based on a single-center retrospective cohort and should be validated in larger, independent studies to confirm their clinical utility.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors in clinic, and its incidence rate ranks first among all malignant tumors according to relevant statistics [1]. In recent years, with the continuous change of people’s lifestyle, the incidence rate of lung cancer is increasing year by year and showing a younger trend, which has a serious impact on patients and their families. At present, the clinical treatment of lung cancer mainly adopts a comprehensive therapy of surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy. However, the postoperative recurrence rate is relatively high, with the emergence of chemotherapy drug resistance as one important reason [2]. Therefore, exploring the pathogenesis of lung cancer in order to find more effective therapies is the focus of medical workers.With the development of medical technology, research on lung cancer has gradually deepened to the molecular level. Studies have suggested [3]– [4] that the expression of multiple factors may be involved in the occurrence and development of lung cancer. Cell division related gene Cell division cycle related protein 2 (NUF2), as a centromere protein, is an indispensable part of regulating spindle assembly inspection and the process of centromere microtubule connection. Research points out that [5]– [6], NUF2 is highly expressed in breast cancer, liver cancer and other malignant tumors, which may be involved in disease progression. The immune related gene CD52 is a glycoprotein antigen. In the study of breast cancer, some scholars detected elevated expression of CD52 and the content of CD52 was significantly related to the prognosis [7]. However, the relevant effects of NUF2 and CD52 in lung cancer are currently unclear.

In the study, lung cancer patients admitted in our hospital during May 2016 to October 2018 were chosen as the subjects. The correlation between NUF2 and CD52 expression in lung cancer tissue with chemotherapy resistance and clinical prognosis was analyzed, in order to provide relevant references for the diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC.

Materials and methods

General materials

This study complied with the principles of Medical Ethics and was ratified by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (No: 2017-039-KY-01). The general information flow chart was exhibited in Fig. 1. This retrospective analysis selected 72 NSCLC patients who underwent surgical resection in our hospital during May 2016 to October 2018 as the study subjects. The surgically excised lung cancer tissue and adjacent tissue were collected, with adjacent tissue as the control group. Among these subjects, there were 54 males and 18 females, with an average age of (58.58 ± 5.61) years. Inclusion criteria: (1) All patients met the diagnostic and treatment criteria for NSCLC [8], and had been diagnosed through pathological examination. (2) The patient’s nervous system was good and could cooperate with the examination. (3) All patients did not undergo chemotherapy or radiotherapy before surgery. (4) The subjects had an Eastern Cancer Association Group (ECOG) score of 0–2 points; (5) Both the patient and their family members were informed consent and signed an informed consent form. Exclusion criteria: (1) Subjects with incomplete pathological data or withdrawal midway. (2) Subjects with infectious diseases such as hepatitis B. (3) The subjects with severe functional impairments in important organs such as the heart, liver, and kidneys. (4) Subjects with coagulation dysfunction. (5) Pregnant or lactating patients. All patients received gemcitabine plus cisplatin chemotherapy after surgery. In patients with stage II–III disease, this was administered as standard adjuvant chemotherapy. For stage I patients, chemotherapy was administered only in those with high-risk features (such as tumor size >4 cm, poor differentiation, vascular invasion, or pleural involvement), in accordance with NCCN guidelines. In addition, a small subset of stage I/II patients received cisplatin plus gemcitabine as salvage therapy upon early postoperative recurrence, based on multidisciplinary team (MDT) decision.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) [9]

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections of 4 μm thickness were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 15 min at room temperature to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed in 1× EDTA buffer (pH 8.0; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) using a microwave for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) for 20 min and then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with the following primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-NUF2 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; catalog no. ab122962; dilution 1:100) and mouse monoclonal anti-CD52 (Abcam; catalog no. ab33978; dilution 1:200). On the following day, sections were washed and incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody using the EliVision™ Plus IHC kit (Maixin-Bio, Fuzhou, China; kit no. KIT-9922) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by color development with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (ZSGB-BIO; catalog no. ZLI-9018) for 3–5 min. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted.

Immunostaining was evaluated under a light microscope in four randomly selected high-power fields (×400 magnification) per slide. Staining was scored using an immunoreactivity score (IRS) calculated as the product of staining intensity (0 = negative, 1 = light yellow, 2 = brown-yellow, 3 = dark brown) and percentage of positive cells (0 = ≤ 10%, 1 = 11–50%, 2 = 51–75%, 3 = > 75%). An IRS ≥ 3 was considered positive expression. To ensure reproducibility, all slides were independently assessed by three experienced pathologists blinded to patient data, and inter-observer agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.87, indicating strong agreement). Discordant cases were reviewed jointly until consensus was reached.

Outcome measures

(1) The expression of NUF2 and CD52 in tissues was detected using IHC. (2) The clinical pathological data of patients was collected to analyze the relationship between NUF2, CD52 and clinical pathological characteristics. (3) The patients received chemotherapy by gemcitabine (Jiangsu Haosen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., batch number: 20160105, specification: 0.2 g) combined with cisplatin (Qilu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., batch number: 20150106, specification: 100 mg). Chemotherapy resistance was assessed based on clinical tumor response using the RECIST (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors) guidelines. Responses were classified as complete remission (CR), partial remission (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Patients who failed to achieve CR or PR after two cycles of gemcitabine plus cisplatin were categorized as non-remission, and the non-remission rate (NRR) was used as a surrogate marker of platinum resistance. Although objective response rate (ORR) and progression-free survival (PFS) are standard endpoints in prospective trials, these could not be consistently evaluated in this retrospective cohort due to incomplete imaging follow-up. NRR provided a uniform and practical measure of resistance across all cases. For prognostic analysis, patients were stratified by 5-year overall survival (OS): those surviving ≥ 5 years were assigned to the good prognosis group, and those who died within 5 years to the poor prognosis group. Of note, approximately one-third of our cohort comprised stage I/II patients who also received cisplatin plus gemcitabine. In these cases, chemotherapy was indicated either as adjuvant therapy for high-risk pathological features or as salvage treatment following recurrence. Although adjuvant chemotherapy is not uniformly recommended for all early-stage NSCLC, inclusion of these patients reflects real-world clinical practice at our center during the study period. Nonetheless, this heterogeneity may introduce bias, and prospective studies with more homogeneous treatment regimens are needed.

(4) Follow up of patients was conducted through telephone, outpatient and other methods, with a follow-up period of 5 years and the last follow-up period in October 2022. The relationship between NUF2 and CD52 expression and patient survival rate was analyzed by Kaplan Meier survival curve. (5) Risk factors affecting poor prognosis in NSCLC was analyzed using COX analysis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables, such as NUF2 and CD52 expression, were expressed as counts and percentages [n (%)], and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. Continuous variables, including survival times, were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) and compared between groups using independent-sample t-tests.

Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Patients who were alive at the end of follow-up or lost to follow-up (n = 4) were censored at their last known contact date. Univariate analysis was performed to explore associations between clinicopathological factors and prognosis. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent predictors of poor prognosis.

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Analysis of NUF2 and CD52 expression in NSCLC tissues

NUF2 was positively expressed in the nucleus of NSCLC tissues as brownish-yellow granules with a positive expression rate of 66.67% (48/72), which was much higher than 38.89% (28/72) in the control group (P < 0.05). CD52 was positively expressed in the cytoplasm of NSCLC tissues as brownish granules with a positive expression rate of 62.50% (42/78), which was much higher than 36.11% (26/72) in the control group (P < 0.05, Table 1; Figs. 2 and 3).

Analysis of the relationship between the expression of NUF2 and CD52 and the clinical and pathological characteristics of patients

The expression of NUF2 was associated with differentiation, TNM stage, lymph node metastasis (LNM), and pleural invasion in NSCLC (P < 0.05). CD52 expression was associated with TNM stage, LNM, vascular cancer thrombus and pleural invasion in NSCLC (P < 0.05, Table 2). We further evaluated the relationship of NUF2 and CD52 expression with histological subtype (adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma) and smoking history. Neither NUF2 nor CD52 expression showed significant associations with these variables (all P > 0.05, Table 2). This suggests that the observed correlations with advanced stage, lymph node metastasis, and pleural invasion are unlikely to be confounded by histological subtype or smoking status.

The median survival of patients in the NUF2 positive group was 35 months, which was much shorter than 46 months in NUF2 negative group (P < 0.05. Table 4; Fig. 4). Discussion

Lung cancer is one of the most extensively studied cancers in immunology. With the development of technology, research suggests that lung cancer is driven by genetic and epigenetic abnormalities [10]. The advancement of immunogenomic technology provides a platform for better understanding of lung tumors at the molecular and genomic levels. Therefore, exploring genes that play an important role in molecular diagnosis and targeted therapy of NSCLC will lay the foundation for early diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis improvement of lung cancer.

As the main component of the NDC80 centromere complex, NUF2 is also necessary for stable spindle centromere microtubule attachment and plays a related role by binding to centromere protein E [11]. Studies have found [12] that NUF2 can inhibit cell viability, induce cell apoptosis, and regulate changes in cell cycle distribution. According to relevant reports [13, 14], NUF2 is highly expressed in various malignant tumors and is closely related to poor prognosis in patients. CD52 is a glycoprotein composed of 12 amino acids and an amino acid terminal oligosaccharide linked to asparagine. CD52 is widely expressed in mature lymphocytes, monocytes, dendritic cells, and NK cells by anchoring glycosylphosphatidylinositol to the cell membrane [15]. Research has shown [16] that CD52 can bind to immunoglobulin like lectin 10, inhibiting T cell activation and resulting certain damage to the body’s immune system. In this study, it was found that the expression of NUF2 and CD52 was markedly increased in NSCLC tissue, suggesting that NUF2 and CD52 might be involved in the onset and progression of NSCLC. Further analysis revealed that the positive expression of NUF2 was associated with the degree of differentiation, TNM staging, LNM, and pleural invasion in NSCLC patients. Positive CD52 expression was associated with TNM staging, LNM, vascular cancer thrombus, and pleural invasion in NSCLC patients. The main challenge that lung cancer poses to public health is due to over half of the patients being diagnosed in the middle and late stages, and over two-thirds of patients having regional lymph node involvement or distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis, resulting in poor prognosis [17]. The high expression of NUF2 and CD52 in NSCLC tissue may indicate a significant increase in the number of tumor cells, high tumor staging and differentiation, and a significant worsening of the condition in patients. Therefore, NUF2 and CD52 could be served as good candidates for molecular targeted therapy and cancer diagnosis. We included histological subtype and smoking history in our analysis, these variables were not significantly associated with NUF2 or CD52 expression. Nevertheless, larger studies may be required to fully rule out potential interactions with these factors.”

Surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy is an important method for treating advanced lung cancer. Gemcitabine, cisplatin, and other commonly used chemotherapy drugs can also have good effects, but drug resistance is commonly occurred [18]. Cell resistance is an important cause of tumor recurrence and death, which can significantly reduce the chemotherapy effect of patients, increase the difficulty and pain of treatment. A study has found [19] that compared to stage I patients, the 5-year survival rate of stage III and IV patients is significantly reduced. Moreover, lung cancer often has resistance to standard treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation therapy, coupled with a lack of successful treatment for metastatic diseases, resulting in poor prognosis. In this study, it was found that, compared to those with negative expression, patients with high expression of NUF2 and CD52 had a significantly increased NRR after chemotherapy and much shorter survival time. The expression levels of NUF2 and CD52 might serve as important indicators for predicting the prognosis. The related mechanism of action might be related to the fact that NUF2 and CD52 positive cells were more likely to bring damage to the immune system of the body than negative cells and escape the surveillance of the apoptosis system, thus aggravating the malignancy of tumor cells [20, 21].

In addition, COX analysis in this study found that platinum resistance, lymph node metastasis, pleural invasion, positive expression of NUF2 and CD52 were independent risk factors affecting the poor prognosis of NSCLC. Exploring its causes, the onset and progression of NSCLC is an important process, which is closely related to multiple factors. The biggest obstacle to targeted cancer treatment is the inevitable emergence of drug resistance. Platinum compounds (cisplatin and carboplatin) are the most effective anticancer drugs used in clinical practice, and can have significant effects in both single and combination therapy for cancer. However, the continuous emergence of acquired drug resistance may limit the duration of tumor response [17– 22]. LNM and pleural invasion are considered the main factors determining tumor staging and prognosis, and are also important causes of patient mortality. A previous study suggested [23] that tumor size and LNM were important risk factors for postoperative recurrence in lung cancer patients. According to related study [24], visceral pleural invasion is an important indicator of poor prognosis in NSCLC. Observing these above risk factors is of great significance for evaluating the prognosis of patients.

The mechanistic basis linking NUF2 overexpression to aggressive tumor phenotypes and platinum resistance in NSCLC remains to be fully defined. Several recent studies illustrate complementary pathways that plausibly intersect with NUF2 biology. Network-level analyses and cellular assays in NSCLC implicate PI3K-AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling in tumor cell survival and treatment response, with pharmacologic modulation of these axes decreasing viability and downstream p-AKT/p-ERK signaling in A549 cells. These pathways are well known to promote proliferation and to buffer genotoxic stress, and thus could cooperate with altered mitotic control to favor treatment tolerance [25]. Work focusing on EMT-promoting axes in NSCLC (miR-451/ETV4/MMP13) highlights how transcriptional programs that drive invasion and stage progression can also be associated with therapy resistance, a process that may co-occur with mitotic dysregulation and genomic instability in tumors [26]. Experimental studies in other tumor contexts further support a link between proliferative kinase signaling and chemoresistance: for example, suppression of RAF1 (and downstream MEK/ERK activation) reduces tumor cell proliferation and chemoresistance, indicating that RAF/MEK/ERK activity can modulate sensitivity to cytotoxic agents. Taken together, these lines of evidence support a testable model in which NUF2 overexpression (through its role in kinetochore function, spindle attachment, and cell-cycle progression) may synergize with activated PI3K/AKT or RAF/MEK/ERK signaling to promote tolerance of cisplatin-induced DNA damage, permit escape from checkpoint arrest, and thereby contribute to platinum resistance. Importantly, the cited studies do not directly examine NUF2; therefore, targeted functional work (NUF2 knockdown/overexpression in NSCLC lines with cisplatin challenge; assessment of checkpoint activation, γH2AX foci, and homologous recombination markers; and evaluation of signaling cross-talk with PI3K/AKT or MEK/ERK) will be necessary to validate this mechanistic hypothesis [27].

In this study, chemotherapy resistance was assessed using non-remission rate (NRR) as a surrogate parameter. While ORR and PFS are widely recognized endpoints in prospective clinical trials, our retrospective study design limited the availability of complete follow-up imaging and uniform assessment intervals necessary for accurate PFS calculation. Therefore, we applied remission status after two cycles of chemotherapy, which was consistently available in all cases, as an alternative metric. Although this approach may underestimate subtle disease progression, it provided a reliable and uniform method to analyze associations between NUF2/CD52 expression and platinum resistance. Future prospective studies with comprehensive longitudinal data will be necessary to validate these findings using standard endpoints such as ORR and PFS. In our analysis, prognosis groups were defined by 5-year OS, which is a conventional endpoint in NSCLC outcome studies. However, this binary classification may oversimplify disease trajectories, as some patients may experience recurrence or prolonged survival despite disease progression. Future work should incorporate disease-free survival (DFS) or progression-free survival (PFS) to provide a more nuanced assessment of prognosis.

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort size was relatively small (n = 72), which may limit statistical power and increase the risk of false-negative findings. Although we used multivariate Cox regression to adjust for key clinicopathological variables and reported hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals, the precision of our estimates remains limited. Second, treatment heterogeneity, particularly the inclusion of some stage I/II patients who received adjuvant or salvage chemotherapy, may have introduced confounding when assessing chemoresistance outcomes. Third, as this was a single-center retrospective study, selection bias and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Therefore, our findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Validation in larger, independent, and preferably multicenter cohorts with standardized treatment regimens and prospective follow-up will be necessary to confirm the prognostic and predictive value of NUF2 and CD52 in NSCLC.In general, highly expressed NUF2 and CD52 in NSCLC tissues were associated with platinum resistance, TNM stage, LNM, and pleural invasion, which were independent risk factors affecting the prognosis. NUF2 and CD52 could be used as indicators to predict drug resistance and prognosis of NSCLC patients. However, this study was a retrospective analysis with a small sample size. Prospective studies could be conducted in the future to reduce errors and increase experimental accuracy, providing a certain basis for the treatment of NSCLC in clinical practice.
