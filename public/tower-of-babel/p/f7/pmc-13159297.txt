MRBEE-TL: improving causal effect estimation in multi-ancestry multivariable Mendelian randomization with transfer learning
Authors: Yihe Yang, Xiaofeng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multivariable Mendelian randomization has been largely applied to individuals of European ancestry, due to the larger sample sizes available in European GWAS. We introduce MRBEE-TL, one of the first multi-ancestry multivariable Mendelian randomization methods, which combines transfer learning with bias-corrected estimating equations to improve power in underpowered ancestries and to assess cross-ancestry heterogeneity of disease risk factors. In simulations, MRBEE-TL consistently outperforms MR methods that rely solely on ancestry-specific GWAS data. In real data analyses, MRBEE-TL not only identifies ancestry-consistent and ancestry-specific causal effects missed by conventional methods, but also improves power in underpowered ancestries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04051-9.

Background

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an epidemiological method that utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer whether an exposure causally influences an outcome [1–4]. Since the genotypes of individuals are randomly inherited from their parents and generally do not change during their lifetime, genetic variants are considered to be independent of underlying confounders and hence can be used as IVs to eliminate confounding bias. In addition, Mendelian randomization can be performed by using only genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics [5]. The growing availability of GWAS summary data through public repositories such as the GWAS Catalog [6] and the database of genotypes and phenotypes (dbGaP) [7] has further contributed to the rapid development of MR research. Moreover, MR has been generalized to handle multiple exposures (known as multivariable MR (MVMR)) which enables one to estimate the direct causal effect of each exposure on an outcome [8, 9]. Existing MVMR methods include multivariable inverse-variance weighting (MVIVW) [2], MVMR-lasso [10], genome-wide MR analysis under pervasive pleiotropy (GRAPPLE) [11], multivariable MR constrained maximum likelihood (MVMRcML) [12], spectral-regularized IVW (SRIVW) [13], and MR using bias-corrected estimating equation (MRBEE) [14], among many others.

Understanding the heterogeneity of disease risk factors across ancestries is important both for improving the generalizability of findings and for uncovering ancestry-specific disease mechanisms. Given that genetic architectures differ across ancestries [15], including different allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns, MR is typically performed separately within each ancestry, and the resulting causal estimates are compared across ancestries [16], rather than conducting a universal MR analysis using the trans-ancestry GWAS data. However, this ancestry-stratified strategy has limitations, such as reducing the statistical power for underrepresented ancestries and increasing the risk of selecting weak instruments. In addition, Hemani et al. [17] recently demonstrated that MR is more likely to yield inconsistent causal estimates across ancestries if not performed appropriately. Such bias can arise when the MR analysis does not adequately address the LD and allele frequency differences across ancestries, valid instrument selection [17], and potential gene-environment and gene-gene interactions (G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Es and G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Gs) [18, 19]. Thus, it remains challenging to determine whether observed discrepancies in an MR analysis genuinely reflect ancestry-specific disease mechanisms. As a result, there is a growing need for statistical methods that not only account for the aforementioned sources of bias, but also enable joint analysis across diverse ancestries, providing a unified framework to disentangle ancestry-consistent from ancestry-specific disease mechanisms.

Information obtained in European (EUR) ancestry can be borrowed for studies in non-EUR ancestry, as demonstrated in constructing polygenic risk scores [20] and fine-mapping of causal variants [21]. This strategy has recently been introduced into MR to improve the power of causal effect estimation in underrepresented ancestries. For example, Hou et al. [22] developed trans-ethnic Mendelian randomization (TEMR), one of the first univariable MR (UVMR) methods designed for this purpose. Nevertheless, TEMR assumes the ancestry-specific causal effect estimates jointly follow a multivariate normal distribution, with a correlation structure determined by the correlation matrix estimated from Z-scores of the selected IVs. The choice of IVs, which differs across ancestries due to distinct LD patterns and allele frequencies, can lead to a biased correlation estimate and consequently a biased causal effect estimate.

IV selection is another significant challenge in MR and is considered to be one of the main sources of spurious, inconsistent causal effect estimates across ancestries. To address this issue, Hemani et al. [17] systematically compared multiple IV selection strategies and found that fixed effects meta-analysis (FEMA) is the most effective strategy for mitigating cross-ancestry heterogeneity, as it selects regionally optimal IVs based on combined evidence across ancestries. In addition, as demonstrated by Zhu et al. [19], the genetic effect sizes are much more consistent across ancestries when the genetic variants interacting with environmental factors are excluded. In other words, IVs exhibiting horizontally pleiotropic effects for the same exposure across ancestries may reflect underlying G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×G or G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×E. An MR-G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×E framework [17, 19] can be used to identify and remove such variants, improving the robustness and generalizability of multi-ancestry MR analyses without relying on external G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×E or G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×G summary data. These improvements in IV selection have been implemented in the cross-ancestry Mendelian randomization (CAMeRa) platform [17]; however, a key limitation is that CAMeRa remains primarily built upon existing UVMR methods.

While improperly conducted MR analyses may yield spurious heterogeneity in causal effects across ancestries, genuine heterogeneity can also arise from environmental and cultural differences, such as diet, climate, and lifestyle [23]. For instance, Smith et al. [24] reported ancestry-specific polygenic mechanisms for type 2 diabetes (T2D), highlighting that East Asians (EAS) ancestry exhibit elevated lipodystrophy-related polygenic risk, which contributes to increased T2D susceptibility even at lower body mass index (BMI) levels. Such genuine differences in disease etiology may lead to substantial heterogeneity in causal effect estimates, making their detection an important scientific goal in MR research. Regarding this issue, Li and Morrison [25] conducted a comprehensive investigation using GRAPPLE as the causal effect estimator and identified several exposures with causal effects that differ markedly in magnitude or in direction across ancestries. Accordingly, it is essential to develop MR methods that not only offer adequate statistical power to detect causal effects, but also enable the identification of ancestry-specific heterogeneity in effect sizes.

To address these issues, we develop MRBEE-TL, to the best of our knowledge, the first multi-ancestry MVMR method. Specifically, MRBEE-TL extends our previous MVMR approach, MRBEE [14], by incorporating transfer learning [26]. MRBEE-TL designates EUR ancestry as the source ancestry and treats EAS, African (AFR), or other non-EUR ancestries as target ancestries. By leveraging causal effect estimates from the source ancestry, MRBEE-TL improves statistical power for underrepresented ancestries when causal effects are consistent across ancestries, while remaining unbiased when effects are ancestry-specific. Moreover, MRBEE-TL adopts FEMA for IV selection while excluding variants likely involved in G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Gs or G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Es to mitigate bias arising from cross-ancestry heterogeneity and invalid IVs.

In our real data analysis of five stroke subtypes, MRBEE-TL not only observed previously reported ancestry-consistent causal effects [17], but also uncovered new biologically plausible causal exposure-outcome pairs of EAS and AFR through transfer learning. In addition, we find that although the dominant causal exposures vary by stroke subtypes, their estimated effect sizes remain consistent across ancestries. These findings suggest that discrepancies in observed causal effects may partly reflect differences in stroke subtype composition across ancestry cohorts, rather than true heterogeneity in causal effect sizes.

In the target ancestry, MRBEE-TL is employed to estimate the causal effects by combining several key components: an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _2$$\end{document}ℓ2 loss function, a bias-correction term that accounts for estimation errors in GWAS summary statistics, a penalty function to reduce pleiotropic effects, and a bi-directional selection penalty (BDSP) [27] that simultaneously shrinks causal effect estimates toward zero and toward the source causal effect estimates (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). From a Bayesian perspective, a BDSP can be interpreted as placing a bimodal prior on each target causal effect, centered at zero and the corresponding source estimate (Methods). This use of BDSP enables the effective borrowing of information from the source ancestry, thereby enhancing the accuracy of causal effect estimation in the target ancestry. Finally, inference is performed through stability selection to estimate the covariance matrix of target causal effect estimates. The outputs of MRBEE-TL include the causal effect estimates, the corresponding covariance matrix, and the entry-wise posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs), among many others.

Compared with traditional MVMR methods restricted to a single ancestry, MRBEE-TL leverages well-powered source summary statistics to construct an empirical prior, thereby enhancing statistical power in underrepresented ancestries. Unlike trans-ethnic UVMR approaches such as TEMR, which estimate total causal effects without accounting for correlations among exposures, MRBEE-TL operates within a multivariable framework and yields direct causal estimates that incorporate the dependency structure among exposures. This distinction is illustrated in Fig. 1, where panel B shows the UVMR framework and panel C presents the MVMR structure assumed by MRBEE-TL. Moreover, MRBEE-TL employs a BDSP which allows each exposure to adaptively determine whether or not to borrow strength from the source ancestry. This avoids imposing unverifiable assumptions about the correlation of causal effects across ancestries, such as equating it to cross-ancestry genetic correlation [22]. Collectively, these features make MRBEE-TL a flexible and powerful tool for disentangling shared and ancestry-specific disease mechanisms.

Simulation

We compared MRBEE-TL with several existing MVMR methods, including the multivariable extensions of MR-Median [2], MR-Lasso [10], GRAPPLE [11], and MRBEE [14]. Non-robust MVMR methods such as IVW [28] and MR-Egger [1] were not considered, as they are known to be susceptible to bias under horizontal pleiotropy. For traditional MVMR methods, GWAS summary statistics from the EAS ancestry were used directly. Real data analysis: causal effect estimates

We applied MRBEE-TL to perform MVMR analyses across five stroke subtypes: all strokes including both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes (AS), all ischemic stroke (AIS), large-artery atherosclerotic stroke (LAS), cardioembolic stroke (CES), and small vessel stroke (SVS), where the GWAS summary data are provided by Mishra et al. [32]. The exposures included atrial fibrillation (AF), serum albumin (ALB), BMI, drinks per week (DRNK), HDL cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL cholesterol (LDL-C), systolic blood pressure (SBP), smoking initiation (SMK), triglyceride (TG), and T2D, all of which are recognized as common cardiometabolic risk factors across the outcomes of interest. GWAS summary statistics were derived from the following studies [15, 33–40]. The data prepossessing procedure is available at Methods and Additional file 1.

Figure 6 summarizes the causal effect estimates across stroke subtypes in three ancestries (EUR, EAS, and AFR). By leveraging EUR as the source population, MRBEE-TL substantially improved the consistency of causal estimates across ancestries relative to ancestry-specific analyses. Overall, most identified exposure–subtype associations exhibited concordant effect directions across the three ancestries, despite moderate variation in effect magnitudes, supporting a largely shared genetic architecture underlying stroke etiology. Notably, LDL-C showed consistent risk effects for LAS, AIS and AS across all three ancestries, with several associations in EAS and AFR recovered only through MRBEE-TL due to improved statistical power. SMK emerged as a systemic risk factor for AS and AIS across populations, consistent with its broad vascular impact [41]. For LAS, a coherent atherosclerotic risk profile was observed across ancestries, characterized by elevated LDL-C and SBP, increased T2D risk, and a protective effect of HDL-C. In contrast, SVS displayed a distinct lipid pattern, with TG identified as a consistent risk factor across populations, highlighting etiological differences between LAS and SVS. We also observed that HDL-C has no causal contribution on stroke subtypes except on LAS. Real data analysis: uncertainty characterization using empirical PIPs

We observed several noteworthy patterns in the uncertainty of MRBEE-TL estimates that warrant attention. Specifically, some exposure–outcome pairs exhibited consistent causal directions across ancestries (e.g., ALB–AS, ALB–AIS, and ALB–LAS in AFR), yet were accompanied by relatively large SEs. Conversely, certain ancestry-specific associations (e.g., DRNK–AS and DRNK–AIS in EAS) also showed visibly larger SEs than those obtained from ancestry-specific analyses. This behavior arose from the variable selection mechanism employed by MRBEE-TL through SuSiE within the bootstrap resampling framework. Specifically, across bootstrap replicates, SuSiE could select ancestry-specific effects in some subsamples, while in others it selected either the source population estimate or shrunk the effect exactly to zero. Consequently, the bootstrap distribution of the causal effect estimate exhibited a mixture structure, and standard hypothesis testing based on asymptotic normality was not valid.

Figure 7A illustrates this phenomenon for the DRNK–AIS association in EAS. Approximately 34% of bootstrap replicates yielded estimates that were exactly shrunk to zero, while the remaining 66% concentrated around the ancestry-specific estimate. The frequency with which the causal effect is nonzero across bootstrap samples can therefore be interpreted as an empirical PIP. Figure 7B shows a scatter plot of empirical PIPs versus p-values, revealing a strong negative linear relationship, while Fig. 7E present the corresponding numerical summaries. In such settings, we suggest that empirical PIP can serve as a complementary measure to conventional hypothesis testing. In particular, exposure–outcome pairs with relatively high PIP values (e.g., PIP > 0.6) are more likely to correspond to genuinely non-null causal effects, rather than null associations. Discussion

Current multi-ancestry MVMR analyses face two major challenges. The first is how to address the limited statistical power caused by the relatively small sample sizes in non-EUR populations. The second is how to distinguish ancestry-consistent causal effects from ancestry-specific ones, which may provide important insights into ancestry-specific disease mechanisms and help inform population-tailored public health policies. To resolve these challenges, we propose MRBEE-TL, an extension of MRBEE that incorporates transfer learning to simultaneously identify ancestry-consistent, ancestry-specific, and non-causal effects. From a mathematical perspective, MRBEE-TL can outperform traditional MVMR approaches as long as a subset of the causal effects is shared between the source and target ancestries [26], an assumption that is broadly supported by growing empirical evidence [17]. Furthermore, MRBEE-TL integrates bias correction and pleiotropy detection, improving robustness in the presence of sample overlap bias and horizontal pleiotropy bias frequently encountered in empirical studies. To reduce bias introduced by inappropriate IV selection, we employ FEMA for IV selection and exclude variants that exhibit G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Es and G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Gs.

Our real data analysis suggests that both causal risk factors and their corresponding effect sizes for stroke are largely consistent across ancestral populations. For example, while Hemani et al. [17] reported LDL-C as a causal factor only for LAS using UVMR, MRBEE-TL revealed consistent causal effects of LDL-C on AS, AIS, and LAS across all three ancestries, indicating that LDL-C likely influences these phenotypes through shared causal pathways. Similarly, SMK showed consistent causal associations with AS and AIS across ancestries, with additional evidence of ancestry-consistent effects in LAS and SVS. Given that smoking accelerates atherogenesis through endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and oxidative stress [41], its broad impact across multiple stroke subtypes is biologically plausible. In contrast, TG was identified as a causal risk factor specifically for SVS, highlighting an etiological distinction between SVS and LAS. LAS shares core pathological mechanisms with atherosclerosis, in which LDL-rich lipoproteins play a central role in plaque formation and progression [44]. SVS, however, is primarily characterized by microvascular injury and lipohyalinosis, processes in which TG-rich lipoprotein remnants and their associated inflammatory effects are thought to play a more prominent role [45]. Together, these findings underscore both a shared genetic architecture of stroke across ancestries and distinct lipid-mediated mechanisms underlying different stroke subtypes.

MRBEE-TL also uncovered significant ancestry-specific heterogeneity. First, as also pointed out by Hemani et al. [17], some of the observed cross-ancestry discrepancies in causal effect estimates, particularly for composite outcomes like all stroke or all ischemic stroke, may reflect differences in subtype composition rather than true heterogeneity in causal mechanisms. For instance, the estimated effects of SBP on AS and AIS were consistently smaller in EUR compared to EAS. In contrast, SBP exhibited broadly consistent effects across ancestries for LAS and SVS. Indeed, the total number of EUR cases for these specific subtypes was only 24,114, substantially less than the 73,652 AS cases and 62,100 AIS cases, which may partly explain their different contributions to overall stroke risk across ancestries. A similar pattern was observed for T2D. While its effects on AS and AIS were consistently detected in EUR and AFR, T2D showed consistent causal associations across all three ancestries for the more specific subtypes LAS and CES. In addition, our analysis identified DRNK as a potential ancestry-specific risk factor for stroke in EAS, a finding that, to our knowledge, has rarely been discussed in the literature. Further investigation of ancestry differences in the genetic architecture of alcohol consumption and its downstream disease mechanisms may offer important insight and represent a promising direction for future research.

While MRBEE-TL adopts the FEMA proposed by Hemani et al. [17] for IV selection, it is noted that this IV selection strategy was originally developed for UVMR analysis. MRBEE-TL extends it to MVMR by jointly testing the association of each variant with multiple exposures across ancestries with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2-statistic. For example, in our analysis involving three ancestries and ten exposures, the joint test was conducted using a 30-degree-of-freedom \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2-distribution. Furthermore, MRBEE-TL excludes IVs with potential G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Es identified either through external interaction studies or via an MR-G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×E framework. Finally, MRBEE-TL builds upon the MVMR framework, representing a significant advance over TEMR and the platform CAMeRa, both of which are based on the UVMR framework.

Both TEMR and MRBEE-TL employ a transfer learning framework to improve power for underrepresented ancestry. Nevertheless, MRBEE-TL is an MVMR method that estimates the direct causal effect, whereas TEMR is a UVMR method that estimates the total causal effect, which is the sum of the direct causal effect and the mediation effects through other exposures. While both quantities are interpretable within a causal inference framework, they reflect different aspects of the underlying biology [8]. In our opinion, an MVMR method could be more suitable than a UVRM method if the goal is disentangling the independent contributions of multiple, potentially correlated exposures. In addition, in this comparison, MRBEE-TL identified more causal exposure–outcome pairs for AFR (19 vs. 8, Additional file 1: Fig. S7), possibly because its use of transfer learning allows for more effective borrowing of information from EUR ancestry.

MRBEE-TL also has several limitations. First, although MRBEE-TL identified several additional exposure-outcome pairs through the SuSiE-based variable selection procedure, such as the LDL-C-AS and LDL-C-AIS associations in AFR, their 95% confidence intervals were wide and included zero, indicating a lack of formal statistical significance. This phenomenon likely arises from the same underlying mechanism: across stability selection replicates, the exposure was not consistently classified as ancestry-consistent, leading to a mixture distribution of the estimated causal effects. In such settings, conventional hypothesis-testing-based p-values may be limited in their ability to characterize the significance. We therefore propose two complementary summary measures, both derived from bootstrap samples: the empirical PIPs of a nonzero causal effect, and the empirical PIP of a nonzero difference between source and target causal effect estimates. These quantities capture, respectively, the stability of effect inclusion and the evidence for ancestry-specific deviation beyond what is reflected by point estimates and SEs. We suggest that exposure-outcome pairs with empirical PIP values exceeding a reasonable threshold (e.g., PIP > 0.6) may be regarded as having supportive evidence for a non-null causal effect or meaningful source-target consistency, even when formal statistical significance is not achieved under the current sample size. Second, MRBEE-TL treats the source causal effect estimates as fixed inputs, without jointly updating them alongside the target estimates. As a result, for exposures with truly ancestry-consistent effects, MRBEE-TL does not leverage information from the target ancestry, leading to a potential loss in statistical power. A unified transfer learning framework that jointly estimates causal effects across multiple ancestries could address this limitation. Third, as Hemani et al. [17] emphasized, IV selection and other improper quality controls in multi-ancestry MR are subject to several inherent challenges. MRBEE-TL, like other MR methods, relies on the validity of IV selection and cannot inherently correct for biases arising from suboptimal IV selection. Therefore, the ancestry-specific heterogeneity identified by MRBEE-TL should also be interpreted with caution, as it may partially reflect biases introduced during IV selection rather than true biological differences.

Conclusions

MRBEE-TL serves as a novel multi-ancestry MVMR framework that integrates transfer learning [26] with the traditional MVMR method [14]. MRBEE-TL resolves two long-standing challenges in multi-ancestry MVMR analysis: 1) overcoming the limited statistical power inherent to underrepresented ancestries and 2) enabling rigorous delineation of genuine cross-ancestry heterogeneity. Across extensive simulations and a real data analysis regarding strokes, MRBEE-TL consistently surpassed leading MVMR and trans-ethnic UVMR methods, revealing additional, biologically credible risk factors in AFR and EAS ancestries. In summary, by enabling rigorous, scalable causal inference across diverse populations, the open-source MRBEE-TL platform advances genomic medicine and promotes equity in genetic epidemiology.

To tune the shrinkage parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}λ, we use a data-driven criterion such as the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) [51], which balances model fit and complexity while accounting for the large model space, allowing the method to select more informative, weak-to-moderate signals that would otherwise be overshrunk under fixed penalization. Similar to the Lasso, other commonly used variable selection penalties also admit corresponding Bayesian interpretations [52], and MRBEE-TL chooses SuSiE [53] among the existing ones.

Although we use lasso as an example to illustrate the principle of BDSP, we actually use SuSiE [53, 58] as the penalty \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{p}(\theta )$$\end{document}p~(θ) in the minimization (14) to identify non-zero entries of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\delta }$$\end{document}δ. In practice, MRBEE-TL adopts the EBIC [51] to select both the optimal number of single effects L in SuSiE and the tuning parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}τ in MCP.

SuSiE’s theoretical PIP is computed via variational inference and represents the PIP under the model’s assumptions. Bootstrap empirical PIP measures the frequency with which an exposure is selected into a level-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}ρ credible set (default to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho =0.9$$\end{document}ρ=0.9) across resampled datasets, quantifying signal stability under data perturbation. When model assumptions are violated (e.g., multiple weak effects or heavy tails of errors), SuSiE may be overconfident about certain variables, while bootstrap exposes this instability, leading to empirical PIPs lower than theoretical PIPs. That is, bootstrap empirical PIP is more robust to model misspecification. Stroke analysis

We applied SBayesRC [65] to impute missing variants using reference panels containing approximately 7.35 million SNPs for EUR, 6.0 million SNPs for AFR, and 4.9 million SNPs for EAS. Next, we followed the idea of FEMA to select IVs [17]. Specifically, for each variant, we computed the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2-statistic in combining association evidence of EUR, EAS, and AFR, and the corresponding p-values were calculated using a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2-distribution with 30 degrees of freedom. The covariance matrix of Z-scores of GWAS effects was estimated from LDSC [46] entry-by-entry. In C+T, we used an alternative LD reference panel of 9,680 European individuals randomly selected from the UK Biobank. Although EAS and AFR individuals were not included, this is consistent with the principle of FEMA described by Hemani et al. [17], which selects variants with strong effects in all ancestries. The covariance matrices of estimation errors used in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2 test were estimated from genome-wide SNPs with statistically insignificant associations [66]. Furthermore, IVs were clumped using PLINK [67] with a 1 Mb window, a threshold of P<5E-8, and a threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^2{<}0.001$$\end{document}r2<0.001. We further excluded the IVs that were in the loci with G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Es [19, 55]. This resulted in 2,870 IVs used in multi-ancestry MVMR analysis.

In addition to the above approaches for excluding variants with potential G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Es and G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×Gs, we also compared two baseline strategies: (i) performing the analysis without any variant exclusion, and (ii) removing ancestry-heterogeneous variants using our proposed MR-G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×E framework. We further explored ancestry-specific IV selection. For each ancestry, we applied an exposure-wise joint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2 test with 10 degrees of freedom and performed LD clumping using ancestry-specific LD reference panels, after which IVs overlapping loci with evidence of G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×E effects were excluded. For this analysis, we used UK Biobank participants from the EAS and AFR ancestries, with sample sizes of 1,816 and 3,935 individuals, respectively.

We considered GRAPPLE and TEMR in the real data analysis. GRAPPLE and MRBEE-TL used the same inputs, namely GWAS summary statistics and the covariance matrix of estimation errors, while all other tuning parameters were set to their default values. For TEMR, we retained the FEMA-based IV selection strategy but applied an exposure-wise joint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document}χ2 test with 3 degrees of freedom. Clumping and thresholding were performed using PLINK, with a 1 Mb window, a genome-wide significance threshold of P\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<5\times 10^{-8}$$\end{document}<5×10-8, and an LD threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^2<0.001$$\end{document}r2<0.001. In addition, IVs overlapping known G\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×E loci were excluded in an exposure-wise manner.
