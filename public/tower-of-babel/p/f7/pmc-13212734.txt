Detoxification of lead (pb²⁺) from simulated and real apple juice using chitosan/bacterial cellulose/MIL-100(Fe) aerogel microbeads
Authors: Zahra Moradi, Milad Rouhi, Mehdi Farhoodi, Fahimeh Ebrahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, several types of aerogel beads were prepared using different ratios of chitosan (CS), bacterial cellulose nanofibers (BCN), and the MIL-100(Fe) metal-organic framework. The prepared beads were then compared in terms of their mechanical stability in water (percentage of retained weight) and their lead removal efficiency from simulated apple juice to select the best adsorbent for further investigation. The selected adsorbent was then used to remove Pb²⁺ ions from both simulated and real apple juice. The effects of contact time, lead concentration, and adsorbent content on Pb²⁺ adsorption were assessed. The effect of integrating MIL-100(Fe) on the surface area, average pore width, and total pore volume of the beads was revealed by the BET (Brunauer, Emmett, and Teller) analysis. Lead adsorption on the beads was confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). The results showed that 95.6% of Pb²⁺ (10 mg/L) was adsorbed after 24 h using 2.5 mg of CS/BCN/MIL beads in an apple juice simulant. Lead adsorption behavior on the beads was well-characterized by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and the Langmuir isotherm model. Recyclability studies demonstrated that CS–BCN (90:10)–MIL-100(50%) beads maintained their high lead removal efficiency even after three cycles.

Introduction

Elements having an atomic density above 4–5 g/cm³ or an atomic weight over 40 amu are regarded as heavy metals. Some heavy metals—such as iron, zinc, copper, manganese, cobalt, molybdenum, chromium, silicon, nickel, and selenium —are essential micronutrients, exhibiting toxicity only when consumed in amounts above specific thresholds. In contrast, elements like arsenic (As), mercury (Hg), lead (Pb), and cadmium (Cd) are non-essential and can exert toxic effects even at minimal exposure levels1. These metals exist in water, soil, and the atmosphere at varying levels. They can also result from human activities such as industry, agriculture, and vehicle exhaust, or contamination during food processing and storage through processing equipment and packaging containers, leading to residues in food products2. Humans are exposed to these metals through the environment, by eating contaminated food, or by drinking contaminated water. The accumulation of toxic metals in the body can lead to cardiovascular diseases, neurotoxicity, brain and kidney damage, and carcinogenesis3,4. Processed fruits, such as juices, can be potential sources of toxic heavy metals. The occurrence of these elements in juices may result from their accumulation in the concentrate, water, and sugar used during processing5. Therefore, the assessment of heavy metals in juices is an important safety consideration. In particular, juices are more susceptible to lead contamination. Most lead compounds have low solubility in water at neutral pH, but they dissolve well in acidic environments such as those found in juices6. The maximum lead level for both original and reconstituted juices, according to the European Union, is 0.05 ppm7. Codex has set different maximum lead levels for various juices. The maximum permissible lead level for berry and small fruits is reported to be 0.05 ppm, while for other juices it is 0.03 ppm8. In 2004, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) established a lead limit of 50 ppb for juices.

A variety of methods are employed to remove lead from solutions, including membrane separation, ion exchange, chemical precipitation, biological methods, advanced oxidation processes, and adsorption. Due to its cost-effectiveness, operational simplicity, and excellent performance, adsorption is considered a favorable method among these options9,10. In general, a suitable adsorbent for heavy metal removal should have the following characteristics: stability under the conditions used (such as temperature, pH, and hydraulic shock), selectivity for target metals, ease of separation from the solution, the possibility of regeneration using simple methods (such as the application of acid or alkali), and appropriate porosity, diffusion capacity, and mechanical strength11. Heavy metals can be removed from polluted solutions using various adsorbents, such as activated carbon, zeolite, polymers, and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs). The major concerns with most of these adsorbents are the use of chemical solvents, complex production processes, and their non-biodegradability. Thus, developing adsorbents that are high-performing, cost-effective, and biodegradable is essential9.

Chitosan is known to be a polyaminosaccharide produced by removing acetyl groups from chitin. It exhibits favorable biological characteristics, including compatibility with living tissues, natural degradability, biological safety, and a strongly cationic character. Chitosan has good adsorption capacity due to its abundant amine groups, which can interact with metal ions to form chelates10,12. However, chitosan alone cannot be used effectively as an adsorbent because it has low mechanical strength and is unstable in acidic environments13,14. Combining chitosan with reinforcing fillers such as BCN can enhance its mechanical properties15. BCN, synthesized by Acetobacter xylinum, is purer than cellulose. The mechanical strength and an extensive surface area of BCN are derived from its 3D nanofibrous structure16. The adsorption capacity of the BCN/CS composite can be further improved by incorporating metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) into the composite formulation17.

MOFs, a novel class of organometallic frameworks, have recently gained significant attention for their applications in pollution removal. They are composed of metal ions and organic ligands, serving as nodes and pillars, respectively18,19. Their large specific surface area, pore structure, chemical tunability, ability to interact with diverse compounds, and abundant metal adsorption sites make them highly efficient materials for removing a variety of pollutants20. Among the various MOFs, MIL-100(Fe) (Materials Institute Lavoisier) is considered a highly promising candidate for lead removal, owing to its excellent chemical stability in aqueous media and superior biocompatibility. In vivo studies have demonstrated its undetectable toxicity following intravenous injection, even at high doses21.

Biosorbents can be fabricated in various forms, including microbeads, nanoparticles, powders, flakes, fibers, and membranes22. Small particles, such as powders or flakes, may exhibit good adsorption rates at the laboratory scale, but they are not recommended for industrial use due to their low porosity and surface area, as well as the potential for membrane clogging11. Among various adsorbent forms, gel beads are widely regarded as the most efficient for removing metal ions from aqueous solutions. The attractive features of this type of adsorbent include its highly porous structure with a large surface area, reusability, and ease of separation by simple filtration due to its relatively larger size compared to powdered adsorbents23.

Various methods, such as extrusion or the droplet method, spray, and emulsification, have been used to produce gel beads. The method of producing the beads should be cost-effective, flexible, repeatable, and simple. The extrusion or droplet method is the most common and simplest technique used to produce gel beads. This method is based on injecting charged polymer droplets into an oppositely charged gelling solution using a syringe. Gel beads are classified into nano-, micro-, macro-, and millimeter-sized beads based on their structure, properties, and applications. Beads produced by the extrusion method typically have sizes larger than 1 mm23. The main problems with gel beads are their instability in acidic solutions and poor mechanical strength24. The chemical stability of microbeads can be enhanced through internal and surface crosslinking using agents such as glutaraldehyde, hexamethylene diisocyanate, and epichlorohydrin. As the most commonly used crosslinker, glutaraldehyde links chitosan, cellulose, and MIL by forming covalent bonds with the primary amine groups in chitosan and the –OH moieties in BCN and MIL. Moreover, glutaraldehyde can participate in adsorption via its free aldehyde groups25.

Extensive studies have been conducted on the adsorption of heavy metals using diverse materials, including biomass-based plant materials (waste tea, coconut shell, peat moss, wheat, and rice husk, activated carbon from Acacia senegal (L.) Willd pods26–28, plantain peels29, papaya seeds30, as well as microbial and biopolymeric materials such as cellulose9,31–34, chitosan3,36,36, alginate37,38, and Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)39,40.

Considering the high consumption of apple juice by both children and adults in the U.S., the FDA, in its draft guidance issued on April 27, 2022, reduced the permissible lead levels to 10 ppb for apple juice and 20 ppb for ready-to-drink juices containing apple juice41.

Although removing lead from apple juices is important, very few studies have been conducted in this area. To the best of our knowledge, only two studies have specifically focused on lead removal from apple juice. In one study, lead removal was achieved through a biological method using lactic acid bacteria composited with EDTA and Fe₂O₃42, while in another, a chitosan–UiO-66 aerogel film was employed ⁴². Considering the significance of this issue, further research is needed in this field. In these studies, the qualitative properties of fruit juice after adsorption were evaluated by measuring parameters such as total soluble solids, light transmittance, color, titratable acidity, and total sugar content. However, none of these studies examined the quality characteristics of fruit juice in terms of polyphenol content, antioxidant activity, nutrient element levels, or vitamin C content, which are important nutritional attributes of apple juice.

In this study, the properties of MIL-100(Fe) and the optimal beads were investigated using SEM-EDX, FTIR, and BET analyses. The lead adsorption behavior and mechanism in an apple juice simulant (pH = 3.8) were first investigated using isotherm and kinetic models to determine optimal conditions in terms of time, concentration, and adsorbent dosage. Subsequently, the performance of the beads in removing lead from real apple juice was evaluated. Additionally, the impact of the beads on apple juice quality parameters, including polyphenol content, antioxidant capacity, ascorbic acid levels, and essential element content, was assessed.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chitosan (medium molecular weight; degree of deacetylation 87%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. A 1% gel of bacterial cellulose nanofiber (purity ≥ 99.9%; diameter 20–50 nm; aspect ratio 200–700; crystallinity 85–90%; crystallite size 5–7 nm), produced by bacterial synthesis in appropriate culture media followed by chemical treatments, was purchased from Nanonovin Corporation (Iran). Sodium hydroxide pellets, trimesic acid (benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid), iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O), lead(II) nitrate, Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) were purchased from Merck (Germany).

MIL-100 (Fe) synthesis

For the production of MIL-100 (Fe), solutions A and B were prepared. Solution A was prepared by solubilizing 1.6 g of trimesic acid in 23 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide. To obtain Solution B, 3.168 g of FeSO₄·7 H₂O was dissolved in 98 mL of deionized water. Thereafter, the colorless Solution A was added dropwise to the green Solution B, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. During stirring, the solution color changed from green to orange-brown, attributed to the oxidation of Fe²⁺ ion21. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 15 min, the precipitates were washed with a mixture of distilled water and ethanol, and then dried at room temperature to obtain the end product. Preparation of aerogel beads

A 2% (w/v) chitosan solution was prepared in acetic acid and added dropwise into a 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution to form chitosan beads. The beads were kept in the NaOH solution overnight to ensure complete gelation, then collected using filter paper and washed several times with distilled water to remove residual NaOH43. Other beads with varying ratios of chitosan, bacterial cellulose nanofibers, and MIL-100 were synthesized following the same procedure as the chitosan beads. The prepared adsorbents and their component percentages are listed in Table 1. To improve acid stability, the beads were immersed in a 5% glutaraldehyde solution at a ratio of 1 g to 1.5 mL and maintained at 25 °C for 24 h. Finally, the beads were freeze-dried at − 20 °C44. The hydrogel beads had an average diameter of approximately 2 mm, with the size consistent across all batches. The preparation method was reproducible, producing beads of similar size in each synthesis.

Evaluation of Pb²⁺ adsorption capacity of the prepared beads

Initially, the beads were added at a ratio of 2.5 mg per 10 mL of the acidic lead solution (10 mg/L, pH 3.8) and shaken. The detoxification time and initial lead concentration were kept constant for all solutions. After the detoxification process, the beads were separated using filter paper, and the adsorbed lead content was determined by ICP-OES45,46.

Assessment of the mechanical stability of the beads

To evaluate the mechanical stability of the aerogel beads, the weight of the dry beads was measured before and after adsorption. For this purpose, after the adsorption process, the solution was passed through a standard No. 10 sieve (2 mm mesh size). During sieving, fine bead particles were lost, and only the remaining beads collected on the sieve were dried in an oven at 40 °C. Characterization

The functional groups of the synthesized MOF and the optimal beads, before and after Pb(II) adsorption, were analyzed using FT-IR spectroscopy. A surface and pore analyzer (Tristar II 3020, Micromeritics, USA) was used to measure the surface area and average pore size of the samples by nitrogen adsorption–desorption at 77.35°K. To remove moisture, the samples were heated at 120 °C for 12 h before analysis. The surface area and average pore size were determined using the BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) and BJH (Barrett–Joyner–Halenda) methods, respectively. The samples’ morphology and elemental profile were characterized using FE-SEM (TESCAN MIRA 3 LMU) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), respectively. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Varian 730-ES) was used to determine the Pb(II) concentration.

Adsorption studies in simulated apple juice

For kinetic adsorption studies, 2.5 mg of beads were added to 10 mL of Pb(II) solution (10 mg/L) and shaken. Samples were collected at various time intervals (10 min, 1 h, 6 h, 24 h, and 48 h) to determine the residual Pb(II) concentration using ICP-OES. After determining the optimum time for the adsorption process, the adsorption isotherm was determined by varying the Pb(II) concentration from 10 to 50 ppm while keeping the amount of adsorbent constant. Then, different weights of adsorbent, ranging from 1 mg to 2.5 mg, were added to aqueous solutions with a constant Pb(II) concentration (10 mg/L). All solutions were adjusted to pH 3.8 with acetic acidto simulate the acidity of apple juice. Recyclability of adsorbent

Recyclability studies of the beads were conducted by adding the Pb(II)-adsorbed beads to a 0.1 M HCl solution, which was stirred for 2 h at room temperature to desorb Pb(II) from the bead surface. Following separation from the acidic solution, the beads were thoroughly washed with deionized (DI) water and reused for Pb(II) adsorption. The adsorption–desorption cycle was performed four consecutive times.

Adsorption experiments in apple juice

To evaluate the Pb(II) removal efficiency of beads, 10 ppm of Pb(II) was spiked into commercially obtained Tetra Pak apple juice sourced from a regional market in Tehran, Iran. Then, for every 10 mL of Pb(II)-contaminated apple juice, 2.5 mg of the adsorbent was added to a beaker and stirred for 24 h. After this period, the beads were separated from the apple juice, and the residual Pb content in the juice was quantified using ICP. The Pb(II) removal percentage was calculated using Eq. 4.

Characterization of apple juice after adsorption

Total polyphenols content (TPC)

Apple juice is a rich source of polyphenols, which significantly contribute to its antioxidant activity47. The TPC of apple juice was measured before and after adsorption, following a modified method by Savatović, Tepić et al. (2009). 0.5 mL of apple juice (diluted 10-fold) was mixed with an equal volume (0.5 mL) of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu’s solution. Following a 5-minute incubation, the mixture was combined with 5 mL of 7.5% sodium carbonate solution. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 90 min, after which the sample’s absorbance at 760 nm was recorded using a spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 2). Gallic acid (10, 20, 30, 50, 80, 100, and 200 mg/L) was adopted as a reference standard to construct a calibration plot, and the equivalent concentration of gallic acid per mL of apple juice was established to express TPC (R² = 0.998)48.

Antioxidant potential

To reveal the effect of the adsorbent on the antioxidant potential of apple juice, the measurement was carried out using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) protocol reported by Belete, et al49., with slight modifications. A 160 mg/L DPPH solution was prepared in methanol. 2 mL of DPPH solution and 4 mL of methanol were added to 1 mL of apple juice. The control sample contained 2 mL of DPPH and 4 mL of methanol. All specimens were kept at ambient temperature and away from light. After 30 min, the sample’s absorbance at 517 nm was measured with a spectrophotometer. To construct the calibration curve, ascorbic acid solutions with concentrations of 5–50 mg/L were prepared from a 200 mg/L stock solution.

Vitamin C Quantification

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Young Lin model) was employed to quantify vitamin C in the original apple juice and in samples treated with the beads according to the method described by Suárez-Jacobo, et al50. For this purpose, the extraction solution (3% w/v metaphosphoric acid and 8% w/v acetic acid) was homogenized with an equal volume of apple juice. After centrifugation and filtration of the mixture, 25 µL of the filtrate was injected into a reversed-phase C18 column (0.1 g/L H₂SO₄ as eluent) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Vitamin C was detected using UV–Vis spectroscopy at 245 nm, and its concentration was expressed as mg per 100 mL of apple juice.

Essential elements content

To investigate the effect of the adsorbent on the main essential elements in apple juice (calcium, magnesium, potassium, and phosphorus), 10 mL of juice containing 10 ppm Pb(II) was treated with 2.5 mg of beads. After 24 h of shaking, the content of the elements was determined using ICP and compared with that in untreated apple juice.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in three repetitions. Statistical results were presented as mean ± SD, and significance differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05). A t-test was conducted to compare treated and untreated apple juice samples, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test to determine statistically significant differences among the means (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Evaluation of Pb²⁺ adsorption capacity of the prepared beads

As shown in Fig. 2, beads containing MIL-100(Fe) exhibited higher lead adsorption capacities than those without it. This improvement is attributed to the extremely high surface area of MIL-100(Fe), which offers numerous active sites for lead ions51. The results also indicated that increasing the BCN content (i.e., decreasing the chitosan content) resulted in a reduction in lead removal. This finding indicates that the amine groups of chitosan play a more significant role in lead ion adsorption than the hydroxyl moieties of cellulose, which can be attributed to the lower electronegativity of nitrogen atoms compared with oxygen52. These findings are consistent with studies Li, et al17. and Yang, et al53.

Assessment of mechanical stability of the beads

Mechanical stability is an important factor for the reusability of adsorbents. Beads with higher mechanical stability can withstand stress and deformation during the adsorption process and remain intact in water31. Table 2 summarizes the retained weight percentages of the beads. Consequently, beads containing bacterial cellulose nanofibers exhibited higher retained weights, indicating improved stability. The addition of the metal–organic framework did not have a significant effect on the retained weight of the adsorbent24,51. Based on the previous results comparing lead removal efficiency, the chitosan–bacterial cellulose nanofiber (90:10)–MIL-100 (50%) adsorbent, which exhibited the highest mechanical stability and lead removal efficiency, was selected for further investigations.

FTIR spectroscopy

Figure 3 illustrates the FTIR spectra of the samples. In the FTIR spectrum of MIL-100(Fe), the absorption band at 470 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibration of Fe–O. Two absorption bands at 712 and 760 cm⁻¹, known as the fingerprint peaks of MIL-100(Fe), are attributed to C–H stretching vibrations54. The absorption bands at 1376 and 1427 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–O stretching and O–H bending vibrations, respectively. The stretching vibrations of C = O were observed at 1560 and 1630 cm⁻¹, while the band at 3400 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the O–H stretching vibration18. For CS-BCN (90:10), the stretching vibrations of N–H and O–H appeared at 3400 cm⁻¹. The asymmetric C–H stretching vibration was observed at 2923 cm⁻¹, and the symmetric C–H stretching vibration was detected at 2852 cm⁻¹55. Reaction between the NH₂ groups of CS and the carbonyl groups of glutaraldehyde generated C = N linkages, as indicated by the characteristic absorption band at 1670 cm⁻¹56. The C–H bending vibrations of CS and BCN appear in the region of 14279,57 cm⁻¹, while the C–O stretching vibrations in CS and BCN are detected at 1080 cm⁻⁻¹55. The absorption band at around 630 cm⁻¹ is generally attributed to the vibrations of the β-glycosidic (C–O–C) linkages in polysaccharides33,58. Almost all the main adsorption bands of the CS–BCN composite, except for the band at 1080 cm⁻¹, overlap with the adsorption bands of MIL-100. After the incorporation of MIL-100(Fe) into the CS–BCN composite, the intensities of several absorption bands changed, particularly those at 1670 cm⁻¹ and 3400 cm⁻¹. These changes can be attributed to the interaction of the –NH and –OH functional groups on the surface of the CS–BCN composite with the carboxyl groups of MIL-100(Fe)20,59. In addition, the decrease in the intensity of the absorption band at 630 cm⁻¹ can be attributed to the coordination of the free Fe metal centers from MIL-100 with the oxygen atoms of the C–O–C groups in CS–BCN60. Following Pb(II) adsorption onto the beads, both the intensity and position of the absorption bands of the CS-BCN-MIL composite changed. The band at 1670 cm⁻¹ significantly decreased and shifted to 1630 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the C = N stretching of the imine groups in chitosan and the C = O vibration of MIL-100(Fe), indicating the coordination of these functional groups with Pb(II). Furthermore, the absorption band at 3400 cm⁻¹ shifted to 3440 cm⁻¹, representing the –OH and –NH groups of CS-BCN as well as the –OH groups of MIL-100(Fe), confirming stronger coordination with Pb(II) ions. Additionally, the intensity of the band at 1080 cm⁻¹ decreased, likely due to coordination of the C–O functional groups in CS-BCN with Pb(II). Since the C–O–C absorption band in CS-BCN overlaps with Fe–O vibrations in MIL-100 in this region, the decrease at 630 cm⁻¹ after Pb(II) adsorption can be attributed to the possible coordination of C–O–C or Fe–O groups with Pb(II). According to the results, Pb(II) adsorption primarily occurs through nitrogen- and oxygen-containing groups, which can be attributed to the coordination of Pb(II) with oxygen and nitrogen atoms. This finding is consistent with results reported by other researchers57,61,63–66,66.

SEM-EDX

The surface morphology and elemental analysis are shown in Fig. 4. According to Fig. 4a, MIL-100 (Fe) appears as sharp-edged octahedral crystals. Its particles vary in size (176/21–663/19 nm) and show no wrinkles in their structure. Particle aggregation can be attributed to crystal growth at high density. Figure 4b shows a regular structure containing bacterial cellulose fibers in the CS/BCN composite. The trapping of MIL-100 (Fe) in the CS/BCN matrix is depicted in Fig. 4c. A stable nanocomposite structure was formed through the uniform dispersion of MOF particles in the CS/BCN composite. The surface morphology of CS–BCN beads (Fig. 4b) and MIL-loaded CS–BCN beads (Fig. 4c) exhibits a porous, cavity-rich structure. These pores serve as channels that facilitate water transport, enabling metal ions to penetrate the bead network and be adsorbed, while simultaneously increasing the contact area between the adsorbent and Pb(II) ions32. Following Pb(II) adsorption, the bead surface became denser with reduced porosity (Fig. 4d), indicating successful lead uptake.

The presence of Fe in MIL-CS–BCN (Fig. 4e) confirms the successful synthesis of the composite, while the emergence of a new peak corresponding to Pb²⁺ after adsorption (Fig. 4f) confirms the adsorption of lead onto the bead surface.

Elemental distribution analysis revealed that after Pb(II) adsorption, the weight% of the elements in the adsorbent changed. Before Pb²⁺ adsorption, the average weight% of iron was 14.76%, and lead was 0%. After Pb²⁺ adsorption, the iron content decreased to 13.03%, while the lead content increased to 26.48%. These results further confirm the successful adsorption of Pb ions on the synthesized beads, which is consistent with the FTIR results. Elemental mapping (Figs. 4g–h) further corroborates the presence of Fe and confirms successful Pb adsorption, as evidenced by the appearance of red and green spots, respectively.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption studies

Figure 5 shows the N₂ adsorption–desorption curves of MIL-100(Fe) and the beads. As indicated in the Fig., MIL-100(Fe) presents characteristics of both Type I and Type IV isotherms. At low relative pressures, a rapid increase in nitrogen adsorption confirms the Type I isotherms, which are characteristic of the presence of micropores. Additionally, the formation of an H4 hysteresis loop at higher relative pressures is indicative of mesopores, aligning with Type IV isotherms. The simultaneous presence of both isotherm types suggests that the MIL-100(Fe) structure comprises both micropores and mesopores. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm of the beads exhibits a hysteresis loop at relative pressures (P/P₀) above 0.8, indicating a Type IV isotherm. This suggests that the beads possess a mesoporous texture. As shown in Table 3, MIL-100(Fe) has a BET surface area of 956 m²/g and a total pore volume of 0.72 cm³/g, in agreement with previously reported values20,67. Pure MIL-100(Fe) exhibited a much higher surface area than the CS/BCN/MIL beads, which can be attributed to pore blockage of the MOF by CS/BCN. However, the surface area of the CS/BCN/MIL composite beads was significantly increased by the incorporation of MIL-100(Fe)—more than twice that of CS/BCN (90:10)—along with an increase in total pore volume from 0.18 cm³/g to 0.31 cm³/g. Pore size distribution analysis was used to determine the average pore widths of the samples. The average pore width of MIL-100(Fe) was 30.92 Å, closely matching the value reported by Tan and Foo (2021). The CS/BCN and CS/BCN/MIL composites showed average pore widths of 47Å and 55 Å, respectively.

Effects of various parameters on lead detoxification

Contact time and adsorption kinetics

Adsorption kinetics are strongly influenced by the contact time of the poison material with the adsorbent. As illustrated in Fig. 6, lead removal is strongly dependent on contact time, with most adsorption occurring within the first 1500 min. This is attributed to the initially abundant vacant adsorption sites on the bead surface, which facilitate diffusion of Pb(II) ions from the bulk solution to the adsorbent surface and promote interaction with active binding sites. As a result, the bulk concentration of lead quickly diffuses to the adsorbent surface, allowing interaction between the adsorptive molecules and the active sites of the adsorbent. As adsorption progresses, these sites become occupied, leading to a continuous decrease in uptake efficiency until equilibrium is reached. At equilibrium, a maximum lead removal efficiency of 95.5% was achieved. The adsorption behavior of the beads can be determined through kinetic models. In the first-order kinetic model, the physical adsorption of Pb ions onto the adsorbent is the rate-determining step, whereas in the pseudo-second-order kinetic model, chemisorption is the rate-determining step. According to the Weber–Morris intraparticle diffusion model, the rate-determining step of adsorption is the penetration of ions into the pores of the adsorbent. Kinetic data are typically fitted using the pseudo-first-order (Eq. 5), pseudo-second-order (Eq. 6), and Weber-Morris (Eq. 7) models.

Initial concentration of lead and adsorption isotherm

Another factor affecting the efficiency of lead removal by the adsorbent is its concentration. According to Fig. 8, the removal efficiency of lead declines from 99.5% to 31% as the Pb(II) concentration in the solution increases from 0.5 mg/L to 50 mg/L, indicating a negative effect of higher concentrations on adsorption. At high concentrations, the active sites of the adsorbent become saturated; thus, the efficiency of the adsorbent in lead removal declines. The adsorption capacity of the beads and their surface properties can be predicted using isotherm models. Although various isotherm models are available, the Langmuir and Freundlich models are the most commonly employed in adsorption studies. The Langmuir isotherm describes monolayer adsorption on uniform sites of the beads in terms of shape and size, without adsorbate–adsorbate interactions, whereas the Freundlich model represents multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous sites, allowing possible interactions between adsorbate molecules.

Recyclability studies

Recyclability is a key factor in evaluating the economic feasibility and application potential of CS/BCN/MIL beads in adsorption processes. For this purpose, adsorption experiments were conducted over four cycles (Fig. 11). The results showed that the beads maintained good lead removal ability even after three recycling cycles, removing up to 90.9% of Pb from the solution. However, recycling for a fourth cycle led to a decrease in the removal efficiency of the beads to 36%. Thus, these beads demonstrate good lead removal ability for up to three recycling cycles.

Analysis of apple juice samples

After the optimal conditions for lead adsorption (adsorbent amount, detoxification time, and initial concentration) were determined, detoxification tests were conducted in apple juice. The results showed that the active ingredients in the juice alone reduced the spiked lead content by 3%, while the use of the adsorbent increased the removal rate to 30%. In general, the lead removal rate in the juice simulator was much higher than that in apple juice. The reduced adsorption capacity of the CS/BCN/MIL beads for lead ions in apple juice is likely due to the occupation of the adsorbent’s active sites by juice components.

Conclusion

The aerogel bead CS–BCN (90:10)–MIL-100(50%) was identified as effective for removing Pb²⁺ ions from both simulated and real apple juice. The reusability of the microbeads (over 3 cycles) strongly supports their potential for cost-effective application. The beads were able to reduce the lead content in juice containing 10 ppm by up to 30% without affecting important quality parameters of apple juice, including polyphenol content, antioxidant activity, and ascorbic acid content. These results suggest that CS/BCN/MIL beads have promising potential for efficiently removing lead from contaminated apple juice without adversely affecting its quality attributes. Despite these promising results, the study had some limitations. The main limitations were related to determining the equilibrium time and the choice of crosslinker. Initially, based on similar studies, short contact times led to low Pb²⁺ removal, with equilibrium achieved after 24 h. Additionally, when attempting to replace glutaraldehyde with sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) as a crosslinker, the hydrogel beads tended to disintegrate during detoxification, indicating that glutaraldehyde was more suitable for this research.

Cost analysis

The main materials used for the synthesis of the adsorbent in this study were chitosan, bacterial cellulose nanofibers, and MIL-100(Fe). The production of 1 g of the adsorbent requires approximately 0.2 g of chitosan, 0.02 g of cellulose nanofibers, and 0.1 g of MIL-100(Fe) MOF. Cost analysis revealed that the estimated production cost for 1 g of adsorbent is around $1, with the majority of the cost attributed to the MOF and chitosan. This low cost suggests that large-scale production of the adsorbent is feasible.
