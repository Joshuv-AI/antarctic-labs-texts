Hospice care support priorities and perceptions of family caregivers of individuals with end-stage heart failure in China: a qualitative study
Authors: Chunyan Chen, Haixiang Zhu, Yehua Wang, Xiaoxue Han, Qijin Xu, Ailing Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo gain an in-depth understanding of the real support priorities and perceptions of caregivers of individuals receiving care with end-stage heart failure regarding hospice care.DesignA qualitative descriptive approach was employed.Participants and settingUsing a purposive sampling approach, 16 primary caregivers of individuals receiving care with end-stage heart failure from a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, Zhejiang province, were selected as interview participants. A semi-structured interview outline based on literature review was used to conduct face-to-face interview. Tape-recorded data with notes were transcribed, and the content analysis method was applied to analyse the data and distil themes.ResultsAfter integration and analysis of 16 interview transcripts (n=16), the thematic analysis resulted in four themes supporting the overall findings, including seeking collaborative decision-making in palliative care, demand for appropriate healthcare resources, search for emotional respite and support in hospice settings and desire for knowledge and information about hospice care and heart failure. Each theme encompasses two subthemes.ConclusionsCaregivers of individuals receiving care with end-stage heart failure have multiple care responsibilities and diverse support priorities. Medical personnel should be aware of the burden that family caregivers bear and provide more targeted and individualised assistance to the caregiver. At the organisational level, administrators support priorities to establish comprehensive, lifelong and compassionate hospice services to ensure that the quality of life for caregivers and the quality of care for individuals receiving care can be further enhanced.Keywords: Heart failure, Caregiver Burden, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, PALLIATIVE CARE

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a significant global health issue. In China, approximately 9.6 million people are currently affected, and this number is projected to rise annually due to population ageing. Among HF patients, around 5%–10% will progress to the terminal stage.1 Despite the implementation of optimised treatment regimens or the administration of diuretics, cardiac-strengthening medications, vasodilating medications and other therapies, it remains challenging to achieve a favourable clinical outcome. Patients frequently experience recurrent cardiac insufficiency manifestations, such as severe respiratory distress, fatigue, cough, muscle weakness and other symptoms, which significantly impair their quality of life.2 In 2015, China’s mainland death quality index was ranked 71st,3 indicating that the quality of death among end-stage patients is poor.

Hospice care, tailored to the support priorities of the terminally ill and their families, offers comprehensive care that encompasses physical, psychological, social and spiritual aspects, aiming to enhance the quality of life and end-of-life experience.4 Literature reveals that hospice care is predominantly used for individuals receiving care for oncology.5 This approach has been shown to alleviate distressing symptoms and enhance the quality of life for those facing the end of their lives.6 In 2014, the Worldwide Palliative Care Alliance published data on the distribution of diseases among adults requiring hospice care, indicating that patients with cardiovascular disease patients constituted 38.47% of the total, exceeding the 34.01% of patients with cancer.7 Additionally, the 3-year survival rate of patients with end-stage HF is 25% lower than that of malignant tumours,8 underscoring the urgent need for hospice care services among patients with HF. The WHO explicitly indicates that patients with end-stage HF should be provided with hospice care.9 Consequently, it is crucial to ascertain the actual necessity for hospice care among patients with end-stage HF and their caregivers, and to incorporate hospice care into the care plan for patients with end-stage HF. Such integration is vital to guarantee that those receiving care receive comprehensive and suitable care.

In China, the primary caregivers of hospice are their immediate family caregivers. However, at this stage, the actual needs and perspectives of these caregivers regarding hospice care have not been sufficiently elucidated. The purpose of this study is to investigate the care burden experienced by caregivers of hospice individuals receiving care with end-stage HF through the lens of qualitative research. This approach aims to elucidate the knowledge, emotions and necessities of these caregivers, enhancing our understanding of the nuances associated with hospice care, advancing the hospice service, improving the quality of life for caregivers and the quality of care for individuals receiving care and establishing a foundation for the provision of a comprehensive range of high-quality, satisfactory hospice care services.

Between October and December 2024, primary caregivers of individuals receiving care with end-stage HF were selected using a purposive sampling method. Inclusion criteria for individuals receiving care: diagnosed with HF and determined to be in the end stage by his attending physician, following the Chinese Heart Failure Diagnostic Guidelines 2024 Edition.10 The end-stage determination was based on whether individuals receiving care had received one of the following interventions: (1) repeated hospitalisations due to HF and unable to be safely discharged; (2) requiring long-term intravenous medication; (3) awaiting heart transplantation; (4) using mechanical cardiac assist devices.

Inclusion criteria for caregivers:11 (1) age ≥18 years (2) absence of remuneration for care (3) normal cognitive and communication abilities and informed consent (4) served as the primary caregiver for at least 3 months and at least 8 hours per day. Exclusion criteria included individuals diagnosed with cancer, other diseases and healthcare professionals, caregivers under the age of 18 (see table 1).

Data collection

Prior to the interview, the purpose and significance of the interview were explained to the interviewees, and it was promised that no personal information would be disclosed. All the information was presented in an anonymous form. At the same time, in order to facilitate the subsequent organisation of the information, it was necessary to make audio recordings of the interviews. The interviewees were guaranteed that the audio recordings would only be used for this study. During the interviews, the researchers did not induce or intervene in the interviews, recorded meaningful non-verbal actions promptly and each caregiver participating in the interview signed an informed consent form for the interview.

The semi-structured in-depth interview method was employed, and the interviews were conducted in a familiar and quiet environment, typically in the nurse–patient communication room. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 min, during which the interviewees were encouraged to express their thoughts and feelings in depth through careful listening, probing follow-up questions and other techniques. The interviewees’ facial expressions and body movements were also recorded. The interview outline was revised and completed based on a literature review, expert argumentation and pre-interviews.12 Data was collected between 5 October 2024 and 26 December 2024 at the workplace. Data analysis

Following each interview, the audio-recorded information was transcribed into text and the field notes were compiled. The data were analysed by using the seven-step analysis method proposed by Colaizzi,13 as follows: (1) the researcher comprehends the content provided by the interviewees through repeated and careful reading of the textual materials; (2) analyses the materials word by word and extracts important statements related to the research questions; (3) constructs meanings for the repeatedly appearing ideas; (4) pools the constructed ideas to find meaningful common concepts, forming themes; (5) describing in detail the themes formed in step 4 and extracting typical original statements from the interviewees; (6) placing similar themes and descriptions together for repeated comparison, extracting similar ideas and then constructing themes; (7) returning the resulting themes to the interviewees for validation to ensure the authenticity of the content.

The sample size was determined based on the principle of data saturation. Data saturation was defined as no more new themes formed after careful data analysis, and two more interviews were conducted to confirm data saturation.

Quality control measures

(1) The female researcher, a licensed nurse with a Bachelor of Science in Nursing, completed a training programme on qualitative research and carried out two preliminary interviews with caregivers. The interview guide and communication method were adjusted based on the feedback received. Then, the interview guide was reviewed by the research team, particularly with a PhD supervisor, to ensure the interview guide would help in obtaining rich content. (2) The interview plan was detailed in advance to ensure caregivers’ full participation. (3) A multidisciplinary collaboration mechanism was established to address caregivers’ problems. (4) The same RN conducted interviews and documented caregivers’ expressions to ensure data reliability.

Ethical considerations

No significant risks were identified in the project implementation. The privacy of interviewees was protected during the qualitative interview process. The research team strictly maintains the confidentiality of participants’ information, using codes C1-16 to replace their names.

Outcomes

The study ultimately included 16 caregivers of individuals receiving care with HF, 8 men and 8 women; the age range of the participants was from 24 to 72 years, with a median age of 54.5 years. In terms of education level, nine caregivers had a middle school education or less, two were high school graduates and five had a college or bachelor’s degree. Theme 1: seeking collaborative decision-making in palliative care

Many caregivers expressed a desire to play a more active role in treatment and care decisions for individuals with HF. They believe that participating in decision-making will help them to better understand treatment plans and ensure that their loved one’s care needs are met. This involvement strengthens caregivers’ sense of responsibility and makes them feel more reassured and prepared when facing medical choices.

Subtheme 1: the importance of involvement in treatment and nursing care decision-making

Caregivers want to be involved in making decisions about the treatment and care, and to express their opinions and feelings.14 Nine caregivers expressed their desire to establish closer communication with the medical team to jointly develop personalised end-of-life care plans for the individuals under their care. C4: ‘I hope that you (referring to the medical staff) can come with us to discuss this with me, and we can express our thoughts, and there is a treatment plan for the follow-up, so that we can feel more at ease’. C9: ‘We are psychologically prepared for the disease, but the back and forth process makes us conflicted and torn, so I hope the doctors and nurses can talk to me more about what treatments are best for him at this stage (end-stage heart failure) and come up with the most suitable plan for the patient’. C10: ‘When the doctors and nurses came to check on my dad, I often can’t help but want to ask what the next steps in treatment will be, but they always hurried away, and I’m not sure if it’s appropriate to ask now, but I think they should be able to talk to our family caregivers in detail, and I hoped that the treatment for my father is based on an understanding of the patient and the family’s needs, rather than blindly’.

Subtheme 2: the requirement of adequate time for decision-making

Sufficient decision-making time enables individuals receiving care and their caregivers to fully understand the advantages, disadvantages and risks of each treatment option. It enhances their sense of control over the treatment process and ensures they can integrate their personal values and preferences into the decision.15 The interview results indicate that eleven caregivers desire detailed communication about the condition and sufficient time to make informed decisions. C3: ‘We need more time to process all the information. Every treatment option the doctor told us about has different risks and benefits, and we don’t want to rush into a decision we might regret. Having more time allows us to truly understand what each option entails, which is extremely important to us’. C11: ‘My father suffered respiratory failure early in the morning, and the ICU doctors pressured us to decide on intubation within half an hour. I collapsed in the corridor, trembling, and ultimately signed the consent form. Now, my father lies in pain in the ICU. Every night, I’m haunted by regret. If we had just had a few more days to align the family’s opinions, might the outcome have been different?’ C16: ‘The doctor spent ten minutes explaining that my father’s class IV heart failure required an LVAD (left ventricular assist device implantation), but terms like ‘cardiac output’ and ‘thrombosis risk’ sounded like incomprehensible vocabulary to us. We needed time to research and consult with other family members. After all, the device would be implanted for years. What if we rushed into signing and made the wrong choice?’ Therefore, during shared decision-making, sufficient time for deliberation must be provided to allow individuals receiving care and families an adequate opportunity for an informed, pressure-free discussion to arrive at the most suitable decision for their specific situation.

Theme 2: demand for appropriate healthcare resources

The interview results indicate that during the process of caring for individuals with end-stage HF, family caregivers commonly expressed a strong demand for home-based hospice care, as well as a need for more explicit medical resource provision and policy support. These needs encompass not only professional nursing human resource but also necessary medical equipment, medications and related services. To alleviate the burden on family caregivers and improve the quality of life for individuals, policymakers and medical institutions should collaborate to ensure the equitable distribution and efficient utilisation of resources.

Subtheme 1: demand for immediate access to home-based hospice services

In China, the scarcity and inequitable distribution of medical resources impedes individuals’ access to timely and high-quality care. Moreover, 10 participants indicated the cultural concept of ‘returning to one’s roots’ influences patient preferences, as the majority of individuals approaching the end of life opt to spend their final days in a familiar setting, accompanied by their loved ones. C5: ‘Since choosing palliative care, I want him to suffer less, so giving him good care at home and accompanying him through the final stage of his life is what I prefer to achieve’. C9: ‘If I can be with him at home and get this care, preferably with your (healthcare professionals) guidance, then I think that’s what my dad would have wanted more than anything’. C10: ‘We plan to go home in the end, if going home means we don’t have to just wait for death and can do something that your hospital can provide (referring to palliative care), it would also be a comfort for the family. When the time comes (referring to death), we won’t be at a loss’. C12: ‘My spouse has been suffering a lot from this illness, and the expenses have indeed been quite high these past few years. After discussing with my children, we decided to bring him home, but we haven’t communicated this decision to him. Sometimes, when I see him sitting there looking uncomfortable, I wonder if the decision we made was right’. C13: ‘We are very eager to take care of him at home according to hospital standards, such as how to arrange the room to alleviate his pain, or what medications and equipment should be prepared, but we are really not clear about how to do it specifically. If medical staff could regularly come to our home to guide us, or teach us how to handle emergencies through video calls, such as when he suddenly has difficulty breathing or his pain intensifies, we would not be so panicked’.

Subtheme 2: desire for clearer medical resource provision and policy support

A review of the literature revealed that caregivers expressed concerns regarding the accessibility and adaptability of existing healthcare resources. Nine participants felt that current healthcare resources have limited support for the provision of hospice care, especially in the area of home care. They expressed a desire for clearer healthcare resource provision and governmental policy support for hospice care to be truly implemented in practice, in order to help them take better care of their individuals receiving care. C1: ‘I am very worried that if my mom has a sudden situation at a later stage, I can’t deal with it by myself. If community hospitals offered this service (hospice care), it would be good to pay for anything’. C4: ‘I haven’t heard the government talking about hospice care. They need to do more to create and implement the right policies. Otherwise, it feels like there’s no one to help, even after leaving the hospital’. C5: ‘After leaving the hospital, I don’t know where to go for information about hospice organizations’. C7: ‘I wish I could get more community support and information so that I know I have a place to go when I have questions to help me take better care of my dad’. C15: ‘I hope the government can do more to provide resources in this area (referring to hospice) in our community so it is easier to get in touch’.

Theme 3: family caregivers search for emotional respite and support in hospice settings

The interview results showed that family caregivers often experience significant psychological distress and negative emotions while caring for individuals with HF. They must deal not only with individuals’ physical pain and discomfort, but also with complex medical information and decisions related to their care. Family caregivers hope to receive professional psychological support and emotional guidance through palliative care services. They also hope to receive practical assistance to alleviate the pressure and burden of caregiving.

Subtheme 1: seeking professional psychological and emotional support

During the interview process, 13 caregivers often expressed experiencing negative emotions, including uncertainty due to disease recurrence and anticipatory grief, while caring for individuals with HF. As family caregivers, it is frequently heart-wrenching to witness their loved ones suffer from illness, and they endure the same recurring pain as the individuals with HF. They must put on a brave face in front of them but often break down emotionally and cry alone behind the scenes. C5: ‘I am the only child in my family. My father has been ill, and I have been taking care of him for over four years. My parents are getting older, and I want to cry, but I don’t dare do so in front of them. Although my husband comforts and helps me, he gets impatient if I talk about it too much. I feel like no one truly understands me’. C7: ‘Taking care of her and watching her repeatedly suffer from illness is agonizing for me, too. I truly hope someone could accompany me, talk with me, and help alleviate some of my psychological burden’. C16: ‘That night, I slept very deeply. At first, when he felt unwell and called me, I didn’t hear him. By the time I woke up and realized what was happening, he was breathing extremely heavily and couldn’t even speak. I felt particularly guilty and self-reproachful’. Caregivers typically have a strong inclination to express negative emotions and seek emotional support. Confronted with the possible reality of losing loved ones, they hope to receive professional psychological counselling to assist them in managing anxiety and grief.

Subtheme 2: hope to receive assistance in alleviating the actual pressure of caregiving

In addition to seeking psychological and emotional support, caregivers of individuals with end-stage HF frequently encounter challenges and stress while performing their caregiving duties. This is due to the need to handle complex situations and the diverse demands of individuals receiving care. 11 participants expressed this sentiment. These pressures include, but are not limited to, physical exhaustion from long-term caregiving, an increased financial burden, challenges in time management and an overwhelming sense of helplessness when dealing with individuals discomfort.16 C1: ‘Over the many years of his illness, the family’s savings have nearly been depleted. I can no longer work, and I don’t know how to plan for the future’. C8: ‘It’s heartbreaking to see him struggle to breathe. I’m worried he won’t make it. But I don’t know how to make him more comfortable’. C12: ‘I don’t have any time of my own. Apart from accompanying and taking care of her, I can’t plan anything’. Caregivers report that they often face multiple challenges that leave them physically and mentally exhausted. They hope to obtain support services for families of individuals with end-stage HF to alleviate the burden during the caregiving process and better support individuals receiving care through this difficult period.

Theme 4: desire for knowledge and information about hospice care and heart failure

The interviews showed that family caregivers often lack knowledge and information about hospice care and HF. They indicated that if they were better informed about hospice services and the specifics of the disease, they would feel more capable of caring for their loved ones.

Subtheme 1: desire for more knowledge related to hospice care

They believe that the primary purpose of hospice care is to ensure patient comfort during the final stage of life. However, nine participants are uncertain about the methods that can be employed to enhance patient comfort. C3: ‘I think that hospice care is to make them suffer less, but I’m not very clear about exactly what to do’. C6: ‘I would be willing to know and learn about hospice if I could know exactly what it does, as long as it can help him (meaning the patient)’. C14: ‘I understand that hospice care should focus on the patient’s physical and psychological well-being, but I am still unclear about the specific service process and team collaboration methods. If we could know what roles doctors, nurses, and social workers each undertake, or how they coordinate with the patient’s needs, it should better help reduce their suffering’.

Subtheme 2: demand for access to specialised knowledge about heart failure

12 participants expressed a desire to acquire more professional knowledge about HF, including information about its causes, prognosis, treatment options for end-stage disease and home care.17 They also expressed an eagerness to obtain professional support through education in the hope that they could better assist the individuals receiving care and make the right treatment choices.18 C4: ‘I would like to know more about the disease, but the doctors and nurses are always busy, and I am too embarrassed to bother’. C6: ‘I would like to know if there are any good treatments for him at this stage (terminal), because if not, we can make other plans earlier’. C8: ‘I want to learn more about disease knowledge and home care knowledge so that I can understand what kind of situations she might encounter and know how to help her better’. C11: ‘He has severe edema and has experienced acute heart failure on his previous hospitalizations. I am concerned that this condition will reemerge during his home care, yet I am uncertain how to proceed. It would be beneficial to have guidance from experienced professionals in this area’.

Discussion

Emphasis on the assessment of caregivers’ needs

In Chinese culture, the value of ‘self-interest through altruism’ is prevalent in the family unit. This cultural norm often leads caregivers to prioritise the responsibility of caring for others over their health.19 On a global scale, several guidelines and policies have recognised the importance of these issues and emphasised the family as the core of care. For instance, Canada’s National Palliative Care Strategy and Australia’s National Palliative Care Standards Guidelines emphasise the importance of assessing and addressing the needs of family caregivers.20 The present qualitative study also revealed the multiple needs faced by caregivers of end-stage HF in hospice care, including a lack of knowledge, physical and psychological stress management support priorities, a desire for emotional support and expectations and suggestions for healthcare services.21 These findings suggest that healthcare professionals should proactively assess the health status of family caregivers, identify their needs, enhance communication with caregivers and implement appropriate supportive measures to address these needs. This approach not only fosters a healthier climate of care but also promotes improvements in hospice services, which in turn enhances the overall quality of hospice care. The overarching goal of this strategy is to ensure an enhanced quality of life for both individuals receiving care and their caregivers.

Building a multidisciplinary hospice team to meet the diverse support priorities of caregivers

This study found that the support priorities of caregivers for individuals with end-stage HF receiving palliative care are characterised by diversification, which is reflected in various aspects, such as acquiring care-related skills, knowledge and information, as well as psychological support and participation in decision-making. The research group’s analysis indicated that the primary need of caregivers is to address the needs of individuals receiving care, with the need for information being the most pronounced. The interviews revealed that caregivers with a longer duration of care experience exhibited superior care skills, more efficient information acquisition and a higher propensity to cope with uncertainty, anxiety and other adverse reactions. In addition, many caregivers reported that counselling with psychologists significantly reduced their stress and promoted emotional well-being. The fundamental purpose of hospice care is to address the physical, mental and social support needs of individuals receiving care and their caregivers, thereby enhancing the quality of life and the experience of dying. However, achieving this goal is challenging without the expertise of healthcare professionals. The efficacy of multidisciplinary team hospice care in enhancing the quality of life for individuals with end-stage disease and their caregivers has been demonstrated in several studies.22 23 Consequently, to ensure the effective and efficient implementation of hospice services within the current framework, establishing a specialised, multidisciplinary hospice team is imperative. This team will provide comprehensive support and counselling services to caregivers, thereby reducing their care burden, facilitating their swift adjustment to new social roles and enhancing their quality of life and satisfaction.

Implementing holistic care and promoting remote management of home hospice care

This interview indicates that both receiving care and their caregivers have expressed a preference for passing away at home rather than in a hospital setting. However, this preference is not easily realised due to a lack of awareness regarding caregiving practices and the persistence of entrenched attitudes.24 Multiple studies suggest that home hospice care offers numerous benefits, including the ability for individuals receiving care to pass away in a familiar environment, reduced medical expenses and a reduction in caregivers’ anxiety, depression and other negative emotions.25 In Europe and the USA, home hospice care has emerged as the predominant and most effective care model for individuals receiving end-of-life care.26 In contrast, home hospice care is still in its nascent stage in China, with considerable potential for future development. In response, there is a support priority to cultivate specialised practitioners, improve their professional skills and implement holistic care,27 including ‘whole person, whole family, whole process, whole team, whole community’ by the care path of ‘scientific assessment - effective intervention - health education’.

Concurrently, leveraging the internet and technological advancements, we are integrating digital healthcare with home hospice care; enabling us to continuously monitor the conditions of receiving care through remote care equipment and support caregivers through door-to-door or video visits. The use of remote care equipment enables continuous and real-time monitoring of individuals receiving care. While simultaneously providing caregivers with guidance on care knowledge and skills through door-to-door or video interactions. This approach has been shown to enhance caregivers’ confidence in their abilities and the quality of home hospice care. Additionally, it fosters a sense of warmth and support for individuals receiving care and their caregivers, thereby enhancing the overall experience of home hospice services.28

Emphasising grief counselling and death education for caregivers of individuals receiving care for end-stage heart failure

Grief counselling, as one of the primary services of palliative care, involves a structured intervention in which trained professionals assist individuals receiving care and bereaved individuals in processing grief within a reasonable time frame, aims to alleviate psychological distress, including anxiety and fear of death, by employing specialised psychological support techniques.29 These techniques are designed to enhance the bereaved individuals’ capacity to cope with sudden and distressing life events. The efficacy of grief counselling in alleviating the grief of end-stage individuals receiving care and bereaved individuals has been well documented in the literature.30 31 The findings of this study indicated that some respondents perceived moral pressure and traditional perceptions to be impediments to their acceptance of the decision to provide individuals receiving care with hospice care. Many family caregivers were not prepared for the patient’s imminent demise despite having been with the end-stage HF patient for an extended period.32 The study emphasises the importance of prioritising support for a comprehensive examination of family support status, a factor that is often overlooked in research and practice. The promotion of hospice care among caregivers of individuals receiving care with end-stage HF is further influenced by social factors that are intricate and multifaceted, particularly within the context of China’s traditional conception of life and death.

Therefore, healthcare professionals can use narrative care methods that involve reading and sharing classic literary stories related to illness and death. This approach guides family caregivers to use narrative imagination to break free from conventional views on life and death, altering their traditional perceptions of life and death and enabling them to face and accept the passing of their loved ones with equanimity. Additionally, encouraging family caregivers to write care diaries can help integrate positive events from the care process, thereby enhancing their sense of self-worth and alleviating or reducing negative emotions such as guilt, self-blame and remorse.33 Moreover, internet information technology can be used to provide online grief counselling,34 thereby breaking the time and geographical constraints of face-to-face grief counselling.

Strengths and limitations

This study delves deeply into the genuine need of caregivers for individuals receiving care with end-stage HF, using qualitative research methods to precisely capture the complex emotional fluctuations and psychological states of the caregivers, filling a gap in existing research regarding the needs of caregivers. With rich clinical nursing experience and keen insight, the research team conducted rigorous analysis of the data, extracted key themes and provided a solid foundation for the development of subsequent targeted nursing interventions, with the potential to significantly improve the quality of life for both individuals receiving care and caregivers. Another strength is the diverse sample of caregivers we included in our study. By recruiting participants from various backgrounds, ages and relationships to the patient, we ensured that our findings reflect a broad range of perspectives and experiences. This diversity enriches our study and increases its generalisability, allowing healthcare providers to apply our recommendations to a wider population of caregivers. Additionally, our rigorous data analysis process, involving multiple rounds of coding and theme development, ensured the reliability and validity of our results. The collaborative efforts of our research team, with expertise in nursing, psychology and palliative care, further strengthened the study’s interdisciplinary approach and contributed to its comprehensive insights.

Despite these strengths, our study is not without limitations. One limitation is the potential for interviewer bias during the data collection process. Although our interviewers were trained to maintain objectivity and neutrality, subtle biases in tone, body language or phrasing of questions may have influenced participants’ responses. To mitigate this, we implemented strategies such as debriefing sessions and reflexivity discussions among the research team, but we acknowledge that some degree of bias may still be present. Another limitation is the regional focus of our study, which took place in Hangzhou, Zhejiang province, China. While this allowed us to gain a deep understanding of the local context and cultural factors influencing caregiving, it may limit the transferability of our findings to other regions or countries with different healthcare systems and cultural norms. Future research could explore the needs of caregivers in diverse geographical locations to build on our work and provide a more global perspective on hospice care for end-stage HF individuals receiving care.
