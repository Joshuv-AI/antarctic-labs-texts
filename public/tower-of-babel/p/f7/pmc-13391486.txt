Consumer awareness, perceptions and purchasing behaviour regarding fortified wheat flour available in the market
Authors: Dhananjay, Monika Choudhary, Prabhdeep Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study examined consumer awareness regarding fortified wheat flour available in the market. A total of 300 households were selected using stratified random sampling. Based on socio-economic status assessed through the Modified Kuppuswamy Scale, 180 respondents (60 each from Upper, Upper Middle, and Lower Middle classes) were selected for assessing awareness, preference, and consumption of fortified wheat flour. Data were collected using a pre-tested structured interview schedule. It was revealed that awareness level was the highest among the Upper Class across all aspects of food fortification, with particularly strong knowledge of macro & micronutrients (91.70%). Awareness decreased across socioeconomic groups, with the Lower Middle Class showing the least understanding, especially regarding the fortification logo (26.70%). The computed Chi-square value for overall awareness (10.97) was found to be non-significant. A majority of respondents perceived fortified wheat flour as costlier, while Lower Middle class respondents more frequently reported a taste difference between fortified and unfortified wheat flour. Fortified flours were perceived as safest for pregnant women (56.10%), lactating mothers (52.20%), and infants (45%). The Chi-square test for these categories yielded a value of 19.82, which was not statistically significant, indicating no significant difference in preference among socio-economic classes. Fewer subjects associated them with specific groups like athletes, aged, or diseased persons, with awareness the highest among the Upper Class. The study concluded that although fortified wheat flour was available in the market, gaps existed in consumer awareness, indicating the need for improved consumer education.

Introduction

Micronutrient malnutrition remains a major public health concern globally, especially in developing countries where diets are predominantly monotonous and cereal-based. These deficiencies—often referred to as “hidden hunger”—occur when individuals do not consume adequate vitamins and minerals essential for growth, immunity, and cognitive function. Deficiencies of iron, folic acid, vitamin B12, zinc, and iodine continue to impair the health of millions, particularly women of reproductive age, children, and adolescents in India1. Food fortification has therefore emerged as an efficient public health strategy to enhance micronutrient intake among large populations. As Gibson2 noted, fortification provides a passive and consistent source of essential nutrients without requiring changes in eating habits. In 2006, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended fortifying wheat and maize flours due to their widespread consumption and potential to improve micronutrient status3. Many countries—including the United States, Canada, Brazil, and South Africa—have implemented mandatory cereal fortification programs with positive outcomes. A higher degree of assurance that the food will provide consistent source of micronutrients relevant to benefits to public health is provided by mandatory fortification4.

India has also advanced its fortification efforts through the Food Safety and Standards (Fortification of Foods) Regulations, 2016, introduced by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI). These guidelines mandate the fortification of staples such as wheat flour with iron, folic acid, and vitamin B12, and require fortified products to carry the “+F” logo for easy identification5. Wheat flour, being a daily staple in many Indian households, offers a practical vehicle for improving micronutrient intake and addressing widespread anemia and related deficiencies. However, the success of fortification depends heavily on consumer awareness and acceptance. Although several wheat flour brands now claim fortification and display the FSSAI logo6, many consumers remain unaware of what the symbol represents, the benefits of fortification, or the availability of fortified options. Purchasing decisions often rely on price, taste, or habit rather than nutritional value, limiting the potential health impact of fortified foods. Given these concerns, assessing consumer awareness regarding fortified wheat flour brands is crucial. Understanding how consumers identify fortified products, what they know about added micronutrients, and what influences their purchasing behavior can help address existing gaps and strengthen fortification initiatives. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess consumer awareness with regard to fortified wheat flour available in the market, recognizing that informed consumer choices are essential for achieving the nutritional benefits of wheat flour fortification in India.

Materials and methods

The present study was conducted in Ludhiana city of Punjab, covering three major residential zones—Old City, Mid City, and Peri-Urban—to represent variation in socio-economic and living environments. A baseline survey of 300 households was carried out by randomly selecting 100 households from each zone. The socio-economic status (SES) of the households was assessed using the Modified Kuppuswamy Scale based on education, occupation, and per capita monthly income7. For the final sample, households were stratified into three SES categories: upper class, upper middle class, and lower middle class. From each SES category, 60 respondents were selected through random sampling, resulting in a total sample size of 180 respondents. Only the individual responsible for purchasing household rations—irrespective of gender—was considered eligible for inclusion in the study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Advisory Committee constituted under the Dean, Postgraduate Studies, Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana. Since the study involved a non-invasive questionnaire-based survey with voluntary participation and no collection of sensitive personal or clinical data, formal institutional ethical committee review was exempted as per university guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

To measure consumer awareness regarding fortified wheat flour, a structured interview schedule was developed. The schedule included questions on demographic profile, recognition of the fortification logo, awareness of fortified wheat flour brands, understanding of nutrient benefits, purchasing preferences, and consumption behaviour. Items were constructed after reviewing existing literature on food fortification and conducting consultations with subject experts to ensure relevance and content validity. Pre-testing of the interview schedule was conducted to identify ambiguous, repetitive, or unclear items. Feedback from respondents and experts was used to refine the schedule. Each awareness question had two possible outcomes—correct or incorrect—where a correct response was assigned a score of 1, while an incorrect or no response was given a score of 0. The total awareness score for each respondent was obtained by summing the correct responses. Statistical procedures were applied to examine the association between consumer awareness and socio-economic groups. The Chi-square test was used to determine relationships among variables such as awareness, preferences, perceived suitability, behaviour, and consumption. The Chi-square values were interpreted using their corresponding p-values to assess significance at appropriate levels. This allowed the identification of meaningful differences across socio-economic categories in relation to awareness of fortified wheat flour available in the market.

Data on general information, of the subjects was tabulated and analyzed. The findings on these characteristics of the respondents were presented below.

Peri-Urban (20%) and Old City (28%) areas had comparatively higher proportions than Mid City (15%). The majority (79%) reported being free from chronic health conditions.

Consumer awareness with regard to fortified wheat flour

Consumer awareness regarding fortified wheat flour reflects the knowledge and understanding respondents have about its nutritional benefits, availability, and usage. The study found that awareness varied across different socio-economic groups, indicating that background factors may influence purchasing and consumption patterns.

The information in Table 2 outlines the awareness of food fortification among respondents from different socio-economic classes. Regarding the Food fortification awareness was highest among the upper class (86.70%), followed by the upper middle class (65.00%) and the lower middle class (41.70%). Overall awareness was 64.40%. A total of 78.30% from the upper class were aware of Fortification in wheat flours, compared to 55.00% from the upper middle class and 35.00% from the lower middle class, with an overall awareness of 56.1%. Awareness about Macro and micronutrients was reported by 91.70% in the upper class, 73.30% in the upper middle class, and 46.70% in the lower middle class, resulting in an overall awareness of 70.60%. Awareness was 71.70% in the upper class, 51.70% in the upper middle class, and 31.70% in the lower middle class, with an overall awareness of 51.70% in the case of Macro and micronutrients in fortified flours. Awareness about Food fortification logo was 65.00% among the upper class, 46.70% among the upper middle class, and 26.70% among the lower middle class, giving an overall awareness of 46.10%. A Chi-square test was applied to determine whether the observed differences in awareness among the three socioeconomic groups were statistically significant. The computed Chi-square value for overall awareness (10.97) was found to be non-significant, indicating no statistically meaningful association between socioeconomic status and awareness levels.

The study in Table 3 examined the preferences of respondents from three socioeconomic groups—Upper Class, Upper Middle Class, and Lower Middle Class—regarding fortified wheat flour. Discussion

The findings of the present study highlight clear socio-economic disparities in consumer awareness and perception of fortified wheat flour, despite its widespread market availability.

The general demographic profile of the respondents indicated that a majority belonged to the middle-aged group, representing individuals in their economically active and productive years. The results by Subramanian et al.8 are similar to present study showing that working-age population (30–50) is the main surveyed age group. Differences in family structure were evident across study areas, with a growing prevalence of nuclear families alongside the continued presence of joint family systems, particularly in more traditional settings. This finding is consistent with the work of Dommaraju9, who highlighted that India is transitioning toward smaller nuclear households, Household size was found to influence lifestyle patterns and access to resources. The occurrence of chronic health conditions among a section of the population, especially in areas characterized by traditional family arrangements, underscores the need for area-specific health and nutrition interventions10,11. These observations highlight the interrelationship between age, family composition, and health status, which should be considered while designing community-based programs.

The results showed that higher levels of education were more commonly observed among respondents belonging to the Upper and Upper Middle Classes. This trend was attributed to better availability of educational opportunities, stronger financial support, and greater encouragement for higher education among higher-income groups. The presence of a considerable number of graduates across all socio-economic categories suggests that the study population generally possessed moderate to high educational attainment, which positively influence awareness and practices related to nutrition and fortified foods. The relatively lower proportion of respondents with only matriculation-level education, particularly in the Upper Middle Class, reflects the socio-economic advantage associated with continued education. Although postgraduates formed a smaller segment, their presence indicates a group with advanced knowledge likely to influence informed health and dietary decisions. The results are in accordance to Mutisya et al.12–14.

Occupational distribution varied across socio-economic groups. The Upper Middle Class recorded the highest proportion of professionals, reflecting better educational qualifications and access to organized and well-paid employment. In contrast, respondents from the Upper Class and Lower Middle Class were more frequently engaged in skilled labor and sales-related occupations, indicating involvement in business, service, or semi-skilled work rather than formal professional roles. The limited representation in technical, clerical, and machine-operation categories suggests lower participation in industrial or administrative employment within the study sample. Overall, these findings illustrate the strong influence of socio-economic status on occupational patterns. This aligns closely with the findings of Barros et al.15 who observed that in Brazil that higher SES groups are much more likely to be employed in stable, professional jobs, which in turn enhances their capacity to make informed health and nutrition choices.

Income distribution reflected clear economic stratification among the three groups. The Upper Class predominantly fell into higher income categories, indicating greater financial stability and access to high-earning occupations. The Upper Middle Class largely represented moderate income levels, suggesting stable but not affluent earnings. In contrast, the Lower Middle Class was mainly concentrated in the lowest income category, reflecting financial limitations and dependence on lower-paying less secure forms of employment. The findings of Barros et al.15 are in accordance to the present study. Such income distribution is expected in socio-economic research, as income strongly influences education, occupation, lifestyle, and consumption behavior16–17. The concentration of Lower Middle Class respondents in the lowest income bracket highlights economic constraints that directly affect purchasing capacity, awareness levels, and access to fortified or nutrient-rich food products when compared to higher-income groups.

The results revealed that awareness of food fortification and its related aspects was higher among individuals from upper and upper-middle socio-economic groups, while it was comparatively lower in the lower-middle class. This difference was attributed to factors such as education, access to information, and exposure to health and nutrition campaigns, which are typically more available to higher socio-economic groups. Awareness of fortified wheat flours and the macro- and micronutrients they contain followed a similar pattern, likely because individuals with better educational backgrounds or urban exposure are more conscious of nutritional benefits and food labeling. Recognition of the food fortification logo was also higher among the upper and upper-middle classes, suggesting that visual cues and packaging information reach these groups more effectively. The lower awareness among the lower-middle class reflect limited access to nutritional information, lesser engagement with packaged foods, and reduced exposure to public health messages. These findings highlight the importance of targeted educational interventions and community awareness programs to ensure equitable knowledge of food fortification across all socio-economic groups18,19. The results were in accordance to Rashmi and Vikas20. They found that awareness of fortified foods was significantly higher among upper and upper-middle-income groups, primarily due to better education and access to information—fully supporting the current study’s results.

The findings about the preferences for fortified flours differ across socio-economic groups, influenced by familiarity, affordability, and nutritional awareness. Respondents from the lower-middle class were more likely to notice a taste difference, which was due to their greater reliance on traditional, unfortified staples and higher sensitivity to changes in everyday food items. The perception that fortified flours are costlier was widespread, especially among those with limited financial flexibility, indicating that price remains a major factor in acceptance. Understanding fortified flours as a complete and nutritionally beneficial food was more common among upper-class respondents, because of its better access to nutrition information. Similar results were reported by Goon et al. (2015). The study found that low-income groups were more sensitive to taste changes in fortified staples. However it was found contradicted to the study by Mannar and Hurrell21 reporting that in some regions consumers do not detect taste differences in fortified flour, regardless of socio-economic status Overall, it suggests that economic conditions and exposure to nutrition knowledge shape consumer attitudes, highlighting the need for awareness efforts that address both taste concerns and affordability.

The results show that people from different socio-economic groups hold varying views about who fortified foods are meant for. Individuals from the lower-middle class more often associated fortified products with athletes, older adults, and those dealing with health problems. Aguayo et al. (2012) found that lower-income consumers often view fortified foods as a targeted health intervention rather than an everyday staple. This reflect the belief that fortified foods serve mainly as specialized nutritional support rather than everyday household products. In contrast, respondents from higher-income groups were more confident that fortified foods are safe for infants, pregnant women, and lactating mothers, which likely stems from better access to health information and greater familiarity with nutrition guidelines. Health-conscious individuals in the upper class showed stronger preference for fortified foods, suggesting that lifestyle choices and regular exposure to health-related messages influence acceptance22,23. On the other hand, lower awareness among the lower-middle class points to possible gaps in nutrition knowledge and misconceptions about fortified foods. Overall, these patterns highlight how education, access to information, and health awareness shape perceptions of who fortified foods are appropriate for.

The results regarding shopping and product-checking behaviors shows the difference across socio-economic groups. People from higher-income households were more likely to shop at supermarkets or online platforms, suggesting greater access to organized retail and digital services. The lower-middle class depended more on local stores, due to convenience and affordability. Label reading and product verification were also more common among upper groups, reflecting higher health awareness and confidence in evaluating packaged foods. In contrast, limited label-related behavior among the lower-middle class stem from lower exposure to nutrition information. These differences highlight how access, awareness, and lifestyle shape purchasing practices across socio-economic levels. Results reported by Gupta et al.24 are in accordance with the present study.

The data also revealed that the consumption pattern of fortified foods reveals clear differences across socio-economic groups, reflecting how awareness, affordability, and access shape dietary choices. Individuals from upper-income households showed the most frequent use of fortified products, with many consuming them daily and several times a week. This was linked to higher nutritional awareness, regular exposure to fortified options in supermarkets, and greater purchasing power, all of which make fortified foods a routine part of their diet25. Upper-middle class respondents displayed moderate consumption, often incorporating fortified foods a few times a week. Their pattern suggests a balance between awareness and practical considerations, such as household budget and shopping habits. They recognize the benefits of fortified foods but not prioritize them to the same extent as higher-income groups. The study by Luthra and Sandhu26 were contradictory and found no meaningful difference in fortified food consumption across income groups in certain urban areas, contradicting your result that consumption was significantly different.

In contrast, the lower-middle class showed a notable tendency toward infrequent or no consumption of fortified foods. This group had more respondents who reported rarely or never using fortified products, which was due to cost limitations, limited access to fortified items in local markets, or lower exposure to nutrition education. Similar results were reported by Shetty et al.27 suggesting that Higher SES groups had more consistent consumption of fortified products; low-income groups consumed less due to cost and availability. Additionally, a stronger reliance on traditional, non-fortified staples contribute to reduced adoption. Overall, the findings illustrate that the regularity of fortified food consumption increases with socio-economic status. Economic capacity, exposure to nutritional information, and availability of fortified products play key roles in shaping consumption behavior. These patterns highlight the importance of improving accessibility, affordability, and community-level awareness to encourage more consistent use of fortified foods across all groups.

Conclusion

The present study assessed consumer awareness, perceptions, and purchasing behaviour related to fortified wheat flour among households from different socio-economic groups in Ludhiana city. The findings revealed that although fortified wheat flour is increasingly available in the market, consumer awareness regarding its purpose, nutritional benefits, and identification through the + F logo remains limited. Socio-economic differences were evident in several aspects of awareness and perception, with upper socio-economic groups demonstrating comparatively better understanding of fortification and fortified brands. Despite this, misconceptions regarding cost, taste, and the belief that fortified wheat flour constitutes a “complete food” persisted across all groups. The Chi-square analysis further indicated that certain perceptions—such as taste differences—were significantly associated with socio-economic status, while other factors showed no meaningful statistical relationship. These patterns highlight uneven knowledge distribution and indicate that awareness alone does not consistently translate into accurate understanding or informed purchasing decisions. Overall, the study underscores the need for strengthened consumer education efforts to enhance recognition of fortified products, clarify their health relevance, and dispel common misconceptions. Public health programs, regulatory bodies, and industry stakeholders must work collaboratively to ensure that information on fortification is more widely disseminated, especially among lower- and middle-income groups. Improving consumer awareness is essential for maximizing the nutritional impact of wheat flour fortification and supporting national efforts to reduce micronutrient deficiencies.
