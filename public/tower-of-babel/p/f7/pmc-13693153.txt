Comparative analysis of generative pre-trained transformers for text- and image-based cephalometric prompts using a novel Artificial Intelligence Based Diagnosis and Treatment Planning Index (AIDTI)
Authors: Ahmet Yıldırım, Orhan Cicek, Yavuz Selim Genç, Ruşen Erdem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of this study was to compare the response capabilities of generative pre-trained transformers (GPTs) for identical lateral cephalograms (LCs) based on text- and image-based prompts, using the newly developed Artificial Intelligence Based Diagnosis and Treatment Planning Index (AIDTI).MethodsA total of 90 LCs from 30 cases each with skeletal Class I, II, and III malocclusions were included. The LCs were presented to GPT-4o, GPT-o3 pro, GPT-5, and GPT-5 pro in two different formats: text-based (numerical data including cephalometric analysis measurements) and image-based (direct image upload) prompts. The responses of the GPTs were evaluated using the newly developed AIDTI, scored on a 0–10 scale and consisting of five criteria: diagnostic accuracy, capability for differential diagnosis, clinical appropriateness of the proposed treatment plan, disclosure of risks and complications associated with the treatment plan, and ability to offer alternative treatment options.ResultsHigher performance was observed in text-based prompts, with the highest score achieved by GPT-5 pro (9.62 ± 1.13). By contrast, the performance of all GPTs on image-based prompts was notably lower, with the highest score being 4.16 ± 4.12 for GPT-o3 pro. Additionally, all models showed a tendency to randomly categorize malocclusions as Class II, suggesting systematic bias in their predictions.ConclusionAIDTI provides a structured and multidimensional framework for the concise, balanced, and clinically meaningful interpretation of GPT performance. Because GPTs are unable to directly analyze LCs, their use as independent or reliable supportive tools in orthodontics remains limited at this stage. GPTs may contribute more reliably to orthodontic practice only when orthodontists prompt the model with text-based cephalometric measurement data.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03587-x.Keywords: Large language model, Generative pre-trained transformer, Artificial intelligence, AIDTI (Artificial Intelligence Based Diagnosis and Treatment Planning Index), Cephalometric analysis

Introductıon

The treatment of orthodontic malocclusions involves three main stages: diagnosis, treatment planning, and treatment implementation [1]. By integrating lateral cephalograms (LCs) into the initial diagnostic phase, it has become possible to perform various analyses for the qualitative and quantitative assessment of malocclusions [1–3]. Due to its capacity for easy analysis of hard tissue structures, performance of measurements, and comparison through superimposition, cephalometric analysis has become the gold standard [1]. However, certain limitations in cephalometric diagnosis have also been highlighted. Proponents of this view argue that reliance on different measurement methodologies for cephalometric analyses can lead to divergent diagnoses, treatment plans, and outcomes from the same image. This complicates determining a single treatment option [1, 2, 4]. Accordingly, a study evaluating treatment plans formulated using different cephalometric analyses has reported only a 40% concordance rate [5].

The variability in treatment approaches resulting from different cephalometric analysis systems can complicate clinical decision-making. This poses significant challenges for clinicians, particularly those with limited clinical experience, in providing accurate diagnostic support and guiding patients toward appropriate treatment. Consequently, artificial intelligence (AI)-based applications show potential for assisting clinicians in diagnostic and treatment planning. A substantial portion of these AI technologies consists of machine learning and large language models (LLMs), which are increasingly being utilized across various fields, including dentistry [6].

Among the increasingly utilized LLMs in recent years, ChatGPT models (OpenAI, San Francisco, Calif) have ushered in a significant shift in the field of language AI, owing to their extensive databases and pattern recognition capabilities [7, 8]. GPTs are based on intelligent human-computer interactions and are designed to simulate conversations with human users over the internet [9]. These models can generate fluent and coherent texts, answer questions in dialogues, perform visual analyses, translate languages, and accomplish numerous other language-related tasks [6, 10]. However, the potential for GPTs to provide incorrect answers, generate nonsensical content, or present misinformation as fact raises substantial concerns in the healthcare domain [6].

Most existing assessment scales primarily use standardized scoring approaches and lack a structured, itemized framework that can comprehensively evaluate diagnostic processes and treatment planning. In an era where AI platforms are expected to contribute to clinical decision support systems, this limitation has become more apparent. Diagnostic processes involve the simultaneous assessment of multiple clinical and contextual parameters, creating a complex, multidimensional structure. Similarly, treatment planning cannot be adequately evaluated using a single criterion or limited numerical score because it requires multifaceted clinical appraisal. Consequently, relying on a single aggregate score derived from an isolated scale does not provide a robust or reliable clinical evaluation. These considerations underscore the critical need for a comprehensive, purpose-specific index, particularly in contemporary clinical settings where AI-based GPTs are being evaluated for diagnostic and treatment support applications [11–13]. In this context, the Artificial Intelligence Based Diagnosis and Treatment Index (AIDTI) was developed to evaluate not only the diagnostic accuracy of GPTs but also their capacity for differential diagnosis, the clinical appropriateness of proposed treatment plans, the disclosure of potential risks and complications, and the ability to provide alternative treatment options. In doing so, AIDTI addresses the limitations of existing scales and enables a more comprehensive and systematic evaluation of GPT outputs in clinical contexts.

The reliability and quality of web-based information are of paramount importance because they influence patient cooperation, adherence to treatment, communication with physicians, and overall doctor–patient trust [14]. When used for diagnostic purposes, unreliable information can lead to erroneous diagnoses. Therefore, GPT-mediated diagnostic support tools should be thoroughly investigated. The aim of this study was to compare the responses of AI-based GPTs to text- and image-based prompts from LCs of skeletal Class I, II, and III malocclusions using the newly developed AIDTI.

Material and methods

For this study, ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University (approval number 2025/12–4, dated June 11, 2025). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study utilized 90 LCs obtained from patients who presented to the Department of Orthodontics at the Faculty of Dentistry, Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University for clinical examination. All LCs were anonymized prior to analysis by removing all personal identifiers. Lateral cephalometric radiographs used in this study were obtained during routine clinical practice using the Veraviewepocs 2D system (J. Morita Mfg. Corp., Kyoto, Japan) and were retrospectively retrieved from archival records. During image acquisition, it is assumed that patients were positioned in centric occlusion with relaxed lips and in a natural head position in accordance with the device’s standard protocol. The radiographic images were analyzed in JPEG format and exhibited variability in pixel dimensions due to their retrieval from clinical archives. Nevertheless, all images were evaluated under standardized viewing and assessment conditions to ensure consistency across analyses.

Skeletal classification was based on the ANB angle, with Class I defined as 0–4°, Class II as greater than 4°, and Class III as less than 0°. For each class, 30 LCs were selected. Class I LCs corresponded to individuals with normal maxillomandibular sagittal relationships, Class II LCs to patients with mandibular retrognathism, and Class III LCs to patients with maxillary retrognathism. The distribution of female and male patients was balanced across the groups. The selection of the GPT models used in this study was based on their frequent evaluation in recent literature and their representation as widely used examples of large language models in clinical and dental research contexts [10, 15–17]. In the first phase of the study, all LCs were directly uploaded sequentially and randomly submitted to the ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-o3 Pro, ChatGPT-5, and ChatGPT-5 Pro platforms using the prompt: “Provide the malocclusion diagnosis and formulate an appropriate orthodontic treatment plan for the patient represented in this lateral cephalometric radiograph.” Following each radiographic evaluation, the chat session was reset, and after initiating a new instance, the subsequent radiograph was uploaded to the respective GPT using the same standardized prompt. This study utilized all models through publicly accessible web-based platform interfaces with their default settings. The generated outputs reflect each model’s platform-defined default inference behavior.

In the second phase of the study, digital cephalometric analyses of all LCs were performed by a single researcher (A.Y.) using NemoCeph software (Nemotec, Madrid, Spain). The SNA, SNB, ANB, U1/NA, L1/NB, and GoGn/SN angles, along with the linear distances of U1/NA and L1/NB, obtained from these analyses were provided as text-based prompts to each GPT with the prompt: “Provide the malocclusion diagnosis and formulate an appropriate orthodontic treatment plan for the patient whose cephalometric measurements are given.” After each evaluation, the chat session was reset, and following the initiation of a new instance, the skeletal measurements of the next LC were submitted to the respective model using the same standardized prompt.

For each prompt presented in the form of either a radiograph or cephalometric measurement data, the responses generated by the respective AI platforms were analyzed, and the malocclusion diagnoses provided as text outputs were categorized as Class I, Class II, or Class III. In all responses, the classification was explicitly stated by the platforms; therefore, no additional clarification or further prompting was required.

All prompts were delivered to the respective LLMs using the same laptop (MacBook Air M3, 16 GB RAM; Apple, Cupertino, Calif), a 4.5 G internet connection, and a virtual private network (VPN) server (version 3.9; Astrill Systems Corp, Santa Clara, Calif). Each text- and image-based prompt was submitted to every GPT only once. When a model failed to provide a response, no additional clarification was given, and no rephrasing of the answer was requested. Furthermore, the prompts were not repeated by any other researcher. All responses obtained from the GPTs were individually recorded in a Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365 Apps, Microsoft Corporation, 2025) spreadsheet.

Development process of the Artificial Intelligence Based Diagnosis and Treatment Planning Index (AIDTI)

This study employed the Artificial Intelligence Based Diagnosis and Treatment Planning Index (AIDTI), a five-item evaluation tool developed by the researchers to assess the responses of AI-based LLM platforms to both direct image-based prompts involving LC uploads and text-based prompts containing cephalometric analysis measurements.

The index items were determined through content analysis conducted by a three-expert panel. A scoring system with a maximum of 10 points was established, where each item was rated using the options 0 (inadequate), 1 (partially adequate), and 2 (fully adequate). Validity and reliability analyses of the scale were performed using content validity index (CVI) [18], inter-rater reliability (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC) [19] and criterion validity (Pearson r) [20]. The content validity of the items was calculated based on evaluations by seven specialists, yielding I-CVI ≥ 0.78 for each item and S-CVI ≥ 0.90 at the scale level. Inter-rater reliability was assessed through independent scoring of the same 30 cases by two different experts and analyzed using the ICC(2,k) method, a two-way random-effects mean measurement model. The analysis results for both indices exceeded the acceptability threshold of ≥0.75, demonstrating a good level of agreement among the expert evaluations.

For criterion validity, two experts scored the responses of 30 cases using a classic 5-point Likert scale, and the relationship between these scores and those of the developed index was analyzed using Pearson correlation coefficients. The results exceeded the acceptance threshold of r ≥ 0.78, demonstrating a strong and statistically significant correlation between both scales and the reference criteria. Gold standard responses were established to enable the comparative analysis of GPTs’ competencies in clinical decision-making processes through the developed index. In this context, for each case, gold standard responses for diagnosis and treatment planning were independently developed by two expert clinicians based on a synthesis of current scientific literature and established clinical knowledge, and were subsequently finalized through consensus.

The responses generated by the GPTs were scored by two independent researchers using the developed AIDTI. In cases of disagreement between the evaluators, a third evaluator was consulted and a consensus score was established. Following the consensus procedure, a high level of agreement between the two evaluators was observed (ICC = 0.87). In addition, intra-examiner reliability was high (ICC = 0.83). Therefore, the scores obtained from a single evaluator were used as the reference scores. The evaluation criteria of the AIDTI and their corresponding descriptions are presented in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were statistically analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26; IBM Corporation, New York, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons between groups, while the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for pairwise comparisons. Within-group comparisons between text-based and image-based evaluations were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, based on the text and image outputs of the models, the ability to accurately classify each malocclusion category was assessed. Accuracy = (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN) (TP, true positives; TN, true negatives; FP, false positives; FN, false negatives). Sensitivity = TP/(TP + FN). Precision = TP/(TP + FP). Specificity = TN/(TN + FP). F1 score = 2 × (Precision × Sensitivity)/(Precision + Sensitivity).

Results

Results for the AIDTI

For diagnostic accuracy

In text-based prompts, the models’ mean scores ranged from 1.94 to 2.00, while in image-based prompts, the means ranged from 0.73 to 0.88. The Kruskal–Wallis test indicated no statistically significant differences between the models in either condition (text: p = 0.15; image: p = 0.716).

For capability for differential diagnosis

In text-based prompts, significant differences were observed between the models (p < 0.001), with GPT-5 pro achieving the highest mean score (1.90 ± 0.36) and GPT-4o the lowest (1.51 ± 0.56). In image-based prompts, the mean scores ranged from 0.40 to 0.67, with no statistically significant differences detected (p = 0.108).

Clinical appropriateness of the proposed treatment plan

In text-based prompts, the models’ mean scores ranged from 1.78 to 1.90, while in image-based prompts, the means ranged from 0.72 to 0.87. The Kruskal–Wallis test indicated no statistically significant differences between the models in text (p = 0.06) or image-based prompts (p = 0.664).

Disclosure of risks and complications associated with the treatment plan

In text-based prompts, significant differences were observed between the models (p < 0.001), with GPT-5 pro achieving the highest mean score (1.95 ± 0.20) and GPT-5 and GPT-4o the lowest (0.57 ± 0.82 and 0.60 ± 0.69, respectively). Significant differences were also found in image-based prompts (p < 0.001), where GPT-o3 pro achieved the highest mean score (1.05 ± 0.94) and GPT-5 the lowest (0.03 ± 0.18).

Ability to offer alternative treatment options

In text-based prompts, the mean scores ranged from 1.87 to 1.97, with no statistically significant differences observed between the models (p = 0.149). In image-based prompts, the means ranged from 0.64 to 0.87, also showing no significant differences (p = 0.32).

Total AIDTI score

Significant differences were observed in text-based prompts (p < 0.001), with GPT-5 pro achieving the highest total score (9.62 ± 1.13) and GPT-4o the lowest (7.83 ± 1.26). Significant differences were also found in image-based prompts (p = 0.007), where GPT-o3 pro obtained the highest mean score (4.16 ± 4.12) and GPT-5 the lowest (2.53 ± 3.19). Discussion

Various AI-assisted systems have been developed for orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. In this context, Li and Wang [22] developed a system that combines multi-task reinforcement learning and explainable AI to provide personalized and data-driven recommendations for orthodontic treatment planning. Although this system demonstrated high accuracy, its technical infrastructure requirements and need for user training have limited its clinical integration. Therefore, this study evaluated the competency of more accessible GPTs that require no technical setup and can process both text- and image-based prompts in orthodontic clinical scenarios.

In the field of orthodontics, LLMs have been able to provide accurate responses to questions involving specific topics such as clear aligners, temporary anchorage devices, and digital imaging [23], and have demonstrated moderate success in assessing bone age directly from hand-wrist radiographs without a text-based prompt [15]. These studies in the literature indicate that the models’ potential is not limited to information transfer but also extends to functions such as diagnosis and treatment planning. In this context, Rakhmatulla et al. [24] tested LLMs on virtual orthodontic cases via an API and reported that the models showed significant progress in diagnostic and treatment planning. However, since the model evaluations were conducted through direct programming rather than via the user-facing web interface, the obtained results may have limited applicability in reflecting real-world usage conditions.

Diagnosis and treatment planning are processes that require accurate interpretation of the clinical context and consideration of patient-specific variables [25]. In order to reliably assess the competencies of LLMs in these areas, studies should be conducted under conditions that align with clinical scenarios and utilize real user interfaces [26]. For such multidimensional analyses, it is necessary to develop a comprehensive and systematic evaluation tool. The AIDTI, developed for this purpose, enables the assessment of GPT performance using more realistic criteria. The index addresses overly simplified or context-insensitive inferences and clinically inappropriate treatment recommendations [6, 26], which are frequently highlighted in the literature, within a broader evaluative framework.

Recent studies have demonstrated the utility of LLMs in risk assessment, diagnosis, anomaly detection, appointment scheduling, and scientific research [27]. Within this scope, LLMs have been evaluated across various clinical domains including restorative treatment planning [28], oral lesion identification [29], and implantology support [30]. Similarly, in orthodontics, model performance has been assessed through responses to questions derived from clinical scenarios [17].

Beyond text-based research, the multimodal reasoning capacity of LLMs has also led to an increase in the number of analyses performed directly on radiographic or clinical images [15, 31, 32]. In a study evaluating oral mucosa lesions from photographs using ChatGPT-4o, Suárez et al. [32] reported a diagnostic accuracy of 58.2%, but noted high accuracy in treatment recommendations for correctly diagnosed cases. Hasei et al. [31], in their study using questions from the Japanese Orthopaedic Association specialization exam, compared the diagnostic accuracy of GPT-4o and o1 pro, reporting that GPT o1 pro achieved higher accuracy, particularly on image-based questions. Parallel to these findings, the present study demonstrated that the ‘pro’ models exhibited better performance in both image-based and text-based prompts. Another noteworthy finding was that GPT-o3 pro outperformed GPT-5 pro in image-based prompts. This result indicates that newer models do not always yield better outcomes and that model performance is not solely dependent on version updates. It is hypothesized that earlier versions, potentially exposed to broader datasets for longer durations, might have an advantage in pattern recognition and inference capabilities.

In the AIDTI evaluation, the high scores obtained by models when using text-based inputs may create a misleading perception of success. Prompts in which cephalometric measurement data acquired through conventional methods are presented in a text-based format may give the impression that GPTs possess the capability to establish diagnoses or formulate treatment plans, as they are able to accurately and consistently reproduce literature-based and textbook knowledge contained within their training data. However, this apparent performance should not be interpreted beyond the models’ capacity to present information and should be approached with caution. Indeed, the present study clearly demonstrates that GPTs are unable to directly extract measurement data by interpreting cephalometric radiographs. Moreover, diagnosis and treatment planning are not limited to the interpretation of a subset of cephalometric measurements, as applied in the present study, nor can they be reliably performed based solely on cephalometric evaluations. The processes of diagnosis and treatment planning are inherently complex and require the comprehensive and integrated assessment of numerous clinical parameters.

LLMs process LCs, which are composed of thousands of pixels with varying brightness levels, at the pixel level by converting them into high-dimensional embeddings and transforming these digitally encoded representations into computational language to generate verbal responses [33, 34]. During this process, attention mechanisms may focus on prominent deviations, leading to their overrepresentation while causing normative structures to remain less distinct within visual representations. Indeed, when evaluating GPT performance in malocclusion classification, all four models produced lower total scores for image-based prompts of skeletal Class I cases compared to Class II and Class III. This finding suggests that the models are prone to detecting orthodontic anomalies in image-based inputs and are less capable of adequately distinguishing Class I relationships, which lack significant sagittal skeletal discrepancies. In line with this observation, Grinberg et al. [35] similarly reported in their study evaluating ChatGPT-4’s differential diagnosis competency for oral mucosal lesions based on text-based inputs that the model exhibited higher sensitivity in detecting malignant lesions.

Regarding model performance in malocclusion classification, all models achieved higher total scores for image-based prompts in the Class II group. However, the applied performance metrics analysis revealed that this did not reflect true classification success. Extremely low specificity values observed in the Class II group indicate that the models tended to label the majority of radiographs as Class II, creating an artificial impression of success in this group. Therefore, evaluations based solely on total scores or a single metric may overlook such erroneous tendencies and lead to misleading conclusions [36]. In this context, model performance should be assessed from a multidimensional perspective, and success should be interpreted based on consistent results derived from multiple performance metrics and statistical analyses.

CNN-based AI models have demonstrated successful outcomes in cephalometric landmark detection and diagnostic assessment in orthodontics. The literature reports that models such as YOLOv3 and multi-stage convolutional neural networks operate with low error rates, comparable to inter-examiner variability [37–39]. In addition, AI systems have been shown to outperform human evaluators in certain challenging landmark identifications [37, 38]. Comparative studies further indicate that AI systems can achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to orthodontists, while semi-automated approaches significantly reduce analysis time [40]. The findings of the present study suggest that, although LLMs have the potential to provide useful outcomes in this domain, they have not yet demonstrated the same level of consistent and reliable performance as CNN-based models. Furthermore, considering the relatively high access costs of certain models and the energy demands associated with LLMs [41], it is important to critically evaluate whether the marginal benefits they provide justify these costs in all contexts.

Visual data are typically represented using Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining (CLIP)-based Vision Transformer (ViT) encoders. However, examination of current results indicates that these encoders are not semantically sufficient in medical contexts [42]. To improve visual analysis performance, training with higher-resolution and contextually rich images [43], using advanced encoders such as DINOv2, SigLIP, or CLIP-ConvNeXt, and employing projection layers like Q-former or Perceiver Resampler instead of simple linear projections for transferring visual information to the language model should be considered. Additionally, techniques such as Instruction Tuning and Direct Preference Optimization during training can enhance the transfer of visual context into the language model. When applied in combination with Multimodal In-Context Learning strategies, these approaches may increase the influence of visual inputs on model outputs and support more reliable clinical analyses [44].

The limitations of this study include the evaluation of GPT performance using only selected cephalometric measurement data, without incorporating additional clinical information such as intraoral and extraoral photographs, digital model analyses, or three-dimensional imaging data. Furthermore, given the stochastic nature of LLM outputs, model responses may exhibit substantial variability across different runs, which should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Additionally, the GPTs evaluated were limited to specific model versions, and their performance may vary with newer or alternative architectures. Validation with larger, multi-center sample sizes is also required to improve the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, because the AIDTI consists of only five items, relevant parameters such as patient satisfaction, treatment duration, and long-term clinical outcomes could not be assessed. In addition, the AIDTI assigns equal weights to all five subdimensions, which may not fully reflect their relative clinical importance in orthodontic decision-making. Future studies are recommended to directly compare LLM models with CNN-based models specifically developed for orthodontic applications in order to enable more comprehensive conclusions.

Conclusion

In text-based inputs, GPTs have the potential to convey information to a limited extent based on the data provided to them. However, this potential is evident only when numerically expressed cephalometric analysis data derived from LCs are directly supplied to the model. The reliable acquisition of such cephalometric measurement values requires that these analyses be performed by an orthodontist. Indeed, the findings of the present study demonstrate that LLMs are not capable of independently performing cephalometric analyses and lack the capacity for autonomous inference or clinical interpretation. Accordingly, they should not be regarded as independent tools for diagnosis or treatment planning.

Another important implication of this study is that the use of AIDTI enabled a systematic evaluation of the parameters assessed across the models. The multidimensional assessment framework provided by this index prevented performance evaluation from relying on a single metric and allowed for a more balanced and clinically relevant interpretation of the findings. Accordingly, AIDTI may serve as an appropriate evaluation index for future AI studies involving both text-based and image-based prompts.
