Learning a distance for the clustering of patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Guillaume Tejedor Y. Dominguez, Hugo Alarcan, Veronika Peralta, Nicolas Labroche, Philippe Corcia, Patrick Marcel, Hélène Blasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease with median survival of 3–5 years. Patient responses to treatments vary widely, highlighting the need for personalized care. Clustering patients based on disease progression could improve prognosis, guide clinical decision-making, and optimize clinical trial design. This study aimed to identify robust ALS patient clusters using ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) scores and to determine diagnostic parameters predictive of cluster membership, enabling earlier stratification and targeted management.MethodsData from the Tours ALS center registry (April 1997–October 2023) were analyzed; after preprocessing, 353 patients monitored every three months between January 2004 and July 2023 with ALSFRS-R, clinical, biological, and demographic data were retained. After preprocessing to handle missing or aberrant data, a weakly supervised approach labeled patient pairs based on their ALSFRS-R sequences. These labels were used to train a classifier to learn a distance for off-the-shelf clustering algorithms. Multiple configurations were tested, varying clustering algorithms, dimensionality reduction method, and number of clusters. Random Forest (RF) model predicted cluster membership from diagnostic parameters. Optimal clustering was selected using silhouette score, validated with Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Stability and robustness were assessed with the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and silhouette score respectively. Predictive performance was evaluated using specificity, sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Diagnostic parameters associated with clusters were identified using Kruskal-Wallis and chi-squared tests for continuous and categorical variables.ResultsThree clusters (n = 139, 121, 93) were identified, demonstrating strong separation (silhouette ≈ 0.6) and high stability of results (ARI ≈ 0.7). Survival differed significantly among clusters: over 50% of patients in the third cluster survived beyond 50 months, compared to less than 25% in the other clusters. Thirteen diagnostic parameters—including ALSFRS-R subscores, IgG levels, albumin quotient, and time to diagnosis—were key predictors of cluster membership. Cluster prediction achieved specificity and NPV ≈ 0.75, with close sensitivity and PPV compared to state-of-the-art methods.ConclusionThis framework successfully stratifies ALS patients into clinically meaningful clusters, revealing underlying disease heterogeneity and providing strong prognostic insight. Such classification can facilitate personalized care, guide therapeutic decisions, and inform the design of targeted interventions to improve outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13040-026-00579-5.

Background

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the degeneration of upper (UMN) and lower (LMN) motor neurons, leading to worsening muscle weakness, respiratory failure, and difficulties with speech and swallowing. The median survival following symptom onset is approximately three to five years. Currently, only Riluzole is approved [1] and offer limited benefits in slowing disease progression. Other therapeutics such as edaravone [2] and tofersen [3] are used in some specific cases. ALS presents with significant clinical variability, in regard to the site of onset, progression rate, and cognitive involvement, making diagnosis complex and often delayed by up to a year [4]. This unpredictability complicates the management of patients, delaying critical interventions like gastrostomy (percutaneous endoscopic and radiological gastrostomy), non-invasive ventilation, which could otherwise improve outcomes. The emotional burden on patients and caregivers is considerable, also further complicating decision-making. The identification of reliable biomarkers is crucial for improving ALS diagnosis [5] and prognosis [6]. Although neurofilament light chain (NFl) appears as a promising and robust biomarker, no single biomarker has been validated for routine clinical use so far [7]. Challenges in clinical translation stem from small sample sizes, a lack of longitudinal studies, and an emphasis on comprehensive rather than optimized data collection. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are driving a shift toward precision medicine in ALS. AI-based models can integrate multimodal biomarkers and cluster patients into clinically relevant clusters, offering individualized disease progression predictions. Several approaches have been proposed to cluster the ALS patients using longitudinal data. Most studies define clusters based on observed patterns of functional decline. Typically, these approaches extract specific variables from ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised (ALSFRS-R) score [8] sequences, using predefined thresholds to categorize patients. Commonly used variables include: the percentage of change from the initial ALSFRS-R score after six months proposed by Gomeni et al. [8] finding two clusters: one representing patients with slow disease progression (i.e., decline < 18.6%) and another with fast disease progression (i.e., decline > = 18.6%); the ALSFRS-R score at one year after the initial ALSFRS-R score introduced by Grollemund et al. [9] finding four clusters: score ≤ 10, 10 < score ≤ 20, 20 < score ≤ 30, and score > 30; and the D50 value1 suggested by Meyer et al. [10] proposing three clusters: severe (D50 < 20), intermediate (20 ≤ D50<40), and mild (D50 ≥ 40). A more advanced method proposed by Halbersberg et al. [11] calculates the dissimilarity between pairs of multi-dimensional sequences, each representing ALSFRS-R subscores, by summing Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) dissimilarities computed independently for each dimension. An off-the-shelf clustering algorithm is then applied varying the number of clusters between 4 and 6. However, none of these approaches converge toward a consensus clustering method, limiting their practical use in clinical settings for reliable prognostication.

ALS progression varies widely across patients, making prognosis and care planning difficult. Although ALSFRS-R scores are routinely collected, clinicians lack tools to translate these measurements into predictions of disease trajectories. Trajectory-based clustering provides a data-driven approach to identify clinically meaningful progression patterns and may support earlier patient stratification using routinely collected data.

Our present work aims to define clusters of ALS patients based on disease progression using innovative clustering methods and to characterize these clusters through clinical and biological data. The first objective is then to identify a robust method to cluster these ALSFRS-R sequences, the second objective is to assess the parameters at diagnosis that could represent these clusters. The final objective is to evaluate, in a preliminary way, the predictability of cluster membership from initial diagnostic data. Such an approach aims to explore whether trajectory-derived phenotypes, defined from longitudinal ALSFRS-R evolution, could be anticipated early in the disease course. Although exploratory, early identification of distinct progression patterns could potentially contribute to refining prognosis at diagnosis, improving patient stratification in clinical trials, and identifying patients more likely to experience rapid functional decline. Finally, we will discuss our findings with particular attention to the comparison of our clustering evaluation pipeline with those reported in the literature and to the exploratory nature of the predictive performances.

Methods

General methodology

Methodology is summarized in Figure 1 and detailed in the following subsections. After extracting the database containing participants’ data, several preprocessing steps were performed. This constitutes the final cohort of prepared data, which will be used for our study. To address the first objective, various clustering methods were applied to the ALSFRS-R sequences. Cluster evaluation was supported by both mathematical criteria and clinical expert assessment. Once clusters were identified, the second objective focused on determining which clinical and biological parameters at diagnosis differed significantly between clusters, suggesting their association with distinct patterns of disease progression. Finally, the third objective was to assess the relevance of these parameters based on their ability to accurately predict cluster membership at diagnosis.

Database and data processing

Data from the ALS reference center at Tours University Hospital were extracted from April 25, 1997, to October 24, 2023. Diagnostic is based on guidelines from revised El Escorial criteria [12]. Patients who fulfilled the criteria corresponding to ‘suspected’ cases were included. The patients’ diagnostic status was continuously reassessed during routine follow-up visits. Cases with revised diagnoses were finally excluded from our study. Although no strict minimum follow-up duration was required, all included patients were monitored longitudinally at the ALS centre every three months, enabling putative diagnostic reassessment over time.

ALSFRS-R assessments are generally conducted on a quarterly basis, although intervals between visits may vary depending on clinical follow-up schedules, disease progression, organizational constraints, or logistical factors such as travel distance for patients within the large catchment area.

All demographic and clinical data—including sex, site of onset, age at onset, diagnostic delay, ALSFRS-R scores and subscores, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), and Forced Vital Capacity (FVC)—as well as biological parameters systematically recorded during patient care were extracted from the information system of the university hospital center. This project adhered to the routine practice ethical protocol. The retrospective use of healthcare data was conducted in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the French regulatory framework for health data (RNIPH). Data collection was based on the principle of patient non-opposition and received approval from the relevant ethical and regulatory authorities.

Data processing was conducted through a series of steps defined and validated by clinical and biological experts.

The parameters of exclusions are the followings (Fig. 1): Patients with fewer than five ALSFRS-R assessments; patients without ALSFRS-R subscores; sequences exhibiting clinically improbable patterns such as increases of more than two points between two consecutive ALSFRS-R assessments; Patients with a gap of more than 30 days between the first visit date and the first ALSFRS-R record, avoiding any bias related to duration.

For the first criterion, a threshold of at least five visits was applied to ensure a reliable characterization of ALSFRS-R sequences. This choice reflects a balance between having enough observations to obtain representative information from sequences (e.g., slopes or shape-related features) and preserving a sufficient sample size, as stricter thresholds would substantially reduce the available data.

For the third criterion, excluding patients with high increases between consecutive ALSFRS-R assessments is applied because such increases are generally considered implausible in the context of ALS progression, which is characterized by a steadily worsening functional trajectory over time [13, 14].

The final criterion aims to minimize bias related to the definition of the disease timeline origin. As the ALSFRS-R is typically assessed during the initial specialized consultation, an interval exceeding 30 days between the first visit and the first ALSFRS-R measurement may reflect disease progression occurring after the initial evaluation, thereby introducing inconsistencies when aligning patient trajectories. The 30-day threshold was therefore selected as a pragmatic compromise to ensure temporal consistency between baseline clinical assessment and functional evaluation, while preserving the largest possible number of complete ALSFRS-R sequences for clustering analysis.

Additional considerations were made to include clinical and biological parameter values at the time of diagnosis. Specifically, for each parameter, a 120-day window centered on the patient’s first visit date (i.e., start of the monitoring process) is defined (i.e., 60 days before and after), and the value closest to the first visit date within this interval was selected. Thereafter, three filtering steps are applied to retain parameters with enough data and without strong correlations to other parameters. First, parameters with more than 50% of missing values are excluded. Second, categorical parameters that consistently convey the same information (i.e., often have the same value for a given parameter) are removed. Irrelevant parameters were removed after discussion with the biologist and clinician. Finally, pairs of numerical parameters with a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.9 or higher are eliminated to reduce redundancy. This correlation analysis was performed on all numerical variables, including clinical (e.g., ALSFRS-R subscores), demographic (e.g., age at onset), and biological parameters.

Weak-supervised learning clustering approach to cluster ALSFRS-R sequences

Clustering method

Figure 2 presents an overview of our framework. The ALSFRS-R sequences from the patients are used as input. For each pair of sequences, descriptive variables (defined later in this section), are extracted to characterize their differences. These variables are then used to learn a distance between patients. This distance is learned by employing a weak-supervised method. The proposed distance is paired with an off-the-shelf clustering method, enabling the identification of clusters of patients.

From the identified features, a set of descriptive variables describing the difference between a pair of ALSFRS-R sequences were proposed. Each descriptive variable is normalized between 0 and 1 using the Min-Max Scaler function. Spearman correlation is used to filter out correlated descriptive variables (i.e., Spearman correlation > = 0.7) if any.

These descriptive variables are then used by a weakly supervised method that employs a voting-based approach [16] to decide whether patient pairs should be clustered together (T), separated (S), or left undetermined (U). Unlike fully supervised learning, which relies on ground-truth labels, and unsupervised learning, which operates without any labels, weak supervision leverages inexpensive but potentially noisy label sources. In this work, expert knowledge serves as the primary labeling source, implemented through multiple labeling functions that process the extracted descriptive variables to assign a label (T, S, or U) to each patient pair. The outputs of these functions are aggregated using a probabilistic voting system to estimate the most likely label. A linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) is then trained on the labeled pairs to learn a weighted distance measure, referred to as the Weak-Supervised Distance (WSD), which captures the relative importance of each labeling function. Further details of this approach are provided in Sect. 5 of the Supplementary materials and in a publication presented at the ABDIS 2025 conference [17].

Clustering application

Protocol

Learned distances are stored in a matrix, which serves as input for various clustering methods. To enhance cluster detection, the use of a dimensionality reduction technique on the matrix is also explored. Concretely, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) is used. UMAP achieves a compromise between preserving local and global structures in the projected data, and the appropriate choice of its parameters can induce space distortions that are beneficial for clusters identification [18]. Clustering methods are then applied to the resulting low-dimensional representations. To evaluate the impact of dimensionality reduction, clustering performances are compared with and without UMAP. Furthermore, the impact of using different clustering methods is also investigated. The following clustering methods are considered:

K-means (KME): Applied only to UMAP representations, as it requires vector space coordinates to compute centroids.K-medoids (KMD): Applied to both precomputed matrices and UMAP representations.Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering (AHC): Applied to both precomputed matrices and UMAP representations, using the “complete” linkage criterion to favor the formation of compact clusters.

Finally, the number of clusters tested ranges from 2 to 6, as state-of-the-art studies suggest different numbers of clusters from 2 to 6. Moreover, using a larger number of clusters would complicate interpretation. All these variations lead to multiple combinations called workflows, producing different clustering outcomes for evaluation. Workflow names follow the format: ‘UMAP, if applied’_’clustering method ‘_’number of clusters’. In total, 25 possible workflows are evaluated.

Validation from quality measure

The classical Silhouette score is used to assess the quality of the clustering. Silhouette score is an internal measure (i.e., assesses the clusters using only the data themselves, without requiring external labels) that favors compact, well-separated clusters [19]. For each patient, it measures the difference between cohesion (average distance to patients in the same cluster) and separation (average distance to the nearest cluster) [20]. To assess the overall quality of the clustering structure, the average of all individual patient silhouette scores is computed. The averaged silhouette score ranges from − 1 to + 1: a positive score indicates a good clustering, with values close to + 1 suggesting well-separated and compact clusters; a score near 0 indicates overlapping clusters, and a negative score indicates that patients are closer to patients in another cluster than to patients in its own cluster.

Out of the 25 workflows we evaluated, workflows with a silhouette score lower than 0.5 are filtered out, as such scores indicate poor clustering structure and suggest that the patients may not be well matched to their assigned clusters or are too close to neighboring clusters. Moreover, those with an entropy lower than 0.5 are also filtered out. The workflow with the best silhouette is selected.

A stability check was applied to the best-performing workflow to evaluate whether the resulting clusters remain consistent across multiple runs. To ensure a stringent assessment, the entire pipeline was re-estimated within each resampling fold. The dataset was split into five subsets. At each iteration, the distance metric was learned using four subsets (training set), and the resulting workflow—including all processing steps such as dimensionality reduction and clustering—was applied to assign clusters to all patients. The resulting partition was then compared with the partition obtained when the workflow was trained on the full dataset. Agreement between partitions was quantified using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) [21]. This procedure was repeated for each possible 4/5 subset, resulting in five ARI values, for which the mean and standard deviation were computed.

To further evaluate the robustness of the selected workflow and limit the risk of optimistic bias due to exploratory workflow selection, we performed an additional validation based on repeated random train–test splits. Using a 3-fold cross-validation strategy, the patient cohort was randomly divided into a training subset and an independent testing subset. The distance metric was learned using only the training data. The learned distance metric was then applied to clustering workflow to the testing subset in order to assign patients to clusters.

For each split, the clustering quality was evaluated on the testing subset using the silhouette score. This procedure was repeated multiple times with different random partitions of the data in order to evaluate the stability of the clustering structure and its associated survival discrimination.

The distribution of silhouette scores was then compared with the values obtained when the workflow was trained using the full patient cohort.

Confrontation with clinical data

For each workflow, we also have evaluated the quality of the resulting clusters with 2 more criteria: Patient distribution: The distribution of patients among clusters is analyzed by counting the number of patients per cluster to detect imbalances. This is important, as highly uneven clusters with very few patients may indicate outliers and make it challenging to draw meaningful conclusions. To quantify this imbalance, we compute the normalized Shannon entropy [22] of the cluster sizes. Normalization ensures that the entropy values are scaled between 0 and 1, providing a standardized quantitative measure of how evenly patients are distributed across clusters: A value of 0 indicates that clusters are completely unbalanced, while higher values approaching 1 indicate more balanced clusters.Survival analysis: This criterion assesses whether the identified clusters exhibit distinct survival curves using Kaplan-Meier. It is a non-parametric statistical tool used to estimate the probability of survival over time, typically in clinical settings. In this context, it helps assess how long patients in each cluster tend to survive and whether there are significant differences in survival curves between clusters. A clear separation between Kaplan-Meier curves indicates that the clusters differ statistically but may also represent clinically meaningful subpopulations.

To compare the survival curves of different clusters, a log-rank test is applied to each pair of curves [23]. A p-value below 0.05 indicates a significant survival difference, suggesting that the two survival curves differ. This test is applied to every pair of curves and we report the maximum p-value among all comparisons. Kaplan-Meier is applied from disease onset to the last date of news (i.e., last date of visit or date of death). Visual presentation of the Kaplan–Meier curves is also used to support observed differences if any from the selected workflow.

To align with the log-rank test, workflows with non-significant p-values (i.e., p ≥ 0.05) are further filtered out. workflows that produce highly imbalanced clusters are also removed. This process results in a final set of workflows.

Characterization of clusters based on parameters at diagnosis

After identifying the optimal workflow, additional statistical methods were employed to analyze the relationship between clinical and biological parameters at diagnosis and the predefined clusters, with the goal of identifying parameters that show significant differences between clusters. Standard descriptive statistics, including the median and interquartile range (Q1–Q3), were computed for key clinical and demographic variables at diagnosis to ensure a comprehensive characterization of the clusters. Subsequently, appropriate inferential statistical tests were applied to assess statistically significant differences between clusters: for quantitative parameters, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test [24] is applied. For categorical parameters, the chi-squared test is used to assess homogeneity and goodness of fit [25]. Given the large number of parameters to test, p-values are adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) method. After computing the p-value for each parameter, non-significant parameters with a p-value greater than or equal to 0.05 are excluded.

Internal validation of cluster prediction

To evaluate predictive models, Internal validation is commonly used to assess model stability and generalizability [26]. Therefore, we then simulate the practical use of the model by predicting the cluster assignment of a part of patients, based on the significative clinical and biological parameters at diagnosis found from the previous section. Identified clusters from patients were then used as ground truth. To evaluate this scenario, internal cross-validation is performed: 80% of the patients were used to train the classifier, and the remaining 20% represent the unseen patients by the classifier where it predicts their cluster membership (i.e., test set). this internal validation used the 20% test data to assess the predictions and the pertinence of the parameters for prediction. For each fold, specificity, sensitivity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) are computed. We then report the arithmetic mean and standard deviation of these metrics across all folds. We propose to split in n=5 folds. Then, quality measures are computed 5 times on different test sets each.

The Random Forest (RF) classifier is used, as it is both widely applied in progression prediction research and consistently among the top-performing models [10, 17]. RF is also used to calculate parameter importance to identify which parameters have the greatest influence on the predictions. This is done by computing the mean decrease in impurity (Gini index) when a variable is permuted.

Another experiment train RF on the totality of the patients, to obtains the importance for each parameter.

Selection and comparison to state-of-the-art approaches

The performance of our workflow was evaluated by comparing it on the same dataset with several state-of-the-art approaches. These approaches were chosen because they also use the ALSFRS(-R) score for patient clustering. To ensure a fair comparison, we re-implemented these approaches, described in the Background section, and applied them to the dataset detailed in the Database section.

For each state-of-the-art approach, we have followed the original settings recommended by the authors. Rule-based approaches such as those from Gomeni et al. [8], Grollemund et al. [9], and Meyer et al. [10] use fixed thresholds and are not combined with clustering algorithms or UMAP. In contrast, the Halbersberg et al. [11] approach employs agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) on a dissimilarity matrix, testing between 4 and 6 clusters. Additionally, we explore whether applying UMAP to the dissimilarity matrix from Halbersberg et al. approach can further improve clustering performance.

We also present survival curves from the state-of-the-art approaches to visually compare them with the selected workflow.

Results

Database after data preprocessing

Experiments were conducted on an initial set of 913 patients. At the time of diagnosis, the data comprise 120 clinical parameters, including 68 categorical parameters such as sex and site of onset, and 54 numerical parameters such as age at onset and diagnostic delay. A statistic description of these parameters at diagnosis are presented in Supplementary materials Tables 1 and Table 2.

Additionally, 16 longitudinal clinical parameters have been recorded since the beginning of patient monitoring, including ALSFRS-R score and subscores, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), and Forced Vital Capacity (FVC). Up to 210 longitudinal biological parameters have been recorded.

The exclusion of patients with fewer than five clinical visits resulted in 375 excluded individuals. 12 patients without ALSFRS-R subscores are also filtered out. The step taking into account the sequences flagged by biologists’ clinicians as abnormal excludes 154 patients. Finally, 19 more patients whose ALSFRS-R monitoring began more than 30 days after their first clinical visit were removed.

At the end, 353 patients are retained, leading to 62,128 pairs. Spearman correlation test did not reveal any highly correlated descriptive variables (with a correlation coefficient ≥ 0.7). Therefore, all variables are retained.

In addition, 99 diagnostic clinical parameters with more than 50% missing values, comprising 59 categorical and 40 numerical variables, were excluded. Additionally, 145 longitudinal biological parameters with over 50% missing data were excluded. Five additional categorical clinical parameters at diagnosis were excluded because they consistently exhibited the same value for the majority of patients. Furthermore, 15 longitudinal biological and 7 diagnostic clinical numerical parameters were filtered out due to high Spearman correlation with other variables. Percentage of missing values for each remaining parameter is depicted in Supplementary Table 3.

For descriptive variables used to cluster patients, spearman correlation analysis did not reveal strong correlations among them; therefore, all descriptive variables were retained to cluster the ALSFRS-R sequences.

After all preprocessing steps, 353 patients monitored every three months between January 2004 and July 2023 with ALSFRS-R, clinical, biological, and demographic data were retained. Duration between consecutive assessments correspond to an average of 87 days and a standard deviation of 66 days.

Sequence-based and sigmoid-based feature extraction

Extracted features from ALSFRS-R sequences are used to form a set of descriptive variables describing the difference between a pair of ALSFRS-R sequences: the absolute differences in the monitoring (DURATION_DIFF), initial score (FIRST_SCORE_DIFF), overall (SLOPE_DIFF), steepest consecutive slope (HIGHEST_CONSECUTIVE_SLOPE_DIFF). Distributions are depicted in Supplementary materials Fig. 3.

Extracted features from the learned sigmoid also provides a set of 3 descriptive variables: Score at one year (ALS_SCORE_M12_DIFF), percentage of change at six months (PC_CHANGE_M6_DIFF), and D50 value (D50_DIFF). Distributions are depicted in Supplementary materials Fig. 4.

Each descriptive variable is normalized between 0 and 1 using the Min-Max Scaler function. Spearman correlation is used to filter out correlated descriptive variables (i.e., Spearman correlation > = 0.7) if any.

The best-performing sigmoid function used to extract features from the sigmoid achieves a mean error below 2 on the ALSFRS-R score for the majority of patients in the cohort. This error margin is comparable to the variability occasionally observed among physicians. The median error is 1.43, indicating that for half of the population, the prediction error remains below this value. This corresponds to a deviation of approximately 1.4348 ≈ 3%, which is insignificant and therefore deemed acceptable for our experiment.

Discussion

Patients and features filtering

We have presented a detailed data preprocessing pipeline to filter out irrelevant patients (Fig. 1). The following patients are filtered out: Patients with fewer than five ALSFRS-R assessments; patients without ALSFRS-R subscores; sequences exhibiting clinically improbable patterns such as increases of more than two points between two consecutive ALSFRS-R assessments; Patients with a gap of more than 30 days between the first visit date and the first ALSFRS-R record, avoiding any bias related to duration.

It should be noted that there is no universal consensus on the minimum number of ALSFRS-R assessments required. Different studies employ varying thresholds depending on their design, statistical methods, and research objectives. This variability highlights the trade-off between ensuring accurate characterization of ALSFRS-R sequences and maintaining cohort representativeness.

Filtering criteria substantially reduced the cohort size, as 353 patients were retained from the initial 913. While this reduction inevitably decreases statistical power, the aim of this study was to construct the analysis on the basis of reliable longitudinal data rather than maximizing sample size. The present work should therefore be viewed as a proof-of-concept investigation focused on establishing a robust methodological framework that could later be replicated and validated in larger cohorts. For features selection, correlation thresholds were selected to reduce redundancy among variables while preserving clinically meaningful information. In our preprocessing, we observed that the distribution of correlation coefficients differed between descriptive variables extracted from ALSFRS-R sequences and clinical/biological parameters at diagnosis, making a single universal threshold inappropriate: applying the same cutoff would either remove too many informative variables or leave strongly redundant features. A threshold of 0.7 was therefore applied to descriptive variables to remove highly correlated features, while a more permissive threshold of 0.9 was used for clinical/biological parameters at diagnosis, allowing retention of variables that, despite moderate correlation, may still carry distinct clinical relevance. These thresholds were guided by established recommendations in the literature regularly used in medicine [27], and reflect an empirical balance between reducing multicollinearity and maintaining interpretability.

Innovative approach of ALSFRS-R clustering

We introduce a method to compare ALSFRS-R sequences using an advanced weakly-supervised approach combining a probabilistic voting system with a linear SVM. The resulting distance matrices are paired with clustering methods. We propose different workflows by varying the number of clusters, clustering methods, and incorporating UMAP for dimensionality reduction. In total, 25 workflows were evaluated; the best-performing one was selected and compared against state-of-the-art methods.

The Presented state-of-the-art approaches (namely GOM, GRO and MEY) mainly focus to cluster the patients using threshold rules applied to the ALSFRS-R score at specific time points. This strategy is limited because it relies on a single criterion and is highly sensitive to small score variations around the thresholds, which may not reflect meaningful clinical differences. Moreover, these methods do not consider multiple criteria or the temporal progression of the disease, which could improve the robustness of cluster assignment [11]. To our knowledge, only HAL approach proposes a comparison measure between sequences for evaluating pairs of ALSFRS subscore sequences using DTW dissimilarities computed independently for each subscore [11]. These precomputed dissimilarities are organized into a matrix, on which an off-the-shelf clustering algorithm is applied, using the full monitored sequence. However, they do not justify why DTW would be more appropriate than other comparison measures to cluster the patients based on ALSFRS sequences. Additionally, DTW was originally developed to align two time series of unequal lengths, under the assumption that the sequences are synchronous with a constant time interval between visits [28]. However, in most ALS datasets, patient visits occur asynchronously at irregular intervals, making DTW not suitable in such contexts.

The clusters identified in our study represent distinct temporal sequences of functional decline corresponding broadly to slow, intermediate, and rapid progression patterns described in ALS. Importantly, our approach suggests that these trajectories may be predicted early in the disease course using routinely collected ALSFRS-R data. For clinicians, this could support earlier anticipation of functional decline and more proactive care planning, including timely introduction of mobility, communication, respiratory, or nutritional support. Beyond individual care, trajectory-based stratification may also help reduce heterogeneity in clinical trials and improve patient selection.

Clustering validation

We have proposed a clustering evaluation pipeline that goes beyond traditional quality metrics by integrating clinically grounded criteria. Rather than relying solely on internal measures of cluster quality, we complement them with survival analysis to assess clinical relevance. This dual perspective ensures that the clusters we retain are not only statistically valid but also meaningful in a medical context, capturing distinct survival patterns that align with physicians’ observations. In doing so, our approach bridges a critical gap between technical evaluation and medical interpretation.

The Kaplan–Meier estimator is an advantage that helps to identify clusters with distinct survival patterns over time, ranging from short to average to long survival, thereby confirming the relevance of the identified clusters and validating the survival curves observed by physicians.

This multi-criteria evaluation framework enables a nuanced, application-relevant assessment of cluster quality, which is rarely addressed thoroughly in the existing literature [29]. To our knowledge, no state-of-the-art contributions incorporate internal metrics such as the silhouette score to assess intrinsic cluster separation, a potentially limiting omission as such metrics provide valuable insights into whether the discovered structure is genuinely meaningful. To validate both performance and relevance, our approach is compared against state-of-the-art methods.

The most effective workflows mainly used UMAP and involved 2–4 clusters, with 3 clusters showing the best separation providing a silhouette ≈ 0.61 (Table 4 Supplementary materials). Notably, WSD_UMAP_KMD_3 workflow depicts well distinct survival curves with confidence intervals that almost never overlap (Fig. 3).

Stability provided promising results with an ARI of 0.659 ± 0.093 showing that obtained clusters are well stable after several iterations.

Robustness studies demonstrate a silhouette of 0.518 ± 0.192. These results shows that the selected workflow captures reproducible well separated clusters.

Compared to state-of-the-art methods (Fig. 5), GRO produces more overlapping survival curves, whereas GOM and MEY yield better-separated curves. This show that having more than 3 clusters, clustering approaches tend to have difficulties to propose well separated curves. GOM and MEY have a more balanced distribution of patients across clusters. However, GOM identifies only two clusters, which may limit its ability to capture a wider range of disease progression forms. It is important to note that WSD_UMAP_KMD_3 show that clustered sequences (Fig. 4) seem to be slightly different from his survival curves. Indeed, we observe that the survival curve for cluster 0 (blue) declines more rapidly than survival curve from cluster 2 (green), although cluster 0 extends longer for a few patients. However, in the clustered sequences, those from cluster 0 tend to end earlier than those from cluster 2. This can be explained by the presence of few patients in cluster 0 whose last follow-up dates end much later than their last recorded ALSFRS-R score.

Having a silhouette score lower than 0.5 and non-significant log-rank p-value, HAL is filtered out showing that proposed clusters are not well-separated with overlapped survival curves. Increasing the number of clusters can lead to a decline in the silhouette score, indicating poorer separation between clusters.

The clusters identified in this study broadly correspond to the well-known slow, intermediate, and fast ALS progression patterns described in the literature. The identification of new phenotypes was not the primary objective of this work. Rather, our contribution lies in the proposed methodological framework for clustering longitudinal ALSFRS-R sequences and, more importantly, in predicting cluster membership at the time of diagnosis. The fact that the identified clusters align with previously reported progression patterns ensures clinical interpretability and allows newly diagnosed patients to be assigned to clinically meaningful trajectory groups. Furthermore, our approach relies on multiple characteristics derived from ALSFRS-R sequences rather than a single criterion, and achieves improved or comparable prediction performance compared with state-of-the-art methods.

Relevant predictors previously described

From our proposed approach (Table 3), Kruskal-Wallis tests identified significative clinical numeric parameters (e.g., Progression rate, ALSFRS-R subscores, FVC, time to diagnosis) between clusters, while categorical variables like disease onset were not predictive, indicating that disease onset may not be a systematic relevant parameter to characterize disease progression even though it is regularly used in studies [26]. Only two biological markers, albumin quotient and CSF IGG, were significant.

In Random Forest models, variable importance is based on the mean decrease in impurity (Gini index), meaning variables that improve prediction accuracy across trees receive higher importance scores. These parameters were therefore highlighted for their predictive value within the available dataset.

Biochemically, the albumin quotient reflects blood–CSF barrier integrity, with higher values indicating increased permeability. CSF IgG levels, particularly when interpreted alongside the albumin quotient, may provide indirect insight into central nervous system immune activity, where disproportionate increases can suggest intrathecal synthesis and potential inflammation.

Thus, their inclusion in the model may reflect differences in barrier permeability or inflammatory status between patient groups.

The presence of the ALSFRS-R score and its subscores is expected by the use of descriptive variables extracted from ALSFRS-R sequences to cluster the patients, which aids in their identification.

State-of-the-art approaches identifies similar significant parameters (Table 5) where progression rate, time to diagnosis and ALSFRS-R are the only common parameters between all approaches. MEY only identify clinical parameters. However, GRO and GOM shows significative biological parameters (i.e., creatinin, LDH, SVC sitting percent predicted, total bilirubin). The number of parameters vary with GRO identifying 18 parameters while MEY identifying seven parameters showing a variation of significant parameters.

These results should be interpreted with caution, as the number of clusters may vary depending on the approach used leading to vary the identified parameters.

Prediction performances

In this exploratory prediction work, RF provided mixed results on the test set with our approach (Table 4). It achieved high specificity (0.747 ± 0.02) and NPV (0.748 ± 0.023). However, sensitivity (0.503 ± 0.041) and PPV (0.5 ± 0.042) were lower. These results highlight the difficulty of providing personalized care to patients when data are missing. They also emphasize the need for better monitoring of clinical and biological parameters to improve diagnostic predictions and avoid losing patients. Interestingly, MEY provided, with the same number of clusters than WSD_UMAP_KDM_3, overall comparable results on four quality measures (Table 6). GRO, with four clusters, outperforms WSD_UMAP_KMD_3 in most metrics except for PPV and specificity. Although GOM shows higher performance due to its use of only two clusters, this limits the identification of a richer and more nuanced representation of patient heterogeneity. It is then more preferable to identify at least 3 clusters.

MEY achieves comparable predictive performance than WSD_UMAP_KMD_3 but with a lower entropy (0.752 against 0.987). Indeed, MEY produces highly unbalanced clusters of 230, 97, and 26 patients. Since most patients belong to one cluster, the RF model has a higher chance of making correct predictions by predicting this majority cluster. For instance, the probability of a patient belonging to the majority cluster is 230 / (230 + 97+26) × 100 ≈ 65% while for the smallest cluster it is only 26 / (230 + 97+26) × 100 ≈ 7%.

MEY’s drawback partly stems from a strong cluster imbalance, which can bias predictions and inflate performance. Our approach can be said to be more thorough, since learning a distance based on several extracted properties from the sequence allows adjusting at which point two ALSFRS-R sequences are considered similar.

We also observe that in this proof of concept, Random Forest was used with default hyperparameters, leaving open the possibility that proper tuning could narrow the performance gap between our approach and those from the state-of-the-art. Finally, as the number of significant parameters vary across the approaches, the corresponding amount of missing data may also differ, which may introduce uncertainty in the reported results.

These findings suggest that routinely collected ALSFRS-R data may support earlier stratification of patients at diagnosis and help anticipate disease progression sequences. Such information could assist clinicians in planning care more proactively, including earlier initiation of respiratory monitoring, nutritional support, and assistive technologies for communication or mobility. In addition, this approach may improve clinical trial design by enabling enrichment of study cohorts with patients exhibiting specific progression patterns, potentially increasing statistical power and reducing trial duration.

It is important to note that the clustering is defined using longitudinal ALSFRS-R sequences, whereas the predictive analysis relies only on information available at the time of diagnosis. In this sense, the model aims to evaluate whether early clinical measures can approximate sequence-defined phenotypes that are otherwise observable only retrospectively from longitudinal follow-up.

Limitations

We acknowledge the following three mild limitations in our work: (1) dataset cleaning leading to a small cohort and parameter exclusion, (2) no external validation and (3) potential optimistic bias due to the exploratory nature of our work. We detail these limitations.

Filtering criteria substantially reduced the cohort size after data preparation, as 353 patients were retained from the initial 913. It is also important to note that the retrospective nature of the dataset introduces potential biases related to data completeness and heterogeneity in clinical assessments. Due to substantial missing data at diagnosis, the predictive model was trained on a restricted subset of variables which limits the finding of additional parameters that could have been missed. Then, parameter exclusion due to missingness may influence the set of variables retained in each method and could potentially bias the interpretation of variable importance. An additional concern is the potential bias introduced by patients with slower progression who contribute to a higher number of timepoints. These individuals may disproportionately influence model estimates.

Another limitation of this study is the absence of external validation, as all analyses were conducted on a single-center dataset. Further studies are needed using a prospective methodology to maximize both the quantity and quality of the data. This includes standardized follow-up and more comprehensive biomarker data to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the findings. We acknowledge that the predictive performance observed in this study remains modest when compared to established prognostic markers. This likely reflects both the intrinsic heterogeneity of ALS and the fact that the clusters are derived from longitudinal functional data, which are only partially captured at diagnosis. While the current study provides valuable insights, the absence of external validation means that the generalizability of the identified clusters remains to be confirmed. It is also worth noting that our analysis is based on a cohort of 353 patients, a sample size that, while modest, reflects the rarity of ALS and the challenges of collecting such data. Given the well-documented heterogeneity of ALS, these preliminary findings highlight the potential of our approach and emphasize the need for future studies across multiple independent cohorts to fully assess its generalizability and clinical utility.

Clustering and performance evaluation were conducted on the same dataset which potentially leads to optimistic performance estimates. However, clustering remained stable across data subsets, suggesting a limited impact on the validity of the results.

Finally, evaluating clustering workflows on the entire dataset may introduce optimistic bias and potentially overestimates the reported evaluation metrics. Since multiple workflows were explored on the same dataset, some observed performances may partly reflect dataset-specific effects rather than fully robust clustering structures. Consequently, the current evaluation framework does not formally assess whether some of the obtained results could occur by chance.

Perspectives

The dataset proposed for these experiments contains relatively few patients, which could limit performance. However, its main advantage lies in facilitating direct collaboration with expert physicians. Moreover, the local and contextual relevance of this dataset makes it particularly valuable for the study of this paper.

Before any clinical application, the proposed approach should be externally validated using an independent dataset. While several datasets have been employed in ALS research, most are either private or accessible only upon request, limiting their availability access [29]. One of the most widely used is the PRO-ACT dataset, comprising data from more than 10,000 patients and covering a wide range of clinical and biological parameters. Other datasets, such as those from hospitals in Paris (France) and Jena (Germany), are also used but contain substantially fewer patients access [29].

Despite its large size, the PRO-ACT dataset has notable limitations. It combines patients from 23 different clinical trials, resulting in inconsistencies in follow-up frequency and unit conventions. Furthermore, it only includes data from the time of diagnosis onward, with no records from the first visit date. Finally, the absence of direct contact with the physicians limits the contextual understanding of the data. Challenges remain, particularly due to the variability of available patient data and the limited predictive value of biological parameters at diagnosis. In this study, neurofilament data, although considered promising biomarkers [26], were not included as they were not yet available. Likewise, data on comorbidities and information regarding supportive interventions (e.g., NIV), which would have been useful for this study, were also unavailable. However, it is important to note that, to date, these factors have not been consistently identified in the literature as independent predictors of survival. Therefore, their potential impact on the clustering structure and associated outcomes is likely to be limited.

Future work should focus on evaluating the proposed framework using larger and more diverse datasets, such as PRO-ACT, often used as a valuable resource for validation [29], refining distance metric by incorporating additional descriptive variables, and exploring alternative classifiers to better capture the similarity between patients. For instance, Henk-Jan Westeneng et al. [30] introduced a model for predicting a composite survival endpoint in individual ALS patients. Testing such a model in the context of predicting clusters of disease progression would be a promising avenue. These enhancements may uncover new clusters that illustrate novel patterns of disease progression and reveal previously undetected relationships between clinical and biological parameters. Moreover, improving the completeness of clinical and biological data at diagnosis is essential to minimize the exclusion of parameters and patients. Ultimately, such improvements could strengthen the characterization of clusters, facilitate the discovery of new biomarkers, and contribute to a deeper understanding and improved management of ALS.

Future work could explore the impact of varying the minimum and number of ALSFRS-R assessments on model performance and cohort composition. By systematically testing different thresholds, it would be possible to evaluate whether predictive accuracy can be maintained while including more patients, particularly those with rapidly progressive disease or shorter follow-up. Such analyses could also help quantify potential biases introduced by patients with a high number of visits and slower progression. Overall, investigating flexible approaches to ALSFRS-R sequences inclusion may improve both the clinical applicability of predictions and the representativeness of study cohorts.

Robustness analyses showed that the clustering structure obtained with the selected workflow remained relatively stable when evaluated using repeated train–test splits. In particular, the mean silhouette scores and their variability were comparable to those obtained when the workflow WSD_UMAP_KMD_3 was trained on the full dataset, suggesting that the identified clusters capture reproducible patterns in the data. However, additional studies should be done to build a statistical test that show that we do not have optimistic bias in results when we consider that a workflow is better than another one. Future works should expand the strategy to assess the stability of all other best workflows (e.g., WSD_UMAP_AHC_2, WSD_UMAP_KME_2).

Finally, our future work will focus on reducing potential source of optimistic bias. We first plan to implement a statistical validation framework based on resampling strategies and repeated evaluations across various subsets of the set of patients. This approach will allow us to estimate the distribution of clustering performance metrics, such as the silhouette score, and therefore assess their robustness and reproducibility and quantify the potential optimistic bias associated with workflow exploration. Secondly, we will more strictly separate data used for clustering from that used for downstream evaluation of prediction.

While our prediction results suggest that missingness of the data does not overtly favor a specific class of variables, a dedicated investigation is warranted to formally assess whether missing data mechanisms introduce systematic bias in feature selection and model outcomes. Such work would help clarify the extent to which observed differences between methods are driven by data availability rather than underlying clinical relevance.

Conclusions

We introduce a method to compare the ALSFRS-R sequences using an advanced weakly-supervised approach combining a probabilistic voting system with a linear SVM. The resulting distance matrices are fed to a clustering method.

One proposed workflow, WSD_UMAP_KMD_3, combining UMAP and k-medoids to form 3 clusters, emerged as the best one, with the highest silhouette score and with well separated clusters showing clinically meaningful survival curves that are confirmed by log-rank test. While it effectively captures several clinical parameters at diagnosis, it struggles with the biological ones. Prediction performance is mixed and slightly below state-of-the-art methods like MEY with the same number of clusters, but remains comparable overall, highlighting its potential for early prediction and personalized patient care. Overall results proposed by our approach show that, given the heterogeneity of the disease, a more robust consensus for cluster assignment is needed. This study also shows that using multi-criteria comparisons from temporal data can improve cluster separation.
