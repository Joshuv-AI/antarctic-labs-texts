Validation of the ICO-OCEX tool for competency-based assessment in ophthalmology residency programs
Authors: Ambreen Gull, Rahila Yasmin, Usman Mahboob, Karl C. Golnik, Muhammad Moin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompetence in medical training requires trainees to independently demonstrate practical skills. The ICO-OCEX (International Council of Ophthalmology-Ophthalmic Clinical Evaluation Exercise) tool, an improved version of OCEX (Ophthalmic clinical evaluation exercise), employs behaviorally descriptive milestones based on the Dreyfus model to address inconsistent grading interpretations and effectively assess ophthalmology residents’ competencies through workplace-based assessments.PurposeTo determine the validity and reliability of ICO-OCEX formative assessment tool for ophthalmology postgraduate residents.MethodsA multiphase mixed-method instrument developmental approach was used. Quantitative content validation was conducted using the modified Delphi technique, while cognitive pretesting assessed response process validity. Pilot testing was carried out to evaluate construct validity, as well as reliability and inter-rater reliability.ResultsThe modified Delphi technique achieved consensus on all 33 items, confirming stability and a scale content validity index of 0.97. Cognitive interviews led to revisions, resulting in a 34-item instrument. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated a good to excellent model fit. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.995 and Fleiss Kappa showed moderate to substantial inter-rater reliability.ConclusionThe ICO-OCEX tool, consisting of 34 items measuring four constructs on a three-point Dreyfus scale, demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability. It may be useful for formative assessment and supporting competency development in ophthalmology residency programs, particularly when faculty receive training for consistent application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09154-2.

Introduction

Competence in medical training goes beyond theoretical knowledge, requiring trainees to demonstrate practical skills independently, with competency-based curricula emphasizing outcomes for professional readiness [1]. Tools like the mini-CEX implemented by the American Board of Internal Medicine in 1995, are used for workplace-based assessments (WPBA) to evaluate clinical skills through real-world interactions, where trainees receive feedback from evaluators [2, 3]. Feedback is a critical component of the WPBA tools, with timely and action-oriented feedback being crucial for fostering clinical skill development and reflective practice among trainees [4]. The rating technique of the mini-CEX tool reflects its specified coverage, level of detail and granularity in providing performance information [5]. In WPBA tools like mini-CEX, checklists, narrative comments, and Likert-type scales are traditional rating methods [6]. The mini-CEX faces challenges related to the ambiguous language of traditional rating scales, which can lead to reliability and validity issues due to distributional rater errors and central tendency bias [7].

Existing tools to assess ophthalmology resident competencies ‘snapshots’’ include the OCEX (Ophthalmic Clinical Evaluation Exercise) based on mini-CEX [8, 9]. OCEX struggles with low inter-rater reliability due to inconsistent grading interpretations and the reluctance of assessors to use derogatory terms like “unsatisfactory” or “poor,” which impacts the assignment of low scores [10, 11]. The OCEX tool also faces issues with inconsistent trainee satisfaction and difficulty in identifying clinical progress over time [12].

A comprehensive review of workplace-based assessment (WPBA) literature highlights that behaviorally descriptive scales and rubrics improve assessment reliability and feedback clarity [13, 14]. Effective implementation of WPBA tools requires newer rating scales and active faculty participation. Methods like behaviorally descriptive milestones and entrustment anchors provide clearer and more concrete assessments [15]. The ICO-OCEX (International Council of Ophthalmology-Ophthalmic Clinical Evaluation Exercise), an enhanced version of the OCEX, utilizes behaviorally descriptive milestones based on the Dreyfus model to effectively assess the competencies of ophthalmology residents. It incorporates a 3-point rating rubric of ‘novice, beginner, competent’ and has shown face and qualitative content validity, although further testing for additional validity and reliability is needed to meet ACGME standards [12]. The three-point scale (novice, beginner, competent) was selected to align directly with the modified Dreyfus model underpinning the ICO-OCEX framework. A simplified scale was intentionally chosen to reduce rater ambiguity between adjacent performance levels, minimize central tendency bias, and enhance feasibility in busy clinical settings [12]. Unlike milestone frameworks used for high-stakes progression decisions (e.g., ACGME or Royal College systems), the ICO-OCEX is designed primarily as a formative workplace-based assessment tool.

The validation of the ICO-OCEX instrument offers significant potential to enhance postgraduate ophthalmology education by providing a robust tool for developing proficient practitioners. Its behavioral descriptors-based rating rubric aligns with competency-based, learner-centered education [16]. Establishing the tool’s validity and reliability will enable targeted feedback, address resident concerns, and improve trainee satisfaction with workplace-based assessments. The study also seeks to enhance faculty evaluation practices through standardized, evidence-based assessment methods.

This study’s conceptual framework is based on the Dreyfus Model of Skill Acquisition, guiding the development and validation of the ICO-OCEX formative assessment tool for ophthalmology residents [17] (Fig. 1).

Methods

The ICO-OCEX assessment instrument underwent psychometric analysis and validation using a multiphase mixed-method study design, incorporating both quantitative and qualitative components in sequential and concurrent strands [18]. Qualitative content validity had been previously established using AMEE Guide 87 [19]. From February to July 2024, the study proceeded in three phases (Fig. 3) with ethical approval granted by the Islamic International Medical College, Pakistan under Reference no. Riphah/IIMC/IRC/24/1006 (See additional file: Appendix A). This multi-center study was conducted across accredited teaching hospitals affiliated with Riphah International University and Rawalpindi Medical University, along with collaborating public-sector institutions from different provinces of Pakistan. These institutions provide structured ophthalmology residency programs recognized by the College of Physicians and Surgeons Pakistan (CPSP). Faculty members and residents from these institutions participated in different phases of the instrument validation study after providing written informed consent. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the research process to protect participants’ privacy.

Phase 1: quantitative content validity

The content validity of the ICO-OCEX instrument was assessed using a modified Delphi technique and calculated through the content validity index (CVI) for individual items and the overall tool.

Modified delphi round 1

Eligibility criteria (inclusion criteria)

A panel of 30 national and international ophthalmology experts was selected using purposive sampling for content validation. Eligible experts had at least 10 years of experience in ophthalmology teaching and postgraduate supervision and were actively involved in residency training and clinical assessment. They also had prior experience with workplace-based assessment tools (OCEX/mini-CEX or equivalent). 24 experts responded within two weeks after reminders were sent.

They rated the ICO-OCEX tool, consisting of four constructs and 33 items, on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “Not at all important” to “Very important”, providing feedback for extreme ratings and additional comments.

Data analysis involved measuring consensus using statistical methods such as mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range (IQR), and percentage agreement calculated using SPSS version 27. Items were selected based on predefined criteria: at least 75% of experts rated them as “very important” or “important,” they had a median score greater than 4, and an IQR of less than 1 [20, 21].

Modified delphi round 2

In the second round, the stability of responses from the 24 experts was assessed to ensure consistency between consecutive Delphi rounds. Experts were provided with individualized feedback, including the median, interquartile range (IQR), their round 1 responses, and percentage agreement on “very important” and “important” ratings. They were asked to confirm their previous scores or re-rank items if they disagreed. Stability was determined by comparing responses between rounds using the McNemar change test, with a significance level of 0.05 [22]. If the p-value exceeded this threshold, it indicated no significant difference between rounds, confirming that the expert panel’s opinions remained consistent [23].

Modified delphi round 3

In the third round, the ICO-OCEX instrument, consisting of 33 items across four constructs, was evaluated for clarity and relevance by 24 respondents using a Google Form. Experts rated each item on relevance using a four-point Likert scale (Very relevant (VR) = 4, Rather relevant (RR) = 3, Slightly relevant (SR) = 2, Not at all relevant (NR) = 1) and on clarity using a three-point Likert scale (Extremely clear (EC) = 3, Item needs revision (IR) = 2, Not clear at all (NC) = 1). The I-CVI (Item-Content Validity Index) and S-CVI/Ave (Scale-Content Validity Index) was calculated using Microsoft Excel. Items with an I-CVI of 0.90 or higher were retained, items between 0.78 and 0.90 were revised, and items below 0.78 were eliminated [20]. Content Clarity Average (CCA) was determined by summing the values assigned to each item and dividing by the total number of experts, and items with a CCA value above 2.4 (80%) were deemed exceedingly clear [24].

Phase 2: response process validity

The response process validity of the ICO-OCEX instrument was assessed through semi-structured cognitive pre-testing interview specifically developed for this research (See additional file: Appendix B).

Eligibility criteria for response process validity faculty

12 ophthalmology faculty members from four teaching hospitals of different states of Pakistan [25, 26] were selected through convenience sampling. They had at least three years of teaching experience and were familiar with workplace-based assessments.

They were introduced to the think-aloud and verbal probing techniques during an orientation session. Twelve interviews, each lasting 15–20 min, were recorded as participants verbalize their thoughts while ranking the items on the 33-item ICO-OCEX instrument, explaining their interpretations and any difficulties encountered. The interviews were analyzed by coding each item as “yes,” “no,” or “insufficient” based on three criteria: item interpretation, congruence of answer choices, and coherent elaboration [25]. Items were scored on a cognitive validity scale ranging from 0 to 3, with 3 indicating consistency in all criteria, 2 for minor issues, and 0–1 for major problems [26]. Two independent reviewers coded the items to ensure inter-rater reliability. Memos were coded to interpret the data according to predefined categories. During cognitive pre-testing, one item under the Interpersonal Skills (IPS-3) construct was found to contain two conceptually distinct behavioral components. Based on participant feedback and reviewer consensus, this item was divided into two separate items to improve clarity and construct representation. As a result, the instrument increased from 33 to 34 items prior to pilot testing. No items were deleted at this stage.

Phase 3: pilot testing

In Phase 3, the ICO-OCEX tool was pilot tested to establish construct validity and reliability. The instrument, consisting of 34 items across four constructs, was organized into six sections on Google Forms, including an introduction, participant demographics and sections for each construct.

Eligibility criteria for pilot testing faculty

National and International Ophthalmology faculty members were selected through purposive sampling to pilot test the tool. Eligible participants had at least five years of teaching experience in ophthalmology, were actively supervising postgraduate residents, and had prior familiarity with workplace-based assessment tools such as the OCEX or mini-CEX through self-reported prior use of workplace-based assessment tools. To minimize bias, faculty who participated in the Delphi rounds or cognitive interviews were not included in the pilot testing phase.

Eligibility criteria for resident videos

Residents included in the videotaped encounters were postgraduate ophthalmology trainees representing three levels of training (early, mid, and senior levels), corresponding to novice, beginner, and competent stages of the Dreyfus model. Residents provided written informed consent for video recording and educational use.

Patients requiring urgent intervention, those with communication barriers, or those unwilling to be video recorded were excluded. The same standardized patient case was used across all three resident encounters to reduce case-complexity bias.

The required sample size for construct validation through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was calculated using the subject-to-variable (STV) ratio guideline [27]. A minimum ratio of 10 participants per item is recommended for factor analysis.

Given 34 items in the final ICO-OCEX instrument, a minimum sample of 340 observations was required. Since each participant rated three separate encounters (novice, beginner, competent), the required number of raters was calculated as:

340 ÷ 3 ≈ 114 raters. To compensate for an anticipated 10% non-response rate, 140 faculty members were invited. Ultimately, 134 faculty members responded (96% response rate), generating 402 completed assessment forms, exceeding the minimum required sample size.

Some guidelines recommend the use of ranges: A sample size of 100 is considered insufficient; 100–500 acceptable; 500 satisfactory; and 1000 exceptional [28]. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s tests were used to assess sampling adequacy, with a KMO value above 0.5 and a Bartlett’s test p-value below 0.05 indicating suitability for factor analysis [29].

Faculty were encouraged to use behavioral anchors strictly rather than relying on global impressions. No formal calibration workshop was conducted; however, standardized instructions and rating guidelines were provided to enhance scoring consistency.

Faculty raters assessed standardized, video-recorded clinical encounters featuring residents at novice, beginner, and competent levels rather than their own trainees. They were blinded to the residents’ actual postgraduate year of training to prevent bias related to seniority expectations and to ensure ratings were based solely on observed performance. The videotaped encounters were recorded using residents who were not directly supervised by the participating faculty raters. The ICO-OCEX items were evaluated using a three-point rubric based on the modified Dreyfus model, and a fourth option for ‘Not applicable’.

Since ICO-OCEX domains were specified and developed by iteration using ACGME core competencies in OCEX development, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was omitted as there were particular assumptions about the number of constructs or factors and items or variables [30]. EFA follows when the factors either are unknown or still undetermined while CFA is recommended in cases with a robust model based on previous data about the constructs and which items correspond to which constructs [31]. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS 26.0 to validate the internal structure of the constructs, with factor loadings ≥ 0.5 considered acceptable [24]. Goodness-of-fit indices were used to assess model fit [32].

Internal consistency was evaluated through Cronbach’s Alpha, aiming for a value of 0.8 or higher [33], and inter-rater reliability was calculated using Fleiss’ kappa [34].

Fleiss’ kappa was selected to assess inter-rater reliability because multiple independent faculty raters (> 2) evaluated the same standardized video encounters using a categorical three-point rating scale. Unlike Cohen’s kappa, which is limited to two raters, Fleiss’ kappa allows estimation of agreement among multiple raters assigning categorical ratings to fixed subjects. Data was analyzed using SPSS version 27 to compute descriptive statistics and evaluate response distributions, with a response rate benchmark of 70%.

Results

Phase 1: quantitative content validity

Emails were sent to 30 experts, 24 of them (80% response rate) consented to participate consisting of 4 international and 20 national experts. There were no distinct comments from any of the participants regarding any item. All items had a median greater than 4, an IQR less than 1, and more than 75% of the experts rated them in the top two categories (See additional file: Appendix C). Consequently, all items met the predetermined criteria and were incorporated into the second round of the modified Delphi technique. The 24 respondents from round 1 were sent personalized word forms via email, and all of the experts completed the forms, resulting in a 100% response rate. All items were stable, with p-values exceeding 0.05, indicating no statistically significant difference between the responses of experts in the two successive rounds (See additional file: Appendix D). In the third round, 24 experts ranked the 33 items on relevance and clarity. As each item had an Item-Content Validity Index (I-CVI) exceeding 0.90 and a Content Clarity Average (CCA) of at least 2.4, none was eliminated or altered. The Scale-Content Validity Index (S-CVI/AVG) was 0.97, and the average clarity score was 2.98 (See additional file: Appendix E).

Phase 2: response process validity

Cognitive pre-testing with 12 participants led to rephrasing of 7 items requiring further clarification or modification, and conversion of one item (IPS 3) into two separate items, resulting in the final ICO-OCEX instrument with 34 items measuring four constructs. No item was categorized as having major problems, so none was merged or removed due to redundancy (See additional file: Appendix F).

Phase 3: pilot testing

134 out of 140 participants responded, resulting in a 96% response rate and a total of 402 forms. Data was gathered from all respondents, with no missing responses across all 34 items and four constructs. Responses were submitted via Google Forms, eliminating the need for manual data entry.

The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin score (0.773) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p = 0.000) indicated that the sample size was adequate. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed that all factors had good regression weights, with no item having a factor loading value below 0.40 (Fig. 4). Various CFA fit indices (RMSEA, SRMR, IFI, PNFI) indicated a good model fit, and other indices (χ2, GFI, AGFI, NFI, TLI, CFI) indicated an acceptable fit (Table 1). The final model, consisting of 34 items, demonstrated an excellent fit for ICO-OCEX set A (Chi-Square value of 2.291) and an acceptable fit for sets B and C (Chi-Square values of 3.217 and 3.023, respectively). A p-value of less than 0.05 indicated a statistically significant correlation among the ICO-OCEX set A, B, and C constructs.

The tool’s internal consistency was high, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.995 Table 2. The internal consistency of individual items was > 0.7, and the corrected item-to-total correlation (CITC) of all items was > 0.2, indicating appropriate assignment to their respective subscales. Although high alpha values may suggest potential item redundancy, subscale-level analysis showed Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.793 to 0.949 across sets A, B, and C, indicating acceptable to excellent reliability within each domain (Interview Skills, Examination Skills, Interpersonal Skills, and Case Presentation Skills). Interrater reliability, measured by Fleiss’s kappa, showed statistically significant results (p-value < 0.05) for all constructs, indicating moderate to substantial agreement among raters (Table 3).

Discussion

Competency-based medical education emphasizes formative assessments to develop skills at the “does” level of Miller’s pyramid. Tools like the mini-CEX are essential for tracking progress, evaluating seven core competencies [35]. The Ophthalmic Clinical Evaluation Exercise (OCEX) was adapted from the mini-CEX for ophthalmology, using a one-page checklist validated by experts to assess medical knowledge, patient care, interpersonal skills and professionalism [8]. The ICO-OCEX, a simplified version of the OCEX, was developed for international use, assessing interview, examination, communication, and presentation skills using a three-point rubric based on the Dreyfus scale. While contemporary milestone frameworks such as ACGME and Royal College models use five-point scales, the ICO-OCEX employs a three-point structure to maintain conceptual alignment with the Dreyfus progression model and improve rater usability in formative contexts. The ICO-OCEX is intended for use in clinical, non-procedural patient encounters across all stages of ophthalmology residency training. It assesses competencies related to history taking, examination, communication, and case presentation. Its face and content validity were improved through global feedback, ensuring broader applicability in ophthalmology training programs [12].

This study aimed to establish the reliability and validity of the ICO-OCEX formative assessment tool for ophthalmology postgraduate residents using a systematic seven-step development process. In the initial phase, 24 experts evaluated 33 items for importance, clarity, and relevance through the modified Delphi technique, resulting in the retention of 33 items with S-CVI/Ave of 0.97 and the average clarity score was 2.98. Cognitive pretesting led to revisions, creating a 34-item instrument. Pilot testing and confirmatory factor analysis validated the tool’s construct validity, with the 4-factor model showing acceptable fit and strong correlations among constructs. The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency, and moderate to substantial agreement among raters.

The psychometric properties ensure the instrument accurately and validly assesses the intended construct, generating valid, reliable, and precise data for evaluating residents’ workplace performance. Content validity ensures that an assessment accurately measures the intended domain or construct [36]. Content validity of the ICO-OCEX tool was enhanced by incorporating feedback from international evaluators through three rounds of the modified Delphi technique, achieving expert consensus and high Content Validity Index (CVI) scores. Unlike other workplace-based assessment tools like mini-CEX, DOPS, OCEX, ICO-OSCAR which rely primarily on qualitative expert validation [37, 38], the ICO-OCEX underwent rigorous quantitative assessment, resulting in robust I-CVI and S-CVI scores. This was achieved by iteratively refining the tool based on expert feedback to ensure accurate representation of the competencies measured.

Additionally, response process validity is crucial for ensuring that assessment items are interpreted as intended, which was ensured through semi-structured cognitive pre-testing, utilizing techniques such as verbal probing and think-aloud during interviews to refine items based on feedback. Notably, response process validity has not been established for other WPBA tools, including mini-CEX, OCEX, ICO-OSCAR, and DOPS [39].

Establishing the dimensionality and construct validity of an assessment instrument requires rigorous statistical analysis, often through factor analysis. Earlier studies utilized either Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) or Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) to establish correlations or distinctions among competencies [40]. The mini-CEX tool had shown strong construct validity with consistent single-factor solutions, significant score variances, and the ability to distinguish between different levels of clinical performance, supported by high factor loadings and correlations among competencies [41]. Likewise, the OCEX and ICO-OSCAR tools had confirmed the consistency and validity of their subscales using coefficient alpha statistics [42].

The current study established the construct validity of the ICO-OCEX tool using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), as the items were developed iteratively. Previously, Khan et al. employed this method to establish construct validity of curriculum viability questionnaire for teachers and students [24]. A four-factor model derived from 34 items showed a reasonable fit, with acceptable Chi-Square values and fit indices. Statistically significant Pearson correlation coefficients among the ICO-OCEX constructs indicated strong correlations, and the factor loadings aligned well with the intended constructs, confirming the tool’s effectiveness in evaluating clinical competencies. This study provided a comprehensive validation of the ICO-OCEX instrument’s theoretical model, unlike previous studies on similar tools that lacked sequential equation models or fit index calculations.

Cronbach’s alpha is often used to measure the internal consistency and reliability of assessment instruments, indicating how well test scores represent true scores according to Classical Test Theory (CTT) [33]. The ICO-OCEX demonstrated high internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.995. While such a high value indicates strong internal homogeneity, it may also suggest potential item redundancy and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Subscale reliability coefficients for Sets A, B, and C were 0.906, 0.826, and 0.890, respectively, indicating acceptable to excellent internal consistency. Most domain-specific subscales showed alpha values between 0.986 and 0.993. Corrected item-to-total correlation (CITC) values exceeded 0.2 across items, supporting adequate alignment of items with their respective constructs. In comparison, the OCEX tool had an overall alpha of 0.81, with subscales ranging from 0.27 to 0.73, while the ICO-OSCAR tool showed a reliability coefficient of 0.92 [38, 42]. The mini-CEX tool’s reliability varied from 0.58 to 0.97 across studies [39], and DOPS had an alpha of 0.94 [37]. The ICO-OCEX demonstrated higher internal consistency in this sample (α = 0.995) compared with previously reported values for OCEX tools (α ≈ 0.81) and the typical range for Mini-CEX tools (0.58–0.97). However, direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in study design, sample characteristics, number of raters, and scale structure.

Inter-rater reliability is crucial in educational assessments to ensure consistency among multiple raters evaluating the same phenomenon [34]. Fleiss’ kappa values for ICO-OCEX sets A, B, and C ranged from 0.40 to 0.714, indicating moderate to substantial agreement among raters across all constructs, with statistically significant results (p < 0.05). In comparison, the Mini-CEX showed Cohen’s kappa values ranging from 0.31 to 0.42, suggesting acceptable to moderate agreement, and intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) from 0.14 to 0.77 for numerical abilities [39]. The OCEX instrument demonstrated significant agreement for most items with 31 out of 33 items meeting 85% agreement across raters, though certain items showed variability, suggesting a need for rater training [42]. Percentage agreement alone doesn’t account for chance agreement, making more robust methods like Fleiss’ kappa, Cohen’s kappa, and ICC preferable. DOPS reported an inter-rater reliability of 0.81, with challenges due to inter-case variations [37]. The ICO-OCEX showed moderate to substantial inter-rater reliability, comparable to previously reported Mini-CEX agreement levels (κ = 0.31–0.42), highlighting the importance of uniform evaluation standards to enhance competency-based assessments in postgraduate medical education. Although moderate to substantial agreement was observed, variability among raters persists, which is consistent with findings in other workplace-based assessments. Structured faculty development and calibration workshops may further reduce rating variability and improve scoring consistency.

The study’s primary strength is its rigorous methodology for instrument development, following established literature-reported steps to minimize bias and enhance credibility and internal validity. It included pilot testing at various phases, member checking, and adequate sampling, with nationwide data collection for factor analysis to ensure geographical diversity.

The validation of the ICO-OCEX tool ensures alignment with ACGME standards, improving the credibility and reliability of assessments in residency programs. By integrating behaviorally descriptive milestones, the tool offers precise feedback, aiding residents in identifying improvement areas while reducing subjectivity in assessments. Faculty training on the tool enhances consistency and effectiveness, ultimately improving the quality of medical education. Aligning the ICO-OCEX with ACGME and CPSP competencies promotes consistent and internationally comparable training and evaluation standards.

The validated ICO-OCEX provides residency programs with a structured, behaviorally anchored formative assessment tool for clinical encounters. Its standardized rubric may enhance feedback quality, promote reflective learning, and support competency-based progression.

Integration into residency curricula could facilitate documentation of progressive skill acquisition while aligning assessment with observable performance behaviors. However, implementation should be accompanied by faculty orientation to ensure consistent application.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Although conducted across multiple institutions, most participants were from Pakistan, which may limit generalizability. Purposive sampling for expert selection and pilot testing may have introduced selection bias. While the sample size met recommended ratios for factor analysis, it remains modest compared to larger psychometric validation studies.

Pilot testing used standardized videotaped encounters rather than real-time workplace observations. Although this improved standardization, it may not fully reflect the complexity of authentic clinical practice. The very high Cronbach’s alpha (0.995) indicates strong homogeneity but may suggest potential item redundancy; future studies using advanced psychometric methods (e.g., item response theory or Rasch modeling) could further refine the tool.

Inter-rater reliability was moderate to substantial, though some variability persisted, underscoring the need for structured faculty calibration. The three-point rating scale may limit discrimination between adjacent performance levels compared with more granular milestone-based systems.

Finally, the ICO-OCEX assesses clinical encounters only and does not evaluate surgical competence. Predictive validity and educational impact were not examined; longitudinal studies are needed to determine its effect on resident progression and patient outcomes.

Future recommendations

Future research potential avenues include focus on improving inter-rater reliability through faculty training programs, studying the tool’s impact on learning outcomes and resident development, and integrating it into digital platforms for easier administration. Validating the tool in diverse contexts with larger samples, conducting in vivo assessments, and implementing longitudinal studies will further ensure its relevance and accuracy. Comparing the ICO-OCEX tool with other assessment instruments will help determine its relative effectiveness and reliability, significantly contributing to improving ophthalmology training and patient care outcomes globally.

Conclusion

In this multi-center validation study, the 34-item ICO-OCEX demonstrated evidence of content and construct validity, along with high internal consistency and moderate to substantial inter-rater agreement. The instrument was developed through a structured, iterative process and is grounded in competency-based medical education principles.

The ICO-OCEX may offer a structured framework for formative assessment of clinical encounters in ophthalmology residency training. However, additional validation in broader international contexts and real-time clinical environments is warranted to further examine its generalizability, discriminative capacity, and educational impact.
