Modeling the impact of environmental and socioeconomic factors on health expenditure in Türkiye
Authors: Metehan Ercan, Ayça Büyükyılmaz Ercan, Gülden Bölük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnderstanding health expenditure (HE) determinants is imperative for sustainable development. This study examines the impact of ecological footprint (EFP), economic growth (GDP), and refugee and asylum-seeker (RAS) population on HE in Türkiye from 1975 to 2023, employing the autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) approach with structural breaks. Long-run results indicate that GDP and EFP increase HE, with elasticities of 2.09 and 1.88, respectively, whereas RAS (−0.08) and the 2011 structural break (−0.10) reduce it. In the short run, GDP raises HE (2.28). VECM causality reveals that HE drives GDP and EFP, with unidirectional causality from GDP to RAS, while EFP and RAS exhibit bidirectional causality. These findings imply that while economic growth and environmental pressures inflate health costs, refugee influxes suppress them, likely due to international aid and youth demographics of refugees. Ultimately, policymakers should adopt a “health in all policies” framework that integrates environmental and migration strategies by prioritizing green healthcare infrastructure and sustainable urban planning.

Introduction

Over the last few decades, achieving sustainable economic growth has become an important goal for both developed and developing countries. Mitigating emissions and improving environmental performance are essential for achieving this objective. Uncontrolled industrialization, rapid urbanization, population increase, and economic growth are among the key drivers of environmental deterioration.1 While energy consumption fuels economic output, it simultaneously generates pollution and exerts pressure on the ecosystem. Moreover, the production and consumption wastes resulting from rapid industrialization, population growth, and urbanization have led to environmental degradation, exceeding the planet’s absorptive capacity. Collectively, these factors strain the environment, while the resulting climate change and declining environmental quality negatively affect human livelihoods and well being.2 Consequently, researchers and international institutions such as the United Nations (UN) and the World Health Organization (WHO) have focused particularly on the public health consequences of exceeding these ecological limits in terms of people’s well being.

As air pollution and environmental problems intensify, demand for health expenditure (HE) rises to ensure a sustainable environment.3 As highlighted by Li et al.4 and Wen et al.,5 environmental risks (i.e., air, water, and soil pollutions in urban area and land) lead to heart diseases and increase cancer risks.6 Therefore, environmental deterioration (beyond solely air pollution) necessitates additional HEs and creates a financial burden. In this context, the pressure that economic activity places on the environment not only affects public health but also imposes costs on the healthcare system.7,8,9

According to the WHO,10 air pollution causes approximately 7 million deaths annually. Moreover, UN estimates that 12.6 million deaths and more than 100 diseases are directly or indirectly linked to unhealthy environment.11 The 2024 WHO report, titled “Compendium of WHO and other UN guidance on health and environment (2024 Update),” incorporates new scientific evidence and policy guidance. The report’s key message states that “environmental risks account for 24% of the global disease burden, making preventive policies essential for improving health.” It found that 31% of heart disease cases, 43% of respiratory infections, and 69% of diarrheal diseases could be prevented through environmental improvements.12 Currently, the health and lives of 8 billion people worldwide are at risk as the effects of climate change intensify.13 This critical situation necessitates increased HE, requiring a significant allocation of economic resources to healthcare services. Furthermore, rising health spending could reduce budgetary allocations for other sectors, potentially leading to a decline in labor productivity and workforce participation. In the long run, these effects could hinder economic growth.8,9 In this context, health is considered an essential component of human capital, contributing to productivity, labor market participation, and sustainable development. Therefore, improving environmental performance is essential not only for ensuring environmental sustainability but also for maintaining economic stability and protecting public health.14

Global healthcare spending has been showing an upward trend since 2000 and poses a threat to countries’ long-run sustainable economic growth.15,16 As noted in various studies, the refugee population can also affect the environmental degradation by altering the patterns of social demand and labor supply, thereby creating significant burden on public HE.17,18,19,20 In this context, United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (hereafter UNHCR) has drawn attention to environmental damage caused by refugees, including deforestation, soil erosion, exploitation of resources (water, soil etc.), and pollution.21 For this reason, the huge increase in the number of refugees worldwide highlights the importance of sustainability aspects of refugees. According to the UNHCR, 117.3 million people were forcibly displaced due to persecution, conflict, violence, human rights violations, etc. Of the 117.3 million people, 36.4 million are refugees, 8.4 million are asylum-seekers, and 6.1 million are in need of international protection.22 Refugees and asylum-seekers are among the most vulnerable groups regarding health due to factors such as poor living conditions, inadequate sanitation, shelter and nutrition deficits, difficulties in accessing health and social services, and exposure to violence. These conditions increase susceptibility to infectious diseases and chronic conditions, leading to the re-emergence of infectious diseases (e.g., measles, tuberculosis, cutaneous leishmaniosis, and poliomyelitis) that had previously been controlled in Türkiye.23 Additionally, the most prominent health issues among refugees include nutritional disorders, growth and developmental delays in children, anemia, respiratory infections, sexually transmitted infections (STIs) including HIV/AIDS, birth complications, chronic diseases, and mental health disorders such as depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder.24 Consequently, the re-emergence of previously controlled infectious diseases and high fertility rates among the refugee population necessitate intensive vaccination and maternal health programs, which, in turn, increase public HEs.

When refugees arrive in a country, their primary goal is to survive and maintain their daily lives, and the environment is not their primary concern. Therefore, a massive influx of newcomers creates pressure not only on the environment but also on housing, education, and health infrastructure. Public HEs have also become a critical challenge in Türkiye, which hosts the largest refugee population in Europe, driven by rapid demographic expansion. The Ministry of Health provides a comprehensive range of services to migrants, particularly Syrians, including emergency care, outpatient clinics, referrals, and immunization. Additionally, services encompass infectious disease control, maternal and child health monitoring, reproductive health, dental and mental healthcare, oncology services, and environmental sanitation.25 According to latest data, an average of 11.7 million treatments and over 336,000 surgeries were performed in refugee health centers during 2016–2023. Furthermore, under temporary protection, residing outside accommodation centers is entitled to free healthcare at all public hospitals in Türkiye. It has been estimated that healthcare expenditure exclusively for Syrians under temporary protection has reached approximately $65.7 billion.26 Therefore, understanding the nexus between economic growth, health outcomes, refugee dynamics, and environmental performance is vital for sustainability. The UN’s 17 Sustainable Development Goals reflect a global plan to achieve a sustainable, equitable, and prosperous world by 2030. Specifically, goals 3 (“good health and well being”), 6 (“clean water and sanitation”), 13 (“climate action”), 14 (“life below water”), and 15 (“life on land”) directly impact health within the context of environmental performance and development. Therefore, protecting the natural environment contributes to public health and well being by facilitating the achievement of these goals.

However, precisely defining the interaction between health and environmental deterioration is challenging due to the diverse economic, social, environmental, and demographic conditions across countries. Nevertheless, existing research indicates that these variables are interconnected, either directly or indirectly. Numerous studies have highlighted the complexity and multidimensionality of the relationship between HE, economic growth, and CO2 emissions. Studies discussing the interaction between the environment and health emphasize that climate change and associated factors constitute not only an environmental problem but also a public health crisis leading to significant morbidity and mortality.27 In this context, researchers have investigated the adverse effects of air pollution on various age groups,28 the combined effects of air pollution and international trade,29,30 and the roles of energy consumption,31 forestry activities,32 renewable energy,33 management quality,34 and urban agglomeration35 on HE. While the majority of these studies have examined the relationship between HE and CO2 emissions using various control variables, fewer studies have focused on the interaction between HE and ecological footprint (EFP).36,37,38,39 The practice of considering emissions solely as environmental degradation indicator has been criticized in environmental economics studies. Although many studies have employed GHGs or CO2 emissions as proxies for environmental degradation, a more comprehensive sustainability indicator, the EFP, is increasingly being adopted to represent environmental deterioration.40 Proposed by Wackernagel41 and Wackernagel and Rees,42 the EFP comprises six components: carbon, cropland, grazing land, built-up land, fishing grounds, and forest.43 While CO2 emissions focus solely on air pollution, the EFP evaluates resource consumption (e.g., food, water, energy, and land) and environmental impact from a broader perspective.44 Thus, studies investigating the interaction between the environment, economy, and society have increasingly utilized the EFP as a measure of environmental pressure, as it offers a more comprehensive representation than CO2 emissions and places a stronger emphasis on sustainability.

In recent years, there has been an increase in disease incidence and mortality rates due to environmental pollution in Türkiye. Annually, approximately 45,000 preventable deaths occur due to environmental pollution.45 While health plays a vital role in economic growth and development at both individual and societal levels, the impact of environmental degradation on HE has not been sufficiently discussed. Previous studies covering Türkiye generally focused on the GHG or CO2 emissions, and the results obtained are contradictory in terms of the determinants of HE (see Table 1). Another factor putting pressure on healthcare services is the number of refugees who have free access to basic healthcare services and who arrived in Türkiye after the civil war in Syria in 2011, reaching very high numbers. According to latest data from the International Organization for Migration, there are more than 4.1 million foreigners in Türkiye, and approximately 2.9 million of them are Syrians seeking international protection.58 This influx has exerted pressure not only on the environment but also on HE as Turkish government has provided free healthcare services to refugees.59 The literature offers limited empirical evidence on how the refugee population interacts with environmental pressures and public HEs. This study bridges this gap by integrating the refugee population variable into the environmental-health nexus in Türkiye. Moreover, our study reveals that the refugee influx exerts a reduction effect on healthcare costs in the long run. This finding is important as it challenges the common assumption that migration imposes a fiscal burden, thereby providing a missing empirical link between international migration dynamics and health Selected studies on the nexus between health expenditure and CO2 emissions in Türkiye

The main contribution of the present study to the topic is the simultaneous incorporation of the EFP and refugee dynamics. While the nexus between CO2 emissions and HE in Türkiye has been explored, the specific combined roles of the EFP and refugee population remain unexamined. Therefore, this study extends the current literature on Türkiye by integrating the refugee population into the HE-EFP nexus. This approach provides a more comprehensive assessment of how not only air pollution but also overall environmental sustainability affects public HEs. Moreover, the current study makes additional contributions to existing literature, adding to the knowledge base in this field. First, this study employs the autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) bounds test approach supported by the Zivot-Andrews (Z-A) unit root test to capture the demographic shock caused by the 2011 Syrian Civil War. Rather than claiming a methodological novelty, this empirical strategy allows us to model structural changes, thereby providing more reliable and unbiased long-term coefficient estimates compared with conventional models that ignore such significant shocks. Second, while previous studies have generally investigated HE as a driver of environmental pollution within the framework of the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) hypothesis, the role of environmental performance as a determinant of health outcomes has not been sufficiently examined. Thus, the study not only identifies a gap but also presents a different perspective linking migration, the environment, and health economics.

Research background

The interaction between HE and environmental deterioration has long been a controversial issue and has become a global problem attracting considerable attention. The literature contains many studies that have analyzed the determinants of HE and the interaction between economic growth, environmental pollution, energy consumption, international trade, foreign direct investment, renewable energy consumption, life expectancy, urbanization, education, etc. This section provides a detailed literature review.

HE and economic growth

The interaction between HE and economic growth has been extensively explored in the literature, with many studies examining how these two factors influence each other and the implications for healthcare systems and economic development. In general, there is a consensus regarding the existence of a positive relationship in the long run. Numerous studies using panel data consistently identify this long-run positive relationship across various economic groups, including Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries60,61 and the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region.62

Within the long-run context, an important debate exists regarding the income elasticity of HE and whether health services are a luxury or a necessity. Extensive evidence from both developed and developing countries indicates that the income elasticity of HE is close to or below one, classifying health services not as a luxury but as a macroeconomic necessity.63,64 This classification is also supported by Mehrara et al.62 who observed that when economic growth declines, the share of HE in GDP decreases directly, thereby confirming that health services are a necessity.

Despite the consensus on a long-run relationship, the direction of causality remains heterogeneous across different country samples. While some studies have demonstrated a unidirectional long-run causal effect from economic growth to HE,64 others provide evidence of bidirectional dynamics. For instance, Nasiru and Usman65 identified a standard long-run bidirectional relationship in Nigeria. In contrast, Wang66 observed a more complex bidirectional dynamic in a panel of 31 countries. According to their results, rising HE stimulated economic growth, yet economic growth was paradoxically associated with a decline in HE. Therefore, while economic growth drives HE as a necessity, specific causal interactions are heavily influenced by the selected samples and methodological approaches.

HE and environment

The impact of environmental degradation on public health and associated expenditures is a central theme in the literature. Previous studies have mainly focused on single-dimensional pollution indicators, particularly CO2 emissions as they are one of the most significant drivers of environmental degradation. Numerous studies covering various methodologies, time periods, and regional panels have consistently demonstrated that rising CO2 emissions exacerbate chronic diseases and increase healthcare expenditure.67,68,69 Although a few studies present contrary findings,70,71 the prevailing view clearly establishes that carbon-based air pollution imposes heavy costs on health systems.72,73,74,75

More recent studies have used the EFP, rather than CO2 emissions, to measure environmental degradation due to the limitations of relying solely on carbon metrics. These recent studies also mostly provide evidence that ecological deficits worsen public health outcomes. For instance, while some studies have demonstrated the detrimental impact of the EFP on health indicators such as child and infant mortality rates in Eastern European and Asian economies,36,37 others have identified EFP as a driver of rising total HE, particularly in countries with the highest health spending in the world.38 Only a few studies present contrary findings.39 On the other hand, it is worth noting that studies on the HE-environmental deterioration nexus have mainly focused on CO2 emissions in Türkiye and neglected the EFP. As shown in Table 1, the impact of EFP on HE remains unexplored.

However, in the context of HE research, relying on CO2 emissions could underestimate the true burden of environmental degradation on HE as it merely represents air pollution, and its health impacts are limited to respiratory and cardiovascular conditions. Indeed, the WHO76 reported that air pollution leads to diseases such as stroke, heart disease, lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and respiratory infections including pneumonia. In contrast, the EFP is a multidimensional indicator that includes all environmental determinants of health, such as water pollution, the degradation of agricultural land, deforestation, and the biocapacity deficit. Thus, the broader environmental degradation captured by the EFP triggers a wider array of severe health issues, including waterborne diseases, malnutrition due to food insecurity, and the emergence of zoonotic infections.77 Therefore, the EFP serves as a more comprehensive indicator than CO2 emissions, as it successfully captures the multifaceted environmental risks that impose a heavier and more systemic fiscal burden on healthcare systems. Furthermore, there is a lack of research analyzing the role of the refugee and asylum-seeker populations on HE within the framework of environmental sustainability. By integrating these dimensions, the current study aims to fill this gap in the literature.

HE and refugee population

As stated in the introduction, the influx of refugees and asylum-seekers exerts pressure on the environmental and economic resources of host countries, particularly straining their healthcare services. A substantial body of literature highlights the direct fiscal burden that displaced populations impose on public expenditures. Studies quantifying these effects have consistently demonstrated that hosting refugees requires budget reallocations for education, infrastructure, and health services and often increases competition for scarce resources such as water and housing.15,16,78 Furthermore, providing healthcare to these vulnerable groups represents a hidden cost for host countries. Empirical evidence from developing countries like Morocco indicates that around 5% of the annual primary healthcare budget is indirectly absorbed by the care of migrants and refugees.79 Interestingly, as also indicated by HE trends in Germany, policies aimed at restricting access to health services for asylum-seekers may lead to higher long-term costs than ensuring regular access.80

Beyond the fiscal burden, the literature emphasizes the role of sustainable humanitarian aid and HE in mitigating adverse outcomes. The empirical link between UNHCR humanitarian expenditure and reduced refugee mortality rates in 17 countries81 highlights the necessity of providing quality health services that focus primarily on reproductive health, primary healthcare, and overall health status.82

Although the effects of the refugee population on HE are well documented, the nexus of demographic shocks, environmental degradation, and HE remains underexplored. In particular, the existing literature predominantly treats refugee inflows and environmental degradation as independent factors influencing HE in Türkiye. As a rare exception, Aydin and Bozatli57 demonstrated that both the refugee population and CO2 emissions jointly increase HE in Türkiye. However, as previously noted, relying solely on CO2 emissions provides an incomplete assessment of the impact of environmental degradation on health. To date, no study has investigated the fiscal burden of refugees on HE in Türkiye through a comprehensive framework of the EFP. By integrating the refugee and asylum-seeker populations with the EFP indicator, this study fills a gap in the literature and offers a distinct perspective on the nexus of migration, environmental sustainability, and health economics within the context of a developing country.

Model specification

To examine the dynamics of HE in Türkiye, this study integrates GDP, EFP, and RAS as explanatory variables into the HE equation. The selection of these variables is grounded in the macro-level extension of the “health production function” framework introduced by Grossman.87 In this theoretical framework, health outcomes and subsequent healthcare expenditures are determined by a combination of economic, environmental, and sociodemographic inputs. First, GDP is posited as the primary economic input, consistent with income elasticity theory. This positive relationship suggests that the rising income levels stimulate demand for healthcare services, as is well documented in the literature.63 Furthermore, EFP is included as the environmental input, serving as a proxy for environmental pressure. Given that environmental degradation decreases health capital and is frequently linked to rising HE,36,38 its inclusion is critical. Finally, RAS is introduced as the sociodemographic input to capture the impact of demographic shocks. Although migration is typically associated with an initial surge in service demand, evidence suggests that long-term outcomes vary significantly depending on the age structure of the migrant population.88 Based on this theoretical framework and existing empirical precedents, the functional form of the model was constructed as follows:(Equation 1)HEt=f(GDPt,EFPt,RASt)where HEt, GDPt, EFPt, and RASt are health expenditure, real GDP per capita, ecological footprint per capita, and refugee/asylum-seeker population, respectively. To minimize scale disparities and improve numerical stability, all variables were transformed using natural logarithms before analysis. The model was transformed into the functional form shown in Equation 2:(Equation 2)lnHEt=α+β1lnGDPt+β2lnEFPt+β3lnRASt+εtwhere ln, t , εt, and β represent the logarithmic form, time dimension, error term, and coefficients, respectively. Considering the existing literature, the sign of the GDP coefficient is expected to be positive (β1>0), as the demand for health services increases as individuals’ income levels rise. Similarly, the coefficient of EFP is also expected to have a positive sign (β2>0) because environmental degradation can lead to health problems. Furthermore, it is considered that the coefficient of RAS could be negative or positive (β3<0or>0) depending on the demographic structure of the RAS and the sources from which the associated costs are financed. According to the findings, KPSS and ERS test results show that none of the variables are stationary at the level, but they become stationary at the first differences. Due to the addition of multiple dummy variables to the ARDL framework, which significantly reduces degrees of freedom and may potentially decrease the statistical power and stability of the estimated coefficients, we relied on the Z-A test to identify the most dominant structural break. The findings are consistent with those of the KPSS and ERS unit root tests, except those for EFP, and there is evidence that the integration level of the variables is I(1). Furthermore, although the sample period (1975–2023) covers several important global and regional shocks, the Z-A unit root test endogenously identified 2011 as the most important single structural breakpoint for the variables in the system. Considering that the Syrian civil war broke out in 2011, this result is quite reasonable, and Figure 2 below also supports this result. While other crises undoubtedly caused short-term fluctuations, the start of the Syrian Civil War in 2011 led to a permanent change in Türkiye’s demographic and economic structures due to the mass influx of refugees, fundamentally changing the long-term trends in HE and population dynamics. Given that the variables exhibit a mixed order of integration and strictly no variable is integrated of I(2), the ARDL bounds testing approach is employed. Determining the optimal lag lengths (p,q,r,ands) is necessary before proceeding with the estimation phase. Akaike information criteria (AIC) and Hannan-Quinn information criteria (HQ) are less sensitive to additional parameters included in the model. Therefore, they tend to choose models with the maximum possible lag length. In contrast, Schwarz information criteria (SIC), due to their higher penalty for additional parameters, tend to select models with the minimum possible lag length. The robust results of SIC in small samples, in combination with the principle of model simplicity, led us to prefer the ARDL (4,1,1,0) model that minimizes the SIC. The long-run results indicate that all explanatory variables have a statistically significant effect on HE. Specifically, the estimated GDP coefficient is 2.0931, indicating that a 1% increase in economic growth leads to an approximately 2.09% increase in HE. In other words, HE is akin to a luxury good, implying that as national income rises, society allocates a larger share of its resources to invest in “health capital” within the context of the health production function. Furthermore, it can be said that a country’s economic growth depends on the health of its population. This observation is also corroborated by the Ministry of Health’s Health Statistics Yearbook 2023,90 which reveals that the total HE rose from $36.617 million in 2022 to $52.965 million in 2023, while its share of GDP increased from 4% to 4.7%. Our findings are consistent with the results of studies by Kutlu and Örün,91 Murthy and Okunade,92 Kapçak,54 and Yang et al.,38 yet contrast with the results reported by Wang,66 Eggoh et al.,93 Wang et al.,94 and Piran et al.95

Regarding EFP, the long-run coefficient is 1.8776 and statistically significant, indicating that an increase in EFP leads to an increase in HE over time. This finding can be explained by the theory of negative externalities and health capital depreciation.87 Environmental degradation, captured by EFP, generates negative externalities, such as air and water pollutions, that accelerate the depreciation rate of the population’s “health capital.” To offset this accelerated depreciation and restore basic health levels, society is compelled to incur preventive HEs. Consequently, the rising health costs observed in our model reflect the social costs of ecological overshoot. Our results align with those of Yang et al.38 and Gündüz96 but contradict the findings of Bouchoucha.71 Regarding the causes of these negative externalities, greenhouse gas emissions (particularly from the energy, housing, transport, and agriculture sectors) are major drivers of this health capital depreciation. Mitigating emissions from these sectors is essential not only for climate stability but also for improving public health and reducing associated healthcare costs. Therefore, energy policies that encourage renewable energy sources and high energy efficiency, together with environmental policies that aim to mitigate degradation and accelerate the transition to a circular economy, are vital instruments in curtailing mounting healthcare costs in Türkiye.

The long-run coefficient for RAS is statistically significant at −0.0779, suggesting that a 1% increase in the RAS population results in a decrease in HE of approximately 0.08%. This finding is plausible for at least three reasons. First, according to UNHCR data,86 only 3% of RAS arriving in Türkiye between 2001 and 2023 were aged 60 or older. Furthermore, individuals aged 0–18 years constitute approximately 45% of this population, while the 18–59 age group accounts for 51%. According to Grossman’s framework, age is a primary determinant of the depreciation rate of “health capital.” Consequently, the refugee demographic consists largely of working-age individuals whose health needs center on the primary and preventive services, rather than high-cost chronic care. Second, a substantial portion of refugee health services is funded by international donors and humanitarian organizations, thereby preventing a direct burden on the national health budget. The European Union’s Facility for Refugees in Türkiye (FRIT) allocated €6 billion between 2016 and 2019 to support refugee-hosting efforts, with significant tranches specifically earmarked for health infrastructure and services.97 Notably, within this framework, the “SIHHAT” project (“improving the health status of the Syrian population under temporary protection) was launched with an initial EU grant amounting to €300 million and subsequently extended.98 These external funds covered primary healthcare provisions, vaccinations, and the operational costs of migrant health centers, effectively mitigating the fiscal burden on the Turkish government. Third, notably, a significant share of medical care for Syrians was provided in informal clinics, especially during the transition period.99 Collectively, these factors suggest that an increase in RAS population may, over time, marginally alleviate the pressure on the national healthcare budget. The negative and significant coefficient of the dummy variable further supports this conclusion, as it likely captures the impact of external funding. Our findings contradict the results of Aydin and Bozatli,57 Camarota and Zeigler,16 Alshoubaki and Harris,15 and Tan et al.,81 while similar conclusions were drawn by Bozorgmehr and Razum.80

In the short run, the error correction term (ECT) is negative and statistically significant (−0.3783), confirming the existence of a stable long-run equilibrium within the model. The adjustment speed of approximately 38% per year indicates that the HE system in Türkiye possesses a resilient self-correcting mechanism. In other words, the short-run shocks (such as sudden economic fluctuations or migration waves) are not permanent but are neutralized over a period of about 2.65 years (1/0.3783). This implies that while immediate fiscal pressures may cause temporary deviations, the underlying structure of HE remains anchored to its long-term determinants. The short-run findings also show that GDP has a significant impact on HE in the short run, as it does in the long run. A 1% increase in GDP leads to an approximately 2.28% rise in HE. Particularly in the post-2000 period in Türkiye, health reforms, investments in private healthcare sector, and modifications to the insurance system, along with economic growth, have contributed to the short-run increase in HE. In this context, it is reasonable to argue that the healthcare system in Türkiye is sensitive to economic cycles. The short-term expansionary impact of economic growth on the health budget persists in the long run, implying that societal demand for health services has structurally increased in tandem with rising income, thereby permanently elevating system costs. However, contrary to the long-run finding, the impact of EFP on HE is not statistically significant in the short run. This finding indicates that the impact of environmental degradation on health is delayed, reflecting the inherent latency period of environmental externalities. Therefore, the depletion of health capital caused by environmental issues should be considered as a cumulative process, rather than a sudden shock. This underscores the need for policymakers to prioritize long-run sustainability goals over short-term fiscal planning. Finally, due to the optimal lag structure of the selected ARDL (4,1,1,0) model determined by the SIC, the RAS variable has an optimal lag length of zero. This indicates that RAS enters only the long-run equation and does not generate a differenced term (ΔlnRAS) in the short-run error-correction equation. Consequently, this formal exclusion confirms the absence of a dynamic short-term effect of RAS population on HE. The absence of RAS variable in the short-run results implies that the fiscal impact of migration is structural, rather than immediate; initial costs were likely mitigated by external funding (e.g., international aid) or existing systemic buffers. Over the long term, however, the younger refugee population contributes to a “demographic rejuvenation” effectively diluting per capita health costs.

To assess the robustness of our long-run findings across alternative regression methods, we employed the DOLS estimator. Unlike other conventional estimators such as FMOLS or canonical co-integrating regression (CCR), the DOLS approach is more flexible in accommodating systems that encompass variables with mixed or higher orders of integration.100 Given that the Z-A unit root test indicated that EFP is integrated at I(0), the DOLS method was selected as the most appropriate alternative for our robustness check. The DOLS estimation results are reported in Table 8. It can be observed that the signs and magnitudes of all estimated coefficients are consistent with the corresponding long-run ARDL outcomes. This consistency underscores the reliability of our ARDL findings. However, it is important to note that the coefficient estimates for lnEFP and lnRAS are not statistically significant within the DOLS framework. This lack of significance can be attributed to the finite sample properties of the estimator. The DOLS technique inherently consumes a substantial number of degrees of freedom by incorporating leads and lags of first-differenced terms into the equation to correct for endogeneity and serial correlation. As explicitly demonstrated by Stock and Watson,100 in small sample sizes (such as T = 42 in their Monte Carlo simulations for the post-war period), the DOLS estimator can suffer from severely distorted confidence intervals and weakened t statistics. The existence of long-run cointegration demonstrates the appropriateness of the VECM-based Granger causality test. Table 9 presents the findings on short- and long-run relationships. In the long run, ECT coefficients for GDP, EFP, and RAS are statistically significant, but only RAS exhibits a negative sign. As suggested by Johansen,101 the stability condition of the error-correction model is that the ECT must lie strictly between −2 and 0. Consequently, the ECT value of −2.4052 for RAS is disregarded. Therefore, the findings demonstrate that GDP and EFP adjust to restore equilibrium following a shock. However, although the ECT for the HE equation is negative and between −0 and −2, it is not statistically significant. This implies that HE acts as a weakly exogenous variable in the system. In the short run, the Wald test statistics provide robust evidence validating the health-led growth hypothesis (HLGH) in Türkiye, which posits that healthcare expenditure positively affects economic growth. As seen in Table 9, there is a strong unidirectional causality relationship running from HE to GDP (χ2=16.4694), confirming this theory. Additionally, a unidirectional relationship is observed from HE to EFP. This indicates that the investments in healthcare services contribute to environmental pressure, likely due to the sector’s high energy intensity and medical waste generation. Furthermore, there is also a unidirectional causality from GDP to RAS. This finding supports the “pull factor theory,” which emphasizes that the migration flows are influenced by favorable conditions in the destination country. Thus, economic vitality and employment opportunities in Türkiye are likely significant drivers attracting the refugee population. Finally, there is a bidirectional causality relationship between RAS and EFP. This finding suggests a feedback loop where demographic changes and environmental pressures reinforce each other.

In conclusion, the relationships between HE, RAS, EFP, and economic growth in Türkiye exhibit distinct dynamics in the short and long runs. The findings highlight that HE acts as an exogenous driver for the system in the long run, while actively fueling economic growth and environmental pressure in the short run. Moreover, the feedback loop between RAS and EFP indicates that demographic policies cannot be isolated from the environmental sustainability goals. Therefore, beyond the direct effects on HE, it is imperative to consider their indirect effects on the environment. While economic growth increases healthcare expenditure, it also causes pressure on the environment through the scale effect.102 The scale effect implies that, in the absence of changes in economic structure or production technology, economic expansion leads to increased environmental degradation.103 Furthermore, considering the negative impact of RAS on HE, the question arises as to whether the demographic structure of the refugee population is predominantly young. The RAS data used reveal that approximately 45% of the RAS population in Türkiye are aged 0–18 years, around 52% are aged 18–59 years, and merely 3% are aged 60 years and above.86 These findings not only explain the negative impact of the RAS on HE but also suggest significant consequences for production dynamics. The influx of working-age refugees into the labor market as a source of low-cost labor is likely to reduce production costs and foster industrial expansion. Such an increase in economic activity, triggered by a labor supply shock, can lead to higher energy consumption and environmental degradation. The finding of bidirectional causality between RAS and EFP also supports this view. In other words, refugee migration characterized by a young demographic profile expands production capacity, thereby contributing to EFP. An increase in EFP negatively affects public health, resulting in higher HEs. Consequently, the reduction in HE driven by the increase in RAS should not obscure the indirect long-term health costs arising from environmental degradation.

Conclusion and implications

This study investigates the impact of RAS, GDP, and EFP on HE in Türkiye. To this end, an ARDL model with a structural break was applied to annual data from 1975 to 2023 to examine the dynamic relationships in the short and long runs. Additionally, a VECM-based Granger causality test was employed to identify the direction of causal relationships. The findings reveal that the nature of these relationships varies significantly over time.

First, the results provide evidence of a long-run, co-integrated relationship among HE, GDP, EFP, and RAS in Türkiye. The long-run analysis indicates that a 1% increase in economic growth and EFP leads to increases in HE of approximately 2.1% and 1.9%, respectively, whereas the RAS is associated with a decrease of roughly 0.08%. In addition, the Z-A test identified 2011 as the structural break. Considering that the Syrian civil war that broke out in 2011 led to an influx of refugees into Türkiye, this finding is reasonable. Contrary to expectations, the long-run coefficient for the 2011 dummy variable is negative (−0.10), indicating a downward shift in the public HE levels post-crisis. This finding contrasts with those of Aydin and Bozatli (2023), and it can be attributed to distinct structural factors. The financing of refugee healthcare largely through international funds and non-governmental organizations (thereby bypassing the central budget) may be the main reason. Furthermore, because the services provided mostly are those related to low-cost primary and emergency healthcare, this may also limit expenditures. Moreover, the expenditure control measures taken in response to the crisis may lead to increased efficiency in the public health budget. Conversely, EFP exerts significant upward pressure on HE, confirming that environmental degradation ultimately materializes as a fiscal burden due to the rising prevalence of pollution-related chronic diseases. Meanwhile, the increasing demand for healthcare services and the spread of technology-intensive treatments with the rise in income levels explain the positive effect of economic growth on healthcare spending.

Second, the positive impact of GDP on HE persists in the short run, whereas EFP exhibits no statistically significant effect. This lack of significance can be attributed to the heavy burden of environmental degradation on health (COPD, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, etc.), which is the result of exposure accumulated over many years. Thus, the diagnosis, treatment, and expenditure effects are delayed. Additionally, because public health budgets and repayment schedules are determined on an annual or multi-year basis, environmental shocks are often incorporated into future budget cycles, rather than affecting current expenditure. Finally, the ECT is significant and negative (−0.3783), indicating that short-run shocks are not permanent and that the system returns to equilibrium in approximately 2.65 years.

Finally, the findings obtained from the VECM Granger causality analysis provide critical knowledge about the direction of causal relationships. Notably, the ECT for the HE equation is statistically nonsignificant. This indicates that HE functions as a policy instrument driven by government decisions, rather than adjusting endogenously to other variables. Regarding the short-run effects, the evidence of unidirectional causality from HE to GDP confirms the relevance of the HLGH in Türkiye. Furthermore, a unidirectional causality from HE to EFP is found, implying that the health sector itself contributes to environmental pressure. Crucially, the bidirectional causality observed between RAS and EFP supports the “scale effect” mechanism whereby the labor supply shock caused by refugees expands industrial production and, consequently, the EFP. These causal links provide an empirical base for further policy recommendations.

Policy implications

Derived directly from our estimated ARDL coefficients and Granger causality results, the policy implications are structured around the three core dynamics identified, which are the environmental-health nexus, the economic role of health investments, and the socio-environmental management of migration. In this regard, we recommend adopting the “health in all policies” (HiAP) framework as an overarching strategy for Türkiye.104 The HiAP approach emphasizes that public health outcomes are shaped by decisions outside the traditional health sector, a perspective supported by our empirical results regarding the macroeconomic, environmental, and demographic determinants.

Guided by this framework, several targeted policy measures can be derived. First, driven by the long-term positive coefficient of EFP (1.8776), our results demonstrate that environmental degradation is a significant cause of the financial burden on the healthcare system. Therefore, environmental policies should be considered as preventive health policies by integrating environmental and health management. Policymakers should implement health-oriented carbon pricing and green subsidies to mitigate the health costs of ecological degradation under the HiAP approach. Considering that the carbon footprint currently accounts for approximately 60% of the EFP, stricter emission regulations in industrial production and promoting decarbonization to alleviate this burden are necessary. These measures will result in financial savings in the Ministry of Health budget. Furthermore, the short-term unidirectional causality finding from HE to EFP indicates that the health sector contributes to environmental pressure due to reasons such as high energy demand and medical waste. In this regard, it is recommended that renewable energy sources be integrated into hospitals and green healthcare infrastructure be developed by adopting circular economy principles in medical waste management to minimize the ecological damage caused by the expansion of healthcare services.

Second, anchored by the positive long-run elasticity of GDP (2.0931), the significant expansionary impact suggests that as national income rises, societal demand for healthcare will increase. Therefore, health budget planning should be indexed to economic growth projections. To manage this income-driven demand sustainably, economic growth policies should be paired with investments in preventive health services. For instance, to reduce dependency on high-cost tertiary care, policymakers could implement a mandatory referral system through Family Health Centers (primary care) prior to hospital appointments. Additionally, the finding of unidirectional causality from HE to GDP indicates that the HLGH is valid in Türkiye. This finding shows that HE should be considered not only as a budgetary cost but also as a factor that stimulates economic activity. Accordingly, the government should avoid austerity policies in health budgets, especially during periods of economic recession. Instead, it should view preventive health services as a strategic tool and allocate greater resources to early screening programs for chronic diseases to contribute to long-term economic performance.

Finally, regarding demographic shifts, the relationship between RAS and HE requires a multidimensional policy approach. On one hand, the negative RAS coefficient (−0.0779) highlights the direct role of external financing and the younger demographic structure. To sustain this protective effect, health diplomacy should secure the continuation of international funding mechanisms (e.g., FRIT and SIHHAT). Concurrently, migration health policies should be localized toward primary and maternal-child care, managing the needs of this demographic without burdening high-cost tertiary care. On the other hand, our bidirectional Granger causality finding between RAS and EFP reveals an indirect effect. This finding implies that the influx of refugees causes a scale effect by increasing the production capacity and urban density that results in an increase in EFP. Because a higher EFP increases health costs, the financial benefit of RAS on HE is eliminated by the health risks caused by environmental degradation. Therefore, strictly in line with the HiAP framework, migration management should be integrated with environmental and urban planning. Policymakers should cooperate with local governments to distribute refugee density more equitably and invest heavily in urban infrastructure such as water treatment, sustainable housing, and public transportation, especially in cities hosting high refugee populations. This integrated approach may help mitigate the ecological consequences of population shocks and prevent subsequent secondary pressures on the national healthcare system.

Limitations of the study

The limitations of this study include the following aspects: first, while the EFP was chosen for its multidimensional coverage of environmental degradation, it primarily focuses on the demand side of nature. Future studies could extend this framework by including supply side indicators such as the “load capacity factor” to assess whether the environmental carrying capacity is sufficient to support the population shock caused by refugees.

Second, this study utilizes total HEs as an indicator of health outcomes. However, expenditures could not always reflect physical health status perfectly. Future research should investigate the impact of refugees and environmental stress on specific physical health indicators, such as infant mortality rates and life expectancy, to validate the economic findings of this study.

Finally, because this study focuses on Türkiye, the generalizability of the results to other refugee-hosting countries may be limited due to structural differences in health systems. Panel data studies covering developing host countries would be valuable for testing the universality of the effects observed here.

Method details

Unit root tests

Although the ARDL method accommodates variables with different levels of stationarity, this method requires that all series are not I(2). Therefore, it is necessary to determine the stationarity properties of the series prior to estimation. For this reason, first, the ERS test was employed. This test was proposed by Elliott et al.,105 who modified the Dickey-Fuller test statistic and then used generalized least squares rationale. They prove that this modified test offers greater statistical power in small samples than the standard Dickey-Fuller test. Additionally, the KPSS test proposed by Kwiatkowski et al.106 served as a robustness check. However, since conventional unit root tests can produce biased results by neglecting structural breaks, the Zivot-Andrews107 (hereafter Z-A) test was incorporated to accommodate a potential single endogenous break in the intercept or trend. In terms of testing frameworks, the ERS and Z-A tests assume a unit root exists under the null hypothesis, in contrast to the KPSS test, which assumes stationarity.

ARDL approach

Traditional cointegration tests that examine the long- and short-run dynamic relationships among variables typically require all variables to be integrated at the same order. However, the ARDL model developed by Pesaran et al.108 relaxes this restriction. Unlike conventional cointegration methods, the ARDL approach can be applied when variables are integrated of different orders (I(0) or I(1)). Moreover, it provides reliable results even with small sample sizes and allows for the simultaneous analysis of both short-run and long-run dynamics.108,109 Based on these advantages, the ARDL method was employed in this study. The ARDL model for the variables used in the analysis is presented in Equation 3:(Equation 3)ΔlnHEt=α0+∑i=1pβ1ΔlnHEt−i+∑i=0qβ2ΔlnGDPt−i+∑i=0qβ3ΔlnEFPt−i+∑i=0qβ4ΔlnRASt−i+λ1lnHEt−1+λ2lnGDPt−1+λ3lnEFPt−1+λ4lnRASt−1+εtIn the ARDL approach, the bounds test is used to determine whether a long-run relationship exists among the variables. The null hypothesis of no cointegration (H0:λ1=λ2=λ3=λ4=0) is tested against the alternative hypothesis indicating the presence of cointegration (H1:λ1≠λ2≠λ3≠λ4≠0), based on the F-statistic value. The F-statistic is compared with the lower and upper critical bounds provided by Pesaran et al.108 or Narayan.89 While the critical values developed by Pesaran et al.108 are used for large samples, critical values of Narayan89 are preferred for small samples. If the F-statistics exceed the upper bound (I(1)), the null hypothesis is rejected, indicating the existence of a cointegrated relationship among the variables. If the F-statistic falls below the lower bound (I(0)), cointegration cannot be confirmed. When the F-statistic lies between the I(0) and I(1) bounds, the result is inconclusive regarding the presence of cointegration. By incorporating the error correction term (ECT) into Equation 3, the following error correction model (ECM), which captures the short-run dynamics of the ARDL model, is obtained:(Equation 4)ΔlnHEt=α0+∑i=1pβ1ΔlnHEt−i+∑i=0qβ2ΔlnGDPt−i+∑i=0rβ3ΔlnEFPt−i+∑i=0sβ4ΔlnRASt−i+λ1lnHEt−1+λ2lnGDPt−1+λ3lnEFPt−1+λ4lnRASt−1+θECTt−1+εt

In Equation 4, α0 denotes the constant term, Δ denotes the difference operator, t represents time dimension, β1,2,3,4 and λ1,2,3,4 represents short- and long-run coefficients, respectively. p,q,r,ands are the optimal lag lengths determined using the Akaike information criteria (AIC), the Schwarz information criteria (SIC) or the Hannan-Quinn information criteria (HQ). θ is the coefficient of the ECT, and εt is the error term. The coefficient θ measures the speed at which the variables return to their long-run equilibrium following a short-run disturbance.

To assess the adequacy of the estimated ARDL model, diagnostic tests for normality, autocorrelation, and heteroscedasticity are performed. In addition, Brown et al.110 developed graphical stability tests such as the cumulative sum (CUSUM) and the cumulative sum of squares (CUSUMSQ) to assess parameter stability. Parameter stability is confirmed if the test statistics remain strictly within the 5% critical bounds, whereas any deviation beyond these thresholds signifies parameter instability. VECM granger causality test

The ARDL approach only shows whether there is a long-run or short-run relationship between variables. It does not provide evidence of a causal relationship between variables. Granger111 argued that if the variables are integrated at the I(1), the VECM is an appropriate approach for testing the direction of the causal relationship between variables. Thus, if a long-run relationship between variables is detected, the direction of causality can be determined using VECM. Additionally, this method facilitates the distinction between long-run and short-run Granger causality. For testing the short-run causalities, it is necessary to examine whether the variables are affected by their own lagged values. Therefore, short-run causalities are investigated using the Wald test. The Wald test involves testing the null hypothesis, which implies the absence of causality in the short run. Rejection of the null hypothesis confirms the existence of short-run Granger causality based on the χ2 statistics. Long-run causality is determined by the error correction term (ECT). Shahbaz et al.112 noted that if the error correction term has a negative sign, this indicates a significant long-run causality relationship. The VECM equation used in our study is as follows:(Equation 6)[Δ(lnHEit)Δ(lnGDPit)Δ(lnEFPit)Δ(lnRASit)]=[δ1δ2δ3δ4]+∑p−1q[θ11θ21θ31θ41θ12θ22θ32θ42θ13θ23θ33θ43θ14θ24θ34θ44]x[Δ(lnHEit−p)Δ(lnGDPit−p)Δ(lnEFPit−p)Δ(lnRASit−p)]+[α1α2α3α4]ECTit−1+[μ1itμ2itμ3itμ4it]where, Δ denotes the first differences, δ, θ, and α are coefficients. Also, (μ1it,μ2it,μ3it,μ4it) refer to the residual terms, which are assumed to be independent and normally distributed. Additionally, ECTit−1 stands for the lagged error correction terms. ECTit−1 not only shows the long-run relationship between variables but also indicates the speed of convergence from the short-run to the long-run equilibrium in all models.
