Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting for Myocardial Bridging 21 Years After the Senning Procedure
Authors: Hisashi Yoshida, Azumi Hamasaki, Michinobu Nagao, Takeshi Shinkawa, Akiko Yamagata, Kensuke Nakamura, Hiroshi Niinami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial bridging in adult patients with congenital heart disease is rare.Case SummaryA 21-year-old man with d-transposition of the great arteries who underwent the Senning procedure presented with exertional fatigue and chest pain. Medical examination revealed ascending aortic stenosis and myocardial bridging of the left anterior descending artery. The patient underwent ascending aortic replacement and coronary artery bypass grafting by saphenous vein–free right internal mammary artery graft. Postoperative examination demonstrated excellent graft patency and improvement of ischemia.DiscussionThis case highlights a rare presentation of symptomatic myocardial bridging decades after the Senning procedure. Careful graft selection and design are essential to achieve durable revascularization in young patients.Take-Home MessagesMyocardial bridging should be considered as a potential cause of myocardial ischemia in adult patients with congenital heart disease. When myotomy is technically challenging or high-risk, carefully planned coronary artery bypass grafting with an appropriate graft strategy can provide effective revascularization.Key Words: coronary artery bypass grafting, myocardial bridging, Senning procedure, transposition of the great arteries

History of Presentation

A 21-year-old man with a surgical history of d-transposition of the great arteries and aortic coarctation was referred to our department due to exertional fatigue and chest pain. He had been followed regularly in the outpatient clinic. His follow-up echocardiography and coronary angiography at 12 and 17 years revealed normal ventricular function, good valve function, no coronary stenosis, and mild ascending aortic stenosis.

Past Medical History

The patient underwent left subclavian flap aortoplasty at 13 days of age and the Senning procedure at 9 months of age. Arterial switch was unsuitable due to complex coronary anatomy (Shaher type 5a).

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included systemic right ventricular failure related to the prior Senning procedure and coronary ischemia due to the complex coronary anatomy of Shaher type 5a.

Investigation

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a systemic right ventricular ejection fraction of 32% and a peak pressure gradient of 25 mm Hg across the ascending aorta. Coronary computed tomography angiography showed a stenotic ascending aorta with a diameter of 16 mm at the sinotubular junction and both coronary arteries arising from the same posterior cusp. A long (length 34 mm) and deep (depth 9.5 mm) intramyocardial segment of the mid–left anterior descending (LAD) demonstrated coronary compression with the tunneled segment adjacent to the right ventricular cavity (Figure 1A). In addition, coronary angiography confirmed “milking” effect in the absence of atherosclerotic or calcified lesions (Figure 1B). Adenosine-stress ammonia positron emission tomography revealed reversible myocardial ischemia of the systemic right ventricular apical and inferior wall (Figure 1C).

Management

Given the combination of ascending aortic stenosis and myocardial bridging (MB) of the LAD with right ventricular myocardial ischemia, ascending aorta replacement and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) were planned. Median resternotomy was performed via redo median sternotomy. Under mild hypothermic (32 °C) cardiopulmonary bypass, the ascending aorta was replaced with a 26-mm Dacron graft (J-graft: Japan Life Line). CABG was performed with a free right internal mammary artery (RIMA)-LAD with a short segment saphenous vein graft (SVG) interposed at the 26-mm Dacron graft anastomosis (Figure 2A).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient had an uneventful postoperative course. He was discharged on postoperative day 17 and returned to full activity within 8 weeks. At 13 months, he remains symptom-free on aspirin, β-blocker, angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist. Postoperative coronary computed tomography angiography at 2 weeks confirmed graft patency (Figure 2B). At 1 year, follow-up coronary angiography, right ventriculography, and adenosine-stress ammonia positron emission tomography revealed a patent composite graft, improved right ventricular ejection fraction from 32% to 46%, and regression of the right ventricular myocardial ischemic area (Figures 3A and 3B).

Discussion

In the present case, MB was asymptomatic and not clearly evident at his coronary angiography and computed tomography in the first 2 decades. However, Corban et al1 reported that asymptomatic MB patients may become symptomatic when pathophysiological changes such as ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and coronary vasospasm develop. In this case, the right ventricle was subjected to a chronically high-pressure load due to the Senning procedure. In addition, ascending aorta stenosis may have further increased afterload, leading to excessive contraction and hypertrophy of the right ventricular wall. This could have aggravated the dynamic compression of the coronary artery, resulting in the development of MB and worsening of the patient’s symptoms. Further research is needed to fully clarify its pathogenesis and mechanisms of symptom exacerbation and development of MB.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of CABG performed for MB in a patient with a prior Senning procedure and left subclavian flap aortoplasty.

Myotomy is generally considered as the first-line surgical treatment. Vaikunth et al2 reported successful myotomy after arterial switch to d-transposition of the great arteries. However, recently, Hemmati et al3 reported a high incidence of recurrent chest pain following myotomy. In addition, intravascular ultrasound studies in children with MB demonstrated atherosclerotic changes at the proximal of the tunneled segment, possibly related to endothelial dysfunction associated with MB.4 These findings suggest that myotomy may be recommended for young patients before structural sequelae develop. Consequently, Sternheim et al5 recommended CABG when intramyocardial tunneled segments are >25 mm in length or >5 mm in depth. As our patient met both morphological criteria, we performed CABG in the patient. In addition, in this case, a deep incision of the ventricular wall was required. There was a possible perforation of the ventricular wall and a ventricular wall aneurysm, which hindered myotomy.

In young patients with MB, the optimal graft strategy remains controversial. Bockeria et al6 suggest that SVG as a preferred graft over the left internal mammary artery(LIMA). However, arterial grafts offer excellent long-term patency and are generally favored in conventional LAD revascularization. Therefore, the internal mammary artery graft was still considered preferable. In this case, the LIMA was not available because the prior left subclavian flap aortoplasty impeded its growth. Hence, the RIMA was selected as an alternative graft. The in situ RIMA was insufficient in length to reach the LAD; thus, we used the RIMA as a free graft. Nevertheless, direct anastomosis of the small and thin RIMA to a large, rigid, and thick prosthesis graft carries a risk of graft stenosis. To reduce this risk, a short segment of SVG was interposed between the prosthetic graft and the free-RIMA to maximize future graft patency and flow reserve.7 Postoperative imaging confirmed graft patency and resolution of ischemia.

Conclusions

Deep and long MB can present with exertional ischemia even decades after the Senning procedure. When myotomy is high-risk or impractical, carefully planned CABG using a composite arterial graft may offer an effective and durable alternative in young patients.
