Expert panel perspectives on management of GERD post esophageal POEM: a national survey study
Authors: Eliza Cricco-Lizza, Gaurav Ghosh, Dennis Yang, Kartik Sampath, B. Joseph Elmunzer, Srihari Mahadev, Reem Z. Sharaiha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsGastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) remains a major issue in the management of patients with achalasia after esophageal peroral endoscopic myotomy (E-POEM). This study investigated practice patterns surrounding empiric acid suppression, medication de-escalation, pH testing, and follow-up endoscopy of expert gastroenterologists managing these patients.MethodsAn online 17-item survey study was conducted in November 2024 with a response rate of 30/60. Expertise was determined by prior E-POEM publications and participation in the Delphi consensus on this topic and was validated by reported years of E-POEM experience and average number of E-POEMs performed annually.Results90% of responders performed E-POEM and 10% solely managed patients afterwards. 72% practiced in an academic institution and 45% performed more than 50 E-POEMs annually. There was no consensus on dose of acid suppression, but the most common regimen was a 40 mg omeprazole equivalent daily until follow-up. Responders had varied approaches to de-escalation, with 44% consistently performing pH testing. 48% performed endoscopic screening for Barrett’s esophagus/cancer, and 83% of these continued at a regular interval indefinitely. 65% of responders reported taking procedural steps to minimize GERD after E-POEM, most often short myotomy (76%), and 24% a “tailored” approach using EndoFLIP.ConclusionsThe majority of experts reported taking measures during E-POEM to limit the risk of GERD. Despite GERD being an important consideration, experts remained almost evenly divided on the role of reflux testing in medication de-escalation and on the practice of endoscopic screening. Prospective studies informed by these expert insights would be especially valuable in determining whether pH testing and endoscopic screening for GERD complications improve patient outcomes.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-026-12747-z.

Achalasia is a rare disease that involves degeneration of the ganglion cells of the myenteric plexus in the esophagus, leading to both impaired esophageal contraction and disordered relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES). It is estimated to affect 1 in 100,000 persons, with typical symptoms of dysphagia, regurgitation, weight loss, and chest pain [1]. Treatment options include pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic heller myotomy (LHM) with fundoplication, and esophageal per-oral endoscopic myotomy (E-POEM). A 2019 international randomized control trial comparing E-POEM to LHM demonstrated noninferiority in clinical success, i.e., control of symptoms, at both 2-year and then 5-year follow-up [2, 3]. Since then, E-POEM has had increasing uptake [4].

GERD is the most common complication after E-POEM estimated at ~ 38% [3, 5]. Multiple studies have demonstrated a poor correlation of symptoms with extent of erosive esophagitis in this population, raising questions around the indications for workup in the asymptomatic patient [6–9]. pH testing has been introduced to obtain more definitive GERD evaluation, though post-operative esophageal stasis leading to fermentation also confounds accurate assessment of true acid reflux [10]. Though most patients do respond to acid suppression, attempts to identify who will develop post-POEM GERD have not found any patient or operative features that reliably predict its occurrence, including type of achalasia, technique of E-POEM (anterior vs. posterior), pre- or post-POEM esophageal manometry features, or patient characteristics [6]. Regarding the long-term implications, there is increasing concern for the hypothetical and yet unclear risk for esophageal cancer. Achalasia patients even without treatment have been shown to have a fivefold increased risk of both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma of the esophagus compared to patients without achalasia [11]. However, limited existing long-term follow-up leaves much of the risk of post-POEM GERD-driven esophageal adenocarcinoma theoretical [12].

In an effort to collate expert experience and practice, a Delphi Consensus was configured in 2024 to address the management of post-POEM care [13]. While they were able to reach consensus on certain decision points such as same-day discharge, clinic follow-up, and need for reflux assessment in clinic, no consensus was achieved on duration or de-escalation of acid suppression, the role of pH testing, or the need for periodic endoscopic screening for esophageal cancer, therefore no guidance on these topics was provided. A subsequent AGA clinical practice update stated that acid suppression at discharge should be “strongly considered” [14]. It also recommended that patients with refractory or atypical reflux symptoms on maximal proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy should undergo additional workup, and that all patients should “undergo monitoring” for GERD post-procedurally, however, no specific guidance regarding pH testing was made. Finally, “long-term post-procedure surveillance is encouraged” given risks of complications from GERD, however, no specific method or timeline of surveillance is stated.

The goal of this study was to explore current practices and beliefs of expert providers to better understand what current patterns exist and what beliefs underly the controversies that remain a barrier to consensus in this largely data-sparse zone.

Methods

Creation of the survey

A meeting group was organized to design the components of the survey based off the findings of the Delphi consensus [13]. The survey contained 17 items of both multiple choice and open-ended questions (see Supplement). These questions solicited physician practice characteristics as well as specific management decisions regarding patients undergoing E-POEM. The online survey platform Survey Planet was used to create the survey. All questions were mandatory but also contained the option to add free text to elaborate on the answer choice. The goal was to easily compile concrete, organized decision points but also to keep the options open to a broad range of practice patterns that were not biased or siloed by the answer choices. The final question of the survey was entirely open-ended and asked participants to provide any aspects of their approach to this issue that may be relevant or were not addressed in the survey.

Selection of the expert panel and solicitation of responses

The panel included 60 advanced endoscopists and esophagologists as determined by the prior Delphi Consensus as well as participation in prior E-POEM publications. This experience was validated within the survey with questions that solicited years of E-POEM performance and average number of E-POEMs performed annually. The survey was distributed over email in November 2024 by the final author (RZS) who is known amongst this community as an expert E-POEM performer as well and was a member of the Delphi Consensus. The email briefly explained the purpose of the study, stated that it was optional, and provided a link to the Survey Planet website where the software prevented multiple responses from a single user. Participants were blinded to other recipients of the survey and did not receive compensation for their participation. Responses were anonymous. One reminder email was sent out 12 days after the original email by the first author (ECL) who was named as an investigator in the prior email. A period of 3 months was allotted for response to the survey before it was closed and the data were collected and analyzed.

Analysis of data

Individual full survey responses as well as compiled question statistics were generated by the Survey Planet software. All free text submissions to questions were reviewed. In occasional circumstances where the answer choice “Other” was selected but the free text met objective criteria of an established answer choice the response was included in the tally for the objective answer choice after discussion amongst the authors to ensure the integrity of the answer was maintained. Percentiles of response rates of each answer choice were therefore manually calculated to ensure correct representation. For example, when reporting the percentage of responders who use specific techniques during E-POEM to minimize reflux, the 3 responders who do not perform the procedure were excluded from the denominator.

STATA version 18 was subsequently used for a chi-square statistic to evaluate the association between practice type or case volume and the decision to perform pH testing or endoscopic surveillance.

The text answers to the final survey question were analyzed using manual qualitative data coding to determine themes of responses with full text primary data reported in Fig. 4.

Results

Thirty (50%) of 60 physicians contacted completed the study. Twenty-seven (90%) of responders performed E-POEM and 3 (10%) were esophagologists who managed patients afterwards. Twenty-two (73%) practiced in an academic institution, 6 (20%) hybrid practice, 1 (3%) private practice, and 1 (3%) other. Thirteen (48.1%) E-POEM performers had an average volume of more than 50 E-POEMs per year (Fig. 3).

Regarding timing of follow-up in clinic, 9 (18.8%) responders see patients at two weeks after the procedure, 11 (22.9%) at four weeks after the procedure, and the remainder at six weeks or later. Twenty-six (86.7%) responders prescribe a PPI at discharge from the procedure, but 4 (13.3%) do not. The duration of acid suppression varied widely, with the most common practice (7 (23.3%)) being “until follow up”. There was no consensus on dose of acid suppression, but the most common regimen was a 40 mg omeprazole equivalent daily until follow-up (10 (33.3%) of responders) (Fig. 1). Responders had varied approaches to de-escalation, with 8 (26.7%) consistently performing pH testing in all patients, 6 (20%) discontinuing if patients are asymptomatic, 6 (20%) using endoscopy to evaluate for esophagitis and discontinuing if none, and 8 (26.7%) continuing a standard duration of medication no matter symptoms or endoscopic findings. When asked about the indications for pH testing, 12 (40%) of responders perform objective testing on all patients or all endoscopy-negative patients, mostly with wireless testing such as BRAVO (10 (83.3%) of pH-testing performers) and 13 (43.3%) only perform on patients with persistent reflux. Five (16.7%) do not perform pH testing as they do not think it affects management (Fig. 1).

Twenty (66.7%) responders perform esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) on all patients after E-POEM and 5 (16.7%) do not perform at all. Four (13.3%) of those who perform EGD do so only in patients with persistent symptoms. When asked specifically about EGD for the purpose of endoscopic screening for Barrett’s esophagus/cancer, 14 (46.7%) endoscopically screen long-term, and 12 (40%) do not (Fig. 1).

Decision for pH testing was not associated with practice type (p = 0.45) or years of E-POEM performance (p = 0.19). Similarly, the decision to perform endoscopic screening was not associated with practice type (p = 0.42) or years of E-POEM performance (p = 0.46). No responders cited lack of availability of pH testing as a reason against its use, even when explicitly asked.

Seventeen (56.7%) responders report indefinite acid suppression is required on 25–50% of their patients. Seventeen (56.7%) responders have referred only one or no patients for anti-reflux surgery, and 8 (26.7%) have referred 2–5 patients.

Seventeen (63%) procedural responders reported taking procedural steps to minimize GERD after E-POEM, most often short myotomy (13 (76%)). 4 (23.5%) make efforts to preserve the sling fibers, and 4 (23.5%) employ a “tailored” approach with EndoFLIP (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Provider practice patterns regarding use of procedural approaches to reduce GERD and report of need for long term acid suppression or anti-reflux surgery

Discussion

Esophageal POEM has risen in frequency as a minimally invasive safe and effective treatment for achalasia. The most common complication, GERD, has been well-demonstrated and is of increasing interest as the procedure continues to be refined and optimized. This survey examined current practice patterns of high-volume E-POEM practitioners and esophageal experts who manage these patients post-procedurally. Our study found a wide range of practice patterns and recommendations behind them, often with two distinct camps existing on topics still in the guideline-free zone.

Certain aspects of GERD after E-POEM are beginning to emerge that make it unique from the natural history of typical GERD. These patients now have an altered anti-reflux barrier (ARB); there are unclear consequences of dissection on sensory nerve function in the lower esophagus; there is documented esophageal stasis leading to fermentation which has different implications from true refluxed acid from the stomach; and a high proportion of patients with documented erosive esophagitis have no symptoms at all. Many questions remain, including what exactly the implications of uncontrolled GERD are in these patients, what procedural modifications have a meaningful impact on preventing it, and what additional therapies may be offered to patients with GERD refractory to PPIs. In the meantime, practical management decision points remain unclear as even the most recent clinical updates lack sufficient data to encourage any more than “strong consideration” for acid suppression after E-POEM and nonspecific recommendations for GERD workup and surveillance long-term [14].

While most providers always discharge patients on acid suppression, a reasonable minority (13.3%) do not. As of the time of this survey in Fall 2024, PCABs have yet to be embraced by this cohort of physicians in the post-POEM realm. This likely reflects that no studies exist for this medication in these patients, and the class has only recently become implemented in the USA after FDA approval for diagnoses such as erosive esophagitis and Helicobacter pylori treatment [15].

Regarding de-escalation of acid suppression, a fifth of experts continue to use symptoms as the benchmark despite definitive evidence for poor correlation of GERD or esophagitis with symptoms. They do not consistently report lack of access to pH testing as the reason. More than a quarter of experts apply a standard therapy to all patients regardless of symptoms or any objective evaluation for GERD. The remaining near half of experts use objective evaluation with endoscopy or pH testing to determine safety of de-escalation (Fig. 1). This reflects the truly diverse practices even among some of the most experienced practitioners, with only half of experts ensuring objective evaluation in their decision. A possible reason for this may be uncertainty over the proper timing of GERD assessment. One study with 10-year data showed that 35% of patients with positive reflux studies at a median of 5 months normalized their study at a median follow-up of 57 months, thought possibly due to the role of expected LES remodeling over time [16]. Only 2 responders of the survey raised timing of pH testing as an important consideration. Despite this, the overall rise in pH testing implementation is reflected in Fig. 1, where > 80% of experts employ pH monitoring in their practice, either universally (40%) or specifically to work up persistently symptomatic patients. With mounting evidence for poor correlation with symptoms, pH testing likely should play a role in management of GERD after POEM.

While the majority of responders perform a follow-up endoscopy after E-POEM, 16.7% do not perform one at all. Regarding the practice of follow-up endoscopy for the specific concern of screening for Barrett’s esophagus/cancer, the responders were split nearly bimodally between performance on all patients (48.3%) (majority screening every 1–2 years indefinitely), or not performing screening at all (40%) (Fig. 1). This polarization of practice suggests two distinct fields of thought, one that the risk of complications is significant and needs close monitoring, and the other that there is insufficient data to justify monitoring of any kind. Given the latency of these complications, it is likely too early for conclusive data regarding this true risk. However, there do already exist case reports of Barrett’s esophagus and even intramucosal carcinoma at alarmingly short intervals in patients who had testing-confirmed de novo GERD post-POEM, raising subsequent concern in the community to clarify this risk in more detail [5, 12, 17–19]. In this data-free zone, the polarized practice patterns likely explain why no majority consensus was previously possible. Free responses indicated the wide spectrum of individualization of approach to this issue, with some providers performing EGD “after 10–12 years of disease every 2 years for squamous cell cancer screening”, “in 1 year is my recommendation and repeat at time of colonoscopy there after”, or in settings of “family h[istory] present (rare) or severe erosive esophagitis or mucosal irregularity seen on prior EGDs.” We predict that in the next 5 years there will become more definitive observational evidence for why endoscopic screening should indeed be performed in this population.

Procedural modifications are an active area of research in this field, both for efficacy as well as postoperative GERD mitigation. Procedural options to prevent GERD show limited success thus far: systematic review data suggests that short myotomy results in lower rates of erosive esophagitis [20], while a recent randomized control trial looking at a novel oblique myotomy to preserve sling fibers showed no significant impact on the incidence of reflux esophagitis when compared with conventional myotomy [21]. Our study shows these approaches are being utilized by 63% of experts, primarily being a short myotomy but with 20% of this group implementing Endoluminal functional lumen imaging probe (EndoFLIP) to tailor the myotomy. There is general thought that the increased risk of GERD in E-POEM compared to traditional LHM is due to the lack of an accompanying anti-reflux procedure [3, 5]. Patients 5 years after E-POEM are twice as likely to have abnormal acid exposure time on reflux testing as those after LHM (62% vs 31%), despite having roughly the same frequency of reflux symptoms [3]. While no providers described empiric fundoplication in their practice, some did report early success with referral for anti-reflux procedures for GERD after E-POEM, which also reflects recent experimental reports of TIF use after or concomitant with E-POEM [22, 23]. EndoFLIP as a live measurement tool may hold promise in guiding the end point for optimal myotomy balancing dysphagia and GERD risk going forward.

The strengths of this study include the internal validation of high-volume and experience of its responders, the blinding of participants to others’ responses, and the qualitative design that allowed for nuances of approach to be revealed without sectioning patients into group responses that may not reflect their true practice. Though multiple choice was deployed for survey questions, every question included a free text for answers to be expanded on if not represented sufficiently in the answer choices. Nonetheless, limitations exist in the ability of a single cohort to reflect the full breadth of existing thought and practice on this issue, and therefore these practice patterns should not be interpreted as necessarily nationally representative of approaches to GERD after POEM. All survey studies are at risk of non-response bias, including our study with a response rate of 50%, however, a variety of practice approaches were represented among our responders. A limitation that was difficult to bypass is the inherent recall bias in self-reporting of responders. Additionally, while further sub-analysis based upon case volume and specific practice patterns may have yielded interesting insights, this was deferred as there was insufficient statistical power to draw meaningful conclusions after multiple stratifications.

This study adds to the literature by revealing the actuality of expert practice for decision points that currently remain largely data-free with too much discrepancy to allow consensus statements to provide insight to new practitioners. The recent Delphi Consensus highlights the importance of expert opinion in areas of uncertainty in clinical practice of novel procedures, however, when consensus fails, qualitative exploration is needed to reveal both possible clinical approaches in the granular as well as the insights gleaned from the highest volume practitioners. In the case of E-POEM, achalasia is a rare disease entity and therefore it is challenging for a single early practitioner to garner the personal experience necessary to adjust stylistic practice patterns without clear guidance from the larger medical community. This knowledge allows for a better focus for future research and exploration to reveal evidence for what approaches truly provide benefit. In the case of post E-POEM GERD management, prospective studies informed by these expert insights that compare the outlined clinical approaches would be especially valuable in determining whether pH testing (and when) and performance of endoscopic screening for GERD complications improve patient outcomes.
