ADNP missense variant p.C687R disrupts chromatin regulation and GABAergic differentiation in Helsmoortel–Van der Aa syndrome
Authors: Qi Chen, Xixi Liu, Mengnan Wu, Daijing Sun, Xingyu Ding, Mengling Zhou, Wenzhu Peng, Yan Cheng, Biqing Xue, Ning Tang, Gang Xu, Yilin Tai, Qiong Xu, Man Xiong, Yan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActivity-dependent neuroprotective protein (ADNP) is a critical regulator of neurodevelopment, and most pathogenic variants reported in Helsmoortel–Van der Aa syndrome (HVDAS) are truncating variants. In contrast, the functional consequences of ADNP missense variants remain largely unclear. We integrated an ADNP variant cohort in China with variants recorded in the NCBI ClinVar database, revealing a major gap in the interpretation of ADNP missense variants.MethodsWe investigated a rare de novo ADNP missense variant, p.C687R, predicted to disrupt the ninth zinc finger domain. In vitro, p.C687R was overexpressed in HEK293T cells to assess subnuclear localization by immunofluorescence and chromatin binding patterns using CUT&Tag, with chromatin interactions inferred from published Hi-C datasets. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated ADNP knockout was performed for comparison. In vivo, wild-type ADNP or p.C687R was introduced into the embryonic mouse cortex at E14.5 via in utero electroporation (IUE) and neuronal development was evaluated at E18.5 and P14. Patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from a de novo p.C687R carrier were differentiated into neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and analyzed by multi-omic profiling (RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq), with lineage-specific markers examined by immunofluorescence.Resultsp.C687R displays altered subnuclear localization and redistributes wild-type ADNP when overexpressed in HEK293T. IUE in the mouse cortical plate revealed impaired neuronal migration and abnormal cortical arborization. Genome-wide profiling in HEK293T demonstrated a p.C687R-specific chromatin occupancy pattern, preferentially targeting histone modification-related genes. Knockout of ADNP led to upregulation of neuronal genes, including GABAergic lineage-associated genes. In patient-derived iPSCs, a distinct set of neurodevelopmental genes, including key regulators of GABAergic differentiation, showed increased bivalent histone marks (H3K4me3/H3K27me3). Although their promoters remained in an open chromatin state, these genes were transcriptionally silent in pluripotent cells but became more activated upon GABAergic differentiation.LimitationsThis study is based on a single patient-derived line in combination with complementary experimental models. In the heterozygous endogenous context, distinguishing increased functional activity from dosage-related effects requires further investigation. Replication in additional patient-derived or engineered lines is required to determine the generalizability.ConclusionsOur results suggest that p.C687R may exert gain-of-function-like effects in experimental systems and underscore chromatin-mediated regulation of GABAergic lineage genes in HVDAS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13229-026-00713-4.Keywords: ADNP, GABAergic neural progenitors, Helsmoortel–Van der Aa syndrome, Histone modifications

Background

Helsmoortel-Van der Aa syndrome (HVDAS; OMIM 615873), also known as ADNP syndrome, is a rare autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by core features such as developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), hypotonia, and sensory processing abnormalities. Additional comorbidities may involve dermatologic, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital systems [1]. HVDAS is caused by pathogenic variants in the ADNP gene, which is one of the highest risk genes of ASD based on large-scale whole exome sequencing (WES) studies [2–4].

ADNP encodes the activity-dependent neuroprotective protein (ADNP), a highly conserved transcriptional regulator essential for embryogenesis [5], particularly during neurodevelopmental processes, including neural tube closure [6], cortical patterning [7], axonal outgrowth [8], and synaptic plasticity [9]. ADNP was first identified via its N-terminal neuroprotective peptide NAPVSIPQ (NAP) [10], which promotes neuronal survival [11]. Structurally, ADNP contains nine zinc finger (ZF) domains, a nuclear localization signal (NLS), and a C-terminal homeobox domain, enabling its function in gene regulation and chromatin remodeling [12]. It is a critical component of the ChAHP complex (CHD4/ADNP/HP1), which modulates chromatin accessibility and 3D genome architecture [13].

Since the first identification of ADNP variants in HVDAS patients in 2014 [3], several cohorts have defined the genetic and phenotypic spectrum of the disorder [1, 14–17]. The majority of pathogenic variants are de novo coding variants, most commonly nonsense and frameshift changes that introduce premature termination codons (PTCs), leading to truncated proteins. A large-scale study, including a cohort of 78 patients from 16 countries [14], and additional case series across Europe [1], North America [15, 16], and Asia [17], consistently shown that truncating variants dominate the ADNP variant landscape. Notably, recurrent variants such as p.Y719* (c.2157 C > G), p.R730* (c.2188 C > T), and p.N832Kfs*81 (c.2491_2494delTTAA)/ p.N832Kfs*81 (c.2496_2499delTAAA) have been observed in multiple unrelated individuals [14–17]. Despite deleterious effects, a most recent study reported a protective effect of p.E931Gfs*12 [18].

Mechanistic studies to date have also primarily focused on truncating variants [19]. The loss of key functional domains, particularly the NLS and homeobox, leads to impaired nuclear import and disrupted chromatin binding [12, 19, 20]. For instance, variants affecting residues 473–719 result in cytoplasmic retention [19], and p.Y719* is associated with more severe motor and sensory deficits [14]. Recent work has also linked variant position to distinct genome-wide DNA methylation signatures (epi-ADNP-1, epi-ADNP-2) [15] and clinical severity [14], suggesting genotype-phenotype correlations. Moreover, a study using newly developed AI-based phenomics framework (PhenoScore) provided evidence supporting differential clinical features for epi-ADNP-1 and epi-ADNP-2 [21]. Functional studies of truncating ADNP variants have shown impaired functions in R-looping [12], Wnt/β-catenin signaling [22], microtubule dynamics [23], and autophagy regulation [24]. These deficits are recapitulated in mouse models including haploinsufficient [9], brain conditional knockout [7], and p.Y718* knock-in mice [25], supporting a loss-of-function (LOF) mechanism in HVDAS pathogenesis.

In contrast, the clinical relevance and functional impact of ADNP missense variants remain poorly understood. While several missense variants have been reported in public databases, few have been linked to HVDAS or studied in detail. Among five missense variants analyzed in a 2019 study, only one de novo variant c.201G > C was predicted to alter splicing and classified as likely pathogenic [15]. The first validated disease-associated missense variant, located next to the terminal residue of the NAP peptide, was identified in a patient with mild neurodevelopmental symptoms [26]. Our previous work identified two additional de novo missense variants, p.C687R and p.R730G, in a Chinese pediatric cohort [17], yet their pathogenic mechanisms remain to be explored.

In this study, we focus on the p.C687R variant to investigate its functional impact and underlying molecular mechanism. This variant lies near the ninth ZF domain and is predicted, based on protein modeling, to disrupt ZF structure, potentially altering the DNA-binding capacity of ADNP. To evaluate its impact, we used overexpression cell models to assess the nuclear localization, chromatin binding, and gene regulatory functions of p.C687R, and conducted in utero electroporation (IUE) in mouse embryos to examine the effects on neuronal differentiation. Moreover, we generated patient-derived iPSCs and differentiated them into GABAergic neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and performed ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq and RNA-seq to profile H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 landscapes, chromatin accessibility, and gene expression, thereby exploring how p.C687R affects neurodevelopmental programs, particularly GABAergic neuron specification. Our findings suggest that p.C687R may exert gain-of-function–like (GOF-like) effects in experimental systems, which may alter the chromatin regulation during the neurodevelopment and attribute to the onset of disease.

Methods

Plasmid construction

Plasmids expressing full-length human ADNP (NM_015339.5) and p.C687R variant were constructed as previously described [17], featured by an HA tag at the N-terminal and either Flag or Myc or both tags at the C-terminal. A second open reading frame encoding EGFP is located downstream of the C-terminal tags and separated by the IRES element. Similarly, plasmids expressing full-length mouse Adnp (NM_001310086.1) were constructed with Flag or Myc at the C-terminal. The mADNP p.C686R (p.C686R) variant was introduced into the same plasmid backbone with the KOD-PlusMutagenesis Kit (TOYOBO, SMK-101). All plasmids were validated by Sanger sequencing.

Cell culture and transfection

The human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell Biotech, FSP500) under the following conditions: 95% air/5% CO2 in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. For immunofluorescence staining, 1 µg of plasmid was transfected into 1–3 × 105 HEK293T by Highgene transfection reagent (Abclonal, RM09014). For anti-Flag CUT&Tag, 2 µg of plasmid was transfected into 3–5 × 105 HEK293T. Cells were cultured for 72 h before use.

HEK293T knockout cell line construct

Guide RNAs targeting ADNP exon 3 (NM_015339.5) were designed with CRISPR Design Tool (https://zlab.bio/guide-design-resources), which specifically targeted the sequence (5’ – CAAGATTGTTGACAGGAAGT − 3’, 5’ – AATACTTAGTGACATTGGGT − 3’). Oligonucleotides derived from guide RNAs were inserted into lentiCRISPRv1 (Addgene, 49535), which was a gift from Dr. Chen at the Brain Science and Intelligence Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. 1 µg of the plasmids was introduced into 1–3 × 105 HEK293T based on the aforementioned transfection method. After 24 h, the cells were treated with 2 µg/ml purinomycin for 48 h. Following digestion and resuspension, the cells were then subjected a gradient dilution to establish a monoclonal culture in a 96-well plate. The monoclonal cells were selected under the microscope after 1 week. After expanding the monoclonal, the knockout was confirmed by PCR and Western blotting.

iPSC reprogramming and neural differentiation

iPSCs derived from patients’ PBMCs were obtained as previously described [27]. The control samples were derived from a healthy pediatric donor and were therefore broadly age-comparable to the patient. However, the control was not sex-matched to the patient. The experimental group was obtained from the single patient carrying the identified variant, from whom three independent inductions were conducted to generate three biological replicates. Briefly, isolated PBMCs were cultured in StemPro-34 SFM Complete Medium (STEMCELL Technologies, 9655), which was refreshed every two days. For reprogramming, 1 × 106 PBMCs were transfected with 18 ug of episomal reprogramming plasmid mixture (Addgene 27077: pCXLE-hOCT3/4-shp53-F, Addgene 27078: pCXLEhSK, Addgene 27080: pCXLE-hUL) by electroporation (Celetrix, 650 V) on day 9. 72 h after electroporation, cells were cultured on 6-well plates pre-coated with Vitronectin (Thermo Fisher, A14700) in Essential 8TM Basal Medium with Essential 8TM Supplement (50×) (Thermo Fisher, A1517001), which was refreshed daily. iPSC clones emerged after approximately 14 days. With another 4–6 days, stable colonies were picked and passaged for expansion and analysis.

To differentiate NPCs, iPSC colonies 4–6 days after passaging were detached into small clumps with dispase and suspended with E8 medium for 3 days to form embryonic bodies (EBs). From day 4 to day 6, SB431542 (Tocris Bioscience, 2 µM) and DMH-1 (Tocris Bioscience, 2 µM) were added into neural induction medium (NIM) containing DMEM/F-12, 1×N2 supplement and 1×NEAA. From day 10 to day 26, SAG (0.15 µM), which is an agonist of the sonic hedgehog pathway, was added to the NIM medium. On day 26, NPCs were fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min and dehydrated with 30% sucrose solutions overnight at 4 °C. After serially sectioning at a thickness of 15 μm by frozen section (CM1950, Leica), the samples were washed 3 times with Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS), then permeabilized by 0.2% DPBST (DPBS, 0.2% Triton X-100) for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking with 10% normal donkey serum in DPBS for 1 h at room temperature, the slices were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-NKX2.1 (Millipore, MAB5460, 1:1000) and anti-SOX2 (R&D Systems, AF2018, 1:800) diluted with 0.1% DPBST containing 5% normal donkey serum. After washing 3 times, slices were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies and Hochest diluted in 5% normal donkey serum at room temperature for 1 h. Images were captured by Nikon AX inverted confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining (IF)

Cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After washing with 1×PBS 3 times and permeabilizing cell membranes for 10 min with 0.5% PBST (1×PBS, 0.5% Triton X-100) at room temperature, the cells were blocked in 0.5% PBST supplemented with 10% normal goat serum (Thermo, 16210064) for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies in PBST supplemented with 2% normal goat serum: anti-Flag (Smart lifesciences, SLAB0101,1:1000) and anti-Myc (Proteintech, 16286-1-AP, 1:1000). After washing with 1×PBS 3 times, the cells were incubated with DAPI (Beyotime, C1002, 1:10000) and fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies: goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor cy3 (Jackson, 115–165 − 146,1:1000) and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Jackson,128–545-003, 1:1000) at room temperature for 1 h in darkness. Following 3 washes with 1×PBS, the images were captured using a Nikon AX inverted confocal microscope. The number and width of puncta were statistically analyzed using imagej Fiji.

For E18.5 embryos, the whole brain was collected and immersed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. Then the 14 μm sections of frozen coronal brain were prepared. After washing with 0.1% PBST (1×PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100) 3 times, the slices were blocked in 0.1% PBST supplemented with 5% normal donkey serum (Yeasen, 36116ES110) for 30 min at room temperature. The slices were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies in blocking solution: anti-Flag (Smart lifesciences, SLAB0101, 1:2000) and anti-GFP (Avesselabs, GFP-1020, 1:2000). After 3 washes with 0.1% PBST, the slices were incubated with DAPI (Beyotime, C1002, 1:10000) and fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies: donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 594 (Jackson, 715-585-150,1:1000) and donkey anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 488 (Jackson, 703-545-155, 1:1000) at room temperature for 1 h in darkness. Following another 3 washes, the images were captured using an OLYMPUS virtual slide system microscope (VS120).

For P14 mice, perfused the heart with 30 ml of cold 1×PBS and 4% PFA. The brain tissue was collected with the meninges being dissected away, and immersed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight, followed by 2 dehydration steps in 30% sucrose solution. 30 μm sections of frozen coronal brain were prepared. The staining procedure was conducted as aforementioned, except that a 0.5% PBST solution was used. The primary antibodies used were: anti-Flag (Smart lifesciences, SLAB0101,1:2000), anti-GFP (Avesselabs, GFP-1020, 1:2000) and anti-RFP (1:10000). The following fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were used: goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 594 (Jackson, 715-585-150,1:1000), goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 488 (Jackson, 103-545-155, 1:1000), goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Jackson, 115-545-003,1:1000) and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (Jackson, 111-585-003). The images were captured using a Nikon AX inverted confocal microscope. The quantification of complexity was analyzed using the Sholl analysis of Fiji.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from HEK293T by boiling cell lysates at 95 °C for 5 min in lysis buffer (2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.0625 M Tris HCl, pH 6.8) supplemented with 1×protease inhibitor (Beyotime, P1005). Protein concentrations were determined with Omni-Easy™ Instant BCA Protein Assay kit (Epizyme Biotech, ZJ102) and 40 µg protein of each sample was loaded for blotting. The total protein and protein marker (Epizyme, WJ103) were electrophoresed on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel (Epizyme, PG112) and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore, HATF00010). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 30 min at room temperature, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies: anti-HA (Abmart, M20003S, 1:5000), anti-Flag (Smart lifesciences, SLAB0101, 1:10000), anti-ADNP (Proteintech, 17987-1-AP, 1:1000) and anti-GAPDH (Beijing Ray Antibody Biotech, RM2002, 1:5000). The membrane was washed with TBST buffer (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20) 3 times after incubation and then incubated with either an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (Proteintech, SA000012, 1:10,000) or an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (Proteintech, SA00001-1, 1:10,000) antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The signal was developed by immersing the membrane in the ECL luminescent solution (Tanon, 180–5001) and was captured with a digital chemiluminescence imager (Tanon).

In utero electroporation (IUE)

The constructed full-length hADNP and variant hADNP p.C687R (p.C687R) plasmids were introduced into embryos by IUE. All mouse work was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Medical College, Fudan University. Mice were housed at 21 to 24 °C with 45% to 55% humidity under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All mice were allowed sterile water and food ad libitum. Both sexes were used. Two mixing strategies were employed: for embryonic experiments, hADNP and p.C687R were mixed separately with pCA-miR-FF at a 4:1 mass ratio; for postnatal experiments, hADNP and p.C687R were mixed separately with pCAG-tdTomato (Addgene, 83029) or pCA-miR-FF at a 4:1 mass ratio. pCA-miR-FF [28] was gifted from Dr. Tai at Fudan University. At 14.5 of gestation (E14.5), the uterine horns of CD-1 female mice were exposed after anesthetizing and kept moist by irrigating with 1×PBS. 1 µg plasmid mixture was injected into the lateral ventricle of embryos through a sharpened glass micropipette with beveled edges. With two 5-millimeter electrode paddles (BTX, 45–0489) applied to both sides of the head (BTX, ECM830), 5 square 50-ms pulses of 45 V were delivered across the uterus, which was separated by 950-ms intervals. Uterine horns were repositioned after electroporation, and the wound was surgically sutured. Pups were sacrificed at E18.5 or postnatal day 14 (P14) for IF.

RNA extraction and RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from 0.5 to 1 × 106 cells with Direct-zol RNA Microprep (ZYMO Research, R2060). Briefly, cells were collected in QIAzol Lysis Reagent (QIAGEN, 74804), then the samples were vortexed thoroughly with the addition of an equal amount of ethanol. The mixture was transferred to Zymo-Spin IC Column and eluted with RNase-free water. The product was quantified by Nanodrop. For RNA-seq, 500 ng RNA was sent to Novogene for mRNA library construction and sequenced with a Novaseq sequencer for 150 bp, paired-end reads (PE150), which yielded an average of 6G data per sample.

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag were conducted with NovoNGS CUT&Tag 3.0 High-Sensitivity Kit for Illumina (Novoprotein, N259-YH01). In general, 1 × 105 cells were collected by concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads after a 10-minute incubation at room temperature. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1 µg anti-Flag antibody (Smart Lifesciences, SLAB0101), followed by an 1 h incubation with secondary antibody at room temperature. After pre-binding with proteinA/G-Tn5 transposase, the DNA was subjected to tagmentation by incubating at 37 °C for 1 h, which was initiated with the addition of MgCl2. The reaction was then terminated by incubating with proteinase K at 55 °C for 10 min. The product DNA fragments was extracted by DNA Extract Beads and subjected to library amplification, which were sent to Haplox and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (paired-end, 150 bp). On average, 10 G data was yielded per sample.

ChIP-seq

About 3 × 106 cells were put into native ChIP-seq and performed as described [29]. Cells were harvested and lysed with NP40 lysis buffer. Briefly, chromatins were fragmented with MNase at 28 °C for 5 min in douncing buffer (10 mM Tris pH8, 4 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2). Samples were incubated with anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore, 07–449) or anti-H3K4me3 (Abcam, ab8580) antibody at 4 °C overnight, and then protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific, 88803) were added to capture the immunoprecipitated complexes. After sequential wash with low-salt buffer (0.1% SDS, 1% Trition X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris pH8, 150 mM NaCl), high-salt buffer (0.1% SDS, 1% Trition X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris pH8, 500 mM NaCl), lithium Chloride buffer (1% NP40, 1% deoxycholic acid, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris pH8, 0.25 M LiCl) and TE buffer (1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris pH8), the products were eluted from beads by elution buffer (0.1 M NaHCO3, 1% SDS). The RNA and protein were separately digested with RNase A (Sigma R6513) and proteinase K (Sigma P2308), under the conditions of 37 °C and 52 °C, for 15 min and 30 min. Finally, the chromatin fragments were collected by ethanol precipitation and subjected to size selection through SPRI magnetic beads (Beckman, B23318). For library preparation, ChIP DNA was blunted and A-tailed using VAHTS Universal End Preparation Module for Illumina V (Vazyme, N203-01) and VAHTS Universal Adapter Ligation Module for Illumina V2 (Vazyme, N204-01), followed by Y-adapter ligation and amplification with VAHTS HiFi Universal Amplification Mix for Illumina (Vazyme N618-02). The library was size-selected as aforementioned and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq (paired-end, 150 bp).

ATAC-seq

ATAC-seq was conducted with TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme, TD501-02) as previously described [30]. The nuclei of 105 cells were extracted with NP40 lysis buffer (0.32 M sucrose, 5 mM CaCl2, 3 mM MgAce2, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10 mM tris-HCl, and 0.1% NP-40) and then treated with Tn5 at 37 °C for 45 min. The DNA was purified with the MinElute Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, 28604) and subjected to PCR. The library DNA was purified with SPRIselect beads (Beckman, B23318) and sent to Novogene for paired-end sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq (paired-end, 150 bp).

Bioinformatic analysis

Statistical plots were generated with Graphpad Prism 6.01 and the R package “ggplot2” (version 3.4.2). Representative images of genome-wide datasets were generated with IGV (version 2.14.0).

Sholl analysis

To evaluate genotype-dependent differences in Sholl crossings across radial distances, we employed a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) fitted via restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The fixed-effect included genotype (hADNP and p.C687R), radius, and their interactions. A second-order orthogonal polynomial for radius was incorporated to eliminate collinearity between the linear and quadratic terms. The random-effect comprised animals and the neurons nested with them. P-values for genotype comparisons at each radius were obtained by conducting t-tests on contrasts of estimated marginal means derived from the linear mixed-effects model.

RNA-seq

RNA-seq data were analyzed according to a previously published protocol [31] with minor modifications. Quality control was conducted by FastQC (version 0.11.9), followed by reads being trimmed with Trim Galore (version 0.6.7, --quality 20 --phred33 --stringency 1 --length 20 –fastqc --paired). Clean reads of high quality were aligned to hg38 with HISAT2 (version 2.2.1) in default settings, which were further binarized, sorted, and indexed with SAMtools (version 1.6). Bigwig files were generated by the “bamCoverage” function of Deeptools (version 3.5.1) (--binSize 10 --normalizeUsing RPKM --effectiveGenomeSize 2913022398 --ignoreForNormalization chrX chrM). Reads on exons were counted with featureCounts (version 2.0.3) (-p -t exon -g gene_id -a gencode.vM20.annotation.gtf). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 (version 1.34.0) in default settings, and gene annotations were conducted with the R package “biomaRt” (version 2.50.3) (biomart = “ensembl”,version = 108,dataset="hsapiens_gene_ensembl”), with promoters defined as TSS ± 3 Kb. Gene enrichment analysis was conducted by ShinyGO (version 0.82) and CellGo [32] (Species, Human; Identifier, 42-prenatal brain cerebral cortex-Major type-Trevino, A. E. et al. https://zhuy-lab.fudan.edu.cn/CellGO).

ATAC-seq/ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag

ATAC-seq, CUT&Tag and ChIP-seq datasets were analyzed according to the published protocol with minor modifications [29]. Quality control was conducted as aforementioned and aligned to hg38 with Bowtie2 (version 2.4.1) in default settings. The data were further binarized, sorted, and indexed with SAMtools (version 1.6), and bigwig files were generated by the “bamCoverage” function of Deeptools (version 3.5.1) (--binSize 10 --normalizeUsing CPM --effectiveGenomeSize 2913022398 --ignoreForNormalization chrX chrM). Narrow peaks of ATAC-seq, CUT&Tag and H3K4me3 ChIP-seq were called with MACS2 (version 2.2.7.1) (ATAC-seq: --shift − 100 --extsize 200 –nomodel; ChIP-seq: default settings). Broad peaks of H3K27me3 ChIP-seq were called with SICER (version 2.0) in default settings. Differential analysis was conducted by the R package “DiffBind” (version 3.4.11). Genomic annotation of peaks was conducted with the R package “ChIPseeker” (version 1.30.3) and “clusterProfiler” (version 4.2.2), where the definition of promoters was consistent as aforementioned. Profiles and heatmaps were generated with “computeMatrix scale-regions” (-a 500 -b 500 --skipZeros) and “plotHeatmap” (for iPSC-ESC, --yMin 0 –yMax 0.6; for iPSC-NPC, --yMin 0 –yMax 2.0) functions of Deeptools (version 3.5.1). Motif analysis was conducted by “findMotifsGenome.pl” function of Homer (version 4.11.1), with all WT peaks in respective datasets as background (-bg). Bedtools (version 2.30.0) (-wa -wb -e -f 0.5 -F 0.5) was used to intersect the differing H3K27me3 and H3K4me3 peaks with the genome-wide promoter (TSS ± 3Kb). Classification of histone modification states and enrichment analysis on regions of interest were performed by ChromHMM. State identification was primarily based on two previously published articles [33, 34].

Hi-C

Hi-C data were analyzed as previously described [35]. Briefly, quality control was conducted as aforementioned. Restriction sites of MboI on hg38 were generated with “hiccup_digester” function of HiCUP (version 0.8.0). High-quality clean reads were aligned to hg38 with Bowtie2 program (version 2.4.1) in HiCUP (max di-tag length: 700, min di-tag length: 50). “.ValidPairs” were generated with the “mapped_2hic_fragments.py” script of HiC-Pro (version 2.11.4) in default settings. “.allValidPairs” were merged with “.ValidPairs”, sorted, deduplicated, and down-sampled to 575.717 million for downstream comparison. “.hic” files were generated with the “hicpro2juicebox.sh” utility of HiC-Pro, and the data were normalized by “juicer_tools_1.22.01.jar” of JuicerTools (version 1.22.01). Contact maps were generated with Juicebox (version 1.11.08). Raw and ICE-normalized matrices (iced_matrix) were built with HiC-Pro in default settings (bin_size: 10000 20000 40000 100000). Iced_matrices were converted into “.h5” and “.cool” files with “hicConvertFormat” tool of HiCExplorer (version 3.7.1). Significant contacts were called in bin_size of 10 Kb by Fit-Hi-C (version 2.0.8) with parameters: -U 10,000,000 -L 50,000 -r 10,000 -p 2 -v.

Protein structure modeling

AlphaFold2 was utilized [36] to predict the wild-type structure of ADNP. For p.C687R variant, OPUS-Mut was employed [37] to model the impact of the p.C687R variant. Briefly, OPUS-Mut reconstructs the side chains of the wild-type ADNP structure, and then rebuild the side chains of the mutant p.C687R structure by substituting the cysteine (C) at position 687 with arginine (R). The side-chain perturbations induced by this variant were quantified, with residues exhibiting substantial perturbations deemed affected. The overall average predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score for the predicted structure is 0.56, while the domain of interest (residues 635–695) shows a higher average pLDDT score of 0.77. The variant is considered to impact a particular function if affected residues are associated with functional sites.

Results

Characterization of ADNP variants with a focus on missense variants

To systematically investigate the distribution and potential clinical relevance of ADNP variants, we compiled and analyzed both previously published and newly collected cases. Previously reported cohorts included 78 cases from Belgium [14], 22 from Canada [15], 22 from the United States [16], 1 from Italy [38] and 15 from our earlier study [17], totaling 138 cases with confirmed ADNP variants and clinical diagnoses. These cases were classified as the ‘Clinical’ group. Additionally, we newly collected 74 cases identified through peripheral blood exome sequencing in China (42 males and 32 females), and retrieved 802 ADNP variant entries from the NCBI ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/, updated as of June 23, 2025). Since these 876 cases lacked confirmed clinical diagnoses, we classified them as the ‘Non-Clinical’ group.

We first analyzed the variant types across both groups (Fig. 1a). Among the 138 ‘Clinical’ cases, the majority of variants were nonsense (N = 72, 52.2%) or frameshift (N = 60, 43.5%), consistent with the known LOF mechanism of ADNP pathogenesis. Only five ADNP missense variants were identified in this group, which are: p.M1V (c.1A > G), a start-loss variant that may impair translation initiation and potentially lead to nonsense-mediated decay (NMD); p.Q67H (c.201G > C), located before the first ZF domain on the N’-end; p.C687R (c.2059T > C), located immediately downstream of ZF domain #9; p.R730G (c.2188 C > G), located in the NLS domain; and p.S802F (c.2405 C > T), located in homeobox domain with predicted stronger DNA binding affinity by in silico analysis [38]. In contrast, in ‘Non-Clinical’ group, missense (N = 492, 56.2%) were the most common, followed by synonymous (N = 164, 18.7%), frameshift (N = 108, 12.3%), and nonsense (N = 44, 5%). We then assessed the predicated pathogenicity of all variants based on the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics. As expected, 97.8% of variants in the ‘Clinical’ group were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP), while only 19.2% of ‘Non-Clinical’ fell into this category. Notably, 45.2% of the ‘Non-Clinical’ variants were categorized as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1a). Focusing specifically on missense variants, all five ‘Clinical Missense’ variants were classified as P/LP. In contrast, among the ‘Non-Clinical Missense’ variants, 69.7% were VUS, 18.9% were benign or likely benign (B/LB), and only 1.6% were P/LP (Fig. 1b). These findings reveal a major gap in interpreting ADNP missense variants, as most remain of uncertain significance in undiagnosed cases.

Prior studies have found that the distribution of ADNP variants correlates with genome-wide DNA methylation patterns [15]. As shown in Fig. 1c, variants located within the c.2000–2340 region (corresponding to p.667–780, which includes the NLS and harbors several high-frequency variants) are associated with global hypermethylation and are classified as ‘epi-ADNP-2’. In contrast, variants outside this region exhibit global hypomethylation and are classified as ‘epi-ADNP-1’ [15]. Among the 137 ‘Clinical’ cases, 54% (N = 74) fell into the ‘epi-ADNP-1’ group and 46% (N = 63) to the ‘epi-ADNP-2’ group. In ‘Non-Clinical’ group, the vast majority (N = 561, 85%) fell into the ‘epi-ADNP-1’ group, while only 15% (N = 101) were located in epi-ADNP-2 (Fig. 1c, Additional file 1: Fig. S1b).

To date, two pathogenic missense variants were reported in coding sequence (CDS) of ADNP, p.Q362E and p.S802F, which were associated with mild developmental delay and HVDAS, respectively [26, 38]. In our earlier work, we reported two CDS missense variants, including a de novo variant, p.C687R, located just one amino acid downstream of the #9 ZF domain. The patient carrying this variant exhibited core clinical features consistent with the diagnosis of HVDAS, including global developmental delay in early childhood, subsequent intellectual disability, autistic traits, distinctive facial dysmorphism, attention deficits, sleep disturbances, hypotonia, and multisystem involvement affecting the skeletal and urinary systems. She showed short stature before 5 years of age, followed by catch-up growth. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated mild ventricular enlargement and minimal mastoid T2 hyperintensity, while electroencephalogram (EEG) findings were unremarkable (Table 1, see Additional file 3 for detailed case report). In this study, we focused on p.C687R to investigate its pathogenicity. Protein 3D structure prediction revealed that the mutant p.C687R adopts a more compact conformation compared to the wild-type hADNP (Additional file 1: Fig. S1c). Notably, in the 601–700 aa region encompassing #8 and #9 ZF domains, four residues were predicted to be structurally affected by the p.C687R substitution. Among them, R688 and M675 showed the highest predicted impact, likely contributing to alterations in the α-helical structure of #9 ZF domain (Fig. 1d). Based on these predictive results, we hypothesized that p.C687R may perturb its ZF conformation and alter genomic binding profile.

p.C687R alters nuclear puncta formation in HEK293T cells

ADNP is predominantly localized in the nucleus, and previous studies, including our own, have shown that pathogenic mutants can alter its nuclear localization [17, 19], potentially contributing to disease. To investigate this, we examined the nuclear localization of p.C687R in HEK293T cells and assessed its effect on wild-type hADNP under co-expression, modeling the heterozygous state in patients. Cells were transfected with hADNP, p.C687R, or both (Fig. 2, Additional file 1: Fig. S2). In all conditions, both proteins localized to euchromatic nuclear regions, consistent with prior reports [39]. However, their intranuclear distribution differed: wild-type hADNP formed numerous small puncta, whereas p.C687R was largely diffusely distributed in the nucleus and lacked puncta, and when present, puncta were larger (Fig. 2a). Co-expression led to strong co-localization and normalized puncta characteristics, including the proportion of cells with puncta (Fig. 2b), puncta number per cell (Fig. 2c-d), and size (Fig. 2e). Notably, in large puncta, hADNP signals concentrated at the periphery, while p.C687R signals were more uniformly distributed (Fig. 2a).

Quantitative analysis confirmed these observations. Only 20% of hADNP-transfected cells lacked puncta, compared to nearly 60% of p.C687R cells; co-expression reduced this close to 40% (Fig. 2b). Violin plots showed that p.C687R significantly reduced the average puncta number, partially rescued by co-expression (Fig. 2c). Puncta number per cell was typically 5–25 for hADNP, 1–10 for p.C687R, and intermediate for co-transfected cells (Fig. 2d). Using 10 μm as a cutoff, puncta in hADNP cells were predominantly small (< 10 μm) with very few large ones; whereas p.C687R cells showed a marked increase in large puncta (> 10 μm); co-transfection restored the size distribution toward wild-type levels (Fig. 2e).

Given the high conservation of ADNP between human and mouse, we repeated these experiments with mouse homologs mADNP and p.C686R in HEK293T cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S3a). While co-localization intensity (Additional file 1: Fig. S3b) and puncta width differences (Additional file 1: Fig. S3f) were less pronounced than in human ADNP, puncta number and distribution patterns (Additional file 1: Fig. S3c-e) were consistent with human findings.

p.C687R impairs neuronal migration and differentiation in vivo

To investigate the impact of p.C687R on neuronal development in vivo, we performed IUE at embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5), a critical stage of cortical neurogenesis when neurons are actively generated and migrate toward the cortical plate. Plasmids expressing either wild-type hADNP or p.C687R were co-electroporated with a GFP reporter into the lateral ventricles of CD-1 mouse embryos, and brains were examined at E18.5 by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3a). In embryos expressing wild-type hADNP, most GFP⁺ neurons successfully migrated into the cortical plate. In contrast, a significant fraction of p.C687R⁺ neurons remained abnormally within the deep layer, indicating impaired radial migration (Fig. 3a). Among neurons that reached the cortical plate, hADNP⁺ neurons exhibited long, slender axons typical of differentiating cortical pyramidal neurons, whereas p.C687R⁺ neurons displayed shortened or absent axonal projections (Fig. 3).

Altered chromatin binding profile of p.C687R in HEK293T cells

As shown in Fig. 1d, protein structure prediction indicates that the p.C687R variant may disrupt the ZF structure of hADNP, which is critical for genomic binding and affect gene regulation. To investigate whether this contributes to the observed cellular phenotypes, we overexpressed wild-type hADNP or p.C687R, tagged with Flag at the C-terminal and HA at the N-terminal, in HEK293T cells, alongside an EGFP control. Western blotting using anti-ADNP revealed low endogenous ADNP expression in untransfected negative control (NC) or EGFP-transfected cells, whereas both hADNP and p.C687R were robustly expressed as full-length proteins, as validated by anti-Flag and anti-HA antibodies (Fig. 4a, Additional file 1: Fig. S4a-b).

Using this system, we performed anti-Flag CUT&Tag to profile genome-wide binding patterns. The clustered correlation heatmap and volcano plots showed clear separation and difference of both hADNP and p.C687R samples from the EGFP control group. Although hADNP and p.C687R clustered closely together, the separation was still evident between the two (Additional file 1: Fig. S5a, S5b). Besides the consensus sites (15,913), peak overlapping analysis further identified unique binding sites for each protein, with 10,584 sites specific to hADNP (“hADNP_only”) and 4,360 sites specific to p.C687R (“p.C687R_only”), demonstrating distinct binding profiles between WT and mutant proteins (Fig. 4b). Genomic annotation showed that only 21.5% of p.C687R_only peaks were located at promoters (Fig. 4c; Additional file 2: Table S1), suggesting these sites act predominantly through distal regulatory mechanisms.

To characterize chromatin context, we integrated ChromHMM segmentation [33, 34] with ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data from wild-type HEK293T cells. hADNP_only peaks were enriched at TSS state (state #2), with strong active H3K27ac and ATAC-seq signals, whereas p.C687R_only peaks were enriched at bivalent chromatin (state #6), marked by both repressive H3K9me3 and active H3K27ac signal (Fig. 4d). Notably, CTCF, a factor mediating long-range chromatin loops, was also highly enriched at state #6, further suggesting potential distal regulatory activity of p.C687R_only peaks. Using published Hi-C data from wild-type HEK293T cells [40–42], we identified genes associated with p.C687R_only peaks either directly or via significant chromatin contacts, defining “p.C687R-associated genes” (Fig. 4e; Additional file 2: Table S2).

RNA-seq analysis of the same overexpression system revealed distinct transcriptional profiles among EGFP, hADNP, and p.C687R groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S5c). Both hADNP and p.C687R induced substantial differential gene expression compared to EGFP (Additional file 1: Fig. S5d; Additional file 2: Table S3-4). Among 193 differentially expressed genes overlapping p.C687R-associated genes, GO analysis showed significant enrichment for “histone modification” (Fig. 4f), including key regulators such as MTF2, KDM5B, and RNF20 (Fig. 4g), which are associated with H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 modifications. In contrast, 746 hADNP-associated genes were identified using the same strategy, showing enrichment for cell cycle and metabolic processes (Additional file 1: Fig. S5e-f; Additional file 2: Table S5-6).

Integration with the bivalent gene sets showed that both bivGenes_DEG and bivGenes_nonDEG loci resided in open chromatin, with slightly increased accessibility at bivGenes_nonDEG regions in mutant iPSCs (Fig. 5g), which suggests that bivalent chromatin domains in p.C687R cells might be epigenetically primed for activation during later stages of neural differentiation. Representative IGV tracks of NEUROD1 and DLX1, key regulators of neuronal lineages, illustrate this poised bivalent state (Fig. 5h).

Activation of BivGenes in p.C687R NPCs associated with GABAergic lineage programs

The identification of poised bivGenes in p.C687R iPSCs (Fig. 5) prompted us to examine whether these loci become activated during neural differentiation and contribute to altered transcriptional programs. To explore the role of ADNP in neuronal gene regulation, we first generated ADNP-knockout (KO) HEK293T cells using CRISPR/Cas9 (Additional file 1: Fig. S7a–b). RNA-seq revealed distinct clustering between WT and KO cells, with 511 genes upregulated and 399 downregulated (Additional file 1: Fig. S7c–d; Additional file 2: Table S11). Notably, upregulated genes were enriched for synapse-related functions even in this non-neuronal context (Additional file 1: Fig. S7e) and showed preferential expression in GABAergic neurons (Additional file 1: Fig. S7f–g), including increased expression of genes critical for GABAergic development (GAD1, NKX2-1, SLC6A1) (Additional file 1: Fig. S7h). These results indicate that ADNP normally represses GABAergic gene programs.

Guided by these findings, we differentiated iPSCs toward a GABAergic fate using SAG and collected NPCs at day 26 (Fig. 5a). ATAC-seq analysis revealed increased chromatin accessibility in p.C687R NPCs (Additional file 1: Fig. S8a–b; Additional file 2: Table S12), particularly at previously identified bivGene_nonDEG regions (Fig. 6a). Motif analysis of these open regions showed enrichment of NKX transcription factor (TF) family, especially NKX2-1 (Fig. 6b), a key regulator of GABAergic lineage specification. RNA-seq of NPCs demonstrated clear transcriptional divergence between mutant and control cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S8c–d; Additional file 2: Table S13). Upregulated genes were enriched in neurodevelopmental, synaptic, and axonal pathways, including GABAergic synapses (Additional file 1: Fig. S8e). Discussion

Our study revisits the pathogenic spectrum of ADNP variants and identifies the de novo missense variant p.C687R as a functionally disruptive allele predicted to alter the ninth ZF domain. In vitro, this variant affects the nuclear localization of ADNP and disrupts the characteristic puncta pattern of the wild-type protein, while in vivo, it leads to impaired cortical migration and abnormal neuronal branching. Genomic profiling further revealed mutant-specific chromatin binding and dysregulation of histone modification–related genes. In patient-derived iPSCs, the variant induced aberrant bivalent chromatin states that prime GABAergic lineage genes for activation, providing mechanistic insight into its pathogenic contribution to HVDAS.

Functional and mechanistic insights into the p.C687R variant

In our current study, we provide multiple lines of evidence indicating that the ADNP missense variant p.C687R disrupts neuronal development through a GOF-like effect in experimental systems. Existing literature has primarily attributed the pathogenesis of HVDAS to dosage insufficiency or a LOF mechanism. Heterozygous microdeletion encompassing ADNP consistently results in HVDAS phenotypes [43]. Most pathogenic variants identified to date are protein-truncating variants that lead to the loss of critical C-terminal domains, including NLS, homeobox domain, and HP1-binding regions, all essential for the nuclear localization and chromatin regulatory functions of ADNP [12, 19]. Truncated mutant proteins are typically undetectable in the patient tissues carrying common variants such as p.Y719* [12, 44], suggesting rapid degradation or failed translation. Some missense variants have also been linked to aberrant splicing, generating truncated products and further reinforcing the LOF model [45]. Additionally, missense variants within the NAP peptide domain have been proposed to impair the neuroprotective function of ADNP, again consistent with LOF effects [26]. Despite the dominance of the LOF hypothesis, there is also evidence supporting gain-of-toxic-function effects in ADNP variants. For example, compared with the haploinsufficiency model, a mouse paralog p.Y718* heterozygote displays more severe and additional deficits [46]. Moreover, a recent study reported a novel ADNP missense variant p.S802F in the homeobox domain, which was predicted through in silico analyses to exhibit increased DNA-binding affinity, supporting a GOF-like effect [38].

In this study, we provide converging experimental evidence that p.C687R exhibits GOF-like effects in experimental systems. Structural modeling suggests that p.C687R perturbs the last ZF domain, critical for chromatin binding specificity. Consistent with this, chromatin binding assays show that the mutant protein associates with a distinct set of genomic loci, and transcriptomic analyses reveal dysregulation of genes involved in histone modification. Functional studies further demonstrate the pathogenic impact of this variant. In HEK293T cells, overexpression of p.C687R alters the subnuclear localization of the mutant protein and disrupts the distribution of co-expressed wild-type ADNP. In vivo, expression of the mutant protein leads to aberrant neuronal migration and disorganized branching during neurodevelopment. A similar phenotype has been reported, with increased neurite numbers observed in the mouse paralog p.P403*-expressing differentiated neuroblastoma-like neuronal cells [20]. Additionally, overexpression of wild-type ADNP may also be detrimental, as it enhances Wnt signaling in a dose-dependent manner in HEK293T cells [22], suggesting that precise ADNP dosage is critical and may complicate future gene therapy.

It is also worth noting that although p.C687R exhibits GOF–like molecular effects in our experimental systems, the clinical presentation of this patient is consistent with the broader ADNP-related neurodevelopmental phenotype, which has largely been associated with LOF variants. A similar pattern has been reported for the recently described p.S802F variant, which was predicted to alter DNA-binding properties yet presents with classical HVDAS features [38]. These observations suggest that distinct molecular mechanisms, whether involving altered binding specificity or reduced canonical activity, may converge on shared developmental pathways and ultimately produce similar clinical manifestations. Such phenotypic convergence despite differing molecular effects has also been described in other genetic disorders, including PTPN11- and SCN1A-related conditions [47, 48]. Together, these findings underscore the complexity of genotype–phenotype relationships in ADNP-associated disorders.

Due to the lack of anti-ADNP antibodies that can distinguish between wild-type and mutant proteins with single amino acid substitutions, it remains technically difficult to directly demonstrate mutant-specific chromatin binding in patient-derived cells. We hypothesize that, owing to altered binding specificity, p.C687R preferentially targets a distinct set of genomic loci enriched with bivalent histone marks in ESCs. Rather than inducing transcriptional repression, ESCs appear to compensate by increasing both H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 levels at these sites, likely because the associated developmental genes are functionally essential. This compensatory response establishes a new epigenetic equilibrium that maintains these loci in a transcriptionally silent in ESCs. Upon differentiation, however, these loci with elevated bivalent signatures become more open and transcriptionally active, such as GABAergic genes. Importantly, whether these effects in patient-derived cells fully reflect GOF-like activity remains uncertain. Additional evidences are required to validate such GOF-like mechanisms in vivo. For example, a knock-in mouse model carrying the p.C687R variant would be a critical next step. A homozygous model, if viable, could provide a definitive system to dissect the functional impact of the p.C687R relative to wild-type ADNP or other known LOF variants, offering important mechanistic insights.

Epigenetic mechanisms underlying p.C687R–mediated neuronal dysregulation

At the molecular level, our findings indicate that the p.C687R variant disrupts neuronal differentiation by perturbing chromatin regulation. ADNP is a central chromatin regulator, influencing both transcriptionally active and repressive regions through histone modifications. In mouse models, ADNP haploinsufficiency reduces H3K79me2 [49], a mark associated with transcription elongation. A relationship between ADNP and H3K9me3 has been implied. ADNP is recruited to H3K9me3-enriched pericentromeric heterochromatin by interacting with HP1 and represses the satellite repeats [50]. In addition, a mouse model harboring ADNP-C-terminal-variant exhibits a decrease in H3K9me2/3, which can be expanded by inhibiting the histone demethylase LSD1, hinting at a regulatory circuit between ADNP and H3K9 methylation [51]. In our overexpression system, p.C687R exhibited a distinct chromatin-binding profile, enriched at loci marked by both H3K9me3 and H3K27ac, and specifically altered the expression of histone-modifying genes. This demonstrates that even a single amino acid change can disrupt ADNP-mediated epigenetic regulation, reshaping chromatin accessibility and transcriptional programs.

Other than affecting histone modifications, ADNP also acts in the form of chromatin remodeling complexes by interacting with other proteins, of which the two most central proteins are HP1 and BRG1. CHAHP complex, composed by ADNP, HP1 and CHD4, participates in the establishment of local inaccessibility in euchromatin and restricts the expression of lineage-specifying genes [39]. Moreover, ADNP interacts with some core subunits of the chromatin remodeling SWI/SNF complex, such as BRG1, which utilizes ATP to mediate nucleosome configuration and render DNA accessibility to enhancer or promoter [52]. In addition, ADNP, BRG1 and CHD4 also form a complex, which regulates the primitive endoderm (PrE) genes during the early development of mouse embryos. Intriguingly, the repressive effect of ADNP-BRG1-CHD4 complex involves the balance of bivalent histone marks, H3K27me3 and H3K4me3, which is a special mechanism in developmental genes of embryonic stem cells (ESCs), assuring the rapid priming during differentiation. The H3K4me3/H3K27me3 ratio increased in promoters of PrE genes upon disruption of the ADNP-BRG1-CHD4 complex, which results in the abnormal up-regulation [53]. Consistent with these mechanisms, in our current study, patient-derived iPSCs carrying p.C687R showed altered bivalent marks at promoters of neurodevelopmental genes. A subset of these genes became transcriptionally activated during differentiation into GABAergic progenitors, indicating that the mutant releases normally repressed developmental programs and biases neuronal lineage specification.

Role of ADNP in GABAergic neuronal differentiation

While earlier studies have primarily focused on the role of ADNP in excitatory neurons, our findings significantly expand this view by demonstrating its critical involvement in GABAergic neuronal development and function. Conditional deletion of Adnp in the dorsal telencephalon during embryogenesis results in a substantial loss of upper-layer cortical neurons [7], highlighting its essential role in excitatory neuronal differentiation. In models of ADNP haploinsufficiency, impaired excitatory synaptic architecture and transmission have been consistently observed, including altered expression and activity of synaptic markers such as VGLUT1 [54] and CaMKIIα [9]. However, accumulating evidence now indicates that ADNP is also indispensable for the development and functional integrity of inhibitory neuronal circuits. Differential expression of GABAergic genes, including Gad1, Gad2, and Pvalb, was observed upon ADNP haploinsufficiency in a brain region- and sex-dependent manner [55, 56]. Mouse model harboring C-terminal truncating variants in Adnp exhibit deficits in both GABAergic and glutamatergic neurotransmission [51]. Notably, recent work using human iPSC-derived neurons carrying the ADNP p.719* variant revealed disrupted GABAergic lineage specification and impaired neuronal maturation [57]. In our study, we provide direct mechanistic evidence supporting the role of ADNP in GABAergic neuronal differentiation. Transcriptomic profiling of ADNP-knockout 293T cells revealed upregulation of genes critical for GABAergic identity, including NKX2-1 and GAD1. Similar patterns were observed in patient-derived iPSCs with the p.C687R variant, as sites enriched NKX2-1 binding motifs abnormally gained accessibility upon variant. Integrated chromatin accessibility and transcriptomic analyses, we identified dysregulated gene networks involved in GABAergic neuron differentiation and synaptogenesis, and immunofluorescent staining further confirmed the transition of cellular state. Our findings in iPSCs strengthen the role of ADNP in specifying GABAergic fate.

In parallel with these findings on neuronal development, previous studies have demonstrated that ADNP exerts neuroprotective effects, particularly through its active peptide NAP, which counteracts glutamate-induced excitotoxicity both in vitro [10] and in vivo [58]. ADNP expression is downregulated in response to NMDA-induced excitotoxicity [59], suggesting a feedback mechanism between glutamatergic signaling and ADNP regulation. Importantly, preclinical studies have shown that low-dose ketamine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, increases Adnp expression levels [60]. Building on these findings, a small clinical trial explored ketamine as a potential therapeutic strategy for HVDAS. In this study, EEG recordings revealed increased 40-Hz gamma-band inter-trial coherence (ITC) and decreased 20-Hz beta-band ITC following ketamine administration [61]. These neurophysiological signatures are likely associated with enhanced glutamatergic signaling and GABAergic modulation. However, contradictory findings have been reported regarding ketamine-induced ADNP expression, and ketamine has also been associated with potential toxic effects [60, 62, 63]. Interestingly, both NAP and ketamine were predicted to directly interact with the zinc finger domains of ADNP via in silico docking analyses.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, overexpression experiments in HEK293T cells revealed GOF-like effects for p.C687R. However, in the heterozygous patient-derived context, the mutation may exert combined loss- and gain-of-function effects, and direct comparison to loss-of-function perturbations within the same cellular context was not performed. In vivo validation, such as a knock-in mouse model carrying p.C687R, will be important to confirm these findings and assess their physiological relevance for neuronal development and behavior. Second, all functional data derive from a single patient and complementary experimental models, so the observed effects may reflect allele-specific mechanism rather than broadly generalizable features of ADNP missense variants. Whether other ADNP missense variants converge on shared or distinct pathways remains to be determined. Third, all patient-derived experiments rely on a single individual, with only one iPSC clone and a single healthy control, which may limit generalizability. The control was broadly age-matched but not sex-matched, and conclusions from this 1-to-1 comparison should be interpreted with caution. While iPSC sequencing-based assays included multiple replicates, these were derived from a single clone with independent differentiations. Validation in patient-derived iPSCs with additional genetic backgrounds or engineered alleles, along with sex-matched controls, will be needed in future studies.

Conclusions

We integrated the largest ADNP variants cohort in China with recorded variants from the NCBI ClinVar database, revealing a major gap in interpreting ADNP missense variants, as most remain of uncertain significance in undiagnosed cases. We also characterized a de novo missense variant, p.C687R, which disrupts the ninth ZF domain and alters bivalent histone modifications on a set of key genes in iPSCs, placing them in a primed state and making them more responsive to differentiation signals. Upon differentiation into NPCs, GABAergic genes become abnormally activated, potentially affecting GABAergic lineage specification. These early alterations in cell fate may contribute to the neurodevelopmental phenotypes observed in patients.

Our findings introduce a new pathogenic mechanism for ADNP-related disorders. Our findings suggest a GOF-like effect of p.C687R, providing new insight into the chromatin regulatory roles of ADNP and complements the existing loss-of-function framework. Future studies using in vivo models and multi-omic approaches will be essential to determine how GOF-like and LOF variants converge on shared epigenetic pathways and how these alterations disrupt neuronal differentiation and E/I balance. Elucidating these mechanisms will provide critical insights for developing personalized therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring proper ADNP–chromatin regulation and neural circuit homeostasis in HVDAS.
