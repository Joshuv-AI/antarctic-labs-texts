Outcome of multiagency investigation for gonorrhoea and chlamydia in prepubertal children: a retrospective review
Authors: Rebecca Corry, Scott Beard, Faiza Thompson, Patrick Kelly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo review the process and outcomes of multiagency investigation of gonorrhoea and chlamydia in prepubertal children.DesignRetrospective review of cases presenting to a hospital multidisciplinary child protection team (CPT).SettingA multiagency centre adjacent to Starship Children’s Hospital, in the city of Tāmaki Makaurau Auckland, Aotearoa New Zealand. The hospital CPT is colocated in this centre with statutory child protection services (CPS), police and forensic interviewers.ParticipantsAll prepubertal children seen with gonorrhoea or chlamydia from 2009 to 2020.ResultsThere were 16 children aged 1 to 9 years: 10 with Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG), 2 with NG and Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) and 4 with CT. Ten presented with vaginal discharge, and four with conjunctivitis. Aside from their infection, 14 had normal examinations. CPT practice varied with respect to history taking, medical tests and follow-up. Urine screening identified at least one positive household member in 14 children. Sexual abuse was substantiated by CPS in 2/4 children with conjunctivitis and 11/12 others. Five children disclosed in forensic interviews, contributing to four criminal convictions: three for rape (penile penetration of the genitalia) and one for indecent assault. None of these four children had anogenital injuries.ConclusionsPrompt, thorough and coordinated multiagency investigation of prepubertal NG and CT infection (including household screening and expert interviewing of children) will often identify the source of infection and almost always establish sexual transmission. However, even in a tertiary centre, cases are rare, leading to variability in practice. Conjunctivitis caused by NG or CT has a high risk of being sexually transmitted, so should be investigated with the same rigour as anogenital infection. Findings in this cohort, where penetrative sexual offences were confirmed by criminal convictions, support the international consensus that penetrative sexual abuse of children seldom causes anogenital injury

Introduction

Outside the perinatal period, infections by Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) or Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) in prepubertal children are rare. In a prospective national study through the British Paediatric Surveillance Unit from 2010 to 2012, 13 cases were identified in children<13 years old, suggesting an incidence of<0.075 cases per 105 children per year.1

International guidelines state that (apart from perinatal transmission) genital, rectal or pharyngeal infection with NG or CT in children is indicative of sexual contact, just as in adults.2 3 This almost always means that the child has been the victim of sexual abuse.4 The significance of conjunctival infection is less certain. The British surveillance study unexpectedly identified five cases, remarking that ‘evidence on whether ophthalmic gonococcal infection is a marker of sexual abuse would be useful’.1 Cases of both sexual and non-sexual transmission of NG and CT conjunctivitis have been reported,58 but reaching firm conclusions is complicated by the fact that non-disclosure, incomplete or delayed disclosure are common in sexually abused children, and some are too young to disclose.911 A thorough multidisciplinary and multiagency assessment for possible abuse is, therefore indicated.12

In the 1990s, cases of prepubertal NG or CT infection were often poorly managed in New Zealand due to problems in interagency coordination and an inability to identify the source of infection, as described in an article published in 2002.13 That year, a multiagency centre was established adjacent to the National Children’s Hospital (‘Starship’) in Auckland, the largest city. Here, the hospital multidisciplinary child protection team (CPT) is colocated with police, statutory child protective services (CPS) and specialist child witness interviewers.14 Also in 2002, an interagency guideline for sexually transmissible infections in prepubertal children was introduced. The guideline recommends urgent confirmation and treatment of infection; a multiagency case conference within 24 hours; specialist interviewing of the child; early screening of household members for infection and coordinated action to assess and support the family and ensure child safety (online supplemental appendix A). Following this guideline, a probable source of infection was identified in 9/10 children seen from 2002 to 2008. A detailed description of that cohort was published in 201115 and was found useful for the 2015 Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health (RCPCH) guidelines on the significance of NG infection in prepubertal children.16 17

In 2014, the CPT was approached by the authors of the RCPCH guideline seeking more information about types of sexual contact associated with transmission of infection. We also encountered cases of NG and CT conjunctivitis while continuing to wrestle with the demands that our approach places on families and the CPT. The enquiry from the RCPCH and our growing experience led to the objectives of this study: to review the process and outcomes of CPT and multiagency practice in children seen since 2009; to describe our experience of prepubertal NG and CT conjunctivitis and to provide more information concerning types of sexual contact confirmed in criminal court proceedings (Level 1 evidence in the RCPCH guidelines)16 and which transmitted NG and CT infection to prepubertal children.

Methods

Study design

Retrospective review of cases of prepubertal NG and CT infection seen by the CPT.

Setting

This study was conducted in the city of Auckland (population 1.64 million), New Zealand.

Participants

All prepubertal children seen by the CPT and found to have NG or CT infection from 1 January 2009 to 31 December 2020.

The CPT sees children with possible NG or CT infection urgently: ideally that day, including after hours.13 An expert paediatrician speaks with the caregivers and examines the child following international guidelines.3 Multisite cultures and/or nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for NG, CT and Trichomonas vaginalis (Strand Displacement Amplification), plus syphilis, Hepatitis B and HIV serology, are taken under chain of evidence. Positive NAATs are confirmed by a second NAAT targeting a different part of the genome and/or culture.15

The paediatrician explains the infection and rationale for household screening. All residents in the same household(s) as the child in the 3 weeks before symptom onset are identified. Those who consent are screened by urine NAAT. Urine NAAT has been used by the CPT for this purpose since it became widely available, as it is easier to collect and more acceptable to household members than tests requiring intimate procedures. Current adult guidelines state that ‘Urine testing with NAATs is at least as sensitive as testing with endocervical specimens, clinician- or self-collected vaginal specimens or urethral specimens in clinical settings’.18

Children who screen positive are seen by the CPT. Adults who screen positive are referred to a sexual health physician for confirmatory testing and treatment. CPS and the police are informed of screening results.15

Following a multiagency case conference, specialist CPS or police interviewers interview developmentally capable children. CPS support child safety and determine whether abuse is substantiated. Police follow their own processes to determine criminal liability.

If both child and household member(s) are NG culture positive, police may request comparative NG molecular genotyping from the Nosocomial Infections Laboratory of the Institute of Environmental Science and Research (ESR), Wellington, New Zealand.15

Outcome measures

Data concerning history, examination, medical tests and household screening were extracted from health records by a CPT Research Fellow (RC).

Data concerning interviews, safety and whether sexual abuse was substantiated were obtained from CPS records by a CPS social worker (FT)

Data concerning police and court outcomes were obtained from police records by a Police Officer (SB), including NG molecular genotyping. The ESR uses macrorestriction analysis of NG DNA: digestion with rare-cutting restriction endonucleases NheI and SpeI and pulsed field gel electrophoresis. Outcomes are ‘indistinguishable’, ‘closely related’ (a 2–3 band difference, consistent with a difference at one site), ‘possibly related’ or ‘distinct’.15

Patient and public involvement

Patients and public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study .

Results

Between 2009 and 2020, 625 prepubertal children were assessed by the CPT for concerns about possible sexual abuse. Of these, 16 were identified with NG and/or CT infection (table 1). All were either symptomatic or household contacts of a symptomatic index child. There were no other cases, that is, no prepubertal children who presented first to the CPT for a disclosure of sexual abuse were then found on assessment to be positive for NG or CT.

Factors leading to assessment

14 children presented to their General Practitioner (GP) with symptoms: 9 with vaginal discharge, 4 with conjunctivitis and 1 with abdominal pain and urinary frequency. Symptom duration ranged from 2 days to 1 month for those presenting with conjunctivitis, and from 1 day to 5 months for those presenting with vaginal discharge or urinary frequency. In 12/14, the infection was first identified on tests taken by a referring health professional, but two with vaginal discharge who told the GP that they had been ‘touched’ were referred to the CPT for testing. Two (cases 11 and 15) were identified via household screening. One of these was found on examination to have vaginal discharge (case 11), the other (case 15) was asymptomatic. Prior to the onset of symptoms in the index children, sexual abuse was suspected in none of the 16 children. On enquiry, families described (nonspecific) behavioural changes in only three children.

Four children with vaginal discharge or urinary frequency (cases 2, 7, 12 and 14) and all four with conjunctivitis (cases 1, 8, 10 and 16) had received antibiotics before NG or CT infection was identified. All children with non-conjunctival symptoms and three children with conjunctival symptoms were referred after diagnosis of infection with a single organism (NG or CT). One child with conjunctivitis was referred after the diagnosis of infection with both NG and CT (case 10). The two males in the study both presented with conjunctivitis (cases 1 and 10). Both had been admitted for periorbital cellulitis and treated with intravenous second or third generation cephalosporins before the organism was identified.

Assessment, treatment and follow-up by the CPT

All children were seen within 26 hours of referral. CPT records documented enquiry about hygiene practices for nappies in 8/11 children≤4 years old (73%); towels/linen in 10/16 children (63%); toileting in 8/16 (50%); bathing in 8/16 (50%) and sleeping in 6/16 (38%), but lacked details, such as who changed nappies, how hands were washed, who bed shared and whether they slept clothed. In case 1 (a 9-year-old male who presented with conjunctivitis), it was suggested that his eye infection may have been caused by the child wiping his face with a shared hand towel into which an adult male had a habit of ejaculating. No other specific hygiene practice was proposed to explain infection.

Except for inflammation, all hymenal examinations were normal. One child had an anal laceration and perianal bruising. One 8-year-old child with CT infection (case 13) also had genital warts. Although genital warts in children can be acquired non-sexually, in an 8-year-old with concomitant CT infection, it is likely that the warts also were sexually transmitted.3

Vaginal samples were taken in all females. One of the two females with conjunctivitis (NG) had vulvovaginitis on examination and a positive vaginal culture and NAAT for NG (case 8). The other female with conjunctivitis (CT) had a normal vaginal examination and negative vaginal NAAT for CT and GC (case 16) but had already been treated with azithromycin in the community. Vaginal samples were positive in 9/10 children with vaginal symptoms, but negative in one child pretreated with amoxicillin and clavulanate (case 2). They were also negative in one child who presented with urinary symptoms (case 14).

Urine samples were taken in 13/16 children. They were positive in one of three children with conjunctivitis tested (case 8, female), and in 9/10 children without conjunctivitis tested.

Throat samples were taken in 12/16 children. Asymptomatic throat infection was found in two of three children with conjunctivitis tested (cases 8 and 10, one male and one female), and in two of the other nine children tested (cases 9 and 11, both female).

Rectal samples were taken in 13/16 children. Asymptomatic rectal infection was found in one of three children with conjunctivitis tested (case 10, male), and in two of the other ten children tested (cases 5 and 11, both female).

Overall, therefore, multisite infection was found in 9/11 children who presented with symptoms away from the eye (cases 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 12 and 13) and in two of four children who presented with infection involving the eye (cases 8 and 10). Of the two children with eye infections where multisite infection was not found (cases 1 and 16), both had been pretreated with antibiotics that might eradicate their infections, and one (case 16) did not have multisite testing, despite a younger sibling with a positive vaginal NAAT (case 15).

HIV, hepatitis B and syphilis serology were checked acutely in ten cases (all negative).

NG infection was treated with IV Ceftriaxone and CT infection was treated with Azithromycin or Erythromycin.2

CPT follow-up occurred in 14 cases, for up to 1 year (median 3 months). At follow-up, all were asymptomatic. There was a NAAT test of cure (urine or vaginal) in 9/12 cases of urogenital infection, 8–30 days after treatment (median 15). The timing of repeat samples was determined by when it was possible for caregivers to bring children back for review, rather than by guidelines for optimal timing of test of cure. Repeat serology for HIV, hepatitis B and syphilis was performed in only one child. In two cases where a GP was asked to perform a test of cure, it did not occur. Two children in non-kin care had no medical follow-up.

Three children had NG-positive follow-up urine NAAT (10, 17 and 30 days). Two were not re-examined or retested, as the result was thought to indicate residual DNA from non-viable gonococci.19 The child who tested positive at 30 days was re-examined. Repeat four-site testing was normal. Negative repeat urine with a different NAAT confirmed a false positive.

No child in this study has since represented with sexually transmitted infection.20

Household screening and comparative molecular genotyping

Urine was obtained from 196/222 contacts (88%) (table 2). Most (60%) were adults. 96% were screened within 72 hours. 23 (12%) screened positive, providing a positive household member in 14 cases. In cases 10 and 11, the same household member (a convicted sex offender) refused all tests. One positive household member refused confirmatory tests.

Case 10, a 3-year-old boy, presented with NG and CT conjunctivitis. After referral to the CPT, his 1-year-old sister (case 11) was brought for assessment. Not only was she positive for both CT and NG, but she was the only child in the study with diagnostic signs of anogenital trauma.

Outcome of statutory investigation

Case conferences took place for 14 children: ten within 24 hours and four within 72 hours (Friday or weekend referrals). Cases 15 and 16 were referred to CPS, but there was no case conference or police investigation as the paediatrician felt unable to exclude vertical transmission. Five children (three of whom had already made a disclosure to a GP) were interviewed forensically by expert interviewers. All five described their experience at the forensic interview, providing a legally robust recording of their evidence which could be played in criminal court (table 3).

Charges were laid in 1 of 4 children with conjunctivitis (case 8, a female aged 4 years), and 3 of 12 children who presented with infection elsewhere (cases 3, 4 and 12, all female). In three children (all of whom presented with vaginal discharge), the charge was rape (‘non-consensual penetration of the genitalia by the offender’s penis’). In the child with conjunctivitis, the charges were for one episode of confessed digital vaginal penetration (charged as unlawful sexual connection, which is ‘any form of non-consensual penetration or oral connection with a person’s genitalia or anus’) and two episodes of indecent assault (‘assault was intentional; the circumstances accompanying the assault were indecent and the conduct was what a ‘reasonable person’ would deem indecent’). All resulted in conviction.

Where no charges were laid, in two cases, the source of infection was unknown. In eight cases, there was a police ‘person of interest’ but insufficient evidence.

All children had CPS safety plans. 12 remained with primary caregivers. Two went to extended family and two siblings to non-kin care. Sexual abuse was substantiated by statutory CPSs (Level 1 evidence in the 2015 RCPCH guidelines) in 13 children, including 2 of the 4 children who presented with conjunctivitis.

Long-term, 14 children remained with primary caregivers. Two were permanently placed with extended family (their mother remained with a convicted sex offender). In one child, the ‘person of interest’ was a brother (disclosure, positive screen). Years later, she also disclosed ongoing sexual abuse from one of the adults in whose care she had remained.

Discussion

This study provides further evidence that, apart from perinatal transmission, prepubertal NG or CT infections (including conjunctivitis) almost always represent sexual abuse. Of 14 children reviewed at an interagency case conference, all but 1 were substantiated as child sexual abuse, and 4/14 (29%) resulted in criminal convictions. This study also confirms that penetrative sexual abuse in prepubertal children seldom causes anogenital injury. Only one child (a 1 year old) had anogenital bruises or lacerations, and in all cases, where sexual abuse involving penile penetration of the genitalia was confirmed in criminal court (Level 1 evidence in the RCPCH guideline), there were no signs of injury on anogenital examination.

Few studies report household screening of the extent described here.152123 The rationale for completing this urgently includes the possibility of other infected children who may be at risk, the possibility that infection in a source may resolve (spontaneously or through treatment) and our experience that household members wish to know their results as soon as possible.13 15 However, this is very demanding for all involved and requires careful consideration of issues of ethics, privacy and informed consent. There are also multiple scientific unknowns, which mean that the results must be interpreted with caution.

First, the choice of 3 weeks before symptom onset as the focus for screening is, necessarily, arbitrary. The incubation period of postnatally acquired NG and CT in children is poorly defined16 because of the difficulty determining when transmission occurred. For NG in children, incubation periods of up to 3 weeks have been reported.24 25 Adult incubation periods for CT may be up to 3 weeks, but perinatally acquired CT may present much later than that.16 Despite the unknowns, in light of the fact that we have now identified likely sources of infection in 23/26 children in two studies, the choice of 3 weeks seems not unreasonable.

Second, the person who was the source of infection may be negative by the time screening occurs, through treatment or spontaneous recovery. If still positive, their molecular genotype may not be identical with the child’s because NG has a high rate of natural mutation over time. Conversely, someone who was not the source may be positive with an identical genotype simply because it is prevalent in that community.2628

Third, finding a positive household member, even if they were the source of infection, does not establish how transmission occurred.28

Despite these caveats, our experience is that screening often identifies potential sources of infection, thus contributing to decisions about safety and (sometimes) prosecution. The rate of successful prosecution was higher than usual for sexual assault in New Zealand (11%),29 but it must be emphasised that screening is only one factor contributing to decisions to prosecute, which are made by the police in consultation with the Crown Prosecutor and are independent of both CPS and the hospital CPT. Similarly, although comparative molecular genotyping was requested by the police in seven cases, only three of these were prosecuted, and the fourth prosecution involved no molecular genotyping. All prosecutions involved a confession.

One important benefit of a multiagency case conference within 24 hours of infection being confirmed is that it facilitates well-informed and speedy arrangements for forensic interviews of children who have the necessary developmental capacity. The evidence obtained in those interviews was central to the ability of the police to lay charges.

In three cases, non-sexual transmission could not be excluded. In case 1, ejaculate on a hand towel might conceivably transfer NG onto ocular mucosa. But while fomite transmission occurs in laboratory accidents30 and epidemic vector spread may occur (possibly a distinct genotype),31 both are rare. Even in sexually active adults, NG conjunctivitis is rare.32 In children, while fomite transmission is sometimes suggested,10 33 34 it is difficult to exclude sexual abuse.7 35 The conclusion in our case may have been more robust if the child had not been treated prior to three-site testing, and if full household screening had occurred (the alleged source screened negative, and many household members were not screened).

In cases 15 and 16, the possibility of vertical transmission was accepted based on a 40-year-old longitudinal study of infants born to CT-positive women,36 where one treated infant became positive again at 28.5 months.37 The authors proposed persistent perinatal infection based on humoral immune responses and consistency with maternal serovars. However, the longest infection in untreated children was 15.3 months, serovars and serology are forensically unreliable,38 and if intrafamilial abuse occurs, the same infection might occur in mother and child. No other studies support such prolonged perinatal infection. In retrospect, the approach to cases 15 and 16 is open to question. The conclusion of vertical transmission may have been more robust if four-site testing, serology and police investigation had all taken place.

Cases 1, 15 and 16 also demonstrate variation in CPT practice. Interagency objectives specified in the guideline were generally achieved consistently and promptly: CPT assessment, case conferencing, screening, action to assess safety and forensic interview. The content of the CPT assessment was less consistent: the history of hygiene practices; the extent of medical tests and screening and the timing and nature of follow-up. Evidence on the optimal timing of test of cure is evolving,39 but our experience suggests that testing at a median of 15 days is too soon.

These cases are complex and uncommon, making consistent practice difficult. Enquiry into hygiene practices must be thorough to identify possible non-sexual transmission and to forestall any fabrications which may later emerge.15 As our data confirm, multisite infection is common, so should always be excluded.40 Although HIV, hepatitis B and syphilis are rare, both acute and follow-up serology is advisable in children with NG or CT infection.2

The most demanding aspect of our practice, and the hardest to achieve, is screening of all household members. In the three cases of possible non-sexual transmission, household screening was incomplete, reflecting the difficulty of pursuing a rigorous diagnostic approach for a possibility that no family wishes to contemplate. However, the danger of making assumptions is shown by the case in our series where sexual abuse continued, even after the child was thought to be safe because she had already identified one offender.9 20

The greatest variability occurred in the aspects of CPT practice not specified in the guideline. Variability is likely to be even greater in smaller centres with fewer cases. The value of checklists in supporting consistent medical practice is well known41; so, we have developed a CPT proforma for these cases that may be helpful to others (online supplemental appendix B).

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include data on a complete consecutive cohort with a rare condition, including data on child protection and legal outcomes collected by coinvestigators from police and CPS. Limitations include the retrospective design (files may not fully document information collected or actions taken), small numbers collected over many years, variation in case management and data from a single tertiary centre. It is also possible that some cases were not referred. However, the CPT has provided 24/7 regional services to metropolitan Auckland for 30 years, and established regional guidelines recommend that cases of paediatric CT or NG are discussed with us. In addition, our experience is that these infections alarm most GPs, who rapidly refer. Taking all these factors together, and given the rarity of these infections, it is unlikely that a significant number of cases were missed.

Conclusions

(1) Our data indicate that prompt, thorough and coordinated multiagency investigation of prepubertal children with NG and CT infection (including household screening and expert interviewing of children) often identifies the source of infection and almost always establishes sexual transmission. However, even in a tertiary centre, cases are rare, leading to variability in practice. Use of a proforma may help to reduce this variability, and clinician anxiety regarding best practice in an area with which many clinicians are likely to be unfamiliar. (2) Conjunctivitis caused by NG or CT has a high risk of being sexually transmitted; so, it should be investigated with the same rigour as anogenital infection. (3) Findings in this cohort, where penetrative sexual offences were confirmed by criminal convictions, support the international consensus that penetrative sexual abuse of children seldom causes anogenital injury.
