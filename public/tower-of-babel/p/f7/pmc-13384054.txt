Sequential multi-site fine-tuning for incremental deployment of large language models for mobility functional status extraction
Authors: Xingyi Liu, Muskan Garg, Eunji Jeon, Heling Jia, Cynthia S Crowson, Jennifer St Sauver, Sandeep R Pagali, Sunghwan Sohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study evaluated sequential multi-site fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs), simulating incremental deployment across institutions for extracting mobility functional status from unstructured clinical notes.Materials and MethodsWe assembled a corpus of 600 clinical notes from 3 institutions, yielding 3810 annotated note sections (1200 training; 2610 testing). Building upon our previously established LLM mobility extraction pipeline, which utilized optimized prompt engineering, we applied parameter-efficient low-rank adaptation (LoRA) fine-tuning. We evaluated sequential multi-site fine-tuning, where the model is trained locally and only the updated LoRA weights are transferred to the next site. We compared this against joint fine-tuning, which requires pooling all multi-site patient data centrally before training. Performance was measured by micro F1-scores for 2 distinct subtasks: mobility extraction and impairment classification.ResultsSequential multi-site fine-tuning yielded comparable results to centralized joint fine-tuning for mobility extraction (F1, 0.861 vs 0.894) and impairment classification (F1, 0.912 vs 0.900). Both sequential and joint fine-tuning outperformed an untuned 70B model. Additionally, single-site LoRA models showed robust cross-site generalization.DiscussionSequential multi-site fine-tuning offers a resource-friendly approach to adapt LLMs to clinical environments. By retaining previously acquired knowledge without severe interference, this strategy allows for local fine-tuning at each institution without the need to pool or share sensitive patient data required for joint fine-tuning.ConclusionSequential multi-site fine-tuning provides a viable solution for the scalable, incremental deployment of LLMs across institutions, showing promising generalizability for clinical information extraction from electronic health record narratives.Keywords: artificial intelligence, large language model, mobility functional status, natural language processing, electronic medical records

Background and significance

Mobility is a critical component of functional status in older adults and those with chronic illnesses. Numerous epidemiological studies have shown that mobility functional status strongly predicts health outcomes and mortality in these populations.1,2 Traditionally, mobility-related functional status has been assessed using standardized questionnaires (eg, patient-reported activity limitations) and performance-based measures (eg, Timed Up & Go test, gait speed assessment3). However, a significant amount of valuable information regarding patient mobility is recorded as unstructured clinical notes rather than structured data, making it challenging to systematically track and analyze.

Early efforts to extract functional status from text relied on rule-based natural language processing (NLP) techniques and keyword matching.4–7 However, rules and keyword lists developed on one institution’s notes often fail to generalize to another’s documentation style, leading to missed detections or false matches. Handling negations, temporal qualifiers, and inferences is difficult to encode with hand-crafted rules.8,9 As a result, traditional approaches were unreliable across different institutions and data sources, especially for complex information like temporal or functional details.10

The advent of large language models (LLMs) has opened new possibilities for capturing mobility-related information from unstructured text. Unlike rule-based systems, LLMs leverage statistical learning from vast corpora, enabling them to interpret clinical language with a high degree of flexibility and context awareness.11,12 Recent LLMs have demonstrated an ability to encode a surprising amount of clinical knowledge and reasoning ability.

While foundation LLMs possess broad language understanding, adapting them to high-stakes medical tasks benefits from fine-tuning on domain-specific data.13–16 Fine-tuning is the process of taking a pretrained LLM and further training it on a custom dataset so that it learns task-specific patterns. This approach has been successful in improving LLM performance on specialized use cases. For instance, Zhang et al. reported that a LLaMA-2 model (70B parameters) fine-tuned on 10 000 examples of hospital electronic health record (EHR) log data significantly outperformed GPT-4 in zero-shot mode for predicting patient discharges.17 Hou et al. proposed a 2-phase domain-specific fine-tuning strategy for LLMs using ICD-10 code descriptions to automate the assignment of medical codes from clinical documentation.18 This domain-specific fine-tuning dramatically improved coding accuracy, boosting exact code-match rates from under 1% to ∼97% in initial tests and achieving ∼69% exact match on real clinical notes.

A key hurdle in healthcare application development is the limited size of labeled datasets for training. In our previous work, we successfully established an LLM pipeline using an optimized “error-informed prompt” strategy to extract mobility functional status from clinical notes.7 While this prompt engineering approach proved effective initially, achieving higher performance and more robust adaptation to the highly variable clinical language across different institutions requires fine-tuning.

A common method for maximizing a fine-tuned model’s performance across multiple sites is joint fine-tuning, a process that requires the centralized pooling of multi-site EHR data. This centralized pooling is frequently infeasible due to strict privacy governance, data barriers, and the legal risks associated with sharing protected health information across institutional firewalls. To overcome this bottleneck, we evaluated a framework utilizing sequential multi-site fine-tuning. To make localized training computationally feasible, we employed low-rank adaptation (LoRA),19 a technique that drastically reduces training overhead by freezing the pretrained model and updating only small, inserted low-rank matrices.20 An institution fine-tunes the model locally using their own data and subsequently shares only the updated LoRA weight matrices with the collaborating institution. This architectural choice allows the model to incrementally accumulate cross-institutional clinical knowledge while keeping raw patient data within its originating site.

The sequential adaptation of LLMs across independent healthcare institutions intrinsically intersects with the paradigm of continual learning in biomedical NLP. A primary challenge in sequential updating is catastrophic forgetting, wherein adapting to new institutional data distributions degrades performance on previously learned distributions. Recent benchmarking efforts, such as MedCL-Bench, emphasize that continual learning outcomes are heavily governed by task order permutations and stability-efficiency trade-offs.21 Furthermore, frameworks like BioMistral-Clinical have successfully utilized incremental learning combined with retrieval-augmented generation to adapt models to clinical notes while preserving foundational knowledge.22

In this study, we evaluated a sequential LoRA fine-tuning workflow for incremental cross-institutional adaptation of an LLM-based mobility functional status extractor. The proposed workflow allows each site to update the model locally and transfer the adapted LoRA parameters to the next site, enabling the model to accumulate cross-site mobility-related linguistic patterns while keeping raw clinical notes within the originating institution.5,7,19 This deployment setting is clinically relevant because mobility limitations are prognostically important and are frequently documented in narrative notes, making scalable extraction across heterogeneous institutions an important informatics objective.1,2,7

The objectives of our study were threefold. First, we aimed to compare full fine-tuning and LoRA fine-tuning applied to the LLaMA-3.1–8B model23 on a multi-site dataset, evaluating performance against both untuned 8B and larger 70B baselines. Additionally, we investigated the generalizability of LoRA models trained exclusively on one site’s data when tested across other sites, assessing robustness to site-specific variations. Finally, we examined sequential multi-site LoRA fine-tuning to explore whether incremental learning across multiple sites improves overall performance by accumulating task-specific knowledge. Our primary aim was to establish sequential multi-site fine-tuning as an effective and practical strategy for incrementally building a shared multi-site model for automated mobility status extraction.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

We utilized a corpus of 600 electronic clinical notes collected from 3 institutions—an academic referral medical center and 2 community-based practices in the upper Midwest—as described in our previous study.7 Legal agreements between these institutions preclude direct comparisons of data across institutions; therefore, the sites are designated by number (sites 1-3). Of these notes, 200 were physical therapy (PT) or occupational therapy (OT) notes from Site 1, 200 were PT/OT notes from Site 2, and 200 were unrestricted clinical notes from Site 3. PT/OT notes typically contain extensive information on patient mobility, whereas the inclusion of general clinical notes from Site 3 helps illustrate the broader applicability of our method. To facilitate focused and coherent annotation, each clinical note was divided into sections. The section-level annotations used in the current study were derived from the same manually annotated mobility functional status corpus developed in our previous work.7 In that work, the detailed annotation guidelines were developed based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)24 taxonomy and refined through an iterative consensus process involving domain experts and biomedical informaticians. We focused on 5 mobility classes: “Changing and maintaining body position,” “Carrying, moving, and handling objects,” “Walking and moving,” “Moving around using transportation,” and “Mobility, unspecified.” In each section, a trained annotator reviewed all mobility classes and assigned the appropriate impairment status. If neither label was applied, it meant that no mobility-related information appeared for that class in that section. A class was marked “Impaired” whenever the text described any difficulties, limitations, potential issues, or challenges; it was marked “Unimpaired” when it explicitly portrayed normal function or ability.

In total, the annotation process yielded 3810 labeled sections of text. For model development, we partitioned the data into training and test sets. We allocated 1200 sections for fine-tuning, with 400 sections from each site to ensure balanced representation. The training dataset did not contain any identifiable, sensitive patient information. The remaining 2610 sections (those not in the training set) were held out for testing. All 3 sites contributed sections to the test set, allowing us to evaluate model performance both overall and on each site’s data.

Model architecture and fine-tuning strategies

We utilized Meta AI’s LLaMA 3.1 language model as the foundation for our experiments. We used the LLaMA-3.1–8B model (8 billion parameters) as the primary model to fine-tune, due to its relatively manageable size for fine-tuning while still being a state-of-the-art Transformer-based LLM. LLaMA 3.1 is a pretrained decoder-style language model trained on a large general corpus; it has demonstrated strong performance on diverse NLP tasks and serves as a robust starting point for adaptation. To ensure a rigorous and highly optimized starting point, all baseline and fine-tuned models in this study utilized the best-performing “error-informed prompt” configuration established in our previous work.7

As a point of comparison, we also included the largest variant, LLaMA-3.1–70B (70 billion parameters), as an untuned baseline. The 70B model was not fine-tuned on our dataset; instead, it was used in a zero-shot fashion to gauge how a very large pretrained model performs on mobility classification without task-specific training. This baseline helped contextualize the benefits of fine-tuning the smaller 8B model with domain data.

We fine-tuned the 8B model using 2 approaches: Standard Fine-Tuning and LoRA Fine-Tuning. The LoRA approach drastically reduced the number of trainable parameters—in our setup, less than 1% of the model’s parameters were updated. For both standard fine-tuning and LoRA fine-tuning, we used the default fine-tuning recipes and configuration settings from torchtune, a PyTorch-native library for post-training LLMs.25 For standard fine-tuning, the default recipe used a learning rate of 2e−5, an effective batch size of 2, and the fused AdamW optimizer. For LoRA fine-tuning, the default recipe used a learning rate of 3e−4, an effective batch size of 16, the fused AdamW optimizer, and a weight decay of 0.01. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 10, with early stopping implemented to terminate training when validation performance no longer improved. All experiments were conducted using 4 NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs. This hardware configuration was sufficient to fine-tune the 8B model using LoRA without out-of-memory issues and also supported standard full fine-tuning of the 8B model.

Experimental setup

We designed 3 experimental scenarios corresponding to our objectives, using the above models and training methods.

Full dataset fine-tuning vs LoRA vs 70B baseline

As shown in Figure 1A, we trained 2 versions of the LLaMA-8B model on the entire training set of 1200 sections (combining all 3 sites): one with full fine-tuning and one with LoRA (rank-8). After training, we evaluated each model on the held-out test sections from all sites (n = 2610), reporting performance on the combined multi-site test set as well as on the individual site-specific test sets. For context, we evaluated the untuned LLaMA-8B and LLaMA-70B models on the same test set. We assessed the performance gains achieved by the 2 fine-tuning methods. We compared the untuned 70B model’s performance to the fine-tuned 8B models to determine whether task-specific fine-tuning of a smaller model can surpass the out-of-the-box capabilities of a significantly larger model.

Overview of 3 fine-tuning strategies used to evaluate large language model performance and cross-site generalizability using multi-site clinical note sections. (A) Multi-site fine-tuning: LLaMA-8B is fine-tuned on the combined training set from all 3 sites using either full fine-tuning or low-rank adaptation (LoRA) fine-tuning, then evaluated on the combined multi-site test set and each site-specific test set, alongside untuned LLaMA-8B and LLaMA-70B baselines. (B) Single-site LoRA fine-tuning: 3 separate LoRA adapters are trained independently using data from a single site (one model per site), and each adapted model is evaluated on all site-specific test sets and the combined multi-site test set. (C) Sequential multi-site LoRA adaptation: starting from the base LLaMA-8B model, LoRA fine-tuning is performed sequentially across sites (Site 1 → Site 2 → Site 3), with interim and final models evaluated on site-specific and the combined test sets.

Single-site LoRA training (cross-site evaluation)

This experiment assesses generalizability across institutions. As shown in Figure 1B, we trained 3 separate LoRA adapters (all on the LLaMA-8B base model), each using training data from only one site (400 sections). In each case, we started from the original pretrained weights and applied LoRA fine-tuning on that single-site subset. We thus obtained 3 distinct models: Model-A (trained on Site 1’s sections only), Model-B (trained on Site 2), and Model-C (trained on Site 3). We evaluated each model on both the individual site-specific test sets and the combined multi-site test set. This means, for instance, Model-A was tested not only on unseen sections from Site 1, but also on sections from Site 2 and Site 3 (which it never saw during training). We were interested in how the performance of a site-specific model drops when applied to other sites’ data, which would indicate domain shift or lack of generalization. For each of the 3 LoRA models, we measured overall metrics on the test set as well as metrics broken down by the site of the test sections. All 3 single-site models used the same LoRA configuration (r = 8, α = 16) and training procedure, just with different data. We did not do full fine-tuning for each site specifically, focusing on LoRA since it is more likely to avoid overfitting on only 400 examples.

Sequential multi-site LoRA adaptation

In the third experiment, we simulated a sequential fine-tuning approach to incorporate data from all sites incrementally, as shown in Figure 1C. We began by fine-tuning the LLaMA-8B model on one site’s training set using LoRA (eg, Site 1’s 400 sections). Instead of stopping there, we then took that resulting adapted model and continued fine-tuning it on a second site’s data (Site 2’s 400 sections) without resetting the weights. This effectively continues the training, hopefully adding Site 2 knowledge while retaining what was learned from Site 1. Finally, we continued to fine-tune the model on the third site’s data (Site 3’s 400 sections). Throughout this process, the LoRA adapters were updated continuously—essentially performing 3 stages of LoRA training one after the other. We refer to this as sequential LoRA fine-tuning across sites. (For consistency, we fixed the order of sites as Site 1 → Site 2 → Site 3; however, we recognize that different orderings could be explored, but that is outside our scope.) We evaluated the 2 interim models and the final model on both the individual site-specific test sets and the combined multi-site test set. The sequential approach mimics a real-world scenario where a model might be deployed in one institution and later adapted to another; we wanted to see if sequential fine-tuning is a viable alternative to pooling all data at once.

Throughout all experiments, no test data was ever seen during training or model selection. The model hyperparameters (like learning rate and epochs) were kept consistent across experiments as much as possible to ensure that differences in performance can be attributed to the training strategy or data differences rather than tuning advantages.

Evaluation metrics and analysis

We calculated performance at the class level to provide a more granular view of the models’ behavior. We also computed bootstrap confidence intervals for all metrics to enable robust performance comparisons. For each mobility class, we conducted a 2-step evaluation aligned with the dual subtasks: Mobility Extraction (Task 1) and Impairment Classification (Task 2).

Mobility extraction

For a given mobility class, we first identified whether the section contained relevant information for that class, using this as an indicator that the class was “Mentioned.” All other sections were considered “Not Mentioned.” We then treated “Mentioned” as the positive class and “Not Mentioned” as the negative class. The LLM’s binary predictions were compared against the human-labeled Mentioned/Not Mentioned status, and we computed F1-scores for each mobility class accordingly.

Impairment classification

This evaluation was restricted to sections that were confirmed as “Mentioned” for a given class. Within this subset, we assessed the LLM’s ability to correctly distinguish between “Impaired” and “Unimpaired.” That is, once a section was identified as pertaining to a particular mobility class, we evaluated whether the LLM-derived impairment status matched the human annotation. For each class, we calculated an F1-score with “Impaired” designated as the positive label. We also evaluated model performance using micro F1-scores across the 5 mobility classes. The micro F1-score aggregates precision and recall, offering a measure of overall classification performance irrespective of class imbalance.

Results

Performance on multi-site data: full fine-tuning vs LoRA vs untuned LLM

Figure 2A illustrates the micro F1-scores for Task 1 (Mobility Extraction) across 4 models (untuned 8B, untuned 70B, LoRA fine-tuned 8B, and standard fine-tuned 8B) on both the individual site-specific test sets and the combined multi-site test set. The standard fine-tuned 8B model achieved a micro F1-score of 0.881 [95% CI, 0.867-0.893], while the LoRA fine-tuned 8B model slightly outperformed it with a score of 0.894 [95% CI, 0.881-0.906]. Both tuned models significantly outperformed the untuned 8B model (0.620 [95% CI, 0.594-0.646] micro F1-score) as well as the much larger untuned 70B model (0.661 [95% CI, 0.637-0.683] micro F1-score), indicating the importance of task-specific adaptation over raw model size.

Micro F1-scores of two tasks across 4 model configurations on site-specific and multi-site test sets. Error bars represent the 95% confidence interval. (A) Mobility extraction performance. (B) Impairment classification performance.

Figure 2B presents the corresponding results for Task 2 (Impairment Classification). Here, the standard fine-tuned 8B model achieved a micro F1-score of 0.892 [95% CI, 0.876-0.908], and the LoRA fine-tuned model performed similarly with a score of 0.900 [95% CI, 0.885-0.914]. Similar to Task 1, both adapted models substantially outperformed the untuned 8B model (0.744 [95% CI, 0.712-0.773] micro F1-score) and the untuned 70B model (0.796 [95% CI, 0.771-0.818] micro F1-score).

Cross-site generalizability: single-site training

Figure 3A presents the performance of the untuned 8B model and 3 LoRA-8B models trained on single-site data for Task 1. All LoRA fine-tuned models demonstrated consistent robustness across test sets (ie, higher performance than the baseline untuned 8B model). The model trained on each single-site dataset achieved the highest micro F1-score on its own test set.

Micro F1-scores of two tasks across 4 model configurations on site-specific and multi-site test sets. Error bars represent the 95% confidence interval. (A) Mobility extraction performance. (B) Impairment classification performance.

Figure 3B shows the corresponding results for Task 2. Again, all 3 LoRA-8B models showed consistently higher performance than the baseline, with each model achieving the highest micro F1-score on its own site.

Across both tasks, each model performed best on its respective training site, with the most substantial home-site advantage observed in the Site 3-trained model. Nonetheless, all site-specific models demonstrated promising cross-site generalizability, with combined test set scores remaining within a narrow and competitive range. These findings suggest that LoRA fine-tuning on individual sites is sufficient to capture institution-specific documentation patterns while retaining strong cross-site performance.

Importantly, while single-site models performed well, they consistently underperformed relative to the multi-site-trained models from Experiment 1 on the combined test set. This emphasizes the benefit of diverse training data: multi-site exposure allows models to learn a broader set of mobility-related linguistic patterns, enhancing robustness and generalization across heterogeneous clinical settings.

Sequential fine-tuning across sites

The sequential LoRA fine-tuning experiment resulted in a single model incrementally adapted across datasets from 3 sites. Figure 4A illustrates the performance trajectory of this model on Task 1 after each training phase. Following Phase 1 (training on Site 1 data), the model exhibited significant performance improvements, with micro F1-scores increasing by approximately 32% for Site 1, 26% for Site 2, and 48% for Site 3, leading to a combined improvement of around 32%, aligning with prior single-site training trends. After further fine-tuning on Site 2 data (Phase 2), incremental but stable gains were observed across all sites. The subsequent Phase 3 fine-tuning with Site 3 data resulted in substantial additional improvements, notably a further increase of about 19% for Site 3, achieving the highest overall performance.

Error bars represent the 95% confidence interval; data points at each stage are slightly offset horizontally to prevent overlap. (A) Mobility extraction performance. (B) Impairment classification performance.

Figure 4B presents analogous trends for Task 2. After Phase 1, micro F1-score increased by about 14% for Site 1, 9% for Site 2, and 24% for Site 3, collectively yielding a 13% combined improvement. Additional fine-tuning during Phase 2 further enhanced performance moderately, and by Phase 3, following Site 3 fine-tuning, the model attained peak performance, particularly marked by a 53% total increase for Site 3 and an overall combined enhancement of approximately 23% from the original untuned state. The greater performance improvement observed for Tasks 1 and 2 after Phase 3 may be due to differences in data source characteristics—ie, Site 3 has general clinical notes and Sites 1 and 2 have PT/OT notes.

Error analysis

We identified key error sources including ambiguous definitions, limited medical knowledge, and improper content inclusion. First, semantic overlaps between mobility concepts led to inconsistent classifications. For example, repeated falls were often misclassified as “Walking and moving” instead of “Changing and maintaining body position,” which better reflects postural control issues. Wheelchair use was inconsistently labeled across “Walking and moving” and “Moving around using transportation”; we resolved this by assigning all wheelchair-related mentions to the latter for clarity. Second, the model struggled with clinical assessments due to limited medical domain knowledge. It often failed to interpret quantitative tools like the Focus on Therapeutic Outcomes (FOTO) and the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, which are key indicators of mobility impairment, especially in the “Mobility, unspecified” category. Lastly, the model frequently misclassified treatment plans, suggestions, and goals as evidence of current mobility status, despite clear prompt instructions to exclude such speculative or advisory content.

Discussion

The main contribution of this study is the evaluation of a deployment-oriented sequential LoRA adaptation strategy for multi-site mobility functional status extraction. Prior functional status NLP work has demonstrated the value of rule-based, hybrid, federated, and prompt-based approaches for extracting functional information from clinical narratives.5,7 Building on this foundation, our study shows that an LLM can be incrementally adapted across institutional datasets using parameter-efficient fine-tuning, achieving performance comparable to centralized joint fine-tuning while supporting a workflow in which raw notes remain local.

Our findings highlight that LoRA-based adaptation not only matches but slightly exceeds the performance of full fine-tuning in this domain-specific classification task. These findings mirror the results reported in the original LoRA paper, in which LoRA yielded equal or better task performance compared to full fine-tuning across models such as RoBERTa, DeBERTa XXL, GPT‑2, and GPT‑3.19 Recent multi‑task and instruction‑tuning work also shows that with proper rank configuration, LoRA can match or even outperform full fine‑tuning on domain‑adapted tasks.26

Furthermore, the results underscore a key insight: large pretrained LLMs like the 70B variant, when left untuned, lack sufficient sensitivity to capture clinically nuanced text classification tasks. In contrast, both standard fine-tuned 8B and LoRA fine-tuned 8B models—despite being substantially smaller—were able to effectively learn mobility-related cues from relatively limited annotated data. Notably, the LoRA-tuned 8B model achieved these results while modifying only a tiny fraction of the model’s parameters, demonstrating the practical value of parameter-efficient tuning in real-world clinical NLP applications.

In Experiment 3, we observed consistent improvements across both tasks without degrading performance on previously learned sites. The only minor fluctuation was in Task 1 on Site 1 data between Phases 1 and 2, where F1 slightly changed from 0.861 [95% CI, 0.833-0.885] to 0.860 [95% CI, 0.832-0.884]. This stability likely reflects the homogeneity of the classification task across institutions and the capacity of LoRA adapters to retain previously acquired knowledge without severe interference.27

When comparing multi-site sequential fine-tuning to all-at-once joint fine-tuning, performance was closely aligned. Notably, sequential fine-tuning outperformed joint fine-tuning on Task 2, whereas the joint fine-tuned model had a slight edge on Task 1. These results suggest that sequential fine-tuning is a viable strategy when simultaneous access to all data is infeasible, such as in settings with privacy, governance, or operational constraints. In practice, institutions could collaborate to incrementally improve a shared model without exchanging raw notes. This workflow is particularly relevant for clinical NLP applications, where institutional documentation styles vary and direct pooling of EHR data is frequently challenging.

We therefore characterize this framework as privacy-aware and data-minimizing: raw clinical text remains within each site, and only the adapted LoRA parameters are transferred between sites. However, transferred model updates may still leak sensitive information, although the risk is generally low. Prior studies have shown that gradients, parameter updates, and fine-tuned language models may be vulnerable to inversion or membership-inference attacks.28–30 These findings highlight the importance of pairing sequential LoRA workflows with additional safeguards such as secure aggregation, differential privacy, and formal privacy auditing in future real-world deployments.31,32

Several considerations should be noted. This study included 3 healthcare sites from a single Midwest region and used 1200 annotated training sections, providing a multi-site proof of concept for sequential LoRA adaptation in mobility functional status extraction. Although this training set is modest relative to large-scale instruction-tuning corpora, it is consistent with prior evidence that pretrained LLMs can be effectively adapted with relatively small, high-quality task-specific datasets. For example, LIMA demonstrated strong supervised fine-tuning performance using 1000 carefully curated prompt-response examples, and LoRA has been shown to achieve performance comparable to full fine-tuning while updating only a small fraction of model parameters.19,33 Recent clinical NLP studies have also demonstrated the utility of parameter-efficient fine-tuning, including LoRA and QLoRA, for specialized information extraction tasks under limited-data settings.34,35 Larger evaluations across additional geographic regions, health systems, specialties, and documentation styles will further characterize robustness and generalizability.

The sequential experiment used a fixed order, which reflects one incremental deployment pathway across the available sites. Because continual learning performance can be influenced by site order, evaluating additional site sequences will provide a more complete understanding of retention and transfer across institutions. Future deployment studies should also incorporate formal privacy auditing to further evaluate potential information leakage from shared model updates.

Ultimately, improving the accuracy of functional status NLP tools has significant clinical implications: it could enable real-time monitoring of mobility in aging patients, early identification of functional decline in chronic illness, and large-scale research on functional outcomes using EHR data.

Conclusion

Sequential multi-site fine-tuning provides an effective approach for incrementally adapting LLMs to mobility functional status extraction from clinical notes. In this 3-site study, sequential LoRA adaptation achieved performance comparable to centralized joint fine-tuning while keeping raw clinical notes within the originating institutions. This data-minimizing workflow offers a practical pathway for scalable functional status monitoring using EHR narratives and supports future multicenter deployment of LLM-based clinical information extraction systems.
